Nanocomposite-loaded hybrid microneedles reshape dermal microenvironment through senolytic delivery and collagen XVII-mediated barrier repair
Authors: Fan Yang, Yahui Xiong, Shuying Chen, Fan Bie, Zhanpeng Li, Hengdeng Liu, Lijuan Liu, Zhaoqiang Zhang, Lei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Skin aging exacerbates chronic skin diseases, malignancies, and systemic aging. During the aging process, ultraviolet radiation induces oxidative stress, DNA damage, and senescence, resulting in dermal extracellular matrix (ECM) microenvironmental dysregulation. Based on this, reshaping strategies aiming at the dermal ECM microenvironment should center on two key mechanisms: (1) remodel the ECM microenvironment by removing senescent cells (SenCs); (2) restore the epidermal barrier function to maintain the homeostasis of the dermal ECM microenvironment. To achieve dual-action therapy, we propose a hybrid hydrogel microneedle system (SC/F-HMNs) that synergizes fisetin-loaded nanoparticles with recombinant human collagen XVII (rhCol17)-reinforced silk fibroin hydrogel. The SC/F-HMNs exhibited superior mechanical strength and sustained drug release, enabling deep dermal penetration. In UVA-irradiated human skin fibroblasts (HSFs), the SC/F hydrogel enhanced cell viability and migration capacity and promoted ECM remodeling, significantly reducing levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and DNA damage, and attenuating senescence. In UVA-irradiated photoaged mice, SC/F-HMNs treatment exhibited improvement in skin wrinkles and elasticity. Additionally, in vivo studies demonstrated that SC/F-HMNs decreased oxidative stress, DNA damage, and restored barrier function, effectively reshaping the dermal ECM environment, including the collagen and elastic pattern, as well as MMP1,3 expression. Our data demonstrate the therapeutic potential of SC/F-HMNs in reshaping the dermal microenvironmental dysregulation caused by oxidative stress in photoaging, thereby providing an innovative therapeutic strategy for treating skin diseases related to dermal microenvironmental dysregulation.

Introduction

Skin aging is driven by a combination of internal and external factors, with oxidative stress playing a key role [1,2]. It not only affects the appearance but is also closely associated with systemic dysfunction of the organism [[3], [4], [5]], thus becoming a significant concern in the modern medical field.

The dysregulation of the dermal ECM microenvironment, with oxidative stress acting as a central molecular driver, constitutes a critical nexus between localized skin aging and systemic pathological manifestations [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Emerging evidence highlights two mechanisms for hindering this process: (1) ECM microenvironment remodeling through selective clearance of senescent cells (SenCs), and (2) maintenance of dermal homeostasis via repairing the epidermal barrier [10,11]. Senescent cells (SenCs) are a dysfunctional state characterized by stable cell cycle arrest [12]. In the dermis, excessive ROS causes double-strand breaks (DSB) of DNA damage, resulting in senescent fibroblasts exhibiting dysfunction in proliferation, migration, and ECM synthesis (impaired transforming growth factor-β signaling) [13,14]. Moreover, SenCs by producing a complex secretome such as pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), collectively defined as senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs) [15,16]. The increasing SASPs (such as IL-6, 8, and MMP-1, 3, 9) in skin promote ECM degradation, and exacerbate the dermal inflammatory microenvironment with fragmented ECM components, creating a vicious cycle of “inflammation-oxidative stress” [[17], [18], [19]]. From an epidermal barrier perspective, UV-induced disruption of epidermal homeostasis by activating excessive self-repair processes of skin [20]. These processes involve initiating inflammatory responses and releasing cytokines and chemokines, which impact the dermis by significantly disturbing the ECM microenvironment homeostasis [21].

SenCs play an important role in the disruption of the ECM microenvironment in various tissues [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Recent researches reported that eliminating SenCs to remodel the microenvironment exhibited therapeutic potential in osteoporosis, fibrosis, and intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) [26,27]. However, few research has been reported for the dermal microenvironment with this strategy. Fisetin, a class senolytic drug to selectively remove SenCs, demonstrated excellent potential for eliminating senescent dermal fibroblasts in vitro studies [28,29]. However, its therapeutic effect is hindered by the poor pharmacokinetic properties (low aqueous solubility, chemical instability) and nonspecific cytotoxicity at therapeutic doses [30]. Previous studies have applied nanotechnology, including nanoemulsions and liposomes, for fisetin delivery through oral or injection administration [[31], [32], [33]], which have limitations such as low local concentration and unclear systemic toxicity for skin application. Other groups have reported application of fisetin-loaded conventional cream, nanovesicles, and binary ethosomes to the skin surface [[34], [35], [36]]. This reveals that the topical administration of fisetin still faces challenges to increase deep skin penetration efficiency, controlled drug release rates, and enhance targeting effect in dermal fibroblasts. Additionally, our previous research found that synergistic epidermal repair has positive effects on the healthy remodeling of the dermal ECM in skin, supporting the importance of synergistic epidermal treatment. This strategy has not been fully developed, and its evidence and applications are still insufficient.

Nanoparticles have been demonstrated to enhance the stability and safety of flavonoids. For dermal fisetin delivery, hyaluronic acid (HA)-modified liposome may be an ideal nano-carrier due to the affinity of HA to CD44, which is widely expressed in dermal fibroblasts [[37], [38], [39]]. To overcome the epidermal barrier and permeate fisetin-nanocarriers into the dermis, achieve even distribution and controlled release, we propose a nanocomposite-hydrogel microneedle system. Emerging evidence reveals the critical role of Col17 in the ECM microenvironment and skin aging [40]. Recent studies demonstrated therapeutic effects of recombinant human collagen XVII (rhCol17) in improving ECM remodeling, anti-inflammatory, and protecting skin basement membrane integrity [41,42]. Given the excellent performance of silk fibroin (SF) in mechanical strength and biocompatibility, the SF-reinforced rhCol17 hydrogel is promising for the flexible regulation of properties and achieving complementary function [43,44]. Nanocarriers with hydrogel microneedles (MNs) offer an effective transdermal drug delivery system. Combining rhCOL17 hydrogel microneedle with the fisetin-loaded nanoparticles described above is expected to produce enhanced anti-oxidative stress and anti-aging modulation from both cellular and ECM dimensions and positive synergistic effects on therapy.

Herein, fisetin-loaded HA-modified liposomes (HL@F) were encapsulated in a hybrid hydrogel-based microneedle system composed of glycidyl methacrylated silk fibroin (SFMA) and methacrylated rhCol17 (rhCol17MA) (Fig. 1). The fabricated SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F)-HMNs were characterized for their physicochemical properties and biocompatibility to ensure effective transdermal drug delivery. Using an in vitro photoaging model induced by oxidative stress, the effects of SC/F-HMNs on the selective removal of Senescent fibroblasts and SASPs were investigated, which is beneficial for remodeling the ECM microenvironment. Experiments in photoaged mice not only confirmed these effects, illustrating the reshaping of the dermal microenvironment, but also demonstrated the homeostasis of the epidermal barrier, which can further maintain the dermal microenvironment. Our data showed that SC/F-HMNs held significant potential for alleviating oxidative stress and ECM reshaping in UVA-induced photoaging. In summary, we presented an innovative therapeutic strategy for addressing skin ECM microenvironmental dysfunction by efficient senolytic delivery and barrier function repair. Addressing those conditions in photoaging may also offer a promising therapeutic avenue for these clinical disorders, which are characterized by oxidative stress-induced dermal ECM microenvironment dysregulation.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis and mechanism of SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F hydrogel microneedles (SC/F-HMNs) in reshaping the dermal microenvironment under UVA-irradiation. Materials and methods

Materials

Hyaluronic acid (HA, MW = 100,000–200000), glycidyl methacrylate, and methacrylic anhydride were purchased from McKlin (Shanghai, China). Recombinant human collagen XVII was purchased from Jiangsu Trautec Medical Technology Co.,Ltd. Fisetin (purity 99.99 %) was purchased from MedChemExpress (USA). 1-hydroxypyrrolidine-25-dione,1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-thyl carbonized diimide hydrochloride were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Monoclonal antibodies including MMP1(10371-2-AP, Biotech) and MMP3(17873-1-AP, Biotech), 4-HNE(bs-6313R, Bioss), 8-OHdG(bs-1278R, Bioss), NRF2(GB113808, Servicebio), HO-1(10701-1-AP, Proteintech), γH2A.X(GB111841, servcebio), Vimentin (GB11192, servcebio), AQP3(GB11395, servicebio), CK-19(60187-1-AP, Proteintech), CK-5(GB111246-100, servicebio), CK-10 (GB112105-100, servicebio), were used for immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining. Monoclonal antibodies against TGF-β (Abcam, ab92486), Collagen I (CST, cst91144s), Collagen III (Abcam, ab7778), and GAPDH (CST, cst2118) were used for protein detection.

Preparation and characterization of HL@F

HA-DOPE conjugation was synthesized according to established methods [45]. The HA solution was mixed with EDC and NHS for activation for 2 h at pH 4. DOPE was then added and reacted at pH 8.6 for 24 h, and HA-DOPE was obtained after dialysis. Potassium bromide powder (5 %, w/v) was added to the HA-DOPE and pressed with a vacuum compressor (20 mmHg, 5 min) to obtain a thin slice sample for further detection under a Fourier infrared spectrometer (4000-5000 cm−1). Liposomes were prepared using the thin-film hydration method [39]. FI, DOPE, and cholesterol were ultrasonically dispersed in methyl alcohol. After filtration and evaporation, thin-film lipids were obtained. HA-DOPE was then used to hydrate the thin-film lipid, and HL@F was produced by intermittent ultrasonic dispersion at 4 °C for 10 min. The solution was homogeneously dispersed, and the particle size and distribution of the liposome solution were determined using a Zeta-Sizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, UK).

Cellular uptake test

HSFs were seeded in 6-well plates divided into three groups: (A)treated with FITC (control); (B) treated with FITC-labeled unmodified liposomes (FITC-L@F); (C) treated with FITC-labeled hyaluronic acid-modified liposomes (FITC-HL@F); (D)pretreated with free hyaluronic acid (HA, 1 mg/mL) for 30 min to block the CD44 receptor, and then incubated with FITC-HL@F(HA + FITC-HL@F) to assess competitive binding. After incubating at 37 °C for 2 h, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS and digested with 0.25 % trypsin to collect the cells by centrifugation. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using the FITC channel, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each group was quantified to evaluate the cellular uptake of different treatments.

Synthesis and characterization of SFMA/rhCol17MA(SC) hydrogel

The synthesis of SFMA and rhCol17MA was carried out by chemically modifying silk fibroin and rhCol17 (Fig. S1a). Silk cocoons were soaked in Na2CO3 solution (0.05 M) and boiled at 100 °C for 30 min to obtain silk fibroin (SF). SF was dissolved in a lithium bromide (9.3 M) solution for 1 h at 60 °C, followed by reaction with glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) for 8 h. The resulting product was dialyzed for 3 days. The obtained SFMA was then freeze-dried for further testing. Similarly, rhCol17 was fully dissolved in distilled water, and its pH was adjusted to 7.0. Methacrylate anhydride (MA) was added to the solution for an overnight reaction. The product was dialyzed (MW: 3500 Da) for 3 days, and rhCol17MA was obtained and freeze-dried. FTIR spectra (Vertex 70, Bruker, Germany) and 1H NMR spectra (Inova 500 M, Varian, USA) were used to characterize the products.

Swelling ability test

The swelling ability of the hydrogels was tested using the gravimetric method [46]. The initial weight of the samples (W0) was recorded. After being immersed in PBS solution at 37 °C and removed at specific time points (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h), the swelling weight (Wt) was recorded. The swelling ratio of the hydrogels was calculated using the following formula:Swelling ratio (%) = (Wt- W0) /W0 × 100 %

Rheological assessment

The rheological behavior of the hydrogel samples was tested using a TA rheometer (Kinexus, UK) with a 25 mm parallel plate. Dynamic strain scanning was performed from 0.1 to 10 rad/s at room temperature. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were obtained through further calculations.

Compression tests

The deformability of SC hydrogels was measured using a universal testing machine (Electroforce3220; Bose, USA). The hydrogel was placed under a compression sensor and pressed at a speed of 0.05 mm/s to 60 % deformation to obtain a stress-strain curve.

Degradation test in vitro

The degradation tests of SC hydrogels were conducted under PBS and collagenase solution. The initial weight of the samples (W0) was recorded. In PBS Group, the hydrogel samples were immersed in PBS solution at 37 °C. Samples are collected on days 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, and 28 of the experiment and then freeze-dried. In the collagenase group, samples are taken at time points of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 68 h and freeze-dried. The weight of the hydrogel is recorded as Wt. The degradation rate of the gel is calculated.Degradation (%)=(W0 - Wt) / W0

Preparation and characterization of SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F) hydrogel

Degradation test of fisetin

Dissolve fisetin in a methanol solution and measure the UV absorption spectrum of fisetin using a UV spectrophotometer. After irradiating the fisetin solution with ultraviolet light for 20 s, measure the full UV absorption spectrum of fisetin again using the UV spectrophotometer.

Preparation of SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F) hydrogel

SFMA and rhCol17MA were added to the LAP solution (0.1 %, w/v) in a specific concentration ratio and mixed with HL@F under light-free conditions. The solution was exposed to UV light (350 nm) for 20 s to form the SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F (SC/F) hydrogel.

Drug release test

Fisetin-loaded samples were placed in PBS solution and incubated at 37 °C. Supernatant samples were collected at specific time points (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, 168 h) and analyzed for fisetin content using a UV spectrophotometer (UV1800, SHIMADZU). The cumulative release rate was calculated as:Cumulative Release Rate (%) = Drug Release/Total Drug Content × 100 %

Cell viability assay

The hydrogel was immersed in a complete medium consisting of basal medium, 5 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1 % antibiotics at a ratio of 10 % (v/v). Hydrogel extracts were obtained after incubation at 37 °C for 72 h on a shaker, according to previous methods [47]. The culture media of different groups were as follows: NC: complete cell medium; SC group: SFMA/rhCol17MA hydrogel extract; SC/F10 to SC/F40 group: SC/F hydrogel with HL@F concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 μM. Human skin fibroblasts (HSFs, iCell Bioscience, China) were cultured in 96-well plates at a seeding concentration of 4500 cells/well for 6 h. The complete medium was then replaced with the corresponding culture medium for each group. After 24, 48, and 72 h of incubation, 10 μL of CCK8 solution was added to each well, and the optical density was measured by a Multiskan Sky Microplate spectrophotometer (Thermofish, USA) at 450 nm.

Live/dead staining

HSFs were co-cultured with different groups of hydrogels for 24, 48, and 72 h based on previous methods [48]. The Calcein AM/PI detection kit (Beyotime, China) was used for live/dead cell staining, and samples were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Hemolysis test

The hemolysis test was conducted according to previously established methods [49]. Briefly, the fresh arterial blood collected from the rats was diluted with saline solution (1:16). The extract solutions (24 h) of SC and SC/F hydrogels were used for hemolysis evaluation, while PBS and deionized water were respectively used as negative controls (NC) and positive controls (PC). 1 mL samples and 0.05 mL diluted blood were cultured for 1 h at 37 °C. The hemolysis ratio was calculated by the formula:Hemolysis ratio (%) = (ODsamples -ODpc)/(ODpc -ODnc) × 100 %

Preparation and characterization of the SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F)-HMN

Preparation of the SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F)-HMN

The hydrogel MN patch was fabricated as previously described [50]. The hydrogel solution was spread on the surface of the MN mold and placed in a vacuum drying oven (-0.1 MPa) for 5 min. This process was repeated to remove air bubbles and fill the tips of the MN. The hydrogel was crosslinked under UV light for 5 min. PVA solution was then added to the surface of the MN mold and dried for 4 h. Afterward, the microneedles were prepared and detached from the mold.

Microneedle appearance and electron microscopy

The morphology of the microneedles was observed by scanning electron microscopy after gold was sprayed on their surface for 30 s. The average pore size of the microneedles was calculated using Nanomeasure Software.

Microneedle fluorescence Stereoscopy

The tip of the rhodamine-loaded hydrogel microneedle was placed face up on a slide and scanned in the x, y, and z axes (wavelength 546 nm) using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany). The scanned images were synthesized in 3D using ZEN software.

Mechanical strength test of microneedle tips

The mechanical strength of the microneedles was tested using a Texture Analysis Machine compression sensor. Compression was applied at 10 mm/s until the microneedle tips deformed by 90 %. Force and displacement were plotted to illustrate the hardness of the MN tips.

Skin penetration test

Rhodamine B was used as a substitute for HL@F. Rhodamine B-loaded MNs were firmly pressed into freshly cut, full-thickness pig skin and remained in place for 2 h. After removing the MNs, fluorescence signals were detected using confocal laser microscopy (Olympus, Japan). Fluorescence images were obtained along the z-axis at scanning intervals of 60 μm to observe the diffusion of the microneedle-loaded drug in the skin.

Microneedle biodegradation test in vivo

MNs were applied to the dorsal skin of mice and taped securely. MNs were removed after 24, 48, and 72 h and observed under a stereoscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Skin press test in vivo

After MN removal, the dorsal skin was observed at 30-min intervals to observe the skin recovery.

UVA-induced photoaging model in vitro

Long wavelengths of UVA can penetrate the dermis, leading to oxidative stress damage, which is considered a major driver of photoaging [51]. We chose human skin fibroblasts (HSFs, iCell Bioscience, China) and divided them into five groups as NC (negative control): no UV exposure, no treatment; UVA group: UV exposure with no treatment; SM group: UV exposure and treatment with SFMA hydrogel; SC group: UV exposure and treat with SFMA/rhCol17MA hydrogel; SC/F group: UV exposure and treat with SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F hydrogel. The HSFs of each group were incubated in 24-well plates at a density of 3.2 × 104 per well overnight.

Based on previous studies, we established a UVA-induced photoaging model using constant UVA doses (3J/cm2) for three consecutive days, with a total of 9J/cm2 UVA (365 nm) irradiation conducted [[52], [53], [54]]. After the exposure of each day, the HSFs were cultured with the corresponding medium. When the process was over, the HSFs were prepared for further experiments.

EdU assay

We applied the EdU-555 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) to assess the proliferative capacity of different groups of HSFs. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the HSFs were incubated with 10 μM EdU solution for 4 h and then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for further staining. Stained cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) in at least five random fields and statistically analyzed using the software ImagePro Plus.

Scratch test

HSFs were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with UV and different hydrogel extracts till the cells covered the bottom of each well. Then, we applied scratches to the bottom of each well to form an approximately 800 μm wide gap. After rinsing with PBS, the cells were incubated and observed under a microscope (Olympus, Japan) at predetermined time points and further analyzed by using ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

The proteins from the HSFs and skin tissues were extracted and lysed by RIPA lysis buffer (MIK, China) for further reaction. The total concentration of the collected protein was calculated using the BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China). According to the protocol previously published, we separated and transferred the proteins to the PVDF membrane (Billerica, USA) [52]. Primary antibodies against TGF-β, Collagen I, Collagen III, and GAPDH were used for protein detection. A secondary antibody labeled with HRP (Abcam, ab205718, 1:10,000) was further used for antibody-reactive protein detection. The protein signals were subsequently detected by the Alpha Innotech FluorChem Imaging System (ProteinSimple, USA). The mean absorbance of each band was measured using ImageJ software. All the protein expressions were normalized to the expression of GAPDH.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Different groups of HSFs were assayed using a 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining ROS kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, intracellular ROS was assessed by DCFH-DA staining and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Evaluation of mitochondrial superoxide

To detect mitochondrial ROS, different groups of HSFs were incubated with MitoSOX™ Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Solarbio, China) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS, and images were captured under Ex/Em = 510/580 nm using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Evaluation of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

JC-1 staining was conducted to measure the mitochondrial membrane potential. Different groups of HSFs were incubated with JC-1 staining working solution (Servicebio, China) at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. After staining, the cells were washed with JC-1 staining buffer, and then JC-1 staining buffer was added to maintain cell viability. The red/green fluorescence ratio was determined using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cellular immunofluorescence analysis

The detection of γH2A.X expression was by using cellular immunofluorescence staining. We fixed HSFs with 4 % paraformaldehyde and increased the permeability of the cell membrane by using 1 % Triton-X100. Goat serum (ZSGB-BIO, China) was used for further protein blocking. HSFs were incubated with primary antibody γH2A.X (C2037S-4, Beyotime) overnight at 4 °C, and then with secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 488 goat antirabbit (C2037S-5, Beyotime) for 30 min at 37 °C. DAPI (C2037S-6, Beyotime) staining was used to label the nuclei at last. Images were collected under an FV3000 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cell cycle assay

We harvested HSFs from different groups and fixed them in 70 % ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Propidium iodide (PI) staining was performed using the Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (Beyotime, China). Then, cells were analyzed by Flow Cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman), and the cell-cycle distribution was statistically analyzed using FlowJo(10.6.2) software.

SA-β-galactosidase staining

We washed different groups of HSFs with PBS and fixed them with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Then, the β-Galactosidase Staining solution was used for CO2-free incubation of cells overnight at 37 °C. Finally, stained cells were imaged under the microscope (Olympus, Japan) to count the SenCs ratio.

RT-PCR analysis

The standard TRIzol method was applied for total RNA extraction. The cDNAs of SASP, including IL-8, MMP1, and MMP3, were designed and synthesized for subsequent PCR experiments by the Servicebio RT First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Servicebio, China), which was normalized with GAPDH. The results were processed by the ΔΔCT method.

UVA-induced photoaging mice model

Male BALB/c nude mice aged five weeks (18–22g) were obtained from Guangzhou Ruige Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China) for use in this study. Male mice were chosen for the study to decrease the potential influence on photoaging generated by endogenous estrogen [55]. All mice were housed in a standard environment (20–26 °C, light/dark cycle 12/12 h and 50 % humidity) and acclimated for seven days. All mice were randomly divided into four groups: NC group: no UVA irradiation; UVA group: UVA irradiation with no treatment. SC group: UVA irradiation and treatment with SFMA/rhCol17MA hydrogel MNs; SC/F group: UVA irradiation and treatment with SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F hydrogel MNs. The experimental area was a marked 5 mm∗5 mm area on the dorsal skin.

Based on previous studies, we established a photoaging mouse model through 8-week repetitive exposure to UVA [56,57]. UVA irradiation was conducted using the 365 nm UVA lamps (Philips, USA) and measured by a UVA radiometer (Linshang, China). In the UVA-exposed groups, the irradiation is 10 J/cm2 each time, repeated three times a week for 8 weeks (240 J/cm2 in total) [58,59]. In the treatment group, MNs were applied to the marked area of the dorsal skin and fixed for 72 h each time, and the procedure was repeated once a week for 8 weeks. At the 9th week, all groups were evaluated by further experiments.

Non-invasive evaluation

At the 9th week, a digital camera was used to take pictures of the dorsal skin. Silicone replicas (ASCH, Japan) were applied to the experimental area of dorsal skin and further observed under a stereoscope (Zeiss, Germany). The images were analyzed using ImageJ software. A non-invasive skin tester (Delfin, Finland) with VAPOMETER, MOISTUREMETERSC, and ELASMETER testers (Delfin, Finland) was used for the evaluation of trans-epidermal water loss(TEWL), hydration value, and elasticity of mice’s dorsal skin as previously described [60].

Histology evaluation

The experimental area of the dorsal skin was extracted and fixed with formalin. Subsequently, the slices were obtained after paraffin embedding and serial sectioning of specimens. We dewaxed and rehydrated the slices and stained them with H&E, Masson, Sirius Red, and Resorcinol staining. Furthermore, we conducted immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining with antibodies including 4-HNE, 8-OHdG, Nrf2, HO-1, γH2A.X, Vimentin, p21, MMP1, MMP3, AQP3, CK-19, CK-5, and CK-10, and observed slices under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). All images were analyzed using the software ImageJ.

Biosafety evaluation

Blood and major organs, including the liver and the kidney, were collected from sacrificed mice for biosafety evaluation. The serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activities, and creatinine (CREA) were measured using a Chemray automatic biochemical analyzer (Rayto, China). The organ tissues were fixed with formalin and further assessed by H&E staining.

ECM patterns analysis

According to the previous method, the ImageJ macro plugin TWOMBLI was used to quantify the matrix patterns in the Masson-stained images at the levels of lacunarity, total length, alignment, end points, and fractal dimension [61].

Data analysis

All experimental parts of this study were subjected to at least three statistical replications. The GraphPad Prism software (version 7.0) and ImageJ software (version 1.5.3) were used for the statistical analysis. Multiple comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The results were expressed as mean ± SD (standard deviation). p < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant, and p < 0.01 was considered extremely significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F) hydrogel

The FTIR analysis of HA, DOPE, and HA-DOPE is shown in Fig. S1. As shown in Fig. 2a, the morphology of HL and HL@F under the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) exhibited a regular spherical shape and were evenly distributed. The particle sizes were further analyzed and the results in Fig. 2b indicated that the particle size of HL is 114.7 ± 0.08 nm(PDI = 0.180), while particle size of HL@F has increased to 125.0 ± 1.22 nm(PDI = 0.168)To observe the cellular uptake and targeting effect of HA-Lip in HSFs, FITC as fluorescent probe was encapsulated in the regular liposomes(L@F) and HA-modified liposomes (HL@F). As shown in Fig. 2c, the increased fluorescence intensity of FITC in HL@F reveals enhanced cellular uptake compared with L@F. According to the specific affinity of HA and CD44, this performance can be attributed to CD44 receptors on HSFs mediated endocytosis. With the addition of HA, this promoting effect is significantly weakened according to the similar fluorescence intensity in HA + HL@F and L@F groups. SFMA and rhCol17MA were successfully synthesized according to the method (Fig. S2). NMR hydrogen spectra analysis of SF, SFMA, rhCol17, and rhCol17MA are shown in Fig. S3a–b, supplementary information. Peaks at 5.59 ppm and 6.02 ppm indicated successful grafting of GMA onto the SF protein, while new peaks at 5.34 and 5.58 ppm confirmed the successful modification of rhCol17 with MA. FTIR analysis results further confirmed the retention of functional groups in SF and rhCol17(Fig. S4a–b). To form stable and rigid tips for HMN, 10 % (v/w) SFMA was chosen for the preparation of SC hydrogel, which was demonstrated with good physical properties and biocompatibility in a previous study [62]. The swelling ratios of hydrogels with 10 % SFMA and varying concentrations of rhCol17MA (5 %, 10 %, and 15 %) were evaluated, showing rapid swelling followed by equilibrium within 10 h (Fig. 2d). The addition of 10 % and 15 % rhCol17MA maintained structural stability, essential for microneedle mechanical integrity. Rheological testing revealed that in all groups, the elastic modulus (G′) was higher than the viscous modulus (G″), indicating the stable gel formation necessary for microneedle tip formation (Fig. 2e). Compression testing showed that increasing the concentration of rhCol17MA significantly enhanced the compression modulus. The results in Fig. 2f showed that the compression modulus of the hybrid hydrogel can be significantly improved with the concentration of rhCol17MA. To avoid tip fragility, the concentration of 10 % SFMA/10 % rhCol17MA(SC) was chosen for further experiments. To further clarify the biodegradability of the SC hydrogel, we conducted degradation experiments on the hybrid hydrogel under PBS conditions and with collagenase. As shown in Fig. 2g, we observed that in a PBS environment, the degradation rate of the SC hydrogel was relatively slow, increasing gradually from 4.64 % to 22.17 % over 28 days, demonstrating good structural stability. In contrast, in an environment containing collagenase, the degradation of the hydrogel significantly accelerated, with the degradation rate rapidly rising to 86.48 % within 8 h. Notably, during the first 4 h, the degradation rate quickly increased from 22.9 % to 50.36 %, exhibiting the rapid response characteristics of enzyme-promoted degradation, indicating that the SC hydrogel as the MN matrix is biodegradable.

To form SFMA/rhCol17MA/HL@F(SC/F) hydrogel base of the HMNs, we conducted fisetin degradation experiments. The results in Fig. S5a indicated that UV exposure during the preparation of the SC/F hydrogel cross-linking step would not affect the stability of fisetin. UV-crosslinking experiment of SMFA/rhCol17MA/HL@F was performed, and the solution was rapidly crosslinked into SC/F hydrogel under UVA exposure (Fig. S5b). SC/F hydrogel encapsulating HL@F demonstrated a slower release of fisetin compared to HL, indicating improved control of drug release, with a linear release in the first 12 h, slowing at 72 h, reaching approximately 50 % (Fig. 2h). Cell viability was assessed by culturing human skin fibroblasts (HSFs) with hydrogel extracts (Fig. S6) and conducting a CCK-8 assay over three days [47]. The viability of HSFs after incubation with SC, SC/F10, and SC/F20 hydrogels was 110.1 ± 4.5 %, 99.6 ± 3.9 %, and 96.1 ± 7.1 %, respectively (vs NC, p > 0.05). However, viability dropped to 64.9 ± 11.0 % in the SC/F40 group, suggesting that 20 μM was the optimal concentration for further experiments (Fig. 2i). As shown in Fig. 2j, HSFs remained in a proliferative state with minimal cell death, consistent with the CCK-8 assay results. The results indicated that the SC/F hydrogel exhibited minimal cytotoxicity to HSFs. Hemolysis tests demonstrated good blood compatibility for the SC/F-HMNs hydrogel (Fig. 2k), with clotting time and hemolysis data shown in Fig. S7a–b.

Reshaping dermal ECM microenvironment by SC/F-HMNs in vivo

As a key feature of skin aging due to oxidative stress, dermal ECM experiences collagen and elastic fiber degradation along with reduced tissue regeneration [[80], [81], [82]]. This study demonstrated that SC/F-HMNs increased elasticity and reduced wrinkle formation in photoaged skin through non-invasive testing. Here, dermis thickness, collagen content, Col1/Col3 ratio, and elastic fibers were evaluated. The results in Fig. 8a-b showed that the dermal thickness was decreased in the UVA group compared to the NC group. SC/F-HMNs treatment brought the dermal thickness close to the normal dermis. As shown in Fig. 8c-d, the collagen content was reduced by UVA irradiation compared to the NC group, which was significantly restored by SC/F-HMNs treatment. The disruption of the dermal microenvironment triggered by oxidative stress can dramatically impact the morphology and distribution of collagen fibers [83]. To further examine the specific structure of collagen fibers, the images of Masson staining were analyzed using the extracellular matrix (ECM) patterns algorithm TWOMBLI [61,84]. As shown in Fig. 8e, the UVA group had the highest void degree and the shortest total length of fibers. In contrast, the SC-HMNs and SC/F-HMNs groups effectively reversed this trend, with a higher degree of reversal in the SC/F group. In terms of collagen fiber morphology, the degree of alignment, fragmentation, and complexity of collagen structure were analyzed. Compared to the UVA group, the collagen fibers of dermis treated in the SC/F-HMNs group were more aligned, with a low degree of fragmentation, and thus had a relatively simple structure, though not significantly different from that of the NC group. These results suggested that SC/F could repair the integrity of the dermal ECM by inhibiting oxidative stress-mediated collagen degradation while promoting ordered collagen fiber remodeling. Col1 and Col3 are the major collagen components of the ECM in the dermis [85]. As shown in Fig. 8f-g, the ratio of Col1/3 increased in the UVA groups. The Col1/3 ratio in the SC and SC/F-HMNs groups decreased compared to the UVA group. We presumed that rhCol17 promoted in situ tissue regeneration, given the evidence of ECM-like biomaterial such as rhCol3 in tissue engineering [56,86]. Another essential component of the dermal ECM is the elastic fiber. In photo-aged skin, elastic fibers degrade and shrink into clump-shaped fragments [82]. As shown in Fig. 8h and i, elastic fibers in the NC group exhibited a continuous linear distribution in the superficial layers of the dermis. In the UVA group, the elastic fibers tended to be fractured and condensed. We analyzed the expression of MMP1 and MMP3 using immunohistochemistry (IHC). To further investigate the oxidative stress-induced DSB in senescent fibroblasts with application of our device in photoaged skin, immunofluorescence staining for γH2A.X and vimentin (expressed in fibroblasts) was performed. γH2A.X+ cells were significantly increased in the epidermis and dermis of the UVA group, indicating UV-induced DNA damage (Fig. 9i). SC/F-HMNs treatment reduced the number of γH2A.X+ cells, particularly in the basal layer and hair follicles, showing enhanced DNA damage mitigation (Fig. 9k). Additionally, SC/F-HMNs demonstrated a stronger capacity to suppress γH2A.X+ expression. Previous studies have linked γH2A.X-marked DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) to the activation of the p53-p21 axis [91]. P21, a cell cycle inhibitor, is a key marker of senescence and upregulated under UVA exposure [92,93]. To further assess cellular senescence, immunofluorescence staining for p21 and vimentin was conducted. Fig. 9j–l shows increased p21 expression in the UVA group, which was suppressed by SC/F HMN treatment. These results collectively demonstrated that SC/F-HMNs treatment rejuvenates skin by reducing UVA-induced senescence in the dermal microenvironment.

The above synergistic mechanisms enabled SC/F-HMNs to exhibit superior oxidative damage and attenuation of cellular senescence. Notably, SC/F-HMNs were also able to reduce senescence in the epidermis, which is critical for maintaining dermal ECM microenvironmental homeostasis.

Repair of epidermal barrier with restored epidermal homeostasis by SC/F-HMNs in vivo

The outermost layer of the epidermis, the stratum corneum, directly reflects or scatters part of the UV rays through a dense arrangement of keratinocytes and lipids, reducing their penetration into the dermis. At the same time, the epidermis reduces UV-induced inflammatory factors through tight junctions and immune modulation [94,95]. Thus, the epidermis plays a crucial role in maintaining the homeostasis of the enhanced ECM microenvironment.

Oxidative stress caused by UV disrupts epidermal homeostasis, resulting in epidermal thickening, impaired barrier function, and hydration loss [21,96]. H&E staining revealed that UVA irradiation caused epidermal thickening, which was alleviated by SC/F-HMNs and SC HMNs treatments, indicating reduced hyperplasia (Fig. 10a-b). Aquaporin 3(AQP3), as an essential protein regulating water content in the epidermis [97], was significantly reduced after UVA exposure but restored after SC/F-HMNs treatment (Fig. 10c-d). The epidermal water content influences the texture of the skin and is essential to the skin’s barrier function [98]. Therefore, we examined transdermal water loss (TEWL) and hydration value. UV irradiation disrupts the epidermal homeostasis which is ensured by EpiSCs and a balanced proliferating and differentiating keratinocytes [20,99]. In Fig. 10g–i, the UVA group was found with a reduction of epidermal stem cells (EpiSCs) marked by cytokeratin 19 (CK19) and undifferentiated keratinocytes marked by cytokeratin 5 (CK5) in the basal layer, with increasing differentiated keratinocytes marked by cytokeratin 10 (CK10) in the spinous layer. Additionally, SC and SC/F HMNs treatments rebalanced CK5 and CK10 expression, reducing UV-induced vertical differentiation of EpiSCs and restoring epidermal homeostasis [100,101]. Compared to SC-HMN, the SC/F-HMN treatment exhibited a more significant effect (Fig. 10j–l), which may be attributed to its capacity to reduce DSB Therefore, the SC/F HMN significantly reduced UV-induced vertical differentiation of EpiSCs, pathological epidermal thickening, AQP3 loss, and epidermal water loss to maintain epidermal homeostasis. These were closely related to Col17, which was a key structural component of hemidesmosomes within the derma-epidermal basement membrane [91]. rhCol17 in SC-HMNs preserved key functional protein sequences of Col17, which may help maintain epidermal homeostasis. More importantly, the restoration of the epidermal barrier and the persistence of homeostasis facilitate the maintenance of the dermal microenvironment after reshaping.

In the present study, we designed a novel biomaterial consisting of fisetin-loaded nanocomposite combined with SFMA/rhCol17MA hydrogel microneedles. This material aims to remodel the dermal microenvironment of photodamaged skin through a synergistic strategy of SenCs clearance and epidermal repair. Compared to current delivery approaches using nanotechnology-assisted diets, local injections, surface application for fisetin [31,32,34,36], we provide an advanced delivery platform that allows for deep skin penetration, sustained drug release, and improved drug uptake by target cells. This platform can facilitate intermittent administration of senolytics, reduce technical dependence and invasive pain, enhance the drug concentration in skin tissues, and minimize systemic side effects. Meanwhile, we fabricated SFMA/rhCol17MA hydrogel to optimize the synergistic effect of rhCol17 on maintaining epidermal homeostasis and barrier function [42]. Comparing with existing approaches, this material exhibited physicochemical tailorability, slower biodegradation with sustained drug release [102]. Our research confirms that the strategy of locally eliminating cellular senescence to remodel the dermal ECM and alleviate the inflammatory microenvironment provides a therapeutic reference for skin anti-aging and other skin diseases related to oxidative stress damage.

Although our study demonstrated SC/F-HMNs promising potential in the UVA-induced photoaging model, there is still a long way to go before its clinical application. Since pathological changes of aging occur throughout the entire lifespan, the 8-week model has certain limitations in reflecting the aging process in human. Although there is no standard aging model in consensus, a prolonged modeling duration would be beneficial for observing the accumulating effects of damage. However, due to the complexity of the mechanisms involved in human aging, longer-term studies may amplify the cumulative impact of other factors over time [103]. The proposed framework in the present study offers a reference to discovery therapeutic approaches to dermal microenvironment dysregulation under UVA-induced oxidative stress. Its long-term validation still requires an optimized aging model with an expanding sample size. Recent studies have reported that the SenCs in skin can exert an effect on other organs, including the brain [3,5]. In observing the systemic therapeutic value of SC/F-HMNs for healthy aging [104], we plan to further investigate the effects of its topical application on the systemic aging burden in other tissues. For precise treatment, smarter biomaterials are under development to achieve theranostic functions by monitoring senescent cells and dynamically responding to the compromised skin microenvironment.

In terms of anti-aging biomaterial development, SC/F-HMN exhibited therapeutic effects in skin aging applications. Single-cell sequencing analysis data from eyelid skin of women of different ages confirmed that fibroblasts exhibit the highest levels of aging-related transcriptional variability, with the most upregulated signaling pathways involving ECM degradation genes such as MMPs [105]. These findings support the anti-aging therapeutic potential of SC/F-HMNs in human skin. Since SC/F-HMNs offer advantages including ease of operation, minimally invasive procedures, painless application, and rapid recovery, they hold certain potential for the topical application around the eyes. According to research, a worldwide annual expenditure for developing skin anti-aging treatment was expected to grow to around $US44.5 billion by 2030 [106]. Against this backdrop, SC/F-HMNs exhibit remarkable translational medicine potential. Beyond aging, previous studies have found that fisetin not only has potential in the clearance of senescent cells but also exhibits therapeutic effects in alleviating inflammation, oxidant, and malignant diseases [[107], [108], [109]]. For chronic inflammatory skin diseases, fisetin and fisetin-based nanoformulations have been reported to alleviate psoriasis and dermatitis in preclinical models [[110], [111], [112]]. Additionally, existing evidences support the effect of fisetin in treating dermal microenvironment disorders such as hypertrophic scars, skin cancer [113,114]. These findings value the further exploration of the therapeutic potential of SC/F-HMN in more extensive preclinical applications for skin disorders and healthy aging.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed SFMA/rhCol17MA hydrogel-based microneedles encapsulating fisetin-loaded HA-modified liposomes (SC/F-HMNs) and evaluated their therapeutic efficacy in reshaping the dermal microenvironment under UVA-induced oxidative stress in photoaging models. Experiments have not only confirmed ECM remodeling, showing that dermal microenvironmental dysregulation can be corrected, but have also demonstrated epidermal barrier homeostasis, which in turn can further maintain the dermal microenvironment. Mechanistically, SC/F-HMNs regulated cutaneous microenvironment homeostasis via two pathways. (1) Targeted clearance of SenCs suppressed aberrant release of ECM proteases mediated by SASP, thereby remodeling the dermal microenvironment. (2) Enhanced epidermal barrier integrity and restored homeostasis reduced secondary damage to the dermal microenvironment from external stimuli while maintaining ECM homeostasis. This “source intervention-barrier maintenance” dual-modality mechanism provides an innovative therapeutic strategy for addressing dermal microenvironment dysregulation under oxidative stress induced in skin aging and has strong translational value.
