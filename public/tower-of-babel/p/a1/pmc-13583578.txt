Impact of Prior Thoracic Endovascular Aortic Repair on Outcomes After Open Thoracoabdominal Aortic Replacement for Type B Aortic Dissection
Authors: Hai Yu, Bo Jia, Haoning Liu, Zhanqi Guo, Yipeng Ge, Yi Yang, Fucheng Xiao, Ruidong Qi, Chengnan Li, Junming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is a primary treatment for complicated type B aortic dissection (TBAD). However, some patients eventually require open thoracoabdominal aortic replacement (TAAR). Comparative outcomes data for TAAR with vs without prior TEVAR remain limited.MethodsIn this single-centre retrospective study of 198 patients undergoing TAAR for TBAD, we compared outcomes between those with (n = 60) vs without (n = 138) prior TEVAR. The primary endpoint was a composite of in-hospital death, spinal cord injury, or stroke.ResultsThe composite endpoint was significantly higher in the prior-TEVAR group (25.0% vs 7.3%, P < 0.001). In-hospital mortality was similar (11.7% vs 5.1%, P = 0.173), whereas spinal cord injury (10.0% vs 2.2%, P = 0.040) and cerebral hemorrhage (6.7% vs 0%, P = 0.012) were higher with prior TEVAR. Prior TEVAR independently predicted this composite outcome (odds ratio 3.72, P = 0.006). Patients with prior TEVAR had higher rates of reintubation (8.3% vs 1.5%, P = 0.046), and dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury also trended higher in this group (23.3% vs 12.3%, P = 0.050).Prior TEVAR was an independent predictor for late reintervention (hazard ratio 2.74, P = 0.030).ConclusionsPrior TEVAR was associated with increased perioperative morbidity and late reintervention risk in patients undergoing TAAR for TBAD. This association underscores the need for enhanced perioperative management and long-term follow-up in this high-risk cohort.Keywords: type B aortic dissection, thoracic endovascular aortic repair, thoracoabdominal aortic replacement, spinal cord injury, treatment outcome

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has become a cornerstone therapy for complicated type B aortic dissection (TBAD), endorsed by contemporary American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association, Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American Association for Thoracic Surgery, and European Society of Cardiology guidance for appropriately selected patients, together with structured imaging surveillance and adjuncts to mitigate neurologic risk.1, 2, 3 Despite early benefits, a subset of patients experience chronic-phase disease progression—persistent false-lumen perfusion, distal aneurysmal degeneration, type I/III endoleak, stent-induced new entry (SINE), infection, or device failure, and ultimately require open thoracoabdominal aortic replacement (TAAR).4, 5, 6

TAAR after prior TEVAR poses distinct operative challenges—adherent endograft and aortic wall changes complicate proximal/distal anastomoses and may necessitate stent-graft explantation; and segmental arterial coverage can constrain the collateral spinal network, heightening risks of spinal cord ischemia, bleeding, and renal injury.5,7, 8, 9 These risks underscore the need for standardized spinal-cord protection bundles—cerebrospinal-fluid drainage, maintenance of spinal perfusion pressure, selective/segmental perfusion, staged strategies—as summarized in recent reviews and technical reports.10, 11, 12, 13

However, robust evidence is limited comparing TAAR outcomes in TBAD for patients with vs without a history of TEVAR. To address this evidence gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study stratified by prior TEVAR to compare early and late outcomes after TAAR.

Methods

The research reported in this paper adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2025229x).

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single centre. From February 2009 to December 2024, a total of 656 patients underwent thoracoabdominal aortic replacement surgery at our centre. The patient enrollment and grouping process is shown in Figure 1. Among these patients, 273 were diagnosed with thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) secondary to TBAD. All patients underwent preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the aorta to confirm the diagnosis and evaluate disease severity. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) having TAAA secondary to TBAD; and (ii) undergoing open TAAR surgery at our centre. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) history of proximal aortic surgery; (ii) preexisting cerebral infarction or spinal cord injury sequelae; (iii) pregnancy; and (iv) concomitant malignancy. After applying these criteria, 198 patients were finally included in the present study. Patients were stratified based on their history of TEVAR into the prior-TEVAR group (n = 60) and the no-prior-TEVAR group (n = 138).

Surgical techniques

The technical details of thoracoabdominal aortic replacement in our centre have been described in detail in previous reports.4,14 Briefly, open TAAR was performed through a standard left thoracoabdominal incision under single-lung ventilation. The extent of open aortic replacement was determined according to the diseased aortic segment identified on preoperative CTA and intraoperative findings, and it was classified using the Crawford classification (types I-V). In general, the replacement extended from the distal descending thoracic aorta to the infrarenal abdominal aorta as required by the extent of aneurysmal degeneration or chronic dissection involvement. Distal perfusion was established using femoro-femoral bypass, aorto-iliac bypass, or left heart bypass, and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest was selectively applied when proximal clamping was not feasible. In less extensive disease, sequential aortic clamping under normothermia was preferred. The diseased aorta was replaced with a 4-branched Dacron graft, and the visceral and renal arteries were reconstructed sequentially, typically in the order of superior mesenteric artery, right renal artery, left renal artery, and celiac trunk. Intercostal or lumbar arteries between T8 and L1 were reattached whenever feasible to preserve spinal cord perfusion, and cerebrospinal fluid drainage was used routinely as an adjunctive protective measure.

In patients with prior TEVAR, the operative strategy was further individualized according to the location of the endograft and the extent of residual distal aortic disease. When the endograft involved the proximal or mid-descending thoracic aorta, the open repair was tailored to incorporate or remove the stented segment as required. If the endograft was removed, proximal and distal anastomoses were constructed after excision of the stented aortic segment. If the endograft was partially preserved, the native aortic wall, endograft, prosthetic graft, and felt strips were sutured together to reinforce the anastomosis and achieve hemostasis.

Thus, the extent of replacement and adjunctive perfusion strategy were individualized according to Crawford extent, prior endovascular treatment, and intraoperative anatomy.

Definitions and follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was a composite adverse event, defined as the occurrence of in-hospital mortality, spinal cord injury (SCI), or stroke. In-hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause during the index hospitalization. SCI referred to newly developed paraplegia or paraparesis attributable to spinal cord ischemia, and cases secondary to acute cerebrovascular events were excluded from this definition. Stroke was defined as a new focal neurologic deficit persisting for more than 24 hours, confirmed by neuroimaging.

Follow-up information was collected through a combination of outpatient visits, structured telephone interviews, WeChat communication, and verification via national medical insurance records. Contrast-enhanced CTA of the aorta was recommended 3-6 months after surgery and annually thereafter to assess graft integrity and aortic remodelling. Survival status, reintervention, and major adverse events were ascertained during each follow-up to ensure accuracy and completeness of outcome data.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) depending on distribution, and they were compared using the Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed with the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Missing continuous data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations before regression analyses. For analysis of the primary endpoint, univariable logistic regression was initially performed for all candidate variables. Candidate variables included all baseline characteristics shown in Table 1 and all intraoperative variables shown in Table 2, except for red blood cell transfusion, plasma transfusion, and platelet transfusion. Variables with a univariable P value < 0.10 were entered into a backward stepwise multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of the primary endpoint. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Long-term mortality and reintervention were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compared by the log-rank test. For time-to-event analyses, univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was first performed for the same set of candidate variables, and variables with a univariable P value < 0.10 were subsequently entered into backward stepwise multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.3.3 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), and a 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population characteristics

A summary of the baseline data is shown in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 40.0 years (IQR, 32.0-48.0), and patients with prior TEVAR were significantly older than those without (44.5 vs 36.5 years, P < 0.001). Male patients were more frequent in the TEVAR group (76.7% vs 48.6%, P < 0.001). The prevalence of hypertension was higher among patients with prior TEVAR (65.0% vs 46.4%, P = 0.016). Baseline renal function was slightly impaired in the TEVAR group, with higher median creatinine levels (70.6 vs 64.7 μmol/L, P = 0.044). Symptomatic presentation was less common in the TEVAR group (51.7% vs 73.9%, P = 0.002). Regarding aneurysm extent, patients without prior TEVAR were more likely to present with Crawford type II aneurysms (60.9% vs 11.7%), whereas those with prior TEVAR predominantly had type III involvement (70.0% vs 13.0%, P < 0.001). Other baseline comorbidities, including diabetes, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and connective tissue disorders, were comparable between groups.

Operative data

The median cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time was similar between groups (135.5 vs 136.5 minutes, P = 0.815). The volume of red blood cell transfusion was lower in the TEVAR group (median 4.0 units [IQR 0.0-6.5] vs 4.0 units [2.0-8.0], P = 0.023), and platelet transfusion requirements were also reduced (0.0 vs 1.0 units, P = 0.013). Intercostal artery clamping time was shorter in the TEVAR group (17.5 vs 25.0 minutes, P = 0.001). Use of renal perfusion (66.7% vs 37.7%, P < 0.001) and visceral perfusion (66.7% vs 37.0%, P < 0.001) was more frequent in patients with prior TEVAR. Reconstruction of intercostal arteries was less common in this group (60.0% vs 88.4%, P < 0.001), whereas cerebrospinal fluid drainage was more frequently applied (91.7% vs 77.5%, P = 0.018). Other operative parameters, such as nadir temperature and CPB modality, were comparable between groups (Table 2).

Postoperative outcomes

The postoperative outcomes in the different groups are summarized in Table 3. The composite endpoint (in-hospital mortality, SCI, or stroke) occurred in 12.6% of patients, with a significantly higher incidence in the prior-TEVAR group, compared with the no-prior-TEVAR group (25.0% vs 7.3%, P < 0.001). In-hospital mortality was 7.1% overall, with no significant difference between groups (11.7% vs 5.1%, P = 0.173). Stroke occurred in 3.5% of patients, and SCI in 4.6%, both of which were more frequent after prior TEVAR (stroke: 6.7% vs 2.2%, P = 0.248; SCI: 10.0% vs 2.2%, P = 0.040). Cerebral hemorrhage was significantly more common in the TEVAR group (6.7% vs 0.0%, P = 0.012). Acute kidney injury requiring dialysis developed in 15.7% of the cohort, with a trend toward higher incidence in the TEVAR group (23.3% vs 12.3%, P = 0.050). Reintubation was more frequent after prior TEVAR (8.3% vs 1.5%, P = 0.046). Median intensive care unit stay was slightly longer in this group (1.72 vs 1.59 days, P = 0.050).

Among the 60 patients with prior TEVAR, preoperative CTA performed at our centre was available for assessment of left subclavian artery coverage in 49 patients (81.7%). Complete left subclavian artery (LSA) coverage consistent with zone 2 deployment was identified in 20 of these 49 patients (40.8%). Among these 20 patients, 9 underwent LSA revascularization and none developed SCI, whereas 1 of 11 patients without LSA revascularization developed SCI. Among the 29 patients without complete LSA coverage on preoperative CTA, 3 developed SCI. Of the remaining 11 patients without available preoperative CTA for LSA assessment, 2 developed SCI. These findings are summarized in Supplemental Table S1.

Multivariable logistic regression identified several independent predictors of the primary endpoint (Table 4; Supplemental Table S2; Fig. 2). A history of prior TEVAR was independently associated with an increased risk of composite adverse events (OR 3.72, 95% CI 1.46-9.48, P = 0.006). Older age also conferred higher risk (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02-1.12, P = 0.006). In addition, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction was an adverse prognostic factor (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01-1.21, P = 0.041). Hyperlipidemia showed a trend toward significance but did not reach a threshold of statistical significance (OR 4.29, 95% CI 0.77-23.90, P = 0.097).

Long-term outcomes

During a median follow-up of 5.0 years (IQR, 2.1-9.2), long-term survival and freedom from reintervention were evaluated in the entire cohort. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in overall survival between the prior-TEVAR and no-prior-TEVAR groups (log-rank P = 0.424; HR 1.32, 95% CI 0.66-2.62; Fig. 5). However, patients with prior TEVAR exhibited a higher cumulative hazard of reoperation over time (log-rank P = 0.024; HR 2.74, 95% CI 1.11-6.77; Fig. 6).

On multivariable Cox regression analysis for long-term mortality (Table 5; Fig. 3), older age (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01-1.08, P = 0.006), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; HR 8.88, 95% CI 1.87-42.19, P = 0.006) and cerebrovascular disease (HR 3.85, 95% CI 1.08-13.71, P = 0.038) were identified as independent predictors of late death. By contrast, higher body mass index (BMI) was protective (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79-0.96, P = 0.004). With respect to reintervention (Table 6; Fig. 4), prior TEVAR itself emerged as an independent predictor (HR 2.74, 95% CI 1.11-6.77, P = 0.030).

Discussion

In this single-centre retrospective cohort of 198 patients with TAAA secondary to TBAD, we examined perioperative and long-term outcomes of TAAR according to prior exposure to TEVAR. The main findings can be summarized as follows: (i) patients with prior TEVAR had significantly higher rates of perioperative composite adverse events; (ii) although overall in-hospital mortality was not statistically different between groups, prior TEVAR emerged as an independent predictor of early adverse outcomes; (iii) long-term overall survival was comparable between groups, whereas prior TEVAR was associated with a higher risk of late reintervention; and (iv) advanced age, cerebrovascular disease, and COPD were associated with higher long-term mortality, whereas higher BMI appeared to confer protection.

The formation of TAAA following TEVAR is a multifactorial process driven by the interplay of surgical technical factors and patient-specific pathophysiological mechanisms.15,16 Key technical contributors include incomplete thrombosis of the false lumen, often due to residual distal re-entry tears, which maintains pressure on the aortic wall and leads to progressive dilation.17,18 The presence of endoleaks, particularly high-pressure type I and III leaks, directly perfuses the aneurysm sac, preventing its regression.19 In addition, improper stent-graft sizing, especially excessive oversizing, can induce high wall stress and aortic injury.20 On the patient side, a persistent systemic inflammatory response, characterized by the activation of neutrophils and macrophages and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps, drives aortic wall degradation.21 This issue is compounded by the dysregulation of the transforming growth factor-β signalling pathway and the enzymatic breakdown of the extracellular matrix by matrix metalloproteinases, which collectively weaken the aortic wall and promote aneurysmal remodelling.22 Additionally, owing to inherent tissue fragility, connective tissue disorders treated with TEVAR are associated with an elevated risk of secondary reintervention.23,24 A study from Mexico reported a favourable aortic remodelling rate of only 37.5% following TEVAR in patients with Marfan syndrome, highlighting the limited durability of endovascular approaches in this subgroup.25

Several series have shown that TAAR after failed or complicated TEVAR is technically more demanding.4,26,27 Stent-graft adherence, altered tissue planes, and possible graft explantation may complicate anastomoses and increase the risks of bleeding, prolonged ischemia, and neurologic injury.28,29 Prior TEVAR, however, may also represent a marker of greater baseline aortic complexity rather than being a device-related risk factor alone, because TEVAR is typically reserved for complicated TBAD, whereas uncomplicated TBAD is generally managed medically. In our cohort, this interpretation was supported by the more frequent use of renal and visceral perfusion in the prior-TEVAR group (66.7% vs 37.7%, P < 0.001), suggesting greater operative extent and technical complexity at subsequent open repair. At the same time, although the extent differed between groups, the overall CPB strategies were not significantly different, likely because adjunct selection in open thoracoabdominal repair depends on not only extent, but also clamp position, branch-vessel anatomy, dissection morphology, prior aortic operations, and surgeon preference.30,31 Accordingly, the worse perioperative outcomes in the prior-TEVAR group likely reflect the combined effects of prior endovascular intervention and greater aortic disease complexity rather than the stent graft itself alone. Consistent with this possibility, prior TEVAR was associated with higher composite adverse events (25.0% vs 7.3%, P < 0.001) and remained an independent predictor of the primary endpoint (OR, 3.72; 95% CI 1.46-9.48; P = 0.006). By contrast, Frankort et al. reported more-favourable outcomes in a staged hybrid setting, suggesting that prior TEVAR may facilitate later open repair in selected patients, whereas in broader TBAD-related TAAA cohorts, it may identify a higher-risk subgroup with more-advanced disease.32

Prior studies by Coselli et al. and Etz et al. emphasized that spinal cord perfusion can be compromised by coverage of segmental arteries during TEVAR, and these arteries, once covered, are difficult to reconstruct during subsequent surgery. This situation leads to a diminished collateral spinal network, thereby increasing susceptibility to SCI during open repair.33, 34, 35 However, staged sacrifice of segmental arteries may also induce collateral remodelling and spinal cord preconditioning, as proposed in the collateral network and in the minimally invasive segmental artery coil embolization (MISACE) and minimally invasive staged segmental artery coil embolization (MIS²ACE) concepts.36,37 In our cohort, prior TEVAR was associated with a significantly higher risk of SCI (10.0% vs 2.2%, P = 0.040). In the prior-TEVAR group, additional review of available preoperative CTA showed that complete LSA coverage consistent with zone 2 deployment was present in 20 of 49 evaluable patients. Although the number of SCI events was small, 1 of 11 patients with LSA coverage without revascularization developed SCI, whereas no SCI occurred among the 9 patients who underwent LSA revascularization. These findings suggest that the effect of prior TEVAR on spinal cord risk may be heterogeneous and may depend, at least in part, on the proximal landing zone, LSA patency, and LSA revascularization status. Therefore, prior TEVAR should not be interpreted as a uniform exposure with respect to spinal cord perfusion risk.

In real-world patients, any potential protective effect of preconditioning may be outweighed by the adverse impact of more extensive prior aortic coverage, reduced collateral reserve, and the greater complexity of subsequent open repair. This risk may be particularly relevant in patients with prior zone 2 TEVAR involving LSA coverage without revascularization, in whom both subclavian and segmental collateral pathways may be compromised. Accordingly, patients with prior TEVAR undergoing TAAR should be considered a high-risk subgroup requiring comprehensive spinal cord protection, including cerebrospinal fluid drainage, maintenance of adequate mean arterial pressure, and hemoglobin optimization to preserve oxygen delivery. Emerging adjuncts such as motor evoked potential monitoring may further enhance intraoperative spinal cord protection and should be considered in future protocols for this challenging population.38,39

Long-term, the survival rate in patients with prior TEVAR was comparable to that in patients without prior TEVAR (log rank P = 0.424), although the reintervention risk was significantly higher in the prior-TEVAR group (HR 2.74, 95% CI 1.11-6.77, P = 0.030). This finding suggests that although early false lumen stabilization via TEVAR may provide initial benefits, it may not prevent the need for subsequent interventions, such as distal TEVAR extensions or open revisions.16,27 Factors such as a higher BMI (HR 0.87, P = 0.004) may offer a protective effect in the setting of postoperative recovery, whereas COPD (HR 8.88, P = 0.006) and cerebrovascular disease (HR 3.85, P = 0.038) are associated with an increased risk of late mortality. These observations support the need for refined risk stratification that goes beyond aneurysm extent, including factors such as preexisting comorbidities. The findings also highlight the importance of vigilant post-TEVAR surveillance, including routine contrast-enhanced CTA at 3-6 months postprocedure followed by annual imaging, to monitor aneurysmal progression and enable timely intervention.1,40 In addition, advances in imaging biomarkers and computational modelling may ultimately enable better prediction of TEVAR failure and facilitate earlier, tailored interventions to improve long-term outcomes.41

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was retrospective and was from a single high-volume aortic centre; it is therefore subject to selection bias, referral bias, and residual confounding. Second, the study period spanned 15 years, during which surgical strategies, organ-protection protocols, and perioperative management may have evolved. Third, because of the relatively limited sample size and number of events, the ability to perform more extensive adjustment or matching analyses was constrained. Fourth, detailed data regarding the index TEVAR procedure were incomplete in some patients, particularly those treated elsewhere or earlier in the study period. Specifically, complete LSA coverage status and proximal landing zone information were available for only 81.7% of patients in the prior-TEVAR group, and we were unable to perform a formal comparison of outcomes between zone 2 and zone 3 deployments. Finally, as our findings reflect the experience of a specialized centre, their generalizability to other practice settings may be limited.

Conclusion

Prior TEVAR was associated with significantly increased perioperative morbidity and a higher risk of late reintervention in patients undergoing TAAR for TBAD, although overall survival remains comparable (Central Illustration). These results highlight the need for refined surgical strategies, comprehensive spinal cord protection, and vigilant long-term surveillance in this high-risk population.

Prior thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) significantly elevates risks of composite adverse events and spinal cord injury in patients undergoing thoracoabdominal aortic replacement (TAAR).
