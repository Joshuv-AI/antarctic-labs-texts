Reporting of ethical aspects in validation studies conducted in Peru
Authors: Jeancarlo Tejada-Flores, Gianfranco Centeno-Terrazas, María F. Fernández, Christeam A. Benites-Ibarra, Alvaro Taype-Rondan, Rubí Paredes-Angeles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe validation of measurement instruments requires not only psychometric rigor but also transparent reporting of ethical principles to ensure the protection of human participants and research integrity. However, the extent to which ethical aspects are adequately reported in validation studies conducted in Peru remains unknown.MethodsA descriptive study was conducted including validation studies published between 2021 and 2024 by authors affiliated with Peruvian institutions and indexed in Scopus. Reporting of ethics committee approval, informed consent, assent, data confidentiality, funding, conflicts of interest, and adherence to ethical guidelines was assessed. Factors associated with reporting ethics committee approval were explored using Poisson regression models with robust variance to estimate prevalence ratios.ResultsA total of 208 studies were included. The majority of the studies were published in English (52.9%), and the most common study populations were students (36.5%). Ethics committee approval was reported in 77.4% of the articles, and informed consent in 91.8%. Only 63.4% specified an ethics approval code, and 61.0% failed to report assent when it was applicable. Compared with articles published in Q1 journals, those published in lower-quartile and non-quartile journals showed a lower prevalence of reporting ethics committee approval, and articles published in Spanish showed a lower prevalence than those published in English, whereas studies with five or more authors and multicity designs showed a higher prevalence of reporting ethics committee approval.ConclusionSubstantial gaps persist in the transparency of ethical reporting in validation studies conducted in Peru. Strengthening editorial requirements and promoting greater awareness among researchers are essential to enhance ethical compliance and protect research participants.

Background

The validation of measurement instruments—such as questionnaires, scales, and standardized tools—is essential to ensure the validity and comparability of research findings across different contexts [1]. This process typically involves the cultural and linguistic adaptation of the instruments, as well as the evaluation of their psychometric properties within specific target populations [2–4].

Beyond the technical aspects, these studies involve the participation of human subjects, which necessitates adherence to, and transparent reporting of, fundamental ethical principles [5]. According to the Declaration of Helsinki and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) guidelines, all research involving humans, regardless of the perceived level of risk, must undergo independent ethical review and obtain informed consent from participants [6, 7].

However, several studies have shown that ethical requirements are not always adequately reported. For instance, up to 27% of social science articles and 11% of health science articles failed to mention ethical approval despite involving identifiable human data [8]. Similarly, reviews of COVID-19 research have revealed deficiencies in reporting informed consent, ethical approval, and data protection measures, suggesting a broader pattern of underreporting across disciplines and contexts [9]. These reporting omissions undermine the transparency and credibility of research practices and may also compromise the adequate protection of study participants [10].

In Peru, national regulations require ethical review for all studies involving human participants, including observational and survey-based research [11]. Nevertheless, there has been no systematic assessment of whether validation studies conducted by researchers affiliated with Peruvian institutions adequately report ethical aspects such as committee approval, informed consent, and participant protection. This gap is particularly relevant given the rapid growth of Peru’s scientific output—rising by an estimated 28% annually since 2022 [12]—and the increasing frequency of validation studies [13, 14].

Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the reporting of ethical aspects in validation studies published by authors affiliated with Peruvian institutions between 2021 and 2024. Understanding current practices in ethical reporting is crucial to identify areas for improvement, inform editorial policies, and strengthen research integrity within the Peruvian scientific community.

Methods

Design and study setting

A descriptive study was conducted to evaluate the reporting of ethical aspects in validation studies by authors affiliated with Peruvian institutions. Peru is a Latin American country that is administratively divided into 24 departments and one constitutional province (Callao), which constitutes the first-level subnational administrative divisions. Each department comprises multiple cities.

Search strategy

The search was carried out in the Scopus database on September 12, 2025, using the following strategy: AFFILCOUNTRY ( peru ) AND TITLE ( validation OR psychometric OR adaptation ) AND PUBYEAR > 2020 AND PUBYEAR < 2025 AND ( ( SUBJAREA ( MEDI OR NURS OR DENT OR HEAL OR MULT ) ) OR SUBJAREA ( PSYC ) ).

Study eligibility

We included all original validation studies published between 2021 and 2024 that met the following criteria: 1) original research article following the IMRD (introduction, methods, results, discussion) structure, 2) published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal, 3) articles published in journals indexed in Scopus within the subject areas of medicine, nursing, dentistry, health professions, or multidisciplinary, 4) reporting the validation, adaptation, or psychometric evaluation of a scale, instrument, questionnaire, or test, 5) including at least one author with institutional affiliation in Peru (regardless of authorship position), 6) conducted wholly or partially in Peru.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan software [15] to facilitate screening. After duplicate removal, study selection was conducted in two phases: a) Title and abstract screening, b) Full-text review. Four authors, working in pairs (MFF/JFT and GCT/CBI), independently screened the studies according to eligibility criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a fifth author (RPA or ATR).

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Excel. A pilot extraction was performed to ensure consistency in variable interpretation. Afterwards, four authors in pairs (MFF/JFT and GCT/CBI) independently extracted data from each article. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved in consultation with the fifth author (RPA OR ATR).

Variables

For each included study, the following information was extracted: publication in a Peruvian journal (yes or no) and the journal quartile according to the SCImago Journal Rank (quartile 1 = Q1, quartile 2 = Q2, quartile 3 = Q3, quartile 4 = Q4, and NC = journal without quartile), considering the highest quartile corresponding to the year of publication.

Additional variables included the number of authors, the primary institutional affiliation of the first author, and international collaboration, defined as the presence of at least one author affiliated with an institution outside Peru (yes or no). Study characteristics were also extracted, including the main topic of the study (health, psychology, education, social sciences, or other), study population (patients, students, general population, health professionals, or other), age group of the population (adults aged 18–64 years; adults, unspecified ≥ 18 years; children and adolescents aged 0–17 years; and older adults aged ≥ 65 years), sample size (excluding expert panels), inclusion of participants exclusively from Peru (yes or no), and the number of cities in which the study was conducted (single city or multiple cities).

Ethical characteristics were extracted from each study. The reporting of approval by an ethics committee was assessed (yes or no), including whether approval was granted by a single ethics committee (yes or no). In addition, the reporting of at least one ethics approval code was recorded (yes or no), as well as whether the ethics committee was located in the same country (yes or no) and in the same department (yes or no), where the study was conducted.

The reporting of informed consent from participants or their legal guardians was evaluated (yes or no). When informed consent was reported, the mode of consent was classified as written (in-person or online), verbal, or other methods. For studies involving minors or participants requiring legal guardians, the reporting of participant assent was assessed and classified as obtained, not obtained, or not applicable.

Finally, the reporting of data confidentiality (yes or no), anonymity (yes or no), funding information (self-funded by the authors, with no external funding, or externally funded), and conflict of interest statements was evaluated. Adherence to ethical guidelines was also assessed and classified as Declaration of Helsinki, American Psychological Association (APA) guidelines, CIOMS guidelines, Committee of Publication Ethics (COPE) guidelines, other international guidelines, national regulations only (e.g., Instituto Nacional de Salud), or a combination of international and national guidelines.

Data analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using Stata version 17. First, a descriptive analysis was conducted. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies, whereas numerical variables were described using measures of central tendency and dispersion, according to the distribution normality. Second, an exploratory bivariate analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with the reporting of approval by an ethics committee (yes or no). Given the binary outcome, Poisson regression models with robust variance were used to estimate crude prevalence ratios (PR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Results

Selection of studies

The database search identified 343 records. Of these, 74 were excluded based on title and abstract screening, and an additional 61 were excluded after full-text review, because they were not conducted totally or partially in Peru. Ultimately, 208 articles were included in the analysis.

Characteristics of the included studies

Most studies were published in non-Peruvian journals (87.0%), and approximately half were published in English (52.9%). Regarding journal quartile, 25.5% of the articles were published in Q1 journals, while 17.8%, 23.6%, and 24.5% were published in Q2, Q3, and Q4 journals, respectively; 8.7% were published without a quartile classification. The median number of authors per article was five (interquartile range: 3.5–6). The most frequent primary affiliations of first authors were Universidad Peruana Unión (16.8%), Universidad César Vallejo (9.1%), and Universidad Nacional Mayor de San Marcos (8.2%). International collaboration was reported in 28.9% of the studies.

Psychology was the predominant study topic (73.1%), followed by health sciences (13.0%). The most common study populations were students (36.5%) and the general population (27.9%). Adults aged 18–64 years constituted the most frequently studied age group (35.6%). Among the 204 studies reporting sample size, the median number of participants was 451 (interquartile range: 289–825.5). Most studies included only participants from Peru (94.7%) and were conducted in a single city (43.8%) (Table 1).

Ethical reporting practices

Overall, 161 articles (77.4%) reported approval by an ethics committee. Among these, 91.3% were approved by a single ethics committee, and 63.4% reported an approval code. Most ethics committees were located in the country where the study was conducted (95.7%), although fewer than half were located in the same department as the study (47.2%).

The reporting of informed consent procedures was identified in 91.8% of the studies, most commonly described as written consent (79.1%). When restricting the analysis to studies in which assent was applicable, 61.0% did not report this information. Likewise, 70.7% of articles reported statements regarding data confidentiality or anonymity.

Funding information was not reported in 40.4% of studies; among those that reported funding, 34.6% were self-funded, and 25.0% reported external funding. A conflict of interest statement was included in 73.1% of articles. Adherence to international ethical guidelines was reported in half of the studies, most commonly the Declaration of Helsinki (34.6%) (Table 2).

Articles published in Spanish had a lower prevalence of reporting ethics approval compared with those published in English (PR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.58–0.81). Studies with five to six authors (PR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.05–1.54) and those with seven or more authors (PR 1.46; 95% CI: 1.23–1.73) had a higher prevalence of reporting ethics approval compared with studies with one to four authors.

Discussion

General characteristics of the studies

Regarding the studied populations, a predominance of research conducted in students (36.5%), including both university and school students, was observed, followed by studies carried out in the general population (27.9%). The frequent use of student samples could be partly explained by their accessibility and logistical feasibility for recruiting large samples within short timeframes, particularly in resource-constrained settings [16]. However, this practice raises important concerns regarding external validity [17]. In clinical or community settings, where populations may differ in terms of health status, socioeconomic conditions, cultural background, and cognitive or emotional profiles, instruments validated predominantly in academic samples may exhibit suboptimal psychometric performance [18]. Because of this, psychometric characteristics found in student samples might not be directly applicable to the populations for which these tools are designed, which could result in measurement bias or incorrect interpretation when used in different settings [16]. Additionally, from an ethical perspective, implicit power dynamics within academic settings may render students a contextually vulnerable population, particularly when recruitment is conducted by faculty members or within academic environments, where perceived authority relationships may influence their autonomy and willingness to participate voluntarily in research [17, 19].

Geographically, validation studies were concentrated in Lima, accounting for 93 investigations (44.7%). This pattern reflects the higher density of research institutions in the capital and aligns with previous evidence indicating that Lima accounts for approximately 70% of the scientific output of Peruvian universities [20]. Such geographic concentration raises concerns for ecological validity in a culturally, linguistically, and socioeconomically heterogeneous country. Instruments validated exclusively in Lima may show limited applicability in rural, Andean, and Amazonian populations, where differences in language, cultural norms, and educational levels can affect item interpretation [21, 22].

Ethical reporting practices in the included studies

Regarding the reporting of ethical aspects, nearly one-fifth of the included studies did not report ethics committee approval, despite involving human participants. Among those that declared such approval, only 63.4% specified the corresponding registration code, which limits the transparency and traceability of the ethical review process. However, given that this study is based exclusively on published reports, the absence of reported information should not be interpreted as evidence that ethical approval was not obtained.

Although International reporting guidelines and editorial standards, including the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors Recommendations [23] and the American Psychological Association Reporting Standards [24], emphasize the importance of transparently reporting ethical approval and informed consent in studies involving human participants, as a key component of research transparency, several factors may influence how ethical information is reported. First, differences in the level of enforcement of reporting standards, editorial oversight, and peer review practices across journals may contribute to inconsistencies in the completeness and clarity of ethical reporting. Second, variability in journal editorial policies, including exemptions for minimal-risk studies or secondary analyzes of anonymized or publicly available data, may also affect reporting practices. Additionally, differences in scientific writing practices across contexts may influence how explicitly ethical procedures are described, even when appropriate safeguards were followed.

Regarding informed consent, although most studies reported obtaining it, nearly one-fifth did not specify the modality used. This indicates that some studies provided limited detail on how this procedure was conducted. In addition, among studies in which assent was applicable, only one third reported it explicitly, suggesting an underestimation of this fundamental ethical component.

Our results are consistent with a systematic review that identified underreporting of both ethical approval and informed consent in clinical trials, indicating that even in experimental research, limitations in ethical transparency persist [25]. Similarly, a review in the field of mental health research reported a high proportion of studies that did not report approval by an ethics committee or the obtainment of informed consent, despite involving particularly vulnerable populations [26].

Factors associated with reporting ethics committee approval

Regarding factors associated with the reporting of ethics committee approval, journal quartile showed a significant association, with the highest prevalence observed in Q1 journals (94.3%) and a progressive decrease toward non-indexed journals (55.6%). The higher reporting rate in Q1 journals likely reflects more rigorous editorial policies. This has been previously documented, showing an association between a higher impact factor and greater completeness of ethical reporting in published articles [25], as well as a higher likelihood of requiring ethics approval in author guidelines [27]. The absence of ethics committee reporting, predominantly in lower-quartile journals, does not necessarily indicate the lack of ethical approval but may instead reflect deficiencies in editorial policies requiring this information during the peer-review process.

Similarly, studies published in Spanish showed a lower prevalence of ethics committee reporting compared with those published in English. This finding likely reflects the increasing number of international journals aligned with global standards that mandate the reporting of ethical approval [28].

Research teams with seven or more authors showed a higher frequency of ethics committee reporting compared with teams of one to four authors. This association may be explained by the fact that larger teams tend to have greater experience in regulatory matters, access to established institutional protocols, and specialized administrative support that facilitates compliance with ethical requirements [8]. However, a higher number of authors does not necessarily guarantee good research practices, as responsibility dilution or nominal authorship may occur [29]. Likewise, multicenter studies showed a higher frequency of ethics committee approval compared with single-city studies, which may be attributable to the need for ethical approval from multiple committees when research is conducted across different jurisdictions, thereby increasing regulatory demands and related documentation [30, 31].

Contrary to expectations, international collaboration was not significantly associated with the reporting of ethics committee approval. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that international studies exhibit reporting deficiencies in ethical aspects similar to those observed in single-center studies [32]. This suggests a global lack of awareness regarding the importance of transparency in ethical reporting, regardless of the scale of collaboration.

Limitations and strengths

Some limitations of this study must be considered. First, we assessed the reporting of ethical aspects rather than the actual implementation of ethical practices, as the latter cannot be directly verified from published articles. Therefore, this distinction is crucial for appropriately interpreting our findings. Second, the search strategy was limited to Scopus, thereby excluding regional databases such as SciELO due to their restricted ability to filter records by authors’ affiliation. Additionally, the analysis focused on studies published between 2021 and 2024 to prioritize recent evidence and reduce bias related to the accelerated publication dynamics during the COVID-19 pandemic [33]. Third, variations in journal editorial policies, author guidelines, and linguistic conventions may affect the dissemination of ethical information, even when the foundational ethical conduct of the studies is robust. Fourth, the predominance of student samples may restrict the generalizability of the findings across various populations and settings, as instruments validated in academic contexts may not exhibit equivalent performance in clinical or community contexts, and students may also constitute a potentially vulnerable group due to implicit power dynamics. Finally, a proportion of studies involved international collaboration, which may introduce variability in ethical reporting due to differing academic norms; Thus, findings should be interpreted cautiously as they may not fully reflect local practices. In contrast, this study has several strengths. It represents the first study to systematically assess ethical issues in validation studies. Furthermore, the independent review process involving multiple reviewers at each stage enhanced methodological rigor and reduced the likelihood of subjective bias, thereby improving the reliability of the results.

Conclusion

Deficiencies in the transparency of ethical reporting were observed in validation studies conducted in Peru, including incomplete reporting of ethics committee approval, the omission of approval codes, and the lack of information on assent when applicable. Variations according to journal quartile, publication language, number of authors, and multicity design indicate that editorial and structural factors may influence the reporting of ethical standards. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening journal requirements and researcher training in ethical reporting in order to ensure transparency.
