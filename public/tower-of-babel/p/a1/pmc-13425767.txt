Changes in usual source of care utilization during the COVID-19 pandemic: a comparative analysis by chronic disease status
Authors: Seokmin Ji, Sewon Park, Munjae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic substantially disrupted healthcare systems worldwide, leading to marked declines in outpatient visits and essential services, particularly among patients with chronic conditions and older adults. South Korea experienced similar reductions in outpatient utilization during the early pandemic period, raising concerns about unmet healthcare needs and continuity of care. These disruptions highlighted the vulnerability of populations requiring regular medical management and underscored the importance of examining utilization patterns across different patient groups and usual sources of care.MethodsOutpatient visit data were drawn from the Korean Health Panel (KHPS) for the period from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2020. An interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) was performed, with stratification by presence of chronic conditions (non-chronic vs. chronic) and age (under 65 vs. 65 and older). Utilization was further analyzed by USC type: clinics, hospitals, and general hospitals.ResultsImmediately following the first COVID-19 intervention, outpatient visits to clinics declined among individuals under 65 in the non-chronic group and among both age subgroups in the chronic disease group, with utilization rebounding in all three subgroups after the second intervention. In contrast, visits to hospitals and general hospitals increased. When not stratified by USC type, overall outpatient utilization increased in the non-chronic group but decreased among older adults with chronic diseases.ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic affected outpatient healthcare utilization in varied ways depending on age, health status, and USC type. The notable decline in clinic visits among older adults with chronic conditions indicates reduced access to primary care during the pandemic. These findings highlight the need for healthcare policies that safeguard continuity of care for vulnerable populations and prioritize primary care during public health emergencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14778-7.

Background

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic disrupted healthcare systems worldwide, bringing dramatic changes to service delivery and patient utilization patterns. In the United States, emergency department visits fell by 42% in early 2020, and patients with severe conditions such as cardiovascular disease and cancer faced heightened risks of deterioration due to delayed or inadequate care [1]. In the United Kingdom, outpatient visits declined by up to 70%, affecting not only acute cases but also essential services such as cancer treatment [2]. In Iran, utilization of primary healthcare services dropped overall, with particularly sharp declines in chronic disease management [3]. South Korea experienced a similar trend: during the early phase of the pandemic in 2020, outpatient visits decreased by an average of 15.7%, hospital admissions by 11.6%, and public healthcare use by 23.4%. These reductions not only restricted access to healthcare but also undermined long-standing policy efforts to ease patient overcrowding in large hospitals [4].

Several factors contributed to reduced access, including fear of infection, social distancing policies, and shortages of medical personnel. The impact was especially pronounced for patients with chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes, who require consistent treatment, and for vulnerable populations such as older adults [5, 6]. As a result, many experienced unmet healthcare needs. For instance, Kim et al. (2021) found that unmet needs increased in Seoul during the pandemic, with the greatest difficulties reported among low-income, elderly, and less-educated groups [7]. Similarly, Lee and You (2021) noted that limitations in healthcare access were more severe for individuals with chronic illnesses than for those without underlying conditions [8].

For people with chronic conditions, continuous and comprehensive access to primary care is a cornerstone of medical management, making them among the most affected by disruptions in outpatient care [9, 10]. The spread of COVID-19 discouraged visits to primary care facilities for routine treatment of mild or chronic conditions, often leading to postponed or canceled appointments. These effects were further amplified during periods of heightened social distancing [11]. When regular care is interrupted, the risk of complications and mortality increases, particularly among older adults and those with weakened immune systems, who face a dual threat from both infection and unmanaged chronic disease [12, 13]. Over time, this places additional strain on the national healthcare system, highlighting the importance of targeted measures for vulnerable groups in infectious disease response policies.

Against this backdrop, the present study examines the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on outpatient healthcare utilization in South Korea, with a focus on identifying groups that experienced limited access to care during the crisis. While previous studies have documented overall declines in healthcare utilization during the pandemic, few have simultaneously examined how utilization patterns varied across different types of usual sources of care, stratified by both chronic disease status and age group [14]. In the Korean healthcare system, clinics are small-scale outpatient-oriented primary care facilities, hospitals are secondary care institutions with 30 or more beds, and general hospitals are tertiary-level institutions with 100 or more beds and multiple specialized departments [15]. The healthcare delivery system is structured around a tiered referral framework, and the role of each facility type differs substantially in managing chronic versus acute conditions [16]. Using an interrupted time series analysis of nationally representative panel data, this study aims to elucidate how pandemic-related disruptions differentially affected outpatient utilization across these facility types and patient subgroups, thereby providing evidence for group-specific healthcare policies to ensure continuity of care during future public health emergencies.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study utilized second-term data from the Korea Health Panel Survey (KHPS), an annual, longitudinal, nationally representative household survey jointly administered by the National Health Insurance Corporation and the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs. The KHPS collects detailed information on healthcare utilization by household members, including the type of service, visit dates, medical institution type, and diagnostic codes, through annual face-to-face interviews.

The study population consisted of individuals who reported a usual source of care and used outpatient medical services at least once during the period from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2020. To ensure comparability across the two-year study period, a balanced panel was constructed by including only respondents who participated in both the 2019 and 2020 survey waves. This resulted in 919 participants in the non-chronic group and 2,589 participants in the chronic disease group. Participants were further divided into two age categories: under 65 years and 65 years or older.

Outcome variable and unit of analysis

The outcome variable was the monthly number of outpatient visits aggregated across all respondents within each analysis subgroup. Individual-level visit records from the KHPS, which include the date and type of each outpatient encounter, were aggregated by calendar month to construct a time series of 24 monthly observations from January 2019 to December 2020. Monthly aggregation was chosen over daily aggregation to reduce noise from day-of-week and within-month variation and to provide a more stable time series suitable for interrupted time series analysis with a limited number of observations. It should be noted that, as the KHPS is an annual survey that collects visit information retrospectively, the monthly visit counts are derived from respondents’ recall of healthcare utilization over the preceding year. This retrospective data collection may introduce recall bias, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Definition of chronic conditions

Participants were classified into two groups based on the presence of chronic conditions: a chronic disease group and a non-chronic group. The chronic disease group included participants who received medical care for one or more of the following seven conditions, identified by diagnostic codes: hypertension (I10–I15), diabetes (E10–E14), hyperlipidemia (E78), arthropathy (M00–M25), tuberculosis (A15–A19), ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), and cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69) (Fig. 1). These conditions were selected because they represent the most prevalent chronic diseases requiring regular outpatient management in South Korea and are consistently tracked in the KHPS. It should be noted that some chronic conditions known to be associated with a higher risk of severe COVID-19, such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cancer, were not included in the chronic disease group, as they are not separately identifiable within the standard chronic disease classification used in the KHPS. The implications of this definition are discussed in the limitations section.

Study design and intervention points

This study employed a single-group interrupted time series (ITS) design to examine changes in outpatient healthcare utilization following three pandemic-related intervention points in 2020. The intervention points were determined based on the progression of COVID-19 epidemic waves and corresponding government policy responses in South Korea. According to Lee et al. (2021), the first wave occurred between February and April 2020, primarily linked to the Shincheonji religious group cluster in the Daegu region, while the second wave occurred from mid-August to late September, centered in the Seoul metropolitan area and largely associated with the August 15th mass rally and church-related clusters [17].

Based on these epidemic patterns and the timing of major policy measures, three intervention points were defined as follows: January 2020, April 2020, and August 2020. The first intervention point, January 2020, marks the onset of the pandemic in South Korea, when the first confirmed case was reported on January 20 and the government began implementing initial response measures including enhanced surveillance and quarantine protocols. The second intervention point, April 2020, corresponds to the transition period following the peak of the first wave, during which the government shifted from enhanced social distancing to eased social distancing as daily confirmed cases declined below 100. The third intervention point, August 2020, coincides with the onset of the second wave triggered by large-scale gatherings, after which social distancing was escalated to Level 2 in the Seoul metropolitan area. Statistical analysis

Interrupted time series analysis (ITSA), a quasi-experimental method that uses segmented regression to evaluate the temporal impact of interventions, was employed to assess changes in outpatient healthcare utilization associated with three COVID-19-related intervention points [19, 20]. ITSA is widely applied in healthcare research to examine utilization trends in response to time-dependent events or policy changes [21–23].

The segmented regression model for each analysis subgroup was specified as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ & \>{Y_t}\> = \>\beta {\>_0}\> + \>\beta {\>_1}t\> + \>\beta {\>_2}{D_1}\> + \>\beta {\>_3}{P_1}\> \cr & + \>\beta {\>_4}{D_2}\> + \>\beta {\>_5}{P_2}\> + \>\beta {\>_6}{D_3}\> + \>\beta {\>_7}{P_3}\> + \>{e_t} \cr}$$\end{document}

where Yt is the monthly number of outpatient visits at time t (t = 1, 2, …, 24); β0 is the intercept, representing the baseline level of utilization at t = 0; β1 is the pre-intervention slope, representing the underlying monthly trend; D1, D2, and D3 are dummy variables indicating the post-intervention periods for each of the three intervention points (January 2020, April 2020, and August 2020, respectively), with β2, β4, and β6 representing the immediate level changes at each point; P1, P2, and P3 are interaction terms between the post-intervention dummies and time, with β3, β5, and β7 representing the changes in slope following each intervention point; and et is the error term.

Separate models were estimated for each combination of disease group (chronic disease group, non-chronic group), type of usual source of care (total, clinic, hospital, general hospital), and age group (under 65, 65 and over), yielding a total of 16 models. This stratified approach was adopted rather than including group indicators or interaction terms in a single model, because the primary objective was to describe the distinct temporal patterns of each subgroup rather than to formally test between-group differences.

Autocorrelation in the time series residuals was tested using the Durbin-Watson statistic. Where evidence of autocorrelation was detected, the Prais-Winsten generalized least squares (GLS) estimation method was applied to account for first-order autoregressive errors (AR [1]). Model fit was compared between ordinary least squares (OLS) and Prais-Winsten estimation using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the model with the lower AIC was selected [24, 25]. In all 16 models, the Prais-Winsten specification was preferred over OLS based on AIC comparisons.

Because the analysis used monthly aggregated data, day-of-week effects are not applicable. Seasonality was not explicitly modeled, as the pre-intervention period comprised only 12 monthly observations, which is insufficient to reliably estimate seasonal parameters. This limitation is acknowledged in the discussion. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/MP 18.0 and R version 4.5.2.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

In 2019 and 2020, the study included 919 participants in the non-chronic group and 2,589 from the chronic disease group. Their general characteristics are presented in Table 2. By gender, the non-chronic group comprised 376 males (40.9%) and 543 females (59.1%), while the chronic disease group included 1,115 males (43.1%) and 1,474 females (56.9%). Across both groups, the number of female participants exceeded that of males. By age, 722 individuals in the non-chronic group were under 65 years (78.6%) in 2019, compared with 695 (75.6%) in 2020. In the chronic disease group, 1,739 participants were aged 65 or older (67.2%) in 2019, rising to 1,827 (70.6%) in 2020. Regarding usual sources of care, responses for clinics decreased in both groups (non-chronic group: -1.2%, chronic disease group: -1.3%), as did responses for hospitals (non-chronic group: -1.2%, chronic disease group: -0.5%). In contrast, responses for general hospitals increased (non-chronic group: +2.4%, chronic disease group: +1.8%).

Changes in outpatient care by usual source of care during COVID-19

Figures 2 and 3 present monthly trends in the total number of outpatient visits for the non-chronic group and the chronic disease group across the study period (January 2019 to December 2020), with vertical reference lines indicating the first (January 2020), second (April 2020), and third (August 2020) COVID-19 intervention points. Figure 4 illustrates the percentage change in the monthly number of visits between the month immediately before and the month of each intervention point. In terms of clinic utilization, after the first intervention, visits in the non-chronic group decreased among those under 65 (− 29.9%) but increased among those 65 and older (+ 17.5%). In the chronic disease group, utilization declined across all ages (− 18.8% under 65; −12.3% aged 65 or older), with a sharper reduction among younger participants. Following the second intervention, utilization in the non-chronic group declined in both age groups (− 5.3% under 65; −11.7% aged 65 or older). By contrast, the chronic disease group showed an increase (+ 0.7% under 65; +5.7% aged 65 or older), with the larger rise among older individuals. After the third intervention, both the non-chronic group and the chronic disease group showed an overall increase in clinic utilization. In the non-chronic group, visits increased by 12.9% among those under 65 and by 14.5% among those aged 65 or older. In the chronic disease group, visits increased by 3.2% among those under 65 and by 4.1% among those aged 65 or older.

In terms of hospital use, following the first intervention, utilization declined in all groups. In the non-chronic group, the reduction was 14.5% among those under 65 and 32.4% among those 65 or older, indicating a greater reduction among older adults. In the chronic disease group, declines were 24.1% among those under 65 and 9.0% among those 65 or older, showing a greater reduction in younger participants. After the second intervention, utilization in the non-chronic group decreased under age 65 (− 11.2%) but increased among those aged 65 and older (+ 13.6%). In the chronic disease group, utilization rose across both age groups (+ 7.7% under 65; +8.3% aged 65 or older). After the third intervention, utilization increased across all age groups in the non-chronic group, with a rise of 11.3% among individuals under 65 and 6.8% among those aged 65 and older. In the chronic disease group, utilization increased among those under 65 but decreased among those aged 65 and older. Specifically, utilization rose by 12.0% in the under-65 group, while it declined by 0.7% in the 65-and-older group.

Following the first intervention, utilization at general hospitals increased across all age groups in the non-chronic group, with a greater rise observed among older adults—18.7% among those under 65 and 37.0% among those aged 65 or older. In contrast, the chronic disease group showed a decrease in utilization across all age groups, with a 13.5% decrease for those under 65 and a 2.1% decrease for those 65 and older, showing a greater decrease in the age group under 65. After the second intervention, utilization increased across all age groups in both the non-chronic group and the chronic disease group. In the non-chronic group, utilization rose by 12.1% among individuals under 65 and by 1.7% among those aged 65 or older, indicating a greater increase in the younger age group. In the chronic disease group, utilization increased by 3.9% among individuals under 65 and by 7.3% among those aged 65 and older, indicating a greater increase in the older age group. After the third intervention, utilization decreased across all age groups in the non-chronic group, with a 9.9% reduction among those under 65 and a 10.8% reduction among those aged 65 or older. In contrast, within the chronic disease group, outpatient healthcare utilization increased by 6.4% among those under 65, whereas it decreased by 2.8% among those aged 65 or older.

Finally, when examining the overall change in utilization without distinguishing between types of usual sources of care, the non-chronic group showed a 16.0% decrease among individuals under 65, whereas those aged 65 years or older experienced a 1.1% increase following the first intervention. By contrast, the chronic disease group showed a decline in utilization across all age groups, with a 19.3% decrease among those under 65 and an 8.5% decrease among those aged 65 and older. Following the second intervention, the non-chronic group showed a decline in utilization across all age groups, with a 2.9% decrease among those under 65 and a 0.1% decrease among those aged 65 or older. By contrast, the chronic disease group showed an upward trend in utilization across all age groups, with a 4.0% increase among those under 65 and a 6.9% increase among those aged 65 or older. Following the third intervention, utilization increased across all age groups in both the non-chronic group and the chronic disease group. In the non-chronic group, utilization increased by 6.1% among those under 65 and by 3.3% among those aged 65 or older, whereas in the chronic disease group, the increase was 7.0% for those under 65 and 0.5% for those aged 65 or older.

Segmented regression analysis according to COVID-19 intervention

Table 3; Fig. 5 present the Prais-Winsten regression results for the segmented interrupted time series analysis. In each model, D coefficients represent immediate level changes at each intervention point, while P coefficients represent changes in the post-intervention slope (trend) relative to the preceding segment. Only statistically significant coefficients (p < 0.05) are shown with bold markers in Fig. 5; faded markers indicate non-significant estimates. Full regression results for all 16 subgroup models are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

In terms of clinic utilization, individuals under 65 in both the non-chronic group and the chronic disease group showed a significant immediate decline at the first intervention (level change: non-chronic, D1 = − 108.771, 95% CI: −203.06 to − 14.48; chronic, D1 = − 96.244, 95% CI: −185.28 to − 7.21), followed by a significant downward change in trend (slope change: non-chronic, P1 = − 105.383, 95% CI: −170.29 to − 40.48; chronic, P1 = − 74.999, 95% CI: −136.05 to − 13.95). After the second intervention, the trend reversed significantly in both groups (slope change: non-chronic, P2 = 136.986, 95% CI: 67.33 to 206.65; chronic, P2 = 101.521, 95% CI: 35.63 to 167.41). Among individuals aged 65 or older in the chronic disease group, clinic utilization showed a significant downward slope change after the first intervention (P1 = − 256.104, 95% CI: −469.61 to − 42.60), a significant upward slope change after the second intervention (P2 = 379.944, 95% CI: 149.20 to 610.69), and a subsequent significant downward slope change after the third intervention (P3 = − 191.617, 95% CI: −334.52 to − 48.71). No significant changes were observed for individuals aged 65 or older in the non-chronic group.

Regarding hospital utilization, pre-intervention trends showed a significant increase over time in the non-chronic group (under 65: T = 7.361, 95% CI: 2.73 to 12.00; 65 or older: T = 8.374, 95% CI: 5.03 to 11.72) and in the chronic disease group under 65 (T = 8.829, 95% CI: 1.17 to 16.49). Among individuals aged 65 or older in the non-chronic group, a significant immediate decline occurred at the first intervention (level change: D1 = − 66.086, 95% CI: −117.06 to − 15.11), accompanied by a significant downward slope change (P1 = − 34.890, 95% CI: −69.71 to − 0.07). This was followed by a significant upward slope change after the second intervention (P2 = 55.577, 95% CI: 17.79 to 93.36) and a significant downward slope change after the third intervention (P3 = − 31.121, 95% CI: −54.56 to − 7.68). In the chronic disease group, individuals under 65 experienced a significant immediate decline at the first intervention (level change: D1 = − 133.380, 95% CI: −243.28 to − 23.48), with no other significant changes observed.

In the case of general hospitals, the non-chronic group showed a significant immediate increase at the first intervention across both age groups (level change: under 65, D1 = 92.152, 95% CI: 35.69 to 148.61; 65 or older, D1 = 77.191, 95% CI: 25.67 to 128.72). Among those aged 65 or older in the non-chronic group, the initial increase was followed by a significant downward slope change (P1 = − 47.410, 95% CI: −82.55 to − 12.27), then a significant upward slope change after the second intervention (P2 = 62.675, 95% CI: 24.45 to 100.90), and a significant downward slope change after the third intervention (P3 = − 25.020, 95% CI: −48.76 to − 1.28). In the chronic disease group, among individuals aged 65 or older, a significant downward slope change occurred after the first intervention (P1 = − 160.841, 95% CI: −316.30 to − 5.38), followed by a significant upward slope change after the second intervention (P2 = 244.861, 95% CI: 73.91 to 415.82).

When examining overall outpatient healthcare utilization without distinguishing between types of usual sources of care, the non-chronic group showed a significant increasing pre-intervention trend across both age groups (under 65: T = 20.879, 95% CI: 11.82 to 29.94; 65 or older: T = 9.867, 95% CI: 3.86 to 15.88). Among those under 65, a significant downward slope change occurred after the first intervention (P1 = − 144.008, 95% CI: −240.28 to − 47.74), followed by a significant upward slope change after the second (P2 = 183.058, 95% CI: 79.63 to 286.49). Among those 65 or older, a significant upward slope change occurred after the second intervention (P2 = 105.863, 95% CI: 38.18 to 173.54), followed by a significant downward slope change after the third (P3 = − 74.224, 95% CI: −116.27 to − 32.18). In the chronic disease group, individuals under 65 showed a significant pre-intervention increasing trend (T = 23.309, 95% CI: 5.88 to 40.74) and a significant upward slope change after the second intervention (P2 = 213.068, 95% CI: 22.34 to 403.80). Among those aged 65 or older, a significant upward slope change occurred after the second intervention (P2 = 823.644, 95% CI: 213.44 to 1433.85), followed by a significant downward slope change after the third (P3 = − 387.260, 95% CI: −768.07 to − 6.45).

Discussion

This study examined the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on outpatient healthcare utilization across three distinct time points, categorized by health status and age group. The findings indicate that, immediately following the first COVID-19 intervention, outpatient visits to clinic-level institutions declined among individuals under 65 in the non-chronic group and among both age subgroups in the chronic disease group. This reduction, however, was confined to the first intervention phase, as clinic utilization rebounded in all three subgroups following the second intervention. This suggests that access to primary care was restricted for these groups during the pandemic. In contrast, outpatient utilization at hospitals and general hospitals tended to increase, implying that these settings were less affected by pandemic-related disruptions. In the overall analysis, without distinguishing between types of care sources, the non-chronic group showed an overall increase in outpatient utilization, although temporary declines were evident during certain periods. Among individuals with chronic conditions, those aged 65 or older experienced a distinct reduction in outpatient visits.

When examining trends at clinic-level institutions across the three intervention phases, individuals under 65 in both the non-chronic group and the chronic disease group, as well as older adults with chronic conditions, showed significant downward slope changes after the first intervention, followed by significant upward slope changes after the second intervention. A further significant decline in trend occurred after the third intervention exclusively among older adults with chronic conditions. These findings align with earlier studies suggesting that COVID-19 interventions restricted access to primary care. Yoo et al. (2023) reported that outpatient utilization in South Korea decreased by 15.7% during the initial phase of COVID-19 interventions, while the use of public healthcare institutions dropped by 23.4%, pointing to an overall decline in access [4]. Lim et al. (2021), in a scoping review of 18 countries, concluded that COVID-19 interventions significantly reduced access to primary care and exacerbated health inequalities, particularly among individuals with chronic illnesses [26]. Cho et al. (2024) likewise found that, compared to the pre-COVID-19 period (2018–2019), outpatient visits among patients aged 65 and older with hypertension decreased during the intervention period (2020–2021) [6]. Similarly, Stachteas et al. (2022) noted that the pandemic disrupted and delayed regular primary care for patients with chronic diseases [11].

In the early phase of the pandemic, most healthcare resources were diverted toward the infectious disease response, leading to reduced healthcare utilization, particularly among younger segments of the non-chronic group as well as among individuals with chronic conditions. Among older adults with chronic conditions, a further reduction in clinic-level trend re-emerged later in the study period following the third intervention. This pattern may reflect the heightened vulnerability of patients with chronic illnesses, who faced not only greater risks of complications if infected but also increased fear of contracting COVID-19. Chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease are often associated with compromised immune function, raising the likelihood of severe illness and prolonged recovery if infected [27, 28]. These concerns likely contributed to reduced healthcare utilization, even when care was necessary. Although continuity of care for chronic conditions was not significantly disrupted, the frequency of visits among older patients with hypertension declined. These findings underscore the need for institutional measures to mitigate disparities in healthcare utilization among vulnerable populations during infectious disease crises.

Second, an analysis of outpatient healthcare utilization trends in hospitals and general hospitals revealed that the number of outpatient visits increased across all age groups in hospitals over time and that in general hospitals, outpatient visits temporarily increased across all age groups following the initial intervention. Among individuals with chronic conditions, outpatient visits to hospitals decreased among those under 65 following the first intervention in early 2020, whereas outpatient visits to general hospitals increased among those aged 65 or older after the second intervention in April 2020. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that smaller medical institutions below the hospital level are more significantly affected by COVID-19, leading to a decline in outpatient healthcare utilization. Oh et al. (2021), analyzing over three billion health insurance claims from 2017 to 2020, reported a marked decline in outpatient visits at medical institutions below the hospital level [29]. This outcome appears to be driven by unclear information regarding COVID-19 as a novel infectious disease and a heightened fear of infection, which contributed to reduced utilization particularly at smaller medical institutions [29, 30]. In particular, uncertainty surrounding infection may have led patients to prefer general hospitals over smaller medical institutions, as larger institutions were observed to be less affected by pandemic-related utilization declines [29]. Whether patients actively sought out general hospitals based on perceived infection-control superiority, however, warrants further investigation through patient-level survey data.

Third, the overall analysis without differentiating institution type revealed that, while the non-chronic group showed fluctuating patterns across intervention periods, outpatient visits generally increased over time. Among individuals with chronic conditions, outpatient utilization increased following the second intervention. This finding is consistent with previous results indicating that even during the COVID-19 pandemic, outpatient visits for major chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and cancer in Korea did not significantly decline [31]. Park et al. (2022) analyzed changes in outpatient utilization among older adults in Korea during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic and found no significant changes in the number of outpatient visits for major chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cancer. This can be interpreted as the result of timely government interventions in South Korea, such as early response measures, transparent information dissemination, and recommendations for personal hygiene practices, which helped alleviate public fear and minimize the decline in outpatient healthcare utilization [30, 32, 33]. These findings suggest the need to strengthen primary care, enhance coordination between healthcare institutions, and implement targeted policy interventions for vulnerable populations to ensure continued outpatient access for patients with chronic diseases, even during outbreaks of infectious diseases such as COVID-19.

This study has several limitations. First, it did not fully account for the detailed characteristics of medical institutions. Institutions were broadly classified as clinics, hospitals, and general hospitals, without consideration of factors such as regional distribution, infection control systems, or departmental specialties, which may have influenced utilization patterns. Second, psychosocial and policy-related factors were not quantitatively controlled. Variables such as social distancing, mask mandates, vaccination rates, and changing public perceptions of infection risk were excluded, limiting explanatory power. Third, the analysis was limited to the period from January 2019 to December 2020, and therefore does not capture the major COVID-19 waves occurring in 2021 and 2022, which were characterized by the emergence of the Delta and Omicron variants, the rollout of national vaccination programs, and shifts in government response strategies such as the transition to a ‘living with COVID-19’ policy. These contextual differences may have substantially altered patterns of healthcare utilization in ways not reflected in the present findings. Accordingly, the generalizability of our conclusions to the later phases of the pandemic should be interpreted with caution, and future research incorporating data from 2021 onward would be necessary to provide a more comprehensive understanding of pandemic-related changes in outpatient healthcare utilization. Fourth, the chronic disease group was defined based on the standard chronic disease classification used in the KHPS, which does not separately identify several conditions associated with a higher risk of severe COVID-19, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cancer. As patients with these excluded conditions often require sustained outpatient care, the observed utilization patterns may not fully reflect pandemic effects across the broader chronic disease population.

Conclusion

This study analyzed changes in outpatient healthcare utilization following the onset of COVID-19, distinguishing between the non-chronic group and individuals with chronic conditions. The findings show that the pandemic led to a marked decrease in outpatient visits at small-scale medical institutions, while clinic-level facilities revealed differing patterns between the non-chronic group and elderly individuals with chronic diseases. In particular, the decline in clinic visits among elderly individuals with chronic conditions appears to reflect fear of infection, raising concerns that continuity of chronic disease management may be disrupted during infectious disease outbreaks. These results highlight the need for systematic measures that clearly define the respective roles of acute and chronic disease care and ensure that primary care institutions can reliably fulfill their functions within an infectious disease response system. In addition, it is necessary to strengthen telemedicine and other remote care infrastructure to prepare for future public health crises. During the COVID-19 pandemic, telemedicine played a critical role in several countries by addressing gaps in healthcare delivery. In South Korea, it was temporarily permitted and played a complementary role in mitigating restrictions on outpatient care. In the future, institutionalizing a digital healthcare system integrated with primary care could help reduce disparities in healthcare access according to region, age group, and health condition, while ensuring continuous care for vulnerable populations.
