Evaluation of margin width in phantom teeth prepared for prosthetic crowns using digital scans: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wioletta Bereziewicz, Joanna Waligóra, Joanna Ryniewicz, Jolanta Pytko-Polończyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProper margin width is one of the key factors determining the correct fabrication of a prosthetic crown. Digital technologies, such as intraoral scanners, are increasingly used to assess parameters of tooth preparation for prosthetic crowns. The study aimed to evaluate the conservativeness of tooth preparation performed by third-year dental students on phantom teeth based on margin width and the utility of an intraoral scanner for its assessment.MethodsA total of 432 model teeth (KaVo) prepared for crowns by third-year dental students were tested: 216 maxillary right first molars (tooth 16) with a chamfer, and 216 maxillary right first premolars (tooth 14) with a classical shoulder. Tooth 16 was prepared first, followed by tooth 14 one week later. After each preparation, students were evaluated and received instructor feedback. All teeth were scanned using a 3Shape TRIOS 3 intraoral scanner, finish line widths were measured and compared to ideal crown preparation standards. Continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Margin widths were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (with rank-biserial effect sizes), and categorical classifications were analyzed with the chi-square test. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted. Calculations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 and R software (version 4.4.2).ResultsFor tooth 16, median chamfer margin widths were: mesial 1.08 mm [IQR: 0.80–1.45], distal 1.17 mm [IQR: 0.79–1.55], lingual 1.17 mm [IQR: 0.87–1.41], and buccal 1.07 mm [IQR: 0.86–1.35]. Optimal widths were achieved by 22.9% (mesial), 21.9% (distal), 16.7% (lingual), and 15.7% (buccal) of students. For tooth 14, median widths were: mesial 0.67 mm [IQR: 0.51–0.93], distal 0.87 mm [IQR: 0.63–1.29], lingual 0.80 mm [IQR: 0.63–1.06], and buccal 0.78 mm [IQR: 0.60–1.01], with optimal widths achieved by 40% (mesial), 32.4% (distal), 41.4% (lingual), and 11.9% (buccal).ConclusionsThe study revealed significant variability in finish line width among students and tooth surfaces, with most preparations being excessively wide. An intraoral scanner may be a useful tool for assessing the margin width in prepared phantom teeth.

Background

Teaching effectiveness is a key issue affecting the education of future dentists. It has a direct impact on the accuracy of medical procedures performed by future dentists and, as a consequence, the quality of the treatment they provide. Dentistry is a primarily a practice-based discipline, and both imparting theoretical knowledge and developing manual skills are crucial to teaching effectiveness [1, 2]. Another vital factor is the method by which students’ progress is assessed. Such a method involves systematic assessments that motivate students and make possible the rapid identification of any gaps in knowledge or practical skills. Equally valuable in the assessment process is clear feedback from teachers, which provides students with opportunities for continuous improvement [3].

One of the most challenging procedures for dental students is performing fixed prosthetic restorations. Studies assessing the self-confidence of fifth-year dental students revealed that they felt most prepared and confident when making direct restorations, followed by endodontics, and, finally, fixed prosthetics [4]. Graduates still require supervision from instructors and demonstrate average levels of self-confidence when it comes to certain aspects of crown and bridge procedures, particularly tooth preparation [5]. Proper tooth preparation guarantees functional and aesthetic success and ensures the durability of future restorations. One of the most important aspects of tooth preparation for prosthetic crowns is the conservation of tooth structure. This procedure involves the removal of only the necessary amount of tooth structure while preserving the natural tooth anatomy to the greatest extent possible [6].

As part of the fixed prosthodontics curriculum in dental courses, students first participate in theoretical classes, which include lectures and seminars. Subsequently, students participate in a series of preclinical practical sessions during which they perform tooth preparations on phantom teeth for prosthetic crowns. Immediately after preparation, each tooth is visually assessed by the instructors, and the student receives feedback on the quality of the preparation. This method allows students to improve their psychomotor skills by applying their theoretical knowledge in practice. This also builds their self-confidence. Students are permitted to begin clinical practice only after receiving a passing grade in the preclinical courses and successfully passing the final theoretical examination [7, 8]. Preclinical classes play an important role in dental education. Velayo et al. reported a positive correlation between students’ initial surgical skills during the phantom stage and their surgical skills in clinical practice [9].

During manual work, students rely on teaching assistants to evaluate their work and identify areas for improvement. Student work is most often assessed visually, comparing each preparation to a reference model of a perfectly prepared tooth. Students either receive a single overall grade for their preparation work, or each important preparation parameter is assessed separately, and a final grade is assigned based on this [10].

Although the primary teaching tool at most universities remains, traditionally, a visual assessment of a student’s work, advances in digital technology have led to the emergence of more advanced methods for assessing tooth preparation. Recent years have seen a significant increase in the use of modern digital devices in dentistry. Universities have implemented various innovations to ensure the highest quality of education and meet student expectations. However, students are usually reluctant to rely solely on digital assessments, as they require adequate demonstrations of incorrectly performed tasks and feedback from their instructors. Students perceive digital methods as a valuable addition to preclinical and clinical classes, and the best solution seems to be to combine traditional assessment methods with digital approaches [11, 12].

The aim of the study was to evaluate the conservativeness of tooth preparation performed by third-year dental students of Jagiellonian University Medical College on phantom teeth with respect to margin width. An additional objective of the study was to assess the utility of an intraoral scanner in evaluating margin width in prepared phantom teeth.

Materials and methods

The study material consisted of 216 premolars and 216 molars provided by KaVo (Biberach, Germany) prepared for crowns by third-year dental students from Jagiellonian University Medical College (Krakow, Poland). A total of 216 students, with an average age of 22 years, participated in the study. The premolars (tooth 14 according to the FDI World Dental Federation notation - the maxillary first premolar on the right) were prepared with a classic shoulder (a straight shoulder on the buccal side, a chamfer on the lingual side) with a recommended width of 1–1.2 mm on the buccal side and 0.5–0.8 mm on the mesial, distal, and lingual sides. The molars (tooth 16 according to the FDI World Dental Federation notation – the maxillary first molar on the right) were prepared with a chamfer with a recommended width of 0.5–0.8 mm around the entire circumference. The preparation procedure was performed on KaVo mandibles (no. OK T12, KaVo) using a drill set from Prof. Stanisław Majewski and Prof. Bartlomiej W. Loster (Olident, Cologne, Germany). Students participated in a series of theoretical and practical sessions. The theoretical sessions comprised three hours of lectures and two seminars (each lasting 45 min) covering the definition, classification, and indications for crown preparation, as well as the general principles of crown preparation. The practical sessions lasted two hours and 30 min each and were conducted under the supervision of academic teachers with many years of experience. The students first prepared tooth 16, after which they were assessed and provided with feedback on the accuracy and possible imperfections of their work. Then, a week later, they prepared tooth 14, which was likewise assessed and the preparation process reviewed.

The prepared teeth were collected, numbered, and then scanned in 3D using a 3Shapes TRIOS 3 intraoral scanner. Based on the resulting scans, the margin widths were calculated at the midpoint of vestibular, lingual, mesial, and distal sides, and compared to the value of the ideal crown preparation with a classic shoulder and chamfer. All measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner using the ruler function in the 3Shape software. To assess intra-examiner reliability, 20 randomly selected scans were remeasured after a two-week interval. To ensure standardisation, measurements were performed at the midpoint of the vestibular, lingual, mesial, and distal surfaces in each scan.

Continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare finish line widths between the first and second preparations, with rank-biserial correlation effect sizes additionally reported. Categorical margin width classifications were analyzed with the chi-square test. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted. Calculations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 and R software (version 4.4.2).

The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and after approval by the Ethics Committee of the Jagiellonian University Medical College (decision no. 118.0043.1.430.2024). Each participant gave their informed consent to participate in the study.

Results

Tooth 16

The margin width ranged from 0.31 to 4.10 mm on the mesial surface, from 0.29 to 2.74 mm on the distal surface, from 0.38 to 2.10 mm on the lingual surface, and from 0.09 to 2.08 mm on the buccal surface. Median finish line widths with interquartile ranges were as follows: 1.08 mm [0.80–1.45] on the mesial surface, 1.17 mm [0.79–1.55] on the distal surface, 1.17 mm [0.87–1.41] on the lingual surface, and 1.07 mm [0.86–1.35] on the buccal surface (Fig. 1).

Comparison

Finish line widths were significantly smaller on tooth 14 than on tooth 16 for the mesial, distal, and lingual surfaces (p < 0.001). The associated effect sizes were large on the mesial (r = 0.69), lingual (r = 0.52), and buccal surfaces (r = 0.51), and small on the distal surface (r = 0.29).

The proportion of obtained optimal margin widths changed significantly between the first and second preparation on all surfaces (p < 0.001). The proportion of optimal preparations increased on the mesial (from 22.9% to 40.0%), distal (from 21.9% to 32.4%), and lingual (from 16.7% to 41.4%) surfaces, whereas the buccal surface exhibited a marked shift toward too-narrow preparations (from 3.3% to 73.3%), reflecting differences in recommended finish line design between the two teeth (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The main aim of the study was to evaluate the conservativeness of tooth preparation of phantom teeth with respect to margin width. Proper tooth preparation ensures the long-term preservation of a prosthetic restoration in the oral cavity and the health of the tooth tissue. Difficulties in preparation often result from striking a balance between the necessary tissue loss and preservation of the tooth structure [13]. Excessive preparation can lead to an increased risk of pulp damage and weakening of the tooth. Insufficient preparation can hinder the proper fabrication of the crown as inadequate space remains for the future prosthetic restoration and this results in an unsightly tooth restoration [6, 14].

In the present study, the majority of students prepared excessively wide margins during their initial tooth preparations. The average widths of individual margins exceeded 1 mm, compared to the recommended optimal values ​​of 0.5–0.8 mm. The largest number of students correctly prepared the mesial margin, but they represented only 22.9% of all the participants. In the case of the remaining surfaces, these values ​​were even lower, below even 20%. During the second preparation, students were required to achieve a margin width of 1–1.2 mm on the buccal side and 0.5–0.8 mm on the other surfaces. A significantly larger number of students achieved optimal results, with the following results: 40% for the mesial side, 32.4% for the distal side, and 41.4% for the lingual side. During the second preparation, students had more experience and could draw conclusions from the assessments obtained after the first preparation, which we believe may have contributed to the better results. Additionally, tooth 14 is located in a more visible and easily accessible area of ​​the oral cavity, so such preparation may have been easier. On the buccal surface of tooth 14, only 11.9% of the students achieved the optimal margin width, and most of the preparations were too narrow. This may have been due to the fact that the straight shoulder differed in shape from the previously prepared chamfer, and this was the first time the students had performed this type of preparatory work. Another factor could be the fact that during the initial preparation, students tended to prepare excessive margin widths and wanted to work more conservatively during subsequent preparation. During both preparation sessions, the widest surface prepared by each student was the distal surface.

Similarly, in a study conducted by Munchi N., students achieved larger margin widths in more inaccessible areas of the oral cavity during tooth preparation for crowns. Munchi N. reported that students prepared mandibular first and second molars for a fully cast crown with a chamfer. In five of the eight locations measured a significant increase in the margin width was observed in the second molar compared to the first. Furthermore, in this study, students re-prepared the mandibular first molar in the following semester and achieved worse results and larger margin widths during the second preparation than during the first preparation. The authors attribute this to the fact that in the second semester the students performed more work that required a wider margin and were more accustomed to this type of preparation [13]. In a study by Tiu J et al., most of the students who prepared teeth for ceramic-veneered cast crowns achieved the optimal margin width. However, many preparations were performed very conservatively with overly narrow margins. The authors also noted that the students most often achieved the greatest widths on the distal and buccal margins [15]. Al-Omari et al. conducted a study in which students prepared molars for ceramic-veneered cast crowns. During the preparation process, most of the margin widths achieved were too small compared to the optimal widths for this type of preparation [16].

The discrepancies in the margin widths obtained by students, as well as the difficulties in achieving the correct margin width, especially in difficult-to-access areas, indicate the need for improved teaching methods and practical exercises. The width of the margin during tooth preparation for crowns is a significant teaching challenge.

Pre-clinical dentistry courses constitute an important stage in students’ education. Research conducted in medical schools in the UK indicates that students’ postgraduate exam results were largely dependent on their prior performance, both in the entrance exams and during their initial studies [17]. While effective teaching is important at every stage of dental training, mastering the fundamentals is particularly important.

In the study, students first participated in theoretical classes, in which they were given a thorough, step-by-step introduction to preparation techniques. Then, in the practical session, they had the opportunity to perform such work independently and receive a thorough assessment of their efforts together with feedback. Appropriately designed teaching sessions and discipline-specific instructional methods may have facilitated improved tooth preparation outcomes during the second preparation session.

Research results indicate that students in the same or related fields of study prefer similar learning styles, regardless of the country in which the study was conducted. Adapting teaching methods to students’ learning styles can aid student learning. Dentistry, as a practical field of study, also requires a practical approach to student education. Dental students prefer active teaching methods and highly valued teacher demonstrations, followed by the opportunity to perform given procedures independently. Among the teacher characteristics that students find most useful, is the ability to motivate and inspire [18]. Other qualities that can play a significant role in student learning and influence teaching effectiveness include being supportive, possessing communication skills, and the ability to provide positive assessment and feedback.

In the present study, students were assessed using a traditional visual method after each preparation and received feedback from their instructors. The width of the prepared margin was measured digitally using an intraoral scanner. Intraoral scanners are effective in measuring objective preparation parameters, such as margin width, and ensure a high degree of accuracy and precision [12]. According to many authors, digital scanners can serve as an additional educational method in preclinical classes and can also be used to assess student work. Students generally have a positive attitude towards digital assessment systems and their use in tooth preparation training [11, 19]. A digital grading system can reliably scan and compare prepared preparations. This method is repeatable and less time-consuming than traditional techniques [20]. Some authors point out the fact that visual assessment of tooth preparations may not be objective. Moreover, feedback and assessments may be inconsistent, and the results obtained are not always repeatable. This is due to such factors as limited experience of the evaluators in fixed prosthodontics, lack of appropriate evaluator calibration, and evaluator fatigue [10, 21]. However, most recent studies indicate that the use of digital methods in the teaching and assessment of students in preclinical and clinical settings provides similar results to conventional teaching techniques based on teacher knowledge and assessment. The key to any of these methods is the development of objective criteria for assessing preparations that are easy to measure, interpret, and repeat [19, 22, 23].

The study had certain limitations. One of these may be the fact that it compared the preparations of two different teeth – a molar and a premolar – and two different types of preparation for different types of prosthetic crowns. Future studies should include the same teeth and the same preparation types. Additionally, although all measurements using the intraoral scanner were performed by the same person and in the same tooth area, it was difficult to measure the margin width at identical points for each tooth.

Conclusion

The results of the study indicate considerable variation in the margin width achieved by students as well as between different tooth surfaces. Most students prepared their phantom teeth too wide. The second preparation (tooth 14) resulted in a higher proportion of optimal finish line width values in the mesial, distal, and lingual surfaces. An intraoral scanner may be a useful tool for assessing the finish line width in prepared phantom teeth.
