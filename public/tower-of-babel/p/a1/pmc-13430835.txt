Postmenopausal bladder endometriosis without exogenous estrogen: a case report and literature review
Authors: Namei Wu, Yuxuan He, Yazhuo Jia, Guoyun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometriosis is a common estrogen-dependent disorder that typically affects women of reproductive age and generally resolves following menopause. This report describes a rare case of postmenopausal bladder endometriosis (BE) occurring in a woman with a normal body mass index and no history of exogenous estrogen use.Case presentationA postmenopausal woman with a normal body mass index and no history of estrogen replacement therapy presented with dysuria. Clinical examination and imaging suggested bladder endometriosis. The patient underwent laparoscopic partial cystectomy with total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. Postoperative histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of endometriosis.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that BE can occur in postmenopausal women in the absence of exogenous estrogen or obesity. The coexistence of liver cirrhosis, associated with impaired estrogen metabolism, and diabetes, characterized by chronic inflammation, may synergistically contribute to a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that sustains local estrogen activity, enabling lesion persistence. Clinicians should consider such systemic factors in non-obese postmenopausal women presenting with symptoms suggestive of endometriosis.Keywords: Postmenopausal, Endometriosis, Urinary bladder, Liver cirrhosis, Diabetes mellitus, Case report

Introduction

Bladder endometriosis (BE), defined as the presence of ectopic endometrial glands and stroma infiltrating the detrusor muscle, is an uncommon yet clinically significant subtype of deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) [1, 2]. While DIE affecting the urinary tract accounts for approximately 1% of all endometriosis cases, BE is the most prevalent form (70–85%), followed by ureteral involvement [2, 3]. The pathophysiology of BE remains incompletely elucidated, with prevailing theories including coelomic metaplasia, lymphatic/vascular dissemination, and iatrogenic implantation; however, the most widely accepted mechanism involves retrograde menstruation of viable endometrial cells with subsequent invasion and proliferation within the bladder wall, often associated with adhesions from anterior DIE [4–6].

Postmenopausal endometriosis, affecting 2–5% of postmenopausal women, has been described predominantly in case reports, with a paucity of large-scale studies [7]. Endometriotic lesions persist and progress within a systemic low-estrogen environment through local estrogen synthesis enabled by enzymes such as aromatase, 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, and steroidogenic acute regulatory protein within the lesion microenvironment [7].

A comprehensive review has systematically delineated multiple endometriosis-related signaling pathways, including NF-κB, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, TGF-β, and MAPK/ERK, which orchestrate chronic inflammation, fibrotic remodeling, and cell survival within ectopic lesions [8]. Beyond estrogen-driven mechanisms, these signaling networks can be activated by non-estrogenic stimuli such as advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) and inflammatory cytokines, providing a molecular framework for understanding lesion persistence in the setting of metabolic and inflammatory derangements.

This report presents, to our knowledge, the first reported case combining postmenopausal BE with compensated cirrhosis and uncontrolled diabetes in a non-obese woman without HRT, and proposes a novel pathophysiological dual-hit metabolic-inflammatory hypothesis. Although postmenopausal BE without hormone replacement therapy has been occasionally reported [9–14], this case presents a unique pathophysiological hypothesis: in the absence of adipose-derived estrogen due to obesity and without exogenous estrogen supplementation, the coexistence of cirrhosis and diabetes, which together produce a chronic inflammatory state, may serve as a promoting factor for maintaining the local microenvironment of postmenopausal endometriosis. This unique metabolic background may provide insights into estrogen-independent mechanisms of lesion persistence.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old woman (6 years postmenopausal) presented with a five-day history of dysuria and dull, intermittent, lower abdominal pain. She denied having hematuria or fever and had never received hormone replacement therapy. Her body mass index (BMI) was 23.2 kg/m².

Her menstrual history was notable for menarche at age 14, natural menopause at age 53, and regular cycles (occurring every 27 days with a 5-day duration) prior to menopause; her obstetric history included two spontaneous vaginal deliveries and no miscarriages (gravida 2, para 2), and she reported a history of tolerable dysmenorrhea.

She had a seven-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus with suboptimal glycaemic control (highest recorded postprandial glucose level, 13 mmol/L), managed with no pharmacotherapy. Her gynecological history included intrauterine device (IUD) retention for more than 20 years, and bilateral tubal ligation performed 10 years before presentation. The patient had a long-standing history of hepatitis B-related cirrhosis. Laboratory tests revealed significantly elevated liver enzymes, with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 122 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 189 U/L, accompanied by a mildly reduced serum albumin level of 35 g/L.

Serum hormonal assays revealed the following: luteinizing hormone, 23.3 mIU/mL (postmenopausal reference: 14.4–62.2 mIU/mL); follicle-stimulating hormone, 45.9 (36.6–168.8) mIU/mL; estrogen 5.61 (< 20) pg/mL; progesterone, 0.06 (< 1) ng/mL; and anti-Müllerian hormone < 0.02 ng/mL. Serum hormone levels confirmed postmenopausal status. Urine occult blood was 0.03 mg/dL.

Gynecologic examination revealed a normal vulva and patent vagina. A tender mass approximately 2 cm in size with limited mobility was palpated in the anterior fornix. The uterus and bilateral adnexa were unremarkable. Transvaginal ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic nodule(4.1 × 3.5 × 1.9 cm) between the anterior uterine and posterior bladder walls, suggestive of DIE (Fig. 1).

The findings also included a postmenopausal uterus and IUD. Abdominal computed tomography revealed normal excretion and no upper urinary tract dilatation. The bladder was adequately filled, but the posterior wall appeared irregular. Pelvic computed tomography (plain and contrast) revealed a morphologically normal uterus with heterogeneous myometrial density and an IUD in situ. The anterior uterine and posterior bladder walls were indistinct, with the latter showing slight thickening, local irregularity, and mild heterogeneous contrast enhancement (Fig. 2).

Transvaginal ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic solid nodule with ill-defined margins, consistent with the fibrotic characteristics of deep infiltrating bladder endometriosis. However, despite the absence of overt mucosal irregularity or papillary projections on preoperative CT and ultrasonography, these imaging modalities were insufficient to reliably distinguish bladder endometriosis from urologic malignancies [15, 16]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with T1-weighted fat-suppressed sequences would have been valuable for detecting T1-hyperintense foci indicative of hemorrhage—a characteristic feature of endometriosis that is rarely observed in urothelial carcinoma [15]. The inability to perform preoperative MRI due to the presence of a metallic intrauterine device represented a limitation in our diagnostic workup. Preoperative cystoscopy revealed an intact bladder mucosa without evidence of tumor, ulceration, or bleeding, thereby effectively excluding primary urothelial malignancy. This case underscores that in postmenopausal women presenting with bladder masses, a high index of suspicion for malignancy must be maintained, and histopathological confirmation is mandatory even when imaging findings suggest a benign etiology.

Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent combined gynecological and urological surgery, including laparoscopic total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and excision of the bladder endometriotic lesion. Intraoperative frozen section analysis confirmed BE. Exploration revealed that the lesion had infiltrated the distal ureter; consequently, concurrent cystoscopy was performed and bilateral double-J ureteral stents were inserted to preserve ureteral patency and prevent iatrogenic injury (Fig. 3). Complete excision is the cornerstone of bladder endometriosis management, as residual disease is associated with a high recurrence rate [17].

Laparoscopic view of the bladder endometriotic lesion. Laparoscopic view showing dense adhesions between the posterior bladder wall and anterior uterine wall. The lesion appears as a grayish-white, firm intramural mass with ill-defined borders and no intact capsule

The postoperative regimen comprised antibiotic prophylaxis; haemostatic agents; continuous catheter drainage with bladder irrigation; insulin for diabetes management; and hepatoprotective and symptomatic support. Histopathological examination revealed endometrial stroma-like tissue with focal foamy histiocyte infiltration in the bladder lesion, consistent with endometriosis, whereas the uterus and adnexa exhibited an atrophic endometrium, chronic cervicitis, and absence of pathological abnormalities. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated weak positivity for the estrogen and progesterone receptors (Fig. 4).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical features of the bladder lesion. a Hematoxylin-eosin staining shows typical endometrial glands and stroma within the bladder muscularis propria, consistent with endometriosis. b Partial bladder epithelium is observed adjacent to the lesion. c Immunohistochemical staining reveals weak positive expression of estrogen receptor in the glandular epithelial cells. d Immunohistochemical staining shows weak positive expression of progesterone receptor in the glandular epithelial cells. Scale bars: 100 μm (a–d)

Follow-up bladder ultrasonography on postoperative day seven demonstrated suboptimal bladder distension with a regular wall contour and an indwelling catheter showing the inflated balloon. The catheter was removed after three weeks of continuous drainage. Vaginal cuff examination one month postoperatively revealed complete healing.

Discussion and conclusions

This case offers a unique pathophysiological perspective that distinguishes it from previously reported postmenopausal bladder endometriosis (Table 1) by several features. First, the patient presented with dysuria as the sole symptom, whereas all previously reported cases presented with hematuria or pyuria. Second, her normal BMI (23.2 kg/m²) argues against significant adipose-derived estrogen synthesis; prior cases were either obese [9] or lacked available BMI data [10–14]. Third, her serum estradiol concentration (5.61 pg/mL) is the lowest reported among comparable cases. Fourth, to our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of postmenopausal BE coexisting with compensated cirrhosis and poorly controlled diabetes. Collectively, these features underscore a central paradox: how can an aggressive lesion persist and remain symptomatic when systemic estrogen is nearly depleted?

The classical “estrogen-dependence” theory posits that the persistence of postmenopausal endometriotic lesions primarily relies on two pathways: first, peripheral adipose tissue converts androstenedione to estrogen via aromatase; and second, the lesion site forms an autocrine positive feedback loop through aberrantly expressed aromatase (CYP19A1) and steroidogenic factor-1 (SF-1), autonomously synthesizing estradiol [18–20]. However, in this case, the normal BMI excludes the first pathway; while the second pathway may still exist, it typically requires a certain level of local estrogen to initiate and maintain it. This patient’s extremely low circulating estradiol level suggests that other mechanisms independent of the estrogen pathway may be acting synergistically or alternatively to the classical estrogen-dependent loop. Indeed, recent advances in endometriosis-related signaling pathways have identified multiple estrogen-independent networks that drive lesion persistence, including NF-κB-mediated chronic inflammation, TGF-β/Smad-dependent fibrotic remodeling, and PI3K/A kt/mTOR-regulated cell survival and anti-apoptotic signaling [8, 21].

Based on the unique metabolic background of this patient, we propose a “dual-hit” hypothesis to explain lesion persistence. The first “hit” comes from cirrhosis. Cirrhosis leads to reduced hepatic sulfation and inactivation of estrogen, altering the estrogen metabolic balance and resulting in a relatively increased proportion of biologically active free estrogen. On the basis of already significantly reduced ovarian-derived estrogen after menopause, patients with cirrhosis may still maintain serum estradiol levels higher than those of healthy postmenopausal women of the same age (although the absolute value remains low) [22]. The second “hit” comes from uncontrolled diabetes. The patient’s long-standing chronic hyperglycemia (postprandial glucose peak 13.0 mmol/L) leads to massive generation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) in vivo [23]. AGEs activate the highly expressed receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) in lesion tissues, triggering HMGB1-mediated NF-κB nuclear translocation, directly driving the expression of inflammatory factors (such as IL-6, TNF-α), profibrotic factors (such as TGF-β), and COX-2 [23]. This process is completely independent of the estrogen-estrogen receptor signaling axis. Furthermore, cirrhosis itself is a chronic systemic low-grade inflammatory state, characterized by elevated circulating endotoxin and inflammatory cytokines [24]. This systemic inflammatory background may trigger a superimposed and synergistic effect of local inflammation, together constituting a powerful pro-inflammatory, profibrotic microenvironment that enables the lesion to persist, progress, and produce clinical symptoms in the absence of typical estrogen drive.

We acknowledge that alternative pathophysiological explanations cannot be definitively excluded. Residual microscopic disease from long-standing endometriosis—potentially dating to the patient’s reproductive years and rendered symptomatic by the metabolic-inflammatory milieu—or müllerian metaplasia of the bladder detrusor could theoretically account for lesion persistence, independent of systemic estrogen or the metabolic derangements described above. However, the absence of prior confirmed endometriosis, the fibrotic rather than metaplastic histology, and the documented endometrial stroma with foamy histiocytes favor a residual endometriotic origin sustained by the proposed dual-hit mechanism. This “dual-hit” hypothesis also mechanistically distinguishes this case from previously reported postmenopausal BE (Table 1). In previous cases, some obese patients could be attributed to aromatization in adipose tissue, while other idiopathic cases had unknown mechanisms. This case clearly demonstrates the synergistic pathogenic role of metabolic-inflammatory-hormonal metabolic disorders, providing a new perspective for understanding the heterogeneity of postmenopausal endometriosis.

At the clinical diagnosis and treatment level, this case also has important implications. First, the clinical presentation of postmenopausal bladder endometriosis is frequently atypical. In this case, the patient presented with dysuria as the sole initial symptom, which increased the risk of misdiagnosis. Therefore, when postmenopausal women present with nonspecific lower urinary tract symptoms (such as isolated dysuria), endometriosis should be included in the differential diagnosis, alongside urothelial carcinoma, interstitial cystitis, cystitis glandularis, urinary tract tuberculosis, and pelvic lipomatosis [25, 26]. Second, transvaginal ultrasound is a key screening tool for BE [2, 27]. CT urography can provide supplementary assessment of renal function and ureteral involvement. Although cystoscopy enables direct visualization and biopsy, it often fails to detect small lesions; in this case, cystoscopy revealed intact mucosa, once again confirming that deep infiltrating BE primarily grows within the muscular layer, and therefore a negative biopsy result should not easily exclude the diagnosis [26]. Thus, histopathological examination combined with immunohistochemistry is the definitive diagnostic method [15, 26]. Finally, multidisciplinary collaboration (urology, gynecology, radiology, and pathology) is essential for accurate diagnosis, optimal surgical planning, and effective management.

Finally, regarding surgical strategy, for such non-hormone-dependent or partially dependent, fibrotic deep lesions, conservative transurethral resection (TUR) carries a high recurrence rate [13, 20], while laparoscopic partial cystectomy can achieve radical excision. Given that the patient was already menopausal and had a retained IUD, concurrent total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is a reasonable choice to completely remove any potential ovarian estrogen source and the lesion “soil”.

This study has certain limitations. First, due to the presence of a metallic IUD in the patient, pelvic MRI could not be performed, which limited our preoperative precise assessment of lesion hemorrhagic foci, depth of infiltration, and other characteristics—MRI being the best non-invasive modality for distinguishing DIE from bladder cancer [15]. Second, this hypothesis is based on clinical and pathological inferences from a single case, lacking direct molecular biological evidence (such as detecting AGEs, RAGE, NF-κB expression levels in lesion tissues, or comparing serum inflammatory factor profiles related to cirrhosis/diabetes). Future validation through basic research or in similar clinical cases is needed.

This case definitively demonstrates that bladder endometriosis can occur in postmenopausal women without exogenous estrogen exposure and with a normal BMI. Against such an extremely low-estrogen background, concurrent cirrhosis and uncontrolled diabetes may sustain lesion persistence through a novel “dual-hit” synergistic mechanism: the first hit, cirrhosis-induced impairment of estrogen clearance, provides a low-level but sustained “permissive” hormonal microenvironment; the second hit, diabetes-driven chronic hyperglycemia activating the AGEs-RAGE inflammatory pathway, superimposed on cirrhosis-related systemic inflammatory status, together constitute a powerful local pro-inflammatory and profibrotic drive independent of the classical estrogen signaling axis.

The implication of this case is that for non-obese postmenopausal women presenting with unexplained lower urinary tract symptoms, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for endometriosis and conduct a comprehensive evaluation. The evaluation should not be limited to the pelvis but should also include systemic comorbidities that may affect local hormonal bioavailability (such as liver function) and systemic inflammatory status (such as diabetes, chronic infections). These comorbidities may act synergistically to break the traditional expectation that “endometriosis inevitably regresses after menopause,” becoming key regulators of lesion persistence and progression.
