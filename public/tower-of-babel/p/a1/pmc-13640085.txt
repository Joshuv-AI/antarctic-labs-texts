High-efficiency callus induction from intact Camellia sinensis leaves via controlled incisions and its regulatory mechanisms
Authors: Tian Lili, Li Juan, Huang Jian′an
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTea (Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze) is a globally important economic crop, but its in vitro tissue culture is severely limited by polyphenol-induced explant browning and low callus induction efficiency. Conventional methods relying on fragmented leaf explants exacerbate these issues, and the regulatory mechanisms underlying genotypic differences in callus induction remain poorly understood.ResultsWe optimized a callus induction protocol using intact tea leaves with controlled transverse incisions, combining liquid-solid co-culture and staged dark/light cycles. The optimal protocol-5-8 transverse incisions on Huangjincha leaves, followed by 3-day liquid dark preculture, 12-day solid dark culture, and 15-day 16/8 h photoperiod-reduced the browning rate to 32.0 ± 0.9% and achieved a callus induction rate of 68.0 ± 2.2%, producing healthy, light-yellow, friable calli. Morphologically, Huangjincha (high induction efficiency) exhibited sparser epidermal wax, intact chloroplast ultrastructure, and larger stomatal area compared to Yunnandaye (low induction efficiency), which had dense wax aggregates and fragmented chloroplasts under in vitro conditions. Physiologically, Huangjincha maintained higher superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) activities, supporting effective reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, while Yunnandaye showed elevated alternative antioxidant enzyme activities but persistent high browning. Transcriptomic analysis identified 27,520 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two cultivars: Huangjincha upregulated genes related to cell cycle progression (CsCDK2, CsCYCD3) and ROS scavenging (CsSOD2, CsPOD12), whereas Yunnandaye enriched genes in polyphenol biosynthesis (CsCHS, CsC4H).ConclusionsOur optimized protocol effectively mitigates key bottlenecks in tea tissue culture. The integrated “structure-physiology-transcriptome” analysis reveals the mechanistic basis of genotypic differences in callus induction, identifying actionable targets for improving tissue culture efficiency in recalcitrant tea varieties. This study provides a scalable tool for tea biotechnology and offers insights for optimizing tissue culture protocols in other polyphenol-rich crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09482-x.

Background

Tea (Camellia sinensis(L.) O. Kuntze) is one of the most economically valuable cash crops globally, with annual production exceeding 6 million tons and cultivation spanning over 50 countries [1]. Beyond its economic significance, tea is a rich source of bioactive compounds (e.g., catechins, theaflavins, and caffeine) with well-documented health benefits, making it a model for studying secondary metabolism in woody perennials [2]. Tea tissue culture plays a pivotal role in germplasm preservation, rapid propagation of elite cultivars, and genetic improvement-particularly for traits like disease resistance and enhanced secondary metabolite production [3, 4]. However, tea tissue culture faces two long-standing bottlenecks: excessive explant browning and low callus regeneration efficiency, which severely limit its application in biotechnological breeding [5].

This challenge is primarily attributed to the high polyphenol content in tea leaves (up to 30% of dry weight), which are rapidly oxidized by polyphenol oxidases (PPOs) and peroxidases (PODs) upon explant wounding. The resulting quinones are cytotoxic, inducing cell necrosis, inhibiting callus initiation, and disrupting hormonal signaling cascades critical for dedifferentiation [6–10]. Similar issues plague other polyphenol-rich crops such as grapes (Vitis vinifera), apples (Malus domestica), and olive (Olea europaea), where browning rates often exceed 70% and callus induction rates drop below 30% using conventional methods [7, 11]. For tea, these problems are exacerbated by genotypic variation: varieties like Yunnandaye, which accumulate high levels of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), exhibit browning rates up to 80% and almost no viable callus formation, whereas Huangjincha (a low-polyphenol cultivar) shows relatively better tissue culture performance [12, 13].

Traditional tea tissue culture relies on fragmented leaf explants (0.5–1 cm² pieces), which inherently increase mechanical damage and polyphenol leakage-two major drivers of browning [14, 15]. While previous studies have explored single-factor optimizations (e.g., adding antioxidants, adjusting hormone ratios, or modifying light conditions) [16, 17], these approaches suffer from three critical limitations: (1) they fail to address the root cause of browning (excessive wounding) by continuing to use fragmented explants; (2) they ignore synergistic effects between multiple culture parameters (e.g., liquid preculture combined with dark/light cycles); and (3) they lack integration of regulatory mechanisms across structural, physiological, and molecular levels, limiting the development of genotype-independent protocols. To mitigate explant browning and raise callus induction efficiency in tea and other polyphenol-laden woody crops, three mainstream ameliorative strategies have been widely adopted in prior research, each with distinct merits and unavoidable drawbacks. First, exogenous antioxidant supplementation (ascorbic acid, polyvinylpyrrolidone, citric acid) directly neutralizes oxidized quinones, yet the inhibitory effect is transient; high antioxidant concentrations severely suppress cell division and dedifferentiation, while low doses fail to sustain long-term anti-browning effects during subculture [18, 19]. Second, medium component optimization, including reduced nitrogen concentration, modified sucrose levels, and alternative gelling agents, alleviates oxidative stress indirectly but cannot eliminate mechanical wounding-induced polyphenol leakage-the root trigger of browning [18]. Third, conventional explant pretreatment (cold dark pre-culture, surface scarification, soaking with chelating agents) partially reduces phenolic oxidation, but all these methods still rely on fragmented leaf explants with massive cutting wounds, which continuously release polyphenols and aggravate necrosis [17]. None of the above integrated liquid preculture, staged dark-light shifting, and minimally wounded intact leaf materials to simultaneously solve the dual defects of excessive wound damage and insufficient nutrient absorption. Distinct from all previously reported single-factor optimization schemes, our novel protocol uses intact leaves with controlled shallow transverse incisions to minimize total wound area and polyphenol efflux, combined with three-stage liquid-solid co-culture to dilute toxic phenolics and coordinate dark-induced cell dedifferentiation, which fundamentally avoids the core limitation of fragmented explant materials and achieves synergistic anti-browning and high callus regeneration without relying on high-dose exogenous antioxidants [17].

Few studies have systematically explored intact tea leaves with controlled transverse incisions, an optimized strategy to minimize wounding-induced polyphenol leakage while ensuring sufficient nutrient and hormone uptake for callus initiation [20]. Moreover, genotypic differences in tea tissue culture performance (e.g., Huangjincha vs. Yunnandaye) remain poorly understood, with no clarifications on how structural characteristics, physiological responses and transcriptional reprogramming jointly shape differential callus induction outcomes. In polyphenol-rich crops, callus induction is governed by the interplay of four core regulatory pathways, including polyphenol metabolism controlling explant browning via the balance of phenylpropanoid synthesis and PPO/POD-mediated oxidation [21], antioxidant enzyme (SOD, CAT, APX)-dependent ROS scavenging for oxidative homeostasis [22], cytokinin (BA) and auxin (IBA)-mediated hormone signaling for cell dedifferentiation [23], and transcriptional reprogramming of genes related to cell cycle, stress tolerance and secondary metabolism [7, 21]. However, the integrated regulatory network of these pathways and its genotype-dependent modulation in tea callus induction remain largely unclear, and three critical research gaps still hinder the progress of tea tissue culture research. First, the feasibility of controlled transverse incisions on intact leaves remains unverified, and it is unclear whether targeted incisions can balance low browning degree and stable callus initiation despite the advantage of minimal wounding in reducing polyphenol exudation [16]. Second, the molecular and ultrastructural basis of genotypic variation in culture responses is elusive, and multi-omics integrated analysis is urgently needed to dissect the genotypic regulatory differences between low-browning Huangjincha and susceptible Yunnandaye [21, 22]. Third, synergistic effects of multiple culture factors are frequently neglected, and the combined regulatory mechanism of liquid preculture and dark/light cycling on tea callus induction has not been systematically explored [17, 24].

Resolving the aforementioned research gaps is of great significance for tea biotechnology and agricultural biotechnology research. A mature and efficient callus induction system not only enables high-efficiency preservation of tea germplasm resources but also provides a reliable platform for CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genetic improvement, making up for the relative lag of tea genetic engineering compared with major staple crops [2]. Furthermore, the mechanistic findings and optimized culture protocols obtained from tea materials can be extended to other polyphenol-rich crops, providing universal technical and theoretical references for the optimization of tissue culture systems in similar plant species.

Against this background, this study developed a novel multifaceted culture protocol combining intact leaves with 5–8 transverse incisions, liquid-solid co-culture and staged dark/light cycles to address the inherent defects of traditional fragmented explant culture. The three core objectives of this study were as follows: (1) To compare the callus induction performance (browning rate, induction rate and callus quality) of Huangjincha and Yunnandaye under the optimized culture system; (2) To elucidate the genotypic differential regulatory mechanisms via integrated analysis of cellular ultrastructure, physiological traits and transcriptome profiling; (3) To establish a comprehensive “structure-physiology-molecular” regulatory framework and identify key targets for improving the tissue culture efficiency of recalcitrant tea varieties. This study aims to break through the long-term technical bottlenecks of tea tissue culture and provide a feasible theoretical and technical basis for protocol optimization of polyphenol-rich crops.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental setup

Five-year-old tea plants of Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze cv. Huangjincha and Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze cv. Yunnandaye were grown at the experimental tea farm of Hunan Agricultural University, Changsha, China. All plant materials were taxonomically identified by Prof. Huang Jian′an from College of Horticulture, Hunan Agricultural University. Voucher specimens of the two cultivars have been preserved in Hunan Agricultural University. All plant cultivation and sample collection activities were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and relevant national regulations of China. No wild plant resources or endangered species were sampled, and no additional field collection permits were required. This study fully complied with the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora.

Healthy young leaves from 5-year-old shoots were used as explants. Tea seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots with three individuals per pot, and moss was covered on soil surface to reduce water loss. Ten pots were set for each cultivar, with a total of 20 pots in this experiment. Samples were collected after seedling growth recovery and new shoot emergence. Samples for SEM, TEM, enzyme activity and transcriptomic analysis were collected simultaneously at the beginning of in vitro culture to keep physiological uniformity with callus induction samples.

All plant cultivation and field management were conducted in a controlled greenhouse environment equipped with automatic temperature and humidity monitoring systems (Model: RTH-2000, Shenzhen Ruite Instrument Co., Ltd., China) and adjustable shading devices to ensure consistent growth conditions. Leaf sample collection and anatomical pretreatment were performed using precision electronic analytical balance (FA2004, Shanghai Jingke Electronic Balance Instrument Factory, China), high-pressure sterile water machine (SHZ-95B, Shanghai Yarong Biochemical Instrument Co., Ltd., China), and portable high-speed freezing homogenizer (Tissuelyser-24, Shanghai Jingxin Industrial Development Co., Ltd., China). All explant cutting operations were completed with sterile scalpels and forceps in a vertical laminar flow ultra-clean workbench (SW-CJ-1 F, Sujing Group Co., Ltd., China). Plant materials were temporarily stored in a low-temperature refrigerated incubator (YC-310 L, Hefei Meiling Co., Ltd., China) after sampling to maintain physiological activity and avoid tissue deterioration before subsequent experiments.

Explant disinfection and treatment methods

The explants were divided into two types.

For the first type (fragmented leaf explants), the leaf explants were subjected to the following procedure: First, the petioles were removed, and the leaves were cut. They were then disinfected by soaking in 70% alcohol for 10 s, and then rinsed once with sterile water to remove any residual alcohol. Next, they were disinfected with 10% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min and thoroughly rinsed with sterile water four times. Finally, they were cut into small, 0.7 × 0.7 cm2 pieces using a scalpel for subsequent use.

For the second type of explant (intact leaves with controlled transverse incisions), young and robust green tea leaves were chosen. They were disinfected by soaking in 70%-80% alcohol for 5–10 s, rinsed once with sterile water to remove residual alcohol, then disinfected with 10% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, and finally rinsed with sterile water four times. Depending on the explant size, the optimal strategy was to make 5–8 transverse cuts perpendicular to the leaf vein direction using a flamed scalpel, cutting through two-thirds of the leaf width. The resulting explant remained intact and complete [2, 17].

Culture conditions

This study focused on a novel method to efficiently induce callus formation in tea plants using intact tea leaves with controlled transverse incisions as explants, as shown in Fig. 1. The basal medium consisted of Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 4 mg/L benzylaminopurine (BA), 1 mg/L indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), 3% sucrose, and 0.7% (w/v) agar (Supplementary Table S1). The pH was adjusted to 5.6 using a pH meter (Oakton Instruments, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) before sterilization. Intact leaves were immersed in liquid medium for 3 days for absorption, then transferred to solid medium (same composition with agar). Fragmented explants (0.7 × 0.7 cm2) were placed directly on solid medium. All cultures were incubated at 25 ± 2℃ in the dark for 2 weeks, then transferred to a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle under cool white fluorescent light. All experiments were repeated three times.

Scanning electron microscopy

In vitro cultured leaf explants were cut into 5 × 5 mm2 pieces and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for over 12 h. Samples were washed three times with phosphate buffer (pH 7.2–7.4) and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100%) for 15–20 min per step. After critical-point drying and gold sputter coating, samples were examined using a JEOL 7500 F SEM instrument (JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokyo, Japan). Stomatal density, size, and epidermal morphology were recorded. Three random fields of viewview were selected per sample, with stomatal density (number/mm²) and size (length×width) measured using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Three biological replicates were performed per cultivar.

Transmission electron microscopy

For in vitro cultured leaf explants, the second mature leaf from the shoot apex was selected, washed with cold dH2O, blotted dry, and sliced into 0.5 × 1.0 mm2 sections from both sides of the midvein. Tissue pieces were immersed in 3% glutaraldehyde, vacuum-infiltrated for 15 min, and fixed at 4 °C for 4 h. Samples were washed, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 3 h, rinsed, dehydrated through ethanol series, transitioned with acetone, infiltrated with Spurr’s epoxy resin, and embedded. Polymerization was performed at 37 °C for 12 h, 45 °C for 12 h, and 60 °C for 24 h. Ultra-thin sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate at 25 °C for 30 min and observed using a JEM-1230 TEM (JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokyo, Japan). Overall, for each target indicator (chloroplast integrity, thylakoid arrangement, and grana structure), 15 fields of view were captured, and the parameters were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Enzyme activity assays

For enzyme activity assays, 0.5 g of fresh in vitro cultured leaf explants was homogenized in liquid nitrogen and extracted with 5 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000gand 4 °C for 20 min, and the supernatant was used for enzyme activity determination.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured according to [25] with modifications. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 130 mM methionine, 750 µM nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), 100 µM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 20 µM riboflavin, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. The mixture was illuminated under two 15-W fluorescent lamps for 15 min, and the reaction was stopped by dark incubation for 20 min. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the enzyme amount inhibiting 50% of NBT reduction. Specific activity was expressed as units per gram fresh weight (FW).

Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined following [26] with modifications. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), 10 mM H2O2, 5 mM guaiacol, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm was recorded at 30s intervals for 2 min using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UV-2550; Shimadzu, Japan). POD activity was expressed as units per gram FW.

Catalase (CAT) activity was assayed by monitoring H₂O₂ decomposition at 240 nm according to [27]. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 10 mM H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. CAT activity was expressed as units per gram FW, where one unit was defined as the amount of enzyme decomposing 1 µmol H₂O₂ per minute.

Following the method outlined by [28], the activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) was determined. The reaction mixture (total volume 1.0 mL) contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM H₂O₂, 0.5 mM ascorbate, and 10 µL protein extract. The reaction was initiated by adding H₂O₂, and the decrease in absorbance due to ascorbate oxidation was recorded at 290 nm for 1 min. Using the molar extinction coefficient of ascorbate (2.8 mM⁻¹·cm⁻¹), enzyme activity was calculated as micromoles (µmol) of ascorbate oxidized per minute per milligram of protein (U/mg protein).

Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity was measured following [29]. Enzyme extraction was performed using pre-chilled potassium sodium phosphate (KNaHPO₄) buffer (pH 7.0) and a homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was used as crude enzyme extract. For the assay, 0.2 mL supernatant was mixed with 0.4 mL 0.1 mM reduced glutathione (GSH) and 0.2 mL 0.067 M KNaHPO₄. A reaction mixture without enzyme served as the control. All mixtures were pre-incubated at 25 °C for 5 min, then 0.2 mL 1.3 mM H₂O₂ was added to initiate the reaction. After 10 min, the reaction was terminated with 1 mL 1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Following centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, 0.48 mL supernatant was mixed with 2.2 mL 0.32 M sodium phosphate and 0.32 mL 1.0 mM 5,5’-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) for color development. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a Shimadzu UV-120-02 spectrophotometer. GSH-Px activity was determined by comparing the decrease in GSH levels with the non-enzyme control.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content determination

The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured following [30]. 0.1 g of tea leaves were homogenized in 0.9 mL of 10% TCA and centrifuged at 3500-4000gfor 10 min. Then, 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of 0.6% thiobarbituric acid solution and incubated in a 95 °C water-bath for 20 min. After cooling, the absorbance at 450, 532, and 600 nm was recorded by a spectrophotometer. The MDA concentration was calculated using the formula:C(µM) = 6.45 (A532 - A600) − 0.56 A450 [2].

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from leaf samples collected simultaneously with in vitro culture using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The quality and integrity of RNA were detected using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, CA, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Illumina, Inc. (San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw sequencing reads were filtered to improve data quality, which included removing low-quality reads, adapter sequences, and ambiguous bases. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome of C. sinensis (CSS_ChrLev_20200506_Genome.fas.gz; http://tpia.teaplants.cn/) [31] using Hisat2 (v2.1.0). The gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) using StringTie (v2.1.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.30.1) with the criteria of |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and false discovery rate < 0.05. The threshold |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 corresponds to a minimum 2-fold expression difference between the two cultivars, filtering out genes with mild transcriptional fluctuation caused by subtle physiological background noise. FDR < 0.05 was adopted to correct multiple testing bias in genome-wide transcriptome analysis and eliminate false positive DEGs generated by random sequencing variation; this dual standard is the universally accepted screening criterion for plant leaf RNA-seq datasets, which balances the sensitivity and reliability of differential gene identification in tea germplasm comparison research [32].

The functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. The GO enrich ment analysis was carried out using the topGO package in R, and the KEGG pathway analysis was performed using KEGG Orthology Based Annotation System (KOBAS) 3.0. The significantly enriched terms and pathways were determined with a P < 0.05.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation of key DEGs

Total RNA extracted from samples collected simultaneously with in vitro culture was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Eight key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected for qRT-PCR validation, including Camellia sinensis Superoxide Dismutase 2, Camellia sinensis Peroxidase 12, Camellia sinensis Catalase 1, Camellia sinensis Ascorbate Peroxidase 2, Camellia sinensis Chalcone Synthase, Camellia sinensis Cinnamate 4-Hydroxylase, Camellia sinensis Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 2 and Camellia sinensis Cyclin D3(CsSOD2, CsPOD12, CsCAT1, CsAPX2, CsCHS, CsC4H, CsCDK2, and CsCYCD3). qRT‑PCR was performed on a LightCycler® 480 II Real‑Time PCR System (Roche, Switzerland) using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, Japan). The reaction system (20 µL) contained 10 µL of 2×SYBR mix, 0.8 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL of diluted cDNA, and 6.4 µL of ddH₂O. The amplification program was set as follows: 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30s; and a melting curve analysis from 60 °C to 95 °C. Camellia sinensis Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (CsGAPDH) was used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCTmethod [33]. Three independent biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed for each gene. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All phenotypic physiological and tissue culture data were recorded as mean ± standard error (SE) of three independent biological replicates. qRT-PCR relative gene expression data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) including three biological replicates and three technical replicates. All significance P values are formatted with italicized symbols throughout the manuscript, tables and figure annotations. The contamination rate was calculated as (number of contaminated explants/number of inoculated explants) × 100. Proliferation fold was defined as the ratio of callus proliferation weight to initial inoculated explant weight. Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2013 and SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was used for multiple comparisons among treatments, with significance defined as P ≤ 0.05.

For qRT‑PCR analysis, relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method [33]. Student’s t‑test was used to evaluate significant differences in gene expression between Huangjincha and Yunnandaye, with significance levels set at P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the consistency between RNA‑seq log₂(fold change) and qRT‑PCR log₂(fold change) values, and the coefficient of determination (R²) was determined to validate the reliability of transcriptome data.

Results

Optimization of callus induction conditions

Effects of liquid preculture and dark culture duration on callus induction efficiency

As shown in Table 1; Fig. 2, conventional culture of fragmented Huangjincha explants resulted in 75.4 ± 2.3% browning rate and 24.6 ± 1.8% callus induction rate, indicating poor proliferation capacity. In contrast, the optimized three-stage culture process (3-day liquid dark preculture, 12-day solid dark culture, and 15-day light culture) significantly reduced browning rate to 32.0 ± 0.9% (P < 0.01) while increasing callus induction rate to 68.0 ± 2.2% (P < 0.01) compared to conventional methods.

Effects of different media on leaf callus proliferation

As shown in Table 2; Fig. 3, MS medium significantly reduced browning rate (P < 0.05) and improved callus quality compared to B5 medium for Huangjincha explants. Healthy, light-yellow friable callus was efficiently induced on MS medium, with central regions showing slight greening. In contrast, B5 medium resulted in slower callus growth with granular red pigmentation and limited white callus formation. The optimal growth regulator combination was determined as 4.0 mg/L 6-BA and 1.0 mg/L IBA. Pre-culturing leaves in liquid medium for 3 days prior to solid culture was identified as a key factor enhancing regeneration efficiency.

Discussion

Improvement in the efficiency of tissue culture of tea leaf explants by combining liquid-solid co-culture with dark culture

This study established a novel protocol for tea leaf tissue culture using intact leaves with transverse incisions combined with liquid-solid co-culture, achieving a 32.0 ± 0.9% browning rate and 68.0 ± 2.2% callus induction rate in Huangjincha-markedly outperforming conventional fragmented explant culture (75.4 ± 2.3% browning, 24.6 ± 1.8% induction). The optimized three-stage culture regime (3-day liquid dark culture→12-day solid dark culture→15-day light/dark cycle) leverages combined advantages of liquid preculture and dark cultivation to address core bottlenecks in tea tissue culture. Liquid preculture mitigates browning through three key mechanisms: diluting intracellular phenolic compounds to reduce oxidative toxicity, enabling uniform contact between explant surfaces and nutrients/hormones for enhanced absorption, and maintaining dynamic nutrient balance via medium fluidity-consistent with transcriptomic data showing suppressed expression of Camellia sinensis Polyphenol Oxidase 2 (CsPPO2), Camellia sinensis Peroxidase 3 (CsPOD3) in liquid-cultured explants [2]. Dark culture during the dedifferentiation phase further enhances efficiency by inhibiting premature cell differentiation, as light exposure triggers photosynthetic self-repair processes that compete for energy and metabolites required for callus formation [36, 37]. This aligns with upregulation of cell cycle regulators and meristem-related genes CsCYCD3 under dark conditions [21], reinforcing the molecular rationale for this culture strategy.

In contrast, Yunnandaye exhibited persistent high browning (78.3 ± 2.5%) and low callus induction (21.7 ± 1.4%) even with the optimized protocol, attributed to intrinsic genotypic traits. Its elevated polyphenol content (particularly EGCG) leads to rapid oxidation at incision sites, forming cytotoxic quinones that induce cell necrosis and hinder dedifferentiation [13, 14]. Future research should explore the correlation between leaf phenolic profiles and regeneration potential to refine protocols for recalcitrant varieties. Beyond polyphenol-rich fruit trees such as grape and olive, this intact leaf with controlled incision plus liquid-solid staged co-culture framework exhibits broad transferable potential for diverse recalcitrant woody perennial species including citrus, litchi, walnut and poplar. Woody plants uniformly suffer from severe explant browning due to abundant phenylpropanoid secondary metabolites, thick epidermal wax layers and fragile chloroplasts under in vitro stress-physiological barriers highly consistent with tea tissue culture obstacles [38]. Minimizing wound area via intact leaf materials reduces massive phenolic leakage, while short-term liquid dark preculture rapidly dilutes cytotoxic oxidized phenolics and improves nutrient penetration through leaf stomata and shallow incisions. The staged dark-light shift further synchronizes cell dedifferentiation and avoids premature photosynthetic energy competition, which can be matched and adjusted according to species-specific wax thickness, phenolic content and photosynthetic characteristics [39]. This multi-factor coordinated culture system provides a universal modular optimization idea for establishing high-efficiency callus induction systems in hard-to-regenerate woody cash crops and forest trees, not limited to Camellia sinensis alone.

Epidermal and subcellular structures: morphological foundations for tissue culture success

Leaf epidermal and chloroplast ultrastructures emerged as key morphological determinants of tissue culture performance, with Huangjincha displaying traits that confer stress resistance and metabolic stability. SEM analysis revealed that Huangjincha’s sparse yet continuous epidermal wax layer and closed stomatal morphology form a physical barrier that minimizes polyphenol leakage and water loss-reducing oxidative browning by limiting contact between intracellular phenolics and extracellular oxidases [40]. At the subcellular level, TEM observations demonstrated intact chloroplasts with well-organized thylakoids and grana in Huangjincha, Huangjincha’s intact chloroplasts support more efficient photosynthesis, providing sufficient energy (ATP) and photosynthates for callus dedifferentiation, while Yunnandaye’s chloroplast fragmentation under in vitro conditions reduces photosynthetic efficiency, limiting the energy supply for callus formation [23]. In contrast, Yunnandaye’s dense, aggregated wax layers, open stomata, and chloroplast fragmentation (with osmiophilic granules) under in vitro culture exacerbate polyphenol oxidation and metabolic inefficiency, directly contributing to its poor tissue culture performance.

Consistent with woody plant tissue culture models [22], Huangjincha’s wax density and intact chloroplast structure (observed in TEM) showed a strong positive correlation with callus induction rate (r = 0.89, P < 0.01), while Yunnandaye’s dense wax and chloroplast fragmentation under in vitro conditions were negatively correlated with induction efficiency (r=-0.85, P < 0.01). This further confirms that genotypic differences in structural and physiological traits are key determinants of callus induction success.

Antioxidant physiology and ros scavenging: resilience to oxidative stress

Tissue culture-induced wounding triggers oxidative stress, and Huangjincha’s superior antioxidant system enables effective redox homeostasis maintenance. Enzyme activity assays demonstrated that Huangjincha’s higher SOD (P < 0.01) and POD (P < 0.001) activities efficiently scavenge superoxide anions and H₂O₂, while higher MDA levels indicate greater membrane lipid peroxidation [23]. This “ROS-scavenging cascade” is complemented by its epidermal barrier, which limits initial ROS production, creating a synergistic “morphology-physiology” defense against oxidative damage. In contrast, Yunnandaye’s lower SOD/POD activities, lower MDA content, and elevated APX/CAT/GSH-Px levels reflect a compromised redox system-where alternative antioxidant pathways are upregulated but fail to mitigate excessive ROS, leading to irreversible cell damage and browning.

Notably, the present study did not directly quantify representative ROS species including H₂O₂ and O₂⁻. Although malondialdehyde (MDA) was used as an indicator of lipid peroxidation, MDA alone cannot fully represent the overall level of oxidative damage, and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) were not determined in this experiment. Despite these limitations, the consistent and coordinated variation patterns among SOD, POD, APX, CAT, and MDA collectively support the conclusion that the two tea cultivars exhibit significantly different levels of oxidative stress under tissue culture conditions.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further confirmed that Huangjincha’s peroxisome pathway (ko04146) is significantly activated, with CsSOD2 (log₂FC = 3.1) and CsPOD12 (log₂FC = 2.8) acting as key regulatory hubs-consistent with findings in stress-tolerant Camellia genotypes [21]. These results emphasize that antioxidant capacity is a critical physiological determinant of tissue culture success, and manipulating ROS scavenging pathways (e.g., exogenous antioxidant application) could improve the performance of recalcitrant varieties like Yunnandaye.

Implications for tea biotechnology: from variety selection to protocol optimization

The multidimensional insights from this study provide actionable strategies to advance tea tissue culture and biotechnology. First, variety selection for tissue culture should prioritize genotypes with robust epidermal barriers (continuous wax, closed stomata) and intact chloroplast ultrastructure, as these traits directly reduce browning and support metabolic resilience. Second, pretreatment strategies for recalcitrant varieties (e.g., Yunnandaye) could include exogenous antioxidants (e.g., ascorbic acid) to enhance ROS scavenging or wax-enhancing compounds to reinforce epidermal protection, mimicking Huangjincha’s protective traits. Third, culture conditions should be tailored to genotypic metabolic needs: for high-performing varieties like Huangjincha, optimizing light intensity to support chloroplast function; for stress-sensitive genotypes, reducing initial light exposure to minimize ROS accumulation.

Notably, our protocol outperforms existing methods [4] by 43% in callus induction rate, offering a scalable tool for CRISPR-mediated trait improvement (e.g., disease resistance) in tea-analogous to successful applications in grape (Vitis vinifera) [11]. Linking fundamental biology with applied biotechnology, this study provides a basis for germplasm conservation and sustainable tea cultivation, and may be informative for related research on polyphenol-rich crops with analogous tissue culture issues.

Transcriptional reprogramming: metabolic and proliferative pathways underpinning callus induction divergence

Transcriptomic analyses revealed that Huangjincha’s superior tissue culture performance stems from coordinated transcriptional reprogramming favoring callus induction, distinct from Yunnandaye’s suboptimal gene expression profile. Of the 27,520 identified DEGs, Huangjincha upregulated key genes involved in cell cycle progression (CsCDK2, CsCYCD3), hormone signaling (auxin/cytokinin receptors), and ROS scavenging (CsSOD2, CsPOD12)-molecular hallmarks of dedifferentiation and stress tolerance [16, 41–46]. These transcriptional changes directly support the cellular processes required for callus formation, explaining its 68.0 ± 2.2% induction rate. In contrast, Yunnandaye enriched genes in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways (CsCHS, CsC4H) (Fig. 10), driving excessive polyphenol production-a known browning trigger and diverting resources from cell proliferation, consistent with its 21.7 ± 1.4% induction rate [47–52].

GO enrichment analysis further confirmed that Huangjincha’s DEGs are clustered in key terms: ‘oxidation-reduction process’ (GO:0055114, adjusted P < 0.001, 128 DEGs), ‘cellular component organization’ (GO:0016043), and ‘response to stress’ (GO:0006950), while Yunnandaye prioritizes stress-response pathways-reflecting divergent adaptive strategies to tissue culture conditions. These transcriptional patterns, including cultivar-specific expression of CsSOD2/CsPOD12 (Huangjincha) and CsCHS/CsC4H (Yunnandaye), provide a molecular framework for understanding genotypic differences in tissue culture competence, identifying target pathways for biotechnological improvement of recalcitrant varieties.

To further validate the reliability and accuracy of the transcriptome sequencing data, eight key DEGs involved in antioxidant defense, polyphenol biosynthesis, and cell cycle regulation (CsSOD2, CsPOD12, CsCAT1, CsAPX2, CsCHS, CsC4H, CsCDK2, and CsCYCD3) were selected for qRT-PCR validation. The results showed that the expression trends of all selected genes determined by qRT-PCR were highly consistent with those obtained from RNA-seq (R2 = 0.92). Specifically, CsSOD2, CsPOD12, CsCDK2, and CsCYCD3 were significantly upregulated in Huangjincha, while CsCAT1, CsAPX2, CsCHS, and CsC4H were downregulated, which was identical to the FPKM-based expression patterns. This qRT-PCR validation confirms the high reliability and reproducibility of the transcriptome data, further supporting the accuracy of the subsequent functional enrichment analysis and key gene regulatory network interpretation in this study.

Antioxidant and stress-responsive transcriptomes: molecular basis for oxidative stress tolerance

Huangjincha’s enhanced stress tolerance is rooted in transcriptional coordination of antioxidant and stress-response pathways. Upregulation of SOD and POD-encoding genes in Huangjincha correlates with its elevated enzyme activities (Fig. 6), yet its higher MDA content indicates persistent membrane lipid peroxidation, creating a synergy coordination that reinforces structural protections (e.g., intact chloroplasts) and mitigates ROS damage [53]. In contrast,Yunnandaye’s diminished expression of core antioxidant genes and heightened stress-responsive signatures (e.g., cysteine protease CsCP1) are accompanied by lower MDA levels, contributing to its high browning rate.

These findings demonstrate that Huangjincha’s transcriptome encodes a balanced program prioritizing cell proliferation and hormone signaling while maintaining effective stress mitigation. In addition, this study focused exclusively on enzymatic antioxidants and their corresponding regulatory genes. Non-enzymatic antioxidants, including ascorbic acid, tocopherol, total phenolics, flavonoids, and other phenolic compounds, which also play critical roles in cellular redox homeostasis, were not measured in the present work. This constitutes a limitation of the current evaluation of the complete antioxidant defense system.For recalcitrant varieties like Yunnandaye, transcriptomic data suggest potential targets-such as suppressing polyphenol biosynthesis genes or overexpressing antioxidant enzymes-to improve tissue culture performance. This molecular insights-driven approach enables rational protocol design, advancing plant biotechnology in polyphenol-rich species by moving beyond empirical optimization to mechanism-based intervention.

Conclusions

This study constructed an optimized three-stage liquid-solid co-culture system using intact tea leaves with standardized transverse incisions to fulfil its three core research objectives: phenotypic comparison of two tea cultivars under the improved culture regime, dissection of genotypic differential regulatory mechanisms via multi-dimensional analysis, and establishment of a multi-layered regulatory framework for enhancing callus induction in recalcitrant tea materials; the novel protocol greatly suppressed browning and raised callus induction efficiency in Huangjincha (32.0 ± 0.9% browning rate, 68.0 ± 2.2% induction rate), while Yunnandaye suffered severe browning and poor regenerative ability (78.3 ± 2.5% browning rate, 21.7 ± 1.4% induction rate), demonstrating prominent genotype-dependent tissue culture competence, and integrated ultrastructural, physiological and transcriptomic analyses revealed the layered regulatory basis for such divergence, where Huangjincha owned beneficial leaf epidermal and chloroplast morphology, efficient SOD/POD-based ROS scavenging capacity, and upregulated carbon metabolism as well as cell cycle genes (CsSOD2, CsPOD12, CsCDK2, CsCYCD3) that collectively support cell dedifferentiation, whereas Yunnandaye had dense epidermal wax, damaged subcellular organelles and highly expressed phenylpropanoid biosynthesis genes (CsCHS, CsC4H) triggering massive polyphenol oxidation and cell necrosis, with qRT-PCR results verifying the reliability of transcriptome data, and the integrated “structure-physiology-molecular” regulatory framework built herein identifies key target genes and culture strategies for improving regeneration of hard-to-culture tea varieties, supplying a scalable technical platform for tea germplasm conservation and CRISPR-based genetic improvement alongside universal theoretical and technical references for tissue culture of other polyphenol-rich woody crops; two main limitations of this work include the absence of quantitative detection of core reactive oxygen species and systematic measurement of non-enzymatic antioxidants, and follow-up research will supplement these indicators to comprehensively clarify redox regulatory pathways during tea callus induction.
