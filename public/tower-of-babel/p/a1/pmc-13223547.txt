Patient-Reported Outcomes in FLAURA2: Osimertinib with or without Chemotherapy in Patients with Previously Untreated EGFR-Mutated Advanced Non–Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Jhanelle E. Gray, Konstantin Laktionov, Sang-We Kim, Terufumi Kato, Jialei Wang, Zhigang Han, Paul Mitchell, Shoichi Kuyama, Jerry Tan Chun Bing, Juan Cundom, Gustavo Pinto, Frances A. Shepherd, Lynne Poole, Rachel Lai, Muna Albayaty, Neha P. Amin, Kunihiko Kobayashi, Chee Khoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:In FLAURA2, first-line osimertinib plus platinum–pemetrexed induction, with osimertinib plus pemetrexed maintenance, improved progression-free survival versus osimertinib alone in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated, advanced non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC; hazard ratio, 0.62; P < 0.001). Combining osimertinib with chemotherapy increased induction grade ≥3 adverse event rates, which reduced during maintenance. We report FLAURA2 patient-reported outcomes (PRO).Patients and Methods:Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was measured using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (QLQ) Core 30 (baseline, week 4, week 7, week 10, then every 6 weeks until progression) and QLQ Lung Cancer 13 (baseline, weekly until week 10, then every 3 weeks until progression). Score changes (baseline to progression/19 months) were analyzed by mixed models for repeated measures. Within-patient ≥10-point changes from baseline were considered clinically meaningful. Tolerability was assessed by PRO-Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE).Results:Patients had intermediate-to-high baseline functioning and global health status (GHS)/QoL (mean scores ≥63), with mild symptomatology (≤35). Most key scales showed nonclinically meaningful improvements; average least-squares mean (LSM) changes [95% confidence interval (CI)] for GHS/QoL and physical function, respectively, were 3.32 (1.67–4.98) and 2.37 (0.70–4.04) with combination therapy and 7.38 (5.70–9.07) and 6.74 (5.04–8.43) with monotherapy. Improvements in cough were clinically meaningful with combination therapy and monotherapy from week 5 (except monotherapy at week 73); average LSM changes (95% CI) were −13.23 (−14.85 to −11.62) and −11.19 (−12.83 to −9.55), respectively. Nonclinically meaningful deteriorations in fatigue and appetite loss were seen with the combination during induction. Both treatments were similarly well tolerated (PRO-CTCAE).Conclusions:In FLAURA2, osimertinib monotherapy and combination with platinum–pemetrexed as first-line treatment for EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC had nonclinically meaningful impacts on HRQoL in mildly symptomatic patients.

Translational RelevanceIn this preplanned analysis of key patient-reported outcomes data from the FLAURA2 study, we demonstrated that adding platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy to osimertinib had a nonclinically meaningful impact on health-related quality of life compared with osimertinib monotherapy as first-line treatment for patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated advanced non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). As anticipated, transient worsening of some symptoms was observed during the chemotherapy induction phase in the combination arm; however, these effects largely resolved during the maintenance phase. These findings highlight the importance of providing supportive care during the induction period when treatment-related burden is greatest. Overall, the clinically meaningful benefit of the osimertinib–chemotherapy combination does not seem to compromise patients’ symptoms and functioning. These data support the use of osimertinib in combination with platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy as a viable and beneficial first-line treatment approach for patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC.

Introduction

Third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKI) are the standard first-line treatment for patients with treatment-naïve metastatic EGFR-mutated advanced non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), offering superior efficacy compared with earlier-generation agents (1–9). Despite these advances, acquired resistance occurs, driving the development of combination strategies aimed at enhancing and prolonging therapeutic benefit. Recent regimens, including osimertinib plus chemotherapy (FLAURA2) and amivantamab plus lazertinib (MARIPOSA), have shown improved progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) for patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC (6–9). However, these efficacy gains come with added toxicity burdens. NSCLC is associated with a high symptom burden, with the majority of patients (≥90%) reporting symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, pain, fatigue, and appetite loss, which have been shown to have a significant adverse impact on disease-specific health-related quality of life (HRQoL; refs. 10, 11). Given the high symptomatic burden of NSCLC on patients, it is important to gain a good understanding of how new treatments and interventions affect patient HRQoL by improving existing disease-related symptoms while potentially causing drug-related symptoms (12).

The randomized, open-label FLAURA2 study (NCT04035486) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in investigator-assessed PFS with osimertinib plus platinum–pemetrexed induction followed by pemetrexed maintenance (osimertinib plus chemotherapy) compared with osimertinib monotherapy [median PFS 25.5 vs. 16.7 months, respectively; hazard ratio (HR), 0.62; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.49– 0.79; P < 0.001] in patients with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC (6). In the final analysis, a statistically significant improvement in OS was observed with osimertinib plus chemotherapy compared with osimertinib monotherapy [median OS of 47.5 vs. 37.6 months, respectively; HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.61–0.96; P = 0.02; data maturity 57%; ref. 7]. The safety profile of osimertinib plus chemotherapy in FLAURA2 was consistent with the established individual treatment profiles. Grade ≥3 adverse events (AE) of any cause, at the time of the primary analysis, were more frequent with osimertinib plus chemotherapy (64%) compared with osimertinib monotherapy (27%; ref. 6). The majority of these events in the combination arm were hematologic toxicities (primarily neutropenia and anemia), as expected from chemotherapy-related side effects (6, 13). This increase in grade ≥3 AE was most pronounced during the induction phase when patients received platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy alongside osimertinib and declined during the maintenance phase, in which treatment continued with osimertinib and maintenance pemetrexed until disease progression.

Treatment with osimertinib monotherapy has been shown to improve the key NSCLC symptoms of cough, dyspnea, chest pain, appetite loss, and fatigue compared with baseline in patients with EGFR-mutated advanced disease (14). As there is evidence that the HRQoL of patients with advanced NSCLC is not adversely affected by chemotherapy despite an increase in toxicity (15), we hypothesized that the addition of platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy to osimertinib in FLAURA2 would be unlikely to have a clinically significant negative impact on patients’ overall symptom burden and HRQoL compared with osimertinib monotherapy. A small adverse impact on HRQoL might be anticipated during induction chemotherapy due to the observed increase in grade ≥3 AE (6, 13); however, this effect is expected to be transitory and may be an acceptable compromise to achieve improved clinical outcomes (higher response rates and longer PFS) with the osimertinib plus chemotherapy combination regimen. Here, we report secondary and exploratory analyses of patient-reported outcomes (PRO) from the FLAURA2 study, evaluating the impact of adding platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy to osimertinib, compared with osimertinib monotherapy, on the experience of patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC. Specifically, we aimed to assess whether adding platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy to osimertinib leads to clinically meaningful changes from baseline in patient-reported symptoms, functioning, and HRQoL. We also explored whether NSCLC-specific symptoms deteriorate at a different rate when treated with osimertinib plus chemotherapy compared with osimertinib monotherapy.

Patients and Methods

Study design

This analysis reports the results of prespecified analyses of PRO from the international, phase III, open-label, randomized FLAURA2 study. The methodology for FLAURA2 has been reported previously (6). In brief, patients aged ≥18 years (≥20 years in Japan) with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC who had not previously received systemic treatment for advanced disease were enrolled. Patients had pathologically confirmed nonsquamous NSCLC, with locally or centrally confirmed EGFR exon 19 deletion or L858R, either alone or in combination with other EGFR mutations. Patients had a World Health Organization (WHO) performance status of 0 or 1. Patients with asymptomatic or stable central nervous system metastases were eligible.

Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive osimertinib plus platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy or osimertinib monotherapy. In the combination arm, patients received oral osimertinib 80 mg once daily plus intravenous pemetrexed 500 mg/m2 with either cisplatin 75 mg/m2 or carboplatin AUC5 administered intravenously on day 1 of 21-day cycles for four cycles. This was followed by osimertinib 80 mg once daily plus maintenance with pemetrexed 500 mg/m2 every 3 weeks. In the monotherapy arm, patients received oral osimertinib 80 mg once daily. Randomization was stratified by race (Asian Chinese vs. Asian non-Chinese vs. non-Asian), WHO performance status (0 vs. 1), and EGFR mutation tissue testing method (central vs. local). Treatment continued until investigator-assessed Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1–defined progression, unacceptable toxicity, or another discontinuation criterion. The cutoff date for the present analysis was April 3, 2023.

The FLAURA2 trial was conducted in accordance with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation, and applicable regulatory requirements. The trial protocol was approved by relevant Institutional Review Boards or ethics committees. All patients provided written informed consent.

PRO assessments

PRO were self-reported by patients using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Questionnaire-Core 30 [QLQ-C30; a 30-item questionnaire that measures general cancer symptoms, functioning, and global health status (GHS)/QoL; refs. 16, 17], the EORTC QLQ-Lung Cancer 13 (QLQ-LC13; a 13-item lung-cancer-specific questionnaire that measures lung cancer–related symptoms and treatment-related side effects; refs. 18, 19), and the PRO version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (PRO-CTCAE; an item library developed by the National Cancer Institute to capture symptomatic AE in patients on cancer clinical trials, specifically relating to their frequency, severity, and interference with usual daily activities; ref. 20). All three tools are frequently used in clinical trials and provide complementary and confirmatory information about symptoms and HRQoL in advanced NSCLC.

To ensure high compliance, each patient in the FLAURA2 study was provided with a handheld electronic device to facilitate the completion of the three PRO questionnaires. At baseline, the electronic PRO were completed while the patient was still in the clinic to ensure their handheld device was correctly set up and working properly. Subsequently, patients completed the questionnaires at home using the same device, following the predefined schedule outlined in Supplementary Fig. S1. In brief, QLQ-C30 was completed at baseline (before dosing); on weeks 4, 7, and 10; and then every 6 weeks until the first disease progression. QLQ-LC13 and PRO-CTCAE were completed at baseline, every week until week 10, and then every 3 weeks until the first disease progression. Following the first progression, PRO data for all three questionnaires continued to be collected every 8 weeks until a second PFS event (PFS2) or data cutoff (∼19 months after randomization), whichever was earlier. The PRO-CTCAE was only administered in countries where a linguistically validated version existed: Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, Chile, China, Czech Republic, France, India, Japan, Peru, Philippines, Russia, South Africa, South Korea, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and the United States. It was not implemented in Slovakia, Thailand, or Vietnam.

Prespecified key scales from the QLQ-C30 were GHS/QoL, physical functioning, fatigue, and appetite loss. For QLQ-LC13, prespecified key scales were dyspnea, cough, and chest pain. Scores for the QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 range from 0 to 100. For GHS/QoL and the functioning scales, higher scores represent a higher GHS/QoL or level of functioning, whereas higher symptom scores represent an increased level of symptom burden. Mouth or throat sores, nausea, vomiting, loose or watery stools, pain in the abdomen, loss of control of bowel movements, dry skin, hair loss, and numbness or tingling in the hands or feet were prespecified as treatment-related symptoms of interest from the PRO-CTCAE.

Statistical analysis

PRO analyses were based on the full analysis set, which included all randomized patients. Changes from baseline and time to deterioration in QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 scores were secondary outcomes of FLAURA2. Assessment of treatment-related symptoms measured using the PRO-CTCAE was an exploratory outcome.

For the QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 questionnaires, a within-patient change in scores of ≥10 points from baseline was considered clinically meaningful based on previously published data (21). Data are expressed as proportions of patients with clinically meaningful changes from baseline in QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 scores in both arms at time points common to both assessments: week 10 (representing the closest available time point to the end of induction in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm), week 34 (the closest time point to the median duration of pemetrexed maintenance therapy in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm), and week 82 (the closest time point to the median duration of osimertinib treatment in the osimertinib monotherapy arm).

Time to symptom deterioration (TTD), based on QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 scores, was defined as the time from randomization until the date of the first clinically meaningful symptom deterioration (within-patient change in scores of ≥10 points from baseline), which was confirmed at a subsequent assessment, or death from any cause. It was evaluated until PFS2 or data cutoff, whichever was earlier, using Kaplan–Meier methodology and a log-rank test stratified by race (Asian Chinese vs. Asian non-Chinese vs. non-Asian), WHO performance status (0 vs. 1), and the method used for tissue EGFR testing (central vs. local). Patients with baseline scores of <10 for GHS/QoL and functioning or baseline scores of >90 for symptom scales were censored at day 1. Patients with a single worsening and no further assessments were treated as deteriorated. Death was only included as an event if it occurred within two assessments of the last PRO assessment in which the change could be evaluated.

To account for repeat measurements across the longitudinal course of the entire trial, least-squares mean (LSM) changes in prespecified key EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 scale scores from baseline to progression or ∼19 months, whichever occurred first, were also analyzed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM). The MMRM included patient, treatment, visit assessment, and treatment-by-visit interaction as explanatory variables, and baseline PRO score by assessment interaction as covariates. Treatment, visit, and treatment-by-visit interaction were fixed effects in the model, and patient was included as a random effect. Using restricted maximum likelihood estimation, overall adjusted mean estimates and corresponding 95% CI for the average changes over all assessments (excluding assessments with >75% missing data) were determined for each score, with each assessment given equal weight. Missing data were not imputed.

Patient-reported tolerability was assessed using nine prespecified treatment-related items from the PRO-CTCAE item library (mouth or throat sores, nausea, vomiting, loose or watery stools, pain in the abdomen, loss of control of bowel movements, dry skin, hair loss, and numbness or tingling in the hands or feet). Data on the frequency and severity of prespecified symptoms of interest from the PRO-CTCAE were presented using descriptive statistics. Data were reported for weeks 13, 37, and 97, which represent the closest available time points to the end of induction, the median duration of pemetrexed maintenance therapy, and the median duration of osimertinib treatment, respectively, for the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm.

Results

Patients and treatment exposure

As reported in the primary analysis (6), 557 patients were randomized to study treatment: 279 to osimertinib plus chemotherapy and 278 to osimertinib monotherapy (full analysis set); 276 and 275 patients received treatment. Demographics and baseline characteristics, as described previously, were representative of the target patient population (Supplementary Table S1) and were well balanced between the two treatment arms (6). Treatment exposure is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Compliance

Overall compliance rates for completion of the EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 questionnaires were high in both treatment arms at baseline (>90%) and remained high, with compliance of ≥80% at week 82 (Supplementary Fig. S2). For completion of the PRO-CTCAE, overall compliance rates were >85% at baseline and remained high, with >78% compliance at week 82 (Supplementary Fig. S2).

PRO-CTCAE results showed that both treatment arms were similarly well tolerated in terms of frequency for the majority of prespecified treatment-related symptoms of interest (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S3). However, PRO-CTCAE data for two chemotherapy-related symptoms showed that symptoms of nausea and vomiting were more common in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm than in the osimertinib monotherapy arm at week 13 (end of induction, Fig. 6). Nausea and vomiting were reported as occurring either “occasionally,” “frequently,” or “almost constantly” in 43 of 204 (21%) and 10 of 204 (5%) patients, respectively, in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm and 14 of 203 (7%) and 5 of 203 (2%) patients, respectively, in the osimertinib monotherapy arm. By week 37 (during maintenance), nausea and vomiting were reported as “never” or “rarely” occurring in 160 of 171 (94%) and 171 of 171 (100%) patients, respectively, who received osimertinib plus chemotherapy and 158 of 168 (94%) and 164 of 168 (98%) patients, respectively, treated with osimertinib monotherapy. This was maintained to week 97, in which nausea and vomiting were “never” or “rarely” reported by 83 of 92 (90%) and 91 of 92 (99%) patients, respectively, in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm compared with 58 of 59 (98%) and 58 of 59 (98%) patients, respectively, in the osimertinib monotherapy arm.

Where reported, the majority of prespecified symptoms were mild or moderate in both treatment arms (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S3). In terms of symptom severity for three chemotherapy-related symptoms, “severe” or “very severe” symptoms of nausea, vomiting, and dry skin were reported in nine of 88 (10%), two of 30 (7%), and nine of 204 (4%) patients, respectively, at week 13; one of 44 (2.3%), zero of eight (0%), and one of 171 (1%), respectively, at week 37; and zero of 22 (0%), zero of 12 (0%), and one of 92 (1%), respectively, at week 97 in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm (Fig. 6). Reports of “severe” or “very severe” symptoms of nausea, vomiting, or dry skin were uncommon at week 13 [0/40 (0%), 0/14 (0%), and 5/203 (2%), respectively], week 37 [1/36 (3%), 0/16 (0%), and 7/168 (4%), respectively], and week 97 [0/9 (0%), 1/6 (17%), and 3/59 (5%), respectively] in the osimertinib monotherapy arm.

Discussion

In this preplanned analysis of key PRO data from the FLAURA2 study, we show that both osimertinib combined with platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy and osimertinib monotherapy as first-line treatment for patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC resulted in nonclinically meaningful changes (within-patient change in scores of ≥10 points from baseline) in HRQoL, functioning, and symptoms reported by patients. In the combination arm, some symptoms worsened during chemotherapy induction, but these mostly settled during maintenance.

The primary FLAURA2 analysis established osimertinib plus platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy as a first-line option for the treatment of EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC (23), based on the demonstration of a statistically significant PFS benefit compared with osimertinib monotherapy (6). Although the safety profile of osimertinib plus chemotherapy was consistent with the known safety profiles of the component drugs (6), an increase in grade ≥3 AE was seen during platinum-containing induction treatment, which subsequently reduced during maintenance therapy with osimertinib and pemetrexed (13). Given that treatment toxicities may negatively affect symptoms, functioning, and HRQoL in patients with cancer, it is important to evaluate PRO during therapy.

In FLAURA2, baseline scores for key EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 scales indicated intermediate-to-high levels of overall functioning and GHS/QoL, with mild or no symptoms, indicating that trial participants had generally better performance status than typical patients with advanced NSCLC, which likely reflected the eligibility requirements for the trial (6). The relatively good level of functioning and GHS/QoL and low symptom burden of the study population at baseline also likely meant that clinically meaningful improvements in PRO would be difficult to demonstrate.

MMRM is a widely accepted analysis method for modeling PRO scores (14, 24, 25), which accounts for repeated measurements across the entire trial. MMRM analyses of LSM changes in prespecified key EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13 scale scores from baseline showed nonclinically meaningful improvements in GHS/QoL, physical functioning, and the lung cancer symptoms of chest pain and dyspnea for both the osimertinib plus chemotherapy and osimertinib monotherapy treatment arms. For cough, the improvement was generally clinically meaningful in both arms from week 5. Although the addition of chemotherapy to osimertinib resulted in nonclinically meaningful trends toward deterioration for QLQ-C30–assessed fatigue and appetite loss, mainly seen during the initial 12-week induction period, symptom levels tended to recover, at least partially, in most patients during maintenance treatment. PRO-CTCAE data indicated that from the patients’ perspective, osimertinib plus chemotherapy and osimertinib monotherapy were similarly well tolerated for most treatment-related symptoms for which data were available. Nausea and vomiting are well-established side effects of platinum chemotherapy, especially cisplatin, and as expected, these were more common during induction chemotherapy (26, 27). It is this increase in expected, well-characterized AE during platinum induction that may explain the faster TTD seen in the combination arm for most PRO scales, given the strong association between chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and HRQoL (28). Reassuringly, the impact of chemotherapy addition on GHS/QoL and tolerability did not affect continuance on treatment, with the majority (76%) of patients in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy arm completing at least four cycles of carboplatin or cisplatin and a median of 12 cycles of pemetrexed (6).

The Kaplan–Meier curves for TTD in the osimertinib plus chemotherapy and osimertinib monotherapy arms initially separated during induction, which coincided with the transient increase in nausea and vomiting. However, following this initial separation, the curves for most prespecified key scale scores (excluding chest pain, in which there was no initial separation) generally ran parallel to one another during long-term maintenance treatment. This suggests no additional detrimental impact on PRO beyond the induction phase of combining osimertinib and pemetrexed. Previous studies have shown that the HRQoL of patients with NSCLC is maintained during pemetrexed maintenance therapy (29, 30); our data suggest that this remains the case when pemetrexed maintenance is combined with osimertinib.

Consistently, other studies have shown that the HRQoL of patients with advanced NSCLC is not adversely affected and can, in some cases, be improved by either chemotherapy or osimertinib. For instance, a review of 32 randomized trials of conventional chemotherapy in patients with advanced NSCLC found that compared with best supportive care, there were consistent improvements in some components of HRQoL with chemotherapy, despite the toxicity of many of the regimens (31).

With respect to osimertinib, the FLAURA study showed that first-line monotherapy with either osimertinib or a first-generation EGFR-TKI (erlotinib or gefitinib) led to numerical improvements from baseline in five key symptoms (cough, dyspnea, chest pain, appetite loss, and fatigue; measured using the QLQ-C30 or QLQ-LC13) in both treatment arms among patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC (14). However, improvements in symptom scores that were both statistically significant and clinically meaningful (defined as a ≥10-point improvement from baseline at two consecutive assessments ≥21 days apart) were not seen in favor of either arm. Symptom improvement and time to deterioration were also similar between treatments. Although there were some differences in the patient population compared with the current study (lower incidences of extrathoracic and brain metastases in FLAURA compared with FLAURA2), the FLAURA trial supports at least a neutral or somewhat positive impact of osimertinib on PRO in patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC. PRO data for osimertinib monotherapy in other EGFR-mutated NSCLC settings [second-line advanced (AURA3) and resected stage IB–IIIA (ADAURA)] also confirm the ability of osimertinib to maintain HRQoL during treatment (24, 32). Moreover, results from AURA3 in patients with EGFR T790M-positive advanced NSCLC who had progressed on first-line EGFR-TKI therapy showed a substantial improvement in HRQoL with osimertinib compared with platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy. Patients treated with osimertinib experienced a significantly longer time to deterioration in key symptoms (chest pain, dyspnea, appetite loss, and fatigue; by the QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13) compared with those receiving platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy. Additionally, a higher proportion of patients in the osimertinib group reported improvements in GHS/QoL (QLQ-C30), functioning (physical, role, and social functioning by QLQ-C30), and some symptoms (dyspnea, fatigue, and appetite loss; ref. 24). As different combination treatments become available in the first-line EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC setting, the impact of these combinations on HRQoL will have to be carefully considered as part of the net clinical benefit analysis.

Patient-reported tolerability in the present study was as expected, based on the known safety profiles of the component drugs (6, 33), and side effects were manageable with standard medical practice. Chemotherapy-related toxicities can place an additional burden on patients, but the observed increase in toxicity seen when adding chemotherapy to osimertinib is counterbalanced by efficacy benefits (6). Although the addition of chemotherapy was associated with an increased frequency of toxicities (such as nausea and vomiting) compared with osimertinib monotherapy, this was mostly during induction and tended to recover during maintenance for most patients. The modest negative effect of treatment on HRQoL and symptoms, along with the manageable toxicity of osimertinib plus chemotherapy, supports the notion that the reported statistically significant improvement in investigator-assessed PFS and the encouraging trend toward OS benefit will be of clinical importance to patients in the real world.

As secondary (QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC13) and exploratory (PRO-CTCAE) endpoints, formal statistical tests are not presented. However, high compliance rates were maintained throughout most of the assessment period, especially for the QLQ-C30 and QLQ-LC-13 questionnaires (i.e., data were collected for many patients and time points), contributing to a robust dataset. It should be noted that as data were only collected up to a PFS2 event or the data cutoff, longer-term effects on HRQoL and symptoms were not assessed.

In conclusion, both osimertinib with the addition of platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy and osimertinib monotherapy as first-line treatment for patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC had nonclinically meaningful impacts on HRQoL outcomes and symptoms. As expected, the addition of chemotherapy to osimertinib led to a worsening of some scores, such as fatigue and appetite loss, but these changes were not clinically meaningful and recovered, at least in part, once the induction period finished. Our data highlight the need to support patients during the induction period, in which treatment burden is the greatest. The clinically meaningful benefit of osimertinib with the addition of platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy is unlikely to be affected by the modest negative effects on patients’ symptoms and functioning. These PRO data support osimertinib with the addition of platinum–pemetrexed chemotherapy as a beneficial first-line treatment option for patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC.
