The structures of ecteinascidin anticancer agents bound to DNA shed light on their mechanism of action
Authors: Tommy Darrière, Federico M Ruiz, Marta Martínez-Díez, Marcelo Lima Ribeiro, Carmen Cuevas, Carlos Fernández-Tornero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEcteinascidins constitute a family of alkaloid compounds, originally isolated from marine tunicates, that exhibit strong antitumor activity. They act through binding to the DNA minor groove and forming covalent adducts with guanine residues. However, the limited availability of structural data restricts mechanistic insights into their mode of action and hampers the discovery of novel compounds. We report crystal structures of duplex DNA adducts with first-, second-, and third-generation ecteinascidins. The structures show that trabectedin, lurbinectedin, and PM54 bind through their shared A- and B-subunits, forming a covalent bond with the N2 atom of guanine and an extensive network of noncovalent interactions, leading to significant minor groove widening. In contrast, their C-subunit, which differs across the compounds, establishes distinct contacts with the modified strand that affect binding strength and sequence specificity. These structural findings, supported by Förster resonance energy transfer and biochemical assays, reveal the molecular determinants underlying differential sequence selectivity and reactivity. Our results provide a mechanistic framework for the anticancer activity of ecteinascidins and a structural basis to guide the design of next-generation analogues with improved therapeutic potential.

Introduction

DNA has emerged as a major and effective target for molecules used in the treatment of various diseases, ranging from infections to cancer [1–4]. Due to its unique double helix structure, DNA provides multiple modes of interaction for therapeutic molecules including alkylation, intercalation, strand-cleaving, and minor groove binding [5]. Among the different classes of DNA-binding compounds, ecteinascidins are marine alkaloids that exhibit strong antitumor activity [6–8].

Trabectedin, also known as ET-743, was the first characterized ecteinascidin following isolation from the Caribbean tunicate Ecteinascidia turbinata and subsequent chemical synthesis [9, 10]. Initially described as a powerful cytotoxic agent against a wide spectrum of cancer cell lines [11, 12], it is nowadays used in the treatment of sarcomas, especially those related to gene translocations [13–17], and ovarian cancer [18]. Trabectedin inhibits cell proliferation and transcriptional activation of several oncogenes at low nanomolar concentrations [19]. It was shown to interfere with transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair and induce DNA breaks in highly expressed genes [20], consistent with its enforced activity in DNA repair-proficient cells [21–23].

Trabectedin is constituted by three tetrahydroisoquinoline rings, referred to as A-, B-, and C-subunits [8] (Fig. 1). The A- and B-subunits are fused by a single-bridged pentacyclic core, connected to a 10-member lactone bridge via a benzylic sulphide bond and attached through a spiro ring to the C-subunit. Trabectedin is known to carry out its activity by interacting with duplex DNA, with selective affinity for specific DNA triplets containing a central guanine [24]. In contrast to alkylating agents binding guanines at positions O6 or N7 in the major groove, trabectedin covalently attaches to the exocyclic N2 amino group in the minor groove, via dehydration of the carbinolamine moiety of the A-subunit [25, 26] (star in Fig. 1). While pyrrolobenzodiazepines such as anthramycin also bind covalently to the N2 atom of guanines in the minor groove, trabectedin was shown to induce DNA bending toward the major groove, considered unique among the other minor groove binding molecules [27]. It was thus proposed that the A- and B-subunits are the major determinants for DNA binding, while the C-subunit projects away from the minor groove and may interact with DNA-binding proteins, promoting the antitumor activity [28, 29]. Additional hydrogen bonds (H-bond) and van der Waals interactions with the neighbouring nucleotides of both DNA strands allow stabilization of the adduct [29, 30], which acts as a functional crosslink between the two strands [31]. This was confirmed by molecular dynamics [10, 32–35], which also showed that binding of several trabectedin molecules compensates DNA bending [10, 27, 34].

The synthetic analogue lurbinectedin, also known as PM01183, has been approved for the treatment of metastatic small cell lung cancer and disease progression on [36–38] or after platinum-based chemotherapy [39–41]. The C-subunit of lurbinectedin consists of a spiro-β-carboline, differing from the tetrahydro-isoquinoline ring of trabectedin (Fig. 1). This compound acts as a selective inhibitor of oncogenic transcription, which induces DNA double-stranded breaks, apoptosis and S-phase arrest in tumour cells [42]. No loss of activity or interaction with NER proteins were observed by modifying this C-subunit [40, 43], while DNA sequence specificity slightly differs from trabectedin [44]. However, lurbinectedin presents in vivo differences with trabectedin including pharmacokinetics [45, 46], suggesting unique therapeutic applications.

Third-generation ecteinascidin analogues have been developed recently, some of which are in initial phases of clinical trials. Among them, PM54 was shown to inhibit proliferation of patient-derived malignant pleural mesothelioma cells and induce immunogenic cell death, activating the STING pathway [47]. Moreover, a recent study showed that third-generation synthetic ecteinascidins, including PM54, had potent anti-proliferative and apoptotic effects on several melanoma cell lines [48]. Interestingly, PM54 deregulates fewer genes than lurbinectedin while preserving cytostatic and cytotoxic effects. This difference has been related to the spiro-benzofuropyridine moiety in the C-subunit (Fig. 1), which likely enhances interactions with biological targets.

Here, we present crystal structures of trabectedin, lurbinectedin and PM54 bound to DNA duplexes, at resolutions ranging from 2.1 to 2.4 Å. The structures reveal the main covalent bond with the alkylated guanine and an extensive network of noncovalent interactions that allow these molecules to bind and remodel the DNA minor groove. Moreover, these structures combined with biochemical analysis show that variability in the ecteinascidin C-subunit plays a prominent role in their specific DNA binding properties.

Materials and methods

Preparation of DNA and ecteinascidins

Trabectedin, lurbinectedin and PM54 were provided by PharmaMar S.A. (Spain) and resuspended in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Merck) at final concentrations of 12.5 mM. The DNA oligonucleotides 5′-AGAGGCCTCT-3′ and 5′-CGATGCATCG-3′ were purchased from Metabion (Germany) and resuspended in milli-Q water to a final concentration of 10 mM. Palindromic duplex DNAs were formed by diluting each oligonucleotide to 2 or 8 mM for interaction or crystallization assays, respectively, in a buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, followed by 5 min incubation at 95°C and slow cooling to 4°C. DNA adducts with ecteinascidins were formed by overnight incubation of the compounds with duplex DNA at 22°C.

Förster resonance energy transfer DNA melting assay

Synthetic oligodeoxynucleotide pairs (Supplementary Table S1), with one strand labelled with the fluorophore 6-carboxyfluorescein on the 5′-end and the complementary strand labelled with the quencher tetramethylrhodamine on the 3′-end, were purchased from Bonsai Technologies (Spain). For the experiments, we followed the methodology previously described [31, 49]. The experimental protocol was performed in a 384-well plate format using a real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) thermal cycler. Oligonucleotides were annealed in TAE buffer (0.08 M Tris–acetate, 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 7.5) at a final concentration of 100 nM for each strand by heating the mixture to 95°C for 5 min followed by gradual cooling to 20°C to allow duplex formation. Compounds were then incubated with the DNA duplex at a range of ten concentrations, prepared as serial 1:3 dilutions from 127 µM to 6.5 nM, for 15 h at 20°C. Thermal denaturation of the double-stranded DNA was subsequently monitored using a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific™), which records fluorescence changes during controlled temperature increases. The melting assay included an initial equilibration step of 5 min at 20°C followed by 200 incremental temperature increases of 0.37°C, each lasting 30 s, until reaching a final temperature of 95°C. Analyses of the raw data obtained for each DNA using an in-house developed Visual Basic Application running on Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, USA) yielded estimates of the increase in melting temperature (ΔTm) resulting from drug binding, relative to the free DNA molecule. The parameter is used to reflect the relative stability of the DNA–ligand complexes [49].

Native gel electrophoresis and quantification

Adducts were formed by incubation of 3 µM duplex DNA and varying ecteinascidin concentrations (0.45, 1.5, 3, 6, 9, 18, and 36 µM) for 18 h at room temperature. Electrophoresis under native conditions was performed in 20% polyacrylamide gels for 2 h at 4°C, in TAE buffer at pH 7.4. Gels were stained with GelRed (Merck, USA) and visualized on a Gel Doc 2000 (Bio-Rad, USA). Experiments were performed in triplicate. Quantification of band intensities was performed with the Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, USA). Individual band signals are expressed as a percentage of the total lane intensity. For plot representation with Sigma Plot (Systat Software, USA), the intensities of all shifted bands in a given lane were merged to compare adduct versus free DNA.

Crystallization, data collection and structure determination

In all cases, adducts were formed by incubation of 1 mM duplex DNA and 2 mM ecteinascidin. Crystals of trabectedin bound to [d(CGATGCATCG)]2 were obtained by mixing equal volume of the sample and reservoir solutions containing 42% 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol, 50 mM 2-(N-morpholino)-ethanesulfonic acid (MES), pH 6.35, 50 mM KCl, 100 mM LiCl, and 12 mM spermine. Crystals of lurbinectedin bound to [d(CGATGCATCG)]2 were obtained by mixing equal volume of the sample and a reservoir solutions containing 2.6 M sodium malonate, 50 mM MES, pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl, 100 mM LiCl, and 12 mM spermine. Crystals of PM54 bound to [d(AGAGGCCTCT)]2 were obtained by mixing equal volume of the sample and a reservoir solutions containing 25% polyethylene glycol 400, 50 mM MES, pH 6.5, 50 mM KCl, 100 mM LiCl, and 12 mM spermine. All crystals were grown at 295 K by vapour diffusion using the sitting drop method and flash cooled by plunging them in liquid N2.

Diffraction data were collected at 100 K at beamline XALOC in the ALBA synchrotron (Spain). The data were processed using XDS [50] and Aimless from the CCP4 suite [51]. A summary of the data collection and processing statistics is provided in Supplementary Table S2. All structures were solved through molecular replacement using Phaser [52] and an ideal B-DNA duplex of the corresponding sequence, generated with Coot [53], as search model. Manual building was carried out in Coot while Refmac [54] was used for refinement. Parameters for lurbinectedin and PM54 were generated using AceDRG [55] from their respective SMILES strings. AceDRG was also used for the generation of parameters for the covalent bond between each of the compounds and the N2 atom of modified guanines. Final refinement statistics are included in Supplementary Table S2.

DNA parameters were calculated using the 3DNA.org web software package [56], while analysis of interacting surfaces and distances was performed with PISA [57]. For trabectedin, reported values are the average of the four adducts present in the asymmetric unit. Structure figures were prepared using PyMOL (Schrödinger, USA).

Results

DNA binding properties of trabectedin, lurbinectedin, and PM54

To investigate ecteinascidin binding to DNA, we performed duplex denaturation assays using Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). Several DNA scaffolds containing one to three guanine bases in their central region (Supplementary Table S1) were tested to explore how different ecteinascidins could influence duplex stability (Fig. 2A). For all sequence contexts, trabectedin increases the melting temperature of duplex DNA more effectively than lurbinectedin or PM54, which both present a similar effect. This suggests that the C-subunit modulates the interaction with duplex DNA, consistent with previous reports [30, 44]. Importantly, the presence of cytosine to the 5′-side of the modified guanine combined with adenine or thymine to the 3′-side (CpGpA/T) drastically reduces duplex stabilization by ecteinascidins, indicating that their binding is strongly weakened or abolished. As altered CpG methylation plays a role in cancer [58], we inquired whether CpG methylation could influence suboptimal binding to CpGpA/T sequences. Three different DNA designs were chosen from our previous FRET analysis, one of which includes a favoured sequence environment for ecteinascidin binding (ACGGA/TCCGT) and two others contain unfavoured environments (ACGA/TCGT and ACGT/ACGT). In all cases, we tested the unmethylated version against three versions with methylated CpG sites, i.e. upper strand methylated, lower strand methylated, and both strands methylated. The results showed no significant differences in ∆Tm between methylated and nonmethylated DNA substrates, indicating that CpG methylation does not significantly affect compound binding under the tested conditions (Fig. 2B).

For subsequent studies, we designed two palindromic duplexes containing six guanines each, i.e. [d(CGATGCATCG)]2 and [d(AGAGGCCTCT)]2 (Fig. 2C). For [d(CGATGCATCG)]2, only guanines at position 5 present preferential binding, as terminal guanines are not bound by ecteinascidins [30] and the ‘CpGpA’ sequence at the 5′-end is unfavoured (Fig. 2A). In contrast, all guanines in [d(AGAGGCCTCT)]2 are favoured according to our FRET analysis. To assess ecteinascidin binding to both duplexes, we performed EMSA where compounds were titrated over DNA. For [d(CGATGCATCG)]2, all compounds slowed DNA migration relative to free DNA (Fig. 2C). Based on our FRET results, we interpret that this band corresponds to binding of these molecules to guanine in position 5. However, incubation with larger amounts of trabectedin produced a minor band with slowed mobility, likely due to minor reactivity toward guanine in position 2 based on our FRET results. Incubation with [d(AGAGGCCTCT)]2 results in increased mobility shifts and heterogeneity, especially for trabectedin and lurbinectedin (Fig. 2C). In contrast, PM54 forms one major band while a second band appears at higher PM54:DNA ratios, suggesting slowed reactivity and enhanced sequence selectivity towards this DNA duplex. Quantification of bands in our EMSA images shows that the three compounds bind in the micromolar range, with trabectedin exhibiting the highest apparent binding affinity (Supplementary Fig. S1), in agreement with our FRET studies. Overall, our findings suggest that the isoquinoline ring in the C-subunit of trabectedin enhances reactivity toward guanines and stabilizes duplex DNA more effectively than lurbinectedin or PM54.

Crystal structures of ecteinascidin–DNA adducts

We obtained crystals of [d(CGATGCATCG)]2 adducts with trabectedin or lurbinectedin, while only [d(AGAGGCCTCT)]2 adducts with PM54 allowed for crystal formation (Supplementary Table S2). In all cases, we numbered nucleotides 1–10 and 11–20 for the alkylated and unmodified strands, respectively. Trabectedin crystals contain four adducts in the asymmetric unit, arranged as two equivalent dimers (Fig. 3A). Within each dimer, the DNA duplexes interact through the first two bases at their edge, with the 3′-end of one strand inserting in the minor groove of the neighbouring duplex (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Each duplex coordinates one K+ ion, which interacts with the O2 atoms of dT4 and dT18, as well as the 3′-end of the entering strand. Clear electron density revealed one trabectedin molecule in the minor groove of each duplex, covalently bound to dG5 (Fig. 3A). However, the orientation of trabectedin molecules bound to the central guanine differs between the two dimers in the asymmetric unit (Supplementary Fig. S2A). In one adduct, an additional piece of weak electron density was modelled as trabectedin bound to dG2 and refined with half occupancy (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Superposition of the four adducts yielded a root-mean-square-deviation (RMSD) value of 0.6 Å, reflecting their overall structural similarities (Supplementary Fig. S2C). The largest divergence involves a different orientation in the C-subunit of trabectedin bound to dG2, which may derive from proximity to the duplex end or the presence of cytosine and adenine to the 5′ and 3′-sides of dG2 (CpGpA), an unfavoured sequence according to biochemical data (Fig. 2).

Lurbinectedin crystals contain a single adduct in the asymmetric unit (Fig. 3B). Symmetry-related adducts interact through insertion of one strand into the neighbouring duplex and K+ coordination, yielding a continuous filament (Supplementary Fig. S3). Two water molecules, observed in this structure due to diffraction data quality (Supplementary Table S2), further coordinate the K+ ion. Strong electron density supports a single lurbinectedin molecule within the minor groove, covalently bound to dG5 (Fig. 3B). Nevertheless, as opposed to trabectedin, no density is observed in the vicinity of guanine dG2 or dG12, consistent with biochemical data (Fig. 2).

The asymmetric unit of PM54 crystals contains two adducts (Fig. 3C). Unlike trabectedin or lurbinectedin, duplexes interact without strand overlap to form a linear filament with symmetry-related molecules (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Electron density reveals two PM54 molecules in the minor groove, covalently attached to guanine in position 4 of each strand, i.e. dG4 and dG14 (Fig. 3C). Additional electron density corresponds to a free PM54 molecule involved in crystal contacts through interaction with PM54 bound to dG14, whereas PM54 bound to dG4 presents no conformational restraints imposed by additional contacts (Supplementary Fig. S4B). The two adducts in the asymmetric unit superpose with RMSD of 0.3 Å, indicating a high degree of structural similarity (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

Structural details of trabectedin–DNA adducts

Trabectedin alkylation of dG5 is supported by continuous electron density between the N2 atom of dG5 and the C21 atom of the compound. This modification does not alter dG5:dC16 base pairing relative to ideal B-DNA. The A- and B-subunits of trabectedin project toward the 5′ and 3′-sides, respectively, of the modified guanine. The three rings forming the B-subunit plus the piperazine ring in the A-subunit snugly accommodate on the unmodified strand, establishing interactions with the three bases of nucleotides dG15–dC16–dA17 (Fig. 4). The protonated N12 atom of the A-subunit [30] forms an H-bond with the N3 atom of dA17, only absent in one of the adducts. The B-subunit isoquinoline establishes van der Waals interactions with the O2 atom of dC16, while the oxygen atom between C8 and C23 in the B-subunit methylenedioxy ring forms a strong H-bond with the N2 atom of dG15, with distances varying between 2.3 and 2.8 Å among the four adducts. In the adduct lacking the H-bond with dA17, the same oxygen atom in the B-subunit forms a weak H-bond with the O2 atom of dC6 in the modified strand. The same regions in the A- and B-subunits also contact the DNA backbone of the unmodified strand at nucleotides dC16–dA17–dT18. First, a weak H-bond and van der Waals interactions are formed between the B-subunit rings and the dC16 ribose. Second, the dA17 ribose establishes a van der Waals contact with the B-subunit acetoxy substituent, which protrudes toward the solvent. Third, a van der Waals contact is established between the O4’ atom of the dT18 ribose and the methyl group bound to N12 in the A-subunit piperazine. Besides, the hydroxyl group bound to the C18 atom of the A-subunit isoquinoline, which projects to the solvent perpendicular to the A-subunit piperazine, forms an H-bond with the O3’ atom of the dA17 ribose.

Interactions with the alkylated strand are more limited (Fig. 4). The 10-member lactone ring, oriented perpendicular to the B-subunit isoquinoline, interacts with the modified strand backbone and the base to the 3′-side of the alkylated guanine. Specifically, van der Waals contacts are established between the lactone ring and the O2 atom in the dC6 base. In addition, the carbonyl oxygen in the lactone ring forms H-bonds with the O3’ or the O4’ atoms of the dC6 ribose. Finally, the flat C-subunit isoquinoline projects away from the minor groove, with one face stacked against the dA7 ribose and the opposite face exposed to the solvent. Importantly, the C-subunit hydroxyl group forms one, two, or three H-bonds—depending on the adduct—with oxygen atoms linked to the phosphate group of dT8. These interactions likely confer the unique DNA-binding properties shown by trabectedin (Fig. 2).

While the trabectedin molecule bound to dG2 exhibits flexibility and reduced occupancy, differences with those bound to dG5 can be identified (Supplementary Fig. S5). Notably, bases in the unmodified strand lie far from the A- and B-subunits, thus hampering H-bond formation. Moreover, the protonated N12 atom does not form H-bonds with dG20, whose amino group induces a minor shift of the A-subunit out of the minor groove. This is partly compensated by tilting of the B-subunit towards the minor groove, such that its methylenedioxy ring forms weak H-bonds with the unmodified strand backbone and the dA3 base in the modified strand. Tilting of the B-subunit is likely induced by the methoxy group in the C-subunit, which lies closer to the B-subunit than that of trabectedin molecules bound to dG5. The absence of a methoxy group in an equivalent position of the C-subunits in lurbinectedin and PM54 could explain their incapacity to attack CpGpA motifs. In conclusion, the reduced binding at CpGpA/T sites can be rationalized by the inability of these sequences to support the key noncovalent anchoring interactions formed on the unmodified strand prior to and during alkylation.

Structural details of lurbinectedin–DNA adducts

In addition to the covalent bond between lurbinectedin and dG5, the overall network of noncovalent interactions resembles that of trabectedin adducts, with minor differences (Fig. 5). Like trabectedin, lurbinectedin engages bases of nucleotides dG15–dC16–dA17 in the unmodified strand. However, its A-subunit piperazine lies closer to DNA, with the N12 atom preserving the H-bond with N3 in dA17 and the N2 atom forming a new, weak H-bond with O2 in dC16. The latter atom also establishes van der Waals contacts with the B-subunit isoquinoline. Interestingly, the B-subunit methylenedioxy ring of lurbinectedin sits closer to the modified strand than that of trabectedin. This weakens the H-bond connecting the N2 atom of dG15 and the oxygen between C8 and C23 in the methylenedioxy ring, which also forms an H-bond with the O4’ atom of the dA7 ribose in the alkylated strand. While interactions of this ring with the dC16 ribose are lost compared to trabectedin, van der Waals contacts between the B-subunit acetoxy substituent and the dA17 ribose are maintained. The H-bond between the ribose O3’ atom of dA17 and the hydroxyl group bound to the C18 atom of the A-subunit isoquinoline is also preserved, and the interaction is reinforced through a water molecule contact. Compared to trabectedin, the H-bond between the lactone and the modified strand is lost, while van der Waals contacts are preserved, including those with the O2 atom of dC6 (Fig. 5). Finally, the C-subunit of lurbinectedin is more exposed to the solvent than that of trabectedin, significantly reducing its interactions with the alkylated strand backbone. A weak water-mediated contact links the oxygen atom of its methoxy group to one oxygen in the dA7 phosphate. An additional water molecule connects the B- and C-subunits of lurbinectedin, potentially constraining their relative orientation.

Structural details of PM54–DNA adducts

While interactions of the A- and B-subunits with DNA are conserved with other ecteinascidins, the overall adduct geometry is altered (Fig. 6). The unrestrained PM54 molecules bound to dG4 interact with the three bases of nucleotides dC16–dC17–dT18, corresponding to dG15–dC16–dA17 in trabectedin or lurbinectedin adducts. The presence of pyrimidines instead of purines flanking the central cytosine remodels the interaction network, mainly around the B-subunit methylenedioxy moiety. By changing the pucker of this moiety, the C23 atom establishes van der Waals contacts with the O2 and N2 atoms of dC16 and dG5, respectively. Moreover, the oxygen between C8 and C23 in PM54 forms an H-bond with dG5 in the modified strand, while H-bonds with the unmodified strand are absent. Besides, the oxygen within the lactone ring establishes a weak H-bond with the dC6 ribose. Finally, the C-subunit in unrestrained PM54 molecules stacks against the dC6 ribose and forms a weak H-bond with its O4’ atom. Despite a conserved arrangement in the A- and B-subunits in the two PM54 molecules bound to each adduct, the position of the C-subunit strongly differs (Supplementary Fig. S4C). The C-subunit of PM54 bound to dG14, whose configuration is influenced by its contact with free PM54, is more exposed to the solvent. In contrast, the C-subunit in PM54 bound to dG4, whose configuration is unrestrained by external contacts, is about 30° closer to the B-subunit, thus enabling van der Waals contacts between these subunits.

Structural comparison of ecteinascidin–DNA adducts

Superposition of the adduct structures reveals variations in the arrangement of ecteinascidins (Fig. 7A). Differences are minimal for the A- and B-subunits, such that the overall network of noncovalent interactions with DNA is maintained, with minor alterations (see above). Nevertheless, ecteinascidins bind DNA with variations of a few degrees in the aperture between A- and B-subunits or in the relative tilt respect to the modified guanine. This likely allows adaptation to different DNA sequence environments or changes in the position of the C-subunit, which encompass up to 40° opening relative to the B-subunit (Fig. 7A). The C-subunits of trabectedin and lurbinectedin exhibit closed and open configurations, respectively, through movements that occur in the plane of their ring systems. As a result, the C-subunit of trabectedin protrudes less from the DNA minor groove and interacts more strongly with DNA, while that of lurbinectedin is more exposed (Fig. 7B). While the C-subunit of unrestrained and restrained PM54 molecules bound to DNA also adopt closed and open configurations, respectively, they differ from those observed in trabectedin and lurbinectedin structures. Unrestrained PM54 is slightly more closed and mildly tilted away from the modified DNA strand relative to trabectedin. Restrained PM54 is less open and strongly tilted away from the modified strand with respect to lurbinectedin. These two PM54 configurations are enabled through torsions in the central lactone ring, yielding to different exposure of its C-subunit to the solvent. Structural differences among ecteinascidins likely account for their distinct DNA binding and stabilization properties observed in our biochemical assays.

Distortions in duplex DNA upon ecteinascidin alkylation

Superposition of adducted DNA duplexes with an ideal B-DNA shows that ecteinascidin binding induces widening of the minor groove by 3–4 Å at the alkylated guanine (Fig. 7C). The aperture fades as the distance increases in both directions from the alkylated guanine (Supplementary Fig. S6). The maximum minor groove widening is induced by PM54, a structure derived from crystals with [d(AGAGGCCTCT)]2 bound to two molecules of the compound. While the structures of both trabectedin and lurbinectedin derive from crystals with [d(CGATGCATCG)]2, the former presents a larger minor groove aperture, likely due to chemical differences in the C-subunit. These results suggest that the DNA sequence around the alkylated guanine has a modest impact on DNA deformation.

Further analysis of DNA deformation shows minor differences across the compounds (Supplementary Fig. S6). DNA roll values are similar to those of ideal B-DNA at the alkylated GpC step, while flanking base pair steps exhibit a strong increase of 10–15°. Tilt values remain close to those of B-DNA for trabectedin, while they change by ± 5° for lurbinectedin and PM54. Twist values at the alkylated GpC step differ minimally from those of B-DNA, while flanking base pair steps present a 5–10° decrease. Finally, the shear and stretch values are equivalent to those of ideal B-DNA, suggesting that base pairing H-bonds are not affected by ecteinascidin alkylation. These results show that DNA alkylation by ecteinascidins induces minor groove widening that alters local base pair geometry, consistent with previous molecular dynamics simulations [35].

Discussion

The structural characterization of DNA-binding compounds in complex with DNA provides insights into their mechanism of action at the atomic level, thus shedding light on their activities. We determined crystal structures of duplex DNA adducts of trabectedin, lurbinectedin and PM54 at high resolution. The three compounds share a conserved binding mode, through interaction and widening of the DNA minor groove. This interaction involves alkylation of the guanine N2 atom, resulting in a covalent link with the C21 atom in the A-subunit of ecteinascidins [25]. A network of H-bonds and van der Waals contacts, generally conserved across compounds, stabilizes the interaction. The main contacts are mediated by the B-subunit and the A-subunit piperazine, which engage the base opposite the alkylated guanine and flanking bases in the unmodified strand. The plasticity of these contacts, which we characterize across different compounds and DNA sequences, enables adaptability to diverse DNA contexts. Importantly, the H-bond connecting the B-subunit methylenedioxy ring and the base in the unmodified strand placed to the 3′-side of the alkylated guanine is stronger in trabectedin, which likely contributes to higher duplex stabilization compared to lurbinectedin and PM54 (Fig. 2A). The methylenedioxy ring also contacts the modified strand, but its interactions are more variable across ecteinascidins.

Despite partial occupancy and poor electron density, the trabectedin molecule at dG2 sheds light on suboptimal ecteinascidin binding to CpGpA/T observed in biochemical analyses. First, the presence of cytosine on the 5′ side of the target guanine in the modified strand alters the geometry of the opposing strand such that the bases available for recognition are no longer optimally positioned to engage the A- and B-subunits. Second, and more critically, the bases flanking the guanine in the unmodified strand do not provide the same hydrogen-bonding opportunities, due to disruption of the specific donor–acceptor geometry required for the conserved A-subunit and methylenedioxy-mediated contacts. In this context, the ligand cannot achieve the same degree of pre-organization within the minor groove, leading to inefficient positioning of the reactive carbinolamine and consequently reduced alkylation efficiency. Thus, the structures indicate that sequence selectivity is not dictated solely by reactivity of the target guanine, but critically depends on the ability of the surrounding sequence to support a stereochemically precise noncovalent recognition interface, particularly on the unmodified strand.

Collectively, our structures indicate that ecteinascidins couple guanine-N2 alkylation to asymmetric duplex engagement, with the conserved A- and B-subunit core establishing most stabilizing contacts with the opposing strand. This provides a structural basis for the proposed “pseudo–crosslinking” behaviour, whereby duplex stabilization can arise from interstrand coupling and localized minor-groove widening rather than from a second covalent link. Furthermore, it rationalizes the reduced binding at CpGpA/T sites as a loss of the opposing-strand hydrogen-bonding network required for productive positioning prior to alkylation. Finally, the compound-specific orientation and exposure of the C-subunit modulate local DNA deformation and solvent accessibility of the adduct, which may contribute to differences in downstream processing, including transcription-coupled DNA repair.

Important differences were also observed among the three ecteinascidins. First, the C-subunit isoquinoline of trabectedin inserts more deeply into the minor groove than the spiro-β-carboline ring of lurbinectedin. The spiro-benzofuropyridine can adopt different configurations due to flexibility of the lactone ring. Second, the C-subunit of trabectedin forms one to three direct H-bonds with oxygens linked to the phosphate positioned three nucleotides downstream the alkylated guanine, whereas the C-subunit of lurbinectedin only establishes a water-mediated contact. The C-subunit of PM54 can form a weak H-bond with the ribose that is two nucleotides downstream the modified guanine, but this interaction is lost in the open configuration. Third, trabectedin partially alkylates dG2 in [d(CGATGCATCG)]2, while lurbinectedin does not, indicating enhanced reactivity of trabectedin toward guanines preceded by cytosine. These structural differences are consistent with our biochemical results, which show increased reactivity and duplex stabilization by trabectedin and minor differences between lurbinectedin and PM54.

The structural insights also provide a framework to explain differential in vivo activities and specificity for various types of cancer across ecteinascidins. The C-subunit has been proposed to mediate interactions with DNA-binding proteins, including transcription and DNA repair factors, thereby enhancing anti-tumour activity of ecteinascidins [28, 29]. Our data show marked differences in C-subunit positioning relative to DNA across ecteinascidins (Fig. 7), which likely plays a relevant role in their specific mechanism of action. The trabectedin C-subunit, which forms strong H-bonds with the DNA duplex, likely has reduced mobility compared to those of lurbinectedin and PM54. Since the C-subunit orientation is constrained by the central lactone ring, chemical changes that restrict flexibility of this ring may impact the activity of these compounds.

Our results underscore that ecteinascidins present a unique mode of DNA binding that is remarkably different from other minor groove alkylators. Mitomycin C and anthramycin, which also insert in the minor groove through alkylation of the guanine N2 atom, present an elongated structure that has minimal impact on minor groove geometry [59, 60], whereas the wedge-like structure of ecteinascidins widens the minor groove by 3–4 Å. Moreover, mitomycin C exhibits CpG sequence specificity through bonding with the guanine paired to the 5′-cytosine, which derives into an interstrand crosslink [60]. In contrast, our FRET and structural data indicate that the CpGpA/T context hinders ecteinascidin binding, highlighting opposed sequence preference. Besides, alkylators from the duocarmycin family covalently bind the adenine N3 atom and occupy a large extension of the DNA minor groove with minimal impact on its geometry [61], as opposed to ecteinascidins. Finally, minor groove-interacting compounds such as chromomycin A3 and mithramycin or its analogues, which bind DNA noncovalently as ion-mediated dimers [62–64], induce minor groove widening equivalent to that observed for ecteinascidins.

Our crystal structures provide optimal starting points for analogue design by directly mapping the contacts that support the conserved alkylation mode and the features that appear to tune affinity and sequence selectivity. Changes within the principal interaction surface, comprising the A- and B-subunits, is expected to affect binding or reactivity by perturbing the noncovalent network. For example, replacing the N12 methyl group with a polar substituent, extending the C17 methoxy into a longer polar group, or introducing hydroxylated variants around the methylenedioxy region would likely increase H-bond formation. Besides, modifications in the C-subunit of lurbinectedin or PM54 may provide a handle to modulate recognition. For example, addition of hydroxyl substituents at C8’ could reinforce the interaction with the alkylated strand, while methoxylation at this position might help accommodate otherwise disfavoured CpGpA/T contexts, consistent with the alternative trabectedin configuration observed at dG2.

In summary, our study uncovers the structural basis of ecteinascidin binding to duplex DNA and provides mechanistic insights into their molecular mode of action. The structures presented here not only rationalize the biochemical and biological differences among trabectedin, lurbinectedin, and the third-generation analogue PM54, but also establish a foundation for the design of novel ecteinascidins with improved activity and selectivity.
