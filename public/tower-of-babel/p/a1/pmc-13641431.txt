Shaping an Early Nephrology Fellowship Curriculum
Authors: Ashley M. La, Suzanne M. Boyle, Kurtis A. Pivert, Anna L. Zisman, Benjamin S. Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Nephrology is an increasingly complex field confronted with an expanding range of therapeutics and rising patient morbidity, requiring both breadth and depth of knowledge. First-year fellows must develop subspecialty expertise while managing a high-intensity workload, often involving greater autonomy, more subspecialized clinical decision making, and increased procedural expectations than in residency. Nationally, there is a movement to construct best practices for the residency-to-fellowship transition, but these have yet to be standardized or widely adopted.1,2

It is estimated that 77% of internal medicine residents have completed ≥2 weeks of nephrology clinical rotations,3 so it would be expected that most nephrology fellows have some previous nephrology experience. However, because no standardized requirements exist before fellowship, incoming fellows differ in their baseline knowledge and skillsets. Nephrology faculty have previously identified medical knowledge deficits and workload management struggles as major barriers to fellows' success.4,5 An early-fellowship or orientation curriculum is often delivered at the beginning of fellowship to address gaps in baseline nephrology knowledge. However, the duration, quality, and content of the curriculum may vary by program. The optimal way to prepare nephrology fellows for the demands of their training is unknown.

To better understand how to address this gap, we surveyed current nephrology fellows to assess self-perceived readiness for nephrology fellowship, characterize contemporary self-directed and fellowship-directed orientation curricula, and identify educational strategies and content that current fellows believe could have helped improve their preparations for nephrology fellowship.

Methods

A survey instrument to measure nephrology fellows' self-assessed preparedness for nephrology fellowship, nephrology exposure in residency, and resources used to prepare for nephrology fellowship was selected for inclusion in the 2025 American Society of Nephrology (ASN) Nephrology Fellow Survey, an annual survey of all current adult, pediatric, and adult/pediatric nephrology fellows disseminated April 29–May 22, 2025 (via Qualtrics). The survey annually collects data on responding fellows' self-reported demographics which were included in the analysis to ensure the respondents were representative of nephrology fellows nationally. The survey also collects data on responding fellows' fellowship program characteristics and career interests.6 Self-assessed preparedness was measured using a Likert scale; fellows responding moderately prepared, very prepared, or extremely prepared were considered prepared for fellowship, and those selecting slightly prepared or not at all prepared were considered unprepared. Finally, participants were asked questions regarding their personal experience with and learner preferences for an early-fellowship curriculum. Response options for questions on learner preferences were derived from previous literature of nephrology fellowship education.7,8 The survey instrument is included in the Supplemental Material.

Statistical analysis was performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Comparisons between cohorts self-assessing as prepared and unprepared for fellowship were conducted using chi-squared test of independence, with α=0.01. The study was determined to be exempt by the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine Institutional Research Board (Study # 00205206).

Early-Fellowship Curriculum

Half of respondents (164 [49%]) reported participating in an early fellowship educational curriculum before starting their clinical rotations. Of those who participated in an early-fellowship curriculum, 120 (73%) fellows participated in a curriculum only at their program's institution, 25 (15%) fellows with multiple institutions, and 19 (12%) both at their program's institution and with multiple institutions.

The most common types of educational methods used in current early-fellowship curricula were didactics/lectures (96%), shadowing senior fellows (62%), case simulation (49%), and procedural training (48%). When asked which educational methods they would use when designing their ideal early-fellowship curriculum, the most commonly selected responses were didactics/lectures (85%), case simulation (71%), shadowing senior fellows (60%), and small group problem-based learning (57%). Discussion

Our survey sought to understand the factors associated with self-assessed preparedness for nephrology fellowship and learner preferences for an early-fellowship curriculum. The only factor surveyed that was associated with self-assessed preparedness was the number of weeks of nephrology rotations completed in residency. As expected, fellows who felt prepared were more likely to have more weeks of prior nephrology experience. Most fellows used peer-reviewed, evidence-based resources to prepare for fellowship including UpToDate, textbooks, and clinical practice guidelines. Interestingly, although about half of respondents participated in an early-fellowship curriculum, participation in a curriculum was not associated with preparedness, as half of those who felt prepared to start fellowship had not participated in an early-fellowship curriculum.

Although self-reported preparedness can provide insight into a potential educational gap, it may reflect more on fellows' confidence or anxiety at the beginning of a new level of training rather than true knowledge deficit. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that learners' self-assessment often correlate poorly with performance evaluations, with some showing a tendency to overestimate competence.9,10 Thus, despite this lack of association between participation in an early-fellowship curriculum and self-reported preparedness, implementing an early-fellowship curriculum is likely beneficial to ensure first-year fellows have the fundamental baseline knowledge required for the rigors of fellowship. Furthermore, although nephrology fellows would undoubtedly benefit from specialized nephrology experience during residency, lack of standardized requirements before fellowship can lead to varying levels of competency at the start of fellowship.

Previous boot camp curricula at the beginning of residency have demonstrated improvement in knowledge and skills using test scores, simulation assessments, and clinical evaluations.11–13 To date, the only published nephrology boot camp is a 4-week curriculum at the beginning of a 3-year fellowship at the Mayo Clinic that includes a wide range of administrative, inpatient, outpatient, and dialysis topics and resulted in positive perceptions from participating fellows.14 Although this curriculum was reportedly successful, most nephrology fellowships are comparatively small, and it may not be possible for every program to afford 4 weeks of education time at the transition of residency to fellowship because of clinical obligations.15 Most fellows who participated in an early-fellowship curriculum did so at their home institution, which is often most convenient for proximity and scheduling. A minority of fellows participated in a combined curriculum with other institutions. Although we did not ask respondents for details on the institutions involved in combined curricula, existing multi-institutional curricula include a 2-day symposium for new fellows from 13 Mid-Atlantic institutions hosted by the National Kidney Foundation, as well as a 1-day session for new nephrology fellows in Michigan hosted by #NephJC. Multi-institutional education to improve fellow readiness has already been widely explored and used by pulmonary and critical care medicine programs, which have demonstrated several benefits including combining expertise and experiences of instructors from multiple institutions and providing the opportunity for fellows to network with peers.16–19

Our research found that didactics, case simulation, and shadowing senior fellows were the most commonly used and most preferred methods among respondents. These educational methods tend to be efficient and flexible and are commonly used throughout medical education. Our results thus demonstrate good concordance between learner preferences and currently used teaching methods. Interestingly, there was a higher proportion of fellows who preferred problem-based learning compared with procedure training, the fourth most-commonly used educational method. This is despite recent data showing that about 25% of graduating fellows were assessed as not ready for unsupervised practice for procedures such as kidney biopsy and temporary dialysis catheter insertions,20 which they may be expected to perform in their practice after graduation. One explanation for a lower preference for procedural training in an early-fellowship curriculum is that fellows may feel they receive adequate hands-on procedural training throughout their fellowship. Because the first several procedures that a fellow performs are typically with one-on-one supervision, perhaps fellows feel that early procedural training, which would likely be in a simulation setting, is not necessary. In addition, the involvement of non-nephrology subspecialists (such as interventional radiologists and intensivists) in performing kidney biopsies and dialysis catheter placement may contribute to a decreased emphasis on procedural training during nephrology fellowship. Problem-based learning has been explored mostly in the undergraduate medical education setting, which often involves case-based learning in which a clinical case is presented to a small group of learners to collaboratively apply knowledge and problem-solving skills for diagnosis and management.21,22 Problem-based learning in nephrology training has been described once in the literature, although this was combined with an online self-study component.23 One benefit of problem-based learning is the application of critical thinking to real-world case examples; early nephrology fellows likely appreciate the opportunity for active experimentation before entering a high-stakes, high-volume clinical environment, as described by Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle.24 Furthermore, depending on the structures of clinical teams, an early-fellowship curriculum may be one of few times nephrology fellows are able to work together in a group setting, and fellows may value collaborative learning as a way to form bonds with each other and develop a supportive team environment.

In terms of early-fellowship curriculum content, the most frequently chosen topics by fellows were dialysis prescriptions and complications, AKI, and electrolytes and acid/base. Of the options provided, these topics represent common inpatient nephrology emergencies that nephrology fellows believe are a priority to learn before beginning clinical rotations. These clinical scenarios often require timely recognition and management that new nephrology fellows may not have significant previous experience with, so it is plausible that fellows desire early fundamental knowledge in these areas to be able to provide prompt, appropriate care. Similarly, the most commonly chosen skills fellows preferred to learn in an early-fellowship curriculum were workflow/efficiency and discussing dialysis and goals of care with patients, skills that pertain to executive functioning in clinical practice rather than procedures. These topics and skills selected for an early-fellowship curriculum appear to represent foundational concepts for a nephrology consultant. It is unclear what aspects of nephrology rotations in residency benefit learners the most during their transition to fellowship, but possibilities include experience-based knowledge acquisition and familiarity with the workflow of a nephrology fellow. However, the fact that fellows selected these topics and skills out of proportion to those who had no nephrology experience in residency demonstrates the retrospectively perceived need for additional education. The topics and skills preferred for an early-fellowship curriculum contrasts with previous nephrology fellow surveys that expressed desire for additional education in home dialysis modalities, ultrasound, GN, and renal pathology before completion of fellowship.8,25 It is important to recognize that these are learner preferences specifically for an early-fellowship curriculum, which may not reflect true learner needs or address core competencies required by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education and for successful independent practice postfellowship.7,26 However, information on learner preferences is valuable because current fellows have recent experience to provide information on the major knowledge gaps and topics that they would have found useful before starting their clinical rotations.

Although, to our knowledge, this is the largest study assessing fellow preparedness, the limitations inherent in such a survey instrument may decrease its generalizability. For instance, there may be differences in residency training experience that the survey fails to capture. However, demographics of survey respondents were largely representative of the nephrology fellowship population as reported by the National Residency Matching Program fellowship match outcomes results and data report, specifically in terms of location of medical school (45% US Medical Graduate, 55% International Medical Graduate).27 Respondents were asked to report self-assessed preparedness at the start of fellowship, which is subject to recall bias and may not correlate with competency. To simplify the analysis, we provided a finite list of options for learner preferences without providing a free-text option; however, the options provided were derived from previously published literature on nephrology fellowship education, and we were able to discern themes regarding learner preferences on the basis of the most common choices selected. Finally, although our survey data provide insight into learner preferences for an early-fellowship curriculum, we do not have data demonstrating that implementing these learner preferences improve educational outcomes among nephrology fellows.

Nephrology training is becoming increasingly complex and rigorous, and it is critical to ensure that new fellows are prepared to provide specialty care for patients regardless of their prior experience. We believe that an early-fellowship curriculum with dedicated teaching time before clinical rotations can bridge essential knowledge gaps and help prepare new fellows for their clinical rotations. This study provides insight into learner preferences from current fellows for an early-fellowship curriculum and emphasizes the desire for education on nephrology emergencies and executive functioning skills to equip fellows for success. Future studies should evaluate the designs of early-fellowship curricula in nephrology education and their effects on learner competency in clinical practice.
