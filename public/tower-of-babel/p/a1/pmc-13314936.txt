Fabrication of medical radiation shielding sheets using reactive densification–based tungsten/PE porous composite materials
Authors: Seon-Chil Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractX-ray shielding materials used in medical institutions are applied in various forms, including garments, sheets, films, and membranes, and recent studies have actively focused on the adoption of environmentally friendly materials and processing technologies. In this study, lead-free radiation shielding sheets were designed and fabricated by introducing an HDPE/W/ADCA-based foaming–calendering process as an alternative to conventional lead-based X-ray shields. The correlation between microstructural characteristics and shielding performance was quantitatively analyzed using a reactive densification model. HDPE/W/ADCA composites were first prepared as foamable preforms with thicknesses of 1.0–2.0 mm through twin-screw compounding followed by single-screw sheet extrusion. Subsequently, multi-stage calendering was applied at temperatures ranging from 100 to 140 °C to produce final shielding sheets with a thickness of 0.3 mm. FESEM analysis revealed that tungsten particles were uniformly distributed throughout the cross-section of the sheets, while residual pores were present only in fine, localized forms. The shielding efficiency evaluated under medical X-ray conditions (60–120 kVp) ranged from 92.63% to 78.80%, which is approximately 5% points lower than that of lead sheets of the same thickness (97.67–84.86%), corresponding to about 95% of the relative shielding performance of lead. According to the reactive densification model, the enhanced flowability of the polymer matrix contributed to the effective dispersion of metal particles and a localized increase in effective density, indicating that a thickness increase of only approximately 0.02–0.05 mm would be sufficient to achieve shielding performance comparable to that of lead. These results suggest that controlling local density and particle dispersion is a critical design parameter in the development of lightweight and flexible lead-free radiation shielding materials.Keywords: Radiation, Medical X-ray shielding material, Tungsten, Densification, DensitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Medical X-ray imaging devices are extensively utilized for diagnostic purposes and are typically operated in close proximity to patients and medical personnel1. Consequently, minimizing medical radiation exposure is critical, necessitating continuous dose monitoring and the use of appropriate protective equipment2. Currently, protective devices, such as aprons and thyroid shields, commonly employed in medical settings, predominantly utilize lead (Pb) as the primary shielding material3. However, presents significant challenges owing to its toxicity and environmental hazards, as well as its high density, which contributes to excessive weight. Prolonged use of lead-based protective gear can result in user fatigue and increased musculoskeletal strain4. Consequently, the demand for lightweight and environmentally friendly radiation shielding materials to replace Pb has increased5.

A representative approach for lead substitution involves the use of lead-free radiation-shielding composites, in which metallic or ceramic shielding fillers are dispersed within a polymer matrix6. These composite materials are comparatively lightweight and offer advantages, such as high formability and design flexibility7. However, because their linear attenuation coefficients (µ) are generally lower than that of lead, achieving equivalent shielding performance often requires increased material thickness8. This increase in thickness inevitably results in greater overall volume and stiffness, thereby reducing flexibility and compromising both comfort and usability. In practical applications, commercial shielding products are typically manufactured as sheets, films, or membranes. Under continuous processing conditions, composite materials offer superior shielding performance and processability compared with single-component materials9.

Polymer–metal composite shielding materials achieve satisfactory wearability by leveraging the inherent flexibility and processability of polymers. However, optimizing these materials necessitates a comprehensive understanding of all constituent components, including the polymer matrix, metal or ceramic fillers, and auxiliary additives, as well as their mutual interactions10,11. Structures incorporating high-atomic-number powders, such as tungsten, bismuth, and tin, are incorporated into polymer matrices to enhance shielding effectiveness. The type and properties of the polymer significantly influence the mechanical and processing characteristics of the composite, and overall shielding performance12. Key processing technologies in the fabrication of shielding material include the strategies for mixing and dispersing metal particles within the polymer matrix, as well as the final shaping methods, such as extrusion, calendering, and molding. These factors directly influence not only the shielding performance but also the uniformity of thickness, density distribution, and flexibility of the finished product13. Accordingly, this study proposes a design strategy to enhance shielding performance by achieving a more uniform dispersion of metal particles within a porous polymer matrix, thereby increasing the effective density while preserving lightweight characteristics14.

Therefore, enhancing radiation shielding performance typically involves either increasing the content of shielding materials or improving the effective density within the shielding structure. However, excessive addition of metal fillers elevates the proportion of the metallic phase relative to the polymer matrix, which can compromise melt flowability, processability, and flexibility15. Moreover, even with a consistent overall composition, non-uniform distribution of metal particles may lead to uneven density distribution, resulting in localized degradation in shielding performance. This heterogeneity also undermines efforts to optimize weight and thickness16. This non-uniformity represents a significant obstacle to achieving reproducible and reliable shielding performance under identical processing conditions. The performance of radiation-shielding materials is not governed solely by average composition (i.e., metal content). Rather, internal microstructural features—particularly the spatial distribution of metal particles and localized density control—have emerged as critical factors influencing both shielding performance and long-term reliability. Nevertheless, shielding structures have traditionally been simplified and characterized using macroscopic parameters such as overall density and thickness. In practice, however, radiation attenuation is highly sensitive not only to metal content but also to interparticle spacing, dispersion uniformity, and pore (void) structure, all of which are key microstructural parameters17. Therefore, achieving superior radiation attenuation under identical mass constraints requires advanced structural design and processing technologies that optimize particle dispersion and local density18.

In this study, considering the characteristics of polymer matrices combined with metal shielding materials, an approach is proposed to enhance effective density and shielding performance within the composite while minimizing any increase in total mass. To accomplish this, a reactive densification model, previously utilized primarily in the field of metal and ceramic composites, is introduced into the design of radiation shielding composites. This model enables quantitative tracking of the internal density enhancement mechanisms19. Generally, designing a polymer matrix with a porous structure reduces the overall mass; however, the associated decrease in average density diminishes the probability of interactions with incident radiation, resulting in reduced shielding efficiency. To address this limitation, metal particles are incorporated into the polymer matrix, and localized high-density regions are formed by partially filling interparticle voids (e.g., pinholes) through processes such as foaming, calendering, and reactive processes. This strategy is intended to achieve locally high linear attenuation coefficients, despite a reduction in the average density of the overall structure20.

In this study, PE/W preform sheets with a controlled porous architecture are fabricated, and a reactive densification model is applied to experimentally validate the changes in shielding performance. The PE/W filler utilized is a composite masterbatch comprising a polyethylene (PE)-based resin blended with PE wax and inorganic fillers (e.g., calcium carbonate and talc), formulated as a PE/W-based foamable compound suitable for foaming processes21. Conventional foaming introduces air layers (cells) that facilitate the dispersion of metal particles; however, this also increases porosity and thickness, resulting in reduced density and, consequently, diminished shielding efficiency, both of which are significant drawbacks for radiation shielding applications22. To mitigate these challenges, this study introduces a partial foaming approach to achieve high density with low expansion, followed by secondary processing, such as calendaring. This methodology is designed to maintain or enhance metal particle dispersion while controlling sheet density and thickness. Accordingly, the core principle of the reactive densification model centers on precise spatial positioning and localized density control to enhance the dispersion of metal particles. In this framework, foaming is employed not to generate highly expanded sponge-like structures, but to form fine pore architectures through low-level foaming. These finely tuned pores facilitate the efficient placement and uniform distribution of metal particles throughout the polymer matrix23. Accordingly, the present approach is clearly distinguished from conventional foaming and calendaring strategies that primarily aim to achieve uniform control of global average density or thickness. In contrast, the proposed process technology incorporates a localized density-control design, in which pore collapse (densification) secondarily increases the local effective density, thereby enhancing the novelty and functional significance of this study.

This study ultimately targets product-level implementation by fabricating high-density, effective shielding layers through multilayer extrusion and calendering processes24. Through process-driven control of the internal structure of the shielding material, this approach demonstrates that high-density and high-shielding performance can be achieved not solely by increasing the filler content, but through deliberate structural design and localized density control. This methodology also enables improvements in flexibility and weight reduction. The integrated process–structure approach presented herein is anticipated to be applicable beyond medical Pb-free shielding materials to a broad range of radiation shielding applications that require both high shielding efficacy and ergonomic performance.

Materials

For experimental validation of the reactive densification model in radiation shielding applications, PE/W-based foamable preform sheets and tungsten metal powder were utilized as primary materials. High-density polyethylene (HDPE, Bell Balls, China) served as the base polymer material, with a density of 0.95 g/cm³32. Tungsten powder (W, Genesis, China) was employed as the metallic filler, featuring an average particle size of 4.36 μm and a density of 19.25 g/cm³33. Azodicarbonamide (ADCA, Juda, China) was chosen as a chemical foaming agent to generate temporary fine-cell structures, with a decomposition temperature of approximately 200 °C34.

HDPE/W/ADCA compounding and fabrication of foamable preform sheets

HDPE/W/ADCA composites were fabricated using a co-rotating twin-screw extruder (100T, Wanplas, China). The target composition of the final shielding sheets contained 86 wt% tungsten, with the proportions of HDPE and ADCA adjusted accordingly. To optimize the foaming and subsequent densification processes, the ADCA content was varied between 0.2 and 0.5 phr relative to the HDPE content35.

Before extrusion, tungsten powder and HDPE pellets were dry-blended in a mixer for approximately 10 min to ensure macroscopic compositional homogeneity. To achieve precise control over the decomposition and dispersion of the foaming agent, ADCA was incorporated as an HDPE-based masterbatch (HDPE/ADCA = 80/20 wt%) and introduced directly into the feed zone. Based on the final composition, the formulation was designed to ensure ADCA constituted approximately 0.36 phr relative to approximately 13.75 wt% HDPE, thereby facilitating effective foaming while minimizing excessive pore formation. The compounded HDPE/W/ADCA pellets were subsequently processed into sheets using a single-screw sheet extruder (SJ-10; Huadwmac, China) equipped with a slit die. Extrusion temperatures were maintained between 180 and 210 °C to promote partial decomposition of ADCA and initiate initial foaming, while ensuring melt stability and continuous processing. The take-up speed was regulated to produce foamable preform sheets with thicknesses of approximately 1.0–2.0 mm before the subsequent calendering process36.

At this stage, foaming was not intended to produce a final low-density foam, but rather to generate a temporary fine-cell structure that facilitates the three-dimensional rearrangement and uniform dispersion of tungsten particles within the HDPE matrix. The gas generated from ADCA decomposition locally reduces the melt viscosity and induces cell formation, thereby enhancing the spatial uniformity of the tungsten particles during melt flow and expansion.

Densification and final sheet forming via calendering

The foamed HDPE/W preform sheets were subsequently densified through a multi-stage calendering process to eliminate residual gases and reduce porosity. Calendering was performed on a two-roll mill (xy-200, Ouli, China), with the roll temperature maintained between 100 and 140 °C, above the softening temperature of HDPE but below the threshold for further ADCA decomposition. This temperature range ensured adequate deformability of the polymer matrix while suppressing additional gas generation. During calendering, applied compressive stress mechanically collapsed the foamed cells, expelling trapped gases toward the sheet edges and surfaces. As the HDPE matrix softened because of the rolling temperature, the cell walls fused and coalesced, transforming the initially porous structure into a nearly fully densified composite37. To minimize defects, such as cracking or delamination caused by excessive shear stress, as well as tungsten agglomeration or segregation, a multistage rolling strategy with stepwise reduction of the roll gap was employed38. Following the final calendering step, the sheets were air-cooled under slight tension to stabilize their thickness and minimize thermal shrinkage. The cooled sheets were sectioned into specimens with dimensions of 300 mm × 300 mm for subsequent evaluation of the mechanical properties and radiation shielding performance. The final target thickness was measured at five distinct locations on each sheet using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo 293 S, Korea), with the average thickness maintained at 0.30 ± 0.02 mm. Evaluation of metal particle dispersion and shielding performance

To examine the dispersion of metal particles within the shielding material, cross-sections of the fabricated shielding sheets were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). This analysis facilitated evaluation of tungsten particle dimension uniformity, identification of particle agglomeration, and characterization of the distribution and morphology of residual pores within the composite structure39.

The radiation shielding performance of the fabricated shielding sheets was evaluated using the experimental setup shown in Fig. 3. Measurements were conducted in accordance with the Korean Industrial Standard (KS) for X-ray protective equipment, specifically the lead equivalence test method for X-ray protective products (KS A 4025:2017)40. An X-ray tube (E7239X, Toshiba Electron Tubes & Devices Co., Ltd., 150 kV, 500 mA) served as the radiation source. Radiation dose detection was performed using a Radical Corporation Model 9517 radiation monitor paired with a Mo10 × 5–6, 6 cc ionization chamber (Radical Corp.), both of which were validated and calibrated before the experiments41. The X-ray source was operated at effective tube voltages of 60, 80, 100, and 120 kVp, varied in 20 kVp increments. All measurements were conducted at a tube current of 100 mA and an exposure time of 0.1 s (10 mAs). To enhance the accuracy of shielding efficiency measurements, radiation doses were recorded both in the presence and absence of the shielding sheet42.

Results

Microstructure and metal particle dispersion of the shielding material

The shielding sheets, fabricated based on the reactive densification model for radiation shielding, were produced in sheet form with a thickness of 0.30 ± 0.02 mm. The cross-sectional FESEM images of the final sheets are shown in Fig. 4. A low-magnification cross-sectional image of the shielding sheet is shown in Fig. 4a. As illustrated, tungsten metal particles are uniformly distributed throughout the entire region. Notably, despite undergoing both foaming and calendering, no significant localized agglomeration or separation was observed, indicating minimal spatial variation in particle distribution.

Visual assessment of metal particle distribution: (a) relatively uniform distribution of tungsten metal particles across the entire cross-section of the shielding sheet, with foamed cell structures formed during the foaming process observed throughout the region; (b) high-magnification image of the red boxed area in (a), showing residual pores between metal particles, highlighted in yellow.

This uniformity can be attributed to the three-dimensional rearrangement of the tungsten particles induced by the fine-cell structure formed during the foaming stage and melt flow, followed by partial pore collapse during calendering (while maintaining a relatively homogeneous particle network).

A high-magnification image of the selected region (red-boxed area) in Fig. 4a is shown in Fig. 4b. Pores and residual cell traces formed during the foaming process were observed in the regions highlighted in yellow, indicating that complete densification was not fully achieved and a small number of residual pores persisted following reactive densification. Although these residual pores accounted for only a minor fraction of the total volume, they may have influenced the local effective density and shielding thickness along the radiation path. Consequently, their presence should be considered when evaluating the shielding performance. In addition, the densities and volumes of the materials before and after the calendaring process were calculated, and the results are summarized in Table 1. Table 1 quantitatively presents the changes in microstructural parameters of the foamed PE/W shielding sheet (thickness: 0.3 mm) before and after calendaring. After calendaring, the porosity decreases from 16.21% to 10.15%, corresponding to a reduction of 6.06% points, while the density increases from 1.923 ± 0.012 g/cm³ to 2.537 ± 0.016 g/cm³, representing an increase of 0.614 g/cm³. These results indicate that calendaring partially collapses and densifies the pores formed during the foaming process while increasing internal structural compactness, thereby improving the effective density of the shielding material.

A standard lead shielding sheet with a thickness of 0.3 mm displays a high shielding efficiency of approximately 97.67% at a tube voltage of 60 kVp, as listed in Table 2. The shielding efficiency gradually decreases to 84.86% at 120 kVp as the tube voltage increases. Under identical thickness conditions (0.3 mm), the foamed HDPE/W shielding material achieved shielding efficiencies of 92.63%, 87.93%, 82.41%, and 78.80% at 60, 80, 100, and 120 kVp, respectively. Across the entire energy range, the foamed HDPE/W shielding material displayed shielding efficiencies of approximately 4.7–6.1% points (%p) lower than those of the standard Pb, corresponding to an average difference of approximately 5%p.

As the incident photon energy increased, a slight increase in the difference in shielding efficiency of the two materials was observed. This difference can be attributed to the lower effective density of the foamed PE/W shielding material, which results from the presence of residual pores, in contrast to the high atomic number (Z = 82) and high density (11.34 g·cm⁻³) of Pb. However, the foamed PE/W shielding material maintained approximately 95% of the relative shielding performance of Pb throughout the tested tube voltage range at the same thickness.

The results indicate that by effectively controlling the porosity and increasing the tungsten particle filling fraction through reactive densification, a shielding performance comparable with that of Pb can be achieved with only a minimal increase in material thickness. Specifically, the current findings demonstrate an average performance difference of approximately 5%p at a thickness of 0.3 mm. This suggests that increasing the thickness of the foamed PE/W shielding material to approximately 0.32–0.35 mm would enable it to attain shielding efficiencies equivalent to, or closely matching, those of a 0.3 mm-thick Pb sheet.

Furthermore, the reactive densification model predicts further enhancements in effective density and mass attenuation coefficient, indicating additional potential for improving shielding efficiency at the same thickness by further reducing the porosity and increasing the local tungsten filling fraction. These improvements can be achieved by optimizing the processing parameters, including the ADCA content, 13alendaring temperature, and applied pressure. These findings highlight the importance of microstructural control—particularly with respect to porosity and particle dispersion—in the development of lightweight, flexible, Pb-free radiation shielding materials, highlighting that these factors are as critical as compositional modification.

Discussion

The reactive densification approach introduced in this study was predicated on the understanding that time-dependent variations in porosity and effective density during the foaming and densification stages directly influence the radiation shielding performance. As shown in Fig. 5, typical phenomena observed during the mixing of conventional polymers with metal particles are presented, notably the agglomeration of metal particles, as shown in Fig. 5a,b. This agglomeration stems from the spatial constraints encountered during both the mixing of metal particles with the polymer matrix and subsequent thermal extrusion processes. Accordingly, as schematically shown in Fig. 6, achieving sufficient spatial separation between metal particles within the polymer matrix is essential for promoting uniform dispersion.

Spatial accommodation between particles observed in a polymer–metal mixture (A), showing displacement of the polymer matrix into the interparticle spacing between tungsten particles.

The porous PE/W preform sheets developed in this study were purposefully engineered not solely for weight reduction, but primarily to enhance radiation shielding performance. The initial porosity and tungsten particle distribution were designed to serve as scaffolds for the subsequent reactive densification process. During this process, the PE phase infiltrated the interconnected pore network upon heating, partially filling the pores and inducing localized density enhancement, which in turn improves radiation shielding efficiency43.

Experimental results confirm that the final shielding sheets, obtained by densifying the foamable PE/W preforms through multistage 14alendaring, demonstrated a relatively uniform dispersion of tungsten particles, with only limited localized residual pores observed in the FESEM cross-sectional images.

These observations are consistent with the reactive densification model proposed in this study, wherein the particle-associated porosity () increased during the foaming stage is progressively modified through mechanical collapse and particle rearrangement during calendering. This process ultimately determines the final local effective density() as a function of processing time. Particularly, microstructural analysis reveals that the cell structures generated during the foaming stage do not simply persist as residual pores in the final material. Instead, they actively promote three-dimensional rearrangement of tungsten particles, aided by the enhanced flowability of the HDPE matrix. This mechanism is crucial for achieving uniform particle dispersion within the densified shielding structure.

Furthermore, the residual pores observed in the final sheets were predominantly present as localized cells or fine micropores, rather than forming a continuous pore network throughout the matrix. This observation suggests that, although the reactive densification process does not achieve densification at the level of complete sintering, it effectively collapses the pores to a degree that does not significantly compromise the effective density along radiation pathways44. Accordingly, shielding performance evaluations validated that the model assumption—a reduction in porosity leads to increased effective density and linear attenuation coefficient—is consistent with the experimentally observed microstructure.

As demonstrated in the results, at a thickness of 0.3 mm, the standard lead shielding sheet achieved shielding efficiencies of 97.67–84.86% over the tube voltage range of 60–120 kVp, whereas the foamed HDPE/W shielding material demonstrated efficiencies of 92.63–78.80% over the same range. The consistent difference of approximately 5%p across all energy levels indicates that the effective density and mass attenuation coefficient of the PE/W shielding material developed in this study remained proportionally lower than those of Pb. Although the foamed HDPE/W shielding material achieved approximately 95% of the radiation shielding performance of lead at equivalent thickness, it offers several engineering advantages beyond what is reflected by the density and attenuation coefficients alone. The HDPE-based matrix notably enhances material flexibility, handling, and processability, while also mitigating health and environmental risks associated with Pb exposure45. In particular, for applications requiring repeated bending and movement, such as medical protective garments, curtains, and shielding covers, the proposed material is well-suited for overcoming the mechanical limitations of lead-based shields46.

However, certain structural and functional limitations persist when compared with fully dense lead-shielding materials. The presence of residual pores and localized microcells, as observed in this study, may compromise long-term reliability, create potential pathways for microleakage, or reduce shielding efficiency, particularly under higher-energy radiation conditions. To address these challenges, further refinement is required, including more precise control of foaming-agent content and decomposition behavior, optimization of calendaring parameters (pressure, temperature, and number of passes), and implementation of post-treatment processes, such as targeted thermal treatments, to minimize residual porosity and enhance densification. In this study, a single tungsten composition of 86 wt% was selected based on a final performance criterion emphasizing shielding effectiveness and was set to the maximum feasible filler content that could be incorporated into the polymer matrix. Future studies are warranted to extend the present model to a wider range of polymer and metal systems, to investigate variations in filler content, and to achieve more accurate quantitative predictions of effective density over time by incorporating coupled physical phenomena such as foaming-agent decomposition, gas diffusion, and viscosity evolution11,47. In addition, minimizing the effects of residual porosity and microcellular structures in the final sheet will require precise control of foaming-agent loading, processing temperature, and residence time, as well as the development of coupled physical models that account for the viscoelastic behavior of the polymer matrix. Furthermore, fine control of calendaring parameters, including roll gap, temperature, speed, and pressure, is considered essential.

In future process optimization stages aimed at product commercialization, it will also be necessary to quantitatively evaluate mechanical and wearability-related properties, such as tensile strength and cyclic bending performance, as a function of processing conditions. proposed model to various polymer matrices and metallic fillers, and incorporate more comprehensive modeling of coupled physical phenomena, such as foaming agent decomposition, gas diffusion, viscosity evolution, and time-dependent changes in effective density, to enable more accurate quantitative predictions of densification behavior11,44. Consequently, the proposed reactive densification model transcends post hoc interpretation of experimental results, providing a design-oriented framework that directly links processing parameters, microstructural evolution within the shielding material, and the resulting radiation shielding performance. If commercialized as a shielding-material manufacturing technology, the proposed process is primarily intended for applications in personal radiation-protection equipment used in medical institutions. In particular, it is expected to be applicable to products requiring repeated bending and movement, such as radiation-protective aprons, thyroid shields, and shielding curtains.

Conclusion

In this study, Pb-free radiation shielding sheets with a thickness of 0.3 mm were fabricated using HDPE/W-based foamable preforms combined with a calendering-based densification process, effectively implementing the reactive densification concept. The formation of fine-cell structures during the foaming stage facilitated a three-dimensional rearrangement and uniform dispersion of tungsten particles. Subsequent calendering partially collapsed the pores, thereby increasing the effective density. This process–structure relationship was substantiated through FESEM analysis and radiation-shielding performance evaluations. The fabricated PE/W shielding material demonstrated shielding efficiencies approximately 5%p lower than those of a 0.3 mm-thick Pb sheet across a tube voltage range of 60–120 kVp (lead: 97.67–84.86%, PE/W: 92.63–78.80%), corresponding to approximately 95% of the relative shielding performance of Pb. The reactive densification model thus established a robust framework for correlating foaming and densification processing conditions with final shielding performance. With further quantitative optimization of the porosity, microstructural characteristics, and mechanical reliability, this approach holds significant promise for the practical design of Pb-free radiation-shielding materials.
