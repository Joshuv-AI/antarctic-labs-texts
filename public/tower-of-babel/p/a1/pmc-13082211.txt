Hypertensive Urgency: Which Patient Will be Fine in a Quiet Room?
Authors: Goran Koracevic, Milovan Stojanovic
Published: 2026

Dear Editor‐in‐Chief:

Prof. Ji‐Guang Wang, Colleagues Prado et al. [1] should be applauded for their work documented in the recent paper in the Journal of Clinical Hypertension. They provided much helpful information. For instance, they found that female patients more often presented with headache and dizziness. Meanwhile, males more frequently had dyspnea or chest pain. It would be clinically very useful to focus more on several aspects.

In the emergency department (ED), very high blood pressure (BP) can (in the absence of antihypertensive treatment): (a) stay as it is; (b) decrease—because of reassurance and tranquilizer; (c) increase, due to stress (resulting from symptoms and fear) or because of worsening of the mechanism that induced/triggered BP increase. How did the authors choose whom to treat? Or the other way round: Who were the patients who did not need any antihypertensive medication to decrease BP, and how were the authors able to pick them up from the heterogeneous hypertensive urgency (HU) group? This is very important, because—by incorrect selecting—we can make at least three errors: One, if we give antihypertensive treatment without the need (because BP will anyway fall spontaneously), we risk hypotension (which may complicate further—proceeding to ischemia of various tissues); second, if we choose “watchful waiting” without any antihypertensive medication, we accept the risk of being late in BP lowering and patient's symptoms and/or BP may worsen and third, if we decide to withhold antihypertensive treatment and we detect acute hypertension‐mediated organ damage (HMOD) later on (using for example, thoracic computerized tomography), it means that we initially made a mistake by leaving this patient with hypertensive emergency untreated (although proper therapy would have been to administer antihypertensive drug intravenously).

The white coat effect is recognized widely [2]. It can be more severe in patients with HU than usual, due to higher stress. In the HU context, the white‐coat effect may explain partially the large BP drop without antihypertensives. Alternatively, the effect may progress and become even more prominent. Several guidelines on HTN recommend a quiet room for HU [3, 4]. Is this a solution for a third of our HU patients? Where can one find a quiet room in busy EDs? Which HU patient will be fine in a quiet room?

What was the percent (or per mile) of HU patients who experienced significant clinical worsening during observation / initial antihypertensive treatment, for example, did any new‐observed acute HMOD occur?

Was any drug (e.g., nitroglycerin, captopril, or nifedipine) administered sublingually? This is not recommended by guidelines, even more, short‐acting nifedipine is recommended against [5].

What were the lowest BP values that allowed diagnosis of HU in this large series? Did they differ in males versus females, particularly among young ones? This is important because females, particularly young, can experience the same symptoms and identical relative increase in BP (as a percent of main arterial BP), but at substantially lower absolute BP values.

Once more, compliments to Prado et al. for their expert synthesis.
