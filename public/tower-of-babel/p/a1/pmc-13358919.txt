Tuning the interfacial electric field via potential-zero-charge engineering for boosted alkaline hydrogen evolution
Authors: Zhicong Huang, Zixian Duan, Linwei Yuan, Yuzhou Cao, Xi Huang, Jinfeng Zhang, Junwei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water dissociation is the kinetic bottleneck of the alkaline hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and is strongly governed by the interfacial electric field. However, its precise and quantitative modulation remains challenging. Here, we report a molecular modification strategy to tune the interfacial electric field using the potential of zero charge (PZC) as a key descriptor. Pyridine sulfonic acid anchors onto CoS2via pyridinic nitrogen, inducing interfacial charge redistribution, while the hydrophilic sulfonate group modulates the local electrostatic environment through interactions with interfacial water. Potential-dependent surface charge analysis reveals a positive PZC shift of 40 mV, indicating an enhanced interfacial electric field under working potentials. In situ Raman spectroscopy shows pronounced changes in water vibrational modes, suggesting a reorganized hydrogen-bond network that facilitates water transport and dissociation. Density functional theory calculations correlate the PZC shift with a reduced water dissociation barrier (from 0.73 to 0.51 eV). As a result, the modified CoS2 exhibits an ultralow overpotential of 23.3 mV at 10 mA cm−2 and a Tafel slope reduced from 151.3 to 54.6 mV dec−1. This work establishes PZC as an effective descriptor for regulating interfacial electric fields and enhancing alkaline HER.

Introduction

Alkaline water electrolysis provides a cost-effective and materials-compatible route to sustainable hydrogen production,1–3 and has substantial potential for large-scale industrial deployment.4–6 However, its practical application still suffers from high overpotentials and limited energy efficiency, which reflect intrinsic kinetic constraints under alkaline conditions.7–9 Water dissociation is required to generate adsorbed hydrogen intermediates yet suffers from intrinsically slow kinetics, thereby imposing a major kinetic limitation on HER.10–12 Enhancing water dissociation efficiency has become a key challenge driving intensive research efforts in alkaline HER.

Water dissociation in alkaline HER is a complex, multistep interfacial process.10,13 Under applied cathodic potentials, polar water molecules are oriented and densely packed by the interfacial electric field, leading to the formation of a structured hydrogen-bond network at the electrode–electrolyte interface.14–18 Such a dense network dominated by highly coordinated water can hinder the transport of low-coordination and free water molecules to the electrode surface.14,17 Subsequently, the interaction between water molecules and surface active sites is governed by the efficiency of electron transfer at metal sites, which directly determines the formation of the key adsorbed H* intermediate.19–22 This electron-transfer efficiency is inherently reflected by the strength of the solid–liquid interfacial electric field.23,24 The interfacial electric field controls interfacial kinetics,25 exerting a decisive influence on the activity and efficiency of alkaline hydrogen evolution.26 However, the interfacial electric field remains difficult to quantify, despite its critical role for quantitative analysis and performance correlation in electrocatalysis.

The potential of zero charge (PZC) is defined as the electrode potential at which the free charge on the electrode surface is zero. At this potential, electrostatic interactions with solution ions are minimized. Therefore, PZC is generally independent of solution pH and serves as a robust descriptor for interfacial electrostatics.27–29 At an identical cathodic working potential, electrodes with more positive PZC values possess higher surface free-charge densities and thus generate stronger interfacial electric fields.30,31 As outlined in eqn (1), the interfacial electric field (Eint), the applied potential (Eappl), and the potential of zero charge (EPZC) are quantitatively related within the framework of the simplified Helmholtz-type electrical double-layer model.31 In this approximation, the relative permittivity (εr) is taken as the effective dielectric constant of the Helmholtz layer (εH), and the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) is partitioned into Helmholtz-layer (CH) and diffuse-layer contributions (Cdiff). Consequently, PZC governs the magnitude of the interfacial electric field and establishes a quantitative link between surface electronic properties and alkaline HER kinetics.30 On this basis, regulating the solid–liquid interfacial electric field through PZC engineering represents an appealing strategy for achieving highly efficient alkaline HER.1

Building on the above insights, we establish a PZC-centered strategy for interfacial electric field regulation, in which rational molecular design enables simultaneous electronic modulation of metal sites and engineering of interfacial hydrogen-bond networks (Fig. 1). Inspired by the coordination chemistry of aromatic heterocycles with transition metals,32–34 a molecular modifier, 2-(2-pyridyl)ethanesulfonic acid (PES), is anchored onto CoS2 to simultaneously optimize surface electronic structure and interfacial solvation environments. As a non-noble-metal electrocatalyst, CoS2 serves as an ideal model system owing to its well-defined pyrite crystal structure, uniform Co–S coordination environment, and relatively well-identified active sites.19,35 At the molecular level, the pyridine ring in PES—an archetypal ligand motif—anchors onto surface Co sites via coordination between heteroatom lone pairs and Co d orbitals,36 establishing robust chemical attachment. Beyond surface binding, the delocalized π-conjugated framework electronically couples with Co centers,37 imparting an electron-withdrawing effect that increases metal-site electron deficiency, shifts the PZC positively, and thereby strengthens the interfacial electric field under cathodic conditions. Concurrently, the terminal sulfonic acid group introduces a complementary mode of regulation by forming strong hydrogen-bond interactions with interfacial water molecules,38 effectively reconstructing the interfacial hydrogen-bond network and increasing the availability of free water for proton-coupled electron transfer (Fig. 1b). Notably, the chemically robust sulfonate moiety remains electrochemically stable under applied potentials, ensuring sustained modulation of interfacial electrostatics.39–41 This work establishes a PZC-governed mechanistic framework in which interfacial electric fields concurrently accelerate water dissociation and charge transfer, defining a general design principle for high-performance non-noble-metal electrocatalysts in alkaline hydrogen evolution.

Results and discussion

Characterization of PES-modified CoS2 and stability evaluation of PES

The CoS2 catalyst was synthesized via a two-step procedure. Specifically, a Co(OH)F precursor was first obtained under alkaline hydrothermal conditions, followed by washing and drying, and subsequently converted into CoS2 through high-temperature sulfidation in tubular furnace (see the Experimental section in SI). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns clearly identify the (111), (200), (210), (211), (220), and (311) planes of crystalline CoS2 (PDF # 41-1471, Fig. S1).19 Notably, no discernible changes in the crystal structure are observed after PES modification, indicating that the intrinsic lattice of CoS2 is well preserved. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that the needle-like morphology remains intact upon PES functionalization (Fig. S2 and S3). The HRTEM image shows clear lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.247 nm (Fig. S4), corresponding to the CoS2(210) plane, which supports the use of CoS2(210) as the surface model in the density functional theory (DFT) calculations. To verify whether PES was successfully immobilized on the surface of CoS2, attenuated total reflection Fourier-transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was employed (Fig. 2a). A comparative analysis of the FTIR spectra of pristine CoS2, PES, and PES-CoS2 reveals that no characteristic vibrational absorption bands are observed for bare CoS2 in the range of 1800–1200 cm−1. Subsequently, to elucidate the nature of the interaction between PES and CoS2, Raman spectra and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were conducted. In the 200–500 cm−1 region, CoS2 exhibits characteristic Raman bands corresponding to the symmetric bending vibration of the Co–S bond (Eg, 287 cm−1) and the symmetric stretching vibration of the S–S bond (Ag, 392 cm−1).43 Upon PES modification, these Co–S and S–S vibrational modes undergo pronounced redshifts to 285 and 384 cm−1 (Fig. 2b). This shift suggests that PES is chemically adsorbed on the CoS2 surface rather than physically adsorbed. Such chemical adsorption makes the Co sites more electron-withdrawing by inducing surface electronic redistribution. Consistent with the Raman results, XPS analysis further corroborates the electronic interaction between PES and CoS2. Compared with pristine CoS2, the Co 2p binding energy shifts from 782.0 to 782.2 eV upon PES modification (Fig. 2c), while the S 2p binding energy correspondingly shifts from 162.5 to 162.7 eV (Fig. 2d). These positive binding energy shifts indicate enhanced metal–ligand interactions and electron transfer from CoS2 to PES, indicating that PES chemisorption effectively modulates the surface electronic structure of CoS2. To quantitatively assess the adsorption behavior of PES, DFT calculations were performed using an implicit solvation model. As shown in Fig. S5, the adsorption energy of PES on the CoS2 (210) surface is calculated to be −0.77 eV. This value falls within the typical range of chemisorption (ΔGads < −0.41 eV).44 These results further support that PES is immobilized on CoS2 mainly through chemisorption.

The electrochemical stability of PES under alkaline conditions was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) using a Pt working electrode to exclude redox signals from cobalt sulfide catalysts (Fig. S6). For all tested concentrations, no PES-related redox peaks were observed within the tested potential window, indicating good electrochemical stability. Within the potential range of 0–1.5 V, the characteristic Pt reduction peak at approximately 809 mV remains unchanged with increasing PES concentration. Notably, the hydrogen oxidation (HOR) peak shifts from 26 to 160 mV as the PES concentration increases (Fig. 3a), suggesting that PES affects the interfacial water environment. At low concentrations, PES may facilitate hydrogen adsorption and improve HOR kinetics by reconstructing the interfacial hydrogen-bond network (Fig. 3c), whereas excessive PES coverage blocks active sites and suppresses HOR at higher concentrations (Fig. 3d). Accordingly, 0.5 mM PES was identified as the optimal modification concentration, at which the best HER performance was achieved (Fig. S7).

In the reductive potential window relevant to HER (−1.5 to 0 V), the electrochemical response is dominated by HER and no PES reduction peaks are detected regardless of concentration (Fig. 3b), consistent with the known stability of sulfonate groups under alkaline conditions. These results indicate that PES is chemically adsorbed on the CoS2 surface and remains electrochemically stable under HER conditions. This stable adsorption enables PES to continuously modulate the interfacial environment, with its polar sulfonate groups interacting with interfacial water molecules to regulate the local reaction conditions.

Interfacial hydrogen-bonding behavior and surface charge regulation of PES-CoS2

Motivated by organometallic chemistry, where aromatic heterocycles are well known to coordinate transition metals and modulate their electronic structure,32–34 we examined PES-induced changes in surface charge distribution to clarify how PES regulates the CoS2 interface and enhances HER kinetics. The potential of zero charge, an intrinsic descriptor of surface charge independent of solution pH, was determined using the persulfate reduction method.27,45 Owing to the limited stability of the PDS probe at high pH,46 the PZC measurements were conducted within the pH range of 1–4. Given the intrinsic nature of PZC, the values measured under these conditions are expected to remain applicable to the actual HER electrolyte, namely 1 M KOH with pH ≈ 14. In this approach, the reduction of negatively charged S2O82− depends on the electrode surface charge (Fig. 4a). At potentials positive, the surface attracts S2O82− and promotes its reduction, whereas at potentials negative, electrostatic repulsion suppresses it. Thus, the PZC is identified as the potential at which the current difference between electrolytes with and without S2O82− changes sign.46 Using this method, we determined the PZC values of CoS2, 2-pyridinesulfonic acid (PYS-CoS2), 3-(3-pyridyl)propionic acid (PPA-CoS2), and PES-CoS2 under different pH conditions (Fig. S8–11). The pristine CoS2 exhibited the most negative PZC value of −60 mV (Fig. 4b), while the organically modified CoS2 materials showed a notable positive shift in PZC, which correlates with the structure of the organic modifiers, as further analyzed alongside their electrochemical performance. The magnitude of the PZC shift is governed by both the molecular chain length and the nature of the functional groups. Among the examined systems, PES treatment induces the most pronounced positive shift, reaching −20 mV (Fig. 4e), suggesting an enhanced responsiveness of the CoS2 surface electronic structure to the applied potential. In contrast, reducing the chain length and altering the functional groups attenuate the positive PZC shift, as evidenced by the values of −50 mV for PYS-CoS2 and −30 mV for PPA-CoS2 (Fig. 4c and d). This suggests the general applicability of pyridine-based compounds in modulating the PZC of electrode materials and highlights the potential for precise tuning of PZC through rational molecular design.

To further elucidate how heteroaromatic compounds regulate the PZC, it is essential to analyze the charge density differences of adsorbed H2O at the solid–liquid interface, as well as the charge transfer during the reaction process. DFT calculations reveal that, after molecular modification, the interfacial electron density of CoS2 is increased, indicating enhanced electronic interactions that facilitate electron transfer (Fig. 4f). As a result, greater charge transfer occurs between H2O and the reaction interface, leading to the water adsorption energy becomes more favorable, changing from −0.33 to −0.46 eV. Meanwhile, the amount of charge transferred from H2O also increases by 0.03e (from 0.08 to 0.11e). Such electronic enrichment effectively strengthens the interfacial electric field and facilitates interfacial charge redistribution. These findings collectively support the feasibility of modulating the interfacial electric field of catalytic materials through the conjugated structure of organic ligands, providing a rational strategy for electronic structure and interface engineering in alkaline HER catalysis.

To clarify the role of PES in modulating the solid–liquid interfacial hydrogen-bonding network during HER on CoS2, in situ Raman spectroscopy was performed, with pristine CoS2 as a reference (Fig. 5a and b). Under cathodic potentials, both CoS2 and PES-CoS2 exhibit a broad O–H stretching band (3000–3800 cm−1), characteristic of interfacial water. Gaussian deconvolution resolves three components corresponding to strongly hydrogen-bonded four-coordinated water (4-HBW, ∼3200 cm−1),47 moderately hydrogen-bonded two-coordinated water (2-HBW, ∼3400 cm−1),48 and free hydrogen-bonded or K+ ion hydrated water (KW, ∼3600 cm−1).49 In this context, KW preferentially adopts an H-down configuration, enabling it to function as a co-catalytic species in HER by sustaining interfacial water availability and facilitating electron transfer, thereby accelerating the Volmer step.47 With increasing cathodic potential, both CoS2 and PES-CoS2 exhibit a progressive decrease in the 4-HBW component concomitant with an increase in the KW population. Quantitative analysis reveals that PES modification significantly increases the population of K+ ion hydrated water (Fig. 5c). Notably, under the same applied potential, PES-CoS2 exhibits a significantly higher proportion of KW species (increasing from 5% to 15%), indicating a more pronounced reconstruction of the interfacial hydrogen-bond network and a more efficient release of KW to promote HER kinetics. This interfacial reconstruction originates from the highly polar sulfonic acid group in PES, which forms robust hydrogen-bonding interactions with interfacial water and disrupts the originally stable hydrogen-bond network on CoS2.38 The ethyl linker further amplifies this effect by extending the spatial reach of the sulfonic acid group once the pyridine moiety anchors PES to Co sites, enabling hydrogen-bond network reorganization over a broader interfacial region. Although reconstruction of the interfacial hydrogen-bonding network increases the availability of reactive water, the intrinsically sluggish water dissociation kinetics on Co-based catalysts remains a fundamental limitation for alkaline HER. Building on the above analysis of surface charge distribution after molecular modification, the dissociation of H2O on different reaction interfaces was further simulated to more clearly demonstrate the promoting effect of organic modification on water splitting (Fig. 5d). Consistent with the charge distribution results, molecular modification significantly lowers the energy barrier for water dissociation on CoS2, decreasing from 0.73 to 0.51 eV (Fig. 5e). This reduction markedly accelerates HER kinetics, thereby enhancing the catalytic performance of CoS2. Collectively, these findings reveal that PES modification integrates interfacial hydrogen-bond network reconstruction with electronic structure regulation of CoS2. The PES-induced positive shift in PZC enhances interfacial electron transfer, while the reconstructed hydrogen-bond network increases the concentration of free water and lowers the barrier of water dissociation, thereby overcoming the intrinsic limitation of alkaline HER kinetics.

HER performance of PES-CoS2 catalysts

To evaluate the effect of PES modification on the HER performance of CoS2 catalysts, a series of electrochemical measurements were carried out. Furthermore, to preliminarily elucidate the structure–activity relationship of aromatic heterocyclic acid-type organic modifiers, additional CoS2 samples modified with PYS-CoS2 and PPA-CoS2 were prepared. This comparison enables systematic assessment of the influence of functional group identity and molecular chain length on the HER performance of CoS2. Fig. 6a presents the representative HER polarization curves of the different catalysts. Among all samples, PES-CoS2 exhibits the most favorable polarization behavior. Replacing the sulfonic acid group with a carboxylic acid results in lower activity than PES-CoS2 but higher activity than pristine CoS2 as observed for PPA-CoS2, followed by PYS-CoS2. Notably, all organically modified CoS2 catalysts outperform pristine CoS2, underscoring the beneficial effect of surface organic functionalization. The overpotentials required to achieve current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm−2 are summarized in Fig. 6b, providing a more quantitative comparison. PES-CoS2 delivers the lowest overpotentials of 23.3 and 193.4 mV at 10 and 100 mA cm−2. In comparison, PPA-CoS2 requires overpotentials of 38.9 and 204.4 mV, while PYS-CoS2 exhibits higher values of 51.3 and 214.4 mV. All modified catalysts show substantially lower overpotentials than pristine CoS2 (71.1 and 223.8 mV), clearly demonstrating the effectiveness of organic modification. Consistent with these trends, the Tafel slopes (Fig. 6c) decrease progressively upon organic functionalization. More importantly, analysis of the Tafel slopes indicates a change in the rate-determining step of the HER. While pristine CoS2 follows a Volmer-limited mechanism, organic modification shifts the rate-determining step toward the Heyrovsky reaction.50 Specifically, the Tafel slope decreases from 151.3 mV dec−1 for CoS2 to 54.6 mV dec−1 for PES-CoS2, indicating that organic modification accelerates HER kinetics on CoS2. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements further support this conclusion. As shown in Fig. 6d, the charge-transfer resistance decreases markedly after organic modification, dropping from 9 Ω for pristine CoS2 to 3 Ω for PES-CoS2. This substantial reduction in interfacial resistance highlights the enhanced electron-transfer capability induced by PES modification and further rationalizes the improved HER activity.

When benchmarked against reported alkaline non-noble-metal HER catalysts (Fig. 6e and Table S1), PES-CoS2 exhibits superior performance in terms of overpotential and Tafel slope. These results emphasize the significant potential of organic molecular modification as a rapid, low-cost, and effective strategy for boosting the activity of non-noble-metal catalysts in alkaline HER. The ECSA of PES-CoS2 is slightly higher than that of pristine CoS2 (17.8 vs. 15.1 m2 g−1), indicating an enlarged electrochemically accessible interface after PES modification (Fig. S12). This increase suggests that PES contributes to the expansion of the accessible surface area, which partially account for the enhanced HER performance. This interpretation is consistent with the positive PZC shift, further supporting the role of PES in regulating the interfacial charge environment of CoS2. In addition, the higher TOF of PES-CoS2 compared with pristine CoS2 at the same overpotential further supports that PES modification improves the intrinsic HER activity, mainly through interfacial charge regulation that facilitates water activation (Fig. S13). Long-term electrochemical stability tests (Fig. 6f) and continuous electrolysis over 200 h (Fig. S14) reveal negligible performance decay, demonstrating the robustness of PES surface coordination on CoS2 under prolonged operation. The characteristic ATR-FTIR bands of PES remain clearly detectable after prolonged electrolysis, confirming the excellent stability of the molecular modification (Fig. S15). The accompanying spectral shifts further indicate persistent PES–CoS2 interactions and continued regulation of the interfacial hydrogen-bond network.62,63 Furthermore, comparative SEM and XPS analyses before and after the long-term stability test show that PES-CoS2 largely preserves its needle-like morphology and overall surface chemical composition after HER operation (Fig. S16 and S17). Remarkably, the enhanced stability of PES-CoS2 is associated with the stable interfacial interaction between PES and CoS2. Chemically adsorbed PES molecules electronically couple with Co centers, while their polar sulfonate groups interact with interfacial water molecules. These effects help preserve the regulated surface charge environment and local hydration structure during HER. Taken together, these results show that pyridine-based organic acids enhance the alkaline HER performance of CoS2, with the activity governed by the nature of the functional groups and the length of the molecular chain. At the same chain length, sulfonic acid modifiers show higher HER activity than carboxylic acid analogues due to their stronger electron-withdrawing ability and hydrogen-bonding capacity, which promote interfacial charge redistribution and hydrogen-bond network reorganization.64 Increasing the chain length from one to two carbon atoms optimizes the spatial arrangement of hydrogen-bonding sites, leading to a more positive PZC shift and a higher fraction of free or weakly hydrogen-bonded water, thereby enhancing water availability and dissociation kinetics.65 These results show that the functional group and chain length govern the catalyst–electrolyte interface and HER activity.

Conclusions

In summary, this work establishes a unified molecular design strategy in which the interfacial electric field governed by PZC is rationally regulated through the synergistic coupling of metal-site electronic modulation and interfacial hydrogen-bond engineering. Molecular grafting of 2-(2-pyridyl)ethanesulfonic acid (PES) onto CoS2 enables the simultaneous tuning of surface electronic structure and the interfacial solvation environment. Comprehensive spectroscopic characterizations demonstrate that PES is chemically adsorbed on the CoS2 surface. Through conjugation within its delocalized π system, electron density is partially transferred from Co active sites to the pyridine moiety, thereby increasing the electron-deficient character of the metal centers. Stability tests further confirm that PES retains its chemical integrity under strongly cathodic conditions. In situ Raman spectroscopy reveals that PES modification induces a pronounced reconstruction of the interfacial hydrogen-bond network, facilitating the liberation of a larger population of kinetically active KW that are favorable for alkaline HER. Complementary PDS reduction measurements combined with DFT calculations indicate that PES effectively modulates the PZC of CoS2, increases the surface free-electron density, and concurrently lowers both the hydrogen adsorption free energy on Co sites and the energy barrier for water dissociation. Electrochemical evaluations, including LSV and EIS analyses, show that PES-functionalized CoS2 achieves a low overpotential of 23.3 mV and a markedly reduced Tafel slope of 54.6 mV dec−1, compared with 151.3 mV dec−1 for pristine CoS2. The cooperative interplay of these effects accelerates water activation and interfacial charge transfer, thereby substantially enhancing alkaline HER kinetics. Beyond the specific PES-CoS2 system, this study delineates a general mechanistic framework in which PZC-regulated interfacial electric fields bridge surface electronic properties and interfacial water structure. This framework offers a transferable paradigm for the rational design of high-performance, non-noble-metal electrocatalysts for alkaline water electrolysis.
