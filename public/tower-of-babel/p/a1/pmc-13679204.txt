Technical safety and efficacy of PFA catheter electrode for airway ablation in a rat model of COPD
Authors: Haoyang Zhu, Xiaoyu Zhou, Linbiao Xiang, Zhenwei Yang, Shiran Tao, Ziqing Chen, Ran Ju, Fenggang Ren, Zhanqin Zhang, Simei Zhang, Mengyun Ke, Huan Chen, Yi Lyu, Nana Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterized by progressive airway obstruction. While current pharmacotherapies provide symptomatic relief, they fail to alter disease progression. Pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a promising non-thermal technology capable of inducing selective cell death and achieving long-term symptom relief in COPD. However, its clinical translation remains hindered by unclear mechanistic foundations and the lack of specialized preclinical devices necessary for detailed mechanistic studies.Methods and MaterialWe developed a novel PFA catheter specifically designed for rat airways, featuring a platinum-iridium electrode ring (inner diameter: 1.95 mm) integrated with a dual-lumen PVC tube (outer diameter: 1.80 mm) to enable continuous ventilation during ablation. Finite element modeling was employed to systematically evaluate electric field distributions and thermal effects across voltage parameters. Safety was assessed in healthy Sprague-Dawley rats through continuous monitoring of hemodynamic responses, respiratory parameters, and tissue temperature changes. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in a cigarette smoke and Lipopolysaccharide-induced COPD model using histopathological analysis, pulmonary function tests, and arterial blood gas measurements at postoperative days 3 and 28.ResultsNumerical simulations demonstrated that increasing voltage (100–500 V) and pulse number proportionally expanded both the effective electric field distribution and ablation depth in airway walls, with no thermal injury observed within this range. In vivo validation confirmed that parameters of 200–300 V with 60–90 pulses achieved effective mucosal ablation, inducing epithelial necrosis and sloughing while preserving submucosal and cartilaginous structures without transmural damage. Vital signs monitoring revealed transient, self-resolving arrhythmias and hypotension. In COPD models, PFA treatment significantly reduced goblet cell hyperplasia and inflammation, restored normal epithelial architecture, and improved both pulmonary function and gas exchange.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that an easily fabricated PFA catheter can achieve complete epithelial ablation without transmural or thermal injury using optimized parameters. The procedure produced significant therapeutic improvement through reduction of goblet cell hyperplasia and restoration of pulmonary function, providing a validated platform for further mechanistic exploration and advancing PFA as a precise, non-thermal therapy for COPD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03821-0.Keywords: Pulsed field ablation (PFA), Catheter electrode, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(COPD), Airway ablation

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a leading global cause of mortality and chronic disability, is projected to affect nearly 600 million people worldwide by 2050 [1, 2], especially in aging populations with high smoking prevalence [3]. Current pharmacological management focuses on symptom relief but does not modify long-term disease progression [4]. Emerging bronchoscopic interventions, including cryospray, balloon debulking, and targeted lung denervation, aim to directly address pathophysiological mechanisms by reducing hyperplastic goblet cells and expanding airways [5]. However, persistent inflammation and epithelial regeneration often lead to disease recurrence [6, 7]. Innovative strategies that sustainably suppress goblet cell hyperplasia and hypersecretion are urgently needed.

Pulsed electric field ablation (PFA) is a non-thermal ablation technology that selectively induces cell death while preserving the extracellular matrix and minimizing collateral damage to adjacent tissues [8]. Beyond its established potential in oncology, recent clinical studies have demonstrated that PFA can effectively ablate hyperplastic goblet cells in COPD airways, resulting in sustained mucus reduction and prolonged improvement in airway patency for over 120 days [9–11]. Compared to thermal ablation, PFA is associated with lower risks of perforation and bleeding. Despite these promising clinical outcomes, the underlying biological mechanisms remain poorly understood, hampering clinical translation. A major obstacle is the lack of miniaturized ablation electrodes suitable for small animal models and the corresponding validation of their safety and efficacy. Although mice are commonly used in biomedical research, their extremely narrow airways make interventional procedures and the development of miniaturized electrodes technically challenging. In contrast, rats are a well-established model for COPD that have larger airway dimensions compared to mice, which facilitates the design and fabrication of miniature catheters and electrodes for airway interventions. Thus, the SD rat represents a clinically relevant model for advancing PFA research and electrode development [12, 13].

In this study, we present an easily fabricated electrode for PFA in rat. Computational modeling was used to analyze the effective electric field distribution, and in vivo rat experiments confirmed the feasibility and safety profile of the PFA electrode. Furthermore, we use a COPD rat model to demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy of this fabricated electrode PFA intervention. This work aimed to provide both a practical electrode design and a methodological framework for PFA application in SD rat airways, thereby establishing an experimental platform for future mechanistic studies into PFA-based treatment of COPD.

Methods and material

Methods overview

This study was conducted to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and therapeutic efficacy of a novel PFA catheter within the airways of a rat model of COPD. The investigation proceeded through four sequential phases. First, a miniaturized PFA catheter was specifically designed and fabricated to match the anatomical dimensions of the rat trachea. Second, finite element modeling was employed to simulate the electric field and temperature distributions, thereby identifying effective and safe ablation parameters. Third, the safety profile of the catheter and selected parameters was rigorously assessed in healthy rats through continuous monitoring of vital signs during in vivo ablation procedures. Finally, the therapeutic potential of PFA was evaluated in a cigarette smoke and lipopolysaccharide-induced COPD rat model, with outcomes assessed via histopathological analysis, pulmonary function tests, and arterial blood gas measurements at postoperative days 3 and 28.

Design of the PFA catheter electrode for rat airway

The anatomical parameters of the rat airway, specifically the diameter and depth, were measured by recording and calculating the mean values from the airways of 7 adult rats with a weight of 250 g±20 g. The mean airway diameter was defined as the mean value of the perimeter of the obtained airway specimen divided by π, while the mean depth was measured as the distance from the inferior margin of thyroid cartilage to the upper border of manubrium. The trachea exhibits an inner mean diameter of 2.15 mm (Figure S1A), and a wall thickness of 0.53 mm determined by average airway thickness measurements from 7 COPD-affected rat airways (Figure S1B). A Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) catheter (Lepu Medical, China) acts as the body of the electrode catheter. Its insulating properties and flexibility ensure the safety of both experimental animals and operators. The double-lumen design of the catheter maintained uninterrupted airway ventilation during PFA procedures. The catheter is engineered with two platinum-iridium alloy electrode rings (Moosen Biomaterials, China) positioned on its outer surface, functioning as positive and negative poles. This electrode ring has an inner diameter of 1.95 mm, with a wall thickness of 0.2 mm, resulting in an outer diameter of 2.15 mm, which precisely matches the inner diameter of the airway (2.15 mm). The length for the electrode is 3 mm, and the inter-electrode spacing is 2 mm. This design not only ensures that the effective ablation length covers half of the airway length, but also guarantees that the tissue specimens obtained from the relatively large ablation zone are indeed representative of the ablated tissue.

Numerical analysis

Ablation model establishment

A finite element analysis (FEA) model for tracheal tissue ablation, utilizing catheter electrodes, was developed using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1 software (COMSOL Inc., Stockholm, Sweden). Simulations were conducted under pulsed conditions with a voltage range of 100–500 V, a pulse interval of 100 µs, and a frequency of 1 Hz to determine the distribution of the effective electric field (EEF: electric field intensity exceeding 500 V/cm), electric injury (EI), and thermal injury (TI). For simplificity, the model comprised three components: dual electrodes, trachea, and skeletal muscle, with the electrodes contacting the tracheal inner wall (Figure S2A). Model geometry was based on rat airway anatomy (Figure S1) and electrode parameters. Due to the lack of layer-specific electrophysiological parameters, the trachea region (yellow in Figure S2A) was modeled as a homogeneous tissue representing the entire tracheal wall including mucosa, submucosa, smooth muscle, and cartilage. The surrounding tissue (pink in Figure S2A) represents the neck skeletal muscle and connective tissue (primarily sternohyoid and sternothyroid muscles in the rat), confirmed as skeletal muscle upon dissection. Although a small amount of fascia exists within intermuscular spaces, its precise location and thickness could not be quantified; thus, we neglected the fascia and used only skeletal muscle electrical parameters for simulation. Of note, this model is trachea-specific, not bronchial. The external tissue layer was set to 10 mm thickness to approximate an infinite muscle band, balancing simplicity and accuracy. The electrodes are characterized by an inner diameter of 1.95 mm, an outer diameter of 2.15 mm, a length of 3 mm, and a spacing of 2 mm. Animal care and ethics

Male adult SD rats, weighing between 250 ± 20 g, were procured and housed at the Experimental Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The animals were maintained under a standard light-dark cycle (12 h light, 12 h dark) and at an environmental temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, with unrestricted access to food and water. All animals were kept under uniform conditions. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experimentation of Xi’an Jiaotong University and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals Published by the US National Institutes of Health.The study protocols received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No. XJTU2024-444).

Ablation protocol for the rat airway

A square wave pulse generator (BTX ECM 830, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA) was employed to generate a single burst of electric pulses. Guided by simulation analysis results, we deliberately selected ablation parameters with an elevated electric field and an increased number of pulses (E: 1000 V/cm, pulse number: 90, pulse duration: 100 µs, frequency: 1 Hz) to evaluate the safety of PFA in airway ablation. Additionally, animal experiments were conducted to validate the efficacy and determine the optimal ablation parameters for specific combinations of these settings, thereby elucidating the effectiveness of the ablation and establishing rational ablation parameters. All other parameters were held constant (pulse duration: 100 µs, frequency: 1 Hz). Prior to PFA, atropine (0.05 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously to inhibit respiratory secretions, followed by dexamethasone (0.5 mg/kg) injected via the tail vein to mitigate acute airway inflammation. The same dose of dexamethasone was also administered to the sham group to control for procedure-related inflammation.For anesthesia, induction was performed with 3% pentobarbital sodium at 40 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection, and maintenance was achieved with isoflurane inhalation at 1.5-2% in oxygen. The procedure in this study was a single intervention lasting approximately 5 min, and no top-up anesthesia was required. During the PFA procedure, rats were manually ventilated, with tidal volume guided by visual assessment of chest wall excursion.Post-surgically, animals were placed in a warm recovery cage and observed until fully awake, then housed individually with free access to food and water. Surgical sites were monitored daily for signs of infection or dehiscence.

Electrocardiograph(ECG) and arterial pressure monitoring for the rat

After the induction of anesthesia, the rats were positioned on the tracheal intubation table to facilitate subsequent procedures. Electrocardiographic (ECG) monitoring was performed using a small animal electrocardiogram monitor (PowerLab DAQ, AD Instruments, Australia). Scalp needles were inserted through the skin of both forelimbs and the left hind limb of the rat, establishing connections to the monitoring electrodes (Figure S2B). Physiological parameters, including electrocardiographic readings and blood pressure, were systematically recorded at four stages: anesthetic state, during PFA, after PFA(1 min) and after PFA(10 min).

To record the arterial pressure, the rat is placed in a supine position with the groin shaved and disinfected. A 1–1.5 cm longitudinal incision is made to expose the femoral artery, which is carefully dissected free from surrounding connective tissue and neurovascular structures. Two sterile silk sutures are placed underneath the artery: the distal one is permanently ligated, and the proximal one is loosely looped for temporary occlusion. Following arteriotomy with micro-scissors, a heparinized PE-50 catheter (pre-filled with saline) is inserted and advanced 1–2 cm into the artery. The proximal suture is tightened to secure the catheter, which is then connected to a calibrated pressure transducer, and arterial pressure was continuously recorded.

Monitoring of respiratory rate and body surface temperature

The respiratory status of the rat was systematically monitored throughout the procedure, with continuous recording of respiratory rate and observation of respiratory mobility. Efforts were made to maintain respiratory stability to the greatest extent possible during the experiment.

For the body temperature monitoring, a high-definition infrared thermography system (FLIR Tools, USA) with a sensitivity of less than 0.03 °C was used. The camera was positioned at a standardized distance of 30 cm from the ablation zone with a 25 angle of inclination, capturing real-time temperature changes at a frame rate of 30 Hz. Prior to each experiment, the system was calibrated using a blackbody reference source with an accuracy of ± 0.1 °C. Temperature profiles were analyzed within a 5 × 5 mm2 region of interest, with the baseline temperature maintained at 36.5 ± 0.5 °C prior to ablation. Finally, a heat map was generated using proprietary software). Environmental conditions were maintained at 22 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 5% humidity during all experiments.

Histopathological staining

Histological evaluation of the airway epithelium was conducted on 5-µm paraffin-embedded sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was utilized for general morphological analysis (with nuclei appearing blue-purple and cytoplasm pink). Alcian blue–periodic acid–Schiff (AB-PAS) staining identified the goblet cells (with acidic mucins within goblet cells stained blue). Masson’s trichrome staining was used for differentiation of connective tissue (with nuclei appearing dark blue, cytoplasm red, and collagen blue-green). Immunohistochemistry for HSP70 was performed on paraffin-embedded airway sections. After standard antigen retrieval and blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-HSP70 antibody (Cat# YM8139, ImmunoWay; dilution: 1:600). Detection was completed using an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and a DAB chromogen. Histological images were acquired using a Leica DM series microscope (DM2500) equipped with associated Leica Application Suite (LAS) imaging software.

Establishment of SD rat model of COPD

COPD model rats were prepared as described in our previous study [23]. Briefly, 200 µg/kg (1 mg/mL dissolved in 1 mL PBS) lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was injected into the trachea (2 times/month). Fragrant smoke was continuously inhaled in a plexiglass closed box (30 × 40 × 50 cm) for 30 min every day, six vials/day, so the smoke concentration in the closed box was about 400 PPM(parts per million). This combined exposure to cigarette smoke and LPS simulates key pathological features of human COPD, including airway inflammation, goblet cell hyperplasia, and pulmonary function impairment.A pulmonary function test was performed at the end of the 28-day modeling period. One or two animals from each group were sacrificed, and airway tissues were collected for histological analysis with H&E and AB-PAS staining to confirm successful model establishment.

Pulmonary function test

The Whole Body Plethysmograph System (DSI BUXC, USA) was employed to assess various pulmonary function parameters in rats in an awake and unrestrained condition. Parameters such as enhanced pause (Penh), peak expiratory flow during breath-holding (PEFb), and mid-expiratory flow at 50% of vital capacity (EF50) were recorded in normal rats, COPD model rats and COPD model rats treated with PFA for 28 days.

Arterial blood gas assay

A heparinized syringe was used to withdraw 0.3–0.5 mL of blood( at femoral artery cannula), avoiding air bubbles. For blood sampling, the initial 0.1–0.2 mL is discarded to eliminate heparin contamination. The sample was promptly analyzed in a blood gas analyzer (Radiometer, ABL90, Denmark) for pH, pO2, pCO2, and SO2. Post-sampling, the catheter is flushed with heparinized saline (10 IU/mL) to prevent clotting.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0, GraphPad Software, SanDiego, California USA). All data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test before statistical analysis. Comparisons between two groups use the unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three groups use the one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Quantitative data are presented as the means±standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

The development of PFA catheter electrode for SD rats’ airway

Designed in accordance with the anatomical features of the rat airway, the PFA catheter electrode (Fig. 1A) consists of a platinum-iridium alloy electrode ring with an inner diameter of 1.95 mm, thickness of 0.2 mm, and width of 3 mm. It is integrated with a dual-lumen PVC tube (outer diameter: 1.80 mm; wall thickness: 0.2 mm), an enameled lead wire, a scalp needle, a power cord, and a connector. One lumen accommodates the lead wire, while the other serves as a ventilation channel, connectable to either a syringe for manual inflation or a mechanical ventilator. The lead wire is laser-welded to the electrode ring, which is then fixed to the PVC tube via UV curing. Two electrode rings are mounted sequentially on the tube surface as anode and cathode, with a 2 mm inter-electrode spacing determined by airway anatomy (Fig. 1B). Prior to in vivo use, the catheter’s electrical conductivity, insulation, and ventilation performance were validated. Conductivity was verified using a digital multimeter (DT92, CZDANG, China), and ventilatory function was assessed by submerging one end of the catheter in water while applying air flow through the opposite end. Results confirmed satisfactory electrical conductivity (Fig. 1C) and ventilation capacity (Fig. 1D).

Numerical analysis for electric fields distribution and estimated electrical and thermal injury

Based on previous research [23], we define an electric field strength exceeding 500 V/cm as effective for ablation, establishing the threshold for irreversible electroporation in cells. Numerical simulations revealed that increasing the voltage amplitude led to a corresponding expansion in both the distribution range of the effective electric field within the tracheal wall and the ablation depth (Figs. 2A, B). Figure 2C illustrates the three-dimensional distributions of the effective electric field (EEF), electroporation index (EI), temperature (T), and thermal damage index (TI) under various voltage and pulse number conditions. The results indicate that the volume of the effective electric field increased with higher voltages. Within the range of 100–500 V, no thermal damage was observed, as confirmed by the absence of TI; although the tissue area exceeding 43 °C expanded with increasing pulse numbers at 500 V, it remained below the threshold for thermal injury. As shown in Fig. 2D, ablation was confined to the tracheal wall between 100 and 300 V, with 300 V yielding the most complete ablation without transmural effects. Additionally, the number of pulses significantly influenced the EI area, with higher voltages requiring fewer pulses to achieve the same ablation depth.

Validation of ablation efficacy with different ablation parameters in vivo

Guided by numerical simulations, which indicated that effective airway tissue ablation could be achieved across multiple combinations of voltage and pulse number, we selected several representative parameter sets to treat the airways of normal rats. Histopathological examination performed 72 h post-ablation revealed outcomes largely consistent with model predictions (Fig. 3): ablation depth increased with higher voltages and more pulses. Since PFA selectively targets cells while sparing extracellular matrix, and given the abundance of resilient cartilage in airways, no transmural damage was observed in vivo, even under transmural electroporation conditions.

C–G Ablation groups with different PFA parameters: C 100 V, 80 pulses; D 200 V, 99 pulses; E 300 V, 60 pulses; F 300 V, 80 pulses; G 400 V, 60 pulses

The designed electrode catheter was successfully inserted into the airway of SD rats using a small animal laryngoscope, and accurate placement was confirmed with fluoroscopic guidance (Fig. 3A). Compared to the sham group (Fig. 3B), which showed only minor mechanical injury, ablation at 100 V with 80 pulses resulted in mucosal sloughing (Fig. 3C). Higher energy delivery (200 V/99 pulses and 300 V/60 pulses, Fig. 3D, E) produced near-complete mucosal epithelial removal, consistent with simulated transmural ablation. However, in regions with inflamed and thickened submucosa, ablation efficacy was reduced. Nearly complete mucosal ablation was achieved at 300 V/80 pulses (Fig. 3F) and 400 V/60 pulses, though the latter also induced cartilage fragmentation (Fig. 3G). Given the submucosal thickening and chronic inflammation typical of COPD airways, and to avoid basal epithelial injury, we recommend operating within the 200–300 V range. These parameters remain well below the standard electroporation threshold (1000–1500 V/cm), enhancing procedural safety.

Evaluation of vital signs during PFA operation on rat airway

Numerical simulations and in vivo experimental results identified 300 V with 80 pulses as an effective and safe ablation parameter. Vital signs were monitored using small animal ECG equipment. A slight bradycardia occurred during endotracheal intubation compared to the anesthetic baseline. During PFA delivery, transient cardiac rhythm disturbances featuring premature beats were observed; however, both heart rate and rhythm recovered promptly after ablation (Fig. 4A). Mean arterial pressure decreased significantly during treatment, likely due to reduced cardiac output associated with arrhythmias, but returned to baseline within one minute post-procedure (Fig. 4B). Insertion of the ablation catheter caused significant airway obstruction and respiratory depression. Manual ventilation was maintained through the catheter’s ventilation channel at a tidal volume of 3 mL and a rate of 30 breaths per minute. Upon catheter removal, respiratory rate transiently decreased for approximately one minute before returning to normal (Fig. 4C).Throughout the procedure, skin temperature overlying the ablation site rose modestly, with a maximum increase of 1.4 °C. Infrared thermography recorded temperatures between 29.4 °C and 31 °C within the PFA zone (Fig. 4D), confirming minimal body temperature effects during endoluminal ablation.

Histopathological analysis confirms the safety profile of PFA

Histopathological assessment of normal SD rat airways following PFA (300 V, 80 pulses) revealed that at 30 min post-treatment, PFA-induced epithelial electroporation resulted in mucosal hyperemia, edema, epithelial detachment, and marked submucosal capillary dilation. Importantly, the structural integrity of the residual submucosal layer, muscular layer, and cartilage remained preserved (Fig. 5A). By 72 h, extensive epithelial necrosis and sloughing were observed, accompanied by significant inflammatory infiltration, yet the overall airway architecture was maintained without perforation or significant hemorrhage (Fig. 5B). Masson’s staining further demonstrated that the basement membrane and portions of the submucosal matrix were partially preserved, and the layered structure of the airway wall remained intact both at 30 min after ablation and at 72 h when the biological effects of PFA were fully manifested (Fig. 5C). Additionally, immunohistochemical analysis showed no significant change in HSP70 expression—a sensitive thermal stress marker—in PFA-treated tissues compared to sham controls, supporting the non-thermal mechanism of ablation at the parameters used (Fig. 5D).

The efficacy of the designed electrode catheter to alleviate COPD

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the fabricated PFA catheter, we applied it in a COPD rat model. Rats were randomly assigned to the PFA treatment (300 V, 80 pulses) or sham (intubation-only) groups, with airway tissues collected on days 3 and 28 post-procedure. To ensure a valid comparison of long-term outcomes, the COPD (sham) group and the COPD (PFA-28d) group were evaluated at the same time point relative to the initial model establishment; specifically, the 28-day assessments were conducted at the corresponding time point after the sham procedure. Gross examination of dissected airways revealed that, compared to the pale pink appearance of airways in normal rats, COPD airways exhibited marked wall thickening, luminal mucus accumulation, and pronounced erythema and congestion. These acute inflammatory changes had mitigated by day 3 post-PFA, and the mucosal surface appeared largely smooth again by day 28 (Fig. 6A). Histologically, untreated COPD airways showed disorganized epithelium with significant goblet cell hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration. At 3 days post-PFA, widespread epithelial necrosis and sloughing were observed, alongside initial signs of regeneration and mild residual inflammation. By day 28, the epithelium was largely restored, with only a small number of goblet cells present, which was significantly lower than that in the COPD group (P = 0.0032 ). AB-PAS staining confirmed a significant reduction in goblet cell numbers following PFA (Fig. 6B, C). Consistent with structural improvement, pulmonary function tests showed a decreased Penh index and elevated PEF and EF50 values after PFA for 28 days, indicating reduced airway resistance and improved small airway patency. Arterial blood gas analysis further demonstrated significant improvement in gas exchange after PFA treatment, characterized by decreased PaCO2 and increased PaO2 and SaO2 (Fig. 6D). Collectively, these data indicate that PFA treatment effectively remodels the airway epithelium and enhances respiratory function in a COPD rat model.

Discussion

COPD is a prevalent global health challenge with no effective disease-modifying therapies [24]. The central pathology of goblet cell-driven mucus hypersecretion remains inadequately targeted by drugs [25]. As a novel strategy, PFA selectively ablates these cells. This selectivity is likely explained by differences in cell size—under the same electric field, larger cells are more sensitive to irreversible electroporation [23]. Early clinical studies indicate that PFA provides long-term symptomatic relief in COPD, an effect that may extend beyond the mere reduction of goblet cells to involve other, yet unexplored, disease-eradicating mechanisms [10]. Progress in elucidating these mechanisms, however, has been hampered by the absence of specialized ablation electrodes suitable for small animal models.

To address these challenges, we have developed an innovative catheter electrode system specifically designed for airway ablation in rats. The device demonstrated optimal luminal fit in adult SD rats, with airway diameters ranging from 2 to 3 mm. Notably, the double-lumen catheter configuration introduced in this study successfully enabled continuous gas exchange through independent ventilation channels during the procedure. This design effectively prevents asphyxia in rats and permits manual control of ventilation. The fabricated catheter electrodes were confirmed to possess excellent conductivity, insulation integrity, and ventilation capability (Fig. 1), enabling safe and accessible PFA application in standard lab settings. Pulsed electric field parameters are closely associated with the induced biological effects. High electric field intensity induces irreversible electroporation in cells, whereas low intensity typically results in reversible electroporation [26]. Moreover, increasing electric field intensity may cause not only electrical damage but also thermal damage [27]. Systematic investigation of these parameters through animal experiments would require a large number of animals, leading to inefficient use of resources and raising ethical concerns regarding animal welfare. Therefore, we initially performed simulation studies prior to in vivo testing. Through integrated computational and in vivo studies, we have established and validated a definitive set of ablation parameters (Figs. 2 and 3). Multiphysical simulation modeling has illustrated the voltage dependence of the ablated area, achieving complete epithelial ablation without inducing transmural damage at a voltage of 300 V. The voltage range of 100–500 V exhibited nearly no thermal effects, as evidenced by a non-significant increase in HSP70 (Figs. 2 and 5D). Furthermore, we delineated the interplay between voltage and pulse number, demonstrating that higher voltages require fewer pulses, thereby establishing an efficiency-safety framework for future research (Figs. 2 and 3). These well-characterized parameters provide a validated reference for immediate adoption in subsequent mechanistic studies, eliminating the need for costly and time-consuming preliminary optimization.

The safety profile of our PFA catheter was rigorously evaluated. During procedures, transient hemodynamic fluctuations were observed via ECG but consistently normalized postoperatively without intervention (Fig. 4A, B). While catheter insertion induced respiratory depression, necessitating brief ventilatory support, this effect was readily manageable. These findings underscore that the procedural risks are predictable and controllable with appropriate anesthetic and respiratory management. The therapeutic efficacy of PFA was substantiated through multi-modal assessment (Figs. 5 and 6). Histopathological analysis confirmed a significant reduction in goblet cell hyperplasia (Fig. 6A-C), while functional improvements were evidenced by enhanced arterial blood gases (elevated PaO2 and reduced PaCO2) and pulmonary function tests (reduced Penh) (Fig. 6D). Crucially, PFA achieves its effects through a non-thermal, tissue-selective mechanism that preserves the airway’s structural integrity, offering a superior safety profile compared to bronchial thermoplasty and a targeted, non-systemic alternative to pharmaceuticals [10].

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, due to the extremely narrow bronchi of rats and the associated technical constraints in electrode miniaturization, the ablation site in this study was the rat trachea, rather than the bronchi typically targeted in human PFA therapy for COPD. Therefore, future development of smaller catheters will be necessary to access more distal airways. Second, while the electric field simulations provided valuable guidance for parameter selection, simulation results may not fully recapitulate actual field distributions in living tissue. Therefore, simulation data should not be relied upon exclusively and must be interpreted in conjunction with histological findings. Third, we did not perform quantitative histomorphometric analysis of airway wall thickness or luminal diameter in COPD rats. Consequently, this study cannot provide a direct estimate of functional diameter reduction or the predicted increase in resistance. Furthermore, although mucus hypersecretion appears to be the predominant contributor to airway obstruction in our model, the mucus layer was not measured in vivo. Future studies incorporating quantitative histology and mucus assessment will be required to address these issues.

While the ablation parameters were established empirically and long-term durability remains to be fully validated, the present work provides a robust foundation for future investigation. Subsequent studies will focus on parameter refinement and on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying epithelial repair, thereby further strengthening the rationale for PFA as a potential intervention in COPD.

Conclusion

This study presents a readily fabricated PFA catheter and defines a safe, effective parameter set for targeted airway ablation in a preclinical COPD model. Integrated computational and in vivo analyses established that a parameter combination of 200–300 V and 60–90 pulses achieves complete epithelial ablation without transmural or thermal injury. The procedure, while inducing transient and manageable hemodynamic and respiratory effects, led to sustained therapeutic outcomes, manifested by significant reduction in goblet cell hyperplasia, improved gas exchange, and enhanced pulmonary function. By providing both a practical tool and a validated operational framework, this work establishes a foundational platform for future mechanistic investigations and underscores the potential of PFA as a precise, non-thermal intervention for modifying the pathological airway epithelium in COPD.
