Real-time interaction with artificial intelligence and its association with learning experience and academic performance among undergraduate nursing students in Peshawar: an analytical cross-sectional study
Authors: Husain Zakir, Hamideh Ebrahimi, Abdullah Khan, Ikram Ullah, Barkat Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) has been widely incorporated in the education domain for offering adaptive learning, personalized feedback and engagement to students. These technologies may help increase students learning experiences, as well as academic outcomes.AimTo investigate the relationship between the real-time interaction with artificial intelligence (AI) tools, learning experience, and academic performance among undergraduate nursing students.MethodologyAn analytical cross-sectional study was done in public and private nursing colleges in Peshawar, from August, 2025 till November, 2025. A total of 320 undergraduate nursing students from 1st to 4th year were selected using convenience sampling. Data were gathered by a validated questionnaire during visits in class. Spearman correlation and regression analysis methods were used to analyze the relationship between real-time interaction with AI (RTI), learning experience (LE) and academic performance (AP).ResultsReal-time interaction with AI showed a moderate positive correlation with learning experience (r = 0.547, p < 0.001) and the academic performance (r = 0.567, p < 0.001). A significant relationship between learning experience and academic performance (r = 0.667, p < 0.001) was also found. Regression analysis showed that RTI had a significant predictive power for learning experience (β = 0.606) and academic performance (β = 0.597).ConclusionLearning experiences and academic performance among nursing students were positively associated with the use of AI tools and their ability to interact with them in real time. Integrating strategies that support AI-assisted learning and promoting responsible use of AI may contribute to improved student engagement and academic outcomes in nursing education.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Real-time Interaction, Learning experience, Academic Performance, Undergraduate Nursing Students

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly emerged as a transformative development in higher education, reshaping how students access information, engage with learning materials, and develop essential academic skills [1]. AI systems are increasingly integrated into educational environments due to their ability to simulate aspects of human reasoning and decision-making, enabling more personalized and adaptive learning experiences [2]. By providing adaptive learning resources, real-time feedback, and data-driven learning support, AI technologies have the potential to enhance student engagement, skill acquisition, and academic performance across various disciplines [3]. In nursing education specifically, AI-supported simulations, intelligent tutoring systems, and automated case-based learning platforms have demonstrated significant potential to improve students’ critical thinking, clinical decision-making, and preparedness for managing complex patient situations [4]. These technologies may also strengthen important cognitive abilities such as analyzing clinical information, recognizing patterns, and interpreting patient symptoms, which are essential competencies for safe and effective nursing practice [5]. Recent literature has further emphasized the transformative role of artificial intelligence in nursing education. For example, De Gagne reported that AI can support personalized learning, enhance clinical skill development through simulations, and improve overall learning efficiency in nursing education [6].

Despite these advantages, the integration of AI into health-professional education raises several ethical, professional, and pedagogical concerns. Although AI can support clinical reasoning and reduce cognitive workload, it may also introduce challenges such as data privacy risks, algorithmic bias, issues of accountability, and overreliance on automated systems [7]. These concerns highlight the need for structured AI literacy education within nursing curricula. Furthermore, the educational impact of AI varies across different regions. While numerous international studies have examined the role of AI in improving students’ learning outcomes and engagement, evidence from developing countries remains limited. In low- and middle-income settings such as Pakistan, a gap persists between theoretical awareness of AI and its practical application in academic environments. Previous studies have reported that although a considerable proportion of nursing students possess basic knowledge of AI concepts, many lack the technical skills or institutional support necessary to effectively integrate AI tools into their learning processes [8, 9]. Institutional barriers, including insufficient faculty training and limited technological infrastructure, may further hinder the meaningful adoption of AI within nursing education programs [10]. Therefore, understanding how nursing students interact with AI technologies and how such interaction influences their learning experiences and academic outcomes is particularly important in these contexts.

Previous literature suggests that real-time engagement with AI tools supports self-regulated learning and enhances students’ learning experiences, particularly in technology-assisted educational environments [11]. In addition, a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials by Jembu and Lee indicated that AI-based educational interventions can improve student skills, reduce cognitive load, and enhance academic outcomes in health education settings [12]. Similarly, studies conducted in South Asia have reported that students who actively use AI applications such as ChatGPT demonstrate improved academic productivity, examination performance, and conceptual understanding [13]. Recent empirical evidence further highlights the effectiveness of AI-assisted learning in nursing education, particularly in developing specific clinical competencies. For instance, a quasi-experimental study demonstrated that AI-supported assessment combined with standardized patient-based training significantly improved nursing students’ therapeutic communication skills compared to traditional methods, with sustained effects over time [14] .However, students’ understanding of the ethical use of AI, its limitations, and its role in supporting learning remains insufficiently explored, particularly in contexts where institutional guidance and training opportunities are limited [15].

From a theoretical perspective, this study is informed by constructivist learning theory and self-directed learning theory, both of which emphasize the active role of learners in constructing knowledge. Constructivist learning theory suggests that students develop knowledge through interaction with learning environments, engagement in meaningful activities, and continuous feedback [16, 17]. AI-enabled learning environments can support such processes by providing real-time feedback, adaptive explanations, and interactive learning opportunities. Similarly, self-directed learning theory highlights that learners who actively manage their learning process and independently seek information tend to achieve better academic outcomes. AI technologies may facilitate this process by offering immediate access to information, personalized guidance, and flexible learning pathways that allow students to regulate their own pace of learning [18].

The conceptual framework of this study (Fig. 1) illustrates the hypothesized relationships between real-time interaction with artificial intelligence tools, learning experience, and academic performance. Guided by constructivist learning theory and self-directed learning theory, the framework proposes that greater engagement with AI tools can enhance students’ learning experiences, which may subsequently contribute to improved academic performance. Learning experience is conceptualized as a mediating variable in this framework, as enhanced interaction with AI tools may improve students’ engagement, understanding, and self-regulated learning processes. These improved learning experiences, in turn, may contribute to better academic performance by facilitating deeper knowledge acquisition and more effective application of learned concepts [19]. In this framework, real-time interaction with AI tools (RTI) represents the interactive learning environment emphasized in constructivist theory, where learners actively engage with content through feedback and dialogue. Learning experience (LE) reflects the internal learning processes, including engagement, understanding, and self-regulation, as described in self-directed learning theory. Academic performance (AP) represents the learning outcomes resulting from these processes [20].

Thus, this study advances existing theoretical frameworks by providing empirical evidence on the role of AI-mediated interaction in enhancing learning processes and academic outcomes. Unlike previous studies that have primarily examined general awareness of AI or isolated educational outcomes [9, 21], this study adopts an integrated approach by simultaneously investigating the relationships between real-time interaction with AI tools, learning experience, and academic performance. Moreover, while the application of AI in education has been widely explored in developed countries, there is a notable lack of empirical research in developing country contexts, particularly among nursing students. This study addresses this gap by examining these relationships within the context of Pakistan. Importantly, this study makes three key contributions: [1] it provides an integrated analysis of AI interaction, learning experience, and academic performance; [2] it offers empirical evidence from a developing country context where such data remain limited; and [3] it focuses on actual student interaction with AI tools rather than merely perceptions or awareness. Peshawar, as a key educational hub in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, provides a relevant setting where awareness of AI technologies is increasing but practical engagement remains limited. Overall, the study contributes to both regional and global literature by highlighting the integrated and context-dependent impact of AI on educational outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the relationship between real-time interaction with artificial intelligence tools, learning experience, and academic performance among undergraduate nursing students in Peshawar.

Methodology

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted in 4 nursing institutions in Peshawar, Pakistan, from August, 2025 till November, 2025. The calculated sample size was n = 320, calculated via Rao-soft (online-sample size calculator) based on the population of N = 1,455 students attending colleges included in the study, with the confidence level of 95% and the margin of error of 5%. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. This approach was selected due to the accessibility of participants within the selected institutions and the exploratory nature of the study focusing on students with prior exposure to AI tools. Although convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of the findings, it allowed for the inclusion of participants who had relevant experience with AI technologies, which was essential for addressing the study objectives.

Additionally, the inclusion of only students with prior exposure to artificial intelligence tools may introduce selection bias, as these individuals may have greater familiarity with technology and higher academic engagement. BSN students in 1st–4th years enrolled in the Northwest College of Nursing, Rehman College of Nursing, the Institute of Nursing Sciences (KMU), and the Postgraduate College of Nursing (PHSA), who had utilized at least one AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT or MetaAI), were included.

The AI Literacy Questionnaire (AILQ) developed by Ng et al. was used as the basis for data collection. The original instrument is a validated scale designed to assess students’ AI literacy across four dimensions: affective, behavioral, cognitive, and ethical aspects. It was developed based on the ABCE theoretical framework and validated through a rigorous multi-stage process, including literature review, expert judgment, qualitative interviews, pilot testing, and both first- and second-order confirmatory factor analysis. The results supported a four-factor structure with acceptable model fit indices, and the instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values reported above 0.90, indicating strong reliability and construct validity [22].

For the purpose of the present study, the instrument was adapted to focus specifically on students’ real-time interaction with AI tools. Therefore, a subset of 17 items relevant to students’ engagement with AI was selected from the original questionnaire. These items capture key aspects of interaction, including frequency of use, type of AI tools, immediacy of responses, and depth of engagement (e.g., asking questions, seeking clarification, and problem-solving).All items were measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from Strongly Disagree [1] to Strongly Agree [5]. The total score of the 17 items was used to quantify the level of real-time interaction, with higher scores indicating greater engagement. The reliability of the adapted instrument was assessed through a pilot study conducted on 45 participants, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The final questionnaire included demographic information, real-time interaction with AI (17 items), learning experience (10 items), and academic performance (10 items). Likert-scale responses were summed to generate composite scores for each variable, which is a commonly used approach in questionnaire-based studies. Likert-scale responses were summed to generate composite scores for each variable, which is a commonly used approach in questionnaire-based studies [23]. Academic performance was evaluated based on students’ self-reported academic grades obtained through the questionnaire. Objective academic records were not utilized, as variations in students’ awareness, usage, and level of interaction with AI tools may not be adequately captured through institutional records alone. However, since academic performance was assessed using self-reported data, the possibility of response bias or over-reporting cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Based on predefined cut-off values adapted from commonly used categorization approaches in Likert-scale and KAP-based studies, each variable was categorized as poor (< 33%), moderate (34–66%), or good (> 66%) [24].

The data analysis was done using IBM SPSS version 27. The demographic and study variables were summarized using descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations). The test of normality (Shapiro-Wilk) showed non-normality of the data, so correlations between real-time interaction with AI tools, the learning experience, and academic performance were analyzed with the help of Spearman correlation. Linear regression was then used to establish prediction relationships. In addition, to assess the potential presence of common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted by entering all measurement items into an unrotated principal component analysis. The results of this test are reported in the Results section.

The final dataset did not contain any missing data; it also included n = 320 completed questionnaires, which is in line with the STROBE requirements of complete reporting.

Association among real-time interaction with AI, learning experience, and academic performance -spearman’s correlation

The rank-order correlation of Spearman was used to investigate how real-time interaction with the AI tools (RTI), learning experience (LE), and academic performance (AP) are related in undergraduate nursing learners (N = 320), as shown in Table 3. The analysis showed that there are statistically significant and positive correlations between all three variables of the study at the 0.01 level (two-tailed). The experience when learning showed that there was a moderate positive relationship between real-time interaction and learning experience (r = 0.547, p < 0.001), which suggests that the higher the real-time interaction with AI tools, the higher the perceived experience of learning. Besides, the academic performance was positively correlated with the experience of learning (r = 0.667, p < 0.001), indicating that students who had a more positive experience with AI were also likely to report higher academic performance. On the same note, real-time engagement with AI tools was also moderately and positively associated with academic performance (r = 0.567, p < 0.001), which means that higher engagement with AI systems is connected with good academic performance. In general, the results indicate that the real-time communication with AI tools is not only meaningfully correlated with improved learning experience but also academic performance among nursing students, thus corroborating the hypotheses of the study and demonstrating the educational significance of AI-based interaction in nursing education.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate a significant association between real-time interaction with AI tools and nursing students’ learning experience and academic performance. Students who engaged more actively with AI technologies reported enhanced learning experiences, which were reflected in improved academic outcomes. These results highlight the growing role of AI as a supportive cognitive tool that facilitates reflective and interactive learning in contemporary nursing education. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred.

These findings can be interpreted through constructivist learning theory, which emphasizes that learners actively construct knowledge through interaction, feedback, and engagement with learning environments. AI tools provide real-time feedback, guided reasoning, and interactive support, aligning with constructivist principles and contributing to deeper learning processes [17]. In addition, Cognitive Load Theory offers further explanation, suggesting that AI can reduce extraneous cognitive load by simplifying complex concepts and providing immediate clarification, thereby enhancing working memory efficiency and facilitating deeper understanding [25].

The present findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the positive impact of AI-assisted learning on academic performance and conceptual understanding [26, 27]. In the context of nursing education, AI-based learning has been shown to improve clinical reasoning, knowledge retention, and self-efficacy among students [28, 29]. Furthermore, experimental evidence indicates that AI-supported assessment methods can significantly enhance clinical competencies while maintaining high levels of reliability and objectivity [14].

Despite these benefits, the relationship between AI use and academic performance should be interpreted cautiously. It is possible that students who are more motivated, digitally literate, or academically advanced are more likely to engage effectively with AI tools. Previous research has also highlighted that disparities in digital competence, access, and motivation may influence the extent to which students benefit from AI technologies [30].

From a theoretical perspective, Self-Directed Learning Theory further supports these findings, as AI tools promote autonomous learning by providing continuous access, personalized feedback, and adaptive support. Students who actively engage in self-directed learning tend to achieve better academic outcomes, which is consistent with the findings of this study [31].

However, the increasing reliance on AI tools raises important concerns. Excessive dependence on AI-generated responses may hinder the development of critical thinking and independent problem-solving skills if these tools are used as substitutes rather than supplements for learning [32]. Additionally, ethical challenges such as data privacy, information security, and potential algorithmic bias must be carefully considered [33, 34]. Therefore, AI should be integrated as a complementary educational tool within structured pedagogical frameworks that promote critical thinking, guided use, and responsible engagement [35].

These concerns are supported by recent evidence highlighting that, although AI offers significant opportunities in nursing education, its implementation is accompanied by important challenges, including issues related to academic integrity, data privacy, and the potential weakening of critical thinking skills. Furthermore, disparities in access to AI technologies may exacerbate existing educational inequalities, particularly in low-resource settings where infrastructure and financial constraints limit effective utilization [36].

In this regard, nursing education programs should incorporate guided AI use, reflective learning strategies, and supervised clinical simulations to ensure that students maintain independent reasoning and human-centered care. Institutions should also implement strategic plans for AI integration, including faculty development, student training, and structured AI-supported learning modules.

Furthermore, the successful integration of AI in nursing education depends on students’ readiness and digital competence. Previous studies have shown that although nursing professionals often demonstrate positive attitudes toward AI, their level of knowledge and preparedness remains limited, which may affect effective utilization [37]. Variations in students’ familiarity with AI technologies may therefore influence engagement and learning outcomes.

Finally, the findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s sampling approach. The inclusion of students with prior exposure to AI tools may have introduced selection bias, as these individuals are more likely to possess higher levels of digital literacy and academic motivation. Previous research has emphasized that the effectiveness of AI integration depends on students’ preparedness, skills, and institutional support [35]. Therefore, the results may not be fully generalizable to all nursing students, particularly those with limited exposure to AI technologies.

Limitations

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationships between real-time interaction with AI tools, learning experience, and academic performance. Second, all study variables, including academic performance, were measured using self-reported data, which may introduce common method bias, response bias, and social desirability effects. Although Harman’s single-factor test suggested that common method bias was not a serious concern, the potential influence of self-reporting cannot be entirely ruled out and may have led to an overestimation of the observed associations. Furthermore, the absence of objective academic performance indicators may limit the precision of outcome measurement. Third, the use of convenience sampling and the inclusion of only students with prior exposure to AI tools may have introduced selection bias, potentially affecting both internal and external validity. Students who are more familiar with AI technologies may also possess higher levels of digital literacy, motivation, and academic engagement, which could influence the observed relationships. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings beyond the sampled population should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the study was conducted within nursing institutions in a single city, which may further limit the applicability of the results to other educational settings or geographical contexts. Finally, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses, particularly in reporting positive attitudes and behaviors toward AI use.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a significant association between real-time interaction with artificial intelligence tools and improved learning experiences and academic performance among undergraduate nursing students. Students who reported higher engagement with AI tools also reported better understanding, increased motivation, and higher academic performance.

These findings suggest that AI-assisted learning may represent a valuable educational support rather than merely an additional tool. However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, these relationships should be interpreted with caution and do not imply causation. To maximize potential benefits, nursing institutions are encouraged to incorporate structured, ethical, and pedagogically appropriate AI-based strategies into the curriculum, with an emphasis on digital literacy and responsible use. As AI continues to shape education and healthcare, its thoughtful integration into nursing education may contribute to enhancing student learning and preparing them for technology-driven professional practice.

Future recommendations

The next step to strengthen evidence in this field is to conduct studies with larger and more heterogeneous samples of nursing students across different provinces of Pakistan to improve generalizability. Furthermore, to better understand causal relationships between real-time interaction with AI tools, learning processes, and academic performance, future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs.

In addition, potential mediators and moderators—such as digital literacy, motivation level, socio-economic background, and institutional support—should be explored to better understand the underlying mechanisms of these associations. Qualitative or mixed-method approaches are also recommended to provide deeper insights into students’ experiences, perceived barriers, ethical concerns, and challenges related to AI use in educational settings.

From a practical perspective, the findings of this study suggest that nursing educators should incorporate guided AI-based learning activities that promote active engagement while preserving independent critical thinking. Structured training sessions on responsible and ethical AI use may help students maximize benefits without over-reliance. At the policy level, educational institutions should develop clear guidelines for AI integration, invest in faculty development programs, and ensure equitable access to AI technologies to support inclusive learning environments. From a research perspective, further studies are needed to evaluate the long-term impact of AI-assisted learning and to identify best practices for integrating AI into nursing curricula in a way that enhances both academic performance and essential clinical competencies. Although the present study employed linear regression analysis to examine predictive relationships, the conceptual framework suggests potential indirect pathways, such as the mediating role of learning experience between real-time interaction with AI tools and academic performance. Future studies are encouraged to apply more advanced analytical approaches, such as mediation analysis or structural equation modeling, to better understand these complex relationships.
