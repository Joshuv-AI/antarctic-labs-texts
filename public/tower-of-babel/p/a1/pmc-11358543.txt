Environmental life cycle assessment of corn production in tropical regions
Authors: Hekmat Younes Hossein, Amir Azizpanah, Majid Namdari, Hamidreza Shirkhani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorn growing is an important cultural and traditional system in the Iran that has considerable environmental impacts. This study was conducted to determine the environmental effects of corn production using life cycle assessment methodology. In the present study, the data have been collected from face-to-face interviews and completing a questionnaire among corn farmers in Ilam province of Iran, in 2021. The functional unit for analysis was defined as one ton of corn produced in the region. As a result, energy efficiency, energy productivity, net energy and specific energy were equal to 4.41, 0.20 kg MJ−1, 675,379.84 MJ ha−1 and 4.88 MJ kg−1 respectively. Direct, indirect, renewable and non-renewable energies was 61.59, 38.41, 16.03 and 83.97%, respectively. In this study, the amount of global warming potential per ton of product produced is estimated to be 4747,505 kg CO2 equivalent. After normalization and weighting, the highest environmental pollutants are related to the marine aquatic ecotoxicity index with 225,273 kg 1,4-dichlorobenzene equivalent and the lowest pollutants are related to the depletion of ozone layer 1,19 × 10–5 kg CFC-11 equivalent. Result showed that chemical fertilizers and electricity led to the highest contaminating emissions.Keywords: LCA, Environment, Energy, Global warming, Sustainability, MaizeSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental impact, Sustainability

Introduction

After wheat and rice, corn is the most important crop in Iran and has received special attention because it has many uses for humans, livestock, industry, pharmacy, food industry and so on. Corn is the most powerful crop in absorbing and storing free energy on earth. For this reason, corn has been called the king of grains1.

The environment and agriculture are intertwined in a complex way due to its dependence on natural resources and processes. The environment can be affected both positively and negatively by agricultural activities. It has numerous negative effects, such as the overuse of agrochemicals, fertilizers, and non-renewable energy sources, the encroachment on land, the devastation of wildlife habitats, and the decrease of ecosystem diversity.2. It is considered increasingly unsustainable to produce and consume food in the manner it is currently being done. In response to population growth, declining arable land, and the desire for higher living standards, the use of high-energy inputs in agricultural production systems has increased significantly3,4. However, the dependence of agricultural systems on the intensive use of energy is one of the main causes of environmental problems such as groundwater pollution and global warming in developing countries5. Groundwater pollution can result from innumerable common practices, such as the improper use of fertilizers, pesticides, inorganic and animal manure, burning of fossil fuels, animal and agricultural waste; and the use of chemicals6. However, the most effective method for reducing environmental risks and energy costs is to increase production efficiency3. Increasing the ratio of energy production to consumption will improve production and productivity and lead to profitability7. Although the main goal of any agricultural system is to increase yield, but the ecosystem of agricultural systems should be able to ensure the sustainability of crop production in the long run, protection of basic resources, reduction of environmental pollution and ultimately the stability of production8. Therefore, it is clear that an activity must be economically and environmentally sustainable. Thus, the environmental effects of different agricultural activities must be analyzed by a range of spatial scales from the farm level to the national level.

There are various methods for assessing environmental impacts which can include Environmental Hazard Mapping (ERM), Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA), Multi-Factor System (MAS), Multiple Linear Programming (LP) Approach, and Environmental-Agricultural Indicators (AEI)9–11. The LCA method has been used extensively in research to investigate the environmental impacts of a variety of energy sources. With this method, it is possible to determine a product's total pollution, which can include air, soil, and water pollution12. Many researchers have used this method to analyze agricultural products' energy consumption. Cereal production was the focus of some of them. For instance, Iranian wheat production was the subject of Taghavifar et al.13 and Khoshnevisan et al.14. Ghasempour and Ahmadi15 studied the environmental performance of corn, wheat and soybean production located in the Alborz Province of Iran. They concluded that fertilization and electricity consumption are the most polluting steps for the three mentioned crops. Another study on forage maize in Tehran, Iran, showed that among environmental impacts, fresh water aquatic ecotoxicity had the highest potential to damage the environment16. Sadeghi et al.17 study aimed to compare different N fertilizer rates on the environmental impact of the production of four corn genotypes. They found that as N rate was increased, the emission rate of NH3, N2O, NOX, CO2, CH4, and SO2 increased, whilst corn yield did not always increased. In the case of Kermanshah, Iran, Yousefi et al.18 concluded that the reducing use of chemicals fertilizer and diesel fuel in corn production are necessary for better management of energy flow, global warming potential and environmental crises. An evaluation of the U.S. corn grain and corn stover life cycle assessment was conducted by Kim et al.9. In southwestern Australia, Biswas et al.19 presented a LCA analysis of wheat production.

Communities are becoming increasingly interested in agriculture and sustainable foods, which means that information (from farmers, policymakers, agricultural jobs, the media, public suppliers, and consumers) about agricultural systems, food chains, and food products will become increasingly important. This study examines corn production in Ilam province, Iran, in terms of energy consumption and environmental impact. Recognition and evaluation of energy efficiency in corn production will be done in order to review the current situation and provide appropriate solutions to improve this index and reduce the amount of input energy.

Materials and methods

Data collection and site description

Dehloran and Mehran tropical cities are the most important agricultural areas of Ilam province. Studied region located in the west part of Iran; within 31° 58′ and 33° 05′ north latitude and 45° 24′ and 48° 10′ east longitude. Rainfall averaged 291 mm and temperatures averaged 25 °C in the region. In 2021, Iran produced 668,369 ton of corn from 89,571 ha of corn cultivation. Among all provinces in the country, Ilam is the fifth largest producer of corn20. The possibility of cultivating corn has made this product one of the most important products of the province. In this study, the required data were obtained through direct visiting and oral questionnaires and on-site interviews with farmers in the 2020–2021 crop year. In order to determine the energy indices, Eqs. (4–6) were used, which included energy use efficiency, energy productivity, energy intensity, and net energy gain22.

As a measure of energy use efficiency, the calorific value of the output products is divided by the total energy consumed in the production factors. This item has no unit and shows the amount of energy obtained per unit of energy consumption for production. The energy intensity of a product indicates how much energy it takes to produce it, in which how much energy we have consumed per kilogram of product produced. Intensity of energy is inversely proportional to energy productivity. Energy productivity is used to compare two identical products23. Direct energy and indirect energy are the two types of energy consumed by the agricultural sector. Direct energy in corn production includes labor, electricity, fuel, oil and water for irrigation; and indirect energy in corn production includes seeds, chemicals, chemical fertilizers, machines. Direct energy is directly used at farms and on fields. Indirect energy, on the other hand, consists of the energy used in the manufacture, packaging and transport of fertilizers, pesticides and farm machinery24. Renewable energy also includes labor energy, seeds and water, and non-renewable energy includes machine energy, electricity, fuel, oil and chemical fertilizers25.

LCA calculation

According to LCA, all stages of a product's lifecycle are assessed in terms of inputs, outputs, and total environmental effects26. In order to calculate the LCA for the corn production system, the ISO 14040 method was used27. Thus, the four steps of LCA calculation were performed as follows:

First stage—Defining the objectives and scope of study: In this stage, the environmental effects of the corn production system were determined based on the consumption of inputs per functional unit (FU) in the climatic conditions of tropical areas of Ilam province. Functional unit was considered equivalent to one ton of corn grain28,29 and system boundary encompasses all inputs utilized and farm operations associated with corn production. Life cycle assessment of corn production from cradle to farm gate was investigated to calculate the environmental emissions. The graphic scheme boundaries of the corn farm system are depicted in Fig. 1. Corn cultivation involves preparing the land, fertilizing the field with farmyard manure, sowing the seeds, irrigating, fertilization with chemicals, managing pests and diseases, and harvesting using combine harvester.

System boundaries of corn production.

Step 2—Life Cycle Inventory Analysis (LCI): In this step, the environmental impacts affected by the performance of the corn production system were estimated. In fact, resource usage was calculated per FU and through emissions coefficients, the production system's pollutant emissions to air, water, and soil were quantified throughout its lifecycle. It should be noted that different sources were used in cases where specific information was not available for corn in the country10,28. As part of the present study, system inputs were actual farm practices and resources consumed, collected via on-site interviews and visits, with straw and corn grain yields as outputs. A conversion coefficient of 3.73 was used to convert gallon per hour (g h−1) to liter per hour (l ha−1). Fuel consumption specific of each tractor has been calculated based on working time and agricultural operation.

Emissions related to chemical fertilizers were calculated by emission factors proposed by the IPCC30. In the course of fertilizing crops, synthetic fertilizers generate ammonia (NH3), NOx, and N2O that are released into the air as air emissions. The importance of air emissions calculations in LCA has been well documented in published literature. There were different methods used to calculate direct emissions from fertilizers. In this study, nitrate (NO3−), ammonia (NH3) and nitrous oxide (N2O) emissions were calculated using guidelines of Brentrup et al.31 relies on methods developed by ECETOC32, Bouwman33, and other authors. Phosphate (PO43−) emissions calculation was performed using Nemecek et al.34.

Issues concerning the allocation of the environmental burdens typically arise when the process under study generates co-products, or a main product and by-products. corn grain is the main product, and corn straw is the by-product in the studied production process. In the present study, allocation was solved on an economic basis. Economic allocation may be the only feasible and suitable allocation method; therefore, we used economic allocation method in the present study35. The prices of product and by-product were considered based on market conditions in the year under study.

Results and discussion

Energy analysis results

The amount of energy consumed by each input for the production of corn and the results related to the calculations of the amount of energy consumed by different inputs are given in Table 3. According to the table, the highest energy consumption was related to nitrogen fertilizer and was equal to 61,296.21 MJ ha−1. Excessive use of nitrogen fertilizer is due to the high need of corn plants for nitrogen fertilizer during the growing season. The results also show that an average of 26.30 h of machine work per hectare is required in the field, about 50% of which has been done for tillage operations and with increasing the area under cultivation due to increasing the efficiency of machinery, working hours have decreased. On average, 934.26 L of diesel and 13.80 L of oil per hectare were consumed, which decreased in proportion to the consumption of these two inputs by increasing the area under cultivation and reducing the working hours of the machines. The application of chemical fertilizers of phosphorus and nitrogen in the field is done mechanically and decreases with increasing the area under cultivation at different levels of energy consumption. Also, seed sowing in fields with an average of 25 kg per hectare is done by machine and with increasing the area under cultivation at different levels of energy consumption is reduced. There is a high rate of fertilizer application in Iran's corn production, which is generally highly mechanized. According to Mekonnen et. al.38, fertilizer manufacturing (primarily nitrogen) and agrochemical production account for the largest share of corn production energy input.

The average yield of corn grains was 6028.81 kg per hectare, which in large lands increased production more than the average. As can be seen from the last column of Table 3, the percentage of energy of each input in relation to the total input energy is displayed. The total energy of inputs was 197,817.25 megajoules per hectare. The highest energy consumption was related to nitrogen fertilizer, fuel and electricity, respectively, with a share of 30.98, 26.59 and 20.57%, respectively, and the lowest energy consumption was related to oil, labor and chemicals, respectively. The share of each was 0.33, 0.34 and 0.58% of the total input energy in corn production, respectively. It was indicated that corn production in the region used a high amount of electricity, diesel fuel, and chemical fertilizers. Results of Kosemani and Bamgboye39, Mekonnen et al.38, Houshyar et al.40 and Jayasundara et al.41 confirm these findings, which illustrate the importance of electricity, diesel fuel, and chemical fertilizers in corn production. According to the study, energy efficiency can be improved most effectively through fertilizers, diesel fuel, and machinery management. In addition to a lack of nutrients in soil, a high price for organic fertilizers and a low price for chemical fertilizers, and farmer ignorance are some of the significant causes of high chemical fertilizer use42,43. Chemical fertilizer consumption can be minimized by selecting the appropriate source, spreading the fertilizer at the correct time, and applying it at the correct rate according to effective agronomic principles44. Using efficient machinery and tractors can also reduce the total input energy of the operation by reducing the amount of diesel fuel used43.

Global warming potential (GWP) is an important environmental impact to consider in the evaluation of corn production system. GWP refers to the potential of the system to trap greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, leading to climate change. Gases which contribute to global warming include carbon dioxide, methane and nitrogen dioxide. In this study, the global warming potential is estimated at 477.505 kg equivalent of carbon dioxide per ton of product produced. GWP consists primarily of emissions from off-farm sources (about 73%). During the raw material extraction, production, and transportation phases, off-farm emissions are generated. For the purpose of comparing the environmental performance of various production processes, the GWP is used as a general index26,45. Based on the study by Li et al.46 on corn cultivation in the US, GHG emissions in corn production amounted to 454.6 kg CO2 eq. ton−1. As reported by Kim et al.9, the GWP for corn in the United States ranges between 257 and 824 kg CO2 eq. ton−1. The type of electricity consumed between Iran and European countries may be the primary cause of the low GWP value. Natural gas accounts for the majority of Iran's electricity generation, with very little coming from renewable sources. According to another study, Liska et al.47 reported that GWP were 301.0 kg CO2 eq. ton−1 for corn production in US. Among other studies, the amount of GWP for US corn production was estimated as 726.6 kg CO2 eq. per ton of corn grain39. Ghasempour and Ahmadi15 argued that the GWP in corn production was 999.3 kg CO2 eq in Alborz Province of Iran. The value reported in their study is about twice as high as the value found in the present study. The excessive electricity use in the research area may be the cause. In the current study, the quantity of electricity consumed was 1698 kWh ha−1, compared to 3969 kWh ha−1in their study.

Figure 2 shows the involvement of each input in the impact categories. Significant share of the effects of depletion of fossil resources (67.5%), terrestrial ecotoxicity (65.1%), ozone layer depletion (57.6%), photochemical oxidation (52.5%) and global warming (46.2%) is related to electricity. Clearly, the use of electricity for corn production has a significant impact on the environment. As a result, it should be consumed in a manner that minimizes the risk to the environment. The majority of electricity consumed in corn production is used for irrigation, so it may be possible to propose new irrigation methods that will increase both water and electricity efficiency in corn production. Moreover, solar panels can be used to generate electricity for farms in order to meet their energy needs [21 Ghasemi-Mobtaker]. The role of phosphate fertilizer (56.3% in marine aquatic ecotoxicity, 51.7% in abiotic depletion, 28.6% in fresh water aquatic ecotoxicity, 27.4% in human toxicity and 16.1% in photochemical oxidation) and urea chemical fertilizer (29.3% abiotic depletion, 24.4% ozone layer depletion, 23.9% human toxicity, 22%, abiotic depletion (fossil fuels) and 20.9% chemical oxidation) can also be considered in these groups. The design of low-emission technologies must take into account the threats associated with chemical fertilizers in particular. Fertilization optimization (considering soil conditions and natural constraints) and crop productivity are important ways to reduce emissions from fields and to reduce raw material consumption.

Normalization and weighting results

This step is optional in life cycle assessment. Since after the classification stage, the importance of the obtained values cannot be realized, so in this stage, the share of environmental effects of the production system of the studied product in the total environmental effects of an area is determined. To evaluate the importance of the previous stage data, the classification index of each parameter of the previous stage is divided by a normalization coefficient. The normalization results are presented in Table 6 per ton of corn. The normalization results are the most reliable ecosystem indicator26. According to the normalization results, marine aquatic ecotoxicity has the most environmental pollutants based on the calculated weight. Phosphate fertilizer plays an important role in increasing this part as well. The results of normalization also showed that the potential for acidification, eutrophication and human toxicity are the next priorities.

Figure 3 shows that which inputs have the most role in impact categories. This diagram shows that phosphate fertilizer, electricity and urea fertilizer had the most role in the impact categories in the production of one ton of corn, respectively. The role of each of these inputs in the impact categories is also shown separately. Irrigation is the primary reason for such high electricity consumption in corn production. These results are consistent with other findings in the literature. For instance, the results of the current study indicate that chemical fertilizers contributed the most to corn production, followed by electricity. According to Pelton48, fertilizer use, particularly nitrogen, contributes significantly to GWP in the US corn production system. Holka and Bieńkowski49 found that the chemical fertilization process could have the greatest impact on greenhouse gas emissions. The Iranian rice production study conducted by Khoshnevisan et al.50 indicated that electricity and chemical fertilizers accounted for a substantial contribution to environmental pollution. To reduce corn production's use of chemical fertilizers, subsidies on fertilizers need to be removed, fertilizers should be used on the basis of plant needs, farmyard or green manure should be applied, and leguminous plants should be planted. Leguminous plants improve the environmental performance by reducing mineral nitrogen fertilization, soil carbon fixation, and total mineral energy inputs to the system51. Electric water pumps that are more efficient and renewable energy sources (such as solar energy and wind) that produce electricity could reduce the burden on the environment. Additionally, integrated farming would help us to produce corn more sustainably due to less energy usage and higher yield.

Conclusions

This study assessed the energy consumption of corn cultivated lands and the corn life cycle in tropical regions of Ilam province. Data were collected from 102 farmers using on-site interview and questionnaire. The results of energy analysis showed that the highest share was related to nitrogen fertilizer, fuel, electricity and water inputs with 30.98, 26.59, 20.75 and 13.57%. The average input energy was 197,817.25 and the output energy was 873,197.10 megajoules per hectare. The energy analysis demonstrate that energy use efficiency was computed 4.41 which indicates the energy efficiency of corn in the tropic regions.

The agricultural sector has a special share in the emission of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. LCA results showed that chemical fertilizers especially phosphate fertilizer also electricity had a significant effect on environmental emissions. The adoption of practices widely recognized as good agricultural practices could significantly reduce the environmental impacts of corn production. A number of practices can be recommended to reduce the environmental impact of cultivation, such as using processed poultry manure and modern irrigation systems, reducing excessive water consumption, and utilizing renewable energy sources.
