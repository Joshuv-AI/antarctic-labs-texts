The Role of Finerenone in Cardiorenal Protection and Urine Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio Modulation
Authors: Ajay K. Singh, Muhammad Shahzeb Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) have a high risk of cardiovascular (CV) and kidney complications, largely driven by inflammation, fibrosis, and albuminuria. Despite advancements in standard therapies, many patients remain at high risk, necessitating new treatments that target these underlying mechanisms. Finerenone exerts antiinflammatory and antifibrotic effects, demonstrating a more favorable hyperkalemia profile than steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs). Studies have demonstrated that finerenone significantly reduces the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), with many patients achieving a ≥ 30% reduction in UACR, which mediates the treatment effect on kidney and CV outcomes. The FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD trials, supported by the FIDELITY pooled analysis, highlight finerenone's ability to improve kidney and CV outcomes across diverse populations with CKD and T2D. Current guidelines, including those from the American Diabetes Association and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes, endorse finerenone for patients with CKD and T2D who have albuminuria and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 25 ml/min per 1.73 m2. These recommendations emphasize its role in reducing the residual risk of CKD progression and CV complications. Finerenone represents an important advancement in the management of CKD and T2D, offering substantial benefits in UACR modulation and cardiorenal protection. By examining its clinical efficacy and safety profile, this review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of how finerenone contributes to improved outcomes for patients with CKD and T2D, emphasizing its position as a pivotal component of contemporary treatment strategies.

CKD is a growing public health crisis, affecting approximately 37 million adults in the United States, as reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.1 This prevalence is expected to increase, driven by an aging population and the increasing burden of obesity and diabetes.2 CKD is characterized by progressive kidney dysfunction and an elevated risk of CV disease, which collectively contribute to significant morbidity and mortality.3 Although kidney failure is often viewed as an important end point of CKD progression, the majority of CKD-related deaths occur because of CV complications rather than kidney failure.4 Studies show that individuals with CKD are 16 to 40 times more likely to die of CV causes than to progress to kidney failure, thereby underscoring the profound interconnection between kidney and heart health.5

Albuminuria, a key biomarker of glomerular injury, serves as an essential indicator of both kidney and CV risk.6 Elevated levels of UACR correlate strongly with adverse outcomes in patients with CKD and are widely recognized as a prognostic marker for disease progression.7

Findings suggest that kidney and CV benefits arise from multifaceted drug-specific mechanisms rather than albuminuria reduction alone, necessitating therapies targeting multiple pathways simultaneously.8,9 However, achieving this goal in patients with CKD has historically been challenging, particularly because of the limited tolerability of traditional therapeutic approaches.

MRAs are a class of drugs that have demonstrated efficacy in reducing albuminuria and improving CV outcomes in patients with heart failure (HF).10 However, steroidal MRAs, such as spironolactone and eplerenone, require careful use because of an increased risk of hyperkalemia, particularly in patients with advanced CKD. This safety consideration necessitates rigorous potassium monitoring and appropriate patient selection, driving clinical interest in nonsteroidal MRAs (nsMRAs) with improved safety profiles.11

Finerenone, an nsMRA, has emerged as a promising therapeutic option. Evidence suggests that it exerts potent antiinflammatory and antifibrotic effects, demonstrating a lower incidence of hyperkalemia and treatment discontinuation rates than steroidal MRAs.12 Unlike its predecessors, finerenone demonstrates high selectivity and affinity for the mineralocorticoid receptor, allowing effective modulation of UACR with a lower incidence of adverse effects, including hyperkalemia.13

Clinical trials evaluating finerenone have consistently demonstrated its potential to enhance kidney and CV outcomes in patients with CKD and T2D. Notably, studies have shown significant reductions in albuminuria and a lower risk of progression to kidney failure or major CV events in patients treated with finerenone compared with placebo.14 Since 2022, finerenone has been included in the American Diabetes Association Standards of Medical Care in Diabetes, recommending its use in patients with CKD and T2D who exhibit elevated albuminuria despite optimal standard-of-care therapy.23 This endorsement reflects a growing consensus on the value of nsMRAs in addressing the multifactorial risks associated with CKD.

This review aims to explore the role of finerenone in cardiorenal protection, with a particular focus on its ability to modulate UACR. UACR

Albumin, a negatively charged plasma protein, represents approximately 10% of total body protein and 50% of plasma protein.6 It plays critical physiological roles, including maintaining plasma oncotic pressure and transporting a wide range of molecules such as hormones, fatty acids, and drugs, thereby facilitating their distribution and delivery to target cells.6 In healthy individuals, albumin filtration into the urine is prevented by the glomerular filtration barrier, a specialized structure consisting of the endothelial glycocalyx, fenestrated endothelial cells, the glomerular basement membrane, and podocytes. This barrier selectively restricts albumin passage while allowing smaller solutes to filter freely.6

The UACR is a well-established diagnostic and prognostic tool for CKD and CV outcomes.4 By normalizing urine albumin concentration to creatinine levels, UACR provides a reliable and practical measure of albumin excretion, mitigating the challenges associated with traditional 24-hour urine collection.7 Normal creatinine excretion is approximately 1 g/d; however, it can vary based on age, sex, muscle mass, and diet. Despite these variations, UACR enables accurate estimation of albuminuria, which is a hallmark of glomerular injury and systemic vascular dysfunction.

Albuminuria is categorized into 3 severity levels as follows: A1 (normal to mildly increased, < 30 mg/g), A2 (moderately increased, 30–300 mg/g), and A3 (severely increased, > 300 mg/g)24 (Figure 2). These thresholds, in combination with eGFR, are critical for staging CKD and guiding therapeutic strategies.24 Elevated UACR is strongly associated with increased risks of kidney progression, CV events, and all-cause mortality, independent of baseline albuminuria levels and other risk factors.Figure 2GFR and albuminuria grid to reflect the risk of CKD progression and requirements for monitoring. Conversely, early reduction in UACR ≥ 30% has been linked to mediation of the treatment effect on kidney and CV outcomes.9 Elevated UACR serves as an early indicator of endothelial dysfunction and glomerular injury, reflecting microvascular damage that often precedes overt disease manifestations.25 This predictive power is consistent across diverse patient populations, including those with hypertension, diabetes, and established CV disease, solidifying UACR as a universal biomarker for risk stratification and monitoring.

Measurement of UACR serves not only as a diagnostic tool but also as a prognostic marker, with higher levels indicating an increased risk of kidney and CV events. Interventions that reduce UACR, such as renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, and nsMRAs such as finerenone, have demonstrated significant benefits in delaying CKD progression and reducing CV risk.6,7,22

The association between UACR and adverse outcomes highlights its important role in assessing kidney health, monitoring disease progression, and guiding therapeutic decision-making in high-risk patients with CKD and T2D. By targeting albuminuria reduction, clinicians can address a critical driver of disease progression, thus improving outcomes for patients with CKD and its associated complications.

Cardiorenal Interactions

CV and kidney diseases are intrinsically linked, sharing numerous risk factors and pathological mechanisms. This interplay, often termed the cardiorenal axis, is underscored by albuminuria, a marker of glomerular injury that reflects systemic and localized vascular dysfunction. Albuminuria is prevalent in individuals with hypertension and diabetes—2 major contributors to both CV and kidney diseases.11 These conditions drive structural and functional changes in the vasculature and kidneys, leading to progressive organ damage and increasing the risk of adverse outcomes (Figure 3).Figure 3Summary of shared risk factors and pathogenesis between cardiovascular and kidney disease.26

In diabetic kidney disease, albuminuria is a hallmark of early kidney damage. However, its association with CV risk extends beyond diabetes. Albuminuria has a strong independent association with hypertension, a condition that affects > 1 billion individuals worldwide and is a leading cause of CKD and CV disease. A study involving 9198 primary care patients found microalbuminuria in 43% of those with hypertension and 51% of those with diabetes. The prevalence was even higher—58%—in patients with both conditions.12 These findings emphasize the significance of albuminuria as a common pathway for disease progression in the cardiorenal axis.

Hyperglycemia and hypertension are central to the pathophysiology of albuminuria. Both conditions induce hemodynamic changes that place mechanical stress on the endothelium of the glomerular filtration barrier. This barrier, composed of the glycocalyx, fenestrated endothelial cells, the glomerular basement membrane, and podocytes, plays a critical role in preventing protein loss into the urine. Persistent mechanical and metabolic stress leads to structural damage, including mesangial expansion, glomerular basement membrane thickening, and podocyte apoptosis or detachment.13 These changes compromise the barrier's integrity, resulting in increased albumin excretion and glomerulosclerosis.

Inflammation further exacerbates this damage. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers, such as cytokines and adhesion molecules, are strongly associated with albuminuria.14 These inflammatory mediators contribute to endothelial dysfunction, promote vascular stiffness, and accelerate the development of atherosclerosis.6 Thus, albuminuria serves not only as a marker of kidney injury but also as a predictor of systemic vascular disease.

The bidirectional relationship between the heart and kidneys is encapsulated in the concept of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, where dysfunction in one organ precipitates dysfunction in the other. CKM syndrome originates from excess or dysfunctional adipose tissue, which secretes proinflammatory and prooxidative products that lead to arterial, cardiac, and kidney tissue damage, alongside reduced insulin sensitivity.27 The pathophysiology of CKM syndrome involves a complex interplay of hemodynamic and neurohormonal mechanisms, including sympathetic nervous system overactivity, activation of the RAAS, formation of advanced glycation end-products, oxidative stress, lipotoxicity, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic systemic inflammation. CKM syndrome is classified into 4 stages based on the presence of risk factors and clinical manifestations, ranging from excess adiposity and metabolic risk factors (stage 1) to established CV disease with accompanying CKM risk factors (stage 4). This staging system emphasizes the progressive nature of the syndrome and the importance of early intervention to prevent progression from metabolic dysfunction to end-organ damage.27

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists have emerged as important therapeutic agents in CKM syndrome management. In patients with moderate to advanced CKD, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists use has been associated with lower annual rates of acute health care utilization; all-cause mortality (hazard ratio: 0.86, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.82–0.90, P < 0.001); and kidney events, including CKD progression (odds ratio: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.56–0.73, P < 0.001).28 Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists reduce major adverse CV events and provide kidney benefits through mechanisms including glucose-lowering effects, weight reduction, blood pressure modulation, and antiinflammatory properties.29,30

Endothelin receptor antagonists represent another therapeutic approach targeting the pathophysiology of CKD and CKM syndrome. Endothelin-1, primarily through endothelin A receptor activation, contributes to renal cell injury, proteinuria, inflammation, and fibrosis in CKD.31,32 Selective endothelin A receptor antagonists such as atrasentan, zibotentan, and sparsentan have demonstrated substantial antiproteinuric effects in clinical trials.33 The SONAR trial showed that atrasentan significantly reduced the risk of doubling serum creatinine or kidney failure in patients with T2D and CKD (P < 0.001).34 The ZENITH-CKD trial evaluated zibotentan in combination with the sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor, dapagliflozin, demonstrating a significant UACR reduction at 12 weeks by 27.0% (90% CI: −38.4 to −13.6; P = 0·0022).35

Chronic cardiac dysfunction, such as HF, often leads to kidney hypoperfusion due to reduced cardiac output and venous congestion. This hypoperfusion triggers a cascade of maladaptive responses, including activation of the RAAS and the sympathetic nervous system, which further impair kidney function and exacerbate albuminuria.15 Conversely, CKD can contribute to cardiac dysfunction through mechanisms such as volume overload, uremic toxins, and anemia, creating a vicious cycle of organ damage.

Albuminuria is closely linked to the development and progression of CV diseases. Studies have demonstrated a strong association between albuminuria and coronary artery disease36 as well as myocardial infarction.37 Two meta-analyses have confirmed that albuminuria significantly increases the risk of stroke.38,39 These findings highlight albuminuria as a valuable marker for identifying patients at heightened risk for CV events.

Beyond macrovascular disease, albuminuria is associated with microvascular dysfunction. In patients with diabetes, it correlates with reduced myocardial flow reserve, an indicator of impaired coronary microcirculation.40, 41, 42 This microvascular dysfunction contributes to increased CV risk and highlights the interconnectedness of kidney and CV health.

Albuminuria has been linked to other CV complications, including HF and arrhythmias. Studies have shown that elevated UACR is associated with worse outcomes in patients with HF, including increased hospitalizations and mortality.43, 44, 45 In addition, albuminuria has been identified as a predictor of atrial fibrillation,46 with its prevalence exacerbated in individuals with T2D. A population-based study in Sweden reported a higher incidence of atrial fibrillation in patients with T2D and elevated albuminuria, further emphasizing the interplay between metabolic and CV disorders.47

Other vascular complications associated with albuminuria include increased arterial stiffness, vascular calcification, and peripheral arterial disease.48, 49, 50, 51 These conditions not only contribute to CV morbidity but also reflect systemic endothelial dysfunction, underscoring albuminuria’s role as a surrogate marker for widespread vascular damage.

The consistent association of albuminuria with adverse kidney and CV outcomes highlights its importance as a diagnostic and prognostic tool. Regular monitoring of UACR in high-risk populations, such as those with hypertension, diabetes, or established CV disease, can aid in the early identification of patients at risk for progressive organ damage. Furthermore, interventions aimed at reducing albuminuria, such as RAAS inhibitors and emerging therapies such as nsMRAs, have demonstrated efficacy in improving outcomes. These findings underscore the need for integrated approaches to managing the cardiorenal axis, with albuminuria reduction as a key therapeutic goal.

Finerenone and UACR Modulation and CV Outcome

Finerenone Provides CV Benefit

Effective management of cardiorenal interactions requires a multifaceted approach. Key treatment strategies include decongestive therapies (e.g., diuretics and ultrafiltration) to reduce fluid overload, neurohormonal modulation with vasodilators and inotropic agents to improve cardiac output, and RAAS inhibition using angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, neprilysin inhibitors, and beta-blockers to mitigate kidney and CV damage.52

MRAs are central to the management of HF with reduced ejection fraction and left ventricular dysfunction following myocardial infarction.53, 54, 55 However, the use of steroidal MRAs, such as spironolactone and eplerenone, is often limited by the risk of hyperkalemia, particularly in patients with CKD.56, 57, 58 This safety concern has driven the development of nsMRAs, such as finerenone, which exhibit greater selectivity and potency for the mineralocorticoid receptor while offering an improved safety profile.59

Clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of finerenone in reducing albuminuria and improving CV and kidney outcomes. For instance, studies evaluating finerenone showed that it reduced serum potassium elevations compared with steroidal MRAs while maintaining similar therapeutic benefits.60 In addition, finerenone demonstrated significant reductions in biomarkers such as B-type natriuretic peptide and UACR, markers of both kidney and cardiac stress.60

In patients with T2D and CKD, finerenone has shown consistent efficacy across multiple end points. Studies demonstrated significant reductions in UACR with finerenone use, reflecting its kidney-protective effects.17,18 In the FIDELIO-DKD study, finerenone significantly reduced the risk of kidney failure, a sustained decline in eGFR, CV death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and hospitalization for HF.19,20 These findings underscore the role of albuminuria reduction as a mediator of kidney protection, highlighting finerenone’s potential to address the residual risk of CKD progression and CV events.

Similarly, finerenone reduced major adverse CV events in the FIGARO-DKD study, although the effects on kidney outcomes were less pronounced than those observed in FIDELIO-DKD.20,21 The pooled analysis from the FIDELITY study, comprising 13,026 patients from FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD, further confirmed these benefits.22 The pooled analysis demonstrated that finerenone significantly reduced the composite CV outcome (hazard ratio: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.78–0.95; P = 0.0018) compared with placebo. For kidney outcomes, finerenone reduced the composite end point of kidney failure, sustained ≥57% decrease in eGFR from baseline, or kidney death (hazard ratio: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.67–0.88; P < 0.001).22 These findings suggest that finerenone’s antiinflammatory and antifibrotic properties play a central role in its therapeutic effects.

In the FIDELIO-DKD trial, treatment-emergent mild hyperkalemia (serum K+ > 5.5 mmol/l) occurred in 21.4% of patients receiving finerenone compared with 9.2% receiving placebo. Moderate hyperkalemia (serum K+ > 6.0 mmol/l) occurred in 4.5% of the finerenone group versus 1.4% in the placebo group.20 The pooled FIDELITY analysis confirmed these findings, with laboratory-detected hyperkalemia occurring in 16.5% of finerenone patients versus 7.7% of placebo patients, representing approximately a 2-fold increased risk (risk ratio: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.88–2.27).22

Finerenone Modulates UACR

Post hoc analyses of major clinical trials have highlighted the role of finerenone in reducing UACR and its subsequent impact on kidney and CV outcomes. Agarwal et al. demonstrated that finerenone-induced reductions in UACR significantly mediated long-term kidney outcomes and modestly affected CV outcomes in patients with CKD and T2D.9 Patients who achieved a ≥ 30% reduction in UACR experienced fewer kidney and CV events than those with less pronounced reductions.61 This finding underscores the therapeutic importance of targeting UACR reduction as a primary goal in managing CKD.

Importantly, the benefits of finerenone were particularly evident in patients with higher baseline UACR levels (≥ 300 mg/g),62 who exhibited substantial kidney protection following treatment. These results emphasize the value of UACR as both a prognostic marker and a therapeutic target in high-risk populations. By reducing albuminuria, finerenone addresses one of the critical drivers of CKD progression, offering significant protection against kidney failure and other adverse kidney outcomes.

Furthermore, finerenone reduced the risk of hospitalization for HF and other major adverse CV events, reflecting its role in mitigating the systemic effects of chronic inflammation and fibrosis. These mechanisms are closely linked to albuminuria, further reinforcing the interconnection between kidney and CV health.

The therapeutic potential of finerenone has been incorporated into recent clinical guidelines. Since 2022, the American Diabetes Association guidelines have recommended the use of nsMRAs, such as finerenone, in patients with CKD who are at elevated risk for CV events or CKD progression (evidence grade A).63 Similarly, a consensus report from the American Diabetes Association and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes advocates the use of nsMRAs in patients with T2D, albuminuria, and an eGFR ≥ 25 ml/min per 1.73 m2, provided their serum potassium levels are within the normal range.64

These recommendations highlight the emerging role of finerenone as a cornerstone therapy in the management of CKD and T2D. Its ability to reduce UACR, coupled with its favorable safety profile, makes it a valuable addition to the therapeutic armamentarium for this high-risk population. Recent meta-analyses have further confirmed finerenone's efficacy in reducing albuminuria and improving kidney outcomes. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 4 randomized controlled trials involving 13,943 participants demonstrated that finerenone treatment significantly decreased UACR from baseline compared with placebo (mean difference: −0.30; 95% CI: −0.32 to −0.28; P < 0.00001), with more pronounced reductions observed with treatment duration > 3 months.73 The same analysis showed that finerenone reduced the risk of eGFR decline ≥40% from baseline by 15% (risk ratio: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.78–0.93; P < 0.05).73

Combination Therapy

The CONFIDENCE trial enrolled 818 patients with CKD and T2D to evaluate whether early dual therapy with finerenone and the sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor, empagliflozin, provides superior UACR reduction compared with either agent alone.71 At 180 days, the combination therapy resulted in a UACR reduction that was 29% greater than with finerenone alone and 32% greater than with empagliflozin alone. These reductions were evident as early as day 14 and exceeded 40% by day 90.74 In addition, the study findings demonstrated that the incidence of hyperkalemia was lower with combination therapy (9.3%) than with finerenone monotherapy (11.4%), while empagliflozin alone had the lowest rate (3.8%). Hyperkalemia caused discontinuation in 1 patient per group; no hospitalizations or serious events were reported. Symptomatic hypotension was rare (1.1%) in the combination arm, and acute kidney injury was uncommon.74

Conclusion

Finerenone has emerged as a key therapy in the management of CKD and T2D, offering significant reductions in UACR and addressing the residual risk of kidney and CV complications. Its favorable safety profile and proven efficacy highlight its role as an essential component of treatment strategies for high-risk patients. Further research is warranted to explore its long-term impact and integration with existing therapies to optimize outcomes.
