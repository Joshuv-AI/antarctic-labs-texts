Enable preclinical, high‐precision image‐guided radiotherapy research with a stand‐alone small animal IGRT system
Authors: Wei Deng, Dong Han, Kevin E. Byrne, Binny Bhandary, Kai Jiang, Yannick P. Poirier, Sina Mossahebi, Junliang Xu, Hem Shukla, Amit Sawant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany preclinical studies involving novel particle radiotherapy techniques have been conducted without precise image guidance due to the lack of an image‐guided small animal radiotherapy research platform for them, such as ultra‐high dose rate radiation (UHDR), mini‐beam, and grid therapy. This limitation restricts the complexity of research questions that can be effectively addressed.PurposeWe developed a workflow to utilize 3D imaging on a Small Animal Radiation Research Platform (SARRP) for precise targeting of internal regions in small animals with a separated treatment beam.MethodsOur approach mimics the workflow of patient treatment to decouple the imaging process from the radiation system. The SARRP cone‐beam CT system was used to image and center the target. Alignment markers were then added to the top surface of the animal platform using the SARRP's well‐calibrated laser system, which was consequently transferred and aligned to a collimator in a clinical treatment room. Validation of the method was performed using BB targets within both a mouse phantom and a euthanized rat. The distance between the center of the radiation field and the BB was measured to determine the setup error for the entire procedure. The use of a collimator reduces the impact of setup uncertainty in the treatment room.ResultsOur approach achieved a setup accuracy of approximately 0.5 mm, ensuring the target was consistently positioned at the center of the collimated radiation field center after transferring from the SARRP system. This is comparable to the isocentricity of the SARRP itself (∼±0.5 mm).ConclusionsBy decoupling the imaging and radiation systems while maintaining sub‐millimeter setup accuracy, this workflow introduces flexibility for conducting high‐precision, image‐guided preclinical studies across different radiation modalities and techniques, for example, protons, electrons, neutrons, UHDR (FLASH), Small‐Field‐RT, etc. The workflow uses simple equipment and can be easily implemented by other centers.

INTRODUCTION

Image‐guided small animal systems are critical tools for preclinical studies in radiation research. Existing commercial products predominantly rely on X‐ray platforms, such as the Small Animal Radiation Research Platform (SARRP) (Xstrahl, Suwanee GA) and the X‐RAD 225Cx (Precision X‐ray, Madison CT). With the advancements in radiation therapy, proton and heavy ion therapies have gained increasing prominence in cancer treatment. 1 , 2 , 3 While numerous in vitro and in vivo experiments have explored the radiobiological properties of proton and heavy particle radiation, 4 , 5 several unresolved biological questions remain due to the absence of a high‐precision image‐guided small animal particle therapy platform. Such questions include uncertainties surrounding the linear energy transfer (LET)‐related relative biological effectiveness (RBE) at the proton distal edge, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 patterns of DNA methylation, and differential immune responses (Girdhani et al., 10 ).

In parallel, emerging technologies such as ultra‐high dose rate radiation (UHDR) therapy and spatially fractionated radiation therapy (SFRT, such as minibeam and grid therapy) have been introduced and are being extensively studied for both preclinical research and clinical trials. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 However, the lack of a precise image‐guided UHDR radiation system for small animals has limited the scope of targeted preclinical studies of the FLASH effect, particularly in transgenic animal models. Therefore, developing high‐precision image‐guided systems for small animal irradiation across different radiation modalities (e.g., proton and heavy ions) is essential for the advancement of cutting‐edge radiation therapy techniques (e.g., UHDR, SFRT).

In recent years, several research centers have integrated a SARRP system with a proton beam nozzle within the same treatment room, utilizing well‐aligned solid tracking and alignment techniques. 16 , 17 , 18 These systems have provided immense value for conducting precise image‐guided proton radiotherapy research in small animals. Nevertheless, these approaches dedicate the whole small animal system to a particular radiation source and are thus not suited to cross‐modality studies. Moreover, the requirement to house the SARRP imaging system within the same treatment room as the therapy beam source restricts its usability to non‐clinical treatment rooms.

In this study, we referred to the patient treatment process to propose a novel approach which decoupled the SARRP cone beam CT imaging system from the radiation source while maintaining sub‐millimeter accuracy in targeting, thus enabling precise and flexible image‐guided irradiations of small animals for different radiation modalities and novel radiotherapy techniques, for example, protons, electrons, neutrons, UHDR (FLASH), SFRT, etc. The workflow uses simple equipment and can be easily implemented by other centers.

METHODS

Unlike most traditional approaches, which align the SARRP system with external radiation sources using various accessories and modifications, we aimed to develop and test a workflow that decouples the SARRP from the external radiation source to provide maximum flexibility while maintaining sub‐millimeter setup accuracy. The first step involves commissioning the SARRP system and accurately aligning the laser system with the imaging isocenter. We then developed a specialized collimator and evaluated setup accuracy using a mouse phantom. Finally, we tested the entire workflow with a euthanized rat to confirm the sub‐millimeter setup accuracy, demonstrating its potential for future preclinical radiation therapy research.

SARRP commissioning: Fine‐tuning the laser system and alignment with the imaging isocenter

The first and critical step in commissioning the SARRP system is fine‐tuning the laser system to ensure alignment between the laser cross center and the imaging isocenter. In addition to aligning the laser cross with the image isocenter, it is essential to adjust the sagittal and axial lasers to be as vertical as possible and the coronal lasers to be as horizontal as possible. Since as described in the following sections, we mark the surface of the animal holder's top cover to indicate the target position, which has several centimeters away from the actual internal target. If the laser lines are not vertical or horizontal, the angular deviation will introduce projection errors when referencing surface markings for setup. In the Supplement Material, we provide the detailed approach to align and fine‐tuning the Laser System.

End‐to‐end test using an acrylic mouse phantom

As an initial step to establish and validate our workflow, we used an acrylic mouse phantom to test the overall feasibility and setup accuracy. The workflow consists of four steps:

In the first step, a BB was inserted into the internal cavity of the mouse phantom to simulate the center of a small target (Figure 1a). Then the phantom was placed and taped on a custom 3D‐printed animal holder which included a bottom plate and a top cover. A piece of radiochromic film was placed underneath the mouse phantom to capture the radiation field (Figure 1b).

(a) Mouse phantom with a BB inserted in the central cavity to simulate an internal target. (b) 3D‐printed animal holder with a mouse phantom in the middle. The laser cross was marked on the surface of the top cover, serving as a surrogate for the internal target position. (c) SARRP CT image showing the alignment of the internal BB to the imaging isocenter.

In the second step, the assembly was placed and secured on the SARRP stage for a CT scan. The internal BB target was then aligned to the CT image isocenter (Figure 1c), and the laser cross was marked on the surface of the top cover, serving as a surrogate for the internal target position (Figure 1b).

In the third step, the mouse phantom and holder were transferred to our Varian ProBeam® proton gantry room and aligned with a collimator based on the external markers previously drawn on the top cover of the animal holder (Figure 2a). The collimator was a custom‐designed, 3D‐printed PLA component (Figure 2b) with dimensions of 10 cm × 5 cm × 5 cm and a central aperture of 1 cm in diameter. To assist with precise alignment, the collimator design included alignment grooves on the top surface and all four vertical sides, corresponding to the center of the collimator aperture. Since the collimator defines the radiation field center, it was critical to ensure precise alignment between the collimator and the surface marker. Two alignment approaches were considered: Laser‐based alignment: This method involves fine‐tuning the sagittal and lateral vertical lasers in the treatment room to ensure they are orthogonal, as done during SARRP laser calibration. The animal holder is then aligned using these lasers, followed by alignment of the collimator using the side grooves. However, this approach requires considerable effort to achieve sub‐millimeter precision due to the large distance (several meters) between the room lasers and the isocenter. Additionally, room lasers may be affected by gantry rotation and mechanical vibrations from daily clinical operations, necessitating frequent recalibration. Cross‐hair based visual alignment (preferred method and used in our following test): To eliminate dependence on room laser calibration, a simpler and more robust alignment method was developed. A piece of semi‐transparent paper tape was affixed to the bottom of the collimator, and a cross‐hair was drawn using the alignment grooves as reference (Figure 2c). The collimator was then positioned so that the cross‐hair visually aligned with the surface marker on the top cover of the animal holder, viewed directly through the collimator aperture (Figure 2d). This method is fast (completed in under one minute), does not require any use of the room lasers, and offers excellent reproducibility.

Additionally, during alignment, care was taken to level the collimator, ensuring the roll and pitch angles remained within 0.2°, which is relatively straightforward to achieve by using a digital level.

In the final step, a single‐energy, square proton field was delivered through the collimator to the mouse phantom, generating a radiation field image on the film that included the BB shadow. This image was subsequently analyzed (as described in the Results section) to quantify the setup accuracy of the entire procedure.

End‐to‐end test using euthanized rats

A small tungsten BB was inserted into the rectum of a euthanized rat. The high‐Z material of the tungsten BB ensures that it will cast a clear shadow on the film after irradiation, indicating the BB's center. The rat was then positioned on a 3D‐printed holder, which was subsequently positioned on the SARRP stage. We gently applied paper tapes on the legs and body of the rat to immobilize it during imaging and room transfer procedures. When applying this method in actual studies, animals need to be anesthetized to make sure they are kept still during the whole experiment, which is similar as the euthanized rats tested here. The immobilization workflow with anesthetized mice imaged in one room and transferred to another treatment room has also been tested in our previous experiments with multiple mice setup. 19 , 20 A CT scan was acquired, and the BB center was aligned to the imaging isocenter (Figure 3a). The position of the lasers cross was marked on the surface of the rat holder's cover to serve as a surrogate for the internal BB position (Figure 3b). The rat holder was subsequently transferred to a proton gantry room, and the collimator was aligned with the surface marker using the same visual alignment method described in Section 2.2 (Figure 3c). In this step, high precision of the gantry laser system was not required, as the uniform proton field delivered to the collimator defined the radiation center by the collimator's aperture. A piece of film was placed beneath the rat holder to capture the radiation field and the projected shadow of the internal tungsten BB. This film was later analyzed to evaluate the setup accuracy of the entire procedure.

(a) CT scan acquired using the SARRP system, with the internal BB aligned to the imaging isocenter. (b) Laser cross marked on the surface of the rat holder, serving as a surrogate of the internal BB position. (c) Rat holder transferred to another proton gantry room, with the collimator center aligned to the surface marker on the top of the holder.

RESULTS:

End‐to‐end test using acrylic mouse phantom and euthanized rat

In this section, we present the analysis of film images from the end‐to‐end test conducted with the acrylic mouse phantom and the euthanized rat, as described in the Methods section. To evaluate the reproducibility of the proposed workflow, the procedure was repeated ten times by using the mouse phantom under identical conditions, and using a rat to validate the procedure and results. Figure 4 shows examples of film images obtained after irradiation of the phantom and rat. In the image, the large circular region corresponds to the collimated radiation field, while the smaller circular shadow near the center represents the tungsten BB. The setup accuracy was quantified as the positional difference between the center of the collimated field and the center of the BB shadow.

One of the scanned film images obtained after irradiating the acrylic mouse phantom (a). Images were enhanced using ImageJ software to improve visibility. Scanned film image obtained after irradiating the euthanized rat (b). In this test, ImageJ processing was not required, as a slightly larger BB and higher radiation dose were used, resulting in a clearer and more distinguishable BB shadow. In both images, the large circular region represents the collimated radiation field, while the smaller circular shadow near the center corresponds to the BB.

To determine these center positions, we manually fitted circular regions of interest (ROIs) around both the field and BB shadows. The center coordinates of the collimated field and BB were extracted from the yellow and red ROIs, respectively. The distance between the two centers represents the overall setup error for each test. In the Supplement Material, we provide all the ten scanned films and fitting results of the end‐to‐end test using acrylic mouse phantom.

The setup accuracy results are summarized in Table 1, with observed deviations ranging from 0.2 mm to 0.6 mm. The average setup accuracy across the ten measurements was 0.39 mm, with a standard deviation of ±0.12 mm. This is generally in line with published results (±0.5 mm) for the isocentricity of the SARRP system. 21 , 22

Summary of workflow implementation and practical tips

This section summarizes the key steps of our workflow and offers several practical tips to help achieve sub‐millimeter setup accuracy more reliably and consistently:

Workflow overview: Figure 5 is a flowchart summarizing the major steps of our workflow for quick reference and implementation guidance.The average setup and imaging time for a single mouse using our workflow is approximately 10–15 min. If all steps are performed by a single individual, completing a typical experiment involving 20 mice would require approximately 4–5 h. However, the four main components of the workflow—anesthetization, animal positioning and securing on the holder, SARRP imaging, and irradiation—can be carried out in parallel by a research team. With coordinated effort, the entire experiment could be completed within approximately 3 h.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first workflow described that decouples the small animal imaging system from the irradiation source, while still achieving sub‐millimeter accuracy without relying on the imaging or laser systems in the treatment room. By incorporating a 3D‐printed collimator system, our approach enables precise conformal radiation studies targeting orthotopic tumors (e.g., tumors in the prostate, lung, etc.) in small animals. This workflow provides significant flexibility for conducting comparative studies across different radiation modalities (e.g., protons, X‐rays, neutrons, etc.) and radiotherapy techniques (e.g., ultra‐high dose rate FLASH therapy, mini‐beam and grid therapy, etc.).

Compared to existing image‐guided small animal platforms for particle radiation, our method mimics the workflow of patient treatment to perform image and irradiation separately, and does not require complex or expensive auxiliary hardware to align the entire small animal platform with radiation sources, while still maintaining sub‐millimeter accuracy. This feature is especially beneficial for other institutions equipped with conventional small animal platforms, as it facilitates high‐precision, image‐guided preclinical studies on any existing and novel radiotherapy technologies. In addition, the principle of this workflow not only works for SARRP system, but also can be applied to any preclinical research platforms with CBCT imaging system.

Furthermore, numerous precision preclinical X‐ray studies have been conducted in recent years using conventional small animal platforms. These studies have explored topics such as identifying tumor recurrence from necrosis, 23 microvascular responses in radiotherapy, 24 immune responses enhanced by radiation, 25 , 26 and combination between radiation and nanoparticles, 27 among others. Our newly developed approach, as outlined in this manuscript, allows researchers to conduct similar precise preclinical investigations using different particle radiation sources and different novel radiotherapy techniques. For example, first, in order to study the LET‐related RBE uncertainty around the protons and ions distal end of the Bragg peak, researchers need high‐precision, image‐guided proton and ion radiation platforms for small animals to quantitatively study the RBE effects in these high‐LET regions, where rapid variation occurs around the Bragg peak's distal end. Second, in order to compare the FLASH effect among different radiation modalities, researchers need a precise and also flexible approach to align small animals with different radiation sources. Our proposed method enables the interrogation of varied radiobiological effects across different radiation modalities, potentially improving our understanding of these mechanisms and ultimately benefiting future clinical applications.

Moreover, with the sub‐millimeter setup accuracy provided by our platform, it is feasible to apply two or three different radiation modalities to different body regions within the same small animal. This capability allows researchers to directly compare radiobiological responses across modalities while eliminating variability introduced by individual animal differences.

CONCLUSION

We present and validate a flexible workflow that achieves sub‐millimeter setup accuracy without the need for complex coupling between small animal platforms and radiation sources. This workflow offers significant versatility for institutions to leverage their existing small animal platforms in conducting precise image‐guided preclinical radiotherapy research across various radiation modalities. Such advancements will substantially improve our understanding of the clinical advantages and limitations of emerging radiotherapy technologies, including particle therapy, FLASH therapy, minibeam therapy, and so on. Ultimately, this will facilitate the translation of preclinical findings into clinical applications, advancing the field of radiation therapy.
