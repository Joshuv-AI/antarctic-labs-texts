Comment on “Beyond Awareness: A Qualitative Assessment of Barriers and Facilitators to Implementing Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Care Pathways in Primary Care”
Authors: Madhuri Tribhuvan, Shashikant N Phatke
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Driessen et al1 exploring barriers and facilitators to implementing metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) care pathways in primary care. The authors should be commended for moving beyond traditional knowledge deficit models and examining the relational, contextual, and cognitive dimensions shaping general practitioners’ (GPs) engagement with MASLD innovations. Their qualitative framework provides important insights into why guideline-concordant MASLD care remains difficult to operationalize despite increasing disease burden. Nevertheless, several methodological and translational considerations may further refine interpretation of these findings and strengthen future implementation strategies.

A central concern relates to the absence of triangulation with observable implementation behavior or organizational metrics. Although the interpretivist approach appropriately captures experiential reasoning, the conclusions are derived entirely from self-reported attitudes and perceptions. This may limit understanding of the discrepancy between stated willingness for innovation and actual adoption behavior in routine practice.2 GPs frequently described themselves as intrinsically motivated toward patient-centered innovation, yet the study did not evaluate whether these attitudes correlate with measurable implementation outcomes such as referral patterns, noninvasive test utilization, or longitudinal adherence to pathway recommendations. This distinction is clinically important because implementation failure often emerges not from attitudinal resistance alone but from hidden workflow fragmentation, competing administrative demands, and variable institutional support structures that may remain underrecognized in interview-based research. Incorporating mixed-method implementation metrics or embedded practice-level observations could therefore provide a more behaviorally grounded understanding of MASLD pathway uptake.

An additional issue concerns the limited exploration of heterogeneity within primary care infrastructure and digital integration capacity. The study frames continuity, workflow integration, and organizational trust as major determinants of implementation success, yet little attention is given to the technological variability that increasingly shapes chronic disease management. Contemporary MASLD care pathways frequently depend on automated fibrosis risk calculation, laboratory interoperability, recall systems, and decision-support integration within electronic health records. Small variations in digital infrastructure may substantially alter feasibility, cognitive burden, and clinician acceptance.3 In practices lacking automated risk stratification tools, even apparently simple strategies such as Fibrosis-4 index incorporation may become operationally inconsistent over time. This omission may reduce the practical applicability of the proposed conceptual framework, particularly for health-care systems undergoing rapid digital transformation. Future studies may benefit from evaluating how health information technology maturity modifies implementation readiness and clinician trust in MASLD algorithms.

The study also interprets GP skepticism toward specialist-derived evidence primarily through contextual Bayesian reasoning and professional identity. While this perspective is valuable, the analysis does not sufficiently address how uncertainty surrounding patient-centered outcome benefit may independently influence implementation reluctance. Current MASLD pathways emphasize fibrosis detection and risk stratification, yet evidence linking widespread primary care screening to improved long-term clinical outcomes, health-care utilization reduction, or mortality benefit remains incompletely established.4 In such settings, clinician caution may represent not merely contextual conservatism but rational concern regarding downstream consequences of overdiagnosis, referral escalation, and health-care resource redistribution. This distinction carries major translational implications because implementation efforts focused primarily on communication and partnership may have limited success if uncertainty persists regarding outcome-level effectiveness. Integrating pragmatic implementation trials with patient-centered end points, rather than pathway adherence alone, may therefore better align specialist priorities with primary care decision-making logic.

Despite these considerations, the study provides an important contribution by demonstrating that MASLD implementation cannot be reduced to education or reimbursement alone. The emphasis on trust, contextual reasoning, and professional identity offers a nuanced framework that may inform implementation strategies across other chronic and underdiagnosed conditions in primary care. These insights may help guide the development of more sustainable, practice-integrated hepatology pathways that better align with real-world primary care dynamics.
