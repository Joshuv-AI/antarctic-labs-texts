Willingness of adults to accept malaria mass drug administration using the theoretical framework of acceptability: a cross-sectional study in an urban municipality, Ghana
Authors: Perfect Amegah, Hainau Iddrisu, Harriet Affran Bonful
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria continues to pose a major public health threat in sub-Saharan Africa, with Ghana accounting for 2.5% of all malaria cases in the region. Mass drug administration (MDA) has emerged as a viable intervention for malaria elimination, yet its success largely depends on community acceptance and adherence, particularly when asymptomatic individuals are required to take antimalarial drugs. The study assessed the willingness of adults in the La Dade-Kotopon Municipality (LaDMA) of Ghana to accept Malaria MDA programs using the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) constructs and explore factors influencing its acceptability in urban Accra.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional study design involving 421 adults from two communities in LaDMA, namely La and Burma Camp communities which served as the strata for this study. A step-by-step stratified procedure was used to select the study participants at random by sampling at the community and household levels. A structured questionnaire was administered to participants. Acceptability of MDA was assessed using Sekhon et al. seven Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) constructs. Both descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted at an alpha level of 0.05.ResultsThe median age of the participants was 33 years and 57.5% were females. Majority (58.7; 95% CI 54.0–63.0) of the participants in LaDMA found the malaria MDA program acceptable and will be willing to participate in it. Individuals aged 50–59 years had 66% lower odds of MDA acceptability compared to those aged 18–29 years (aOR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.11–0.95, p = 0.040). Those with tertiary education had 72% lower odds of MDA acceptability compared to those with primary education (aOR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.86, p = 0.027). Ewes had 68% lower odds of MDA acceptability compared to Gas (aOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.12–0.82, p = 0.019).ConclusionWillingness to accept malaria MDA is moderate, with over 40% of participants finding the intervention unacceptable. Residents who endorsed community wellbeing as a reason to participate were substantially more likely to accept MDA. Programmatic strategies should prioritize targeted engagement to address barriers among non-accepting groups, while emphasizing community-wide benefits to sustain participation among those already willing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28308-x.Keywords: Acceptability, Willingness, Malaria, MDA, LaDMA, Ghana

Introduction

Malaria remains a significant global health challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where it contributes to elevated rates of morbidity and mortality. According to the World Health Organization, there were an estimated 263 million cases of malaria and 597,000 deaths worldwide in 2023, with approximately 95% of these fatalities occurring in the African Region [1]. Despite the intensified effort in reducing the global malaria burden, a substantial number of individuals at risk in this region continue to lack access to essential services for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of the disease [1].

Malaria remains endemic in Ghana contributing about 2.5% to the global prevalence [1]. However, the country has made progress in malaria control over the past decade through key interventions such as distribution of Long Lasting Insecticidal nets (LLINs), indoor residual spraying, improved case management, malaria vaccination programs, and strengthened surveillance systems [2]. Although these efforts have contributed to a decline in malaria prevalence in the country [3], sustained transmissions in areas that are potential hotspots for asymptomatic transmissions [4] may require additional interventions such as Mass Drug Administration (MDA).

MDA involves the provision of a complete therapeutic course of antimalarial medication to all individuals within a defined population or geographical area, irrespective of the presence of symptoms or infection status, except for those for whom the medication is contraindicated [5]. This intervention typically occurs simultaneously and at repeated intervals [5]. The primary objective of this strategy is to minimize community-level transmission by reducing the human reservoir of the malaria parasite, thereby preventing future infections [6]. Current malaria treatment strategies primarily utilize artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), which are recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. One notable ACT is dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DHAP), recognized for its high efficacy in eliminating Plasmodium falciparum infections while providing extended prophylactic protection against subsequent infections [7]. The prolonged elimination half-life of piperaquine sustains drug levels in the body, rendering it particularly advantageous for MDA programs [7].

In 2020, DHAP was adopted as a second-line antimalarial treatment for uncomplicated malaria in Ghana following a comprehensive review of the country’s antimalarial medicines policy [8]. The utilization of DHAP in MDA is substantiated by its effectiveness in significantly decreasing the malaria parasite reservoir within a community [9]. By administering DHAP to entire populations, including asymptomatic individuals, MDA can disrupt transmission cycles and contribute to the prevention of new infections. Previous MDA campaigns employing DHAP have yielded positive outcomes, resulting in reductions in both the incidence and prevalence of malaria, thereby underscoring its potential as a crucial component in malaria elimination strategies [9].

Despite the potential benefits of DHAP in MDA, community acceptance is crucial for the success of these interventions [10]. Community acceptance is essential for health interventions that depend on high population coverage, such as vaccination campaigns and MDA programs, because low uptake can limit the achievement of herd or community-level protection [11]. A systematic review involving 10 countries, 3 regions in Africa revealed that MDA coverage ranged from 75% to 91% per round in the four-week low frequency MDA arm and 84% to 97% per round in the two‐week high frequency MDA arm [12]. Factors such as fear of side effects, a lack of perceived need, and cultural beliefs can influence individuals’ decisions to participate in MDA campaigns [13–17]. Previous studies in Ghana have also indicated that community perceptions and the willingness to embrace new treatments significantly impact the effectiveness of malaria control programs [18–20]. Furthermore, the acceptability of health interventions has been reported to be mainly driven by intensified community engagement campaigns constantly adapted to the local context, and a delivery strategy that promotes community ownership and trust [21]. Therefore, understanding these factors is essential for designing effective community engagement strategies that enhance the acceptance and adherence to this intervention.

To better understand individual’s perceptions of health interventions, theory-based approaches have increasingly been recommended. The Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) developed by Sekhon and colleagues defines acceptability as a multi-dimensional construct reflecting individual’s cognitive and emotional responses to an intervention [22]. The framework identifies seven key components that influence intervention acceptability: affective attitude, burden, perceived effectiveness, ethicality, intervention coherence, opportunity costs, and self-efficacy [23]. By examining these dimensions, the framework provides a useful conceptual approach for evaluating how individuals perceive and respond to health interventions [23]. The TFA constructs is useful in assessing malaria MDA acceptability in urban LaDMA because it captures the multidimensional and context-dependent nature of acceptability [23]. This is particularly relevant in urban settings where health behaviours are influenced by diverse socio-economic conditions and heterogeneous access to healthcare services. The TFA has been widely applied to complex public health interventions to systematically assess acceptability across multiple domains and contexts [23, 24].

With the shift towards malaria elimination in Ghana and the adoption of malaria MDA as one of the elimination interventions, empirical evidence on population willingness to participate in malaria-specific MDA programs using dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine is a prerequisite for program planning and implementation. While previous studies have examined malaria knowledge, prevention practices, and treatment behaviors in Ghana [25–27], limited research has applied a theory-driven framework to assess the acceptability of malaria MDA interventions, particularly in urban settings. Furthermore, earlier studies on MDA acceptability in Ghana have focused largely on neglected tropical diseases [17, 28–30]. The current study contributes to knowledge on the willingness of adults to accept MDA for malaria elimination and explores factors influencing its acceptability in urban Accra aiming to inform the planning and implementation of malaria MDA programs in Ghana.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional study design and was conducted in the La Dade-Kotopon Municipal Assembly (LaDMA), which is one of the 261 Metropolitan, Municipal, and District Assemblies (MMDAs) in Ghana. LaDMA has a population of 140,264 with 48.6% males [31]. The National Malaria Elimination Strategic Plan (NMESP) lists LaDMA as one of the 21 districts under the very low malaria burden zones (1–49 cases/1,000 population, < 1% parasite prevalence) and Mass Drug Administration (MDA) is set to be implemented in these zones [3]. Data were collected from October to November 2025.

Sample size calculation and sampling procedure

The sample size for this study was estimated using Cochran’s formula for sample size determination (Cochran, 1977). A study on implementing population-based mass drug administration for malaria in North East Uganda recorded an average prevalence of 80% across the four rounds of MDA intervention [32]. Using this prevalence, the minimum sample size required for this study was 246. Adjusting for a design effect of 1.5 (a common conservative estimate for public health surveys using cluster sampling) [33], the sample size was calculated as 369. A 10% non-response rate was considered, bringing the sample size for this study to 406 participants.

A step-by-step stratified procedure was used to select the study participants by sampling at the community and household levels. La and Burma Camp communities were purposively selected on the basis that they are the two most populated communities among the nine communities in LaDMA. Number of households in each zone were estimated and samples allocated proportionately across each zone. A random starting point was chosen by tossing a coin after which every 5th household was sampled. While coin tossing is a pragmatic approach to household-level selection in resource-limited field settings, it is recognized that this method may introduce unintentional selection bias — for instance, through differential willingness to participate in the coin toss or variation in how research assistants applied additional rounds.

These limitations should be considered when assessing the external validity of the acceptability estimates. When more than one eligible participant was present, individuals were asked to toss a coin and, those who obtained “heads” proceeded to next round. Additional rounds of coin tossing were conducted where necessary until one participant was selected for the interview.

Eligibility criteria

Individuals who were 18 years old and above and had lived in the selected community for more than 6 months were included in the study. Apparently sick community members were excluded from the study.

Data collection and analysis

A theory-informed questionnaire for assessing the acceptability of healthcare interventions [22] was adapted. The questionnaires were administered to adults aged 18 years and above residing in the selected communities in LaDMA. Questionnaires were structured to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics of participants as well as their lived experiences with malaria and cultural beliefs. The outcome variable, acceptability of MDA was measured using the seven TFA construct of acceptability; perceived effectiveness, affective attitude, intervention coherence, self-efficacy, ethicality, opportunity cost and burden. Detailed descriptions of the seven constructs were captured on the questionnaire to give the participant’s the exact understanding of what each measures. The seven TFA construct of acceptability were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (Table 1) [22]. Two research assistants were recruited to collect data using the KoboCollect app after training and pretesting data collection tool at Teshie.

Statistical analysis

Data was downloaded from KoboCollect tool onto an excel sheet and imported to Stata version 17. Data was cleaned by verifying completeness, accuracy, and consistency. All incomplete responses were deleted. Each variable was appropriately coded for efficient input into statistical software. The codings used were the validated codes from the TFA by Sekhon et al., [22]. For this study, an aggregate score was computed as the sum of scores across the seven TFA constructs, each measured on a 5-point Likert scale, yielding a possible range of 7 to 35. The aggregate score was dichotomized into ‘Unacceptable’ (7–24)and ‘Acceptable’ (25–35). The threshold of 25 was selected based on two considerations: (i) it represents the midpoint of the upper half of the theoretical score range, aligning with a ‘tipping point’ toward net positive responses across constructs, and (ii) it is consistent with guidance from the TFA instrument developers [22] on interpreting construct-level scores as reflecting favorable acceptability when responses trend toward the positive anchors. It should be acknowledged that this dichotomization involves a degree of author discretion, and no single validated cut-off has been established for aggregate TFA scores in malaria MDA studies to date. The Supplementary Information provides further detail on the scoring rationale. Sensitivity to this threshold should be considered when interpreting the acceptability prevalence estimate. Descriptive statistics of the socio-demographic factors lived Malaria experiences and cultural beliefs, as well as the seven TFA constructs were computed. Chi-square test was used to determine the association between the independent variables and the outcome variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression was used to further identify the predictors of willingness to accept MDA. For categorical predictors with multiple levels, the overall significance was assessed using Likelihood Ratio (LR) tests, comparing the full model to nested model excluding the variables in question. Finally, model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using Hosmer-Lemeshow test (g = 10). A non-significant p-value (p > 0.05) on the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to indicate that the model’s predicted probabilities were well-calibrated with the observed data. Analysis was performed at 95% CI and 0.05 precision level.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

Majority (72.0%) of participants were residents of La. The median (IQR) age of participants was 33 (27–44). More than half (57.5%) of the participants were females. With level of education, participants who attained only primary education formed the majority (34.4%). Nearly 80% of the participants were employed. Majority of the participants (28. 0%) refused disclosure of their monthly income while the least represented group (5.7%) earn 5000 Ghana cedis and above. Christianity was the most represented (72.7%) religion and majority (40.1%) of the participants were of the Ga tribe (Table 2).

The seven TFA constructs of MDA acceptability

More than half (53.9%) of the participants reported being comfortable with taking malaria medicine even when they do not feel sick. Factors associated with malaria MDA acceptability

From the multivariate regression model, several factors were significantly associated with malaria MDA acceptability; however, the strength of these associations varied. The most pronounced predictor was participants’ perception of community wellbeing, as those who agreed that they would participate in MDA for the benefit of their community were substantially more likely to accept the intervention (aOR = 46.29, 95% CI: 18.32–116.99, p < 0.001). Religious affiliation also showed a strong association, with Muslims (aOR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.07–0.46, p < 0.001) and participants belonging to other religions (aOR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.04–0.25, p < 0.001) being markedly less likely to accept MDA compared to Christians. Age was another important factor, as participants aged 50–59 years (aOR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.11–0.95, p = 0.040) and those aged 60 years and above (aOR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.02–0.78, p = 0.027) had significantly lower odds of acceptability compared to those aged 18–29 years. Similarly, participants with tertiary education were less likely to accept MDA compared to those with primary education (aOR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.86, p = 0.027). Tribal differences were also observed, with Ewe participants being less likely to accept MDA compared to Ga participants (aOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.12–0.82, p = 0.019) (Table 7).

Discussion

This study assessed the willingness of adults to accept MDA for malaria elimination and explores factors influencing its acceptability in urban Accra aiming to inform the planning and implementation of malaria MDA programs in Ghana. Findings revealed that the willingness to accept malaria MDA in LaDMA is generally moderate. Most participants demonstrated a favourable emotional disposition, recognized the intervention as coherent, effective, and aligned with their values, and felt capable of participating, suggesting that a meaningful proportion of the population may be willing to participate if the intervention is well-communicated and implemented. However, it is equally important to note that over 40% of participants think the intervention is unacceptable, indicating that substantial programmatic effort will be needed to achieve the population-level coverage required for effective malaria transmission reduction. Findings from this study are similar to those of Legendre et al., a recent mixed-methods study conducted in the high-malaria burden region of Kedougou, Senegal, utilizing the same seven-construct framework where acceptability was found to be good [34]. Additionally, they identified that a comprehensive understanding of MDA, a strong perception of effectiveness, and acknowledgement of malaria as a significant burden were key facilitators of acceptability. Conversely, the primary barriers identified were concerns regarding side effects and the reluctance to take medication while asymptomatic [34].

In this study, age was associated with willingness to accept malaria MDA. Participants aged 50 years and above had lower odds of accepting MDA. This could be because older adults may find it difficult to adapt to new health interventions. The findings are consistent with the findings from another study which indicates that age can influence health behaviors, with younger individuals often demonstrating greater adaptability to new health strategies [35]. However, it is also essential to acknowledge that older adults may possess distinct health beliefs and needs [35].

Education is correlated with health outcomes, as higher educational attainment is associated with improved health knowledge, increased access to resources, and enhanced health behaviors [36]. Contrary to evidence in the literature suggesting that higher education generally predicts greater health intervention uptake, individuals with tertiary education in this study were less likely to accept MDA. The reasons for this inverse association were not directly measured and therefore cannot be confirmed from these data alone. One possible explanation, suggested by prior literature on health intervention acceptability, is that individuals with higher educational attainment may engage more critically with population-wide health programmes, seeking more detailed information about rationale, safety, and evidence before accepting them [37]. Another possibility is that higher-educated participants may perceive their personal malaria risk as lower, or may have greater access to individual treatment options, reducing their perceived need for a community-wide intervention. These explanations, however, remain speculative in the context of this study and should be treated as hypotheses for future investigation rather than as direct implications of the current findings. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, this finding highlights the importance of designing MDA communication strategies that are responsive to varying information needs and that present clear, evidence-based rationale for the intervention across all educational groups. Income levels varied significantly; however, a substantial portion (28%) chose not to disclose their income. This socio-economic diversity suggests that any mass drug administration (MDA) program in the municipality must effectively reach a wide range of livelihoods, from low-income informal laborers to higher-income professionals. Consequently, the implementation of MDA cannot assume uniform participation based solely on economic or occupational status. Individuals employed in unskilled or informal sectors, particularly those with low or unstable incomes, may exhibit a higher risk of non-participation due to factors such as mobility, absence during distribution times, or limited access to information. On the other hand, higher-income or formally employed individuals may also choose to opt out, especially if they perceive the risk of malaria as low or harbor mistrust toward the intervention. This highlights the necessity for tailored and adaptable outreach and distribution strategies. Prior research indicates that non-participation in MDA is strongly linked to occupation such as livestock breeding or other mobility-related trades, and lower income [38].

The observed variation in MDA acceptability across religious affiliations — with Muslims and those belonging to other religions showing substantially lower acceptability than Christians — is a noteworthy finding that warrants careful interpretation. Importantly, this study did not directly measure religious beliefs, doctrinal perspectives on medicine, or the role of religious leaders in shaping health decisions; the survey captured religious affiliation only as a demographic variable. As such, any explanation for these differences remains speculative and should be framed as such. Prior literature has documented, more broadly, that religious and community-based belief systems can shape responses to population-wide health interventions [39, 40], and that in some contexts, concerns about taking medication while asymptomatic, or perceptions that illness reflects spiritual rather than biological causes, have been associated with lower uptake of preventive treatments. However, these patterns cannot be assumed to explain the differences observed in the current study, as the mechanisms may be distinct and may reflect unmeasured social, contextual, or structural factors that co-vary with religious affiliation in this setting. Future research employing qualitative or mixed-methods designs would be better positioned to explore the specific social and cultural pathways underlying these associations. What this finding does underscore is the importance of community-sensitive implementation strategies that engage diverse community members including religious community leaders without making assumptions about the basis of hesitancy within any particular group. These findings highlight the need for culturally sensitive and context-specific implementation strategies. Therefore, any MDA program in this context must not only communicate benefits but also actively address religious and cultural concerns to maximize coverage and compliance. In contrast, acceptance of MDA tends to be higher among individuals and communities that recognize malaria as a significant burden, understand the rationale behind MDA, and view it as beneficial to community wellbeing [34]. Overall, understanding these nuances is essential for the design of effective and inclusive MDA initiatives.

The use of the mean TFA score derived from the seven TFA constructs as the acceptability score is one major strength of this study as this considers various factors and nuances that inform an individual’s acceptance of a health intervention behavior like MDA. A few limitations were identified in this study. This study relied on self-reported data and this may have introduced response bias, potentially leading to overestimation of acceptability toward mass drug administration. Additionally, the findings of this study may only be generalizable to communities similar to the study area. The final scoring of acceptability is partly based on the TFA construct author guidance as well as the discretion of the authors of this study and as such this scoring may not be appropriate in all instances. Future MDA acceptability studies using the TFA framework are encouraged to report the full distribution of construct-level and aggregate scores alongside any dichotomised outcome, so that readers and policymakers can assess the robustness of findings to different threshold choices. Also, the results reflect a single time point and do not capture changes over time. The purposive selection of only two communities and the use of coin tossing at the household level may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the sample. While this maximized participant availability and logistical feasibility, findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. With respect to generalizability beyond populations similar to La and Burma Camp communities, particularly smaller or less urbanized communities that may differ in socio-economic composition, mobility patterns, and health-seeking behaviors. Accordingly, the prevalence estimate of acceptability should be interpreted as indicative of the most populated urban communities within LaDMA rather than as a municipality-wide figure.

Conclusion

The findings from this study suggests that a malaria MDA programme has a moderate likelihood of acceptability in this study area, but its success would depend on addressing specific factors identified in this study. A substantial proportion of the population remains uncertain or unwilling, underscoring the need for targeted strategies to improve acceptability. The strongest predictor of MDA acceptability was agreement that participating in MDA benefits the wider community. In contrast, several sociodemographic characteristics were associated with lower odds of acceptability: older age (particularly those aged 50 years and above), higher educational attainment (tertiary level), Ewe ethnic identity, and minority religious affiliation (Islam and other religions) compared to Christianity. These groups represent priority populations for targeted community engagement and communication efforts. While a significant number of adults are willing to participate in malaria MDA programs, achieving the community level high coverage require targeted interventions aimed at reducing both cognitive and practical barriers. Tailored communication strategies that enhance understanding of MDA, address concerns about safety and necessity, and emphasize community benefits may be particularly important. In addition, engaging community and religious leaders, and adopting flexible delivery approaches that accommodate different occupational and social groups, could help improve participation and ensure acceptability.
