Signals, tracks, and trams: public transport signal priority impact on job accessibility over time
Authors: Michał A. Niedzielski, Sławomir Goliszek, Anna Górka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Improving travel time by public transport is a major task for city planners and policy makers to increase its competitiveness relative to cars which can be facilitated by implementing public transport preferential infrastructure treatments such as signal priority. We study the impact of one such preferential treatment, signal priority, on accessibility to jobs between 2015 and 2022 in Warsaw, Poland, which implemented signal priority on 187 intersections during this time period. We develop a method to extract inter-stop travel times from the General Transit Feed Service. We find that signal priority implementation lead to (1) a travel time decrease over the network by 6.7%, (2) an increase in accessibility by 5–8.5%, (3) a full signal priority setting drives the accessibility change over time, and (4) the location of SP and travel time segments at the entry point to high-density jobs drives accessibility change. Our analysis provides a method to help decision makers evaluate the impact of signal priority on accessibility to jobs. Our results indicate significant effectiveness of signal priority implementation.

Introduction

Accessibility to and by public transport (PT hereafter) contributes to positive environmental, health, social, and economic outcomes1. As an important component of urban mobility, PT is important for increasing the general quality of life of urban residents because it serves as a catalyst for increasing productivity and wages2–4, increasing out-of-home activity participation5, increasing physical activity6, increasing well-being7, lowering energy use and greenhouse gas emissions8, and increasing employment9. However, the benefits of PT can be stymied because at-grade PT is slowed by congestion, increasing delay and unreliability. To reap the maximum PT benefits, infrastructure-based PT preferential treatments such as PT-dedicated lanes or signal priority (SP hereafter) are typically proposed as a solution. For a 99% exclusive lane network like Warsaw’s trams, SP is a viable preferential treatment that allows trams to maintain or increase their travel speed in order to increase their advantage relative to cars. It is crucial to evaluate the impact of SP on accessibility by PT in order to provide science-based evidence for city policy makers about the effectiveness of SP implementation to support their sustainable transportation plans by justifying travel time increases for other road users. Prioritizing rail PT through SP implementation and buses through bus lane construction over cars is part of Warsaw’s sustainable transport strategy (e.g., ref.10).

Even though PT infrastructure changes are frequently investigated in accessibility analyses, the accessibility impacts of SP remain largely ignored. Approaches for measuring PT infrastructure impacts can be grouped into four types, including (1) impact on accessibility due to (1a) linear infrastructure or network changes or additions, such as new routes or extensions of regular bus, bus rapid transit or rail (e.g., ref.11), and (1b) point infrastructure changes such as changes in stop spacing (e.g., ref.12) or retrofitting an additional station entrance/exit (e.g., ref.13), (2) impact on economic outcomes such as unemployment, wages, or housing values (e.g., ref.14), (3) impact of SP on (3a) service reliability (e.g., ref.15), (3b) on travel time of all road users by optimizing network wide SP locations (e.g., ref.16) or (3c) single intersection or corridor microsimulations (e.g., ref.17), and (4) mechanical, operational, and engineering issues18. However, geographers assessing infrastructure impacts on accessibility have largely neglected SP, while engineers have ignored accessibility in their SP analyses. To our knowledge, only one study analyzed the impact of SP on accessibility11, though in this case SP was implemented only on one bus route corridor.

Many accessibility studies rely on PT scheduled timetables in General Transit Feed Service (GTFS hereafter) data to calculate accessibility metrics to investigate socioeconomic inequity or transport disadvantage. However, some studies have pointed out that significant differences exist between schedule-based and GPS-based real-time travel time data19–21. These authors call into question the use of schedule-based data because the error may have important implications on policy findings. Nevertheless, GTFS data is a viable alternative because the same set of off- and on-vehicle travel time influence factors apply to both travel time data sets, and GTFS is open-source, free and available to all PT agencies and researchers while GPS-based automated vehicle location (AVL) data may not be.

One potential reason for why the PT impacts on accessibility have rarely been studied is the difficulty in isolating SP effects from other travel speed influence factors in origin–destination PT travel time matrices. Such matrices for accessibility indicator calculation are typically computed between census zone centroids which means that the total travel time between origins and destinations is influenced by off- and in-vehicle factors including stop wait times, transfer walk times, route frequency, route network, operational speed, and origin departure time. Thus, simply using pre-packaged GTFS data will conflate multiple travel time influences, which the single accessibility impact of SP study11 does not remove. A simplified network based on inter-stop travel times extracted from GTFS is needed. While several papers have extracted data from GTFS for visualization22, for space–time trajectory conversion23, for PT performance metrics24 or edited/developed GTFS timetables based on real-time data19, only a single paper, to our knowledge, has provided a universal methodology for extracting inter-stop travel times from GTFS in order to assess the impact of infrastructure-based PT preferential treatments25. Once this simplified network has been created, it can be used to calculate an origin–destination travel time matrix using any available calculator, such as opentripplanner, r5r, QGIS or ESRI’s ArcPro Network Analyst, just like typical analyses based on networks using GTFS-based26 or real-time-based data.

While Arias et al.25 provide a general and universal methodology of extracting inter-stop travel times from GTFS, they do not measure accessibility and they do not measure the impact of preferential treatments implemented in real life. Rather, they measure the potential for travel time savings by comparing minimum (off-peak) and actual (peak) inter-stop segment travel times in order to identify segments for potential implementation of such treatments. Furthermore, they do not specify which of the three treatments, bus lanes, queue jumping or SP, to implement and in which specific locations. Our study follows this vein, in that we do not suggest new SP locations or propose changes to existing SP locations. Rather, by evaluating accessibility impacts of SP, we intend to enrich decision-making support tools by providing a framework for an accessibility-based transport performance metric. Too often, transport policy and the metrics that support it are based on the travel time between places, or mobility, rather than the opportunities that can be reached, or accessibility27.

Adjustments to timetables may occur for various reasons. Both increases and decreases in travel time in timetables may be caused by track changes, traffic lights, speed limits or significant changes in traffic and passenger flows, as well as due to a previously incorrect timetable. In Warsaw, corrections are made based on punctuality measurements from the Tram Traffic Supervision System. Since the tram network and punctuality have been constantly monitored for many years, corrections involving shortening travel times practically do not occur except in situations where the infrastructure has been improved, which in the case of our study always means introducing SP and that no comprehensive modernizations of the tracks took place in the period under study, or significant changes to transfer points affecting traffic flows. In turn, we can observe tram travel time increases in Warsaw due to track degradation and, consequently, the setting of speed limits and the construction of new traffic lights without priority. Thus, we are confident that travel time decreases and consequently accessibility increases in our study are due to SP.

In this study, we extend existing methodology of creating a simplified PT network based on inter-stop travel times extracted from GTFS data to assess the impact of SP on accessibility using the tram network in Warsaw, Poland, where 187 intersections were treated with SP between 2015 and 2022. We use static schedules within GTFS because Warsaw’s tram punctuality is 92%. Our results provide evidence for planners and policy-makers about the effectiveness of SP in light of it being a contentious issue considering it may increase travel time for some road users.

Results

Travel time

Figure 1a shows the decreasing trend in system-wide travel time. The total inter-stop segment travel time (adding the travel time for all segments) decreased by 67 min (6.7% decrease), from 1005 min in 2015 to 938 min in 2022. Figure 1b shows annual implementation of SP, with a maximum of 57 SP implemented in 2016, and a minimum of 8 implemented in 2017. After the large SP roll-out in 2016, the SP implementation trend is increasing. Larger travel time decreases occurred mostly in even years when more SP were implemented. This reflects Warsaw Trams’ bi-annual SP implementation cycle, in which the first year is for algorithmic design (odd years) and the second is for construction (even years), though as the Warsaw Trams SP implementation team gained experience over time the year-to-year difference started to even out by the end of the time period. Figure 1c uncovers the true impact of SP on travel time change because it disaggregates the net travel time into increases and decreases. In Warsaw’s tram network, traversal of each segment takes one minute, Fig. 1c in essence shows the number of segments with travel time increase and decrease. Decreases are due to SP, while increases are due to increasing car congestion at intersections, dwell time, and tram congestion. In total, SP resulted in 168 min of travel time savings, while other factors contributed to 101 min of travel time increase. These divergent factors varied over time, though the SP effect always at least neutralized the negative effects. Perhaps not surprisingly, the largest net effect coincides with the largest SP implementation, such as in 2016 and 2020.

What is the net travel time impact of one intersection treated with SP? By dividing the net travel time change by the number of SP, we find an average travel time reduction of 21 s (Fig. 1d), which varies from a low of 0 s in 2017 to a high of 38 s in 2022. The standard deviation over study period is almost 30 s, which is expected given the range of increase and decrease segments shown in Fig. 1c. Interestingly, the travel time reduction shows an increasing trend indicating increasing effectiveness of additional SP on the whole system. The dashed lines show the theoretical scenario of only travel time decrease driven by SP without the influence of any travel time increase factors. Here, the average travel time decrease is 56 s. The largest decrease in 2019 is due to a large travel time decrease but a very small net travel time decrease. By dividing the net effect by the decrease effect, we calculate the net effect rate or how much of the potential maximum decrease was realized. Over the whole period, the net effect rate is 39.9%.

A map of SP locations and travel time change segments shows several spatial patterns (Fig. 2; numbers on the map are specific examples referenced below). First, there is a time lag between SP implementation and segment travel time change. While our end of the year cutoff caught the travel time decrease for the Bielany and Targówek segments implemented in 2016 (1) or the three full SP in northern Praga Północ in 2019 (2), in other cases schedule updates occurred the year following SP implementation, such as four high SP in the center (3) (2016/2017), the SP in Praga Południe (4) (2021/2022), or the 5 full and 2 high SP in northwestern Śródmieście (5) (2021/2022). This is most clearly seen in the 11 SP implemented in November and December of 2022 for which schedules were not updated before our cutoff date.Fig. 2Travel time change on tram network segments.

Second, there is also a spatial lag between SP locations and the location of segments with a schedule change. This occurs because the accumulation impact of several SP needs to be added up to allow a minute reduction in the schedule (schedules are minute-based). Examples include the minute decreases in Śródmieście (6) (2016), in north Żoliborz (7) (2016), or in Praga Północ (2) (2019). Third, SP sometimes may not result in travel time reduction, the effect is neutral, but without it travel time would have increased. Essentially, this is a counterbalancing effect. Examples include the full SP in northcentral Śródmieście (8) (2020), or the two high SP in Praga Północ (9) (2016).

Job accessibility

The overall net travel time reduction unsurprisingly has a positive impact on job accessibility (Table 1). Accessibility increased by 5–8.5% over the time period, depending on the travel time threshold. The increase for the 15 and 30 min thresholds are almost identical and higher by 3.3–3.5 pp. than the accessibility increase for the 45 min threshold. The lower job accessibility gain of the 45 min threshold is due to its catchment area already covering the main job centers in the city center for most of the study area, so there is less to gain in the longer distance, while the opposite is true for the 15 and 30 min thresholds. On an annual basis, the first three years show relative stability in accessibility change, with a 1–2% increase (Fig. 3a). Despite a net gain of 1 min in 2019, accessibility dropped by 1–2% as the travel time increase segments are located mostly in and around the job-rich city center countering the impact of decrease segments located mostly outside the center. The large drop is compensated by the 1–3% increase in 2020, because travel time has decreased by 19 min and because the reverse pattern is observed with travel time decrease segments at/near major job centers in and around the city center. This is also broadly similar in 2022. Conversely, a few zones have a large accessibility loss (< − 20%) and are very close to SP locations, only 188 m away from a full SP (24), and 278 m on average away from three high SP (24, 25). Two other areas also have accessibility loss while being very close to SP (north and south of 24; 26). But why do these areas have accessibility loss over the study period despite being close to SP locations? This is because these areas are impacted by locations of travel time increase segments more than travel time decrease segments. Notice (Fig. 6b) that in all three cases, the travel time increase segments locations are located at entry points to the highest job density in the city center. Area 24 is affected by travel time increase segments (24 s) to its east near the northern and western edges of high-density jobs. Area 25 is affected by travel time increase segments (25 s) to its north near the city center, and to its southwest near a major office complex in the south of the city. Area 26 is affected by travel time increase segments (26 s) to its north near the southwestern edge of high-density jobs. In all three cases, travel time decrease segments are located in these areas and their sides opposite high-density job clusters. Fascinatingly, a mirror image occurs (27) where an accessibility gain area is also the location of travel time increase. However, in this case, travel time decrease segments occur to its north at the entry point city center high-density jobs. Clearly, it is the SP and travel time change segments locations near high-density jobs that drive accessibility change rather than more residential and peripheral locations. The net effect reflects the dynamic back-and-forth nature of SP implementation and travel time degradation from other sources. In some instances, more recent accessibility loss did not erase the accessibility gains at the beginning of the study period (18), while in other cases, recent accessibility gains overcame earlier loss (19).

Discussion

The contribution of this study is a method and case study. We provide a generalized and universal approach to investigating the impact of SP infrastructure on accessibility to jobs through travel time. Using publicly available data (GTFS, road network, home/job locations), we show how to create an inter-stop travel time network. Our approach can be applied to other PT modes, like regular buses, BRT, or rail and also in a multi-modal way. It also allows for impact analysis of other infrastructure types apart from SP. Then, we apply the inter-stop travel time network method to analyze accessibility to jobs using the tram network in Warsaw, Poland. We study the impact of SP on job accessibility annually at the city and zonal levels. We find that between 2015 and 2022 overall job accessibility increased by 5–8.5% as travel time decreased by 6.7% due to SP implementation. Quantifying the accessibility impact of SP is a first in the literature. Furthermore, two major conclusions are reached. First, we show that the quality/type of SP matters, since the accessibility impact is driven by full SP. Second, we show that the location of SP and travel time change segments matters, since the accessibility impact is driven by SP and travel time decrease segments at the entry point to high-density jobs and not residential and peripheral areas.

It is difficult to compare our results to previous work, since almost no previous study analyzed the accessibility impact of SP. There are three closest comparisons analyzing the accessibility impact of PT infrastructure. An analysis of a bus network redesign with simpler more direct routes and higher frequency led to a 2% accessibility decrease, while the addition of SP along one bus corridor increased accessibility by 26%11. This rather high accessibility increase may be due to confounding factors not being removed. The large travel time increase due to SP may have a significant impact relative to wait time lost at the origin stop and at transfers. Another analysis finds that SP has reduced tram travel time in Melbourne and Toronto by 6–10%28. Another analysis of the impact of retrofitting an additional entrance at commuter rail stations in Sydney, Australia13 finds a 10% accessibility increase. Our findings can also be put in the context of other savings reaped by Warsaw Trams from SP implementations. They found that electricity use decreased by 13%, and that they needed 36 fewer trams and 108 fewer full-time employees.

In the case of Warsaw, all travel time decrease and accessibility is due to SP. We thought about removing all travel time increases in order to show the maximum SP impact, but decided against it because we want to show the net benefit of SP. A city is a living organism with several factors that cause travel time increases, thus quantifying the net accessibility effect of SP implementation provides evidence about the SP effectiveness for policy makers. Another justification for SP implementation is that it is also cost effective. While we do not have an estimate of the monetary cost of SP implementation in Warsaw, the return on this investment is a 5%-8.5% increase in accessibility. Without SP, travel time on the network would have increased and accessibility to jobs using trams would have decreased.

Thus, although SP is effective in improving PT travel time, investments in other infrastructure and PT operation aspects also need to be considered as part of a comprehensive package of investments over the long term in order to increase or to even maintain PT’s competitiveness against the car. Policy makers, thus, should also invest resources in track maintenance, decreasing dwell time by implementing full off-board payments and increasing frequency among other initiatives in order to allow the full potential of SP to be realized. For example, SP is much less effective if intersections are blocked by cars who could not move off tracks due to congestion.

Several implications follow from our research. First, our inter-stop method fills the gap between accessibility analysis using off-the-shelf GTFS and micro-simulation analysis of SP impacts. Furthermore, it shows the value of extracting valuable information from GTFS to enable more enhanced analysis. Second, our empirical tram accessibility case study fills the gap between all PT mode accessibility analysis and bus-specific analysis, and offers a network-wide analysis compared to previous mostly corridor or route only analysis. Thus, our method can be applied to an entire network, other modes and to study the impact of other infrastructure changes. Furthermore, we provide evidence of the effectiveness of SP implementation showing its positive net impact on job accessibility. Specifically, we show the value of full SP which provides the biggest impact. Thus, while researchers and planners have a new method for investigating accessibility changes, city officials and policymakers are provided with tangible outcomes from SP investment.

This study has some limitations. First, our end-of-the-year cutoff may not catch the schedule updates due to SP implementation for that year. We were unable to identify when schedules were updated for particular segments with SP implementation. This may skew the annual results and require careful year-to-year interpretation. Second, we overestimate accessibility because we did not subtract dwell time from our travel times due to lack of ridership data. Third, we did not perform a multi-modal analysis because our focus is strictly on tram accessibility as SP directly impacts tram travel. We did not include metro, rail, and bus calculations because this would conflate the impact of SP which is clearly designed to improve tram travel. Additionally, we did not measure the SP impact on car and bus travel. Indirectly, SP may result in slower car and bus travel time and thus lower accessibility for workers traveling by car and bus, but quantifying the modal spillover effects is beyond the scope of this study. Furthermore, the decrease in car travel time and accessibility as a consequence of tram preference is a potential outcome in line with the municipal Sustainable Development Strategy of Warsaw’s Transport System10. One of the strategy’s goal is to significantly reduce residents’ car travel reliance by increasing the attractiveness of PT, biking and walking because these modes are more economically and environmentally sustainable. The strategy provides SP as a way to privilege PT against car travel. Finally, while the results are unique to trams in Warsaw, we argue that the inter-stop travel time methodology is universal and can be applied to other spatial context and modes to uncover accessibility impacts of SP. However, the general applicability of our methodology depends on prompt schedule update by the agency, and on comparable padding and on-time performance before and after schedule change.

There are several avenues for future research. First, the impact of other types of PT infrastructure and network elements on job accessibility should be considered. Second, since Warsaw is a monocentric city, such impacts in other cities with different urban forms should be considered. Third, spatial equity impacts in cities with disadvantaged population groups should be considered.

Methods

Study area and data

Warsaw, the capital of Poland, covering an area of 517 km2, increased its population from 1,744,000 in 2015 to 1,862,345 in 202229. It possesses a large public transport network comprising four modes (buses, trams, metro, commuter trains). ZTM Warszawa (ZTM hereafter) is the municipal agency tasked with organizing public transport in the city and cooperating with suburbs and the voivodeship-owned train company Koleje Mazowieckie. ZTM determines the route network, fares, and schedules. The main operators are Tramwaje Warszawskie (Warsaw Trams LLC), which is the single municipally-owned tram operating company, and Miejskie Zakłady Autobusowe (a municipally-owned company), which is the largest bus operator along with several private companies.

Warsaw’s tram fleet and network is large and extensive. In 2022, TW operated 414 trams (792 tram cars) during a typical weekday morning rush period on 26 routes with 545 stops using 136 km of track in 11 of Warsaw’s 16 districts30. Exclusive right-of-way amounts to 97% of the track. It performed 22.48% of all vehicle-kilometers and provided 22.14% of all seats. Trams moved through 187 intersections treated with SP out of a total of 261 tram-street intersections (71.6% of the total). A total of 181 intersections were treated with SP in the 2015–2022 period. Figure 6 shows the tram network along and Warsaw’s district names. We excluded a new section of track built in multiple stages across the Wisła River to Winnica in western Białołęka during 2015–2022 (purple in Fig. 6) in order to focus on accessibility changes due to existing network travel time changes rather than due to network extensions. Also, when a section of track was closed due to the construction of the second metro line (Siedmiogrodzka, Prosta, and Kasprzaka Streets; blue in Fig. 6) during 2017–2018, we filled in the travel times for these years with those from 2016 in order to eliminate the impact of the track closure on accessibility. Data for the analysis were obtained from several sources. We used GTFS data from ZTM (available from https://www.transit.land), while the road network and data on the number of resident workers and number of jobs by zone were sourced from the 2015 Warsaw Traffic Survey (WTS)31. Apart from cellular phone data, which is cost prohibitive, this is the only and most recent source of locational job-related data available. Even if more recent were available we would use data from only one year to remove the effect of changing home and job spatial distribution in order to focus only on SP-driven schedule changes. There were 722,348 resident workers and 804,760 jobs distributed across 798 transport zones in Warsaw based on the 2015 WTS. SP locations were obtained from Warsaw Trams along with their types and implementation date.

We focused on the 2015–2022 period in our analysis for two reasons. First, 2015 is the earliest year for which GTFS data was readily available. Second, 2015 is the year between when TW finished pilot programs and when it started implementing network-wide SP schemes in 2016. SP changes traffic signals at intersections to prioritize PT vehicles in order to reduce delay and travel time variability leading to an increase in travel speed and a decrease in travel time32. SP is either passive or active. Passive SP provides priority for routes with large PT frequency, typically without major technology, while active SP gives preferences to PT vehicles after detecting them as they approach an intersection and precisely gives permission signal only when it is needed33. Irrespective of the technology, SP can be divided into three levels of effectiveness: full, high, and low. Of Warsaw’s 187 signal priority intersections, 80 are full, 72 are high, and 35 are low. Full priority ensures that a tram travelling through an intersection virtually does not lose any time. In other words, the average control delay per tram, which is the difference between the undisturbed and actual travel time between intersections, is close to zero, since the signaling does not disturb tram movement, as if it is invisible to it. Full priority does not mean absolute priority, which functions on railroads when all road users are stopped at crossing gates. In the tram case, absolute priority would lead to a traffic-jammed city, that is why a full priority guarantees trams will pass the intersection without stopping most of the time, but sometimes it may stop for other road users. That is why full priority has an average time loss close to zero. With high priority, trams stop more often, so the average control delay is up to 5 s. This situation most often occurs at intersections where the tram is not moving in the main direction, and the size of the intersection or other crossing collisions with the tram mean that obtaining full priority would cause too much loss for other traffic participants. In other cases, where the benefit to vehicles for various reasons is not so great, it is said to be low priority (average control delay of up to 15 s).

The idea of full priority is to influence the control process in such a way that when a priority vehicle arrives, a phase is implemented that allows transit without stopping. This requires blocking the possibility of activating a conflict stage to the tram at the time of its arrival. A key condition for the correct prioritization is the early tram detection, which allows modification of the control strategy. Process of creating simplified inter-stop network and calculating accessibility

Following Arias et al.25, we adapt the process of extracting inter-stop travel time from GTFS and modify it to suit our purpose of calculating accessibility to jobs. An important component of our simplified network is a “network segment” which we define as the tram track segment between two consecutive tram stops.

Step 0: Preprocessing. We removed trips starting and ending in tram depots.

Step 1: Select tram network from GTFS data. Since the GTFS for Warsaw contains four modes (tram, commuter rail, metro, bus), the tram network was selected out.

Step 2: Calculating inter-stop travel time. For our calculations, we used a Wednesday schedule for the week preceding Christmas day each year except 2015 when we used November 18 due to data availability. A GTFS dataset is comprised of seven mandatory comma-separated values (.csv) files, and up to 15 optional files. The required files provide data on the agency, stops, stop times, routes, trips, service dates based on a weekly schedule, and calendar exceptions. Optional files provide data on fares, frequency, transfers, route networks, and rules for mapping route alignment among others. We used the stop times file which provides arrival/departure times for each stop in sequence for each trip on each route. The inter-stop travel time was calculated by subtracting the time at the starting stop from the ending stop for each stop pair of a network segment for each route. Since we did not have ridership data available, we did not subtract dwell time from these travel times.

Step 3: Create simplified inter-stop network from tram GTFS data. Since multiple routes share the same network segments, there are multiple travel time values for each segment throughout the day. We took the minimum travel time across all trips for each segment during the morning rush-hour period, specifically between 7:30am and 7:40am. We chose the morning rush hour period to match the direction of travel with the goal of measuring accessibility to jobs and because interference from car congestion at intersections is highest during this time and thus higher need for SP.

Step 4: Intersecting tram routes at intersections. The inter-stop network created in Step 3 has eliminated stop wait times at the beginning of a trip, and now we eliminate transfer walk and wait times by bisecting network segments at tram intersections. Transfer walk times would conflate with our stated goal of analyzing the impact of SP. Travel times on these split segments were updated using linear interpolation. Step 7: Calculating origin–destination matrices. We used ArcPro Network Analyst to generate origin–destination matrices of tram travel times between the 908 zone centroids for each year using the simplified inter-stop network, which has eliminated stop wait times, frequency, route network and origin departure times. A walking speed of 4.8 km/h was used for walking between zone centroids and tram stops36. Walking times replace tram travel times if they are faster for a given centroid pair. Since this will never change between years, it will not have an impact on the analysis.

Step 8: Calculating accessibility to jobs. We use the cumulative opportunity (CO) accessibility metric which counts the number of jobs within a specific travel time threshold from each origin37. It is simple to calculate and understand and its results are very similar to other metrics such as the gravity model metric38. To measure the impact of the types of priority settings, we analyze the relationship between distance to SP and accessibility gain and loss. We calculate distance from zone centroids to the closest SP location of any type. Then we calculate the average distance to SP by SP type (all, full, high, low) and by accessibility gain and loss type. We average twice, for simple gain (positive percentage change) and loss (negative percentage change), and for highest gain (≥ 20%) and highest loss (≥ − 20%).
