Quality of working life and associated factors among higher education professors (QUALIS-DOC): A scoping review protocol
Authors: Jackson Epaminondas de Sousa, Alan Maicon de Oliveira, Lauren Giustti Mazzei, Cristiane de Cássia Bergamaschi, José Fernando Salvador Carrillo, Barbara Protocevich Pina, Rafael Duarte Paes, Luciane Cruz Lopes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quality of Working Life (QWL) is closely linked to the health and well-being of higher education professors, yet the available evidence remains fragmented across heterogeneous indicators and measurement approaches. This scoping review protocol aims to map and synthesise factors associated with professors’ QWL, using Walton’s eight-dimensional model as the analytical framework to identify patterns, gaps, and underexplored areas in the literature. The review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and will be reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Empirical studies involving professors working in undergraduate and/or graduate programmes at public or private higher education institutions will be considered, regardless of country, area of knowledge, or professors’ age, provided their findings can be mapped onto Walton’s model. Only quantitative and mixed-methods studies will be included to map measurable indicators and statistical associations. The review is planned to start in July 2026 and end in July 2027.•Map how QWL has been assessed among higher education professors and how measures align with Walton’s eight dimensions.•Identify factors associated with QWL and highlight gaps to guide future research and institutional actions to improve working conditions in higher education.•The review will identify gaps in the application of Walton’s eight dimensions, particularly regarding social integration and constitutionalism, which remain underexplored in higher education QWL research.

Background

Quality of Working Life (QWL) has been associated with academic staff health and their ability to perform professional activities with satisfaction and well-being [1,2]. By addressing human needs across material and emotional dimensions, QWL supports work environments that foster fulfilment and has become a strategic factor for organisational development and service quality [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. This protocol integrates human factors/work organization, occupational well-being and higher education perspectives to examine how working conditions shape QWL among academic staff.

In higher education, academic work is complex and requires intensive dedication, involving interconnected teaching, research, extension (community outreach) and service activities. Beyond preparing and delivering classes, professors undertake administration, assessment, institutional governance and projects, alongside pressures for scientific productivity and performance evaluation [7,8].

Combined with limited institutional resources, technological innovations and work extending beyond teaching and institutional duties, these demands intensify academic work and increase physical and mental workload [[9], [10], [11]]. This intensification has been associated with elevated stress, impaired performance and adverse effects on professors’ physical and psychological well-being [12,13], as well as difficulties in maintaining work–life balance [3].

The International Labour Organization has highlighted teaching as an occupation associated with several occupational health hazards, with studies linking teachers’ occupational illness and burnout to psychosocial and organizational factors, including low wages, inadequate working conditions, high workload, overcrowded classrooms, lack of autonomy/control, limited workplace support and low reward [[14], [15], [16]]. In higher education settings, professors may also experience work-related health problems, including anxiety, mental fatigue, musculoskeletal complaints and vocal disorders, which may affect QWL and overall health [17,18].

Despite increasing interest, the literature on QWL in higher education remains fragmented. Existing reviews often focus on specific angles such as institutional dimensions, organisational change or work–life balance, with limited conceptual integration across determinants of academic work [7,8,[19], [20], [21]]. Other syntheses emphasise job satisfaction, organising factors into institutional, individual and educational process-related dimensions, without systematically addressing the multidimensional nature of QWL [22]. Integrative and systematic reviews also underscore the topic’s relevance; however, thematic or associative approaches may limit comparability and hinder a comprehensive overview [23,24]. Unlike Bobarnat (2023) [7], which examined academic QWL without applying a prescriptive theoretical framework to categorise findings, the present protocol adopts Walton’s eight-dimensional model to systematically map how reported factors align with specific QWL dimensions.

No existing scoping review has systematically mapped factors associated with professors’ QWL onto Walton’s eight dimensions while also documenting which dimensions are most frequently addressed and which remain neglected. Building on Walton’s multidimensional model, which conceptualises QWL as the result of interactions among key work dimensions [1], this protocol will chart how QWL has been operationalised and measured across studies and will synthesise associated factors within a consolidated theoretical structure. The review will also summarise how findings vary by context and key characteristics (e.g., gender, academic position, institutional type and national context) [7,19].

To address these gaps, a scoping review is appropriate to systematically map factors associated with professors’ QWL. By organising evidence according to Walton’s eight-dimensional model [1], the QUALIS-DOC protocol aims to integrate findings, identify patterns and underexplored areas, and support evidence-informed institutional policies for healthier and more sustainable academic work environments.

Description of protocol

Design

This scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the methodological guidance proposed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [25]. Reporting of both the protocol and the final review will follow the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [26], which are used solely to guide transparent and standardised reporting.

The protocol was registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) [27]. This scoping review aims to address the following research question: “Which factors are associated with QWL among higher education professors?”.

The review is planned to start in July 2026 and end in July 2027. Searches will be executed in July 2026; screening and data extraction will occur from August to December 2026; synthesis, visualisation and manuscript preparation will be conducted from January to July 2027.

We will conduct a scoping review focusing on quantitative and mixed-methods studies because the field predominantly uses standardised instruments and measurable indicators, which allow for the identification of patterns, frequencies and associations among factors related to the QWL of higher education professors. Quantitative evidence is particularly useful for mapping how QWL has been operationalised and assessed across different institutional and national contexts. It should be emphasised that the aim of this review is not to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions or to establish causal relationships, but rather to provide a comprehensive overview of the existing literature, clarify how the topic has been investigated, and identify gaps in current knowledge.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for the inclusion of studies in this scoping review were established based on the PCC framework (Population/Problem, Concept and Context), as detailed below:

Population

Studies involving higher education professors who work in undergraduate and postgraduate courses will be included regardless of the area of knowledge or professors’ age. Full-time, part-time, adjunct, and visiting professors will be included, provided their primary role involves teaching and/or research in higher education.

Concept

Quality of Working Life (QWL) is a field of study concerned with organisational conditions and practices aimed at improving the work environment and employee satisfaction. By emphasising well-being, health, and health-related working conditions that affect physical, mental, and social functioning, QWL seeks to promote efficiency in task performance, the balanced use of human capabilities, and increased productivity [1,28].

For the purposes of this review, QWL is understood as a multidimensional construct grounded in the model proposed by Walton, which integrates multiple and interrelated dimensions of working life [1]. Accordingly, factors such as occupational stress, burnout, job satisfaction, work–life balance, organisational conditions, institutional support and opportunities for professional development [1,19,[29], [30], [31], [32]] will be mapped and categorised within their respective Walton dimensions, as defined and operationalised in the included studies, while respecting the conceptual and methodological approaches adopted by the original authors. Given the multidimensional nature of QWL, studies that investigate multiple dimensions associated with the phenomenon will be included.

Context

Participants must be affiliated with Higher Education Institutions (HEIs), whether public or private, with no restrictions on country or region, and regardless of field of knowledge. For the purposes of this review, higher education refers to degree-granting institutions that offer undergraduate and/or graduate programmes, including universities and degree-granting colleges. Technical or vocational institutions that do not award undergraduate or graduate degrees will be excluded unless they are explicitly described as equivalent to degree-granting higher education within the national context. Institutions offering both degree and non-degree programs will be included only if the study sample specifically comprises professors teaching in degree-granting programs.

Considering that academic titles and the organisation of academic work vary across countries and educational systems, terms such as professor, teacher, lecturer or faculty will be treated as contextual expressions of academic roles in higher education. The analysis will prioritise the function performed in higher education rather than the formal job title. When available, information on academic position, type of institution and employment regime will be extracted descriptively to support the interpretation of contextual differences observed across studies.

Types of studies

Primary studies of observational or experimental design, as well as preprints, dissertations and theses, will be included. Review articles, opinion papers, commentaries, letters to the editor, case reports, methodological studies, instrument adaptation studies, theoretical essays, books, book chapters and conference abstracts will be excluded. Studies published up to the date of the final searches (planned for 31 July 2026) will be eligible.

No publication-year restrictions will be applied to capture the evolution of QWL conceptualisation and measurement in higher education and to avoid excluding foundational instruments and earlier empirical evidence that remain relevant to current practice. To support interpretability, findings will be reported descriptively by publication period (e.g., decades), highlighting temporal patterns in study designs, instruments used, and the distribution of mapped factors across Walton’s eight dimensions.

No language restrictions will be applied. For non-English studies, AI-assisted translation tools may be used as operational support during screening and data extraction. Whenever feasible, translations will be checked by team members with proficiency in the source language. For languages not covered by the team, dual independent machine translations will be used and key extracted items will be verified against the original text. If no team member can verify the translation and dual machine translation yields inconsistent results, the study will be excluded and listed in an appendix as “unavailable due to language barriers.” If critical uncertainty persists, the study will be retained but flagged in the synthesis as having limited extractability due to language constraints.

Only studies with quantitative and mixed-methods designs will be considered eligible, provided that the quantitative component examines QWL and/or factors associated with QWL. Exclusively qualitative studies will be excluded. This decision aligns with the objectives of the review, which focus on mapping measurable indicators, instruments, and statistically examined associations reported in the literature. This exclusion does not imply a devaluation of qualitative approaches, which are recognised as important for understanding academic work experiences; rather, it reflects the protocol’s scope and its emphasis on systematically charting how QWL has been operationalised and measured across studies.

Databases consulted

The following databases will be searched: SCOPUS; LILACS – Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (via the Virtual Health Library – VHL); SciELO; EBSCO; MEDLINE (via PubMed); ERIC – Education Resources Information Center; Google Scholar; BDTD – Brazilian Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations; the Networked Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations/Open Access Theses and Dissertations; Web of Science; and Educ@ – Digital Library of the Carlos Chagas Foundation. These databases cover multidisciplinary and domain-specific fields, such as health, education and social sciences, ensuring access to peer-reviewed and grey literature sources representative of diverse contexts.

A structured search strategy was developed collaboratively by the research team in consultation with a professional librarian and adapted to the specific characteristics of each database to ensure sensitive and precise retrieval of studies relevant to the scope of the review. HRQoL-related terms (e.g., “hrqol”) were retained because some studies apply health-related quality of life instruments to capture work-related health outcomes among professors, which may include QWL-relevant indicators and associated factors. The strategy was assessed in advance by running test searches to confirm feasibility and verify that it retrieves studies consistent with the review question and eligibility criteria. These tests informed minor adjustments to key terms and database-specific syntax. Although broader health-related terms may retrieve non-relevant records, these will be filtered during screening based on the PCC eligibility criteria and the operational definition of degree-granting higher education institutions. Search strategy and key terms used across databases.

Searches will be conducted primarily in English across international databases. For regional and language-specific databases, such as LILACS, SciELO, Educ@ and the Brazilian Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations, search terms will be adapted and applied in the relevant local languages when appropriate, ensuring comprehensive retrieval while maintaining consistency of the core search concepts.

Study selection and data extraction process

Identified citations will be organised using Rayyan software [33], and duplicates will be removed. Prior to formal screening, reviewers will undergo a calibration exercise to standardise eligibility decisions. Calibration will be performed on a random sample of 100 records pooled from Web of Science, Scopus, and ERIC (titles and abstracts) to ensure eligibility criteria are applied consistently across database-specific document types. This will be followed by the evaluation of a minimum of 10 full-text articles selected from the pooled sample. Any disagreements will be discussed to refine operational definitions and decision rules, and calibration will continue until a consistent approach to inclusion and exclusion decisions is achieved.

Potential sources of bias will be mitigated through duplicate screening and extraction by independent reviewers, calibration prior to formal screening, and arbitration by a third reviewer when needed. Language-related bias will be reduced through structured translation procedures and a predefined contingency for records that cannot be reliably translated, which will be listed in an appendix. Classification bias in mapping factors to Walton’s dimensions will be addressed using explicit coding rules (including cross-dimensional coding) and inter-coder agreement checks (Cohen’s kappa on a 10% sample). To reduce publication bias, grey literature sources (e.g., theses/dissertations) will be included and described separately where relevant.

Title and abstract screening will be performed independently by two reviewers working in pairs, in accordance with the eligibility criteria. Records deemed potentially eligible by either reviewer will proceed to full-text assessment. Full texts will be screened independently by two reviewers. Disagreements at any stage will be resolved through discussion and consensus; if consensus is not reached, a third reviewer will arbitrate. Data extraction

Data will be extracted independently by pairs of reviewers using a form developed by the research team (see online supplemental material I). The extraction will capture QWL measures and instruments, QWL-related outcomes, and factors statistically associated with QWL and/or its dimensions, as reported by the original studies, and these data will be mapped to Walton’s eight dimensions. Extracted items will include author, year, study location, publication type, study design, setting, instruments used, relevant outcomes, and associated factors. Factors and outcomes will then be coded and organised according to Walton’s framework to support a transparent and reproducible synthesis.

The data extraction form and any protocol amendments may be refined during the review; all changes will be dated, described, and documented in the OSF registration and transparently reported in the final manuscript. Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus or, if needed, with input from an additional reviewer. When appropriate, study authors may be contacted to obtain missing or clarifying information.

Data analysis and presentation

Results will be synthesised descriptively and presented according to Walton’s eight QWL dimensions framework, allowing comparison of how QWL factors are operationalised across different contexts globally. The geographical distribution of studies will be represented using a global heat map. Quantitative and contextual variables related to the QWL of higher education professors will be systematically extracted and analysed, encompassing not only working conditions and organisational characteristics, but also the individual, sociocultural and institutional contexts in which academic work is performed. These include factors such as country and world region, type of higher education institution, academic position, disciplinary field, employment arrangements and broader cultural and organisational settings that may shape how QWL is experienced and measured. Whenever available, results will be presented in a disaggregated manner according to these contextual characteristics, allowing for a more nuanced interpretation of similarities and differences across settings. Information related to participants’ gender, as well as other contextual and potentially interacting variables reported in the included studies, will be extracted and synthesised descriptively in accordance with the data extraction form in supplementary material. The analysis will encompass both positive and negative factors associated with QWL, recognising the dynamic coexistence of resources and demands within academic work environments.

Analyses and visual representations will be produced using RStudio software, including (i) a heatmap showing the frequency of factors mapped to each of Walton’s eight dimensions across studies, (ii) a bubble plot summarising factor prevalence by country or world region, and (iii) the PRISMA flow diagram documenting study selection. If a factor is described in a way that spans multiple Walton dimensions (e.g., workload affecting both working conditions and work–life balance), it will be coded under all applicable dimensions and flagged as “cross-dimensional.” Inter-coder agreement for dimension coding will be assessed using Cohen’s kappa on a 10% random sample of included studies.

Protocol validation

To support protocol validation, we ran test searches in October 2025 to confirm feasibility and verify that the strategy retrieves relevant studies on QWL among higher education professors. These tests helped refine key terms and database coverage and suggested that the retrieved records are expected to be amenable to organisation using Walton’s eight-dimensional model as the guiding analytical framework. Based on the combined yield across all selected databases and sources, the preliminary test searches returned approximately 13,000 records, indicating a feasible volume for screening while capturing key publications in the field. The final searches will be updated and executed at the start of the formal review.

Limitations

As a scoping review, this study will map concepts, measures, and evidence characteristics and will not conduct a formal risk of bias assessment or estimate pooled effect sizes. In addition, limiting eligibility to quantitative and mixed-methods studies may reduce the coverage of qualitative evidence on subjective experiences of QWL; this trade-off reflects the protocol’s focus on mapping measurable indicators and how they are operationalised across studies. Excluding qualitative studies may overlook how professors subjectively experience and negotiate QWL-related challenges, particularly regarding social integration and constitutionalism, which are often explored in narrative research. The exclusion of unpublished studies (except theses/dissertations) may introduce publication bias, as studies reporting no associations between factors and QWL may be underrepresented. Moreover, some factors may be coded across multiple dimensions when their conceptual scope overlaps, meaning that frequency distributions across Walton’s dimensions will not necessarily represent mutually exclusive categories. Finally, Walton’s model was originally developed for industrial and corporate settings in 1973, and academic-specific dimensions (e.g., academic freedom, teaching–research balance, and peer review pressure) are not explicitly represented; therefore, some findings will need to be mapped onto existing dimensions, which may impose interpretive constraints.
