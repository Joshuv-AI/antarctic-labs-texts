Degradable polymers via controlled alternating ring-opening metathesis copolymerization of bio-sourced oxa-norbornenes and 2,3-dihydrofuran
Authors: Yun Chen, Jia-feng Chen, Zhenshuai Tang, Xiaoyong Zhang, Haiwang Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To mitigate the environmental footprint of the polymer industry, developing sustainable and eco-friendly bio-based polymers has become imperative. Herein, we report the synthesis of degradable, bio-based copolymers via the alternating ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) of biomass-derived oxa-norbornenes (synthesized from itaconic anhydride and furfuryl alcohol) and 2,3-dihydrofuran (DHF). Under optimized conditions, the polymerization proceeded in a controlled manner, yielding predictable molecular weights from the monomer-to-initiator feed ratio and enabling block copolymer synthesis. The unsymmetrical oxa-norbornene monomers underwent ring-opening with moderate regioselectivity. DFT calculations revealed that this selectivity is governed by kinetic control. The thermal and mechanical properties of the copolymers were also investigated. Furthermore, the copolymers underwent degradation under acidic conditions to yield small molecules derived from the original monomer segments. This work establishes a versatile strategy for producing bio-derived, degradable polymers with potential for sustainable applications.

Introduction

The increasing environmental impact of petroleum-based polymers, such as their long-term persistence in ecosystems and dependence on finite fossil fuels, has spurred the need for sustainable polymer economy1. Biomass-derived polymers have emerged as a viable solution, offering renewability, a lower carbon footprint, and potential biodegradability2–4. By using monomers derived from natural sources like plant-based sugars, oils, and lignin, researchers are working to create degradable polymers that maintain strong mechanical and functional performance while mitigating plastic waste challenges5–8. Developing these polymers involves strategic monomer selection and polymerization methods to ensure controlled breakdown under specific environmental conditions9,10. This approach supports the principles of a circular economy and aligns with global sustainability goals in materials innovation.

Sugar or cellulosic biomass can be converted into valuable chemical intermediates, such as furfuryl alcohol, itaconic anhydride, and 2,3-dihydrofuran (DHF), enabling the production of biorenewable polymers (Supplementary Scheme 1)11–13. A biobased oxa-norbornene (oxaNB) has been synthesized from furfuryl alcohol and itaconic anhydride via a tandem Diels–Alder addition and lactonization with a 100% atom-economic efficiency14,15. Esterification of the carboxylic acid on the oxaNB with methanol yielded a monomer (M1 in Scheme 1) suitable for producing a bio-based polymer via ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP). However, polyM1 was found insoluble in any solvent tested, which rules out the characterization and formation of block copolymers14. While copolymerization or derivatization strategies have yielded soluble and functional materials14,16–20, the resulting polymers typically possess non-degradable backbones, which may limit their environmental sustainability.Scheme 1Background of alternating copolymers of DHF and comonomers, and this work. a Alternating copolymerization of DHF with other monomers via ROMP reported in the literature. bAlternating ROMP of DHF with bio-sourced oxaNBs.

To impart degradability on polymers via ROMP, a cyclic monomer featuring a cleavable linkage is generally copolymerized to introduce sites susceptible to degradation21. Among these cyclic monomers, cyclic enol ethers, including DHF, stand out as excellent candidates due to their bio-based origin22 and easy commercial availability23. DHF was reported to undergo alternating (-rich) copolymerization with (oxa)norbornenes24–29, cyclobutenes26, enynes30, and diynes31 to incorporate acid-degradable enol ether into the polymer backbone (Scheme 1a). Notably, these copolymerizations typically exhibit excellent regioselectivity, wherein DHF exclusively inserts in a head-to-tail manner. This regioselective alternating mechanism ensures precise placement of the cleavable enol ether linkage along the polymer backbone, which is important for predictable degradation profiles. Critically, a significant gap remains in the copolymerization of DHF with oxaNBs bearing substituents at the bridgehead position (as in M1). This structural motif is distinct from the previously studied allylic substitution (e.g., methyl at the allyl carbon). In fact, seminal work by Choi et al. demonstrated that oxaNBs with an allylic methyl group fail to copolymerize with DHF, attributed to the deactivated nature of the Ru-Fischer carbene after DHF insertion26. This precedent highlights a fundamental challenge and underscores the unexplored reactivity of bridgehead‑substituted, biomass‑derived oxaNBs like M1 with DHF. Moreover, given the unsymmetrical, monosubstituted nature of M1, the potential impact on the established regioselectivity during its alternating copolymerization with DHF remains an open and important question. This regiochemical uncertainty reflects a common challenge in (co)polymerizing unsymmetrical monomers, where achieving perfect alternation and regioselectivity is often non-trivial10,32–34.

In the present work, we investigate the copolymerization of oxaNBs derived from furfuryl alcohol and itaconic anhydride with DHF. Under optimal conditions, the ROMP initiated with G3 catalyst achieves an alternating sequence (Scheme 1b). We also reveal that oxaNBs can undergo regioselective copolymerization with head-to-head enchainment between oxaNBs and DHF. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate this regioselectivity is kinetically controlled. The polymerization also demonstrated a high degree of living character, enabling precise control over molecular weight and the synthesis of block copolymers. The thermal and mechanical properties of the resultant alternating copolymers were tunable by changing the substituents on oxaNBs. It was found that the copolymers can be readily degraded into derivatives of the original repeating units under acidic conditions.

Results and discussion

Copolymerization behavior

Three oxaNB monomers (Scheme 1b), M1 (methyl-functionalized), M2 (n-butyl-functionalized) and M3 (tetrahydrofurfuryl-functionalized), were synthesized following a previously reported method14. Their characterizations were shown in Supplementary Figs. 10–12. The copolymerization of M1 with DHF was first conducted at a [M1]0:[DHF]0:[G3]0 ratio of 520: 480: 10 mM (from 1H NMR spectrum) in CH2Cl2 at room temperature, targeting a total degree of polymerization (DP) of 100. A rapid copolymerization with near-quantitative conversion of M1 was achieved within 10 min. The reaction was then quenched by adding isobutyl vinyl ether (iBVE), followed by precipitation in methanol. The resultant copolymer was soluble in common solvents including CH2Cl2, CHCl3 and THF, in contrast to the insolubility of polyM1. This successful copolymerization suggests that Fischer-type Ru complexes can still catalyze the metathesis ring opening of M1, because of its significant ring strain. This was confirmed by the calculation of the ring strain energy (RSE) by DFT. The RSE was calculated to be 21.93 kcal/mol, which is 2.5 kcal/mol higher than the unsubstituted oxaNB (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 3). 1H NMR spectrum of the isolated copolymer showed a minimal existence of homosequence of DHF and absence of any homosequence of M1 (Supplementary Fig. 14). The composition ratio of M1 to DHF was calculated as 45:55. We then sought to optimize the polymerization condition to achieve an alternating copolymerization.

Previous studies have indicated that homopolymerization of DHF can be avoided at monomer concentrations lower than 200 mM due to the low ring strain of DHF31,35. The copolymerization was then performed with [M1]0: [DHF]0: [G3]0 = 50: 150: 2 mM. M1 was fully consumed within 1 h. The molecular weight of the copolymer was determined by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) with molecular weight distribution (Đ) < 1.10 and number-average molecular weight (Mn) = 8200 Da, which was close to the theoretical value of 7400 Da (Supplementary Table 1). The micro-structure of the copolymer was characterized by NMR spectra (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 15–19). The composition ratio of M1 and DHF was calculated from peaks 3, 4 and 10 to be 50:50. The disappearance of signals at 5.98 and 6.30 ppm (Fig. 1a–c) indicates the complete suppression of the homoaddition of DHF. The homosequences of M1 were also not observed in the 1H NMR spectra of poly(M1-DHF). 13C NMR spectra of the olefin carbon in poly(M1-DHF), polyM2 and polyDHF were compared in Fig. 1d–f (polyM1 was not used because of its low solubility for 13C spectrum measurement. The 1H NMR characterization of polyM2 was shown in Supplementary Fig. 13). The downfield shift of the methine proton adjacent to oxygen (-O-HC=) clearly indicates the absence of DHF homosequences in the copolymer. Unfortunately, geometric isomerism obscures the detection of M1 homosequences via the characteristic olefin peaks (7 & 8) in the 13C NMR spectrum, despite their absence from the 1H NMR spectrum. Additionally, a retarded copolymerization by the addition of excessive 3-bromopyridine (3-BrPy) relative to G3 was performed to monitor the conversion of both monomers during copolymerization. As shown in the conversion-time plot in Supplementary Fig. 1, the conversion of M1 remains nearly equivalent to 3 times that of DHF, further demonstrating an alternating copolymerization. These results collectively demonstrate that M1 and DHF undergo efficient alternating copolymerization under optimal conditions to give an alternating copolymer. Regio and stereoregularity

Given that both M1 and DHF are unsymmetrical monomers, a regioregular structure from preferred orientation during propagation is expected. Previous reports have documented that the addition of DHF proceeded with complete regioselectivity (i.e., always generating a Fischer carbene) during homo- or copolymerization23,36. While the regioregularity of the propagating DHF chain cannot be elucidated due to peak overlap, the regioselectivity of DHF enchainment can be inferred from the structure of the resulting Ru complex. We first polymerized a short chain of M2. DHF was then added in situ after near-complete consumption of M2 to monitor the signal of the alkylidene proton. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, the alkylidene proton appeared at 15.13 ppm after M2 oligomerized to G3, while the carbene proton shifted upfield to 13.48 ppm upon addition of DHF23, indicating full conversion to alkoxymethylidene. This suggests that during copolymerization, DHF propagates with complete regioselectivity.

To reveal the regioregularity on M1, we measured the 13C, DEPT, COSY, HSQC and HMBC NMR spectra of poly(M1-alt-DHF) as shown in Supplementary Figs. 15–19. We first identified the quaternary carbon in poly(M1-alt-DHF) corresponding to the substituted bridgehead of M1 (C12 and C12’ in Fig. 2a–c). C12 and C12’ are formed by the head-to-head (HH) and head-to-tail (HT) ring opening of M1 with alkoxymethylidene, respectively. The ¹³C NMR, DEPT-90, and DEPT-135 spectra revealed C12/C12’ resonances at ~87 ppm. The signals at 6.50 and 6.66 ppm were assigned to the methine proton (-O-HC = ) of the E-double bond isomer, whereas the signal at 6.15 ppm was assigned to the analogous proton of the Z-isomer. The dominant peak at 6.66 ppm exhibited strong correlation with C12 and was attributed to HH addition of M1 (9(E) in Fig. 2d), whereas the minor peak at 6.50 ppm (9’(E) in Fig. 2d) corresponded to HT addition. The HH:HT ratio of M1 incorporation was thus determined to be 74:26 by peak integration from the 1H NMR spectrum. These results indicate that the ring-opening of M1 by the alkoxymethylidene complex proceeds with moderate regioselectivity.Fig. 2Evaluation of the regioselectivity of M1 propagation.a–d zoomed-in 13C, DEPT 90 and 135 and 13C-1H HMBC NMR spectra of copolymer of M1 and DHF.

On the other hand, the stereoregularity control was also estimated. E content at the vinyl ether double bond in poly(M1-alt-DHF) was calculated to be 45.0% by the peak areas of disproportionated proton 9 (Fig. 1a), which is almost equal to that of the DHF homopolymer (46.8%, Supplementary Fig. 14). Notably, the G2-catalyzed homopolymer showed an E content of 41.0%35, suggesting that the catalyst influences stereoregularity, albeit slightly. While determining the exact E/Z ratio at the alkene double bond remains challenging, future studies could explore alternative catalysts to achieve stereoregularity control across both double bonds.

Mechanistic insights through DFT investigation

To elucidate the origin of regioregularity observed for monomer M1 during copolymerization, we conducted DFT calculations to evaluate the energy barriers for each elementary step (Supplementary Table 4). The propagating chain was modeled as an ethyl-substituted Fischer carbene complex to simplify the system. Note that dissociation of the 3-BrPy ligand from complex G3 was omitted in the calculation, as this step is not involved in the energy-determining process. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, M1 can approach the Fischer carbene in two distinct orientations and form the corresponding metallacyclobutane intermediates (HH-INT1 and HT-INT1). The subsequent retro-[2 + 2] cycloreversion of the metallacyclobutane was identified as both the turnover-limiting and selectivity-determining step (Fig. 3b). The calculated activation barriers for this step are 9.23 kcal/mol for the HH pathway and 11.59 kcal/mol for the HT pathway. Consequently, the HH pathway is kinetically favored by 2.36 kcal/mol, which could be rationalized by analyzing the rate-determining transition states. The HT-TS2 geometry is destabilized by notable steric repulsion between the mesityl ligand and the substrate, manifesting as multiple non-bonded C–H---C contacts shorter than 2.80 Å (Supplementary Fig. 9). Livingness of polymerization

The controlled nature of the copolymerization was assessed by systematically varying the M1-to-G3 ratio. With the increase of M1-to-G3 ratio, the signal belonging to the phenyl end group becomes more and more trivial in 1H NMR (Supplementary Fig. 20). SEC was used to determine the molecular weight. All SEC chromatograms showed unimodal shapes. As summarized in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1, increasing the M1-to-G3 ratio from 12.5:1 to 400:1 resulted in a rise in molecular weight from 6 kDa to 123 kDa, while the Đ remained narrow ( < 1.3) across this broad range (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1). A linear correlation between the M1-to-G3 ratio and the Mn further confirmed well-controlled polymerization. The high livingness of the system was further validated through block copolymerization. After copolymerizing M1 and DHF, the solvent and residual DHF were removed, and a second feed of M2 and DHF was introduced for chain extension. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, the Mn increased from 15.7 kDa to 35.6 kDa while Ð remained below 1.3. The 1H NMR spectrum of the block copolymer contained all characteristic proton signals from the M1-DHF and M2-DHF repeating units (Supplementary Fig. 21). The successful block copolymerization demonstrates excellent chain-end fidelity and control. We further attempted to extend the system by targeting a higher DP of 800 for a diblock copolymer (400 for each block). While the polymerization of the first block remained controlled, the chain growth during the second block deviated from living behavior. Thermal and mechanical properties

The thermal properties of P1, P2 and P3 were assessed with thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). TGA revealed that three alternating copolymers have excellent thermal stability with Td above 330 °C (Supplementary Fig. 6). The glass transition temperature (Tg) was measured with DSC. The Tg values of P1, P2, and P3 correspond to their respective side-chain architectures. P1 exhibits the highest Tg (81.7 °C), as expected for its rigid, unsubstituted oxaNB backbone. The Tg of P2 is significantly lower (47.6 °C, Supplementary Fig. 6), which is attributed to the internal plasticization effect of its flexible n-butyl side chain. P3 shows an intermediate Tg of 52.4 °C, a slight increase relative to P2. This is consistent with the replacement of the flexible alkyl chain with a more conformationally restricted, cyclic tetrahydrofurfuryl substituent.

The mechanical properties of P1, P2, and P3 were evaluated by uniaxial tensile testing of rectangular strips (Supplementary Fig. 7), and the results are summarized in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 2. As depicted in Fig. 5, all polymers behaved as viscoelastic materials. P1 gave elastic modulus (E) = 25.4 MPa, ultimate tensile strength = 0.77 MPa and toughness = 5.54 MJ m-3. P2 exhibited slightly better mechanical properties, with E = 27.3 Ma, ultimate tensile strength of 0.86 MPa and toughness of 7.25 MJ m-3. P3 was characterized by a significantly lower modulus (E = 10.2 MPa) yet the highest ultimate tensile strength (0.88 MPa) and toughness (11.27 MJ/m-³) in the series. Notably, all polymers showed excellent ductile properties with elongation at break (ε) being 640%, 671% and 1286% for P1, P2, and P3, respectively. Stability and degradation

The chemical stability and degradability of the synthesized polymers were subsequently investigated to evaluate their environmental persistence and potential for triggered decomposition. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, the SEC traces of a representative copolymer of M1 and DHF (initial Mn ≈ 54 kDa) showed a clear shift to a lower molecular weight (Mn ≈ 21 kDa) after storage under ambient conditions for 14 days, indicating backbone degradation. This spontaneous instability aligns with previous reports by Xia et al., which established that poly(enol ether) backbones undergo hydrolysis under ambient or acidic conditions29. Consequently, storage under slightly basic conditions is required to prevent unintended degradation during handling.

To verify the acid-triggered degradability, copolymer P1 was treated with HCl solution in a mixed solvent of MeOH/THF. As shown in Fig. 6a, the SEC peaks changed to a peak with products of <1 kDa, which was attributed to the products of repeating units. The degradation products were isolated and then characterized with NMR and mass spectra. The 1H NMR spectra of the crude and the main isolated product were very close, implying a complete degradation (Fig. 6b, c). Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated the regioselective, alternating, and living copolymerization of biomass-derived oxaNBs with DHF using a G3 initiator, yielding degradable copolymers. DFT calculations revealed that the ring-opening of oxaNBs by the alkoxymethylidene during ROMP is kinetically favored in a head-to-head (HH) orientation, governing the observed regioselectivity. The resulting copolymers showed good thermal stability and tunable Tg dependent on the side chain on oxaNB repeating units. The copolymers were fully degradable under acidic conditions, breaking down into small molecules. Given the bio-based origin of both monomers and the degradability of the copolymers, this work offers a sustainable strategy to reduce reliance on petroleum-derived polymers and address plastic pollution.
