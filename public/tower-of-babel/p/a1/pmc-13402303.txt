Anti-interference framework for weakly supervised thermal defect segmentation on high-rise building facades
Authors: Zeming Zhao, Yongqiang Jin, Daiming Liu, Kaimu Wei, Dingcheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfrared thermography is crucial for ensuring the structural safety of high-rise building facades, yet accurate defect quantification typically requires expensive pixel-level fully supervised annotations. While weakly supervised semantic segmentation reduces annotation costs, existing methods struggle with low-quality pseudo-labels and inaccurate boundaries in infrared images. To address this problem, this paper proposes an anti-interference-driven segmentation framework for infrared facade defects, in which the SAM 2 segmentation stage is trained under weak supervision using box prompts and pseudo-mask guidance. Firstly, an improved DeepLabV3+ interference rejection module is constructed, incorporating a boundary-aware triplet loss and simple linear iterative clustering superpixel consistency constraints to eliminate pseudo-thermal anomalies. Secondly, YOLOV11 is trained to localize defects on the interference-suppressed images and provides bounding box prompts for the subsequent SAM 2 segmentation stage. Finally, a weakly supervised fine-tuning strategy based on the segment anything model 2 is proposed, where box prompts and pseudo-mask supervision are used for pixel-level mask learning without relying on manually annotated defect masks during the segmentation training stage. Validated on 180 infrared images from 50 high-rise buildings, the method achieves interference rejection (mean Intersection over Union of 76.2%) and precise localization (mean average precision at 0.5 of 86.5%). Furthermore, it attains pixel-level segmentation with 63.4% Miou, 87.0% F1, 83.1% precision, 91.3% recall, and 92.2% mAP@0.5 using only box-level annotations. This study demonstrates the feasibility of high-precision thermal defect segmentation for large-scale facade inspection.Keywords: DeepLabV3+, Defect detection, Infrared thermography, SAM 2, Weakly supervised segmentation, YOLOV11Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the acceleration of urbanization, the number of high-rise buildings has increased rapidly, and their facades, long exposed to complex and dynamic environmental conditions, are highly susceptible to structural defects such as hollow, cracking, water seepage, and detachment due to sudden temperature variations, rain erosion, and material aging1. In recent years, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) equipped with infrared thermal sensors have been increasingly adopted for facade inspection of high-rise buildings owing to their non-contact measurement capability, long-range sensing, and high inspection efficiency2. Infrared sensors detect anomalies by capturing variations in surface thermal radiation, providing an effective means for identifying latent defects that are difficult to observe using visible-light imaging. However, under practical high-altitude flight conditions and complex outdoor environments, infrared imaging is inherently affected by environmental temperature fluctuations, non-uniform thermal radiation, sensor noise, and limited spatial resolution. These sensor-related factors often result in infrared images characterized by significant noise, pseudo-hotspot interference, and blurred defect boundaries. Such uncertainties arising from the sensing mechanism and imaging characteristics make pixel-wise manual annotation extremely challenging and costly3. Moreover, the inspection of a single high-rise building typically involves covering hundreds of meters in height and acquiring thousands of infrared images, leading to a massive volume of data and further exacerbating the dependence of fully supervised learning methods on extensive high-quality annotations. Although accurate quantification of defect areas is critical for assessing structural risk levels and dynamically monitoring defect evolution, and is commonly achieved through pixel-level semantic segmentation techniques4 to support refined maintenance strategies such as defining localized repair scopes, estimating reinforcement corrosion protection lengths, and delineating waterproof layer renewal regions5, traditional fully supervised segmentation approaches struggle to meet the efficiency and scalability requirements imposed by sensor-induced data limitations in real-world applications6.

Distinct from fully supervised segmentation methods, weakly supervised semantic segmentation (WSSS) utilizes weak annotations such as image-level labels, bounding boxes, points, or scribbles to replace costly pixel-wise labeling7, and progressively recovers pixel-level predictions through pseudo-label generation, class activation mapping (CAM) mining8, constraint propagation, and prompt-driven fine-tuning9. Compared with fully supervised frameworks, WSSS reduces annotation cost while improving scalability and robustness to annotation noise10, making it suitable for large-scale and real-world scenarios where fine-grained annotations are difficult to obtain11. As a result, WSSS has been widely applied in medical imaging12, remote sensing interpretation13, and industrial defect detection14, and provides a feasible solution for high-rise building facade defect segmentation15. However, existing studies are still mainly limited to visible-light scenarios and suffer from incomplete supervision, blurred boundaries, and limited generalization capability. For example, Zhang et al.16 proposed a point-seed-based framework with superpixel-based CRF propagation, which is sensitive to initial seed placement and prone to noisy pseudo-labels; Wang et al.17 introduced a self-attention-based weakly supervised network for point cloud data, where sparse representations often lead to inaccurate boundary delineation; Li et al.18 developed a CAM-based iterative refinement method, but the generated pseudo-labels remain unstable under complex illumination conditions. Although these approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of weak supervision in visible-light tasks, they cannot be directly applied to infrared inspection due to thermal noise interference, radiation non-uniformity, and boundary diffusion effects inherent in infrared imaging19, highlighting the need for WSSS methods specifically designed for infrared characteristics.

Compared with visible-light scenarios, infrared thermography depends on surface thermal radiation distribution, making images more sensitive to environmental temperature variations, thermal diffusion, and radiation non-uniformity, which leads to blurred boundaries and low signal-to-noise ratios. Under such conditions, only a limited number of studies have explored weakly supervised semantic segmentation for infrared defect detection on high-rise building facades. For instance, Kirimtat et al.20 combined a meta-learning convolutional neural network with bounding-box-level annotations for defect classification and detection; however, their method lacks pixel-level segmentation capability, limiting quantitative analysis of defect regions. Shi et al.21 proposed a semi-supervised framework based on perturbation invariance to suppress pseudo-label noise and enable pixel-level segmentation, but it remains sensitive to thermal gradient variations and still requires a relatively large proportion of annotated samples. Overall, existing methods still fail to adequately address the technical gap of pixel-level defect segmentation in infrared facade images under limited manual supervision. Compared with visible-light images, infrared images are more affected by pseudo-hotspot interference, thermal diffusion, radiation non-uniformity, low contrast, and blurred boundaries. These characteristics increase the difficulty of dense pixel-wise annotation and reduce the reliability of generic weakly supervised segmentation methods in complex facade inspection scenarios.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes an anti-interference-driven weakly supervised semantic segmentation framework tailored to infrared thermography. Specifically, an improved DeepLabV3+ is developed by incorporating a boundary-aware triplet loss and SLIC superpixel consistency constraints to suppress pseudo-thermal interference and enhance spatial consistency. In addition, a category-adaptive multi-strategy pseudo-mask generator is designed to integrate thermal intensity and gradient-based priors, bridging the gap between coarse bounding-box supervision and fine-grained defect structures. Furthermore, a composite loss combining IoU score regression and Focal loss is introduced to handle class imbalance, boundary ambiguity, and confidence calibration. These components collectively enable accurate defect localization and pixel-level segmentation under bounding-box supervision in infrared imagery.

In “Weakly supervised segmentation method” section elaborates on the proposed weakly supervised segmentation method, focusing on the pseudo-mask generator and the composite loss function. In “Experimental dataset” section describes the construction of the dataset. In “Experiments and results analysis” section analyzes the experimental settings and results. Finally, in “Conclusion” concludes the paper.

Core models

Improved DeepLabV3+ model

DeepLabV3+ is a representative encoder–decoder architecture for semantic segmentation, designed to capture long-range contextual information while maintaining high-resolution predictions. Its basic architecture is shown in Fig. 1. It employs atrous convolution to enlarge the receptive field without reducing feature map resolution, and integrates the Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) module to aggregate multi-scale contextual features, enhancing robustness to objects with varying sizes22. The decoder further refines object boundaries through upsampling and fusion with low-level features. However, when applied to infrared images, the model is susceptible to blurred boundaries caused by thermal diffusion and the dominance of large-area structures, leading to reduced sensitivity to small-scale defects23. To address these limitations, this study improves DeepLabV3+ by incorporating a boundary-aware triplet loss and SLIC-based superpixel consistency constraints, which enhance boundary discrimination and spatial consistency, thereby improving defect representation in infrared scenarios .

DeepLabV3+ architecture.

YOLOV11 model

YOLOV11 is a recent real-time object detection model that provides efficient and accurate object localization, particularly for small targets in complex backgrounds24. In this study, YOLOV11 is employed as a defect localization module and further adapted through lightweight fine-tuning. Specifically, interference-suppressed images are used as inputs to reduce background noise, a bounding box quality-aware loss is introduced to improve localization accuracy for blurred infrared defect boundaries, and MixUp data augmentation is applied to enhance generalization across different facade materials.

In this study, YOLOV11 serves as the defect localization module between interference suppression and SAM 2 segmentation. The YOLOV11 detector was trained using manually annotated bounding boxes of defect regions rather than bounding boxes derived from pixel-wise masks. During the subsequent SAM 2 stage, the bounding boxes predicted by YOLOV11 are used as box prompts, and pseudo-masks are generated within these predicted regions for weakly supervised segmentation training.

Segment anything model 2

Released by Meta AI in July 2024, SAM 2 represents the latest generation of universal segmentation frameworks built upon the large-scale visual foundation model, SAM. Compared to its predecessor, SAM 2 achieves comprehensive improvements in segmentation precision, cross-scenario adaptability, prompt responsiveness, and the processing of video and sequential images. Its architecture comprises a Hiera image encoder, a prompt encoder, and a lightweight mask decoder. It supports arbitrary prompt forms—such as points, bounding boxes, or scribbles—and generates high-quality segmentation results in a single inference pass. These capabilities enable the model to flexibly adapt to weakly supervised or semi-automated segmentation requirements under diverse task conditions25.

Weakly supervised segmentation method

The core methodology of this study is an anti-interference-driven framework structured into three progressive stages: infrared interference suppression, weak supervision signal localization, and fine-grained boundary reconstruction. The methodological novelty lies in the transformation of existing architectures into an infrared-specialized pipeline through targeted algorithmic interventions. In the first stage, the improved DeepLabV3+ serves as an anti-interference engine that employs boundary-aware triplet supervision and superpixel consistency constraints to suppress the pervasive thermal noise and reflection artifacts common in high-rise environments. In the second stage, an optimized YOLOV11 architecture is utilized to generate high-confidence bounding boxes on the interference-suppressed feature maps, which provides a filtered and reliable weak supervision signal by mitigating background confusion. The critical innovation occurs in the third stage, where we propose a weak supervision fine-tuning strategy for the segment anything model 2 (SAM 2). This strategy utilizes a category-adaptive pseudo-mask generator that exploits the distinct thermal radiation patterns of different defect types—such as the orange–yellow concentration of hollows and the natural diffusion of seepage—to drive the model’s responsiveness to infrared-specific gradients. Combined with a composite loss function for multi-task gradient optimization, this framework achieves a stable transition from coarse localization to pixel-level recovery without requiring expensive manual masks, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Three-stage flowchart.

Following interference suppression and defect localization, a pseudo-mask generation mechanism is introduced to provide structured supervision for SAM 2 fine-tuning. By integrating thermal distribution characteristics with local structural priors, the regions within the bounding boxes are further analyzed to generate more informative pseudo-masks. Based on this design, a multi-strategy fusion pseudo-mask generator is constructed to produce high-confidence initial masks for defect regions.

A multi-strategy fusion pseudo-mask generator for infrared defects

To overcome the limited adaptability of generic foundation models to the low-contrast and blurred-boundary characteristics of infrared imagery, we designed a category-adaptive multi-strategy fusion pseudo-mask generator. The fundamental contribution of this module is its ability to utilize YOLOV11 bounding boxes as an entry point for parallel extraction of multi-physical prior features. Unlike traditional prompt-based segmentation methods, our approach constructs four specialized heuristic paths for hollow, water seepage, cracks, and detachment. The pseudo-mask generation process uses the defect category label of each bounding box to select the corresponding defect-specific strategy. The category labels are obtained from the box-level YOLO-format annotations, where each bounding box contains both location and class information. These labels are used only to guide the selection of physical priors for pseudo-mask generation, including high-temperature features for hollow, low-temperature uniform regions for water seepage, linear structures for cracks, and contour or edge-density features for detachment. No manually annotated pixel-level masks are used in this process. By integrating these defect-specific physical priors into a structurally stable initial probability map, the generator provides SAM 2 with high-confidence supervision that reflects the actual physical boundaries of thermal defects, addressing the “information scarcity” problem in weakly supervised learning.

Defect-specific pseudo-mask generation strategies

For hollow defects, leveraging their characteristics of orange-yellow high temperatures and local concentration, hotspot regions are extracted using color thresholding, high luminance quantiles, and texture enhancement, which are then combined with GrabCut and edge information to obtain blob-like regions with clear contours26. Specifically, for hollow defects, the HSV color range is defined from 10, 40, 150 to 30, 255, 255, and pixels above the 75th percentile of grayscale intensity are selected as high-temperature candidates. For water seepage defects, low-temperature seeds are defined using pixels below the 40th percentile of grayscale intensity and further constrained by a local standard deviation threshold of 0.15, followed by region growing to recover diffusion boundaries. For crack defects, Canny edge detection, Hough transform, and skeletonization are jointly applied, and linear structures are filtered based on an aspect ratio threshold of 3.0 and a maximum width of 50 pixels27. For detachment defects, adaptive thresholding based on the Otsu method, compactness filtering with a threshold of 0.6, and edge density analysis with a threshold of 0.4 are used to extract irregular regions. These strategies jointly utilize multi-modal information to provide more reliable initial supervision for SAM 2.

Category-adaptive fusion and morphological post-processing

To integrate multi-path features into a final mask for SAM 2 fine-tuning, a category-adaptive fusion and morphological post-processing strategy is adopted, where weighted fusion is applied to multi-modal features according to defect types. For hollow defects, color features are assigned a weight of 0.5, while texture, GrabCut, and edge features are assigned 0.2, 0.2, and 0.1. For seepage defects, color, texture, and GrabCut features are equally weighted at 0.3, with edge features weighted at 0.1. For crack defects, texture features dominate with a weight of 0.5, while color and GrabCut features are assigned 0.2 each and edge features 0.1. For detachment defects, color, texture, and GrabCut features are equally weighted at 0.3, with edge features assigned 0.1. The fused probability map is binarized using a threshold of 0.5, while a threshold of 0.4 is used for crack defects to preserve thin structures. Morphological opening and closing operations are then applied using elliptical structuring elements with kernel sizes of 3 and 12, followed by connected component analysis to retain the largest region. In addition, pseudo-masks are filtered using a minimum area of 100 pixels and bounding box coverage ratios from 0.15 to 0.92 to suppress noise and remove invalid regions.

In the proposed fine-tuning strategy, the SAM 2 model is not used as a frozen inference module but is further optimized to adapt to infrared defect characteristics. Specifically, the image encoder, prompt encoder, and mask decoder are jointly updated during training, rather than being kept fixed as in standard prompt-based inference. The bounding boxes generated by the YOLOV11 detector are used as box prompts, while the pseudo-masks serve as supervisory signals for pixel-level learning. During training, the predicted masks from SAM 2 are directly compared with the corresponding pseudo-masks, and the composite loss function is computed to guide optimization. This differs from standard SAM usage, where no parameter update is performed and the model relies solely on prompt guidance. By introducing pseudo-mask supervision and enabling end-to-end optimization, the proposed strategy allows SAM 2 to better adapt to infrared-specific patterns and improves segmentation accuracy.

Experimental dataset

The experimental data were collected from over 30 residential communities in Chengdu, covering 50 high-rise buildings with various facade materials. Infrared images were acquired under stable weather conditions using a DJI Matrice 350 RTK drone equipped with a Zenmuse H30T thermal camera. During data collection, the flight distance was maintained at approximately 10 m, and images were captured at fixed intervals to ensure consistency. The system provides reliable high-resolution thermal imaging for accurate facade defect detection.

Interference and defect characteristic description

Interference characteristics: The five types of interferences detected in this study exhibit the following characteristics: (1) Sky (the region marked by green dashed lines on the right side of Fig. 3a), which is characterized by uniform temperature distribution and is mostly located at the left or right edges of the image; (2) Windows (the area outlined by green dashed lines in Fig. 3b), which present as strip-like or block-like regions with uneven temperature distribution; (3) Channel-shaped structural components (green dashed rectangular boxes arranged regularly in Fig. 3c), typically exhibiting regular rectangular shapes and periodic arrangement; (4) Filler wall interference (the green rectangular area in the middle of Fig. 3d), featuring straight boundaries and high regional connectivity; and (5) Circular apertures (small circular holes within the green square in the bottom right corner of Fig. 3e), mostly manifesting as local small-scale thermal anomalies with irregular shapes but clear boundaries.

Examples of five types of interference.

Defect Characteristics: Facade defects are primarily categorized into four types: hollow, water seepage, cracks, and detachment. Specifically: (1) Hollow (the yellow region presented within the green box in Fig. 4a) primarily occurs in building materials such as sandstone and brick masonry; its formation often stems from insufficient tensile strength of the materials themselves. (2) Water Seepage (the dark black region within the green box in Fig. 4b) occurs where rainwater penetrates into the wall interior through weak points in the external facade. (3) Cracks (the linear region within the green box in Fig. 4c) are generated due to reasons such as uneven foundation settlement, thermal stress, or excessive loads. (4) Detachment (the region in the green box in Fig. 4d) results from the separation of the finishing layer from the main body under environmental effects, caused by the loss of adhesion between the finishing layer and the substrate or by hollow. Dataset construction

To facilitate research on infrared defect detection for high-rise building facades, this study constructed a dataset containing both interference regions and defect regions. All interference and defect regions were annotated using the LabelMe tool, including both pixel-wise masks and bounding-box labels. In the first-stage interference suppression task, the DeepLabV3+ model was trained using only bounding-box-level annotations of interference regions, while the pixel-wise masks were used for performance evaluation. In the second-stage defect localization task, YOLOV11 was trained using box-level annotations of defect regions. During the third-stage SAM 2 training process, manually annotated pixel-wise defect masks were not used for optimization. Instead, the bounding boxes generated by YOLOV11 and the generated pseudo-masks were used for weakly supervised training. The pixel-wise defect masks were used only for the final quantitative evaluation of segmentation performance.

The interference annotations are categorized into five classes with a total of 392 instances: 106 sky, 172 window interferences, 52 channel-shaped structural components, 32 filler wall interferences, and 30 circular apertures, which fully cover common pseudo-thermal anomaly patterns in actual scenarios, providing diverse samples for the training and validation of the interference segmentation model. The defect annotations include four classes with a total of 595 instances: 416 water seepage, 124 hollow, 43 cracks, and 12 detachment, encompassing typical defect evolution stages ranging from early-stage seepage to severe detachment, reflecting significant differences in morphology, scale, and thermal characteristics. To ensure experimental fairness and the credibility of results, the dataset was randomly divided into a training set (125 images) and a test set (55 images) at a ratio of 7:3. For hyperparameter tuning, the training set was further divided into training and validation subsets. The validation subset was used for grid search of the loss weights and pseudo-mask thresholds, while the test set was used only for the final performance evaluation. The test set contains instances of all five interference categories and four defect categories, covering typical pseudo-thermal anomalies and defect patterns; by balancing class diversity with distributional representativeness, it provides a comprehensive and equitable testing benchmark for evaluating the model’s performance and verifying its generalization ability in both interference rejection and defect segmentation tasks.

To quantify the annotation burden, this study compared box-level annotation with pixel-wise mask annotation. The dataset contains 595 defect instances. Based on the annotation process in this study, drawing a bounding box required approximately 10 s per instance, while pixel-wise mask annotation required approximately 60 s per instance. Therefore, box-level annotation required approximately 1.65 h in total, compared with 9.92 h for pixel-wise mask annotation, reducing the annotation time by approximately 83.3%.

Experiments and results analysis

Hyperparameter settings

During the training of the first-stage DeepLabV3+ interference segmentation network, input images were uniformly resized to 640 × 640 pixels to ensure consistent feature extraction scales. The optimizer employed was mini-batch Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with a batch size set to 12. The initial learning rate was set to 1 × 10−4, with a weight decay of 1 × 10−4 applied to suppress overfitting. The superpixel auxiliary module was implemented based on the Simple Linear Iterative Clustering (SLIC) algorithm, with the number of superpixels fixed at 300 and the compactness parameter set to 60, balancing regional regularity with segmentation fineness. A minimum region area threshold of 20 pixels was used to eliminate invalid tiny superpixels.

For the training of the YOLOV11 defect detection model in the second stage, the interference-free images output from the first stage were used as input, with the training process iterating for 300 epochs. An SGD optimizer was adopted with a batch size of 12 and 8 data loading workers, and the training was deployed on a single GPU to enhance efficiency. In the inference phase, the confidence threshold was set to 0.2 and the NMS IoU threshold to 0.6; these settings effectively eliminated low-confidence predictions and suppressed redundant boxes. To mitigate potential overfitting on the limited dataset, several regularization and augmentation strategies were implemented. Firstly, we adopted the MixUp data augmentation strategy to randomly superimpose images from different building facades, effectively expanding the feature space and enhancing the model’s generalization across various finishing materials. Secondly, rather than training from scratch, we performed lightweight adaptive fine-tuning on pre-trained YOLOV11 and SAM 2 architectures, which significantly reduces the data requirement for convergence. Furthermore, gradient clipping with a threshold of 1.0 and a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler were employed to ensure stable optimization and prevent the model from getting trapped in local optima or overfitting to specific noise patterns.

In the third stage of defect segmentation, a fine-tuning strategy based on the SAM 2 architecture was adopted to achieve precise pixel-level segmentation of building facade defects within the detection boxes from the second stage. During training, the input image scale remained consistent with the detection stage. The model was trained for a total of 100 epochs using mini-batch gradient descent, with a batch size of 4 and 4 data loading workers to optimize memory usage. The AdamW optimizer was selected with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4, combined with a 1000-step linear learning rate warmup and a cosine annealing scheduling strategy to stabilize the early training process and facilitate later convergence. To fine-tune the image encoder, prompt encoder, and mask decoder of SAM 2 simultaneously, a multi-component collaborative training mode was employed. Regarding the loss function design, a four-fold weighted combination of Dice Loss, Binary Cross Entropy Loss, Focal Loss, and IoU Score Loss was constructed with weights set to 0.35, 0.3, 0.15, and 0.1, respectively. This configuration aimed to synergistically optimize the region overlap of segmentation masks, pixel-level precision, the balance between hard and easy samples, and prediction confidence. Furthermore, mixed-precision training was enabled to improve efficiency, and a gradient clipping threshold of 1.0 was set to ensure the stability of large-scale model training. To ensure the statistical significance of the results, all ablation experiments and comparative experiments were conducted through three independent repetitions. The performance metrics reported in the subsequent tables are presented as mean ± standard deviation across three independent runs. In addition, a threefold cross-validation experiment was conducted to further evaluate the performance variation of the proposed framework under different data partitions.

Model evaluation metrics

Model performance evaluation employs a multi-dimensional metric framework to comprehensively measure the precision and robustness of both interference segmentation and defect detection tasks. The effectiveness of interference segmentation is quantified by precision (Pre), recall (Rec), and mean intersection over union (Miou). These three metrics respectively characterize the overall correctness of pixel classification, the capability to capture positive samples, and the overlap consistency between predicted regions and ground truth annotations in terms of shape, location, and scale. Furthermore, the F1-score is introduced to evaluate the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a comprehensive measure of the model’s balanced performance in defect segmentation. Meanwhile, Miou and F1 are considered the primary metrics for assessing pixel-level segmentation quality, while mask-level mAP@0.5 is used as a supplementary metric for segmentation consistency.

To comprehensively verify the effectiveness of the improved DeepLabV3+ segmentation method, four sets of experiments were designed to analyze the results of the proposed improvement schemes. The comparison primarily relies on three metrics: precision (Pre), recall (Rec), and mean intersection over union (Miou). Training parameters and environmental conditions were kept consistent across all experimental groups.

Comparative experiments on defect segmentation

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed weakly supervised segmentation framework, this study includes comparisons with both fully supervised instance segmentation models and representative weakly supervised segmentation approaches. In addition to instance segmentation models (YOLOV11-seg, YOLOV12-seg, and Mask R-CNN), we further incorporate typical weakly supervised methods, including box-supervised segmentation (BoxInst), CAM-based segmentation (SEAM), and recent SAM-based weakly supervised frameworks (SAMed), to provide a more comprehensive and task-relevant evaluation.

All comparison methods, including YOLOV11-seg, YOLOV12-seg, Mask R-CNN, BoxInst, SEAM, and SAMed, were retrained or fine-tuned on the constructed infrared facade defect dataset. The same training and testing split, input image size, and evaluation metrics were used for all methods. For weakly supervised methods, the required supervision signals were derived from the same training annotations under the corresponding settings of each method, while the pixel-wise masks were used only for evaluation.

The quantitative results in Table 3 show that interference removal affects segmentation performance across different models. Discussion on error propagation in the multi-stage pipeline

Since the proposed framework consists of multiple sequential stages, potential error propagation between stages should be considered. In the first stage, if the interference rejection module incorrectly removes weak thermal responses belonging to true defects, the subsequent detector may receive incomplete or overly simplified inputs, which can reduce downstream recall. Conversely, if some pseudo-thermal anomalies are not fully suppressed, they may still interfere with the defect detector and introduce false-positive box prompts for the segmentation stage. In the second stage, YOLOV11 predictions provide the box prompts for SAM 2 and define the regions used for pseudo-mask generation. Therefore, detection errors from YOLOV11 can propagate to the segmentation stage. Missed detections cannot be recovered by SAM 2, while inaccurate bounding boxes may produce incomplete pseudo-masks or include irrelevant background regions, thereby affecting the final mask quality.

Nevertheless, the experimental results suggest that the proposed design provides a certain degree of robustness against such error accumulation. The interference rejection stage improves the overall input quality for most downstream detectors and segmentation models, while the category-adaptive pseudo-mask generator and quality filtering mechanism help suppress low-quality supervision before SAM 2 optimization. In addition, the ablation results in “Ablation study on pseudo-mask generator” to “Stage-wise ablation and error propagation analysis” sections show that the full pseudo-mask generator, full SAM 2 fine-tuning, and composite loss design all contribute to stabilizing the final segmentation performance. These components partially mitigate the effect of upstream errors by improving boundary refinement, region consistency, and prompt adaptation. Nevertheless, the proposed framework remains a cascaded system, and its final performance still depends on the reliability of earlier stages. Future work will therefore consider uncertainty-aware interaction or more tightly coupled joint optimization across stages to further reduce error propagation.

Limitations

Several limitations remain in this study. Firstly, the dataset contains 180 infrared images from 50 high-rise buildings, which is limited for a multi-stage deep learning framework. Although threefold cross-validation was conducted, images from the same building may still appear across different folds, which may affect the evaluation of cross-building transferability. Future work will expand the dataset and investigate cross-building evaluation protocols under independent building conditions.

Secondly, the defect categories are imbalanced, with 416 water seepage, 124 hollow, 43 crack, and 12 detachment instances. The per-class results show that segmentation performance differs across categories due to both sample distribution and defect-specific thermal characteristics. Future work will include additional samples for underrepresented categories and class-balanced training strategies.

Thirdly, the pseudo-mask generation module contains manually defined thresholds, including HSV ranges, grayscale quantiles, morphology kernels, edge-density thresholds, and binarization thresholds. These parameters were selected based on the validation subset and defect-specific thermal characteristics. The sensitivity analysis of binarization thresholds shows that segmentation performance is maintained within the tested threshold range. However, the joint influence of multiple threshold parameters under different infrared acquisition conditions still requires further evaluation. Future work will investigate multi-parameter sensitivity analysis and adaptive threshold selection strategies.

Fourthly, the full fine-tuning strategy updates more SAM 2 components than decoder-only or prompt-and-decoder fine-tuning and therefore requires higher computational resources. Future work will further evaluate the trade-off between segmentation accuracy and computational cost under different deployment conditions.

Conclusion

An anti-interference-driven weakly supervised semantic segmentation framework is developed for infrared defect detection on high-rise building facades, consisting of three stages: interference suppression, defect localization, and weakly supervised segmentation. Experimental results verify the effectiveness of the overall design.

The improved DeepLabV3+ model achieves 76.2% Miou in interference segmentation, improving input quality by suppressing pseudo-thermal regions. The YOLOV11 detector achieves 86.8% Precision, 81.2% Recall, and 86.5% mAP@0.5 on interference-suppressed images, providing reliable bounding box prompts. Based on these prompts, the SAM 2 segmentation stage, guided by the proposed pseudo-mask generator and composite loss function, achieves 63.4% Miou, 87.0% F1, 83.1% Precision, 91.3% Recall, and 92.2% mAP@0.5 under box-level supervision. The results indicate that the proposed SAM 2 segmentation stage achieves pixel-level defect segmentation under box-level supervision and pseudo-mask guidance in infrared images. The framework provides a method for defect inspection and quantitative analysis of high-rise building facades.
