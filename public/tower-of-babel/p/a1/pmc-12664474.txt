Authors: Rossella Rispoli, Massimiliano Visocchi, Francesco Signorelli, Barbara Cappelletto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The craniovertebral junction (CVJ) is an anatomically complex and surgically challenging region, where critical neurovascular structures converge. Traditional surgical approaches to this area have involved high risks of morbidity due to the intricate anatomy and limited surgical corridors. Recent advancements in minimally invasive techniques, imaging, robotic assistance, and artificial intelligence are revolutionizing CVJ surgery. This article reviews current innovations shaping the future of CVJ surgery, emphasizing improved safety, precision, and patient outcomes.

Introduction

The craniovertebral junction (CVJ) forms the critical interface between the skull base and the upper cervical spine, housing vital neural and vascular elements such as the brainstem, spinal cord, vertebral arteries, and multiple cranial nerves. Pathologies affecting this region, including traumatic fractures, congenital malformations like basilar invagination, inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis, and tumors, pose unique surgical challenges due to the region's complex anatomy and high stakes (Tibbs et al., 2017; Goel, 2017). Historically, surgical access to the CVJ involved extensive open approaches, often associated with significant morbidity and prolonged recovery. However, technological progress in surgical methods, imaging, and adjunct tools is transforming the management of CVJ disorders. This article presents an overview of recent advancements, focusing on minimally invasive surgery, intraoperative imaging and navigation, robotics, artificial intelligence, and advanced visualization, highlighting their clinical impact and future directions.

Minimally invasive approaches to the CVJ

Traditional open approaches to the CVJ, such as the transoral or transmandibular routes and extensive posterior exposures, often require large incisions and extensive soft tissue disruption. These approaches carry high rates of postoperative complications, including infection, dysphagia, velopharyngeal insufficiency, and prolonged hospitalization (Visocchi, 2019; Schwartz et al., 2005). Moreover, the complex anatomy and restricted surgical corridors challenge the surgeon's ability to achieve complete decompression and safe instrumentation. To overcome these challenges, minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) techniques have emerged, aiming to reduce approach-related morbidity while preserving surgical efficacy. Among these, the endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) has transformed anterior CVJ surgery by using natural nasal corridors (Fig. 1) to access ventral lesions with minimal tissue disruption. Compared with traditional transoral routes, the EEA offers lower infection rates, reduced postoperative discomfort, and faster recovery (Visocchi et al., 2017, 2018a). It enables direct decompression of odontoid and basilar invagination lesions while avoiding the morbidity of open transoral procedures (Visocchi et al., 2024). Similarly, posterior MISS techniques, including percutaneous fixation and tubular retractor-assisted approaches, allow atlantoaxial fusion through limited exposures, minimizing muscle injury and blood loss (Khan et al., 2014; Elliott et al., 2014). By preserving key musculoligamentous structures that contribute to spinal stability, these methods reduce postoperative pain and promote earlier mobilization (Goldstein et al., 2015). The continuous refinement of endoscopic visualization, specialized instrumentation, and navigation systems has further expanded the safety and indications of MISS at the CVJ. These developments represent a paradigm shift in spinal surgery, one that emphasizes maximal decompression and stabilization with minimal collateral morbidity. Ultimately, MISS techniques are reshaping the surgical management of the CVJ, contributing to shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and improved postoperative quality of life (Kim and Benzel, 2013; Abou-Al-Shaar et al., 2020).Fig. 1Endoscopic trans-nasal video assisted odontoidectomy (personal case).Fig. 1

Despite their advantages, minimally invasive approaches present important limitations. They require advanced technical expertise and carry a steep learning curve, as restricted working angles and limited exposure demand exceptional precision and coordination. Dependence on high-definition optics, specialized instruments, and navigation technology also increases procedural complexity and cost, which may restrict access in some centers. Moreover, extensive tumors, severe deformities, or cases necessitating broad reconstruction remain unsuitable for minimally invasive strategies and still require traditional open surgery for adequate management. Procedure-specific risks must also be acknowledged. Cerebrospinal fluid leakage and vascular injury are recognized concerns in EEA, while percutaneous fixation carries a risk of screw misplacement, particularly during the early phases of surgeon experience. Nonetheless, ongoing technological advances, such as improved visualization, angled optics, real-time navigation, and robotic assistance, continue to enhance the precision, safety, and applicability of MISS at the CVJ.

Advanced imaging and navigation

Precision in CVJ surgery is paramount; millimeter-scale errors may cause catastrophic neurological or vascular injury. Preoperative imaging modalities like CT and MRI provide detailed anatomical information but translating this data intraoperatively remains challenging (Smith and Shaffrey, 2013; Greenberg, 2016).

The introduction of intraoperative 3D imaging technologies, such as the O-arm system (Fig. 2), has greatly enhanced surgical accuracy. These systems generate real-time volumetric scans during the procedure, allowing immediate verification of screw placement, such as at the C1 lateral mass or C2 pedicle, and significantly reducing malposition rates and the need for revision surgery (Verlaan et al., 2016; Kerezoudis et al., 2019). Indeed, studies have shown that intraoperative CT guidance can lower screw malposition from rates as high as 20 % with freehand techniques to below 5 % when navigation is employed (Heller et al., 2017; Shin et al., 2013). Navigation systems fuse preoperative imaging with intraoperative data (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), providing dynamic real-time guidance analogous to a GPS system (Hicks et al., 2010). This technology is especially valuable in complex anatomy or revision cases, improving accuracy, shortening operative time, and reducing radiation exposure (Gelalis et al., 2012; Visocchi et al., 2023). The adoption of high-end imaging and navigation systems in CVJ surgery is not without obstacles. Their high cost and maintenance requirements limit availability in many centers, while setup, calibration, and registration procedures can extend operative time and increase technical complexity. Effective use demands specialized training, and excessive reliance on navigation may diminish manual dexterity and hinder adaptability in unexpected intraoperative scenarios. Moreover, precision can be affected by registration errors, patient movement, or anatomical instability, factors especially relevant in cases of severe deformity or trauma.

Emerging technologies such as augmented reality (AR), artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted navigation, and predictive software are now being developed to address these limitations. By integrating multimodal data and providing enhanced visualization, these tools have the potential to improve intraoperative decision-making, optimize screw trajectories, and reduce human errors. However, their clinical adoption remains at an early stage and requires further validation through larger, multicenter studies (Visocchi et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2016; Elmi-Terander et al., 2016).

Robotic assistance in CVJ surgery

Robotic-assisted surgery at the craniovertebral junction (CVJ) provides unprecedented precision in instrumentation placement. By executing preplanned trajectories with submillimeter accuracy, robotic platforms overcome the spatial constraints and anatomical variability that make CVJ procedures particularly challenging (Lonjon et al., 2016). These systems also minimize reliance on fluoroscopy, reducing radiation exposure, enhancing reproducibility, and enabling complex screw trajectories even in difficult anatomy (Urakov et al., 2018). Modern robotic platforms often incorporate tactile feedback and semi-autonomous functions, further improving safety and streamlining workflow (Marcus et al., 2016). Clinical studies consistently demonstrate superior screw placement accuracy with robotic assistance compared with both traditional and navigated freehand techniques (Yeager et al., 2016; Schoenfeld et al., 2020). In addition, robotics enhance surgeon ergonomics and reduce fatigue during lengthy or technically demanding operations. Their benefits are maximized only when combined with meticulous preoperative planning, advanced imaging, navigation, and expert surgical judgment, emphasizing that robotics complements rather than replaces traditional surgical skill. With continued technological refinement, structured training programs, and rigorous clinical validation, robotic assistance could redefine the standard of care for complex CVJ procedures.

While robotic-assisted craniovertebral junction (CVJ) surgery offers significant benefits, some challenges continue to restrict its widespread adoption. High acquisition and maintenance costs pose substantial financial barriers, and the complexity of these systems requires dedicated technical support and specialized staff training (Soliman et al., 2020). Gaining command over robotic workflows, particularly when integrated with existing navigation and imaging modalities, remains challenging, with a steep learning curve. Robotic platforms also have limited capacity to adapt intraoperatively to unexpected anatomical variations, extreme instability, or gross deformities, necessitating that surgeons retain full proficiency in manual techniques. Setup and registration procedures can further prolong operative time, potentially offsetting efficiency gains in shorter procedures. Semi-autonomous and AI-integrated systems hold considerable promise for enhancing precision and intraoperative decision-making, yet they remain in the early stages of clinical adoption, and robust evidence on long-term outcomes and safety is still limited (Menger et al., 2019).

Artificial intelligence and data-driven surgery

Artificial intelligence is increasingly transforming spine surgery by enabling data-driven, precision approaches. In CVJ surgery, AIapplications include automated preoperative planning, intraoperative decision support, and postoperative outcome prediction (Liu et al., 2021). Machine learning algorithms can analyze complex imaging datasets to optimize screw trajectories and predict anatomical variations, reducing the risk of iatrogenic injury (Staartjes et al., 2019). During surgery, real-time AI alerts may help surgeons avoid critical structures and ensure accurate instrumentation (Mobbs et al., 2019), while postoperative risk models guide personalized rehabilitation and complication-prevention strategies (Arvind et al., 2020).

The advantages of AI are substantial: it offers enhanced precision, consistency, and safety, particularly in anatomically complex or high-risk cases, and it has the potential to integrate seamlessly with navigation, robotics, and imaging systems to create a comprehensive smart operating environment. Moreover, AI can serve as a cognitive aid, reducing cognitive load and assisting in real-time decision-making during technically demanding procedures. However, several limitations currently constrain its clinical impact. The accuracy and reliability of AI algorithms depend on the quality, diversity, and volume of the training datasets, which may limit performance in rare anatomical variants, revision surgeries, or severe deformities. Ethical, legal, and regulatory challenges, including data privacy, informed consent, accountability, and standardization, remain unresolved barriers to widespread adoption (Langlotz et al., 2019; Rispoli and Cappelleto, 2025).

It is important to emphasize that AI serves as a powerful adjunct rather than a replacement for surgical expertise. Its value can be realized only through rigorous validation, careful integration with existing technologies, and continued oversight by experienced surgeons.

Enhanced visualization and ergonomics

Visualization improvements have significantly impacted CVJ surgery. The introduction of 3D exoscopes (Fig. 4) provides surgeons with high-definition, magnified views of the surgical field without the ergonomic constraints of traditional operating microscopes (Rubino et al., 2018). These systems improve surgeon comfort, reduce fatigue during prolonged procedures, and enhance team communication through shared screens (Liebmann et al., 2019). Augmented reality (AR) technologies overlay anatomical landmarks and navigation data directly onto the surgical field, providing intuitive intraoperative guidance (Molina et al., 2020). AR can improve orientation in the complex CVJ anatomy, decrease reliance on multiple monitors, and potentially reduce operative times and errors (El-Tecle, 2023). These technologies also enhance surgical education by allowing trainees to visualize the anatomy and surgical steps in real-time alongside the operating surgeon (Meola et al., 2017). Future developments may include integration with AI-driven guidance, haptic feedback, and predictive modeling, further refining surgical accuracy and expanding the scope of safely treatable CVJ pathologies.Fig. 4Exoscope assisted CVJ microsurgical approach (personal experience).Fig. 4

Significant constraints of exoscopes and augmented reality (AR) systems persist, as these technologies require substantial financial investment and institutional resources, which can limit their widespread adoption. Effective utilization demands a learning curve, as surgeons must adapt to altered depth perception, novel visual perspectives, and the interpretation of digital overlays. Overreliance on screen-based visualization may reduce hand-eye coordination, particularly in cases with bleeding, distorted anatomy, or unexpected intraoperative findings. Integration with navigation, robotics, or AI requires careful workflow planning and can prolong setup times, while technical failures or calibration errors in AR overlays carry the risk of misguiding surgical maneuvers (Meola et al., 2017).

Training and education

The complexity of CVJ surgery demands advanced training modalities to ensure proficiency with evolving technologies. Virtual reality (VR) simulators offer immersive, risk-free environments where surgeons can practice endoscopic, navigated, and robotic techniques, gaining procedural experience without compromising patient safety (Calatayud et al., 2017). AI-driven training platforms complement VR by providing adaptive, individualized learning experiences, tailoring scenarios to each surgeon's skill level and accelerating mastery (Smith et al., 2019). Together, these technologies allow repeated practice, scenario-based problem solving, and early correction of errors, all of which help reduce intraoperative complications and enhance patient outcomes (Hashimoto et al., 2018). Integration of these technologies into formal surgical education, residency programs, fellowships, and continuing medical education, is important to maintaining high standards of care. Simulation-based curricula can supplement traditional mentorship, providing trainees with structured exposure to complex procedures and fostering confidence before hands-on operative experience (Satava, 2001). Longitudinal tracking within these platforms enables objective assessment, targeted feedback, and performance benchmarking, supporting progressive skill development and mastery over time. Next-generation technologies, such as haptic feedback, AI-guided scenario planning, and performance analytics, will further enable realistic, personalized, and effective training for upcoming spine surgeons.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. High acquisition, maintenance, and software update costs may restrict accessibility, particularly in resource-limited settings. Simulation fidelity is inherently imperfect; tactile feedback, tissue response, and anatomical variability may not fully replicate real-life surgical conditions, potentially limiting skill transfer. Effectiveness depends on thoughtful curricular integration, faculty supervision, and robust assessment frameworks to ensure that simulated practice translates into clinical competency. Overreliance on simulation without sufficient hands-on operative experience could leave gaps in decision-making under the dynamic conditions of live surgery (Smith et al., 2019; Satava, 2001).

Challenges and future directions

Despite the remarkable technological advances in craniovertebral junction (CVJ) surgery, several persistent challenges remain. The high acquisition and maintenance costs of advanced imaging, navigation, robotics, and AI systems, along with substantial infrastructure requirements, continue to limit their accessibility in many centers worldwide (Fehlings et al., 2019). These barriers are particularly pronounced in resource-constrained regions, where the availability of high-end equipment and trained personnel is limited. Moreover, the technical complexity of these technologies necessitates dedicated training, ongoing skill maintenance, and multidisciplinary team coordination to ensure safe and effective use.

Evidence regarding long-term clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and quality-of-life improvements remains limited. While early studies suggest that robotics, AI, and advanced visualization can improve surgical precision and reduce complications, robust, prospective data are needed to validate these benefits and to quantify their impact on patient-centered outcomes, healthcare efficiency, and economic sustainability. Without comprehensive validation, widespread adoption may be hindered, and disparities in access could persist.

Looking ahead, the next frontier in CVJ surgery lies in the integration of multiple technologies into seamless surgical ecosystems. Combining robotics, AI, navigation, and enhanced visualization promises to create coordinated, real-time operative platforms that improve precision, reduce operative time, and support intraoperative decision-making. Such integrated systems could enable more complex procedures to be performed safely and efficiently, even in anatomically challenging or revision cases (Visocchi et al., 2018b, 2018c).

Conclusions

Among the most demanding areas in spinal surgery, CVJ surgery, is undergoing an amazing transformation driven by technological innovation. The advent of minimally invasive techniques, real-time intraoperative imaging, robotic assistance, and artificial intelligence has significantly improved surgical precision, safety, and outcomes, enabling interventions once deemed inoperable. Yet, while technology offers unexpected opportunities, it does not represent a substitute for clinical expertise. Rather, it is the experienced surgeon who interprets, adapts, and ultimately leads innovation, tailoring technological tools to the unique anatomical and pathological challenges of each case. The surgeon's knowledge and judgment remain central to the safe and effective application of these advances. Looking ahead, developments in molecular biology, targeted therapies, and bone-regenerative strategies may further reshape the management of CVJ pathologies, potentially reducing the need for complex reconstruction or invasive approaches. Together, these clinical, technological, and biological advances are conducting in a new era of precision, safety, and possibility in CVJ surgery.
