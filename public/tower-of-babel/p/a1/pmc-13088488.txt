Reduced eccDNA content in idiopathic asthenozoospermia sperm is associated with compromised DNA repair capacity and elevated DNA damage

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite representing approximately 30% of male infertility cases, idiopathic asthenozoospermia (iAZS) remains etiologically uncharacterized. Extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA) is mobile and circular DNA outside of linear chromosomes. Although eccDNA has been identified in human sperm, its biogenesis and potential role in iAZS pathogenesis requires further study.MethodsWe enrolled 31 patients with idiopathic asthenozoospermia (iAZS) and 31 healthy controls (normozoospermia, NZS), collecting sperm samples with progressive motility (PR) ranging from 0.3% to 90.9%. Sperm eccDNAs were purified and characterized using Circle-seq. Outward PCR, Sanger sequencing, and Nanopore long-read sequencing were employed to investigate eccDNA biogenesis and its potential genomic effects. In addition, spermatozoa RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and immunofluorescence staining were conducted to identify DNA repair-related candidate genes. A CRISPR/Cas9-mediated APLF knockout model was established to explore DNA repair mechanisms in eccDNA formation in vitro.ResultsComprehensive analysis of eccDNAs derived from the sperm samples revealed a significant positive correlation between sperm motility and eccDNA abundance. Larger eccDNAs (≥ 3 kb) showed inverse associations with meiotic recombination rates and coding gene density, while smaller eccDNAs lacked these trends. Although eccDNA formation broadly aligned with transposable element (TE) densities, larger eccDNAs (≥ 3 kb) were negatively correlated with short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) (mainly Alu elements). Microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) likely drove eccDNA biogenesis, as 58% of eccDNAs harbored 3 bp direct repeat (DR) pairs. Nanopore and variant analyses suggested eccDNAs may arise from genomic deletions and later reintegrate. Importantly, sperm motility and eccDNA abundance correlated positively with DNA repair capacity but negatively with DNA damage. Finally, we identified APLF, a downregulated DNA repair protein in low-motility sperm, as a key regulator of eccDNA formation in vitro.ConclusionsThese findings emphasize the potential interplay between genomic elements and sperm eccDNA formation, highlight that impaired DNA repair and elevated DNA damage level may be major causes of reduced sperm motility and pathogenesis of iAZS, and offer new insights into strategies for improving male fertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00857-4.

Background

Sperm motility represents a vital capability of human spermatozoa as they embark on their journey through the female reproductive tract following ejaculation [1], with progressive motility (PR) serving as a crucial marker of sperm functionality [2, 3]. Alarming trends have emerged from recent studies, revealing a decline in sperm motility and associated parameters [4], which could negatively impact male fertility. Asthenozoospermia (AZS), distinguished by progressive motility (PR) falling below 32% (PR < 32%) [2, 3], is a notable abnormality in sperm motility frequently observed in male infertility cases. The causes of AZS are diverse, including varicocele, endocrine disorders, environmental influences, inflammation, drug-induced damage, and specific underlying health conditions [5, 6]. However, in many cases, routine clinical evaluations fail to identify a clear cause, resulting in a diagnosis of idiopathic AZS (iAZS) [6]. The underlying pathogenic mechanisms of iAZS and the development of effective treatment strategies remain areas of significant uncertainty.

Extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA) is increasingly recognized as a pivotal element in genome biology [7–9], with its presence documented across a variety of cell types [10–15], including sperm [16–18]. Both extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) [8, 19–21] and eccDNA [12, 22–25] play a critical role in increasing cancerous genome heterogeneity and contributing to genomic instability [8, 19, 20] due to their unique capacity to harbor and amplify genetic sequences beyond chromosomal DNA. These circular DNA molecules arise from a wide range of genomic regions, encompassing repetitive elements, gene fragments, and even entire genes [21, 26, 27], thereby creating a genetic mosaic that transcends conventional chromosomal boundaries. In sperm, eccDNA holds the potential to influence genome integrity and alter the genetic material transmitted to offspring [16, 17]. These processes may drive genetic variability and have profound implications for male fertility. To date, research specifically investigating the relationship between eccDNA and sperm motility remains absent, highlighting an important gap in current knowledge.

In this study, we analyzed eccDNA profiles of 62 human sperm samples exhibiting varying motility levels to investigate potential correlations. We examined the biogenesis of eccDNAs in the context of meiotic recombination rates, coding gene density, and the distribution of TEs across chromosomes. In addition, we explored the relationship between eccDNA abundance and insertion/deletion (indel) variations in the human genome to assess eccDNA’s potential role in shaping genomic architecture. To further elucidate functional implications, we integrated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and immunofluorescence assays to evaluate the associations between sperm motility, eccDNA abundance, DNA repair capacity, and DNA damage. Notably, we studied the regulatory role of the DNA repair protein APLF in genome-wide eccDNA formation in cultured cells.

Methods

Human semen samples subjects

Two groups of patients and healthy volunteers were enrolled on the basis of sperm parameters and their clinical histories. The idiopathic asthenozoospermia (iAZS) group (n = 31, aged 33.2 ± 6.3 years) showed progressive motility (PR) lower than 32% in sperm parameters according to the WHO laboratory manual for the examination and processing of human semen (5th ed., 2010) and EAU Guidelines on Sexual and Reproductive Health (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The normozoospermia (NZS) group (n = 31, aged 30.2 ± 4.8 years) showed PR greater than or equal to 32%. All enrolled males had no other abnormal sperm parameters including oligospermia, azoospermia, hemospermia, leukocytospermia, etc., as well as showing no other diseases which could affected sperm motility and hereditary materials, such as urinary infection, varicocele, endocrine abnormality, environmental factors, inflammation, drug injury, and some other basic diseases. We collected their fresh semen to isolate spermatozoa and purify eccDNAs and analyzed their clinical data to investigate the association. The iAZS group were denoted as A1–A32 and the NZS group were denoted as C1–C32 (A21 and C19 were excluded). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seventh Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Semen collection

Before ejaculate collection, the specimen container was kept at ambient temperature (20–37 °C) to avoid temperature fluctuations that could affect the sperm. The container should be a clean, wide-mouthed plastic container from a non-toxic batch. Both the specimen container and the work sheets were labeled with identifiers to prevent mix-ups, following proper sample reception and handling procedures. At reception, the following details were recorded and included in the final report: the man’s identity (name, birth date, personal code), confirmation that the sample was his own, the period of ejaculatory abstinence, the date and time of collection, completeness of the sample, any collection difficulties, and ejaculate volume.

Sperm motility assessment

The sperm motility was detected by computer-aided sperm analysis (CASA) for fresh semen. According to the results of CASA system, spermatozoa were categorized into four categories: grade A, B, C, and D, which were marked by red, green, blue, and yellow, respectively. Red refers to grade A, rapidly progressive sperm covering a distance of at least 25 µm in 1 s; green refers to grade B, slowly progressive sperm covering a distance of 5–25 µm in 1 s; blue refers to grade C, non-progressive sperm covering a distance of less than 5 µm in 1 s; and yellow refers to grade D, immotile sperm (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Progressive motility refers to the ratio of A and B (red and green). According to WHO semen guidelines, 32% was regarded as the cutoff value between iAZS and NZS [28].

Sperm morphology assessment

Sperm morphology was assessed on sperm smears stained with the Papanicolaou method [29]. Briefly, the smears were fixed in 95% ethanol for at least 15 min. They were then immersed in acidic ethanol 4–8 times, followed by necessary staining with orange-yellow G6 and EA-50. Subsequently, 2–3 drops of a slide-sealing agent were applied, a coverslip was placed, and excess xylene was wiped from the slide. The sealed slides were placed horizontally in a fume hood to dry on a rack or on absorbent paper for 24 h. Morphological assessment was performed by two independent observers using a microscope.

High-molecular-weight DNA isolation from spermatozoa

The fresh semen samples were divided into spermatozoa in the lower layer and seminal plasma in the upper layer through centrifugation according to WHO recommended guidance for intra-uterine insemination (IUI). The residual spermatozoa were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer three times and spun at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. High-molecular-weight DNA (HMW DNA) was isolated from 300 μL spermatozoa in PBS using an HMW DNA Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) and finally eluted in 50 μL RNase-free ddH2O. The concentration of extracted product was measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies UK), followed by correlation analysis with sperm concentration. The isolated product was detected by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. S2A–D; Supplementary Table S1). The concentration of HMW DNA was adjusted to 20 ng/μL.

Spike-in plasmids

The pAAV-saCas9-Backb (P895, 7447 bp, 100 copies, 0.01 ng/μL, 1 μL) and BE4-Gam (P1035, 9444 bp, 100 copies, 0.01 ng/μL, 1 μL) plasmids were pre-added into the digestion reaction system as the external reference to assess the quality of eccDNAs.

Rolling-circle amplification of eccDNA

In order to remove only linear genomic DNA and purify circular DNA, 800 ng (40 μL) of isolated high-molecular-weight (HMW) DNA samples were processed by plasmid-safe adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent (PSD) exonuclease (10 U/μL) in the digestion solution with enough ATP and reaction buffer. The substrates were added more than five times every 32 h, including PSD reaction buffer (5 μL, 10×), PSD exonuclease (2 μL), and ATP (25 mM, 2 μL). After more than 160 h of digestion, the efficacy was detected by agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. S2E, F). If the linear DNA had not been fully digested, we continued to the next cycles until excellent efficacy, as shown by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Purified circular DNAs were amplified by phi29 DNA polymerase at 30 °C for 72 h. The reaction system, consisting of purified circular DNAs (14 μL), phi29 buffer (4 μL, 10×), and Exo-Resistant Random Primer (2 μL), was denatured and optimized by gradient PCR (95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 30 s with −1.0 °C/cycle × 20 cycles, 75 °C for 30 s with −0.6 °C/cycle × 40 cycles, 50 °C for 30 s with −1.0 °C/cycle × 20 cycles, and finally, 4 °C on hold). The annealed circular DNAs (20 μL) were rolling-circle amplified by phi29 DNA polymerase with dNTP (4 μL, 10 mM) and DTT (0.8μL, 100 mM) at 30 °C for 72 h in 40 μL. The product was detected by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B, E).

The rolling-circle amplified products were assessed by NdeI restriction endonuclease. The purified rolling-circle amplified product (1 μL), along with color buffer (1 μL, 10×) and NdeI (0.5 μL) in 10 μL, was enzyme digested at 32 °C for 2 h. The product was detected by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. S3C, D, F).

Circle-map analysis of eccDNA sequencing data

Quality control filtering of the raw sequencing data was performed using the SOAPnuke (v1.5.4 software) eccDNA detection: clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM. The Circle-Map software (v1.1.4) was used to detect the coordinates of eccDNA from sorted BAM files. To improve the quality of eccDNA detection, several filtering steps were implemented with the following specific criteria: split reads ≥ 2, circle score ≥ 20, and coverage continuity ≤ 0.1. eccDNA annotation: the number of eccDNA in each region was calculated by Bedtools, and the proportion of the whole genome in the region and the total number of reads in the sample were standardized.

Detection of mitochondrial DNA copy number by qPCR

In this study, two types of mitochondrial DNA with high copy numbers of 16S and L394/H475 were selected for real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qPCR) detection, so as to exclude the influence of mitochondrial DNA copy number difference on sperm motility and eccDNA molecular characteristics. The primer sequences were as follows: 16S (F: 5′-CGAAAGGACAAGAGAAATAAGG-3′, R: 5′-CTGTAAAGTTTTAAGTTATGCG-3′), L394/H475 (F: 5′-CACCAGCCTAACCAGATTTC-3′, R: 5′-GGGTTGTATTGATGAGATTAGT-3′), and β-actin (F: 5′-CAACTTCATCCACGTTCACC-3′, R: 5′-GAAGAGCCAAGGACAGGTAC-3′). Sperm HMW DNA was selected as the reaction substrate to detect mitochondrial DNA copy number. The specific reaction system was as follows: HMW DNA (100 ng) 5 μL, Master Mix 10 μL, and ddH2O 4.2 μL, forward primer (10 μM) 0.4 μL, and reverse primer (10 μM) 0.4 μL. qPCR was used to detect the relative expression of 16S and L394/H475 (95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 30 s with 20 cycles, 75 °C for 30 s with 40 cycles, 50 °C for 30 s with 20 cycles, and finally, 4 °C on hold).

EccDNA genome and sequence characteristics

After comparing the sequencing data with the human reference genome, the types and total number of eccDNA were determined by starting and ending sites and genome sequences. In order to eliminate the bias of the total number of eccDNA caused by sequencing depth, this study corrected the total number of eccDNA to obtain the values of per million mappable reads (EPM). The proportion of eccDNA derived from the genomic element in the sample was used to compare the abundance of coding genes, transposable elements, and nonrepeating elements between the two groups.

The nucleotide sequences motif of eccDNA flanking junctional sites

Bedtools 2.29.2 was used to extract 10 bp DNA sequences above and below each eccDNA junction site. The 7,636,734 eccDNAs identified from 62 samples were analyzed and repeated ten times, and the average frequency of single nucleotides per base was calculated using HomerTools. Nanopore sequencing and profiling

The total genomic DNA extracted was utilized to construct libraries and conduct sequencing on the CycloneSEQ platform. Quality control of the raw sequencing data was conducted using NanoFilt software with the parameters “−q 10 −l 150”. Subsequently, Minimap2 was employed to align long reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38), followed by structural variant detection using cuteSV.

Verification of specific eccDNA by outward PCR electrophoresis

The primer sequences were as follows: eccMIR433 (F: 5′-CACTCGAAGGCCTCTTGCTC-3′, R: 5′-GATCCTACGGCAAGAGCTGC-3′) and eccRBBP8 (F: 5′-GAGTCCTGGAGCCATAGTTGC-3′, R: 5′-GGCTGGAGTTAAAGGCTGAG-3′). The specific Green-Taq reaction system was as follows: 2× Green Taq 10 μL, primer-F (10 μM) 0.5 μL, primer-R (10 μM) 0.5 μL, DNA Template + ddH2O 9 μL. PCR program: 95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 15 s with 32 cycles, 62 ℃ for 15 s with 32 cycles, 72 °C for 3 s with 32 cycles, 72 °C for 5 min, and finally, 4 °C on hold. Following this, 5μL of each PCR product was run on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Extraction of sperm RNA

Sperm RNA was extracted using the high-efficiency Trizol method. Briefly, 1000 μL fresh semen was centrifuged to remove the seminal plasma. The remaining sperm pellet was washed twice with PBS and then lysed in 500 μL Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). After room temperature incubation and liquid nitrogen freezing, samples were stored at −80 °C. RNA was extracted via chloroform phase separation, precipitated with isopropanol, and washed twice with 75% ethanol. The RNA pellet was air-dried and dissolved in 20 μL RNase-free ddH2O for downstream use.

RNA-seq and data analyses

RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The purified RNA was fragmented and synthesized into cDNA. The cDNA fragments were end-repaired, adenylated, and ligated with Illumina adapters. After PCR amplification, the libraries were quantified and qualified. The libraries were sequenced on Illumina HiSeq platform to generate paired-end reads. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 in R programming language (version 4.2.1). Genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 and |log2 fold changes|> 1 were considered statistically significant.

Immunostaining

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-induced DNA damage was used as a positive control, following protocols from Belardin et al. [30] and Coban et al. [31]. Briefly, 100 μL fresh semen from a healthy donor was mixed with 100 μL of 1 mmol/mL H2O2 and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Then, the sample was centrifuged to remove the supernatant, the remaining sperm pellet was washed twice with PBS and fixed in 100 μL 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, then washed and resuspended in PBS before storing at −20 °C or −80 °C. For smear preparation, 25 μL sperm suspension was loaded into a cytospin funnel, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, air-dried, and marked. Immunofluorescence staining involved sequential washing, blocking (1 h), primary antibody incubation at 4 °C overnight, washing, secondary antibody incubation (1 h, dark), DAPI nuclear staining (10–20 min), and final sealing with antifade medium. Stained slides were stored at 4 °C or −20 °C in the dark. For quantification, sperm were observed using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Ten fields at 400× magnification were photographed and analyzed. Sperm were counted on the basis of DAPI staining (approx. 200–1000 cells/slide), and the number of gene-positive cells was recorded on the basis of fluorescence. The expression rate was calculated as the ratio of positive cells to total sperm in the observed fields. All counting was independently performed by two blinded researchers, and final values were averaged.

Cell culture

The HepG2 cell line was bought from the BIOSPECIES company (China, cat. no. HEPG2) with an STR identification report. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and passaged every 2 days or when cell confluency reached ~80%. For subculture, cells were washed three times with sterile PBS and treated with 1 mL of 0.25% trypsin until detachment; trypsinization was neutralized with complete medium. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, and the pellet was resuspended in 3–4 mL fresh medium. Cells were then seeded into two new T25 flasks and returned to the incubator after confirming viability and density under an inverted microscope.

APLF gene knock out

Two sgRNAs targeting the exon 4 and exon 5 of the APLF gene were designed through CRISPOR (http://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/) with a high on-target score and a minimized off-target score. The name and sequence of the two sgRNAs are human ALPF-gR1: GACTACTGGTGCCTCACAACTGG and human ALPF-gR2: AGCAGCCAATCCTTGCCGAGAGG. Italicized nucleotides are the PAM sequences. The two sgRNAs were synthesized in the Genscript company (without the PAM sequence). The CRISPR/Cas9 knockout was conducted by ribonucleoprotein (RNP) nucleofection using a Lonza 4D nucleofector, as previously described [32]. Nucleofection of the spCas9 plus a nontargeting sgRNA (an sgRNA targeting our ECC biosensor plasmid: ECC-sgRNA1: AGAGCCCCAGAGACCGGCACCGG [33]) was treated as the negative control group. The cells were harvested 2 days after RNP nucleofection and were subjected to PCR genotyping. The primers to amplify the genomic region harboring exon 4 and exon 5 were human APLF-scrF: GTAGTTCTAAGGCAGCAGAAGTG and human APLF-scrR: GCTAGGCTATACACTTACACAACC.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using the R programming language (v4.2.1) and the figures were generated through the ggplot2 package. The pod diagram was analyzed through the stats and car packages, and the Venn diagrams were generated by the VennDiagram package. The comparative analyses and correlation analyses were performed by t-test and Spearman’s correlation test, respectively. ImageJ (×64) was used for immunofluorescence staining for sperm count, and K-Viewer (1.7.1.1) and OlyVIA (V4.1) were used for fluorescence scanning image system. In this study, eccDNA differential gene |log2FC|> 0.5 and p < 0.05 were considered to have statistical significance. Among RNA-Seq differential genes |Log2FC|> 1 and P adj. < 0.05 were statistically significant.

Results

Higher sperm motility is associated with increased eccDNA abundance

Semen samples were collected from normozoospermic volunteers (NZS, n = 31, age 30.2 ± 4.8 years, PR > 32%) and patients with iAZS (n = 31, age 33.2 ± 6.3 years, PR < 32%), with PR values ranging from 0.3% to 90.9% (Supplementary Table S1). Supplementary Fig. S1B shows motility and morphology assessments of semen samples from the NZS and iAZS groups. Supplementary Table S2 provides a detailed overview of sperm parameters for both groups. There were no significant differences in semen volume or sperm concentration between the groups, but the PR value, total motility, and rapid progressive sperm (A-grade) were significantly lower in the iAZS group compared with the NZS group (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

We identified 2,693,133 eccDNAs in the iAZS group and 4,943,601 in the NZS group (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S1). The iAZS samples exhibited significantly fewer eccDNAs (median: 64,662 ± 13,430) compared with the NZS group (median: 120,021 ± 24,830) (Fig. 1C). To account for sequencing depth bias, we normalized eccDNA counts as eccDNA per million reads (EPM), which confirmed a significantly lower median in iAZS (733.6 ± 163.7) versus NZS (1218 ± 269.6) (Fig. 1D). In addition, the NZS group generated more eccDNAs per chromosome than iAZS (Fig. 1E). Importantly, Spearman correlation analysis further revealed a positive association between eccDNA abundance and sperm motility parameters (PR and total motility [PR and NP]) (Fig. 1F), suggesting that highly motile sperm tend to harbor a greater diversity of eccDNAs.

Meiotic recombination rate and coding gene density constrain large eccDNA formation

A total of 98.11% (7,490,440/7,634,734) of eccDNAs detected in our study were < 3 kb, showing six characteristic peaks (192, 359, 565, 737, 936, and 1108 bp; Fig. 2A), which align with reports from blood plasma [34] and cancerous tissues [12], supporting their nucleosomal origin. Cumulative distribution analysis revealed NZS had significantly more small eccDNAs (< 3 kb, dashed line) than iAZS (Fig. 2B). It has been reported by Birgitte Regenberg’s group that meiotic homologous recombination rates and coding gene-rich regions correlate negatively with eccDNA frequency in sperm [16]. However, their findings are based primarily on eccDNAs > 3 kb, leaving smaller species uncharacterized. We therefore analyzed the relationship of eccDNA abundance (classified by size) with three independent measures of meiotic recombination rates [35–37] and the coding gene densities across autosomes. Notably, while eccDNAs ≥ 3 kb exhibited a significant inverse correlation with meiotic recombination rates in both NZS and iAZS groups (Fig. 2E), smaller eccDNAs (< 3 kb) showed no correlation (Fig. 2F). However, coding genes were present in ~60% of eccDNAs, their distribution across functional genomic elements (3′ UTR, 5′ UTR, CpG islands, exons, introns, and 2 kb flanking regions) showed no significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 2G). Consistent with the trend observed for meiotic recombination rates, eccDNAs ≥ 3 kb exhibited a significant negative correlation with coding gene density, whereas eccDNAs < 3 kb showed no significant association (Fig. 2H). These results suggest that recombination sites and gene-rich regions may block large eccDNA formation, protecting sperm gene integrity.

Transposable elements and sperm eccDNA origins

Transposable elements (TEs) are mobile genetic elements that propagate within genomes via transposition, shaping genomic architecture and function through mutagenesis and regulatory rewiring [38]. In human sperm, approximately 80% of eccDNAs harbored various types of TEs, though the ratio did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 3A). We then analyzed the correlation between TE density across chromosomes and the presence of eccDNAs (< 3 kb and ≥ 3 kb). Most eccDNAs showed a positive correlation with TE densities, including long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), DNA transposons, long terminal repeats (LTRs), and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs, primarily Alu elements). However, eccDNAs ≥ 3 kb exhibited an inverse correlation with SINEs and Alu (Fig. 3B). Highly frequent short direct repeats flank eccDNA breakpoints

eccDNA is generally considered to be randomly generated, with varying sizes and genomic origins, making it difficult to have the same start and end points [39, 40]. In this study, however, we identified a minor subset of eccDNAs with same start and end coordinates across different individuals, which we refer to as “identical eccDNA.” A total of 4037 identical eccDNAs were identified between the NZS and iAZS groups, indicating their presence in at least one sample from each group (Supplementary Fig. S5). In addition, 4376 identical eccDNAs were identified exclusively within the NZS group, while 1467 were found solely in the iAZS group, and 30 identical eccDNAs were shared in both groups (Supplementary Fig. S5).

To investigate potential DNA motifs that may influence the formation of eccDNAs, we analyzed the 10 bp sequences flanking both the start and end positions of all eccDNAs and identical eccDNAs. These flanking 10 bp intervals were divided into four regions: I, II, III, and IV. This shows that total eccDNA and identical eccDNAs have different DNA motifs flanking the start and end positions, accompanied with a 4 bp spacer in the total DNA group, whereas a 3 bp spacer is present in the identical eccDNA group (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S5 and Table S3). Furthermore, regions I and III, as well as regions II and IV, are likely to share uniform 3 bp motifs (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S5 and Table S3). We speculate that eccDNA formation involves recombination and DNA ligation with small direct repeats (DRs) flanking the junction site (Fig. 4B). In sperm, 57.5% of eccDNAs contain paired 3 bp direct repeats (DRs) (DR-I = DR-III or DR-II = DR-IV), with 17.1% and 3.8% having 4 bp and 5 bp DRs, respectively. Notably, 35.35% exhibit 3 bp DRs in the combined DR-I = DR-III and DR-II = DR-IV model. Potential impact of eccDNA on sperm genome integrity

Evidence suggests that endogenous eccDNA can originate from genomic deletion events [7, 33]. In some cases, it may reintegrate into different chromosomal regions, potentially being transmitted to offspring [16]. Here, we sought further evidence of eccDNA reinsertion events in sperm and their possible associations with genomic deletions. All known human insertion and deletion variants larger than 150 bp were compared with the coordinates of eccDNAs we detected in sperm. Those insertion events with coordinates falling within the range of specific eccDNAs were defined as “putative reinsertions” (Fig. 4D), while deletion variants encompassing the range of certain eccDNAs were classified as “putative deletions” (Fig. 4E). We observed that the higher eccDNA content in a sperm sample, the more predicted reinsertion and deletion events were found (Fig. 4F, G). Across the 62 sperm samples, we identified 1741 putative reinsertions and 29,673 putative deletions, demonstrating that deletion events occur far more frequently than reinsertions. Notably, the number of putative deletions showed a positive correlation with sperm motility (PR value) (Fig. 4H), while reinsertion events exhibited no such association (Fig. 4I), likely owing to the limited number of detectable reinsertions.

For example, we detected an eccDNA of 1575 bp in size, eccDNA [chr12:121,863,392–121,864,966], which may reintegrate a 387 bp portion of itself into the genomic site at chr1:63,292,234, resulting in an insertion fragment spanning chr12:121,864,166–121,864,552. Another example is a 203 bp eccDNA [chr1:177,419,230–177,419432], which may insert in a position nearby its original start site and form a tandem duplication (Supplementary Fig. S8A). Another two examples show that two detected sperm eccDNAs (eccDNA [chr17:817,399–817,986] and eccDNA [chr17:34,164,635–34,164,981]) may arise from the deletion events Del_chr17:817,399–817,988 and Del_chr17:34,164,636–34,164,988 (Supplementary Fig. S8B).

To further validate the association between eccDNA and sperm genomic variations, we performed Nanopore sequencing on pooled genomic DNA of sperm from four healthy volunteers (C5, C9, C15, and C17), yielding 8,594,605 long reads with an average length of 920 bp (ranging from 150 bp to 100,037 bp). We identified 11,619 structural variations (SVs), including 6921 deletions (DEL, 59.57%), 415 insertions (INS, 3.57%), 3007 indels (INDEL, 25.88%), and 1276 breakend (BND, 10.98%) events (Fig. 4J). Overall, 46 deletion events were found to be associated with the detected eccDNA across the four samples (Supplementary Table S4). For example, three eccDNAs, including eccDNA [chr4:62,806,480–62,808,129], eccDNA [chr4:62,808,196–62,808,576], and eccDNA [chr4:62,808,608–62,808,832], may have originated from the chr4:62,803,710–62,810,405 deletion, as supported by an 8933 bp Nanopore sequencing read (Supplementary Fig. S8C). These findings show that SVs are common in the sperm genome and may contribute to eccDNA formation.

Discussion

eccDNA is present in the mammalian germline [16–18], but its link to sperm motility and male fertility remains unclear. We show that high-motility sperm contain more small eccDNAs (< 3 kb), with formation influenced by chromosomal features and often flanked by 3 bp direct repeats, suggesting a microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ) mechanism. DNA repair capacity positively correlates with both sperm motility and eccDNA abundance, while DNA damage shows an inverse relationship. Notably, the DNA repair gene APLF, downregulated in low-motility sperm, regulates eccDNA formation. These results underscore that impaired DNA repair and elevated DNA damage are likely key contributors to reduced sperm motility and the pathogenesis of iAZS (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Schematic presents the interconnection of DNA repair capacity and eccDNA in shaping sperm motility. iAZS is a distinct subcategory of asthenozoospermia, marked by reduced sperm motility without identifiable causes. Previous studies reported a negative correlation between sperm motility and mtDNA copy number, suggesting a compensatory increase in low-motility sperm [41], but none directly addressed iAZS. Our previous study shows that iAZS sperm motility is linked to mtDNA rearrangements and point mutations [42], while this study showed mtDNA copy number is unaffected, providing the first direct evidence of mtDNA alterations in iAZS.

In cancer, it is likely that extensive DNA damage and high genomic instability give rise to increased amounts of eccDNA, some of which, in turn, confer growth and survival advantages to cancer cells [9, 12]. In this study, we observed that higher eccDNA abundance correlates with better sperm PR motility, reduced DNA damage, and greater integrity of the DNA repair machinery. As the genome of spermatogonia and sperm cells are comparatively stable, this might be a different story from what is observed in cancer. Human somatic DNA faces continuous endogenous and exogenous genotoxic insults, up to 105 lesions per day [43], necessitating efficient repair to preserve genomic integrity—an ability crucial for spermatogonia and sperm to faithfully transmit an intact, functional genome to the next generation. Several DNA repair genes, such as DNA ligase 3 [44], NBS1, MLH1, PARP1, RAD54 [45], and APLF in this study, have been verified to participate in the formation of eccDNA. In this context, we propose that the increased eccDNAs in highly motile sperm could be a consequence of the efficient DNA repair system.

DNA damage disrupts chromatin packaging and mitochondrial function, leading to abnormal sperm morphology, reduced motility [46], and lower fertilization and embryo development potential in assisted reproductive technology [47]. Among different types of DNA damage outcomes, linear DNA fragments pose a greater threat to sperm genome integrity, as their open ends can facilitate easier integration into genomic double-strand breaks (DSBs) [48], further compromising the genome integrity. However, it has also been demonstrated that endogenous linear DNA arising from DSBs can serve as an important source for eccDNA formation [33, 49–51]. Therefore, the presence of abundant eccDNA in sperm may reflect a byproduct of efficient DNA repair activity—a “safe” circularized outcome following the repair of DSBs, serving to protect the sperm genome from secondary and deleterious integration events and maintain its integrity.

In cancerous or somatic cells, elevated eccDNA abundance may serve as a dynamic reservoir for genome plasticity and adaptive changes of host cells [10, 12]—for example, increased eccDNAs carrying TTN (titin) genic segments are found in physically active people and may enhance the muscle strength by transcription of full-length or truncated titin [10]. It has also been reported that the overall population of eccDNAs derived from high-eccDNA abundance cells can facilitate the proliferation of recipient cells by activating the STING pathway [52]. Aside from eccDNAs with different origins, a distinct class of small eccDNAs with the same start and end coordinates, termed “identical eccDNAs,” has emerged as a research focus owing to their roles in pathogenesis and their potential as biomarkers. Specific identical eccDNAs may occur both within and across different cancer types—for example, eccDNA[Chr6:46819468–46819807] is detectable in the plasma of patients with both colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma [53]. eccSORBS1[chr10: 97206791–97208025] is present in serum from both healthy individuals and patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, with higher levels in the latter [54]. Bin Shi et al. identified eccVPS41[chr17:44148731–48208624] as a cellular defender to protect the skin from radiation-induced apoptotic cell death [55]. In this study, we found several specific identical eccDNAs present in either the NZS or the iAZS group, such as the eccDNA[chr1:235078245–235078548] carrying an enhancer element (EH38E1434071) and eccDNA[chr6:39845257–39845371] carrying the DAAM2 genic segment in the iAZS group (Supplementary Fig. S6). They may exert adverse effects on sperm motility. As these specific eccDNAs often carry potentially functional genomic elements, their molecular roles in abnormal spermatogenesis and iAZS pathogenesis need to be further investigated.

Although this study revealed that highly motile sperm tends to carry more eccDNAs < 3 kb in size, the abundance of < 3 kb eccDNAs is not associated with the density of meiotic recombination sites and coding genes on chromosomes, a finding which specifically pertains to genomic feature density and does not address potential sequence damage or variations. While existing research has established that both reduced meiotic recombination rates and abnormal gene expression (e.g., SPATA18) are associated with impaired sperm motility [56], our focus on the eccDNA–iAZS relationship meant we did not experimentally assess mutations in meiotic recombination sites or genes. Therefore, it is not warranted to conclude that the pathogenesis of asthenozoospermia is unrelated to these genomic regions. Similarly, regarding TEs, although we observed more < 3 kb eccDNAs containing TEs (eccTEs) in NZS than in iAZS, we cannot assert that TEs contribute to iAZS pathogenesis. This is because the generation of these eccTEs may merely be a byproduct of DNA repair processes—where higher repair capacity could yield more eccTEs—making them part of the overall increased eccDNA population rather than a specific pathogenic mechanism.

At last, despite significant advances in understanding male infertility, about 30% of cases are still classified as idiopathic and lack effective therapeutic treatments [57]. In the presented study, we found that the increased eccDNA level correlates with an efficient DNA repair system in sperm and reflects a reduced level of genomic DNA damage. These mechanisms may not only enhance the sperm motility, but also help ensure that the fertilized egg inherits a complete and functional genome, thereby reducing the risk of miscarriage, developmental abnormalities, or genetic diseases in the offspring. Moreover, eccDNAs carried by highly motile sperm may be delivered to the zygote upon fertilization, subtly contributing to genomic diversity in the offspring. For instance, eccDNAs inherited from germ cells could be unequally distributed between monozygotic embryos, potentially leading to minor phenotypic differences between twins.

Conclusions

This study unveils the associations of sperm eccDNA dynamics with DNA repair, DNA damage, and sperm motility. It deepens our understandings of sperm eccDNA biogenesis and its potential impact on the off spring, offering additional insights into improving male fertility.
