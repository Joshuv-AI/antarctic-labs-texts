Vein-to-vein data: what predicts platelet response?
Authors: Nicole Relke, Richard J. Cook, Donald M. Arnold
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Roubinian et al1 used a large “vein-to-vein” database to investigate the role of blood donor, recipient, and product characteristics on 2 platelet transfusion outcomes: platelet count increment >20 × 109/L and the need for red blood cell (RBC) transfusion within 24 hours, a pragmatic surrogate for bleeding. The main findings were that a higher body mass index (BMI) and prior severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection in blood donors were associated with higher platelet increments and reduced 24-hour RBC transfusion requirements in recipients. Conversely, pathogen reduction of the platelet product reduced the odds of achieving the targeted platelet count increment and increased the odds of RBC transfusion. These findings highlight the possible role of donor and product characteristics in understanding the effectiveness of transfusion interventions.

The authors used multivariable logistic regression models to investigate the association between a list of donor, product, and recipient characteristics, and the outcomes of interest. Logistic regression enables examination of associations between explanatory variables and a binary outcome (eg, adequate/inadequate platelet increment). The authors used generalized estimating equations to account for possible dependencies in response across multiple transfusions within the same patient, although the details of the correlation structure were not reported. A potential pitfall is that individuals who tend to respond poorly to transfusions may require more platelet transfusions and consequently be overrepresented in the analysis. Modeling dependence over time or using inverse probability weighting to mitigate such selection effects can help address this limitation.2 In addition, the authors considered a large set of explanatory variables and, as is often the case with large data sets, many factors were found to be significant. With large data sets the emphasis should be on the effect size, the biological plausibility, and the clinical utility of the findings, rather that the achievement of statistical significance.3 Exploration of the predictive performance of the model through use of an external validation sample or by splitting the available sample into a training and test sample to assess predictive accuracy4 would add information on the robustness and utility of the findings.

The study by Roubinian et al raises intriguing biological questions about blood donors. First, by which physiological process might increased donor BMI improve recipient platelet count increments and reduce RBC transfusion? Previous plateletpheresis studies have not demonstrated higher platelet yields with increasing donor weight5,6; the authors postulate that obesity is a proinflammatory state associated with several biological response modifiers that may improve platelet function. Although the effect of donor BMI was modeled as a single continuous covariate (per 10-unit increase), a more nuanced study of the dose-response effect of BMI on response would be of interest to support or refute any causal interpretation. Second, how might prior SARS-CoV-2 infection in the donor lead to better platelet count increments in the recipient? Evidence shows that SARS-CoV-2 proteins can directly bind and activate platelets, potentially enhancing platelet reactivity and function. Whether and how these biological modifiers can affect platelet count yield remains unexplained. The authors also directly examined platelet unit concentration in the subset of products where these data were available. As expected, they found that a higher platelet concentration in the product and “unsplit” platelet units were associated with an increased odds of a platelet increment >20 × 109/L.

With respect to product characteristics, the authors found that pathogen reduction of platelets, use of platelet additive solution and platelet storage for 5 to 7 days (vs <5 days) were associated with a lower likelihood of achieving a platelet increment >20 × 109/L.1 Prior studies have shown that pathogen reduction resulted in lower platelet increments but did not increase the risk of bleeding.7,8 In contrast, Roubinian et al found that pathogen reduction and irradiation were associated with an increased odds of 24-hour RBC transfusion, although only in recipients without a cancer diagnosis. These differences may reflect heterogeneous practice patterns or residual confounding in real-world data sets.

Recipient characteristics that were associated with transfusion outcomes were consistent with previous literature. Major ABO-incompatible platelet transfusions were associated with a reduced odds of achieving the target platelet increment, consistent with previous evidence.9 As expected, there was no association with donor-recipient rhesus D-antigen mismatches because rhesus antigens are not expressed on platelets. Together, these data support the common blood bank strategy of providing fresh, ABO-identical platelets for patients with platelet refractoriness.

The strength of this study lies in its use of a large vein-to-vein data set that allows data to be tracked from blood donor to blood product to transfusion recipient. This represents an excellent resource enabling the discovery of associations that may have subtle yet important impacts on improving blood transfusion outcomes.10 Other investigators have used similar data sets to investigate platelet storage duration11 and age of RBC units12 on clinical outcomes. The need for comprehensive transfusion data has been recognized as a priority area for transfusion surveillance and research in many countries including the United States (The REDS Program),13 Canada (CANDAT),14 Sweden and Denmark (SCANDAT3-S),15 and the Netherlands (DTD warehouse).16 Even with detailed databases, however, distinguishing causation from correlation remains a formidable challenge.17

Although intriguing and potentially impactful, the study by Roubinian et al may leave readers wondering: are the results credible? An important next step will involve attempts to replicate findings in other data sets with additional statistical models and methods. This can be difficult given the many factors that influence the outcomes,18 but reproducing the key findings will increase reader confidence and potentially set the stage for targeted donor recruitment for better transfusion outcomes. The role of donor characteristics could ultimately be tested directly through randomization of platelet units from donors across BMI categories (eg, normal, overweight, and obese).

In conclusion, Roubinian et al provide a timely and ambitious evaluation of donor, product, and recipient factors that impact platelet transfusion outcomes in real-world practice. Their study highlights the power of vein-to-vein data sets for obtaining insights into potential determinants of transfusion effectiveness. The specific findings identify novel donor-associated signals related to obesity and prior SARS-CoV-2 infection that warrant further study—confirmation of these results could have important implications for transfusion medicine policy.
