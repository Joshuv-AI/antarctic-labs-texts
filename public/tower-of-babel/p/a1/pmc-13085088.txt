Factors influencing the implementation of best clinical practices for hip fracture management in resource-constrained hospitals in China and co-design of quality improvement interventions: a qualitative study

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHip fractures are a significant health issue for ageing populations, with high mortality and severe long-term effects, placing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide, including in China. Although a multidisciplinary co-management model is regarded as best practice for hip fracture management, it typically involves shared care between orthopaedic teams and perioperative medical specialists such as geriatrics or internal medicine, working closely with nursing and rehabilitation staff. Its success depends on well-established medical and human resources, making implementation challenging in many settings. This study investigates the current management practices for hip fractures in county-level hospitals in China to inform development of targeted quality improvement interventions aimed at improving care.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in three sequential steps. In the first step, focus group interviews were conducted with multidisciplinary healthcare providers and hospital administrators from ten county-level hospitals across China. Guided by national clinical guidelines, these interviews aimed to identify barriers and facilitators influencing the quality of hip fracture management in older adults. The second step employed patient journey mapping to systematically analyze the treatment and management processes for hip fractures within these hospitals. In the third step, two rounds of clinician- and manager-led, evidence-informed co-design workshops were held, bringing together clinical staff including orthopaedics, internist and general practitioners, public health professionals and health management professionals to collaboratively identify areas to be intervened and develop specific interventions.FindingsA total of ten focus group discussions were conducted, one in each province, involving 137 participants. These discussions identified ten key barriers and ten facilitators influencing the quality of hip fracture care. The patient journey mapping documented the treatment process for hip fractures in older adults across ten county-level hospitals, highlighting issues such as surgical delays and inadequate multidisciplinary collaboration. The co-design workshops led to the development of three interventions: (1) optimization of in-hospital management processes, (2) empowerment of surgeons and physicians, and (3) enhancement of awareness among patients and their families.InterpretationThis study provides a comprehensive understanding of the challenges related to the management of hip fracture in resource-constrained county-level hospitals in China. The proposed interventions are expected to improve care quality and outcomes for patients with hip fractures in resource-constrained settings. Future efforts will focus on conducting randomized controlled trials to evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions.FundingThis study was supported by the Beijing Hospitals Authority Clinical Medicine Development of Special Funding Support (code: ZLRK202310).Keywords: Hip fracture, Co-design, Patient journey mapping, Quality improvement, China

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Hip fractures are a leading cause of disability and mortality among older adults, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where ageing populations coincide with constrained healthcare systems. Multidisciplinary co-management models involving orthopaedic and geriatric teams have improved outcomes for older patients with hip fractures in many countries, including China, with evidence showing shorter time to surgery and reduced mortality. However, implementing these models often require considerable resources and the involvement of geriatric specialists, both of which are often unavailable in resource-constrained hospitals. Although the importance of adapting best practices to low-resource settings is increasingly recognized, few studies have examined the practical barriers to delivering high-quality hip fracture care in such hospitals or explored how care models can be effectively adapted to local conditions.

Added value of this study

This study develops an intervention framework to improve hip fracture care for older adults in resource-limited hospitals in China. Using multiple qualitative methods, including focus group interviews, patient journey mapping, and clinician-and manager-led, evidence-informed co-design, it highlights the importance of tailoring interventions to local contexts and engaging stakeholders throughout the process. The co-designed interventions focus on three areas: optimizing in-hospital management processes, building the capacity of surgeons and physicians through training, and enhancing health education for patients and their families. The findings provide practical insight into how context-specific interventions can be designed and implemented to meet the needs of hospitals with limited resources.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study offers a practical, stakeholder-informed strategy to improve hip fracture care in resource-limited hospitals. Strengthening in-hospital care processes, enhancing clinical capacity through training, and improving patient and family awareness through health education are key strategies identified. These interventions are designed to be scalable and adaptable to similar settings in China and other low- and middle-income countries, supporting future implementation research and informing policy efforts to improve outcomes for older patients with hip fractures.

Introduction

Hip fractures in older people are a significant global public health concern, characterized by high morbidity and mortality, placing a substantial economic burden on both patients, their families and healthcare systems.1,2 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the dual pressures of rapid population ageing and limited medical resources have made hip fracture management particularly challenging.3, 4, 5

In China, the growing ageing population has intensified this burden, with the number of adults aged 60 and older in rural areas reaching 121 million in 2020. Compared to urban areas, rural regions face more severe ageing-related challenges, leading to a higher incidence of hip fractures.6 Many countries, including China, have explored strategies to improve hip fracture management.7,8 One widely adopted approach is the multidisciplinary team co-management model. This model typically refers to shared inpatient care led jointly by orthopaedic teams and perioperative medical specialists, most commonly geriatricians or internists, and supported by nursing, rehabilitation, and other allied health professionals.9,10 In particular, multidisciplinary team co-management models have been evaluated and shown to reduce mortality, enhance patients’ quality of life, shorten hospital stays, and lower healthcare costs.11,12 However, these models rely on substantial medical resources, including dedicated geriatric specialists and strong intra-hospital collaboration. In resource-limited hospitals, the absence of key professional roles, such as the geriatrician, and lack of context-tailored clinical pathways makes the adoption of best practices for hip fracture management difficult.

To address these challenges, this study employs qualitative study methods to identify factors influencing the implementation of quality hip fracture management in resource-constrained hospitals in China and further to co-design context-tailored quality improvement interventions.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study consisted of three steps (Fig. 1). First, we used focus group interviews, informed by a qualitative descriptive methodology, to identify barriers and facilitators to the implementation of guideline-concordant hip fracture care in county-level hospitals.13 Second, we conducted direct observation using patient journey mapping to characterise the in-hospital care pathway for older adults with hip fracture in resource-constrained hospitals and identify bottlenecks against guideline-recommended best practices. Third, we held two rounds of clinician- and manager-led, evidence-informed co-design workshops with multiple stakeholders, informed by findings from Steps 1–2, to prioritise challenges and develop interventions.14, 15, 16, 17 In Step 1, focus group interviews were conducted with physicians, nurses, and hospital administrators. The conceptual framework for the focus groups was adapted from the five core measures in the Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment, and Management of Hip Fractures in Older Adults (2022 Edition). These measures were used as a priori domains to structure the interview guide and to support deductive mapping during the analysis. Analytically, we applied an inductive–deductive content analytic approach to identify key barriers and facilitators influencing the uptake of best clinical practices for the management of geriatric hip fractures, and to generate insights into challenges in clinical practice and administrative processes.18,19 In Step 2, patient journey mapping was used to examine care processes across the hospital pathway; findings from this step informed prioritisation and intervention development in Step 3.15,20 In Step 3, the first workshop brought together experts in orthopaedics, internal medicine, general practice, public health, and hospital management, as well as orthopaedic surgeons, internists, and nurse representatives from resource-constrained hospitals. Participants used an importance–feasibility prioritisation matrix to identify the most pressing challenges influencing hip fracture care in these settings. The second workshop built on these priorities and focused on the development of context-tailored interventions and implementation recommendations through structured discussion and consensus-building. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No [K2023] No [378]-00). All participants provided informed consent before participation. The study findings were reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (Supplementary File S1), which provide a framework for transparent and comprehensive reporting of qualitative studies.21

The logic model of this three-step qualitative study.

Study sites

This study was conducted in 10 provinces of China, including Heilongjiang, Hebei, Henan, Shandong, Shanxi, Shaanxi, Hubei, Chongqing, Zhejiang, and Guangdong provinces (Supplementary Figure S1), representing diverse geographic regions and levels of economic development. Provinces were first identified based on geographic distribution and prior collaborations with our research team, and one county-level hospital (county is the administrative unit below province in rural areas) was then purposefully selected from each province. These hospitals are the main local public medical institutions in the respective areas, providing essential healthcare, treating common illnesses, managing emergencies, and referring complex cases to higher-level hospitals.22 For this study, eligible hospitals were defined as county-level public general hospitals with grade 3 B or below (grade 3 A hospitals are considered as the most well-resourced, top-tiered hospitals in the Chinese hospital grading system), while those that had already implemented hip fracture–related quality improvement interventions, such as multidisciplinary co-management, were excluded.

Step 1: focus group interviews

Participants and sample size

Focus group discussions were held at each participating hospital, involving key stakeholders from three categories: (1) hospital leadership, defined as senior executive decision-makers (e.g., the hospital president or vice president) responsible for organisational strategy, governance, and high-level resource allocation; (2) hospital administrators, defined as managers responsible for day-to-day operational administration and implementation of hospital policies and processes relevant to hip fracture care (e.g., managers from the medical affairs office, nursing administration, quality management, operations, or information departments); and (3) clinical experts involved in managing hip fracture, such as professionals in the field of orthopaedics, emergency medicine, internal medicine, geriatrics, anesthesiology, and rehabilitation. Only individuals who had been in their position for at least three months and were directly involved in hip fracture care delivery, clinical decision-making, or hospital management processes relevant to hip fracture care were invited. Participants were excluded if they were unavailable on the day of the interview or unable to provide informed consent. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, aiming to ensure representation across leadership/administrative and clinical roles within each hospital.

Sample size adequacy was guided by the principle of data saturation, defined as the point at which additional focus groups no longer generated substantively new codes or themes (code/meaning saturation). To operationalise saturation, data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively: after each focus group, the analytic team reviewed whether new codes/themes emerged and whether subsequent discussions added new conceptual insights versus merely elaborating existing categories; recruitment ceased when successive focus groups yielded no substantively new themes and the coding structure was judged stable.23,24

Interview framework

The focus group interviews followed a semi-structured format, guided by the Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment, and Management of Hip Fractures in Older Adults (2022 Edition).25 The interview questions were developed by a guideline-driven approach, embracing the guideline's five core measures for improving hip fracture management. These measures were used to explore potential barriers and facilitators to quality improvement. The core measures included: (1) multidisciplinary co-management model involving orthopaedic and internal medicine teams; (2) early surgery within 48 h of admission; (3) selecting appropriate surgical procedures based on the fracture type; (4) early mobilization and weight bearing post-surgery; (5) osteoporosis and falls risk assessment and treatment to prevent occurring fractures.

Data collection

Focus group interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary Materials 1) that explored the care process for patients with hip fracture and the barriers and facilitators to improving care quality. Two experienced qualitative researchers (MT: male, PhD, public health and MY: male, MD, orthopaedics) led all interviews, supported by trained team members (TL: male, DrPH candidate, public health and nursing; NT: female, DrPH candidate, public health and health management; and BN: male, MPH, public health) who assisted with notetaking, documentation, and time management. Interviews were held in designated meeting rooms at the participating hospitals, scheduled to ensure minimal disruption to routine clinical work. Each session was audio-recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim in Chinese. After each interview, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire (Supplementary Materials 2). Data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively, allowing insights from earlier interviews to inform subsequent sampling, interview prompts, and refinement of the interview guide.24

Data analysis

We used an inductive–deductive content analytic approach.18,19 All focus group discussions were transcribed verbatim, yielding 10 transcripts, which were managed and coded using NVivo 12 (QSR International). The analytic team comprised three trained coders (TL, NT and BN). All coders had previously received training in qualitative interviews, co-design facilitation, and qualitative data analysis (Supplementary Table S1) and had participated in and completed qualitative research projects.

Analysis proceeded iteratively. First, TL, NT, and BN independently coded 2 of the 10 transcripts (20%) to develop initial code interpretations. The team then met to compare coding outputs and to develop an initial codebook. TL subsequently served as the primary coder and coded the remaining eight transcripts, with iterative refinement of the codebook as analysis progressed. Using an inductive content analytic approach, codes were clustered into themes and subthemes capturing key barriers and facilitators to quality improvement. Finally, as a deductive analytic step, these themes were mapped onto the five domains of the interview framework to support structured interpretation and reporting. Mentioning frequency was only used to describe the recurrence across discussions, but interpretation considered the content and context of participants’ accounts not frequency alone. Discrepancies related to code boundaries, coding granularity, and theme allocation were addressed through structured analytic meetings. These meetings involved revisiting the original excerpts and their surrounding context, testing alternative coding options across transcripts, and iteratively refining the codebook (e.g., merging, splitting, or renaming codes; adding definitions and inclusion/exclusion criteria). When consensus could not be reached, disagreements were resolved in team meetings and, where necessary, through further discussion with senior qualitative researchers (MT and MY) to achieve group consensus.

Step 2: patient journey mapping

Participant

The participants were older adults aged 65 years and above who were diagnosed with a hip fracture upon arrival at the emergency department (ED) of the participating hospitals. Diagnosis was confirmed through imaging, including X-ray or Computed Tomography (CT) scan. Patients were excluded if they had a pathological fracture, an old (chronic) fracture, or a periprosthetic fracture.

Recruitment

A consecutive sampling approach within a fixed observation window was applied. In each participating hospital, all consecutive patients aged 65 years or older who were diagnosed with a hip fracture and admitted during the seven consecutive days following the start of observation were enrolled. Eligible cases were identified at the ED at each site and approached for informed consent prior to observation. Cases were not observed if either the patient or treating physician declined, no audio or video recordings were made. This approach allowed standardized and feasible recruitment across sites while capturing consecutive admissions during the observation window.

Data collection

Data collection employed a direct observation of the patient journey approach.15 Three researchers (TL, NT, and BN) received pre-study training in patient journey mapping observation methods, data collection, and data analysis, delivered by an experienced researcher (JZ). Prior to formal observations, the three researchers jointly conducted a one-week practice in one hospital (Acheng District People's Hospital, Harbin, Heilongjiang Province) to ensure proficiency and accuracy in applying the protocol. All data were derived from researchers' objective observations and contemporaneous records, focusing on the entire treatment process from admission to discharge. Observations began when the patient entered the emergency department (ED) and continued until discharge. Each step in the patient's journey was documented. Four key data elements were recorded: (1) Location—where the patient was situated within the hospital (e.g., ED, orthopaedic ward, intensive care unit, operating theatre); (2) Activities—the examinations or treatments the patient received; (3) Professionals involved—the healthcare professionals engaged in the patient's care; and (4) Duration—the time spent on each activity (recorded to the minute). Due to time constraints, observations were limited to patients admitted during the first week at each hospital. In each hospital, at least one patient's journey was fully documented. All observational data were recorded in Microsoft Excel.

Data analysis

After data collection, the research team conducted a descriptive analysis of all observational data. The analysis first summarised the shortest and longest time intervals observed across patients at three key stages of care: (1) from arrival at the emergency department (ED) to hospital admission, (2) from hospital admission to surgery, and (3) from surgery to discharge. Next, the full treatment pathway for each patient was examined, and common care processes across hospitals were consolidated. These processes included ED admission, ED assessment, ward admission, preoperative assessment, operation, postoperative management, rehabilitation, and discharge. Finally, the minimum and maximum durations for each stage of care were calculated and visually presented using a patient journey map. During construction of the patient journey maps, findings were cross-validated with clinical orthopaedic experts (MY and GL). Bottlenecks and delays along the current patient journey were identified by comparing observed processes and time intervals with best practices recommended in hip fracture clinical guidelines (e.g., multidisciplinary management and surgery within 48 h of admission).9,25,26 This visual tool helped to identify delays and variations in care delivery and highlighted opportunities for improving clinical workflows in resource-constrained hospital settings. All analyses were conducted in Microsoft Excel (16.91), and the patient journey maps were produced using Adobe Illustrator (2023).

Step3: Co-design workshop

Participants

We used purposive stakeholder sampling to ensure representation across multiple disciplines, including clinical medicine, public health, and health management. The workshops were designed as a clinician- and manager-led, evidence-informed co-design process informed by findings from Steps 1–2. Participants in the co-design workshops included: (1) clinical staff, including internist and general practitioners from grade 3 A hospitals, orthopaedic surgeon, and nurse representatives from participating hospitals; (2) public health professionals from academic institutions; and (3) health management professionals from government agencies. Eligible participants were required to have a minimum of five years of professional experience in their respective fields and to be directly involved in hip fracture care pathways, service management/quality improvement, or health system functions. All workshop participants were identified through professional networks established by three senior authors (MT, MY, YW) and were invited via WeChat or telephone. Patients and caregivers were not included as participants in the co-design workshops because participation in a 2–3-h session is often not appropriate for many patients in this context from an ethical perspective, considering their age, complex clinical condition, perioperative care and rehabilitation needs, and mental health after an acute condition. To ensure that intervention development was informed by patient experiences, findings from patient journey mapping were presented and used as core inputs to the workshops. Written informed was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the workshops.

Data collection

Two rounds of co-design workshops were held, each lasting 2–3 h. Both rounds of the co-design workshops were conducted in person (face-to-face) in Beijing, China, and were held in a neutral meeting room that was not located within any participant's workplace (i.e., not within any hospital, academic institution, or government agency), the same 11 stakeholders attended both rounds. Before each workshop, participants were briefed on the project background and findings from prior two steps, including a detailed presentation of the patient journey mapping and the barriers to quality improvement identified through focus-group interviews. The discussions were facilitated by an experienced moderator (MT) and supported by trained researchers (XZ and TL).

In the first round of workshops, participants reviewed 10 identified barriers (from focus group interviews) to improving the management of hip fractures in county-level hospitals. Using an electronic questionnaire, they rated each barrier on two criteria: importance (how critical it is to address the barrier to improve quality) and feasibility (how practical it is to resolve the barrier), both scored on a scale from 0 (not important/feasible) to 10 (highly important/feasible). This structured scoring operationalised an importance–feasibility prioritisation matrix. An open-ended question was also included to gather suggestions for overcoming each barrier.

Before the second round, the workshop canvas was updated with the prioritized barriers and potential interventions derived from the first round. These interventions were based on literature or suggestions from the open-ended responses. Participants were invited to discuss, propose additional interventions, and consider factors that could influence the implementation of solutions. The workshops used multiple tools, including PowerPoint presentations, electronic questionnaires, workshop canvases, and post-it notes to collect feedback.

Data analysis

In the first workshop, two sets of mean scores (importance and feasibility) were calculated for each barrier. These scores were used to construct an importance–feasibility prioritisation matrix to guide the selection of priority barriers for further discussion.27 We then used the median value of all barrier scores (for importance and feasibility, respectively) as thresholds; barriers falling below the median on either dimension were deemed lower priority and excluded from further discussion. All quantitative summaries were conducted in R (version 4.3.1).

In the second workshop, the barriers prioritised in the first round were further explored to develop and refine intervention options. Workshop artefacts were systematically documented, including (i) electronic questionnaire data (importance/feasibility ratings and open-ended written responses), (ii) completed workshop canvases, (iii) post-it notes, and (iv) meeting minutes. Quantitative ranking outputs were used to confirm which barriers were taken forward, while qualitative materials (open-ended responses, minutes, canvases, and notes) were analysed using qualitative coding by three researchers (TL, NT, and BN). Codes were consolidated and synthesised into three implementation-oriented outputs: (1) areas to intervene, (2) specific intervention components, and (3) recommendations for implementation strategies. These outputs were iteratively reviewed by the authors team (MT, MY, YW, XZ, TL, NT, BN, and GL) to resolve interpretive differences and to finalise the context-tailored intervention package.

Reflexivity and power dynamics

Reflexivity was considered throughout focus group interview data collection, qualitative analysis, and co-design workshops. The team included public health researchers, researchers with nursing or health management training, and an orthopaedic surgeon; MT (public health) facilitated with an implementation-oriented focus on system processes and feasibility, while MY (orthopaedics surgeon) contributed clinically informed perspectives. MT and MY are not employed by any of the participating hospitals, and are based at other institutions (Harbin Medical University and Capital Medical University affiliated Beijing Jishuitan Hospital as their respective primary affiliation), and have no managerial or supervisory relationships with the study participants; however, we recognised that hierarchical dynamics could still influence group discussions. To mitigate this during focus groups, facilitators emphasised voluntary participation and confidentiality, used neutral prompts, and asked hospital leaders to leave the room after management-related questions so that further discussion could proceed more openly with remaining participants. During analysis, reflexive debriefings explicitly examined how disciplinary perspectives shaped interpretation and the evolving analytic structure, informing iterative refinement of code definitions and theme boundaries (e.g., refining overly broad codes into more specific, action-oriented codes). We also recognised potential power imbalances in co-design workshops involving stakeholders with differing professional authority; to support equitable contribution, we used structured facilitation and multiple input channels, including an importance–feasibility prioritisation matrix (0–10 ratings via electronic questionnaire), open-ended written responses, workshop canvases, and post-it notes, and documented workshop artefacts (questionnaire data, written inputs, canvases/notes, and meeting minutes) for synthesis into intervention components. A more detailed description of our reflexive practices and co-design procedures is provided in the Supplementary Material 3.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, writing of the report, or the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Step 1: focus group interviews

Data saturation was reached after the eighth interview, with two additional interviews completed to confirm the findings. A total of 137 participants from 10 county-level hospitals participated in the focus group interviews (range: 9–19 participants per hospital; characteristics of participating hospitals and number of participants in Supplementary Table S2), each lasting around 90 min. The number of participants per hospital ranged from 9 to 19. The participants' average age was 46.1 years (SD = 7.5), with 25.5% female (n = 35) and 88.3% having over 10 years of work experience (n = 121). The professional backgrounds of the participants spanned departments involved in the treatment and management of hip fractures, with nearly 60% representing orthopaedics and internal medicine (n = 80), others include anesthesiology (n = 12), nursing (n = 2), rehabilitation physician (n = 10), emergency medicine (n = 16), geriatrics (n = 6) and hospital administration (n = 11). Participants’ characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table S3. Through data analysis, 10 barriers (Fig. 2) and 10 enablers (Supplementary Figure S2) were identified as influencing the uptake of quality hip fracture management. Issues related to delays in surgery and challenges in postoperative rehabilitation were the most discussed obstacles to improving care. The most frequently cited challenges were.

Step 2: patient journey mapping

Observations were collected with 39 older patients with hip fractures across 10 county-level hospitals, with 2–6 patients observed per hospital, hospital-level counts are reported in Supplementary Table S2. The journey mapping (Fig. 3) revealed that patients typically stayed in the hospital for 7–20 days after entering the ED. The time taken to transfer patients from the ED to the orthopaedic ward ranged from 20 min to 60 min. The wait time between admission and surgery varied significantly, ranging from 18 h to 6 days, with only 16 patients (41%) undergoing surgery within the recommended 48 h. Additionally, 7 patients (18%) did not receive surgery during their hospital stay. The total length of stay ranged from 7 to 27 days, with an average of 14 days. Only a few patients (n = 4) received postoperative rehabilitation and osteoporosis assessments. None of the hospitals implemented fall risk assessments or falls prevention measures.

Step 3: Co-design workshop

Participant characteristics

The co-design workshop was attended by 11 experts, with 6 (55%) being clinical staff members, 4 (36%) from academic institutions and 1 (9%) from government agency. The participants had a median of 15 years of professional experience, ranging from 10 to 38 years, across fields including orthopaedics (n = 4, 36%), with two orthopaedic specialist from county-level hospital, public health professionals (n = 5, 45%), hospital management (n = 1, 9%; who also had a professional background in both internal medicine and general practice), and nurse (from county-level hospital, n = 1, 9%).

Identifying quality improvement priorities

During the first round of the co-design workshop, participants rated 10 challenges affecting the quality of hip fracture management. The importance scores ranged from 6.7 to 9.1 (median = 7.7, IQR = 1.45), while feasibility scores ranged from 2.8 to 8.5 (median = 6.1, IQR = 2.95). Four key challenges were identified as both important and feasible to be intervened (Fig. 4). First, prolonged waiting times for in-hospital examinations and consultations (importance score = 7.5, feasibility score = 7.4). Second, difficulties in managing complex comorbidities in older hip fracture patients (importance score = 9.1, feasibility score = 7.0). Third, patients and their families have limited knowledge of the harms of hip fractures and optimal treatment methods (importance score = 8.1, feasibility score = 8.5). Lastly, lack of interdisciplinary coordination in clinical decision-making (importance score = 8.7, feasibility score = 8.0).

Co-designed interventions

In the second round of the co-design workshop, 13 potential interventions were identified to address the four key challenges for quality improvement (Fig. 5). These interventions were based on the recommendations from the 2022 edition of the Guidelines for the Treatment and Management of Hip Fractures and insights from the literature and the first workshop. The interventions were grouped into three primary objectives: (1) optimization of in-hospital management processes, (2) empower the surgeons and physicians, and (3) health education for patients and families.

(1)Optimization of in-hospital management processes

Optimization of in-hospital management processes

Identification of intervention strategies from co-design workshops.

To optimize the management of hip fracture cases in county hospitals, participants agreed that promoting multidisciplinary collaboration was crucial. A multidisciplinary team, led by hospital administrators, should be formed to oversee the entire treatment and management process for hip fracture patients. This team would develop standardized procedures for diagnosis and treatment, establish multidisciplinary work models, define surgical indications, and hold regular team meetings to assess and improve care.

A second recommended intervention was creating a “fast track” for hip fracture patients, similar to those used for stroke and heart attack patients, to expedite diagnostic tests and surgeries.

“County hospitals should start with multidisciplinary teams led by hospital administrators to leverage resources across departments.”

Hospital Management Specialist

“Each hospital should develop its own multidisciplinary approach, with regular meetings to ensure the team is effective.”

Orthopaedic Specialist, National Orthopaedic Center

“For tests like Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), hip fracture patients should have priority, as stroke patients do.”

Orthopaedic Specialist, County Hospitals

(2)Empowering surgeons and physicians

Empowering surgeons and physicians

Participants emphasised the need to strengthen clinicians’ capacity for guideline-concordant care through blended training and a structured audit-and-feedback mechanism. Training was proposed to build shared understanding of best practices, while quarterly audit-and-feedback was recommended to review key care-process indicators and reinforce implementation over time.

“We now have evidence-based guidelines, and training is essential for multidisciplinary teams to implement them effectively.”

Orthopaedic Specialist, National Orthopaedic Center

“In many cases, physicians are not familiar with the guidelines, making training essential for adherence.”

Orthopaedic Specialist, County Hospitals

It was suggested that training should combine online and in-person formats to ensure continuous learning. A quarterly feedback mechanism was recommended to reinforce the training content.

“A mix of online and offline training, with quarterly feedback, would help consolidate learning.”

Health education for patients and families

Most participants recommended that education should extend beyond the patients to their families, focusing on the benefits of timely surgery, early postoperative rehabilitation, and the long-term management of hip fractures. Ensuring that educational materials are engaging and relatable is essential for enhancing patients’ acceptance of health education materials.

Discussion

This study used patient journey mapping, focus group interviews, and clinician- and manager-led, evidence-informed co-design workshops to identify four major barriers to high-quality hip fracture care in resource-constrained county-level hospitals in China: excessive waiting time for preoperative assessments, difficulties in managing complex comorbidities, limited patient and family knowledge, and lack of multidisciplinary collaboration. Based on these challenges, we co-developed a suite of intervention consisting of three major components: optimization of in-hospital management processes, empowerment of surgeons and physicians, and enhanced health education for patients and families.

Time from admission to surgery in the county-level hospitals was up to six days, as identified from the patient journey mapping. These delays may be linked to system-level barriers in these resource-constrained hospital. First, older patients with hip fractures often require assessments from multiple hospital departments, including emergency medicine, orthopaedics, anesthesia, cardiology, and endocrinology.10,28 Most county-level hospitals do not have standardized “fast-track” services or coordinated multidisciplinary processes.29, 30, 31 Despite prior research supports multidisciplinary team models and shows that they promote early surgery in hip fracture care,11,32,33 county-level hospitals often do not have formal communication channels between departments. Second, another contributing factor is related to the performance management system within the county hospitals. Each clinical department in the county hospitals focus on generating revenues within the department. Collaboration across departments receives little recognition or incentive.34,35 Third, diagnostic capacity is also limited. In the focus group interviews, we found that, due to limited medical equipment in the county-level hospitals, CT and MRI scans often require scheduled appointments, which further delays preoperative evaluation. Lastly, patient-related factors add to this challenge. Comorbidity management among older hip fracture patients was one of the most frequently raised issues in the focus group interviews. Individuals with cardiovascular disease frequently use anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents, which require repeated assessments before surgery. Discontinuation of these medications before surgery commonly contributes to surgical delays.36,37 Streamlined in-hospital management processes, including simplified perioperative pathways and fast-track systems, have been shown to shorten surgical waiting times and reduce mortality.11,32,33 Similar approaches have been widely adopted in the acute management of myocardial infarction and stroke and have demonstrated good feasibility and effectiveness even in resource-limited settings.38,39

The awareness and uptake of the up-to-date guidelines among the healthcare professionals in the county level hospitals remain scarce. From the patient journey mapping, less than half of the patients underwent surgery within the guideline recommended 48 h, while only a few patients received postoperative rehabilitation. These observations were consistent with the focus group interviews that preoperative decision-making tended to be conservative and postoperative rehabilitation was initiated late. The lack of familiarity with the clinical guidelines may, to some extent, contribute to prolonged surgical delays and inadequate early postoperative rehabilitation. Although current guidelines and substantial clinical evidence strongly recommend early surgery and early weight-bearing rehabilitation for older patients with hip fractures,40, 41, 42 clinical decision-making in resource-constrained hospitals tends to follow conventional practices. For example, in the preoperative stage, internist and anesthesiologist incline to adopt conservative approaches when managing patients with comorbidities, which prolongs preoperative preparation and surgical delays.43,44 In the postoperative stage, orthopaedic surgeons in county-level hospitals favor extended bed rest rather guideline-recommended early mobilization. Evidence indicates that structured training programs help to improve clinicians’ awareness of current guidelines and evidence from clinical research, together with better interdisciplinary communication.32,45 Training focused on strengthening guideline literacy and adherence among healthcare professionals is thus essential for promoting evidence-based best practices in resource-constrained county-level hospitals. However, from a behaviour change perspective (e.g., the COM-B model), training primarily targets capability and is unlikely to be sufficient on its own.46 We therefore paired training with system-level supports to make guideline-concordant care easier to deliver in routine practice. These supports include standardised pathways, multidisciplinary coordination, and quarterly audit-and-feedback based on key care-process indicators.46 In addition, our patient journey mapping showed clear gaps in secondary prevention processes in the participating hospitals, including limited osteoporosis assessment and the absence of falls risk assessment and falls prevention measures. Fracture liaison services have been widely recognised as one of the most effective clinical care models for secondary fracture prevention, and should be fully considered in future efforts to strengthen hip fracture secondary prevention systems in China.47,48

Insufficient knowledge of the perceived benefits of early surgery and early mobilization among patients and their family members is another key barrier to improving the quality of hip fracture care, as raised in the interviews. This was mainly reflected in inadequate awareness of the importance of timely surgery, postoperative rehabilitation, and secondary prevention after fracture, which in turn contributed to delays in surgery and early rehabilitation. A large body of evidence demonstrates that poor disease knowledge is closely associated with delayed or refused surgery.49,50 Many patients fear long-term immobility after surgery, hold misconceptions about anesthesia risks, or mistakenly believe that conservative treatment is safer, leading to delays in receiving appropriate and prompt surgical care. Patients and caregivers with inadequate understanding of the importance of rehabilitation are less willing to accept early mobilization.51, 52, 53 Older adults who lack health knowledge demonstrate poorer adherence to rehabilitation and have higher complication rates.53,54 Multifaceted health educational tools, such as short videos, peer storytelling and multimedia resources, may help address this barrier and improve patient engagement and adherence, as suggested by the co-design workshops and supported by previous studies.55, 56, 57

Our intervention package was co-designed to reflect the current realities of hip fracture care and the barriers identified in resource-constrained county-level hospitals in China. It is likely to be applicable to resource-limited hospitals in other low- and middle-income countries with similar health system, although local adaptation will be required. The care standards underpinning our framework were drawn from the Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment, and Management of Hip Fractures in Older Adults (2022 Edition) issued by China's National Health Commission.25 These standards align with internationally recommended best practices, including coordinated multidisciplinary care, timely surgery, early mobilisation and rehabilitation, and secondary prevention.9,10,22,41 In other health systems, implementation should be adapted to local workforce capacity, perioperative support, rehabilitation resources, and referral pathways.

This study has several limitations. First, due to the required logistics for the patient journey mapping, including study personnel and funding, we were unable to conduct a large number of observations in each study site. Nonethless, the current available observations identified a consistent pattern of the patient journey across all ten participating provinces. Second, because county-level hospitals vary substantially in staffing, service capacity, and organizational structure, the findings may not fully represent all settings of similar resource levels. Efforts were made to mitigate this by including hospitals from multiple provinces with diverse geographic and administrative profiles. Third, patients were not included in the co-design workshops. Despite intended end users of the interventions are older hip fracture patients, it is often not appropriate for many patients to participate in a 2–3-h workshop ethically. To partially address this limitation, we incorporated findings from the patient journey mapping into the co-design workshops, ensuring that intervention discussions were informed by observed patient flow, delays, and care experiences. The absence of direct patient or caregiver involvement in co-design may have limited patient-centred insights. Future work may involve patients and caregivers using feasible and ethically appropriate approaches, such as brief interviews during admission, post-operative or post-discharge engagement, caregiver participation, or consumer representatives. Fourth, perspectives from nursing and other allied health professionals were limited. Nursing participation in the focus groups was low, which may have reduced the breadth of views on bedside care processes. In addition, rehabilitation and nutrition professionals were not included in the co-design workshops because these roles are not consistently available across county-level hospitals. We sought to mitigate this by including a nurse representative and general practitioners in the co-design workshops to incorporate broader care-delivery perspectives into intervention development. Lastly, typical of much qualitative research, the purposive sampling strategy used for the co-design workshops may limit generalizability. However, the inclusion of multiple stakeholders ensured that a wide range of perspectives relevant to hip fracture care.

Conclusion

This study employed qualitative interviews, patient journey mapping, and co-design methods to examine the current state of hip fracture management in resource-constrained county-level hospitals in China, and further to develop context-tailored interventions aimed at improving care quality for older hip fracture patients. The identified interventions focus on three key areas: (1) optimization of in-hospital management processes, (2) empowerment of surgeons and physicians, and (3) health education for patients and families. Future research should prioritize evaluating the effectiveness of these interventions through large-scale randomized controlled trials in pragmatic settings.
