Defining hotspot for estimation of Ki-67 proliferation index of neuroendocrine tumors: QuPath algorithm vs. manual assessment
Authors: Ruben Oganesyan, Osman Yilmaz, Yevgen Chornenkyy, Kanchan Kantekure, Yuho Ono, Buthania Hakamy, Nandan Padmanabha, Vikram Deshpande, Raul S Gonzalez, Monika Vyas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKi-67 proliferation index (PI) is essential for grading well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (WD-NETs). Pathologists traditionally assess Ki-67 PI by identifying hotspots and manually counting the positive cells among negative cells, which is expressed as a percentage. We developed an algorithm to objectively determine Ki-67 hotspots and calculate PI in WD-NETs, comparing its results with pathologists' selected hotspots to assess reliability.MethodsHotspots for gastroenteropancreatic WD-NETs (n = 20) were manually annotated on whole-slide images (WSIs) by six pathologists and compared with algorithm-selected areas. Ki-67 (DAKO, MIB-1 clone, and prediluted) scoring was performed using QuPath's custom object classification algorithm. Pathologists identified hotspots on WSI, captured images, and submitted them for PI determination using the same algorithm. Ki-67 PI was translated to grade per WHO classification (G1: <3%, G2: 3–20%, G3: >20%). A pathologist's consensus grade was determined based on majority pathologist grading (>3/6) for each case. Fleiss's Kappa was used to assess inter-pathologist agreement, Cohen's Kappa was used to evaluate the agreement between pathologists and the algorithm, and Friedman test was used for hotspot area variability analysis.ResultsPathologists showed moderate agreement (Fleiss's Kappa = 0.42, 80% agreement), whereas pathologist–algorithm agreement was fair (Cohen's Kappa = 0.32, 58.9% agreement). Among cases with pathologist consensus grade (n = 19), the algorithm assigned a higher grade in 8 cases (42%). In 60% of cases, hotspots overlapped between methods. There was significant hotspot area variability (Friedman statistic: 95.97, p < 0.001).ConclusionManual Ki-67 hotspot assessment is subjective, leading to grading variability. Algorithm-based assessment enhances reproducibility, though this occasionally leads to tumor upgrading, highlighting the need for standardization and further validation.Keywords: Well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors, Computer assisted grading, QuPath, Ki-67

Introduction

Well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (WD-NETs) of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract comprise a subset of neuroendocrine neoplasms that arise from the neuroendocrine cells present throughout the GI tract. Tumor grade is a strong and well-established predictor and is a critical decision-point in predicting clinical outcomes in GI-NETs.

According to the WHO 2019 and 2025 guidelines, WD-NETs are classified into three grades based on their mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index (PI): low-grade (G1), intermediate-grade (G2), and high-grade (G3).1, 2, 3 The grading of WD-NETs is important for prognostication and informing therapeutic strategies. Traditionally, pathologists evaluate the Ki-67 PI by manually counting the number of positive cells within visually identified hotspot areas of the tumor.4, 5 Whereas this method remains the gold-standard, it is quite cumbersome and inherently subjective, leading to inter-observer variability. This can result in inconsistencies in tumor grading, potentially impacting treatment decisions and patient care. Matsukuma et al. demonstrated that inter-observer agreement on Ki-67 proliferative index using Ki-67–only stained slides of WD-NETs was moderate, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.51 (95% CI: 0.35–0.66).6 The tediousness of manual counting (MC) can also lead to adoption of non-standard practices such as eyeball estimate (EE). Tang et al. compared MC of more than 2000 cells with digital image analysis (DIA) and showed that the ICC for inter-observer concordance among pathologists using EEs was weak (0.13), highlighting significant variability.4

To mitigate these challenges and enhance the accuracy of Ki-67 assessment, we utilized an advanced algorithm tailored to automatically identify Ki-67 hotspots within WD-NETs. This algorithm not only detects the hotspot region consistently but also computes the Ki-67 PI, aiming to deliver a more objective and reproducible quantification compared to manual methods. By comparing the hotspot areas and Ki-67 scores generated by our algorithm with those determined by pathologists, we sought to evaluate the algorithm's performance in terms of accuracy and reliability, with an aim to improve the consistency and quality of tumor grading in clinical practice.

Methods

The study was approved by the institutional review board and conducted over a 1-year period (January 1, 2023–December 31, 2023). Eligible cases were identified by querying the pathology information system (SoftPath LIVE2) using the terms “neuroendocrine tumor” and “well-differentiated”. Fig. 1 outlines the study design.

An overview of the study. H&E- and MIB-1 stained slides were scanned using a Philips® digital slide scanner. Six GI pathologists manually selected “hotspot” regions for Ki-67 PI estimation, and their selections were compared with an image analysis algorithm for concordance.

Case selection and histological preparation

Twenty WD-NETs (10 small intestinal and 10 pancreatic) meeting inclusion criteria were identified. Specimens were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Selected blocks were immunostained for Ki-67 (DAKO, MIB-1 clone, and prediluted) as part of routine diagnostic evaluation. All H&E and Ki-67–stained slides were retrieved for review.

Slide scanning

H&E- and Ki-67–stained slides were digitized using a Philips® digital slide scanner (SG300, Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, Netherlands). Images were reviewed for quality in Philips IntelliSite Pathology Solutions (PIPS) before analysis. Whole-slide images (WSIs) were exported from PIPS in TIFF format for downstream image analysis.

Manual hotspot selection by pathologists

To assess variability in manual hotspot selection, six GI pathologists independently annotated Ki-67 hotspot regions on WSIs using a pathology image-sharing platform and submitted screenshots of their selections. These annotations were transferred onto corresponding WSIs and used to calculate Ki-67 PI using QuPath (Fig. 1). Hotspots selected by pathologists and the algorithm were classified as concordant (overlapping) or discordant.

Computer-assisted digital image analysis

All image analyses were performed by a single operator (RVO) to minimize inter-operator variability. QuPath (version 0.5.1) was used for annotation, cell detection, and quantification. Whole tumor areas were annotated using the brush tool, excluding ink, lymphocyte aggregates, and non-neoplastic mucosa. Pathologist-selected hotspots were manually annotated to match their exact locations.

Cell detection was performed using the hematoxylin optical density channel with a pixel size of 0.5 μm/pixel. Background radius was set to 8 μm, Gaussian smoothing to 1 μm, and size filters excluded objects <10 μm2 or >400 μm2. A detection threshold of 0.2 units and maximum background intensity of 2 units were applied. Cytoplasmic regions were approximated by 5 μm nuclear expansion, and shape-based splitting was enabled to resolve overlapping nuclei. Morphometric features were extracted for downstream analysis.

Object classification and training

A Random Forest classifier (“RTrees”) incorporating all available morphological and intensity features was trained using QuPath's OBJECT CLASSIFICATION framework. To prevent bias, classifier training was performed on regions excluded from quantitative analysis. Objects were manually labeled as Ki-67–positive tumor cells, Ki-67–negative tumor cells, stromal cells, or blood.

Automated hotspot detection

After cell classification, hotspot detection was performed in QuPath using a density map-based hotspot algorithm within the annotated tumor region (Fig. 2). A circular sliding window (0.3 mm2; ∼1300 cells depending on local density) was used to calculate local Ki-67 positivity across the tumor ROI. The hotspot was defined as the location with the highest Ki-67 positivity and recorded as a circular annotation of fixed area (0.13 mm2), constrained to remain entirely within the tumor annotation. Stromal and other non-tumor cells were excluded by restricting the analysis to tumor cell detections.

Automated Ki-67 hotspot detection. (A) Tumor ROI annotation. (B) Automated cell detection. (C) Machine-learning classification of detections into Ki-67–positive, Ki-67–negative, and other classes. (D) Density map-based hotspot identification highlighting the region of maximal Ki-67 positivity.

Validation and application

Classifier performance was evaluated on independent non-analytical regions with iterative parameter refinement based on visual assessment. Once optimized, all analyses were executed using scripted workflows without further manual intervention. The trained classifier was applied to define regions of interest and subsequent determination of Ki-67 PI/ tumor grade (described below). Algorithm-defined hotspots were compared with pathologist-selected hotspots for spatial overlap and grading concordance.

Grading

The tumors were assigned a grade per the WGO 2019 classification of tumors; grade 1 (G1): Ki-67 <3%, mitoses <2/mm2, Grade 2 (G2): Ki-67—3–20%, mitoses—2–20/2 mm2, grade 3 (G3)—Ki-67 >20%, mitoses >20/2 mm2.1 A tumor grade was determined for each case based on each pathologist's selected hotspot. A consensus grade was defined as agreement among more than three of six pathologists. Cases for which a consensus grade could not be reached were excluded from comparative analyses.

Statistical analysis

Inter-observer agreement among pathologists was assessed using Fleiss's Kappa and percentage agreement, whereas agreement between pathologists and the algorithm was assessed using Cohen's Kappa. Kappa values were interpreted using standard thresholds.

Differences in grading among individual pathologists, consensus grades, and algorithm-assigned grades were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Grade variance within hotspot groups was calculated based on spatial overlap (groups A, B, C, and Z) and compared using Kruskal–Wallis testing.

Hotspot area size variability among raters was assessed using the Friedman test. Due to non-normal data distribution, results were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Inter-observer agreement among pathologists and between pathologists and algorithm

There was moderate agreement between pathologists (Fleiss's Kappa = 0.42, percentage agreement: 80%) and fair agreement between pathologists and QuPath algorithm (Cohen's Kappa = 0.32, percentage agreement: 58.9%) (Table 1).

Comparison of pathologists' hotspot determined grading vs. algorithm-assigned grade

Among cases with a pathologist consensus grade (n = 19), there was complete agreement between the QuPath classifier and the pathologist consensus grade in 7 cases (37%; 6 G1 and 1 G2) (Fig. 4). Discordance was observed in the remaining 12 cases (63%). In 2 cases (11%), the pathologists assigned a higher grade than the algorithm (G2 vs G1). Grade variance within hotspot groups

The results revealed overall low variance within overlapping hotspot groups (A, B, and C), with most values near or equal to zero (average grade variance, A = 0.026, B = 0, and C = 0), indicating high intra-group grading consistency. This finding indicates that the consistency of grading among pathologists is influenced by the spatial concordance of hotspot selection. In particular, group Z, which represents pathologists who selected non-overlapping hotspots, demonstrated a higher intra-group grade variance compared to groups A, B, and C, where hotspot selections overlapped. These results suggest that disagreement in hotspot selection contributes to greater variability in assigned tumor grades. Pathologists who selected similar areas also tended to assign the same grade, highlighting the subjectivity of tumor grading and its strong dependence on the hotspot. In contrast, group Z, which represented discordant or independent hotspot selections, exhibited greater variability in grade assignments (average grade variance, Z = 0.156). Notably, cases such as 4, 8, and 11 showed increased variance within the Z group, suggesting that a lack of spatial agreement may be associated with decreased grading consistency. A Kruskal–Wallis H-test revealed a statistically significant difference in grade variance among the groups A, B, C, and Z (H = 11.28, p = 0.0103). Overall, the data suggest that spatial concordance among pathologists is associated with more consistent tumor grading, whereas non-overlapping selections may introduce greater variability. Moreover, Kruskal–Wallis H-test revealed a trend toward higher grading by rater 5 as compared to other raters (H= 2.8, p: 0.094). In 10/20 cases, there was complete grade concordance among the 6 pathologist and the assignment of consensus grade was unanimous. In one case, a consensus grade could not be reached as there was an even split between pathologists assigning G1 and G2 grades (Table 1, case 8).

Hotspot area size comparison

Comparison of hotspot area sizes selected by the six raters revealed statistically significant differences, as determined by the Friedman test (χ2 = 95.97, p < 0.001). The median hotspot areas, converted to mm2, and their percentage differences from the overall median were as follows: Rater 1 selected a median area of 0.252 mm2 (−6.13%), Rater 2 selected 0.250 mm2 (−6.72%), and Rater 3 selected 0.269 mm2 (+0.29%). In contrast, Rater 4 selected 0.423 mm2 (+57.40%), Rater 5 selected a substantially larger area of 1.085 mm2 (+303.88%), and Rater 6 selected the smallest area at 0.175 mm2 (−34.92%). These findings highlight considerable inter-rater variability in hotspot selection, with some raters selecting areas more than three times larger or smaller than the overall median. Discussion

Our study underscores the challenge of inter-observer variability in the manual assessment of Ki-67 PI in WD-NETs and highlights the potential of automated image analysis tools in providing a more consistent and objective assessment.7 National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend that Ki-67 immunohistochemistry should be analyzed in the areas of highest activity, referred to as hotspots, and that tumor differentiation, mitotic rate, and Ki-67 index should all be included in the pathology report to guide clinical decision-making.8

Whereas manual hotspot detection by pathologists is integral to current clinical practice, it is inherently subjective and introduces variability in grading—both in terms of area selected as the hotspot and the size of the hotspot, as evidenced by the statistically significant difference in hotspot areas chosen by the pathologists (Friedman statistic: 95.9714, p < 0.001). The moderate agreement among pathologists (Fleiss's Kappa = 0.421) further illustrates this variability, which has critical implications for tumor grading and, consequently, patient management. Studies have shown that manual methods, including eyeballing, can result in inconsistent Ki-67 indices and tumor grades. Whereas the “eyeballing” method is not desirable, MC is still regarded as the gold-standard.2 The manual method, however, is not without limitations. For instance, Tang et al. demonstrated that MC methods had lower concordance compared to DIA, with ICC of 0.13 for individual observers using eyeballing, indicating poor agreement.4 Similarly, Govind et al. highlighted the challenges in distinguishing tumor from non-tumor cells, which can further exacerbate the variability in Ki-67 assessment.9

The algorithm we developed offers a potential solution to this issue by introducing objectivity into the process of hotspot identification and Ki-67 PI calculation. The fair agreement between the pathologists and the QuPath algorithm (Cohen's Kappa = 0.32) indicates that whereas there is still room for improvement, the algorithm demonstrates a reasonable level of reliability compared to current practice. Interestingly, in 60% of cases, the areas selected by at least one pathologist overlapped with those identified by the algorithm, suggesting that the tool often targets biologically relevant regions. Furthermore, in 35% of cases, the algorithm and pathologists provided one concordant grade. This shows that the algorithm can replicate human performance in many instances, but MC lacks consistency, both in terms of area selection and manual count. Studies have shown that DIA can serve as a more objective and reproducible method for Ki-67 quantification. Studies have shown that DIA methods have higher concordance with MC when stringent criteria are applied. For example, Boukhar et al. found that DIA platforms showed excellent correlation with MC, with ICCs indicating high reliability.7 Additionally, Matsukuma et al. demonstrated that using synaptophysin–Ki-67 double staining improved inter-observer agreement, suggesting that this method could mitigate some of the subjectivity inherent in traditional Ki-67 assessment.6

There are discrepancies between the algorithm and pathologists, particularly in cases where the algorithm upgraded the tumor grade (e.g., from G1 to G2 or G2 to G3), raising important questions regarding the clinical implications of algorithm-driven assessment. In 42% of the current studies cases, the algorithm identified a higher grade hotspots than the pathologists, which could reflect either the algorithm's heightened sensitivity to subtle differences in proliferation or a potential overestimation of the grade. Whereas upgrading may reflect a more accurate assessment of the tumor's biological behavior, it also highlights the need for further validation of the algorithm against clinical outcomes, including disease progression and patient survival. The validation of algorithms for Ki-67 assessment in gastroenteropancreatic WD-NETs against clinical outcomes, including disease progression and patient survival, has been explored in several studies. Zhang et al. reported that DIA of Ki-67 heterogeneity provided better diagnostic and prognostic insights for such tumors. This study found that higher heterogeneity indices correlated with poor disease-free survival, highlighting the prognostic value of DIA in assessing Ki-67.10 Longitudinal studies have also validated the prognostic significance of changes in Ki-67 over time. Daskalakis et al. observed that an increase in Ki-67 indices and grade progression over time was associated with worse survival outcomes in small intestinal WD-NETs. Holmager et al. further supported this by showing that a doubling of the Ki-67 index at disease progression was independently associated with higher all-cause mortality.11, 12

The findings from this study also emphasize the necessity of standardizing hotspot selection criteria. The variability in selected hotspot size and location among pathologists not only affects grading consistency but also introduces uncertainty into therapeutic decision-making. Whereas in many cases, variability in hotspot detection may not impact the grade, this may become important in borderline cases. In such cases, pathologists often manually assess 2–3 hotspots to determine the highest PI value. Our results suggest that implementing an automated system for hotspot detection, alongside clearly defined protocols, could significantly reduce inter-observer variability and improve the reproducibility of Ki-67 PI assessment. In addition to routine clinical practice, this could be particularly valuable in multicenter studies, where consistency is paramount. Owens et al. demonstrated that different anti-K67 antibody clones and variations in hotspot sizes can lead to discrepancies in Ki-67 PI, with non-MIB1 clones overestimating the PI compared to the MIB1 clone. This study also found that increasing the hotspot size from 500 to 2000 cells resulted in a decrease in Ki-67 PI, which is not unexpected but still underscores the need for standardized hotspot criteria.13 Boukhar et al. highlighted the importance of stringent case and hotspot level concordance requirements in DIA for Ki-67 assessment.7 Their study showed that DIA methods, when optimized and validated, can achieve high concordance with MC, but emphasized the need for standardization to ensure reproducibility.

Despite these promising results, there are limitations to our study. The sample size was relatively small, and the study was conducted using retrospective data from a single center. We also asked the pathologists to submit a 200× image of their selected hotspot region instead of actually counting the cells manually. Whereas we acknowledge this is a deviation from practice, this modification was employed to boost participation in this study. We also felt the goal was to demonstrate the variation in the area and size of hotspot selected, which could be accomplished without asking the pathologists to count the cells in the hotspot. The significance of upgrading the tumor based by DIA is not known and whether new grading thresholds are necessary given the increased sensitivity. Larger studies are necessary to determine the clinical importance of grade assignment by DIA and to validate the applicability of this method in lieu of MC. Whereas introducing AI-driven tools in diagnostic work-up of tumors has its advantages, wider application of this technology is subject to limitations in funds and resources. If DIA is to become standard of care in routine diagnostic work-up, supporting technology and tools must be available at minimal cost to pathologists worldwide to ensure uniformity in implementation of grading systems.

In conclusion, our study shows that there is significant variation in hotspots selection (location and size) for determination of Ki-67 PI among pathologists. This variation introduces significant discordance in PI assessment and grade assignment in addition to the actual method of counting (manual vs. DIA). This difference is more pronounced in the borderline (G1/G2) cases. By utilizing an automated Ki-67 hotspot detection tool, we demonstrate reduction in subjectivity in the grading of WD-NETs, offering a more reproducible and objective alternative to manual assessment. Further research is required to validate this approach in larger, more diverse populations and to investigate its impact on patient outcomes.
