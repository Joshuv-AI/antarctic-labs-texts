Osteoarthritis and analgesic consumption in haemochromatosis HFE C282Y homozygotes with normal or low iron parameters
Authors: Jens Helby, Mathis Mottelson, Stig Egil Bojesen, Børge Grønne Nordestgaard, Mikkel Faurschou, Jesper Petersen, Christina Ellervik, Marie Warny, Andreas Glenthøj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We studied 132,525 Danish general population individuals to examine whether risk of osteoarthritis and/or use of pain-relieving medication was increased in haemochromatosis C282Y homozygotes with normal or low plasma iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin. We genotyped all 132,525 individuals for the HFE C282Y and H63D variants. During a median follow-up of 40 years, 31,636 individuals had osteoarthritis. Risk of osteoarthritis was increased even in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low plasma iron (hazard ratio:1.37;95% confidence interval:1.12-1.68 compared to non-carriers with normal/low iron), transferrin saturation (1.55;1.10-2.16), or ferritin (1.96;1.11-3.45). Here we show that risk of osteoarthritis is increased in those C282Y homozygotes not usually recommended for genotyping according to clinical guidelines, challenging the presumption that the increased risk of osteoarthritis is mainly caused by systemic iron accumulation. Indeed, C282Y homozygotes with normal or low ferritin levels had a particularly high cumulative incidence of any osteoarthritis (24% at age 60 years, 60% at age 80 years).

Introduction

Osteoarthritis and arthropathy are frequent complications in individuals with hereditary haemochromatosis, and can cause chronic pain, impaired mobility, and overall reduced quality of life1–4. The most common cause of hereditary haemochromatosis is genetic variants in the HFE gene, which encodes the human homeostatic iron regulator protein5,6. Such variants can cause increased iron absorption from the intestines, potentially resulting in iron accumulation in the liver and other organs5,7,8. In individuals of European origin, homozygosity for the HFE C282Y variant is found in around 90% of individuals with hereditary haemochromatosis, while compound heterozygosity for the HFE C282Y and the H63D variants is found in less than 5% of individuals with hereditary haemochromatosis2,9.

Most clinical guidelines are based on the presumption that complications from haemochromatosis, such as liver cirrhosis, diabetes, and osteoarthritis, are caused by systemic iron accumulation, reflected by increased levels of transferrin saturation and ferritin in the blood. According to guidelines, HFE genotyping is therefore usually recommended only in individuals with increased transferrin saturation and/or ferritin, and the primary treatment for hereditary haemochromatosis is therapeutic phlebotomy, aiming to reduce ferritin, and if possible also transferrin saturation, to normal or low levels2,10–12. However, the long-standing recommendations on treatment with therapeutic phlebotomy are based mainly on observational studies, as randomised clinical trials examining the effect of phlebotomy on risk of complications in hereditary haemochromatosis have not been performed8. Surprisingly, we recently found that individuals with C282Y homozygosity had increased risk of diabetes even when having normal levels of transferrin saturation and/or ferritin, and that C282Y homozygotes with normal levels of plasma iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin were at increased risk of infections13,14. These findings indicate that the above-mentioned complications may not be caused solely by progressive iron accumulation, and the strategy outlined in current clinical guidelines may fail to detect some individuals with hereditary haemochromatosis who are at increased risk of complications. Although often assumed to be caused by progressive iron accumulation, the mechanism(s) causing an increased risk of osteoarthritis and arthropathy in C282Y homozygotes are currently unknown15–17. However, since osteoarthritis and arthropathy are among the most common complications in individuals with hereditary haemochromatosis, it is clinically important to identify individuals with hereditary haemochromatosis at particularly high risk of osteoarthritis, as this may allow intervention against osteoarthritis and other complications17–19.

Therefore, we studied 132,525 Danish general population individuals to examine whether risk of osteoarthritis and/or use of pain-relieving medication was increased in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of plasma iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin. We genotyped all individuals for the C282Y and H63D variants, and individuals were followed for a median of 40 years (range 0–45) when studying risk of osteoarthritis, and a median of 27 years (range 0–27) when examining use of pain-relieving medication.

Relative risk of osteoarthritis

During a median follow-up of 40 years (range 0-45 years), 31,636 individuals were diagnosed with osteoarthritis as part of a hospital admission, emergency room visit, or outpatient hospital consultation. Risk of any osteoarthritis was increased in C282Y homozygotes when compared to non-carrier individuals (hazard ratio 1.39;95% confidence interval[CI]:1.16-1.67; P = 0.0003) (Fig. 1), and C282Y homozygotes were diagnosed with osteoarthritis at an earlier age than non-carrier individuals (mean age at osteoarthritis diagnosis 61.0 years in C282Y homozygotes vs 64.1 years in non-carriers;P = 0.004) (Supplementary Fig. S1). In contrast, risk of any osteoarthritis was not increased in H63D heterozygotes (1.00;0.97-1.02), H63D homozygotes (1.03;0.95-1.12), C282Y heterozygotes (1.03;0.99-1.07), or C282Y/H63D compound heterozygotes (1.08;0.99-1.18) (Supplementary Fig. S1).Fig. 1Relative risk of any osteoarthritis and osteoarthritis at specific locations (knee, hip, spine, hand, and other joints) comparing C282Y homozygotes (C282Y/C282Y) with non-carriers for both C282Y and H63D (non-carrier/non-carrier).The sum of individuals diagnosed with osteoarthritis at specific joint locations exceeds the number of individuals diagnosed with any osteoarthritis since each individual could be diagnosed with osteoarthritis at more than one joint location during follow-up. As seen from Fig. 1, the majority of the 120 C282Y homozygous individuals that were diagnosed with osteoarthritis during follow-up had osteoarthritis of the knee (N = 61 C282Y homozygotes) or hip (N = 48 C282Y homozygotes). When examining risk of osteoarthritis in specific locations, the risk of osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes was most pronounced for osteoarthritis of the hip (hazard ratio 1.69;95% CI:1.27-2.24; P = 0.0003 for C282Y homozygotes vs non-carriers) and knee (hazard ratio 1.38;95% CI:1.07-1.78; P = 0.01), while the risk was not increased for osteoarthritis of the spine (hazard ratio 0.97;95% CI:0.58-1.65; P = 0.92), hand (hazard ratio 1.34;95% CI:0.64-2.83; P = 0.44), or other joints (hazard ratio 1.21;95% CI:0.85-1.74; P = 0.29)(Fig. 1).

We had limited capacity for examining risk of osteoarthritis in specific joints of the hand as our information on osteoarthritis diagnoses were from the nationwide Danish National Patient Register where diagnoses are classified according to the World Health Organisation’s International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. For the time period from January 1, 1977 until December 31, 1993, diagnoses in the Danish National Patient Register were classified according to ICD-8 codes, where all types of osteoarthritis of the hand were categorised into a single category (ICD-8 code: 71303), making us unable to analyse risk of osteoarthritis in specific joints of the hand for this time period. From January 1, 1994 onwards, diagnoses were classified according to ICD-10, which contain specific codes for osteoarthritis of the first carpometacarpal joint (ICD-10 codes M180-M189), but does not contain categories for osteoarthritis in other joints of the hand. As seen from Supplementary Fig. S2, C282Y homozygotes were not at increased risk of osteoarthritis of the first carpometacarpal joint (hazard ratio 1.34;95% CI:0.64-2.82 when compared to non-carrier individuals) when analysed in the time period where information regarding this specific joint was available.

We further examined whether C282Y homozygotes without other complications from haemochromatosis were also at increased risk of osteoarthritis by stratifying the analysis on risk of any osteoarthritis according to whether or not individuals had been diagnosed with diabetes and/or liver disease at any time before or after study enrolment. We found similar relative risk estimates for any osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes never diagnosed with diabetes or liver disease (hazard ratio 1.33;95%CI: 1.09-1.63 for C282Y homozygotes without diabetes and liver disease vs non-carriers without diabetes and liver disease) as for C282Y homozygotes diagnosed with diabetes and/or liver disease (hazard ratio 1.61;95%CI: 1.07-2.43 for C282Y homozygotes with diabetes and/or liver disease vs non-carriers with diabetes and/or liver disease)(Supplementary Fig. S3). When stratified according to age, risk estimates for any osteoarthritis was increased to a similar degree for C282Y homozygotes younger than 60 years of age (hazard ratio 1.55;95%CI: 1.20-2.02 for C282Y homozygotes with age<60 years vs non-carriers with age<60 years) and for C282Y homozygotes aged 60 years or older (hazard ratio 1.27;95%CI: 0.99-1.62 for C282Y homozygotes with age≥60 years vs non-carriers with age≥60 years)(Supplementary Fig. S4).

When examining risk of any osteoarthritis separately in men and women, relative risk of any osteoarthritis was of similar magnitude for C282Y homozygous men (hazard ratio 1.59;95%CI: 1.22-2.08 for C282Y homozygous men vs non-carrier men) and women (hazard ratio 1.26;95%CI: 0.99-1.61 for C282Y homozygous women vs non-carrier women), and we found no significant interaction at the P < 0.05 level between sex and genotype on risk of any osteoarthritis (P for interaction with sex: 0.19)(Supplementary Fig. S5). Likewise, when examining risk of any osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin stratified according to sex, we found similar risk estimates for women and men and observed no indication of an interaction between sex and genotype (Fig. 5). When further examining risk of any osteoarthritis in women stratified according to menopausal status, we found no interaction between menopausal status and HFE genotype (P for interaction: 0.93)(Supplementary Fig. S6). Risk of rheumatoid arthritis

During follow-up, 2586 individuals were diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis. Risk of rheumatoid arthritis was not increased in C282Y homozygotes (hazard ratio 1.02;95%CI: 0.51-2.04 for C282Y homozygotes vs non-carriers) (Supplementary Fig. S7). Likewise, we did not find increased risk of rheumatoid arthritis in H63D heterozygotes (0.93;0.85-1.03), H63D homozygotes (1.03;0.77-1.37), C282Y heterozygotes (0.94;0.82-1.08), or C282Y/H63D compound heterozygotes (1.30;0.99-1.71).

Discussion

We studied 132,525 general population individuals genotyped for HFE and found that C282Y homozygotes had increased risk of any osteoarthritis and increased consumption of NSAIDs. Importantly, and a novel finding, risk of osteoarthritis was increased even in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin, meaning that risk was increased in C282Y homozygotes not usually recommended for genotyping according to current clinical guidelines. These findings challenge the presumption that the increased risk of osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes are mainly caused by systemic iron accumulation and are therefore potentially ameliorated by therapeutic phlebotomy. This is especially relevant as C282Y homozygotes with normal or low ferritin levels had a particularly high absolute risk of any osteoarthritis, with a cumulative incidence for any osteoarthritis of 24% at age 60 years and 60% at age 80 years. Also a novel finding, the consumption of NSAIDs was similar or higher in C282Y homozygotes with normal/low transferrin saturation or ferritin when compared to the overall group of C282Y homozygotes.

Our finding of an overall increased risk of osteoarthritis in general population C282Y homozygotes is supported by a previous study from the UK Biobank, including more than 450,000 individuals, which is the largest previous general population study on risk of complications in C282Y homozygotes20. Importantly, in contrast to our study, the previous study could not examine whether risk of osteoarthritis was increased in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin, as the UK Biobank did not measure any of these biomarkers. When studying absolute risk of any osteoarthritis in the overall group of C282Y homozygotes, we observed a cumulative incidence for any osteoarthritis of 14% at age 60 years and 44% at age 80 years, while we found even higher cumulative incidences in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of transferrin saturation (16% at age 60; 49% at age 80 years) or ferritin (24% at age 60; 60% at age 80 years). Our findings indicate a much higher absolute risk of osteoarthritis than previously reported from a follow-up study to the aforementioned UK Biobank study, which estimated a cumulative incidence for osteoarthritis at age 75 years of 10.4% in male C282Y homozygotes and 9.2% in female C282Y homozygotes21. The markedly higher cumulative incidences observed in our study may likely be explained by differences in study design, as our follow-up period was a median of 40 years and we included both inpatient admissions, emergency room visits, and outpatient visits due to osteoarthritis, while the UK Biobank study calculated the cumulative incidence based on a median follow-up of 11.5 years and included only inpatient admissions with osteoarthritis.

According to most clinical guidelines on haemochromatosis, genotyping of HFE is currently only recommended for individuals with high levels of transferrin saturation and/or ferritin (with the exception of genotyping close relatives to patients diagnosed with hereditary haemochromatosis)2,10–12. Our findings on increased risk of osteoarthritis even in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin may challenge these recommendations, as our results indicate that the recommended testing strategy may possibly fail to identify C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of transferrin saturation and/or ferritin, even though these individuals are at increased risk of osteoarthritis. Due to the observational design of our study, it is not possible to make any conclusions regarding the mechanisms that cause the increased risk of osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes. However, our findings regarding C282Y homozygotes with low or normal levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin may indicate that other mechanisms than systemic iron accumulation could possibly explain a substantial part of the increased risk of osteoarthritis and increased use of NSAIDs in C282Y homozygotes. Although clinical guidelines are often based on the presumption that complications from haemochromatosis are mainly caused by systemic iron accumulation and therefore recommend therapeutic phlebotomy as the primary treatment2,10–12,15–17, our findings indicate that the increased risk of osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes could be caused by other mechanisms, which may not be ameliorated by therapeutic phlebotomy. The HFE gene is located on chromosome 6p21.3, near the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region, which includes the TNFA gene encoding tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α). Polymorphisms in TNFA have been linked to increased susceptibility to osteoarthritis22,23, which raises the possibility that a genetic linkage between HFE C282Y and TNFA polymorphisms could hypothetically contribute to the increased risk of osteoarthritis observed in C282Y homozygotes. HFE C282Y homozygosity may also influence immune function via macrophages, where HFE controls intracellular iron through hepcidin-ferroportin. Homozygosity for the C282Y variant disrupts intracellular iron homeostasis, leading to increased ferroportin activity and subsequent depletion of intracellular iron. This may influence macrophage phenotype and cytokine release, hypothetically contributing to the increased risk of osteoarthrosis in HFE C282Y homozygotes24,25. However, these mechanistic considerations remain purely speculative and warrant further investigation. With regards to the mechanisms causing osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes, it has been debated whether osteoarthritis is also a component of non-HFE hemochromatosis. As non-HFE hemochromatosis is typically associated with more severe iron overload than HFE hemochromatosis, it would be expected that non-HFE hemochromatosis patients had a high frequency of osteoarthritis or arthropathy if these complications were mechanistically caused by systemic iron accumulation26,27. An early report on 8 patients with juvenile hemochromatosis reported that 7 of the 8 patients had arthralgia or osteoarthritis (88%), and 6 of the patients had involvement of the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints, as also often described in HFE haemochromatosis28. Another study found a prevalence for arthropathy of 26.9% among 26 patients with juvenile hemochromatosis, and 36.3% among 11 patients with transferrin receptor 2 (TFR2)-related haemochromatosis, both of which were similar to the prevalence in a control group of C282Y homozygotes, but without specifying the affected joints27. In contrast, a later and larger study found that only 16% of the included 114 non-HFE haemochromatosis patients had arthropathy29. Likewise, a literature review including 156 genetically confirmed non-HFE haemochromatosis patients found prevalences for arthropathy or osteoarthritis of 16% in patients with haemochromatosis due to HJV (hemojuvelin) or HAMP (hepcidin) mutations and 26% in patients with TFR2-related haemochromatosis26. However, as the above-mentioned studies have not compared the risk of osteoarthritis or arthropathy between non-HFE haemochromatosis patients and general population individuals without haemochromatosis, it is unclear to which extent patients with non-HFE haemochromatosis are at increased risk of joint complications, and further studies on joint complications in non-HFE haemochromatosis are needed.

When examining specific joint locations, the majority of C282Y homozygotes diagnosed with osteoarthritis had osteoarthritis of the knee or hip, which is also among the most commonly affected joints in non-carriers. Previous studies based on patients already diagnosed with HFE haemochromatosis found that the ankle and metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints were also commonly affected, and arthropathy involving these joints in patients with HFE haemochromatosis were about as frequent as arthropathy of the knee or hip1,16,30. As our study defined osteoarthritis as having a hospital contact with osteoarthritis, it is likely that we underestimate the number of individuals with osteoarthritis affecting smaller joints, for example the joints of the hand. Therefore, our study is likely best suited for studying osteoarthritis of larger joints such as the knee and hip, as osteoarthritis in these locations are commonly diagnosed and treated in hospitals due to the common use of knee and hip replacement surgery. We could not specifically study risk of osteoarthritis of the metacarpophalangeal or ankle joints since osteoarthritis in these joint locations do not have specific ICD-8 or ICD-10 codes and our diagnoses of osteoarthritis were categorized according to ICD-8 and ICD-10 codes based on information from the nationwide Danish National Patient Register, which covers all hospitals in Denmark31. However, when studying risk of joint replacement surgery, we found an increased risk of ankle replacement in C282Y homozygotes (as well as an increased risk of hip replacement), indicating that C282Y homozygotes likely have an increased risk of severe chronic joint disease of the ankle, which can lead to ankle replacement surgery. It should also be noted that the overwhelming majority of C282Y homozygotes who were diagnosed with osteoarthritis during follow-up were never diagnosed with haemochromatosis, indicating that most C282Y homozygotes diagnosed with osteoarthritis in our study were diagnosed by doctors who likely used standard criteria for diagnosing osteoarthritis and did not suspect underlying haemochromatosis. Therefore, further studies focusing on C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin are needed to specifically examine their risk of osteoarthritis and other joint disease in smaller joints, including the metacarpophalangeal joints and other joints of the hand. Likewise, further studies are needed to better clarify the clinical and radiological features distinguishing osteoarthritis and other joint disease in C282Y homozygotes from non-carrier individuals, which has recently been described in the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification criteria for haemochromatosis arthropathy that may lay a foundation for such studies32.

Among the strengths of our study is the large general population cohort and the availability of comprehensive information on hospital contacts with osteoarthritis and use of pain-relieving medication from nationwide Danish registries, with no individuals lost to follow-up. Another strength is our consecutive genotyping of general population individuals who were not selected based on their health or hospital visits, which markedly decreases the risk of selection bias. Such selection bias may be a substantial limitation in hospital-based cohorts of haemochromatosis patients, where individuals are often specifically chosen for genotyping because they experience symptoms that give suspicion of complications from haemochromatosis.

Our study is limited by solely having information on hospital contacts due to osteoarthritis, as we did not have information on osteoarthritis cases that were handled exclusively by general practitioners, which may cause us to miss especially some of the less severe cases of osteoarthritis. However, these might have been captured in our analyses on the consumption of NSAIDs. Nevertheless, our reported cumulative incidences should be interpreted as conservative estimates, and the true cumulative incidence of osteoarthritis is likely even higher if individuals diagnosed and treated solely by general practitioners were included. The analysis on the use of NSAIDs is potentially limited by only including information on NSAIDs purchased using a prescription and not on NSAIDs bought without a prescription (over the counter). However, a nationwide validation study examining both prescription and non-prescription NSAID consumption in Denmark found that non-prescription NSAID consumption was much lower than prescription NSAID consumption, and that the Danish National Prescription Register was a valid data source for measuring NSAID consumption, as there was only negligible misclassification due to non-prescription use33. Since measurements of iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin were based on a single blood sample obtained at study enrolment and we do not have measurements of these biomarkers at end of follow-up or time of osteoarthritis diagnosis, we cannot exclude that the levels may have changed between time of blood sampling and time of osteoarthritis diagnosis. Nevertheless, when restricting the analysis to osteoarthritis diagnosed before study enrolment, risk estimates for any osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of plasma iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin were similar to those obtained for the full follow-up period. This indicates that gradual accumulation of iron after study enrolment is not a likely explanation for the increased risk of osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin. In a previous study based on the same general population cohorts as our current study, we examined change in plasma iron and transferrin saturation in C282Y homozygotes who had repeat blood samples obtained after a median of 10 years after study enrolment. Among 76 C282Y homozygotes with normal plasma iron at study enrolment, 88.2% (N = 67) persistently had normal plasma iron in the repeat blood sample after a median of 10 years, and among 24 C282Y homozygotes with normal transferrin saturation at study enrolment, 62.5% (N = 15) persistently had normal transferrin saturation13. However, as the median time between the first and repeat blood sample in our previous study13 was 10 years, which is substantially shorter than the length of follow-up in our current study, the analysis on repeat measurements from our previous study does not provide definitive conclusions regarding change in iron parameters over longer time periods. Since only 11 C282Y homozygotes were diagnosed with haemochromatosis before study enrolment, we had insufficient statistical power to study risk of osteoarthritis specifically in the C282Y homozygotes already diagnosed with haemochromatosis before study enrolment. However, when examining the overall group of C282Y homozygotes that had been diagnosed with haemochromatosis at any time before or after study enrolment (N = 92 individuals), we found a similar relative risk of any osteoarthritis as in the 330 C282Y homozygotes never diagnosed with haemochromatosis.

In conclusion, C282Y homozygotes had increased risk of any osteoarthritis and increased consumption of NSAIDs. Importantly and a novel finding, risk of osteoarthritis was increased even in C282Y homozygotes with normal or low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, or ferritin, meaning that risk was increased in C282Y homozygotes not usually recommended for genotyping according to current clinical guidelines. This is especially relevant as C282Y homozygotes with normal or low ferritin levels had a particularly high absolute risk of any osteoarthritis, with a cumulative incidence for any osteoarthritis of 24% at age 60 years and 60% at age 80 years.

Methods

We studied 132,525 individuals from the following three prospective cohort studies of Danish general population individuals: The Copenhagen City Heart Study (N = 9172 individuals examined from 1991–1994)14,34, the Copenhagen General Population Study (N = 103,296 individuals examined from 2003–2015)35,36, and the Danish General Suburban Population Study (N = 20,057 individuals examined from 2010-2013)37,38. This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The research was approved by Danish ethical committees: Specifically, the study procedures for individuals in the Copenhagen City Heart Study and the Copenhagen General Population Study were approved by the Scientific Ethics Committees for the Capital Region of Denmark (KF-01-144/01 and H-KF-01-144/01), while the study procedures for individuals in the Danish General Suburban Population Study were approved by the Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Region Zealand (SJ-114). All individuals gave written, informed consent. Participants did not receive financial compensation.

Study procedures for the three general population studies were highly similar, and no individuals were overlapping between the three studies. Individuals were aged 20-100 years at study enrolment, and more than 99% of individuals were white and of Danish descent. Among those invited to the Copenhagen City Heart Study, 61% of invited individuals participated, while corresponding numbers were 43% for the Copenhagen General Population Study, and 43% for the Danish General Suburban Population Study.

At day of study enrolment, individuals filled in a detailed questionnaire on health and lifestyle, a physical examination was performed, and blood samples were drawn13,14. For most individuals, additional blood samples are stored by the three general population studies (procedures for applying for access to blood samples are described in the Data Availability statement).

Covariates

Information on age, sex, and vital status until December 31, 2021, was obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System, which is 100% complete39. In the Danish Civil Registration System, assignment of sex is registered as the biological sex at birth, but it is possible for individuals later in life to change the assigned sex by self-reporting a different gender identity. In the subgroup analysis on risk of osteoarthritis in women stratified according to menopausal status, menopausal status was defined based on the questionnaire completed at study enrolment. For the baseline characteristics table (Table 1), body mass index was calculated based on measurements of height and weight from the physical examination at study enrolment.

Genotyping

All individuals were genotyped for the HFE C282Y (rs1800562) and H63D (rs1799945) variants using DNA extracted from blood samples obtained at study enrolment13,14,38. When testing for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for the C282Y variant using the chi-squared test, P values were 0.28 for the Copenhagen City Heart Study, 0.27 for the Copenhagen General Population Study, and 0.58 for the Danish General Suburban Population Study. Corresponding P values from the chi-squared test for the H63D variant were 0.35 for the Copenhagen City Heart Study, 0.03 for the Copenhagen General Population Study, and 0.68 for the Danish General Suburban Population Study. However, if adjusting for the three multiple comparisons (due to the three general population studies included) using the Bonferroni-method, none of the P values for testing Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium were significant at the P < 0.05 level (required P value < 0.017 = 0.05/3).

Measurements of plasma iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin

Plasma iron was measured in 130,930 individuals, transferrin saturation in 130,886 individuals, and ferritin in 33,389 individuals. All blood samples for measuring iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin were obtained at study enrolment with the exception of 4784 individuals from the Copenhagen City Heart Study (enrolled in the present study from 1991–1994) who had blood samples used for ferritin measurements obtained at an earlier examination of the Copenhagen City Heart Study in 1981–1983. Plasma iron and transferrin (used for calculation of transferrin saturation) were measured in the Copenhagen City Heart Study and the Copenhagen General Population Study using a Kone autoanalyzer, while an Advia Centaur was used for measuring ferritin. In the Danish General Suburban Population Study, the Cobas 6000 system was used for measuring iron, transferrin, and ferritin. Precision of the assays were monitored each day using internal control samples, and accuracy was monitored approximately monthly through an external quality control programme. To obtain a functional single-calibrator measurement, the raw measurements of iron, transferrin, and ferritin were normalised across calibrator lots and measurement platforms. More information on normalisation, sample handling, assays, quality control, and calibration has previously been described in detail13,14.

Disease endpoints

Information on all inpatient hospital admissions since 1977 and all emergency room and outpatient hospital visits since 1994 in Denmark is registered in the Danish National Patient Register, covering all Danish hospitals31. From this registry, we retrieved information on all hospital contacts due to osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis from January 1, 1977, to December 31, 2021, with osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis classified using the World Health Organisation’s International Statistical Classification of Diseases 8th revision and 10th revision (ICD-8 and ICD-10) codes listed in Supplementary Table S140,41. Diagnoses were classified using ICD-8 codes from January 1, 1977 until December 31, 1993, while diagnoses were classified using ICD-10 codes after January 1, 1994.

To examine whether C282Y homozygotes without other potential complications from haemochromatosis were at increased risk of osteoarthritis, we also retrieved information from the Danish National Patient Register on hospital contacts due to diabetes and/or liver disease from January 1, 1977, to December 31, 2021 (see Supplementary Table S1 for ICD-8 and ICD-10 codes defining diabetes and liver disease13).

National Danish guidelines mandate that treatment of hereditary haemochromatosis should exclusively be performed in public hospital departments42,43. To enable stratified analyses according to whether or not C282Y homozygotes had been diagnosed with hereditary haemochromatosis and thereby potentially treated with therapeutic phlebotomy, we retrieved information from the Danish National Patient Register on hospital contacts due to hereditary haemochromatosis from January 1, 1977, to December 31, 2021, with hereditary haemochromatosis defined using ICD-8 code 27329 and ICD-10 code E831A.

Joint replacement surgery

Information on joint replacement surgeries were retrieved from the nationwide Danish National Patient Register for the time period January 1, 1996, to December 31, 2021, with joint replacement surgeries classified according to the Nordic Classification of Surgical Procedures (NCSP) codes shown in Supplementary Table S2. As we focused on studying joint replacement surgery due to osteoarthritis or other chronic joint disease and not due to fractures, we ignored any joint replacement surgeries where the patient had been registered with a diagnosis of traumatic injury in the same anatomical region during the same hospitalisation or within 7 days prior to the date of joint surgery (ICD-10 codes for traumatic injuries are listed in Supplementary Table S2).

Pain-relieving medication

For each individual, we retrieved information on use of prescription pain-relieving medication for the time period January 1, 1995 to December 31, 2021 from the Danish National Prescription Register, which contain detailed individual-level information on all redeemed prescriptions in Denmark, covering all Danish pharmacies since 199544. We examined the use of two types of pain-relieving medication: systemic non-aspirin non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids. As done previously, NSAIDs were defined as any systemic non-aspirin NSAIDs using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification (ATC) code M01A, but excluding ATC code M01AX05 (glucosamine)45. Opioids were defined using ATC codes N02AA01-N02AX0646.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18 and all statistical tests were two-sided. Relative risk of osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and joint replacement surgeries were modelled separately using Cox proportional hazards regression with left-truncated age as the underlying timescale. In addition to adjusting for age by using age as timescale, all Cox models were further adjusted for sex and study cohort. We decided a priori before performing the analyses to only adjust for age, sex, and study cohort since HFE genotypes are randomly distributed at conception and therefore not influenced by lifestyle factors, comorbidities, etc. Likewise, we decided a priori that main analyses would only be sex-stratified in case our test for interaction between HFE genotype and sex on risk of osteoarthritis showed significant interaction at the P < 0.05 level, which was not the case as we found no formal interaction between sex and genotype (P for interaction with sex: 0.19)(Supplementary Fig. S5). However, based on reviewer comments, we added a new figure in the revision process, showing risk of any osteoarthritis in C282Y homozygotes and non-carriers with normal/low levels of iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin with further stratification by sex (Fig. 5).

When calculating P for difference between two relative risk estimates, we used the Z-test described by Altman and Bland47. For studying absolute risk of osteoarthritis, we estimated the cumulative incidence of osteoarthritis using competing risk models as described by Fine and Gray, taking the competing risk of death from any cause into account48. When studying risk of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, follow-up began at January 1, 1977 or age 20 years, whichever came last, and follow-up ended on first hospital contact with the disease being studied (osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis), emigration (N = 501), death from any cause, or December 31, 2021, whichever came first. When studying risk of joint replacement surgery, follow-up began at January 1, 1996 or age 20 years, whichever came last, and ended on date of joint surgery, emigration, death from any cause, or December 31, 2021, whichever came first.

When studying use of pain-relieving medication, follow-up began at January 1, 1995 or age 20 years, whichever came last and ended on emigration, death from any cause, or December 31, 2021, whichever came first. For each individual, use of NSAIDs and opioids in Defined Daily Doses (DDDs) per person-year were calculated for each category of medication as the total number of DDDs for which the individual had redeemed prescriptions, divided by the individuals’ length of follow-up. For each category of individuals with HFE variants (for example C282Y homozygotes), we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to calculate P for difference in use of NSAIDs and opioids when compared to individuals who were non-carriers for both C282Y and H63D (non-carrier/non-carrier).

Due to the limited number of endpoints included in the present study and since there is considerable evidence from prior publications regarding an overall increased risk of complications including osteoarthritis/arthropathy in C282Y homozygotes, we did not correct for multiple comparisons when analysing risk of osteoarthritis, joint replacement surgery, rheumatoid arthritis, or use of pain-relieving medication in C282Y homozygotes. However, based on reviewer comments, we implemented the strategy for multiple comparison correction for studying intermediate genotypes other than C282Y homozygosity that was previously described by Lucas et al.49. This was done by adding multiple comparison correction using the Bonferroni-method when analysing risk in individuals with intermediate HFE genotypes other than C282Y homozygosity (that is H63D heterozygotes, H63D homozygotes, C282Y heterozygotes, and C282Y/H63D compound heterozygotes), as the clinical importance of these genotypes are unclear. Notes regarding multiple comparison correction are therefore listed next to the relevant P values in the two figures (Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10) in which we found associations with endpoints showing uncorrected P values < 0.05 in individuals with the intermediate genotypes. When using the Bonferroni method to correct for multiple comparisons, the cut-off corresponding to significance at the P < 0.05 level was corrected to be P < 0.01 ( = 0.05/4) when adjusting for 4 multiple comparisons due to the 4 intermediate genotypes studied (H63D heterozygosity, H63D homozygosity, C282Y heterozygosity, and C282Y/H63D compound heterozygosity).

Information on age, sex, and study cohort was 100% complete for all individuals, meaning that there were no missing data for any of the main analyses on risk of osteoarthritis, risk of rheumatoid arthritis, risk of joint replacement surgery, or use of pain-relieving medication. For the subgroup analysis on risk of osteoarthritis in women stratified according to menopausal status, we had information on menopausal status for more than 99% of all women and excluded the remaining women with missing information regarding menopausal status ( < 1%). No individuals were lost to follow-up. We found no major violations of the proportional hazards assumption when examined by first visually plotting -ln(-ln(survival)) against ln(analysis time) and performing further testing based on Schoenfeld residuals in case the visual inspection gave suspicion of non-parallel curves.
