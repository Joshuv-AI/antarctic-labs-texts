Retinal astrocytic hamartoma with/without tuberous sclerosis: A comparative analysis by multimodal imaging
Authors: Dibya Prabha, Vijitha S Vempuluru, Swathi Kaliki, Vishal Raval
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To compare the clinical and imaging characteristics of isolated retinal astrocytic hamartoma (RAH) versus tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC)-associated RAH.Methods:This retrospective multicenter study included 38 patients diagnosed with RAH between 2000 and 2023. Clinical data and imaging findings were evaluated, including color fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), B-scan ultrasonography, and fundus autofluorescence. Statistical comparisons were performed between isolated and TSC-associated RAH.Results:The mean patient age was 26 years, with a slight male predominance (55%). Bilateral involvement was more common in TSC-associated RAH (55%). Overall, optical coherence tomography (OCT) of 18 eyes revealed optically empty cavities (44%), inner retinal disorganization (13%), outer retinal disorganization (8%), complete retinal disorganization (8%), and associated subretinal (SRF) and intraretinal fluid in 28% and 17% of eyes, respectively. Autofluorescence showed hyperautofluorescence in 86% and hypoautofluorescence in 14%, corresponding to calcified and noncalcified regions. Compared with isolated RAH, TSC-associated RAH demonstrated a higher prevalence of multifocal lesions (47% vs. 29%), superior quadrant involvement (86% vs. 40%), complete retinal disorganization (71% vs. 20%), and presence of SRF (100% vs. 0%).Conclusion:TSC-associated RAH exhibits distinct morphological and imaging features, including greater multifocality and superior quadrant involvement. TSC-associated RAH exhibits more extensive retinal disorganization, frequent loss of deeper retinal layer visibility, and association with SRF, which may serve as an important diagnostic marker for systemic screening. Recognizing these differences aids ophthalmologists when evaluating RAH and associated TSC.

Retinal astrocytic hamartoma (RAH) is a benign tumor originating from the glial cells of the retina.[1] Ulbright et al.[2] first described this condition in a case series of 42 patients presenting with isolated or multiple glial tumors involving the optic disc or peripheral retina. Most cases are asymptomatic and are often detected incidentally during routine eye examinations. Tuberous sclerosis (TSC) is a neurocutaneous syndrome in which multiple hamartomas involve the skin, heart, kidney, and other organs.[3] From an ophthalmology viewpoint, retinal hamartomas are considered one of the major diagnostic criteria and are recognized as a hallmark ocular feature of TSC.[45] However, RAH can also occur as an isolated entity or rarely in association with neurofibromatosis.[26] Differentiation from other retinal conditions, such as choroidal osteoma, retinoblastoma, chorioretinitis, and amelanotic melanoma, is crucial for accurate diagnosis. Although RAH generally remains stable over time, rare cases may show aggressive progression, leading to complications, such as retinal detachment and neovascular glaucoma.[78]

Clinically, RAH appears as a sessile or slightly elevated lesion within the retinal nerve fiber layer. It exhibits significant variability in presentation, ranging from unilateral to bilateral, solitary to multifocal, and transparent to opaque, with or without calcified areas within the lesions.[1] Based on its morphological appearance, RAH is categorized into three types[9]: Type 1, which is the most common type and is seen as a flat, smooth-surfaced, salmon-colored, semitransparent, and circular or oval-shaped lesion located in the superficial retina, most commonly near or at the posterior pole. Type 2 is a well-defined, elevated, opaque white lesion with a multinodular, calcified part in the posterior pole resembling a mulberry appearance. Type 3 shows features of a type 2-like appearance in the center of the lesion, surrounded by a type 1-like appearance. Based on the optical coherence tomography (OCT) features, Pichi et al.[10] recently classified RAH into four types: Type 1, a flat lesion with no evidence of retinal traction; Type 2, a slightly elevated lesion measuring up to 500 microns in height and displaying signs of retinal traction; Type 3, a significantly elevated retinal mass, exceeding 500 microns in height, with the presence of inner retinal calcification; and Type 4, significantly elevated retinal mass, also exceeding 500 microns in height, but distinguished by the presence of an optically empty cavity.

The clinical characteristics and various imaging features are essential for clinicians to enhance diagnostic accuracy, predict disease behavior, and tailor management strategies for patients with RAH. TSC-associated RAH may exhibit a broader spectrum of clinical variability, including multifocality than isolated RAH. Therefore, the role of an ophthalmologist is very critical as RAH may be the first manifestation of TSC.[11] Pichi et al.[10] studied the association between different OCT types of RAH and systemic features. They reported a strong association between type II lesions and cutaneous fibrous plaques, type III lesions and subependymal giant-cell astrocytomas (SEGA), and type IV lesions and pulmonary lymphangiomyomatosis.

This study aimed to explore the distinct ocular features, imaging patterns, and clinical outcomes of isolated RAH versus TS-associated RAH.

Methods

This retrospective, multicenter study was conducted at three tertiary eye centers of the LV Prasad Eye Institute, India, from 2000 to 2023. Thirty-eight patients were diagnosed with RAH during this period, and their data were retrieved. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Demographic data included age, gender, lesion laterality, and family history of ocular or systemic conditions. The ophthalmic evaluations included the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA in logMAR) and anterior and posterior segment examinations. Slit-lamp biomicroscopic documentation of corneal clarity, anterior chamber details, and lens status were reviewed. Posterior segment evaluation including the indirect ophthalmoscopic, slit-lamp biomicroscopic assessments was reviewed to note the tumor laterality, number of lesions, their color, location, morphology, and adjacent retinal and optic nerve involvement.

Imaging modalities, such as color fundus photography and fundus autofluorescene (FAF), were reviewed to gather information on the lesion’s morphology, including its size, color, margins, location, presence of calcification, and surface characteristics (e.g., granular or smooth). Ultra-widefield imaging was performed predominantly using the OPTOS system, while in selected cases, Zeiss Clarus imaging was also used for a detailed analysis of lesion distribution and associated retinal changes. ImageJ software was utilized to measure lesion size on fundus photographs using the disc size as a reference for calibration. Swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) (DRI-OCT Triton Swept-source; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized to obtain high-resolution cross-sectional images of the tumor and surrounding retina. The evaluation focused on tumor characteristics, such as optical reflectivity, presence of calcification, and posterior shadowing. There were anatomical variations within retinal structures, such as transition from the normal retina to tumor, status of retinal layer organization, and structural alterations, like disorganization within the inner, outer, or complete retinal layers, presence of optically empty cavities, hyperreflective foci within retinal layers or the vitreous, presence of subretinal fluid (SRF), intraretinal fluid, and vitreomacular/retinal traction. SS-OCT was performed at each visit and during the annual follow-up. Subsequent scans were performed at the exact topographical location to ensure precise qualitative and quantitative assessment of changes over time.

B-scan ultrasonography (Sonomed B 3000; Sonomed Technology Inc., Lake Success, New York, NY, USA) was used to confirm lesion calcifications, assess tumor dimensions, and rule out other posterior segment pathologies, such as choroidal osteoma or retinoblastoma. These imaging techniques evaluated the lesion’s location, laterality, and staging while identifying associated features like peripapillary involvement, optic disc pallor, and calcium deposits. The primary exclusion criterion was the unavailability or poor quality of imaging that precluded accurate morphologic or OCT-based assessment.

The differentiation of isolated RAH from TSC-associated RAH was achieved through detailed ophthalmic evaluation, systemic assessment, and advanced retinal imaging. Systemic correlates, including dermatological and neurological evaluations, were also assessed to identify features indicative of tuberous sclerosis complex. A thorough medical history was obtained from each patient to identify systemic or familial features indicative of TSC. This included a history of seizures, developmental delay, skin lesions (such as ash-leaf spots or facial angiofibromas), and other TSC-associated manifestations. The general examination included dermatological evaluation of TSC markers, such as ash-leaf spots, shagreen patches, and periungual fibromas. Neurological evaluation was also performed to detect signs of TSC-related brain involvement, such as seizures, behavioral abnormalities, and cognitive impairment. All patients with systemic or neurological abnormalities were referred to a neurologist for further evaluation.

Imaging modalities to rule out TSC included brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT), to identify hallmark features of TSC, such as cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, or subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs). In some cases, an abdominal ultrasound or CT scan was performed to detect renal angiomyolipoma or other TSC-related findings. Based on the presence/absence of family history, systemic examination, and ancillary imaging findings, which form the major/minor criteria, all patients were evaluated for confirmation of TSC.[411] For patients with no systemic or imaging evidence of TSC, a diagnosis of isolated RAH was made.

All data were included in our statistical analysis when available; analysis for any given variable only included patients for whom information was known. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and imaging findings. Continuous variables were described via mean, median, and range. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Two-tailed paired t-tests were used to compare tumor size, as measured during fundus photography. Snellen BCVA was converted to equivalent LogMAR for further analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, and all P values were 2-tailed. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM, SPSS statistics, Version 26; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software.

Results

Demography: A total of 38 patients (57 eyes) were included in the study, with a mean age of 26 years (range 6–72 years, SD, 16.7 years). There were 21 males (55%) and 17 females (45%). Bilateral involvement was observed in 20 (53%) patients. A family history of ocular or systemic conditions was noted in 5 (13%) patients. Of the 38 patients, 22 presented with ocular RAH (31 eyes) and 16 patients (26 eyes) with optic disc pallor secondary to CNS astrocytoma had a genetically confirmed diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis [Table 1]. The most common presenting complaint was diminished vision in 28 (74%) patients, followed by strabismus in 4 (10%), and pain or associated symptoms in 5 (13%) patients. Of the cases, 5 (13%) were initially referred for provisional diagnosis of retinoblastoma. Visual impairment levels varied, with 19 (33%) eyes experiencing mild impairment (<20/70), 18 eyes (32%) having a moderate impairment (>20/70 to 20/200), and 20 eyes (35%) presenting with profound visual impairment (>20/200 to 20/400). The causes of visual impairment in our cohort included optic atrophy secondary to CNS astrocytoma, coexisting retinitis pigmentosa, and lenticular opacities.

Morphological Features (31 eyes): Tumor color was yellow in 23 (74%), white in 7 (22%), and gray in 1 (3%) eye [Fig. 1]. The tumor size ranged from 0.137 to 9.748 mm, with a mean of 2.75 mm. The surface characteristics varied, with a granular surface observed in 13 (34%) eyes and a smooth surface in 3 (8%) eyes. The margins were irregular in 10 (26%) eyes and distinct in 5 (13%) eyes. The most common tumor location was the superior or superotemporal quadrant (n = 10, 26%), followed by the peripapillary and inferior regions (n = 5, 13%). Solitary lesions were observed in 14 (37%) eyes, whereas multiple lesions were noted in 8 (21%). Calcium specks were observed in 19 (50%) eyes, and associated disc pallor was observed in 7 (18%) eyes [Table 2].

Clinical features of retinal astrocytic hamartoma

Imaging Findings: OCT (18 eyes) revealed inner retinal disorganization in 7 (39%) eyes, outer retinal disorganization in 3 (17%) eyes, complete retinal involvement in 3 (17%), and complete retinal involvement with no view of deeper layers, accompanied by back shadowing in 5 (28%) eyes. Associated features, such as the presence of subretinal fluid (SRF), were observed in 5 (28%) eyes, and intraretinal fluid adjacent to the tumor in 3 eyes (17%). Optically empty cavities were seen in 8 eyes (44%). B-scan ultrasonography, performed in 5 eyes, confirmed the presence of calcifications and clearly delineated the lesion boundaries, whereas autofluorescence imaging performed in 22 eyes revealed hyperautofluorescent in 19 eyes (86%) and hypoautofluorescent patterns in 3 eyes (14%), corresponding to calcified and noncalcified regions, respectively [Table 3].

Multimodal imaging characteristics

Pichi OCT Classification: Using the Pichi et al.[10] staging system, Stage 1 lesions were identified in 33% of isolated RAH and 28% of TSC-associated RAH. Stage 2 lesions were present in 20% and 43% of the patients, Stage 3 in 20% and none, and Stage 4 in 7% and none, respectively.

Comparison of isolated RAH and TSC-associated RAH

In this study comparing isolated astrocytoma (n = 15) with TSC-associated RAH (n = 7), significant differences were observed in age, with the mean age of patients in the isolated RAH group being 32 ± 14 years compared to 18 ± 14 years in the TSC-associated RAH (P = 0.037). Laterality analysis showed that unilateral cases were more common in both groups (53% in the isolated group and 71% in the TSC group). Bilateral involvement was noted in 47% of isolated cases and 29% of TSC cases [Fig. 2].

The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) averaged 0.3 ± 0.3 in the isolated group and 0.4 ± 0.4 in the TSC group, with no significant difference (P = 0.56). In terms of BCVA classification, >20/200 vision was noted in 58.82% of isolated RAH cases compared with 57.14% in the TSC-associated RAH group, while <20/200 vision occurred in 33.33% and 42.85% of cases, respectively.

The mean lesion size was similar, measuring 2.9 ± 2.7 in isolated RAH and 2.7 ± 2.1 in the TSC-associated RAH group (P = 0.92). The most common lesion color in the RAH group was yellow (45%), white (20%), and gray (20%). Yellow lesions were most frequently observed in both groups, with a higher proportion in TSC-associated RAH (57%) compared to isolated RAH (45%). The most common location in the isolated RAH group was the peripapillary area (40%), followed by the inferior mid-periphery quadrant (34%). In the TSC-associated RAH group, the lesions were predominantly in the superior quadrant (86%), and rarely in the peripapillary area (14%).

Comparison of optical coherence tomography (OCT) features between isolated RAH and TSC-associated RAH revealed distinct patterns of retinal involvement. Based on the Pichi et al. staging system, isolated RAH exhibited a broader range of stages, with 33% of cases classified as Stage 1, 20% as Stage 2, 20% as Stage 3, and 7% as Stage 4. In contrast, TSC-associated RAH cases predominantly occurred in earlier stages, with 28% in Stage 1 and 43% in Stage 2, whereas Stages 3 and 4 were not observed. The retinal disorganization patterns also varied significantly between the two groups. In isolated RAH, inner retinal disorganization was observed in 33% of cases [Fig.3], outer retinal disorganization in 13%, and complete retinal involvement in 13%. In addition, 20% of the cases demonstrated full-thickness disorganization with shadowing and loss of deeper layer visibility. In comparison, 71% of TSC-associated RAH cases showed full-thickness disorganization with shadowing, indicating more severe structural changes. Notably, SRF was exclusively present in five eyes, all in the TSC-associated RAH group, distinguishing it from isolated RAH [Table 4].

Factors that correlated with TSC association:

Systemic findings strongly correlated with TSC included seizures (n = 15), ash-leaf macules (n = 5), a history of brain tumor resection (n = 15), and ventriculoperitoneal shunts (n = 4). Discussion

RAHs are benign, slow-growing tumors arising from the glial components of the retina and optic nerve head.[12] Although most cases remain asymptomatic, diagnosis of RAHs is critical because they can be an early manifestation of systemic conditions, such as tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC).[11] This study compared the morphological and imaging characteristics of isolated RAH with TSC-associated RAH to better understand their clinical distinctions, progression, and systemic correlations. Since TSC is a multisystem disorder with a variable phenotype, including neurologic and cutaneous manifestations, ophthalmologists play a key role in early detection and multidisciplinary referral.[12]

In our cohort of 38 patients, the mean age was 26 years, with a slight male predominance (55%), slightly younger than the mean age reported in other literature, 33 and 37 years old.[213] Bilateral involvement was observed in 55% of cases, which is higher than reported by a previous study.[1314] The lesion sizes ranged from 0.137 to 9.748 mm, averaging 2.75 mm. Our study found that RAH exhibited significant heterogeneity in lesion color, surface characteristics, and calcification patterns. The tumor morphology varied in different colors, including yellow (42%), white (34%), and gray (20%). These findings align with previous descriptions in which RAHs typically appear as slightly elevated, yellowish lesions that may calcify over time.[13] These results are consistent with descriptions by Shields et al.,[1516] who noted that RAHs can appear yellowish, white, or gray due to their glial origin and variable calcification.

The surface morphology of RAHs in our cohort varied between smooth and granular textures, with calcifications noted in 50% of cases, which is higher, as reported by Semenova et al.[13] Calcification is a well-recognized feature of RAH and is believed to develop progressively over time, serving as a marker of chronicity.[12] Our findings complement those of Shields et al.,[715] who reported that TSC-associated RAHs frequently exhibit more extensive calcification, likely attributable to prolonged astrocytic proliferation and chronic glial degeneration.

In terms of lesion distribution, most lesions were found in the superior quadrant (54%), followed by the peripapillary (16%) area. This anatomical distinction is clinically relevant as previous studies have reported that peripheral astrocytomas did not exhibit growth during follow-up, whereas those located near the optic disc did.[7]

OCT imaging provided critical insights into the structural alterations associated with RAH. In our study, OCT imaging revealed inner retinal disorganization in 39% of eyes, outer retinal disorganization in 17%, and complete retinal involvement in 17%. Additionally, as documented in previous studies, 28% of patients exhibited full-thickness retinal disorganization with shadowing.[10131617]

Additional imaging modalities, such as FAF, are valuable for delineating tumor borders. Approximately half of the tumors displayed well-defined borders, which is consistent with findings in the literature.[5] Cyst-like opacities observed on SDOCT appeared hyperfluorescent on FAF, with those located more superficially showing even brighter autofluorescence. This correlation is associated with the presence of lipofuscin in degenerating tissue and the contribution of other fluorophores.[14]

While comparing isolated RAH with TSC-associated RAH, we found that yellow-colored lesions were the most frequently observed in TSC-associated RAH (57% vs. 45%), followed by white and gray lesions. This finding corroborates the findings of Shields et al.,[7] who reported that RAHs in TSCs tend to be more calcified (yellow), likely due to prolonged astrocytic proliferation and glial tissue fibrosis. In our cohort, solitary lesions were more prevalent in isolated RAH, whereas TSC-associated RAH exhibited a higher tendency for multifocality but bilateral involvement more noted in isolated RAH (47% vs. 29%). These results are comparable to those of previous studies in the literature.[121518] Regarding location, peripapillary lesions were more common in isolated RAH (40%), whereas superior quadrant involvement was predominant in TSC-associated RAH (86%). In our study, retinal disorganization of the inner retina was noted in 33% of isolated cases, outer retinal disorganization in 13%, complete retinal involvement in 13%, and complete involvement with no visibility of deeper layers in 20%. Among patients in the TSC group, 71% exhibited full retinal disorganization with no deeper layer visibility. These findings highlight that extensive retinal disruption is more characteristic of TSC-associated RAH, a trend reported by Pichi et al.,[10] who proposed a four-stage OCT classification system. Our study findings reinforce this classification as most Stage 3 and 4 lesions were found in isolated cases, whereas TSC-associated RAH was more likely to present with diffuse retinal involvement.

Despite providing valuable insights, our study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, the study’s retrospective design may introduce selection bias as only patients with available multimodal imaging were included. Third, genetic studies to confirm TSC were not conducted in all patients, highlighting that some cases classified as isolated RAH may have undiagnosed TSC. Future studies with larger cohorts and genetic analyses are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed comparison between isolated RAH and TSC-associated RAH, highlighting key differences in morphology, imaging characteristics, and systemic associations. TSC-associated RAH was found to occur at a younger age, exhibit more diffuse retinal disorganization, frequently lose deeper layer visibility, have a unique association with SRF on OCT, and strongly correlate with neurological comorbidities. Peripapillary lesions were more common in isolated RAH, whereas superior quadrant involvement was predominant in TSC-associated RAHs. Multimodal imaging is crucial in differentiating these entities, with OCT and fundus autofluorescence being particularly valuable. Given the significant overlap between ocular and systemic manifestations, patients with extensive retinal disorganization should be evaluated for underlying TSC. Further, longitudinal studies are needed to assess lesion progression and its impact on visual prognosis.
