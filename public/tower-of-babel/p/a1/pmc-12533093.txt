An investigation on amyloid beta plaque dissociation in U87MG cells of in-vitro digested pomegranate peel
Authors: Ünkan Urganci, Fatma Isik, Sevki Arslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the bioactivity of pomegranate peel, particularly after simulated gastrointestinal digestion, in an in-vitro Alzheimer’s disease model. Human glioblastoma U87MG cells exposed to Aβ1−42 were used to assess the effects of both digested and non-digested pomegranate peel on Aβ aggregation and inflammatory responses. In-vitro digestion simulated oral, gastric, and intestinal phases, and polyphenol profiles were analyzed before and after digestion. Cytotoxicity was examined using MTT assays, Aβ disaggregation by ELISA, and fibril morphology by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM). Anti-inflammatory activity was evaluated via qRT-PCR in LPS-stimulated Raw264.7 macrophages, focusing on iNOS, IL-6, TNF-α, NF-κB, COX-2, and IL-10 expression. Despite partial degradation during digestion, both forms of peel retained bioactivity. At non-toxic concentrations, they supported cell viability and reduced Aβ aggregation (57.21% for non-digested, 42.11% for digested peel). FESEM confirmed fibril disruption, and both forms downregulated pro-inflammatory genes while enhancing IL-10 expression. These findings suggest that pomegranate peel, even after digestion, preserves anti-amyloidogenic and immunomodulatory activities in-vitro, supporting its potential as a dietary ingredient that merits further investigation in the context of Alzheimer’s disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20215-2.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive and irreversible neurodegenerative disorder, primarily affecting the hippocampus and cortex, and is the leading cause of dementia in the elderly population1. AD is characterized by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau protein, leading to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. Currently, over 40 million individuals worldwide are affected, with projections indicating this number will exceed 100 million by 20502. AD presents with symptoms such as memory loss, impaired decision-making, and disrupted spatial awareness. The disease is classified into early-onset (familial) and late-onset (sporadic) types. Familial AD, which accounts for 2–10% of cases, is linked to autosomal-dominant mutations in the presenilin 1 (PS1), presenilin 2 (PS2), and amyloid precursor protein (APP) genes3,4. These mutations enhance γ-secretase activity, increasing Aβ1−42 production. Environmental and metabolic factors such as diet, inflammation, insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, and obesity also contribute to AD risk5,6. Although numerous hypotheses—such as the amyloid, tau, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance pathways—have been proposed, the exact mechanisms underlying AD pathogenesis remain unclear. The amyloid hypothesis is particularly prominent, suggesting that Aβ toxicity contributes to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and synaptic impairment7–9. These complexities underscore the need for new preventive and therapeutic strategies that can target multiple pathological processes simultaneously10,11.

Polyphenols, secondary metabolites abundantly found in fruits, vegetables, seeds, and oils, have garnered attention for their potential neuroprotective effects. With over 10,000 identified compounds, polyphenols are structurally diverse and classified into flavonoids, phenolic acids, stilbenes, curcuminoids, lignans, and tannins12,13. These compounds exhibit potent antioxidant properties, scavenging reactive oxygen species and chelating metals, thereby mitigating oxidative damage14. Studies suggest that polyphenols may inhibit Aβ aggregation, prevent its production, and disrupt the oxidative stress cycle associated with Aβ toxicity14,15. Compounds such as quercetin and myricetin inhibit β-secretase activity, while epicatechin, resveratrol, and curcumin form non-covalent complexes with Aβ, reducing its aggregation and toxicity16. Additionally, polyphenols have shown efficacy in attenuating neuroinflammation and Aβ toxicity in in-vivo and in-vitro models17,18.

Among natural sources, pomegranate is particularly notable for its high polyphenol content, including hydrolyzable tannins (e.g., punicalagin and ellagic acid) and anthocyanins, which have been reported to exert antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties18,19. For example, ellagic acid has demonstrated the ability to reduce Aβ plaque formation and neuronal apoptosis, improving cognitive function in transgenic models18,20. However, the stability and bioavailability of these polyphenols remain critical challenges, as they undergo substantial transformation during digestion. Addressing this gap is essential to understand whether the bioactive potential observed in purified compounds or crude extracts is preserved under physiological conditions. Most previous studies have focused on solvent-based extracts of pomegranate peel as mentioned above, which are valuable for mechanistic investigations. In this study, we instead used dried peel to preserve the natural food matrix, as this may affect the stability and release of polyphenols during digestion. This approach complements extract-based work by providing a more dietary-relevant perspective, particularly since pomegranate peel is already incorporated into foods and supplements, supporting its feasibility for human consumption21,22.

Here, we aimed to evaluate the stability of pomegranate peel polyphenols following in-vitro gastrointestinal digestion and to assess their biological activity in an in-vitro AD model using human glioblastoma U87MG cells exposed to Aβ1−42. By integrating digestion simulations with cell-based assays, this work builds on previous in-vivo and in-vitro findings and aims to provide additional insight into whether dietary pomegranate peel polyphenols might contribute to strategies relevant for AD.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pomegranates (Punica granatum L. cv.), locally known as Hicaz nar, were cultivated and collected from Kocadere Village in Denizli/Türkiye (37°50′32.96″ N 29°11′45.95″ E) in October 2022. The plants were botanically identified by Prof. Dr. Gürkan Semiz deposited a voucher specimen (GSE 2530) in Pamukkale University Herbarium, Denizli/Türkiye. The pomegranates were then peeled, cut into 3 × 3 cm pieces, and dried in a cabinet dryer (Yucebas Machine Analytical Equipment Industry, Izmir, Türkiye) at 50 °C until reaching < 10% moisture content. Heat-drying was selected as it is a commercially feasible method for large-scale collection and distribution, reflecting industrial food-processing practices. Dried peels were kept at −18 °C until use.

Gastrointestinal digestion simulation

In this study, an in-vitro gastrointestinal digestion system was performed based on an international consensus protocol developed by23 with little modifications on quantities. In the oral phase, 5 g of dried pomegranate peel was mechanically ground in a blender with 3.5 mL of SSF (simulated salivary fluid) electrolyte stock solution to simulate chewing. 0.5 mL of salivary α-amylase (1500 U/mL) was added to the SSF solution. Subsequently, 25 µL of 0.3 M CaCl₂ and 975 µL of water were incorporated into the mixture. In the gastric phase, 10 mL of the oral bolus was mixed with 7.5 mL of SGF (simulated gastric fluid) stock solution. To this mixture, 1.6 mL of porcine pepsin stock solution (25,000 U/mL), 5 µL of 0.3 M CaCl₂, 0.2 mL of 1 M HCl (to adjust pH to 3.0), and 0.695 µL of water were added and mixed. In the intestinal phase, 20 mL of the gastric bolus was mixed with 11 mL of SIF (simulated intestinal fluid) electrolyte stock solution. Then 5.0 mL of pancreatic solution (800 U/mL), 2.5 mL of bile salt, 40 µL of 0.3 M CaCl₂, 0.15 mL of 1 M NaOH (to adjust pH to 7), and 1.31 mL of water were added. The reactions were terminated at the end of the process with 8 mL 4-(2-Aminoethyl) benzenesulfonyl fluoride hydrochloride (AEBSF) inhibitor. The compositions of SSF, SGF and SIF were given in Supplementary Table S1. The digested samples were freeze dried then kept at −18 °C until use.

Determination of polyphenol profile

Following the drying of pomegranate peel, the identification and quantification of phenolic compounds contained in the peel were conducted both before and after in-vitro digestion. The method proposed by24 was employed for this analysis. The extract (8 mg/mL in 80% methanol) was filtered (0.45 μm). Quantitative and qualitative analyses of 14 target polyphenolic compounds were performed using a Shimadzu high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), equipped with an LC-20AT binary pump and an SPD-M20A diode array detector (DAD), all controlled via the LC-Solution software suite (CBM-20 A system controller). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Inertsil ODS-3 analytical column (4 μm, 4.0 mm × 150 mm) accompanied by a matching guard column of the same stationary phase. The column compartment was maintained at 35 °C throughout the runs. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% acetic acid in ultrapure water) and solvent B (0.1% acetic acid in methanol), applied through a gradient elution program as follows: 2% B (0–3 min), 2–5% B (3–6 min), 5–6% B (6–8 min), 6–10% B (8–12 min), 10% B (12–13 min), 10–25% B (13–18 min), 25–30% B (18–25 min), 30–40% B (25–30 min), 40–42% B (30–36 min), 42–54% B (36–41 min), 54–55% B (41–42 min), 55–56% B (42–52 min), 56–65% B (52–56 min), 65–75% B (56–59 min), 75–85% B (59–61 min), 85–95% B (61–66 min), 95% B (66–68 min), 95–100% B (68–69 min), 100% B (69–74 min), 100 − 80% B (74–76 min), 80 − 50% B (76–78 min), and 50 − 2% B (78–81 min). The flow rate was maintained at 1.0 mL/min and the injection volume was set to 20 µL.

Spectral detection was conducted using a DAD within the range of 200–600 nm to enable multi-wavelength monitoring of analytes. Prior to analysis, all samples and calibration standards were filtered through 0.45 μm PTFE syringe filters (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to remove particulate matter and ensure baseline stability. The identification of phenolic compounds was conducted using the DAD, correlating with the UV data of each commercial standard reads; pyrocatechol (≥ 99.0%), punicalagin (%98.0), 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (≥ 99.0%), 4-hydroxy benzaldehyde (≥ 98.0%), vanillic acid (≥ 97.0%), epicatechin (≥ 90%), ferulic acid (≥ 99.0%), cynarine (≥ 98.0%), propyl gallate (≥ 98.0%), hesperidin (≥ 80.0%), rutin (≥ 94.0%), trans-2-hydroxy cinnamic acid (97.0%) ellagic acid (≥ 99.0%), trans-cinnamic acid (≥ 99.0%), and retention times reads; 24.66, 28.67, 30.87, 33.37, 34.76, 35.28, 42.56, 44.50, 46.98, 47.38, 47.53, 48.24, 50.01, 56.20, all of which were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich-GmbH (Sternheim, Germany). Each analytical measurement was conducted in triplicate, and the identified phenolic compounds were characterized based on their retention times. The resulting data were quantified and expressed in µg/g of dry weight of the extract. The linearity, accuracy, and precision, along with the limit of detection (LOD), the limit of quantitation (LOQ), and effectiveness were considered to validate the reproducibility of the developed method. Cell culture and evaluation of cytotoxicity

U87MG human glioblastoma cell line (ATCC-HTB-14) was cultured in DMEM medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) with 10% FBS, 1% of L-glutamine, 50 µg/mL streptomycin, and 50 IU/mL penicillin into 100 mm flask at 5% CO2 incubator. The effects of both non-digested and digested pomegranate peel extracts on the viability of U87MG cell line was assessed by performing MTT assay25. The cytotoxic effect of Aβ1−42, as well as the simultaneous effect of Aβ1−42 and digested pomegranate peel were also determined at 24 h and 48 h time period. 4000 cells per well were sown to 96-well-plate. After 24 h of incubation increasing concentrations of samples (for pomegranate peel samples 1000 µg/mL − 15.625 µg/mL and for Aβ1−42 10 µM and 20 µM) were applied 1% into the wells. Following the 24 h of incubation, medium was disposed, 10 µL of MTT solution was applied to stain attached cells and incubated for another 3 h. The resulting Formazan crystals were dissolved in 10 µL DMSO, and the absorbance was measured at 590 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch, BioTek, USA). The percentage of living cells was calculated, and concentrations of samples applied that led to 80% viability of U87MG cells were accepted as non-toxic concentrations.

Aβ1−42 disaggregation efficiency of pomegranate peel consumption

U87MG cells were seeded in a 96-well, after 24 h, cells were incubated with Aβ1−42 for 24 h. Afterwards, cells were treated with non-toxic concentrations of PP and DPP for another 24 h. The media were removed, and cells were lysed in PBS. The concentration of aggregated Aβ1−42 was determined using sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Elabscience, Cat# E-EL-H0543), based on antigen-antibody reactions with colorimetric detection. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, lyophilized standards were reconstituted in 1 mL of standard dilution buffer to prepare a 5.7 ng/mL stock solution, which was allowed to dissolve for 10 min. A series of seven two-fold serial dilutions (2.9, 1.4, 0.7, 0.36, 0.18, and 0.09 ng/mL) were prepared. Experimental samples were diluted 1:500 in PBS and subsequently 1:2 in assay diluent before use. A total of 100 µL of standards, blank, and diluted samples were added in duplicate to the coated 96-wells and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After four washes with 350 µL of wash buffer, 100 µL of biotin-conjugated anti-aggregated Aβ antibody was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Wells were washed again four times, followed by the addition of 100 µL of streptavidin-HRP conjugate and incubation for 30 min at room temperature. After another four washes, 100 µL of chromogenic substrate was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 µL of stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader within 15 min. The results were compared to the control cells which were not treated with pomegranate peel.

Microscopic imaging of U87MG cells and Aβ1−42 formation

In field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), electrons emitted from the electron source are directed onto the sample through lenses under a vacuum. As the electron beam interacts with atoms on the sample surface, various types of electrons are generated and detected by the detector, producing an image of the sample surface that is displayed on the monitor26. The levels of Aβ1−42 fibril and plaque formation after incubating with Aβ1−42 and Aβ1−42 disaggregation efficiency of non-digested and digested pomegranate peel were visualized using FESEM. Approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells were seeded into 6-well plates containing sterile coverglasses and incubated with Aβ1−42, PP or DPP. Following treatment, the coverglasses were carefully removed, washed with PBS, and fixed with 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 30 min. The samples were then subjected to a graded ethanol dehydration series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) and subsequently rinsed with hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) for 5 min27. After complete air-drying, the specimens were sputter-coated with gold using a high-vacuum coater (Q150R ES, Quorum Technologies Ltd., UK) for 120 s to ensure conductivity. Imaging was performed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; Zeiss Supra 40 VP, Carl Zeiss GmbH, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Gene expression analysis

The expression levels of genes associated with inflammation in Alzheimer’s disease were quantified at the mRNA level using the Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) method28. Total RNA isolation was performed using an RNA kit following the manufacturer’s recommended procedure. Raw264.7 murine macrophages were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS at 5% CO2 incubator. To induce inflammation, cells were treated with 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 1 h, followed by the application of non-toxic dose of DPP for 24 h. RNA samples were extracted from approximately 1 × 10⁶ Raw264.7 cells, both treated and untreated with in-vitro digested pomegranate peel, and stored at −80 °C. The quality of the isolated RNA was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis by examining the integrity of the 18 S and 28 S bands. The RNA quantity was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer at 260/280 nm, ensuring that 5 µg of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis. cDNA synthesis was carried out from the isolated RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The synthesized cDNA was stored at −20 °C for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis. To determine the impact of the above-mentioned proteins on gene expression levels, appropriate primer sequences for selected genes were identified by searching GenBank/EMBL databases. To this end, TNF-alpha (NM_000594), NF-kappa-B (NM_002502), iNOS (NM_010927), COX-2 (NM_011198), IL-6 (NM_031168), IL-10 (NM_010548) genes were investigated. The primer sequences for target genes analyzed in this study were listed in Supplementary Table S2. The RT-qPCR process was performed using a kit and adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The results were given as means ± SD of at least three replicates. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software package (https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/) was used for statistical analyses. The statistical differences between the groups were analysed by one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey’s test. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Polyphenol profile changes during in-vitro digestion

HPLC analysis of both non-digested and in-vitro digested pomegranate peel confirmed the presence of punicalagin, ellagic acid, epicatechin, 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, and rutin (Fig. 1). Following in-vitro digestion, the concentrations of the major polyphenolic compounds were reduced: punicalagin decreased by 91.83%, ellagic acid by 66.22%, epicatechin by 74.02%, and rutin by 72.22%. Although 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde was initially detected at a very low concentration, its level increased after digestion. Pyrocatechol, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillic acid, ferulic acid, cynarine, propyl gallate, hesperidin, trans-2-hydroxy cinnamic acid, and trans-cinnamic acid were not detected in either PP or DPP samples.

Cytotoxic effects of Aβ1‑42 on U87MG cells

The effects of 10 µM and 20 µM Aβ1−42 applied at 24, 48 and 72 h on the viability of U87MG cells were examined by MTT analysis. U87MG cells without Aβ1−42 application were used as the control group (Fig. 2.). With 10 µM Aβ1−42 application, 88% cell viability was observed at 24 h, 29% at 48 h and 9% at 72 h. With 20 µM Aβ1−42 application, 80% cell viability was observed at 24 h, 24% at 48 h and 8% at 72 h. It was determined that 20 µM Aβ1−42 and over 24 h of application duration created a very significant toxicity on U87MG cells. In the light of MTT results, it was found that the application time and Aβ1−42 dose that caused approximately 85–90% cytotoxicity in U87MG cells were 24 h and 10 µM.

Cytotoxic effects of pomegranate peel application on U87MG cells

To identify doses that maintained cell viability at or above 80%, cytotoxicity analyses were performed across a concentration range for both non-digested and digested pomegranate peel. The results indicated that concentrations up to 250 µg/mL preserved ≥ 80% viability after 24 h (Fig. 4). Based on these data, the 24 h application and polyphenol doses corresponding to ~ 80% viability were selected for subsequent experiments.

Cytotoxic effects of Aβ1‑42 and pomegranate peel application on U87MG cells

The synergistic effects of selected doses of both non-digested and digested pomegranate peel and Aβ1−42 application to U87MG cells on cell viability were re-evaluated by MTT analysis. As a result of co-application, hours in which viability did not decrease below 50% were evaluated29. In co-application experiments with Aβ1−42, lower viability values were considered relevant, since Aβ1−42 itself is highly cytotoxic and reduces viability to < 30% after 48 h. Thus, testing PP and DPP under these stringent conditions allowed us to evaluate their protective potential rather than absolute non-toxicity. It was determined that 250 µg/ml PP + 10 µM Aβ1−42 combination showed 70.94% after 24 h and decreased to 40.06% after 48 h; while 250 µg/ml DPP + 10 µM Aβ1−42 combination showed 79.85% after 24 h and decreased to 47.31% after 48 h on cell viability of U87MG cells (Fig. 5.). Results showed that 48 h application of both pomegranate peel samples significantly decreased cell viability. Therefore, it was decided to examine the Aβ1−42 plaque dissolution activities of pomegranate peels after 24th hour only.

Aβ1‑42 disaggregation effects of pomegranate peel consumption on Alzheimer’s disease model of U87MG cells

ELISA analysis showed that both non-digested (PP) and digested pomegranate peel (DPP) significantly reduced aggregated Aβ1−42 levels in U87MG cells (p < 0.05). Aβ1−42 concentration in control cells was 2185 ng/mL, which decreased to 935 ng/mL with PP and 1265 ng/mL with DPP. This corresponded to disaggregation capacities of 57.21% for PP and 42.11% for DPP (Fig. 6).

Verification of Aβ1‑42 disaggregation effects of pomegranate peel consumption

In the U87MG AD model, treatment with 250 µg/ml of both PP and DPP (Fig. 7.) was observed to disaggregate Aβ1−42 plaques by transforming them into fibril-like structures resembling their pre-aggregation state, and further fragmenting the plaques into smaller components. These morphological changes visually confirmed the ELISA test results, which indicated a decrease in the concentration of Aβ1−42 plaques.

Discussion

In this study, simulated gastrointestinal digestion caused a marked reduction in the major polyphenols of pomegranate peel. The observed reductions in punicalagin, ellagic acid, epicatechin, and rutin are consistent with previous studies reporting decreases in major polyphenols after in-vitro digestion of pomegranate and its derivatives. This decline has been attributed to pH conditions and interactions with other dietary constituents in the digestive system30–32. The increase in 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde may reflect degradation or conversion of other polyphenolic constituents, a phenomenon also described in earlier work33. The absence of several polyphenols, despite their natural presence in pomegranate peel, has likewise been observed in previous research, likely due to degradation during powdering or removal from the peel matrix32.

In our model, exposure to Aβ1−42 induced cytotoxicity in U87MG cells, broadly consistent with earlier studies, although some differences in magnitude were noted. Earlier studies reported 60–70% viability after 24 h exposure to 10–20 µM Aβ1−4234,35, whereas in our study viability was slightly higher, reaching ~ 80–88% survival at 24 h. Such discrepancies may be attributed to factors such as passage number, robustness of the U87MG cell line, or variations in the brand and source of Aβ1−42 used. These findings highlight the importance of experimental conditions in determining the extent of Aβ-induced cytotoxicity, which should be carefully considered when comparing across studies.

FESEM images revealed plaque formation after 24 h, corresponding to the terminal stage of Aβ aggregation. However, literature reports vary regarding the timing of plaque development. Some studies describe early fibril formation followed by plaque accumulation from 24 h onwards29, whereas others have observed plaques only after extended incubation periods36. Such variability may reflect differences in experimental design, including the cell line type and passage number, as well as the concentration and source of Aβ1−42.

Treatment with both digested and non-digested pomegranate peel preserved U87MG cell viability even at relatively high concentrations, indicating low cytotoxicity in this in-vitro model and supporting their use in subsequent mechanistic experiments. This is consistent with earlier research showing that polyphenol-rich food extracts generally exhibit low cytotoxicity35,36. While pharmacological treatments remain essential for AD management, dietary polyphenols may represent a complementary approach with potentially fewer adverse effects. At the same time, reports of cytotoxicity from isolated polyphenols such as epigallocatechin gallate and ellagic acid at high concentrations37,38 highlight the importance of dose and matrix context. Our results suggest that whole-food extracts may provide a complementary approach, where the complex matrix could mitigate potential cytotoxic effects. Together, these results suggest that pomegranate peel may be applied at biologically relevant doses in-vitro without compromising cell viability, supporting their suitability for further preclinical studies.

Co-treatment with PP or DPP maintained ~ 70–80% viability in the presence of Aβ1−42 at 24 h, suggesting a potential short-term protective effect against Aβ-induced cytotoxicity. However, the marked decline in viability by 48 h indicates that this protective effect is time-limited, which is consistent with the progressive nature of Aβ toxicity reported in earlier studies29. These results highlight that short-term co-treatment may provide a window of cellular resilience, but longer incubation times overwhelm this effect. This finding supports the decision to conduct plaque dissolution assays at 24 h, as this time point allowed clearer differentiation between treatments.

The ability of PP and DPP to disaggregate Aβ1−42, despite substantial degradation of polyphenols during digestion, suggests that some biological activity is preserved. This observation is consistent with earlier studies reporting anti-amyloidogenic effects of pomegranate peel polyphenols, particularly punicalagin and ellagic acid39,40. Our findings extend this literature by showing that digested peels also preserve activity, suggesting that dietary polyphenols from pomegranate peel may remain effective under physiological conditions.

The visual evidence by FESEM of plaque disruption by PP and DPP supports the biochemical findings and supports the possibility of anti-amyloidogenic activity of pomegranate peel. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting that pomegranate-derived polyphenols, including punicalagin, ellagic acid, and epicatechin, can interact with Aβ peptides via aromatic and hydrophobic interactions, thereby inhibiting fibril formation and promoting disaggregation14,18,41.

Gene expression profiling demonstrated significant down-regulation of multiple pro-inflammatory markers, together with up-regulation of IL-10, indicating a pronounced anti-inflammatory effect of both PP and DPP. Such modulation of inflammation-associated genes correlates with the reduction of neuroinflammation commonly observed in AD pathology17,42,43. These findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating the anti-inflammatory gene regulatory properties of pomegranate peel in both in-vivo and in-vitro models17,44–46. Importantly, the preservation of this activity even after in-vitro digestion suggests that dietary pomegranate peel may retain functional efficacy in modulating inflammatory pathways under physiological conditions.

It should be emphasized that the present findings are limited to in-vitro digestion and cell culture models. While our analysis of polyphenol profile changes after simulated digestion provides useful insights into potential bioavailability, this approach cannot capture the full complexity of in-vivo absorption, metabolism, and blood-brain barrier transport. Although several animal studies have reported neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of pomegranate peel polyphenols, further investigations, particularly clinical trials, are needed to confirm whether these effects translate to humans and to assess their potential relevance for AD prevention or therapy.

Conclusions

This study indicates that pomegranate peel, even after simulated gastrointestinal digestion, retains measurable biological activities relevant to AD pathology, including reduced Aβ1−42 aggregation and modulation of inflammatory gene expression in cell line model. While polyphenols contribute to these effects, they may also be influenced by the broader food matrix of the peel, which affects stability, release, and bioactivity. The findings suggest that peel, as a sustainable and widely available by-product of pomegranate processing, could be explored as an ingredient in functional foods or dietary supplements. However, these results are limited to in-vitro digestion and cell line model; animal and clinical studies remain necessary to confirm bioavailability, brain accessibility, and long-term efficacy. Within these boundaries, the work contributes to growing evidence that food-derived components such as pomegranate peel may provide complementary, sustainable approaches to modulating AD-related processes.
