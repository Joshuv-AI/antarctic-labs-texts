Development of an injectable thermosensitive hydrogel depot system for controlled and sustained subcutaneous drug delivery
Authors: Quoc Lam Vu, I-Hui Chiu, Le Thuy Do, Thi Phuong-Quynh Nguyen, Thi Hai Nguyen, Dang Van Thanh, Muhammad Suhail, Pao-Chu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Arthritis conditions are widely treated with oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, yet long-term therapy is often limited by gastrointestinal toxicity and variable systemic exposure. To overcome these issues, an injectable thermosensitive chitosan/β-cyclodextrin-graft-poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) hydrogel was developed as a subcutaneous depot for sustained diclofenac sodium delivery. The hydrogel was synthesized via free-radical polymerization using glutaraldehyde as a crosslinker. FTIR and 1H NMR confirmed network formation and drug incorporation, while DSC, TGA, and DTG analyses demonstrated enhanced thermal stability. SEM revealed a porous, interconnected structure, and EDX mapping verified uniform drug distribution. Rheological and swelling studies indicated shear-thinning behavior, good injectability at room temperature, rapid sol–gel transition at 37 °C, and reversible temperature-responsive swelling. In vitro release, with profiles best fitted by the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, indicating a non-Fickian diffusion–relaxation mechanism. Enzymatic degradation confirmed sufficient structural stability to support drug release for more than 10 days. HET-CAM and hemolysis assays demonstrated good biocompatibility. In vivo studies showed reduced peak plasma concentration, prolonged Tmax, mean residence time, and half-life, with an approximately three-fold increase in relative bioavailability compared with the control, supporting depot-like sustained release. Overall, this thermosensitive hydrogel represents a promising platform for controlled and sustained subcutaneous drug delivery.

Introduction

Arthritis conditions (AC), including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, are among the leading causes of chronic pain, functional limitation, and loss of independence worldwide. Global Burden of Disease analyses have shown a steady rise in both the prevalence and disability burden of osteoarthritis over the last three decades, emphasizing the increasing clinical and socioeconomic impact of these disorders (Ma et al., 2025). Pathophysiologically, arthritic joints are characterized by a persistent inflammatory microenvironment involving synovial activation, cartilage matrix degradation, and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1β, and interleukin-6 promote cyclooxygenase-driven prostaglandin synthesis, which amplifies pain and inflammation, while oxidative stress and extracellular matrix-degrading enzymes further accelerate structural joint damage (Ansari et al., 2020). These intertwined processes result in chronic pain, stiffness, and progressive loss of mobility, making long-lasting control of inflammation and pain a central therapeutic goal in AC management.

Diclofenac sodium (DS), a widely used nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), exerts its therapeutic effect primarily through inhibition of COX enzymes and the subsequent reduction of prostaglandin synthesis (Altman et al., 2015). However, conventional oral DS therapy is associated with several pharmacokinetic and safety limitations. Following oral administration, DS undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism, resulting in moderate systemic bioavailability and a short elimination half-life of approximately 1–2 h, which necessitates repeated dosing to maintain therapeutic efficacy (Willis et al., 1979; Yuan et al., 2017). Injectable (intra-articular or periarticular) and topical formulations have been developed to overcome some of these limitations; however, conventional parenteral products mainly offer short-term relief, and topical preparations often fail to reach sufficient concentrations in deeper joint tissues, while systemic exposure still carries a risk of gastrointestinal and cardiovascular adverse events, including ulceration, bleeding, myocardial infarction, and stroke during chronic therapy (Kolodziejska and Kolodziejczyk, 2018; Small, 1989). These limitations highlight the need for alternative drug delivery systems capable of providing sustained and controlled DS release while minimizing systemic exposure.

Overall, subcutaneous depot administration represents an attractive alternative for chronic AC because it is less invasive than intra-articular injection, may in principle allow self-administration, and can be engineered to form a local drug reservoir after injection. In this context, the subcutaneous depot serves as the site of drug storage and controlled release, while the intended therapeutic benefit is sustained systemic exposure over an extended period. Such systems therefore have the potential to reduce dosing frequency and mitigate the adverse effects and compliance issues associated with conventional DS regimens. Moreover, combining subcutaneous delivery with advanced carrier platforms—such as polymeric nanoparticles (Dewangan et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2019), biodegradable microspheres (Shang et al., 2012; Sousa-Batista et al., 2018), in situ forming hydrogels (Nasir et al., 2012; Pula et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2024), liposomes (Aleandri et al., 2022; Shilo-Benjamini et al., 2023) or polymeric micelles (Dong et al., 2016) —offers additional opportunities to fine-tune drug loading, release kinetics, and depot stability while limiting peak systemic concentrations and improving overall treatment adherence in patients with AC.

Currently, injectable in situ-forming hydrogels have emerged as a promising platform for subcutaneous drug delivery targeting musculoskeletal and inflammatory diseases (Dimatteo et al., 2018; Qi et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2025). Hydrogels are considered prime candidates for controlled drug delivery systems due to their unique features such as tunable biodegradability, excellent biocompatibility, high structural flexibility, and substantial water uptake capacity (Choi et al., 2024). These three-dimensional, water-swollen polymer networks can be administered as low-viscosity sols and undergo gelation under physiological conditions, thereby forming a depot capable of entrapping and gradually releasing the loaded drug (Dimatteo et al., 2018). Thermosensitive hydrogels, in particular, exploit a temperature-triggered sol–gel transition: they remain injectable at room or low temperature and rapidly solidify into a semi-solid gel at body temperature (Kocak et al., 2020). Numerous thermosensitive systems based on synthetic and natural polymers—including poloxamer polymer system, poly(ethylene glycol)–poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PEG-PLGA), poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM), and chitosan derivatives—have been investigated for the controlled delivery of small molecules, peptides, and biologics (Fan et al., 2022; Lv et al., 2025).

Among these platforms, each system offers distinct advantages but also presents important trade-offs. PEG-PLGA-based systems are biodegradable and translationally attractive, but their synthesis and manufacturing may be relatively complex and costly. Poloxamer-based systems are easy to formulate and highly thermosensitive, yet they often show limited structural stability and relatively rapid drug release. Chitosan/β-glycerophosphate systems are injectable and capable of rapid gelation, although their predominantly physical crosslinking may lead to weaker mechanical strength and faster erosion. Pure PNIPAM systems exhibit sharp thermoresponsiveness near physiological temperature, but their limited biodegradability and biological functionality may restrict broader biomedical use. Therefore, hybrid thermosensitive systems that combine the rapid temperature responsiveness of synthetic polymers with the biocompatibility and structural tunability of natural polymers are increasingly considered advantageous for balancing injectability, gelation, depot stability, and sustained-release performance (Panyamao et al., 2020; Sarvepalli and Kandagatla, 2025).

In this context, PNIPAM-based hybrid hydrogels are particularly attractive because they retain sharp thermoresponsive behavior while allowing integration with natural polymers to improve biodegradability, mechanical properties, and release regulation. PNIPAM is a prototypical synthetic thermoresponsive polymer that has been widely exploited across many research and biomedical applications (Ansari et al., 2022). Among temperature-sensitive polymers, PNIPAM is particularly attractive because its lower critical solution temperature (LCST) lies close to physiological temperature (∼ 32 °C) (Lanzalaco and Armelin, 2017). Below the LCST, hydrogen bonding between the amide groups of PNIPAM and surrounding water molecules predominates, resulting in a highly hydrated and expanded polymer network. When the temperature rises above the LCST, hydrophobic interactions among the isopropyl groups become dominant and polymer–water hydrogen bonding is reduced. Consequently, the PNIPAM chains undergo conformational collapse, accompanied by water expulsion and increased interchain association, leading to network contraction and the formation of a denser gel state. This sol-gel transition underpins the use of PNIPAM-based systems as injectable thermogels and on–off drug release matrices. To overcome the limited biodegradability and mechanical weakness of PNIPAM alone (Ansari et al., 2022; Lanzalaco and Armelin, 2017), it is frequently combined with natural polymers. Grafting PNIPAM segments onto backbones such as chitosan, collagen, alginate, or hyaluronic acid yields hybrid hydrogels that retain the sharp thermal responsiveness of PNIPAM while benefiting from the biocompatibility, enzymatic degradability, and tunable mechanical properties of the natural matrix (Chen et al., 2017; Sarwan et al., 2020). These graft copolymers allow fine control over gelation temperature, gel strength, and degradation behavior, making them particularly attractive as in situ forming depots for controlled drug delivery.

Chitosan (CS) and β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) were selected as complementary components for constructing the injectable hydrogel network. CS, a cationic polysaccharide derived from chitin, is widely recognized for its biocompatibility, biodegradability, low immunogenicity, and favorable safety profile, making it a well-established backbone for injectable thermosensitive hydrogel systems and depot formulations (Naderi-Meshkin et al., 2014; Salamat et al., 2026). In addition, the abundant primary amino groups on the CS backbone readily participate in Schiff base reactions with aldehyde- or ketone-containing crosslinkers, enabling the formation of mechanically reinforced hydrogel networks under mild aqueous conditions (Abdalla et al., 2022; Duceac and Coseri, 2022). Complementing this polymeric backbone, β-CD is a cyclic oligosaccharide with a hydrophobic inner cavity and hydrophilic outer surface that can form inclusion complexes with drug molecules, thereby improving drug solubility, stability, and release modulation. When incorporated into CS-based carriers, β-CD can further enhance drug retention within the matrix and contribute to safer injectable formulations by modulating drug release and reducing local irritation (Sahu et al., 2023). Importantly, β-CD inclusion complexes with diclofenac sodium have already shown translational relevance in parenteral products, further supporting its selection in the present study (Blair and Plosker, 2015; Hamilton et al., 2018; Zeitlinger et al., 2012). Glutaraldehyde (GA) was chosen as the crosslinking agent owing to its widespread use, efficient reactivity with CS via Schiff base formation, and cost-effectiveness. Its strong crosslinking capabilities enable robust mechanical reinforcement of the hydrogel structure, which can be achieved efficiently at relatively low concentrations. These practical advantages have contributed to GA's continued popularity in the development of CS-based biomaterials (Xue et al., 2025).

Based on the knowledge presented above, the hypothesis of this study is as follows: a thermoresponsive hydrogel system constructed through the rational integration of chitosan, β-cyclodextrin, and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM) could be developed as an injectable thermosensitive depot system capable of undergoing a temperature-triggered sol-to-gel transition after subcutaneous administration, thereby enabling sustained drug release by combining the thermoresponsive properties of PNIPAM with the biocompatible chitosan backbone and the release-modulating capacity of β-cyclodextrin. The hydrogels synthesized via free-radical polymerization using ammonium persulfate (APS) as the initiator and glutaraldehyde (GA) as the crosslinking agent. Subsequently, hydrogels are optimized to achieve an appropriate sol–gel transition temperature and suitable syringeability, followed by systematic characterization of its physicochemical and rheological properties. In vitro release profiles under physiological conditions are quantified and interpreted using relevant kinetic models. Biodegradation studies assess the degradation behavior of the hydrogel over multiple days. Biocompatibility and safety are assessed to evaluate potential irritation and blood compatibility of the formulation. The in vivo performance of the formulation is further evaluated via pharmacokinetic studies in an appropriate animal model, which demonstrate that the hydrogel depot can prolong the duration of drug exposure, extend the apparent elimination half-life, and reduce peak plasma concentrations compared with the conventional solution, thereby supporting its function as a sustained-release subcutaneous depot system.

Materials and method

Materials

Chitosan (CS: Mw 100–300 kDa, degree of deacetylation ≥75%, and purity ≥99%), N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM: AR, purity ≥99%) and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS: purity ≥99%) were gained from ThermoFisher (Waltham, United States). β-Cyclodextrin (β-CD: purity ≥98%) and glutaraldehyde (GA, 50% aqueous solution) were obtained from Alfa Aesar (Haverhill, United States). Ammonium persulfate (APS: purity ≥98%), sodium chloride (NaCl: purity ≥99.5%), and potassium chloride (KCl: purity ≥99.5%) were purchased from Showa Chemical (Tokyo, Japan). Diclofenac sodium (DS: AR, purity ≥99%) was sourced from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4: purity ≥99%) and disodium phosphate (Na2HPO4: purity ≥98%) were obtained from Acros Organics (Carlsbad, CA, US). Glacial acetic acid was procured from Scharlau (Barcelona, Spain).

Synthesis of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM injectable thermosensitive hydrogel

A series of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel formulations were developed by combining CS, β-CD, and NIPAM as the primary polymer and monomer components, with GA serving as the cross-linker and APS acting as the free radical initiator (Ahmad et al., 2019). The detailed composition of the thermosensitive hydrogel is presented in Table 1. CS was first dissolved in 1% (w/v) aqueous acetic acid under magnetic stirring to obtain a clear, homogeneous solution. In a separate beaker, a weighed amount of β-CD was completely dissolved in a fixed volume of deionized water. The β-CD solution was then mixed with the CS solution under continuous stirring to give the CS/β-CD copolymer solution. NIPAM monomer, which is readily soluble in water, was added directly to the CS/β-CD solution while stirring to ensure uniform dispersion of the monomer in the polymer matrix. Afterwards, APS, previously dissolved in a small amount of deionized water, was introduced into the CS/β-CD/NIPAM mixture as a free-radical initiator. The mixture was stirred until completely homogeneous. GA (50% aqueous solution) was then added dropwise as a crosslinking agent under vigorous stirring. After the addition of GA, the reaction mixture was purged with nitrogen gas for about 30 min to remove dissolved oxygen and to favor free-radical polymerization. The flask was then sealed, and the reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 24 h, resulting in grafting of PNIPAM chains onto the CS/β-CD backbone and formation of a three-dimensional hydrogel network. For purification and freeze-drying, the freshly prepared hydrogels were transferred into dialysis bags (molecular weight cutoff [MWCO] ∼12–14 kDa) and dialyzed against a large excess of deionized water for 3 days. During the first 24 h the external medium was replaced every 6–8 h, and then twice daily thereafter, until no GA odor remained, indicating removal of unreacted GA, residual monomer, initiator and other low-molecular-weight impurities. After dialysis, the purified hydrogels were frozen at approximately −20 °C and lyophilized for a further 3 days by freeze dryer EYELA FDU-1200 (EYELA, Tokyo, Japan). This freeze-drying step produced lightweight, porous hydrogel discs suitable for subsequent characterization and swelling studies.

In vitro characterization

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to examine the molecular interactions and structural organization of the hydrogel components in both blank and drug-loaded formulations. Such evaluation confirms the successful formation of the polymeric matrix and provides insights into the stability of the drug delivery system. To achieve this, spectra of the pure drug, individual excipients, the synthesized hydrogels and the drug loaded hydrogel will be recorded. Measurements will be performed using attenuated total reflectance (ATR) over the range of 500–4000 cm−1 on a NICOLET 380 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ishioka, Japan) (Khan et al., 2019).

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis

The chemical structures of the starting materials and the synthesized hydrogel were analyzed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy using a Varian 400-MR NMR spectrometer (Varian Inc., USA) operated at 400 MHz. The samples were dissolved in D2O and transferred into standard 5 mm NMR tubes for measurement. All spectra were acquired at room temperature.

Morphology

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) will be performed for the lyophilized thermosensitive hydrogels at various magnifications in order to know the surface morphology of hydrogels. JSM-5300 model (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to scan the hydrogels. The sample was mounted on an aluminum stub using conductive carbon tape to ensure stability and minimize charging. The mounted samples were subsequently sputter-coated with a thin gold layer to improve surface conductivity and enhance SEM image quality. Finally, the prepared sample was examined by photomicrographs (Suhail et al., 2022a).

Thermogravimetric analysis

The TGA test of NIPAM, CS, β-CD, and the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel was carried out using a thermal analyzer TGA/DSC 2-HT (Mettler-Toledo, Columbus, USA). Approximately 3–6 mg of each sample was placed in an open pan connected to a microbalance. The temperature was increased from 25 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C/min, while nitrogen was continuously purged at a flow rate of 30 mL/min throughout the experiment (Chen et al., 2023).

Differential scanning calorimetry

The thermal behavior of NIPAM, CS, β-CD, and CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel was assessed by high-temperature thermal analysis. Approximately 5 mg of each sample was accurately weighed into standard aluminum pans and subjected to a programmed heating cycle from 25 °C to 600 °C at a constant rate of 20 °C/min. All measurements were performed under a dynamic nitrogen atmosphere maintained at a flow rate of 30 mL/min (Chen et al., 2023).

Determination of sol–gel transition temperature and gelation time

The sol–gel transition behavior of the hydrogels was evaluated using the vial inversion method. Briefly, thermosensitive hydrogel formulations were transferred into glass vials and placed in a thermostatically controlled water bath. The temperature was gradually increased from room temperature to physiological temperature (25–40 °C). At each interval (1 °C step), the vial was gently inverted, and the state of the sample was observed. The sol–gel transition temperature was defined as the lowest temperature at which the hydrogel showed no visible flow upon inverting the vial 180° within 30 s (Yang et al., 2025).

The gelation time was determined by maintaining the hydrogel solution at 37 °C and recording the time required for the sample to reach a non-flowing state after inversion (Liu et al., 2024b).

Optical transmittance analysis

The optical transmittance of the thermosensitive hydrogel was determined at 450 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer U-5100 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Hydrogel samples were transferred into quartz cuvettes and maintained in a digital water bath. The temperature was gradually raised from 25 °C to 37 °C, with each sample equilibrated at the target temperature for 5 min prior to measurement. Transmittance values were recorded to monitor changes in optical clarity associated with the sol–gel transition of the hydrogel (Khan et al., 2025).

Syringeability

Syringeability was qualitatively assessed as a preliminary screening test based on the relative manual force required to expel the formulation through a 23G needle. This method was used only for comparative ranking of formulations during optimization and not as a quantitative measure of clinical injection performance. Briefly, a 3 mL syringe was filled with 1 mL of the hydrogel and fitted with a 23G × 1¼” (0.6 × 32 mm) needle. The formulation was then manually injected onto clean glass slides at 37 °C to mimic physiological conditions. During administration, the resistance felt on the plunger was used to qualitatively evaluate the injection force, allowing comparison of the ease of injection among different hydrogel formulations (Morello et al., 2021).

Rheological measurement

Rheological properties of the thermosensitive hydrogel were evaluated using a rotational TA HR20 rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, US) equipped with a parallel-plate geometry with 25 mm diameter. In oscillatory rheological analyses, the storage modulus (G′) represents the elastic or solid-like behavior of the material and its ability to store deformation energy, whereas the loss modulus (G″) reflects the viscous or liquid-like behavior associated with energy dissipation through flow. The relative balance between G′ and G″ provides valuable insight into the viscoelastic properties and structural integrity of the hydrogel (Cheng et al., 2025). All rheological data were recorded and processed using TRIOS software (TA Instruments, New Castle, US).

Oscillation temperature sweep

To investigate the effect of temperature on the viscoelastic behavior, oscillatory temperature sweep experiments were carried out over a range of 25–40 °C under constant strain (1%) and frequency (1 Hz). Changes in G′ and G″ were recorded to determine the thermal response of the hydrogel.

Shear-thinning behavior (Flow sweep)

To investigate the shear-dependent viscosity profile, flow sweep measurements were performed at 25 °C over a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1. The viscosity of the hydrogel was recorded as a function of shear rate to evaluate shear-thinning behavior, which is critical for syringeability and injectability.

Dynamic swelling experiments and oscillatory swelling–deswelling–reswelling cycles behaviors

Dynamic swelling and oscillatory swelling-deswelling–reswelling behaviors of the hydrogels were evaluated by alternately immersing freeze-dried hydrogels in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at room temperature (25 °C) (swelling condition) and physiological temperature (37 °C) (deswelling condition) (Promdontree et al., 2024). At predetermined time intervals, the hydrogels were removed, blotted gently with filter paper to remove excess residue water on surface, and weighed. The swelling ratio was calculated according to the Eq. (1):(1)Swelling ratioSR=Wd−WtWt

Here, Wt denotes the weight of the swollen hydrogel at time t, and Wd represents the initial dry weight of the hydrogel.

After each swelling–deswelling cycle, the hydrogels were transferred back to the alternate medium, and the process was repeated for several cycles to evaluate the reversibility and stability of volume changes.

Porosity measurement

A porosity study was performed on the hydrogel to assess its ability to allow fluid to penetrate through its pores and enter the internal hydrogel network. This evaluation provides insight into the hydrogel's porous structure and its capacity for fluid absorption and transport (Huang et al., 2025). Briefly, freeze-dried hydrogel samples of known weight (W1) were immersed in absolute ethanol for 3–5 days to allow the solvent to penetrate the pores completely. The hydrogels were then removed, and the surface ethanol was gently blotted with filter paper before weighing (W2 ). The porosity (%) was calculated using the following Eq. (2):

Porosity (%) = W2−W1ρV x 100 (2).

Here, ρ denotes the density of absolute ethanol and V represents the swelling volume of the hydrogel discs.

In vitro drug release study and kinetic models

Drug release studies of DS loaded in thermosensitive hydrogel were carried out using the dialysis bag diffusion method. Briefly, 2 mL of drug-loaded hydrogel was placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO∼12–14 kDa) and immersed in 35 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) solution containing 0.003% lysozyme (to mimic physiological enzymatic conditions) and maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C under 100 rpm continuous stirring. At specified time points, 1 mL of the release medium was sampled and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh, pre-warmed PBS in order to preserve sink conditions throughout the experiment. The collected samples were diluted appropriately and analyzed quantitatively at 280 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Jung et al., 2017).

In vitro biodegradation

To examine the degradation characteristics of the thermo-responsive hydrogels, a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) immersion approach was employed. Initially, the hydrogels were synthesized, purified, lyophilized to remove residual water, and weighed to determine the starting dry mass (W1 ). Each sample was then submerged in 20 mL PBS (pH 7.4) solution with 0.1% lysozyme and kept at 37 ± 0.5 °C in a shaking incubator to reproduce the physiological environment of degradation process. The incubation medium was renewed every 48 ± 1 h to preserve stability. At scheduled intervals, the samples were removed and gently rinsed with deionized water to eliminate any residue surface salts, and freeze-dried until a constant mass (W2) was obtained (Kalantarnia et al., 2025). The percentage of degradation was estimated using Eq. (9):(9)Degradation%=W1−W2W1x100where W1 is the initial dry weight and W2 represents the remaining dry weight at a given time. The degradation kinetics were represented as the percentage weight loss plotted versus incubation duration.

In vitro biocompatibility testing

Hemolysis assays (Blood compatibility assessment)

The blood compatibility of the hydrogels was examined through a hemolysis assay using freshly collected Sprague-Dawley rats red blood cells (RBCs) (Haloi et al., 2023). Whole blood was drawn into EDTA-coated tubes and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the RBCs. The cell pellet was repeatedly washed three times with sterile 0.9% NaCl solution until the supernatant appeared clear, ensuring complete removal of plasma residues and free hemoglobin. The purified RBCs were then resuspended in saline to prepare a 2% (v/v) working suspension.

For the assay, hydrogel samples were incubated with 1 mL of the RBC suspension at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged and the absorbance of the resulting supernatant was measured by a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 545 nm to quantify the extent of hemoglobin release. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) served as the negative control (0% hemolysis), whereas 1% Triton X-100 was employed as the positive control (100% hemolysis). The percentage of hemolysis was calculated by comparing the absorbance of the test samples to those of the controls in Eq. (10):(10)Hemolysis rate%=As−AnAp−Anx100

Here, As is the absorbance of the test sample, An and Ap are the absorbance values of the negative and positive controls, respectively.

Hen's Egg Test – Chorioallantoic Membrane (HET-CAM)

The irritation potential of the thermosensitive hydrogels was evaluated using the HET-CAM assay, an acceptable alternative method to assess local irritation by monitoring vascular responses of the chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) in fertilized hen's eggs (Eichenbaum et al., 2013). Eggs were incubated for 7–9 days in a rotating incubator at 37 °C. After incubation, the shell above the air sac was carefully opened, a few drops of 0.9% NaCl were added to soften the membranes, and the inner shell membrane was carefully peeled away to expose the CAM.

A volume of each group (300 μL) was applied onto the CAM. The following groups were tested: (1) 1% w/v sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) as positive control, (2) 0.9% NaCl as negative control and (3) hydrogel extract. The membranes were observed under a stereomicroscope for 5 min to record the onset of hemorrhage, vessel lysis, and coagulation. The Irritation Score (IS) was calculated using the following Eq. (11):(11)IS=301−thx5300+301−tlx7300+301−tcx9300where th , tl , and tc are the times (s) to first observation of hemorrhage, lysis, and coagulation. Irritancy was classified as no irritation (0.0–0.9), slight (1.0–4.9), moderate (5.0–8.9), or severe (9.0–21.0).

In vivo pharmacokinetic study

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Kaohsiung Medical University (Approval No. 114115). The study was designed as a preliminary proof-of-concept pharmacokinetic evaluation to compare the plasma exposure profile of diclofenac sodium (DS) following subcutaneous administration of a conventional DS solution and the optimized DS-loaded thermosensitive hydrogel.

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (7 weeks old, 250–300 g) were obtained from BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd. (Taipei, Taiwan). Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions (25 ± 1 °C, relative humidity 55 ± 5%) with free access to water. Food was withdrawn overnight before dosing. Animals were randomly allocated into two experimental groups (n = 3 per group). Group I (control) received a subcutaneous injection of DS solution (0.5% w/v in water for injection, 10 mg/kg). Group II (test) received a subcutaneous injection of the DS-loaded hydrogel at the same DS concentration (0.5% w/v), corresponding to an equivalent dose of 10 mg/kg. The selected hydrogel formulation was HG-06, which was chosen based on its overall formulation performance in terms of syringeability, thermosensitive gelation behavior, sustained release, biodegradation profile, and biocompatibility. Before dosing, animals were pretreated with atropine (0.04 mg/kg, intramuscularly) to reduce salivary and bronchial secretions, followed 15 min later by anesthesia with Zoletil (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). For blood collection at 4 h after administration, animals were lightly anesthetized with inhalational isoflurane using a controlled anesthesia system (Midmark Matrx VIP 3000 Vaporizer, Midmark Corp., Dayton, OH, USA), thereby avoiding repeated or excessive Zoletil administration. Approximately 0.25–0.30 mL of blood was collected from the jugular vein at predetermined time points. The total sampling volume did not exceed 1% of body weight. Blood samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the separated plasma was stored at −20 °C until analysis.

For sample preparation, 100 μL of plasma was mixed with 100 μL methanol, followed by the addition of 100 μL flufenamic acid solution (5 μg/mL) as the internal standard and 100 μL of 0.1 N HCl. After vortexing for 10s, 1 mL tert-butyl methyl ether was added to extract DS; the mixture was vortexed for 10 min and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. An aliquot of 800 μL of the organic layer was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuged tube and evaporated to dry in a vacuum oven EYELA CVE-3000 (EYELA, Tokyo, Japan) at 40 °C for 1 h. The residue was reconstituted in 100 μL methanol, and an appropriate volume was injected into the HPLC system. Plasma DS concentrations were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan) using a LiChrospher® 100 RP-18 endcapped column (250 × 4 mm, 5 μm; Merck). The mobile phase consisted of acetic acid (pH 3.3) and acetonitrile (30:70, v/v), delivered at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. A 50 μL aliquot of the reconstituted sample was injected for analysis, and detection was carried out at 280 nm, with retention times of approximately 5.3 min for DS and 6.5 min for the internal standard.

Pharmacokinetic parameters, including the area under the plasma concentration-time curve (AUC), maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to reach Cmax (Tmax), mean residence time (MRT), and elimination half-life (t1/2), were calculated from the plasma concentration-time data obtained in the in vivo study. The concentration-time profiles and derived pharmacokinetic parameters were generated using Microsoft Excel.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated, and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the in vivo pharmacokinetic study, data from three animals per group were analyzed and are also expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (SPSS Inc., USA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple-group comparisons where applicable. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM thermosensitive hydrogels

CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM thermosensitive hydrogels were successfully prepared by a free-radical polymerization route. The proposed chemical structure of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel is given in Fig. 1. In all formulations, the β-CD concentration was kept constant, whereas the contents of NIPAM, CS, and crosslinker (GA) were systematically increased in a stepwise manner. The three-dimensional network is formed by free-radical–initiated chain growth of NIPAM and grafting onto the CS/β-CD backbone, together with covalent crosslinking through Schiff base reactions between the amino groups of CS and the aldehyde groups of GA. The resulting hydrogel sols are clear yellow, a typical feature of imine (C=N) chromophore formation, confirming successful Schiff base linkage and hydrogel formation (Martínez-Mejía et al., 2019). Subsequently, the hydrogel solutions were freeze-dried to obtain lyophilized hydrogel discs, which were used for the relevant characterization experiments.Fig. 1Proposed network structure of the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel formed via PNIPAM grafting and Schiff base crosslinking.Fig. 1

Thermal behabior of hydrogels

Thermal behavior of the individual components and the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel was examined by DSC, TGA, and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) and presented in Fig. 2B-D.

In the DSC thermograms (Fig. 2B), the NIPAM monomer exhibits two sharp endothermic peaks below 200 °C, corresponding to its melting and subsequent volatilization (Sousa et al., 1998). CS and β-CD both show broad endothermic signals below 120 °C, which are attributed to the evaporation of physically bound water. Beyond this region, CS presents a wide transition centered around 300 °C, indicative of polysaccharide backbone degradation, whereas β-CD displays its principal degradation peak at approximately 330 °C, associated with breakdown of the cyclic oligosaccharide structure (David et al., 2019; Guinesi and Cavalheiro, 2006). By contrast, the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel shows an additional, well-defined endothermic peak at around 32 °C that is absent in the individual components. This transition is characteristic of the lower critical solution temperature (LCST) of PNIPAM and confirms successful grafting and the thermoresponsive nature of the network. At higher temperatures, a broad decomposition event appears above 400 °C, reflecting the thermal degradation of the crosslinked CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM matrix. The shift of this major degradation process to higher temperature relative to the native polymers demonstrates the enhanced thermal stability of the grafted hydrogel system.

Thermal stability of the individual components and the grafted hydrogel was further assessed by TGA/DTG (Fig. 2C-D). Derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) helps to clearly resolve mass-loss events such as decomposition, evaporation, or oxidation at specific temperatures, thereby sharpening the peaks observed in the original TGA curve, called the peak degradation temperature. All samples showed an initial mass loss of approximately 10% below about 120 °C, attributed to the removal of surface and weakly bound water. The NIPAM monomer then underwent a single predominant degradation step, with about 90% weight loss occurring between roughly 120 and 180 °C and leaving less than 10% residual mass at high temperature (Ahmad et al., 2019), consistent with its DTG maximum around 176 °C. In comparison, CS displayed a more gradual, multistep degradation profile: after dehydration, an additional 50–55% mass loss was observed between approximately 330 and 390 °C (Coelho et al., 2007), resulting in a total weight loss of about 70% by 700–800 °C, in agreement with a DTG peak near 308 °C. β-CD exhibited a similar pattern, followed by a major decomposition stage between about 330 and 350 °C that accounted for roughly 70–75% weight loss, leaving less than 15% residue at around 530 °C (David et al., 2019) and corresponding to a DTG maximum close to 322 °C. In contrast, the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel showed a delayed onset and a broader range of thermal degradation. After a modest weight loss of around 10% below 120 °C, a major decomposition step occurred between approximately 380 and 410 °C, during which about 40–50% of the mass was lost, and leaving a comparatively higher char residue of about 20% above 450 °C. The principal DTG peak for the hydrogel was located at around 419 °C, markedly higher than those of the native components. The shift of the maximum degradation temperature to higher values, together with the increased residual mass, clearly indicates that formation of the grafted, crosslinked CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM network confers enhanced thermal stability relative to the individual polymers.

NMR data analysis

Fig. 3 presents the 1H NMR spectra of β-CD, CS-g-PNIPAM, and β-CD/CS-g-PNIPAM. In the spectrum of β-CD, the multiplet resonances in the range of δ 3.0–4.0 ppm (brown circle) were assigned to the H2–H6 protons of the glucopyranose units, whereas the signal at δ 4.92 ppm was attributed to the anomeric H1 proton of β-CD. For CS-g-PNIPAM, the resonances observed in the δ 3.0–4.0 ppm region (blue circle) were assigned to the H2–H6 protons of the glucosamine units of chitosan, while the signal at δ 4.75 ppm corresponded to the anomeric H1 proton of chitosan. The PNIPAM grafts exhibited characteristic signals at δ 1.04 ppm, corresponding to the methyl protons of the isopropyl groups, and at δ 1.45 and 2.09 ppm, which were assigned to the backbone –CH2– and –CH– protons of PNIPAM, respectively (Fathi et al., 2019). The coexistence of the characteristic resonances of both chitosan and PNIPAM demonstrates that PNIPAM chains were successfully grafted onto the chitosan backbone. Syringeability

The syringeability and temperature-triggered in situ gel formation onto clean glass slides at 37 °C is presented in Fig. 5B, confirming that the formulations can be smoothly injected through a fine needle and rapidly transform into a stable gel under physiological conditions. The qualitative syringeability scores in Table 2 (from “+” = difficult, “++” = moderate to “+++” = easy) are consistent with this observation and reflect the influence of composition on injectability.

Formulations HG-01, HG-02, and HG-06, which contained moderate amounts of NIPAM and CS with relatively low GA content, were rated as “+++”, indicating comparatively lower resistance to flow and better handling during manual injection. In contrast, increasing the content of NIPAM or CS (HG-03, HG-04, and HG-05), or using the highest level of GA (HG-07), reduced the qualitative syringeability score to “++” or “+”. This trend suggests that higher monomer/polymer content or increased crosslinking density led to greater viscosity and, consequently, greater resistance during injection. These results were useful for preliminary formulation screening and for identifying compositions that balanced gel-forming ability with acceptable manual syringeability. However, it should be noted that this syringeability assessment was qualitative and intended only for comparative formulation screening. Quantitative extrusion-force or injection-force measurements would be required in future studies to provide more reproducible and clinically relevant injectability data.

These hydrogels were subsequently loaded with DS, and their DL amount (mg/g) and LE (%) (Table 2) were determined to identify the most suitable system for the following in vitro studies.

Optical transmittance analysis

The temperature-dependent optical behavior of HG-06 hydrogel was further evaluated by UV–Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 5C). At low temperatures (< ∼30–32 °C), the dispersion remained clear and showed very low absorbance, indicating high optical transmittance and a homogeneous sol state. As the temperature increased beyond this range, a sharp rise in absorbance was observed, accompanied by visual turbidity of the sample. This abrupt change reflects the LCST-type phase transition of the PNIPAM segments: As the temperature rises above the LCST, enhanced hydrophobic interactions among the isopropyl groups and disruption of polymer–water hydrogen bonding lead to chain collapse, water expulsion, and microphase separation, which increases light scattering and reduces transmittance. The sigmoidal absorbance–temperature profile therefore confirms the thermoresponsive sol–gel transition of the hydrogel near physiological temperature.

Rheological profiles

Temperature-sweep rheological analysis revealed a clear thermally induced sol–gel transition for all hydrogel formulations (Fig. 6A–D). At low temperatures (25–31 °C), both the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") remained very low, with G" exceeding G', indicating a predominantly liquid-like sol state. As the temperature approached approximately 31.5–32.5 °C, a sharp increase in G' was observed, followed by a crossover where G' surpassed G", confirming gelation triggered by the LCST behavior of the PNIPAM-based network. This transition temperature was also generally consistent with the gelation range obtained from the inverted-tube method described above. As shown in Fig. 6A, increasing the NIPAM content (HG-01 → HG-02 → HG-03) resulted in a steeper rise and higher final G' values after gelation, reflecting the formation of a denser and more elastic network due to enhanced hydrophobic interactions among PNIPAM chains. Similarly, increasing the chitosan content (HG-02 → HG-04 → HG-05; Fig. 6B) led to a pronounced enhancement in G' after the sol–gel transition, suggesting that the polysaccharide backbone contributes to additional chain entanglement and network reinforcement. Moreover, elevating the glutaraldehyde crosslinker level (HG-06 → HG-02 → HG-07; Fig. 6C) further increased the post-gelation G', indicating that a higher crosslinking density strengthens the elastic gel structure without significantly shifting the gelation temperature. The complex viscosity (η) profiles (Fig. 6D) were consistent with the oscillatory rheological results, showing low viscosity in the sol state followed by a sharp increase near the LCST, thereby corroborating the formation of a structured gel network upon heating. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the mechanical strength of the thermosensitive hydrogel can be effectively tuned by adjusting the NIPAM content, chitosan concentration, and crosslinking density while maintaining an appropriate gelation temperature for injectable applications.

Fig. 6E shows the flow behavior of the hydrogel formulations at 25 °C. As the shear rate increases, shear stress rises while the apparent viscosity progressively decreases, indicating non-Newtonian, shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) behavior. Such a response means the gels become more fluid under applied stress—facilitating injection—yet retain higher viscosity at low shear, which reflects strong interactions and entanglements between polymer chains within the network, favorable for maintaining the depot after administration. At low shear rates, formulations containing higher CS or GA (HG-05, HG-07) exhibit the highest viscosities, consistent with their stronger gels in the oscillatory tests, whereas HG-01, HG-02, and HG-06 remain comparatively less viscous and thus more readily injectable—findings that are consistent with their superior syringeability score.

Overall, the rheological data confirm that the relative amounts of NIPAM, CS, and GA allow fine control over the sol–gel transition, gel strength, and flow behavior of the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogels. It should be noted, however, that the storage modulus reported here was used primarily to evaluate viscoelastic behavior and thermally induced gelation, rather than to represent the load-bearing mechanical strength of the hydrogel. In the context of the present study, this is acceptable because the system was designed as a soft subcutaneous depot for sustained drug release rather than as a mechanically supportive scaffold. Nevertheless, the relatively low gel stiffness of the optimized formulation may limit its persistence under more dynamic physiological conditions. Therefore, for future applications requiring greater resistance to deformation or motion, additional reinforcement strategies such as secondary crosslinking, double-network design, or nanocomposite incorporation may be considered to improve mechanical strength and long-term structural stability while preserving injectability and thermoresponsive behavior.

Effect of polymer, monomer and cross-linker on the swelling

The influence of formulation variables on equilibrium swelling at 25 and 37 °C is shown in Fig. 7B. Increasing the NIPAM content resulted in a progressive decrease in swelling ratio at both temperatures, which can be attributed to enhanced hydrophobic interactions and a more compact network, that restricts water uptake (Fig. 7B1). In contrast, higher CS content led to greater swelling, consistent with the hydrophilic nature of CS and its ability to bind water through abundant hydroxyl and amino groups (Fig. 7B2). Raising the GA concentration produced the opposite effect, reducing the swelling ratio due to a higher crosslinking density that restricts network expansion (Fig. 7B3). Similar relationships between hydrophilic/hydrophobic monomer/polymer/crosslinker content, crosslinking density, and swelling behavior have also been reported by Minhas et al. for chemically cross-linked PVA-co-poly(methacrylic acid) hydrogels designed for oral 5-fluorouracil delivery (Minhas et al., 2013).

Temperature-dependent swelling behavior

For all formulations, the swelling ratio decreased monotonically as the temperature was raised from 25 to 45 °C, confirming that the hydrogels contract upon heating above the LCST (Fig. 7C), in accordance with the thermoresponsive behavior of the NIPAM-based hydrogel network described in the preceding section. This decrease reflects the thermoresponsive transition of the PNIPAM segments from a hydrated and expanded state at 25 °C to a more compact conformation at elevated temperatures, which is accompanied by water expulsion and a reduction in the effective free volume available for fluid uptake within the hydrogel matrix. This temperature responsiveness near physiological conditions is therefore a key feature that is expected to influence both drug uptake and release from the thermosensitive hydrogel matrix.

Swelling–deswelling–reswelling cycles of hydrogels

Finally, the reversibility of the thermoresponsive behavior was further evaluated by cyclic swelling–deswelling studies (Fig. 7D). Selected freeze-dried HG-06 hydrogels were alternately immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) at 25 °C and 37 °C, with each temperature step maintained for 0.5 h before switching. At 25 °C, the disks absorbed water and swelled, whereas at 37 °C they released water and contracted. This alternating uptake and loss of water was reproducible over multiple consecutive cycles. Although the swelling ratios measured after each 0.5 h period do not correspond to full equilibrium values, the nearly superimposable profiles across cycles clearly demonstrate a reversible, temperature-dependent response of the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel. This behavior indicates that the hydrogel maintains its structural integrity under repeated thermal stimuli, and that the hydrophilic CS backbone provides effective pathways for water molecules to diffuse in and out of the matrix—features that are advantageous for on–off, temperature-modulated drug release.

Porosity percent

The porosity results of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogels as a function of NIPAM, CS, and GA content are shown in Fig. 7E. Increasing either NIPAM or GA concentration led to a gradual reduction in porosity. For NIPAM, a higher fraction of the thermoresponsive monomer promotes stronger hydrophobic interactions and tighter chain packing, yielding a denser network (Fig. 7E1). Similarly, higher GA levels increase the crosslinking density, further tightening the structure and limiting pore formation (Fig. 7E2). This finding is consistent with the swelling data, where increasing NIPAM or GA content resulted in lower swelling ratios. In contrast, porosity increased with rising CS content (Fig. 7E3). During gel formation, additional CS raises the viscosity of the reaction mixture and can hinder bubble escape, favoring the development of interconnected voids and channels within the matrix.

In vitro biodegradation

Degradation experiments (loss of weight) of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogels were carried out by determining the residual weight (%) of selected formulations (HG-01, HG-02 and HG-06) during incubation in PBS (pH 7.4) containing 0.1% lysozyme at 37 ± 0.5 °C under shaking, to mimic physiological degradation conditions (Fig. 8A). All three hydrogels showed a gradual decrease in mass over 10 days, indicating slow enzymatic erosion of the polysaccharide-based network. During the first few days, only a slight loss in weight was observed, followed by a more pronounced but still progressive decrease with longer incubation, with the remaining weight still above ∼65% at day 10. The observed mass loss should not be attributed to degradation of PNIPAM, which is generally considered non-biodegradable (Lanzalaco and Armelin, 2017). Rather, the gradual decrease in residual weight is more reasonably attributed to enzymatic degradation of the CS/β-CD-containing components and to progressive erosion or loosening of the hybrid network structure in the lysozyme-containing medium. In addition, the results suggest that the Schiff base linkages formed between CS and GA remained relatively stable under neutral PBS conditions, thereby contributing to a controlled, rather than rapid, breakdown of the hydrogel matrix (Kim et al., 2022). Overall, the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogels are biodegradable yet sufficiently robust to support sustained drug release until over 10 days.

In vitro drug release and kinetic modeling

The cumulative release profiles of DS from the thermosensitive hydrogels are shown in Fig. 8B–D. To further clarify the individual roles of β-CD incorporation and PNIPAM grafting in controlling drug release, additional compositional comparison experiments were performed using CS/β-CD without PNIPAM and CS-g-PNIPAM alongside the optimized CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM formulation. As shown in Fig. 8B, the three systems exhibited clearly different release behaviors. Although CS/β-CD without PNIPAM was capable of loading drug (Liu et al., 2023), it exhibited the fastest release and the highest cumulative drug release, indicating that, in the absence of PNIPAM, the matrix could not establish an effective thermoresponsive diffusion barrier. In contrast, CS-g-PNIPAM released DS more slowly, confirming the major role of PNIPAM grafting in retarding drug diffusion through temperature-responsive network formation. Notably, the complete CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM system exhibited slower release and a lower initial burst effect than CS-g-PNIPAM, indicating that β-CD incorporation provided an additional release-modulating benefit within the grafted matrix. Although direct spectroscopic confirmation of DS inclusion within the β-CD cavity was not obtained in the present study, the compositional comparison results clearly demonstrate that β-CD improves early-stage drug retention and further retards DS diffusion in the PNIPAM-grafted hydrogel network. Taken together, these findings indicate that PNIPAM is the primary contributor to thermosensitive sustained release, while β-CD provides an additional functional advantage by suppressing the initial burst release and enhancing overall release control.

In Fig. 8C, the optimized formulation was tested at 25 and 37 °C to examine the influence of temperature on release behavior. A modest initial burst was observed at both temperatures, reflecting diffusion of drug located near or at the hydrogel surface. During the first 24 h, release was clearly faster at 37 °C, indicating that temperature has a pronounced effect on the rate of drug liberation, although it has a much smaller impact on the ultimate amount released. This acceleration can be attributed to two concurrent phenomena. First, an increase in temperature enhances the mobility of both water and drug molecules, thereby facilitating diffusion of DS through the hydrated hydrogel network. Second, above the LCST, PNIPAM segments undergo hydrophobic association and partial chain collapse; this volume contraction exerts a “squeezing” effect on the surrounding medium and can expel a fraction of the drug embedded within the gel. Together, these effects lead to a higher initial release rate at 37 °C, while the long-term plateau remains primarily governed by drug loading and network structure rather than temperature itself, in agreement with observations reported by Liu et al. for dexamethasone-loaded PNIPAM-based hydrogels (Liu et al., 2025). At longer times, however, the curve at 25 °C overtook that at 37 °C, reaching nearly complete release (∼80%) by 120 h, whereas the 37 °C profile plateaued at lower values. This inversion is consistent with the swelling data. Consistent with the findings of Zhang et al., the higher drug release observed at 25 °C can be attributed to the fact that, above the LCST, PNIPAM chains contract and generate a more compact hydrogel network, which narrows the diffusion channels and thereby slows 5-flurouracil release (Zhang et al., 2014).

Fig. 8D compares the cumulative DS release from the three selected formulations (HG-01, HG-02, and HG-06) at 37 °C. All hydrogels show a modest initial burst during the first day, followed by a slower, sustained release phase extending to 120 h, confirming their suitability as long-acting depots. In the early stage, HG-02 releases drug slightly faster than HG-01 and HG-06, but its profile soon levels off and reaches the lowest final release. HG-01 displays a more gradual initial release and ultimately attains the highest cumulative value within the experimental period, whereas HG-06 presents the slowest early release but continues to rise, suggesting further release capacity beyond 120 h. These behaviors are consistent with their compositions. The lower GA content in HG-06 likely resulted in a more hydrated and less compact network structure, allowing more gradual DS transport through the matrix. In particular, the relatively open structure and higher porosity of HG-06 may explain its more prolonged release profile. Overall, the results indicate that HG-06 still has considerable potential for extended drug release and that fine adjustment of NIPAM and GA levels allows tuning of both the early and sustained release phases of CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogels.

To further elucidate the release mechanism, the DS release data from HG-01, HG-02, and HG-06 were fitted to to different kinetic models and the corresponding parameters are summarized in Table 3. Among the tested models, the Korsmeyer–Peppas equation provided the best fit for all formulations, with R2 values between 0.965 and 0.996, higher than those obtained for the Higuchi, first-order, or zero-order models. According to the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, the nature of the diffusion mechanism—whether Fickian or non-Fickian (anomalous)—is inferred from the diffusional exponent “n”. In general, an “n” value ≤0.45 is indicative of Fickian diffusion, whereas “n” > 0.45 denotes anomalous transport, in which drug release proceeds through a combined mechanism of diffusion and polymer relaxation (Garcia-Couce et al., 2022). In vitro biocompatibility

Hen's Egg Test – Chorioallantoic Membrane (HET-CAM)

The HET-CAM assay was employed to evaluate the local irritancy of the polymers and the developed CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogels, as shown in Fig. 9A. In this test, membranes treated with 0.9% NaCl (negative control) maintained normal vascular architecture throughout the 0–5 min observation period, with no evidence of hemorrhage, vessel lysis, or coagulation. In contrast, 1% SDS (positive control) produced rapid and severe irritation, characterized by prominent hemorrhage and vessel damage within the first minute and marked coagulation by 5 min. Following application of the HG-06 hydrogel extract, the CAM remained comparable to the negative control, showing no visible vascular disruption during the entire observation period. These qualitative observations were supported by the irritation score (IS): the negative control scored 0, the positive control showed a high irritation index of about 14.59, whereas HG-06 exhibited a very low value of 0.08. Similar minimal-irritation scores for related hydrogel systems were reported by Suhail et at. (Suhail et al., 2022c), further corroborating our findings. Hemolysis assays

Hemolysis testing was performed in accordance with ISO 10993-4. As shown in Fig. 9B, both the blank and DS-loaded HG-06 hydrogels exhibited hemolysis rates below 5%, markedly lower than the 1% Triton X-100 positive control and within the range classified as slight or non-hemolytic, indicating good hemocompatibility of the formulations. This threshold is consistent with the criterion reported by Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2024a), which defines hemolysis values below 5% as acceptable for biomedical applications.

After subcutaneous injection of the DS solution, plasma concentrations increased rapidly and reached an early sharp peak, indicating fast systemic absorption (Zeitlinger et al., 2012). By contrast, HG-06 produced a markedly lower Cmax and a delayed Tmax, reflecting slower and more gradual drug absorption from the thermosensitive hydrogel depot. This prolonged input phase was further supported by the increased MRT0–∞ and extended apparent half-life observed in the hydrogel group relative to the DS solution.

Mean exposure indices, including AUC0–∞ and AUMC, were also higher in the hydrogel-treated group, suggesting improved maintenance of systemic DS exposure over time. Although the calculated relative bioavailability was higher for HG-06, this parameter should be interpreted with caution in view of the exploratory nature of the study and the limited number of animals per group. Taken together, these findings indicate that embedding DS within the thermosensitive CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM network modified the pharmacokinetic profile in a manner consistent with sustained-release depot behavior, namely by blunting the initial plasma peak and prolonging drug residence in the systemic circulation. Thus, the in vivo PK results provide preliminary support for the suitability of HG-06 as an injectable subcutaneous depot formulation for controlled and sustained delivery.

Conclusions

In this study, an injectable thermosensitive CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel system was successfully designed and comprehensively characterized using DS as a model drug. FTIR and 1H NMR analyses confirmed the successful formation of the CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel network and verified the structural integration of its constituent components, while DSC and TGA/DTG measurements demonstrated that the grafted hydrogels possessed greater thermal stability than the individual starting materials. Porosity and morphological analysis revealed a highly porous and interconnected network, and EDX elemental mapping further confirmed the successful incorporation and uniform distribution of DS within the hydrogel matrix. Rheological studies demonstrated appropriate LCST behavior near physiological temperature, good syringeability at room temperature and rapid gel formation at 37 °C after administration, supporting the suitability of the system for subcutaneous injection. The swelling behavior was governed by the relative contents of monomer, polymer, and crosslinker, in agreement with the porosity results, and the hydrogels also exhibited reversible swelling–deswelling behavior under temperature cycling. Among the investigated formulations, HG-06 was identified as the optimized candidate based on its favorable overall balance of syringeability, thermosensitive gelation behavior, rheological characteristics, controlled release performance, biodegradation profile, and biocompatibility, and was therefore selected for subsequent in vivo evaluation. In addition, compositional comparison in the in vitro drug release study demonstrated that PNIPAM grafting was the primary factor responsible for thermosensitive sustained release, whereas β-CD incorporation provided an additional release-modulating effect by improving drug retention and suppressing the initial burst release. The hydrogels exhibited high drug-loading efficiency and a controlled, sustained release profile, with DS release kinetics best described by the Korsmeyer–Peppas model, indicating a non-Fickian diffusion–relaxation mechanism. Enzymatic degradation in lysozyme-containing medium demonstrated sufficient structural stability to support prolonged release for more than 10 days. Negligible irritation in the HET-CAM assay and hemolysis values well below the 5% ISO 10993-4 threshold further supported the favorable biocompatibility of the system. Moreover, in vivo pharmacokinetic studies with DS demonstrated that the optimized thermosensitive hydrogel significantly lowered peak plasma concentration, prolonged Tmax, MRT, and half-life, and achieved approximately three-fold higher relative bioavailability than the DS solution, thereby supporting prolonged management of arthritis conditions. Taken together, these findings identify the developed CS/β-CD-g-PNIPAM hydrogel as a promising injectable thermosensitive depot platform for controlled and sustained subcutaneous drug delivery, with potential applicability beyond DS to other therapeutics requiring prolonged release.
