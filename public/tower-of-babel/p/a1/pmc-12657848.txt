A bufalin and CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein-loaded calcium lactate nanomedicine for pyroptosis/apoptosis and synergistic cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Yichen Liu, Dahai Hou, Hui Wang, Qiuying Yan, Yunzhu Xu, Qibiao Wu, Dan Dong, Weixing Shen, Weiwei Tao, Haibo Cheng, Dongdong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer, a highly invasive malignancy, poses significant treatment challenges due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Tumor-associated macrophages, which primarily exhibit the M2 phenotype, play a key role in cancer progression and immune evasion. Reprogramming these M2 macrophages to the tumor-suppressive M1 phenotype represents a promising therapeutic approach to enhance antitumor immunity. In this study, we developed a calcium lactate nanoparticle-based delivery system (CLBRP) to codeliver bufalin, a potent Na+/K+-ATPase inhibitor with anticancer properties, and CRISPR-Cas9, which targets the immune checkpoint CD47. The system releases its payload under low pH conditions typical of the tumor microenvironment, increasing extracellular Ca2+ and intracellular osmotic pressure, thereby inducing pyroptosis and immunogenic cell death. Bufalin induces cancer cell apoptosis, inhibits Na+/K+-ATPase, and triggers pyroptosis, which activates a strong immune response. Additionally, bufalin and D-lactic acid synergistically promote macrophage repolarization to the M1 phenotype. CRISPR-Cas9-mediated editing of the CD47 gene on tumor cells blocks antiphagocytic signals, enhancing M1 macrophage phagocytosis and increasing the antitumor immune response. This multimodal strategy triggers strong local antitumor effects and controls metastatic lesions via systemic immune activation, demonstrating significant potential for colorectal cancer treatment. Furthermore, this approach offers a new direction for overcoming resistance to immunotherapies and improving clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a complex and life-threatening malignancy that poses a significant global health burden because of its increasing incidence and profound impact on patients’ quality of life [1]. Despite advances in clinical management, current treatment modalities for CRC are often hindered by suboptimal efficacy, severe toxic side effects, high recurrence rates, and postoperative complications. These limitations underscore the critical need for the development of safe, effective, and durable therapeutic strategies [2]. In recent years, the emergence of immunotherapy has revolutionized CRC treatment, resulting in improved patient outcomes. This paradigm shift emphasizes the importance of not only directly targeting cancer cells but also modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which plays a pivotal role in disease progression and treatment resistance [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Macrophages are essential components of the innate immune system and play pivotal roles in the clearance of invading pathogens and the elimination of cells [7,8]. However, within the TME, these cells often adopt an M2-like polarization state, which contributes to tumor progression, immune suppression, and therapeutic resistance. This phenotypic shift underscores the potential of targeting tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) as a promising therapeutic strategy. By reprogramming TAMs from the protumorigenic M2 phenotype to the antitumorigenic M1 phenotype, it is possible to remodel the immunosuppressive TME, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity and addressing the limitations of conventional therapies [9,10].

Bufalin (C24H34O4), a bioactive steroidal lactone isolated from the traditional Chinese medicine Chan Su (toad venom), serves as a potent and selective inhibitor of Na+/K+-ATPase with well-documented cardiovascular applications [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Emerging evidence highlights its expanding therapeutic potential, particularly in oncology, where it demonstrates multimodal antitumor efficacy through (I) suppression of malignant cell proliferation, (II) activation of intrinsic apoptotic pathways, and (III) circumvention of multidrug resistance mechanisms [15,16]. Notably, recent advances have revealed its immunomodulatory capacity in TME remodeling. Bufalin orchestrates macrophage polarization by shifting TAMs from the protumoral M2 phenotype to the antitumoral M1 state, thereby potentiating host immunosurveillance against neoplastic cells. This dual functionality as both a cytotoxic agent and immune system modulator positions bufalin as a candidate for combination cancer therapies [17,18]. However, the application of Bufalin in cancer therapy is limited by its poor solubility, rapid distribution, and severe cardiotoxicity [14,15]. Thus, improving the accumulation of Bufalin at tumor sites to maintain its therapeutic efficacy while significantly reducing side effects remains a critical challenge that needs to be addressed.

In recent years, the rapid development of nanotechnology has provided new opportunities to enhance drug delivery. Nanotechnology can effectively improve the solubility, stability, and targeting of drugs. The combination of cancer immunotherapy with nanotechnology is considered a highly promising therapeutic strategy [19]. Among these, calcium ion-engineered nanoparticles have emerged as promising platforms for immunotherapeutic applications. Calcium-based nanomodulators employ an ion interference strategy to trigger pyroptosis through spatiotemporally controlled Ca2+ overload in cancer cells. This intelligent nanosystem capitalizes on mitochondrial permeability transition pore activation, subsequently initiating immunogenic cell death cascades that potentiate dendritic cell maturation and cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration, establishing a robust platform for combination immunometabolic therapy [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Given that bufalin can disrupt Na+/K+-ATPase and increase intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, this mechanism provides a potential basis for linking bufalin and calcium overload-mediated pyroptosis.

On this basis, we designed and synthesized a delivery system based on calcium lactate nanoparticles, named CLBRP NPs. This system uses calcium lactate nanoparticles (CL) to encapsulate bufalin (B) and CRISPR-Cas9 CD47 (R), with surface modification via DSPE-PEG (P). The specific antitumor mechanism is as follows (Scheme 1): surface DSPE-PEG modification allows CLBRP NPs to accumulate effectively at the tumor site. After delivery into tumor cells, the released bufalin not only induces cell apoptosis but also inhibits Na+/K+-ATPase activity, leading to increases in Na+ and Ca2+ concentrations. This results in a significant osmotic pressure surge and induces pyroptosis. The subsequent release of large amounts of DAMPs and inflammatory cytokines fully activates the antitumor immune response. In addition, bufalin and D-lactic acid released into the TME synergistically promote macrophage repolarization to the M1 phenotype. Furthermore, the effective gene editing of tumor cell CD47 by CRISPR-Cas9 inhibits antiphagocytic signals, further enhancing the phagocytic efficiency of M1 macrophages. Overall, CLBRP NPs significantly enhance the antitumor immune response and generate long-term immune memory effects, achieving precise, long-lasting, and safe antitumor therapeutic outcomes.Scheme 1(A) Schematic illustration of CLBRP preparation. Results and discussion

Construction and characterization of CLBRP nanoparticles

To construct phospholipid-modified nanomaterials (CLBRP NPs) coloaded with Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) and bufalin, a coprecipitation strategy was employed. Specifically, an aqueous solution of Ca(OH)2 was added dropwise into a lactic acid solution containing Cas9/sgRNA and bufalin under controlled conditions. Finally, DSPE-PEG was added to the washed solution to increase the stability and good dispersion of the nanoparticles in aqueous solution (Fig. 1A). Scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 1B) was employed to characterize the morphology, particle size, and dispersion behavior of the CLBRP NPs. The images revealed that the nanoparticles had a spherical morphology after drug loading. The results were further confirmed by transmission electron microscopy, as shown in Fig. 1C and D, and the N and P in the loaded Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) were evenly distributed within the nanoparticles, indicating successful encapsulation of the drug and protein. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was utilized to analyze the elemental composition, oxidation states, and relative abundance of surface chemical species. As illustrated in Fig. 1F and Fig. S1, these results were completely consistent with the TEM images of the CLBRP NPs. To evaluate the surface electrostatic properties of the nanoparticles, zeta potential measurements were conducted. The average zeta potential of the CLBR NPs was measured to be +4 mV, whereas the CLBRP NPs displayed a negative zeta potential of −8 mV following surface modification with phospholipids (Fig. 1E). In addition, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed that the hydrodynamic size distributions of the CLBR and CLBRP NPs were narrow and consistent with the SEM and TEM results, which were 91 and 110 nm, respectively (Fig. 1G). Moreover, CLBRP maintained excellent colloidal stability in both PBS (pH 7.4) and serum containing 10 % FBS, with negligible changes in particle size over time (Fig. S2). The ultraviolet spectra showed that bufalin and CL NPs had characteristic absorption peaks at approximately 298 nm and 255 nm, respectively, and the CLBR NPs also contained these two absorption peaks (Fig. S3). To evaluate the release capacity of bufalin, Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) and Ca2+ ions from CLBRP nanomaterials in acidic tumor environments, we selected gradient pH buffer solutions at pH = 5.5, 6.5 or 7.4 to simulate the TME [25,26]. Ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy and inductively coupled plasma‒mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) were utilized to determine the drug release capacity (Fig. 1H–J). We found that the release percentages of bufalin, Cas9 protein and Ca2+ ions sharply increased within 2 h, the release curve gradually stabilized between 2 h and 12 h, and the release percentages of bufalin and Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) ultimately exceeded 80 %. The drug loading efficiency was also calculated as the mass of bufalin or Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) in the nanoparticles divided by the mass in the feed; the mass of bufalin or Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) in the nanoparticles divided by the mass of the nanoparticles was defined as the loading content. The loading efficiency and loading content of bufalin were calculated to be 51.25 % and 12.42 %, respectively. The loading efficiency of Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) was 60.12 %, and the loading content of Cas9/sgRNA (CD47) was 6.68 % (Table S1). In vitro antitumor activity of CLBRP

After characterization of the morphology and properties, the antitumor mechanisms of CLBRP at the cellular level were further investigated (Fig. 2A). To assess its cytotoxicity, an MTT assay was performed to evaluate cell viability. The results demonstrated that CLBRP exhibited markedly greater cytotoxicity in the CT26 colon cancer cell line than in the CLP group. Specifically, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of CLBRP was markedly reduced to 53.862 μM, in contrast to the 245.5 μM for CLP (Fig. 2B), indicating its superior ability to inhibit CT26 cell proliferation. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of CLBRP significantly increased in a concentration-dependent manner at higher concentrations. Cellular uptake evaluation revealed time-dependent internalization dynamics when CT26 cells were incubated with EGFP‒Cas9 CD47 RNP-encapsulated CLBRP nanoparticles. Flow cytometric (FCM) analysis demonstrated the progressive accumulation of fluorescence-positive cells, which achieved signal saturation within an 8 h incubation period (Fig. S4). Quantitative assessment revealed a characteristic time‒kinetic uptake profile, with the cellular fluorescence intensity reaching a plateau phase at 8 h posttreatment, which is consistent with receptor-mediated endocytic transport mechanisms. Notably, nanoparticles are internalized primarily by cells via the endolysosomal pathway, a major challenge in nanomedicine due to their eventual degradation within lysosomes. However, the D-lactic acid in the CLBRP played a crucial role in overcoming this limitation. As the endosomal environment gradually acidified, the release of Ca2+ increased endolysosomal membrane permeability, thereby promoting the efficient escape of CLBRP from these vesicular compartments. To assess the lysosomal escape ability of EGFP-labeled CLBRP in CT26 cells, lysosomes were stained with LysoTracker™ Red, and their intracellular localization was analyzed via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) at 4 h and 12 h. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, at the 4 h time point, there was substantial colocalization between LysoTracker™ Red (red fluorescence) and EGFP (green fluorescence), with a Pearson correlation coefficient (Rr) of 0.856, indicating predominant accumulation of CLBRP within lysosomes. Bufalin (C24H34O4) is a cardiotonic steroid isolated from the skin and parotid gland secretions of Chansu [27]. Previous studies have demonstrated that bufalin can inhibit cell proliferation, induce the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and trigger apoptosis, thereby exhibiting therapeutic potential against various types of tumors. To elucidate the intracellular cytotoxic mechanism of CLBRP, the ROS probe 2,7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) was employed to detect the generation of intracellular ROS [28,29]. The results demonstrated that in CT26 cells treated with CLBRP, the fluorescence intensity of DCF (the oxidized product of DCFH-DA) was noticeably stronger than that of the free bufalin and CLP treatment groups (Fig. 2D and E), indicating that CLBRP has an excellent ability to induce ROS production. Furthermore, cell apoptosis was assessed via Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining followed by FCM, which revealed that the apoptosis rate in cells treated with CLBRP was 35.95 %, which was significantly greater than that observed in the other control groups (Fig. 2F and G). Additionally, key apoptotic proteins, including cleaved caspase-3 and caspase-3, were examined [21,22]. Western blot analysis further revealed notable increases in the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and caspase-3 proteins in cells treated with CLBRP (Fig. 2H and I). Moreover, pretreatment of CT26 cells with the apoptosis inhibitor Ac-DEVD-CHO reduced the percentage of apoptotic cells to 11.82 %, indicating that the cytotoxicity of CLBRP could be partially diminished (Fig. S7A and B). On the basis of the above results, we conclude that CLBRP induces tumor cell death through apoptosis. Notably, compared with that in the PBS group, the necrosis rate in the CLBRP-treated group increased sharply from 1.21 % to 24.03 % (Fig. 2F). Therefore, it can be inferred that CLBRP may also promote tumor cell death through mechanisms other than apoptosis.

Osmotic pressure modulation and pyroptosis induction ability of CLBRPs

Following the confirmation of the potent in vitro antitumor efficacy of CLBRP, the underlying mechanism of its antitumor activity has become the subject of further investigation (Fig. 3A). Substantial Ca2+ release was observed during CLBRP dissolution in tumor cells. Verification of this phenomenon was conducted via the calcium-sensitive fluorescent probe Fluo-4 AM, where intracellular Ca2+ fluctuations were quantified via CLSM detection of green fluorescence intensity. CT26 cells were subjected to different treatments followed by fluorescence analysis. Compared with the PBS-treated controls, both the CLP- and CLBRP-treated groups presented dramatically greater green fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3B and C), demonstrating the effective Ca2+ release capability of the nanoparticles into the cellular environment. As a control, cells treated with free bufalin exhibited moderate green fluorescence, confirming that bufalin, a specific and effective inhibitor of the Na+/K+-ATPase ion transporter, can inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase activity, reduce Na+ efflux, and activate the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger, thereby enhancing Ca2+ influx [17,30,31]. Further investigation of Na+/K+-ATPase activity revealed a marked reduction in enzyme activity in both the bufalin and CLBRP groups (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that the synergistic effect of bufalin and Ca2+ in CLBRP dramatically increases Ca2+ levels in CT26 cells, thus achieving effective ionic interference therapy. The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is highly sensitive to alterations in the intracellular osmotic pressure. A rapid increase in the intracellular Ca2+ concentration can trigger a notable increase in osmotic pressure, which in turn profoundly influences cellular physiological functions, particularly the mitochondrial membrane potential [[32], [33], [34]]. To verify this, we used the mitochondrial membrane potential fluorescent probe JC-1 to detect changes in the ΔΨm [35]. JC-1 emits red fluorescence at high ΔΨm, whereas it emits green fluorescence when the ΔΨm decreases. Our data revealed that, compared with those in the PBS group, the cells treated with CLBRP presented robust green fluorescence with minimal red fluorescence, indicating that the marked increase in the Ca2+ concentration led to substantial mitochondrial damage (Fig. 3E and F). To assess cell selectivity, LDH and ATP release levels were measured in the supernatants of normal intestinal epithelial cells (FHC) and CT26 tumor cells. Further investigations revealed that significant alterations in intracellular osmotic pressure could trigger pyroptosis in tumor cells. Pyroptosis is a distinct form of programmed cell death characterized by the formation of membrane pores, cellular swelling, and lytic rupture, which are typically accompanied by the release of intracellular components and proinflammatory cytokines [36]. Among the mediators of this process, gasdermin (GSDM) proteins play a central role by binding to membrane phospholipids and forming transmembrane pores. The caspase-mediated cleavage of GSDMs is widely recognized as a key event initiating pyroptosis [37]. To explore whether CLBRP induces this type of cell death, CT26 cells were incubated with CLBRP, and changes in membrane permeability were assessed using a propidium iodide (PI) uptake assay. It was observed that treatment with Ca2+ alone did not result in a significant increase in fluorescence intensity, which may be attributed to the strict regulation of intracellular Ca2+ transporters, effectively maintaining ionic homeostasis and preventing cellular damage. In contrast, cells treated with the combination of Ca2+ and bufalin exhibited a markedly higher PI uptake rate, indicating that bufalin-induced inhibition of Na+/K+-ATPase activity disrupted ion transport, thereby synergistically enhancing Ca2+ influx and leading to membrane pore formation. Furthermore, the CLBRP-treated cells displayed the strongest PI uptake signal (Fig. S6), suggesting the most pronounced alteration in membrane permeability and confirming that CLBRP effectively induces activation of the GSDMD-dependent pyroptotic pathway. Consistently, ELISA results demonstrated a significant increase in the secretion of IL-18 and IL-1β. Furthermore, pretreatment of CT26 cells with the caspase-1 inhibitor Ac-YVAD-CMK markedly reduced IL-18 release in the supernatant (Fig. S7), confirming the involvement of caspase-1 in this process. Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed a robust elevation of caspase-1 expression in the CLBRP-treated cells (Fig. 3I, J), a finding corroborated by Western blot results, which showed significant upregulation of cleaved caspase-1 and GSDMD-N expression (Fig. 3K, Fig. S8). Notably, inhibition of intracellular Ca2+ influx using the chelator BAPTA-AM significantly attenuated CLBRP-induced GSDMD-N cleavage (Fig. 3K), suggesting a critical role for Ca2+ overload in the initiation of pyroptosis. These results provide compelling evidence that CLBRP triggers pyroptotic cell death by inducing Ca2+ overload, which promotes GSDMD cleavage and subsequent membrane pore formation.

Evaluation of ICD and macrophage M1 repolarization

A rapid surge in intracellular ion concentrations and the activation of pyroptosis represent pivotal triggers for the initiation of immunogenic cell death (ICD) [23,24]. ICD is a distinct subtype of programmed cell death characterized by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which synergistically enhance the immunogenicity of tumor cells. Specifically, ATP acts as a strong chemoattractant, driving the recruitment of immune effector cells; CRT exposure on the cell surface promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation; and HMGB1 facilitates stable binding between DCs and apoptotic tumor cells (Fig. 4A) [38,39].Given the ability of CLBRP to effectively increase the number of explosive ions and trigger pyroptosis, we further investigated its capacity to trigger ICD. First, CRT and HMGB1 were examined via cellular immunofluorescence. A significant increase in CRT fluorescence within the cells was observed in the CLBRP group, indicating enhanced CRT exposure (Fig. 4B, Fig. S9A). Additionally, a marked release of HMGB1 was noted (Fig. 4C, Fig. S9B). Simultaneously, the extracellular levels of HMGB1 and ATP in the culture medium were markedly elevated, confirming the release of DAMPs (Fig. S10). Moreover, pretreatment of CT26 cells with the ICD inhibitor Verapamil led to a notable decrease in the release of HMGB1 (Fig. S7D). These results indicated that CLBRP effectively induced ICD. In antitumor immunity, the infiltration of M1 macrophages and their phenotypic reprogramming play pivotal roles. Tumor cells are directly targeted by M1 macrophages through the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-12 and TNF-α, and reactive oxygen species (ROS), while effector T cells and NK cells are activated, leading to enhanced antitumor immune responses. However, within the immunosuppressive TME, macrophages are frequently polarized toward the M2 phenotype, promoting tumor progression and immune evasion. The depletion of M2 macrophages or their conversion to the M1 phenotype has been demonstrated to markedly restore antitumor immunity and transform the TME into an immune-activated state. Emerging evidence indicates that bufalin and D-lactic acid, which have been identified as potent immune modulators capable of facilitating macrophage repolarization toward the M1 phenotype, play important roles in immune regulation [17,[40], [41], [42]]. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) stimulated with IL-4 demonstrate properties analogous to those of the M2 phenotype and are extensively employed in research related to TAMs [43,44]. To evaluate the ability of the CLBRP to induce M1 macrophage repolarization (Fig. 4D), mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages were isolated and stimulated with IL-4 to establish an M2 macrophage model. CT26 cells treated with different drugs was introduced into the M2 macrophage model. FCM analysis revealed that, compared with the PBS group, both the Free bufalin and D-lactate groups promoted the repolarization of CD86+F4/80+ macrophages toward the M1 phenotype. In contrast, the combination of bufalin and D-lactate markedly enhanced M1 macrophage repolarization, showing a superior effect compared with either component alone. Conversely, treatment with L-lactate did not induce M1 macrophage repolarization (Fig. S12). Importantly, the CLBRP group exhibited a synergistic effect of bufalin and D-lactate, achieving an M1 repolarization rate of 32.2 %, which was comparable to that observed in the LPS-positive control group (Fig. 4E). Concurrently, ELISA analysis of macrophage-related markers revealed marked downregulation of M2-type functional markers (Arg-1 and IL-10), alongside pronounced upregulation of M1-type functional markers (inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), IL-1β, and IL-6) (Fig. 4F–J). These findings demonstrate that CLBRP effectively induces the conversion of M2 macrophages to the M1 phenotype. To further verify whether CD47 gene knockout could enhance the phagocytic activity of M1 macrophages, tumor cells subjected to different treatments were co-cultured with M1 macrophages, and their phagocytosis was quantitatively analyzed by FCM. The results showed that the CLBRP-treated group exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity, indicating that tumor cells in this group were more readily phagocytosed by M1 macrophages (Fig. 4K and L). These findings suggest that CLBRP synergistically enhances macrophage phagocytic function and antitumor immune activity through CRISPR-Cas9 CD47-mediated gene editing combined with M1-type immune activation, providing direct and compelling experimental evidence for its multi-mechanistic cooperative antitumor strategy.

Nonetheless, tumor cells often upregulate immunosuppressive signals that impair immune surveillance and clearance. Among these, CD47 is prominently overexpressed and functions as a key “don't eat me” signal, enabling tumor cells to evade phagocytosis by immune cells. It interacts with SIPRα receptors on macrophages, thereby protecting tumor cells from phagocytosis [45,46]. In contrast, CD47 knockout reduces this protective effect. Considering the strong ability of CLBRP to modulate M1 macrophage repolarization, the CRISPR-Cas9 system was utilized to knock out CD47 gene expression in CT26 cells to increase M1 phagocytosis, thereby facilitating the cascading amplification of the antitumor immune response. The release, nuclear internalization, and expression of RNP-CD47 were evaluated via Western blotting, which further confirmed the efficacy of this approach. CD47 protein expression in CLBRP-treated cells was notably lower than that in PBS-treated cells (Fig. S13A and B). The gene editing efficiency was further assessed via the T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) assay. In CT26 cells treated with CLBRP, the CD47 gene locus exhibited numerous mutations, with the editing efficiency reaching 53.4 % (Fig. S13C). The sequences of the PCR amplicons from the target loci following CLBRP treatment were then displayed via Sanger sequencing. In the PBS group, the DNA fragments were located almost precisely at the target site, whereas mutations and numerous mixed peaks appeared near the target site in the CLBRP-treated group (Fig. S13D).

These findings further validated the feasibility of CD47 gene editing mediated by CLBRP, reinforcing its potential for in vivo applications. In summary, CLBRP exerted antitumor effects through multiple mechanisms, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ICD induction. CRISPR-Cas9-enabled disruption of CD47 effectively attenuated the “don't eat me” signal, enhancing macrophage phagocytosis. In conjunction with the released bufalin and D-lactic acid, CLBRP promoted M1 polarization, further augmenting phagocytic activity and demonstrating strong antitumor efficacy in vitro.

In vivo targeting and gene editing influence of the CLBRP

Given the potent anticancer activity of CLBRP observed in vitro, its in vivo tumor-targeting capability, biodistribution, and gene-editing efficiency were further evaluated using a CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mouse model. To assess targeting performance, EGFP-labeled CLBR or CLBRP was intravenously administered, and real-time fluorescence imaging was conducted at 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection usng the IVIS system. The results showed that CLBRP increased its accumulation in tumor tissues, with fluorescence signals gradually increasing between 4 and 8 h and maintaining a high intensity even at 48 h (Fig. 5A and B). Compared with CLBR treatment, CLBRP resulted in enhanced fluorescence signals in the tumor region at every time point, indicating that PEG surface functionalization effectively improved its tumor-targeting ability. At 48 h post-injection, major organs and tumor tissues were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. CLBRP exhibited preferential accumulation within the tumor site, with markedly reduced distribution in normal organs, highlighting its enhanced tumor-targeting efficiency relative to CLBR (Fig. 5C and D). After confirming the effective accumulation of CLBRP in tumor tissues, its in vivo gene editing efficiency was further investigated (Fig. 5E). The inhibition of CD47 gene expression and enhancement of macrophage-mediated tumor cell phagocytosis were evaluated through T7EI enzyme digestion analysis, Sanger sequencing, and Western blot experiments [47]. Ex vivo Sanger sequencing revealed substantial alterations in the intensity of the main peaks, along with the clear appearance of mismatch peaks (Fig. S14B). Furthermore, to obtain spatially resolved evidence, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of CD47 expression was performed on tumor sections. The results revealed a pronounced reduction in CD47 expression in both the tumor core and peripheral regions of the CLBRP-treated group compared with the control. In vivo antitumor evaluation of CLBRP

CLBRP has been demonstrated to possess potent tumor cell-killing capabilities, with tumor cell pyroptosis and ICD being effectively induced. Additionally, this nanomaterial has a remarkable M1 macrophage polarization potential. In light of the aforementioned experimental results, its favorable tumor targeting and gene editing ability prompted us to explore its in vivo antitumor activity. To assess the therapeutic impact of CLBRP on tumors and its influence on the immune system, a study was conducted using subcutaneous CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice. The mice were randomly divided into five groups: I) PBS, II) free bufalin, III) CLP, IV) CLBP, and V) CLBRP. Each group received an intravenous injection of the corresponding treatment every three days for three weeks, as depicted in Fig. 6A. During the experiment, tumor growth and body weight were monitored for each group. The results revealed that tumor suppression was observed to varying degrees in all treatment groups, with the most pronounced inhibitory impact on tumor growth being demonstrated by the CLBRP group (Fig. 6B–D). Notably, although free bufalin also exhibited certain antitumor effects, it induced pronounced drug toxicity, including body weight loss (Fig. S15), histopathological abnormalities in cardiac tissues observed by H&E staining (Fig. S16), and a significant elevation of cardiac troponin I (cTnI) levels (Fig. S17). In contrast, no significant body weight loss or cardiac abnormalities were observed in the other treatment groups, indicating that nanoparticle-mediated bufalin delivery effectively alleviated bufalin-induced cardiotoxicity and enhanced its therapeutic potential. Next, H&E staining of tumor tissues was performed to assess the tumor-killing effect, which indicated that the CLBRP group had a notably lower tumor cell density and the highest degree of tissue damage (Fig. 6E). In addition, Ki67 and TUNEL immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues indicated that the CLBRP group had greater tumor cell proliferation inhibition and apoptosis induction than the other groups did (Fig. 6F–I). Furthermore, cytokine levels in tumor tissues were assessed via ELISA. The proinflammatory cytokine IL-6 was upregulated, whereas the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was downregulated in the CLBRP group (Fig. 6J and K). In vivo activation of tumor immunity for therapeutic efficacy

The immune response induced by tumor immunotherapy is largely potentiated by the release of inflammatory mediators and cellular contents during pyroptosis, as well as DAMPs during ICD. To further investigate this process, the maturation status of dendritic cells (DCs) within tumor tissues was evaluated. As key orchestrators of antitumor immunity, DCs bridge innate and adaptive responses, and their maturation is a crucial indicator of immune activation. The results showed that after CLBRP treatment, the proportion of mature DCs (CD11c+ CD80+ CD86+) increased significantly from 4.28 % to 18.10 % (Fig. 7A and B, gating strategy shown in Fig. S18A), which indicated that CLBRP markedly enhanced the immune response to tumor therapy by promoting the maturation of DCs. Next, we assessed whether CLBRP, through the synergistic release of bufalin and D-lactic acid, caused increased infiltration of M1 macrophages. The results demonstrated a marked polarization of macrophages in the CLBRP group, with the proportion of M2 macrophages decreasing from 40.44 % to 20.59 %, while that of M1 macrophages increased from 21.00 % to 57.70 % (Fig. 7C–F) [23,24]. This result is consistent with the M1 polarization of BMDMs observed in in vitro experiments. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues revealed that CD47 gene expression was significantly reduced in CLBRP-treated tumors, with only faint CD47 fluorescence signals detectable, indicating that CLBRP efficiently downregulated CD47 gene expression (Fig. S19). To promote the phagocytic function of M1 macrophages and further enhance the antitumor immune response, M1 macrophage polarization can increase T-cell immune responses within the TME for immunotherapy. On the one hand, CD8+ T cells, which are the primary effector cells for tumor killing, directly eliminate tumor cells by recognizing tumor antigens. On the other hand, the antitumor immune response is primarily augmented by CD4+ T cells, which also contribute to the regulation of other immune cell functions, including those of macrophages and DCs. The interaction between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells not only contributes to the establishment of an effective immune microenvironment but also helps to overcome the immunosuppressive features of the tumor, thereby enhancing the persistence and breadth of the antitumor immune response. Therefore, the degree of tumor infiltration by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells is an important indicator for evaluating the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy. In this study, changes in tumor-infiltrating CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were evaluated. After CLBRP treatment, the ratio of CD8+/CD4+ T cells in tumor tissues significantly increased. This change in immune cell composition suggests that CLBRP can significantly enhance the cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) response in the tumor microenvironment, effectively promoting the activation of anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 7G and H; the gating strategy is shown in Fig. S18C). These findings suggest that the CLBRP is capable of recruiting immune cells to the tumor site and significantly enhancing adaptive immune responses, demonstrating not only strong therapeutic efficacy but also excellent biocompatibility and safety. As shown in Fig. 7J, the treatment duration was prolonged to 60 days to assess potential long-term toxicity in murine models. The results demonstrated that the median survival of mice treated with CLBRP was 51 days, whereas it was 31, 32, 34, and 41 days for the PBS, free bufalin, CLP, and CLBRP groups, respectively, highlighting a statistically significant increase in survival with CLBRP treatment. Notably, no significant alterations in body weight were observed in CLBRP-treated mice, and histopathological analysis revealed no abnormalities in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys (Fig. S21A). To further evaluate systemic toxicity, comprehensive analyses of liver and kidney function biomarkers were conducted. The results revealed no statistically significant differences in the serum levels of total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), or blood urea nitrogen (BUN) between the CLBRP-treated mice and the PBS control group (Fig. S21B–G). These findings underscore the favorable biocompatibility profile of CLBRP.

In summary, the antitumor mechanism of CLBRP can be described as follows: (I) Intracellular degradation and osmotic pressure effect: CLBRP undergoes intracellular degradation, releasing Ca2+ and bufalin. Bufalin inhibits Na+/K+-ATPase, reducing Na + efflux and significantly increasing the extracellular Ca2+ concentration, which induces an increase in the intracellular osmotic pressure, thereby triggering pyroptosis and ICD. (II) Release of ICDs and DAMPs: ICDs result in the substantial release of DAMPs, which facilitate the maturation of DCs, thereby augmenting the immune response. (III) Macrophage M1 Repolarization: The stimuli-responsive release of bufalin and D-lactic acid synergistically facilitates the repolarization of M2-polarized macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive TME. (IV) CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing: CRISPR-Cas9 efficiently targets and edits the CD47 gene in tumor cells, further increasing the phagocytic efficiency of M1 macrophages. Collectively, these mechanisms significantly inhibit tumor growth and are anticipated to elicit long-term immune effects, underscoring the substantial potential of CLBRP in anticancer therapy.

Evaluation of CLBRP for in vivo immunotherapeutic application

Tumor metastasis and recurrence remain the most formidable challenges in cancer therapy and are among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality [48]. To further evaluate the efficacy of the proposed therapeutic system in suppressing metastatic tumor progression, two in vivo models—a tumor rechallenge model and a lung metastasis model—were established in CT26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice. In the rechallenge experiment, after completion of treatment, the primary subcutaneous tumors were surgically excised, followed by re-inoculation of CT26 cells into the contralateral flank to simulate distant tumor recurrence or metastasis (as illustrated in Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B–D, the CLBRP-treated group exhibited markedly suppressed growth of contralateral tumors compared with the control groups, indicating that CLBRP effectively elicited a systemic antitumor immune response with significant immunotherapeutic benefits. Furthermore, to assess the antimetastatic efficacy, a lung metastasis model was established by intravenous injection of 100 μL PBS containing 3 × 105 CT26 cells following primary tumor resection, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 8E. By isolating and analyzing lung tissues from the PBS, CLP, and CLBRP groups, we evaluated and compared the extent of lung tumor metastasis. Compared with the PBS group, CLBRP treatment markedly reduced the number of lung nodules (Fig. 8F and G). Consistently, H&E staining of lung sections further confirmed that the CLBRP group exhibited the fewest metastatic lesions (Fig. 8H and I). These findings suggest that CLBRP, through its immune-synergistic mechanisms involving the induction of ICD and the promotion of M1 macrophage polarization, not only effectively inhibits subcutaneous tumor growth but also significantly mitigates the risk of lung metastasis. Conclusion

In summary, we constructed a pH-responsive CRISPR-Cas9 RNP and bufalin loaded with calcium lactate nanoparticles. The liberated bufalin could not only induce cell apoptosis but also inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase activity, leading to the accumulation of intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, triggering pyroptosis and a strong immune response. In addition, the released bufalin and D-lactic acid in the TME synergistically promote macrophage repolarization to the M1 phenotype. Subsequently, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing effectively targeted CD47 in tumor cells, suppressing antiphagocytic signaling and thereby significantly enhancing the phagocytic capacity of M1 macrophages. In vivo antitumor studies demonstrated that the CLBRP nanomedicine exhibited excellent biocompatibility and biosafety profiles while effectively inhibiting tumor proliferation and suppressing pulmonary metastasis. This study aims to expand the biomedical applications of calcium-based nanomedicine, offering innovative strategies and insights for cancer treatment through pyroptosis, apoptosis and immunotherapy.

Characterization of CLBRP nanomaterials: The morphology and elemental distribution of CLBRP nanomaterials were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), while surface structure was analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Quanta 250, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA). Synthesis of cas9/sgRNA-CD47: The Cas9 protein and sgRNA-CD47 (weight ratio = 3:1) were added to Tris–HCl buffer (1 M, pH = 7.0) and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h to obtain the Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 complex. The sgRNA sequence of the CD47 target site was 5′-UAUAGAGCUGAAAAACCGCA-3’. For the corresponding fluorescence experiments, the EGFP-labeled Cas9 protein was mixed with a sgRNA-CD47.

Preparation of CLBRP nanoparticles: D-lactic acid aqueous solution was combined with 30 μL of Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 and 100 μL of bufalin ethanol solution (640 μg/mL), and the mixture was homogenized, slowly stirred and designated Solution A. Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2, 0.068 g) was dissolved in 2 mL of ultrapure water under ultrasonic dispersion, yielding Solution B, which was added dropwise into Solution An under continuous magnetic stirring (500 rpm) at room temperature. The reaction proceeded for 2 h to allow the formation of CLBR nanoparticles. After that, the product was centrifuged (10,000 rpm) at room temperature for 8 min to collect the samples, which were subsequently washed twice with ethanol. Finally, the samples were mixed with 1 mg/mL DSPE-PEG dropwise under magnetic stirring and stirred for 24 h to achieve phospholipid modification on the surface. The final product (CLBRP nanoparticles) was stored at 4 °C for subsequent characterization.

Loading efficiency and loading content of bufalin and Cas9/sgRNA-CD47: The UV‒Vis and fluorescence intensities of bufalin and Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 were measured. The absorbance or intensity of bufalin or Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 was subsequently recorded as A1. After centrifugation of the CLBRP nanomaterials at 10000 r for 8 min, the UV absorption or fluorescence intensity of the supernatant was remeasured and recorded as A2, and the loading efficiency of bufalin or Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 was calculated via the following formula: (A1-A2)/A1∗100 %. The EE% of sgRNA was determined with a Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA assay kit (R11490, Thermo Fisher, USA).

Cas9 protein (3 μL) and sgRNA-CD47 (3 μL) were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1 and added to 50 μL of Tris-HCl buffer (1 M, pH = 7.4), after which Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 was stored at −20 °C. The loading content (LC) was determined via the following formula: LC = (mass of bufalin or Cas9/sgRNA-CD47/mass of CLBRP NPs) × 100 %.

Release behavior of Bufalin, Cas9/sgRNA-CD47, and Ca2+: To evaluate the drug release process in acidic tumor environments, different PBS buffer solutions (pH = 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4) were prepared to observe the drug release ability of the CLBRP nanoparticles. At specific time intervals of 720 min, 400 μL of the supernatant was collected, and the buffer was replenished to maintain a constant volume. The UV‒VIS absorption spectrum of bufalin (298 nm) was determined, and a release curve was established. Similarly, the percent release of Cas9/sgRNA-CD47 (labeled with EGFP dye) was measured via fluorescence spectroscopy (EX WL: 470.0 nm, EM Start WL: 490.0 nm) in the PBS buffer solutions mentioned above.

CLBRP nanoparticles were incubated in PBS solutions with pH values of 5.5, 6.5, or 7.4 at 37 °C for varying time intervals. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged to obtain the supernatants, and calcium ion release under different pH conditions was quantified using ICP–MS.

Cell culture: CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every two days, and cells were passaged using trypsin–EDTA upon reaching approximately 80 % confluence. FHC cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin under the same incubation conditions.

Cellular uptake: CT26 cells (2.5 × 105 per well) were seeded into 12-well plates and allowed to reach ∼80 % confluence before incubation with EGFP-labeled CLBRP. Cells were collected at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h post-treatment and analyzed by FCM to evaluate nanoparticle uptake.

Lysosomal escape: CT26 cells (5 × 105 cells per dish) were seeded into confocal culture dishes. When the cell density reached 80 %, EGFP-labeled CLBRP was added for incubation. After 4 or 12 h of incubation, the cells were stained with a lysosomal fluorescent probe (LysoTracker Red) for 20 min. Subsequently, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 for 20 min. Imaging was performed via CLSM. The following fluorescence signals were detected: red fluorescence from LysoTracker Red (excitation/emission = 577/590 nm), green fluorescence from EGFP (excitation/emission = 488/509 nm), and blue fluorescence from Hoechst 33342 (excitation/emission = 350/460 nm).

In vitro cell viability assay: Cell viability was evaluated using the MTT assay. CT26 cells (5 × 103 per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight. The following day, cells were exposed to varying concentrations of CLP or CLBRP for 24 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h. After removing the medium, 200 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

Measurement of intracellular ROS levels: CT26 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were plated in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Once the cells reached 80 % confluence, they were treated with PBS, free bufalin, CLP, or CLBRP for 12 h. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with 1 μL of 10 mM DCFH-DA in fresh medium for 15 min. After staining with Hoechst 33342, images were captured via an inverted fluorescence microscope (Ex/Em = 495/529 nm).

Cell apoptosis assay: CT26 cells (1 × 104 per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight. Upon reaching ∼80 % confluence, cells were treated with PBS, free bufalin, CLP, or CLBRP. Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit, followed by FCM analysis to determine the apoptotic rate.

Propidium iodide (PI) penetration assay: To evaluate cell membrane integrity. Briefly, cells were collected, washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended at a density of approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. PI was added to the suspension at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL, followed by incubation for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. For positive control, cells were treated with 70 % ethanol or 0.05 % Triton X-100 to induce membrane permeabilization. After staining, cells were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry using a PE or PerCP-Cy5.5 channel (Ex = 535 nm, Em = 617 nm). The percentage of PI-positive cells represented the proportion of cells with compromised membrane integrity, indicating late apoptosis or necrosis.

Cellular immunofluorescence: CT26 cells were washed, fixed, and permeabilized, followed by blocking with 1 % BSA at room temperature for 30 min. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by treatment with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. After DAPI staining for 5 min and subsequent PBS washes, images were acquired using CLSM.

Western blot analysis: Western blotting was conducted to evaluate protein expression in CT26 cells. Cells were lysed on ice for 20 min using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and total protein concentrations were quantified with a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12 % SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5 % nonfat milk at room temperature for 2 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following day, membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, washed with TBST, and visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Gel Logic 2200 Pro, Cambridge, USA). Primary antibodies included those against Cleaved-caspase-3, Caspase-3, Cleaved-caspase-1, Caspase-1, GSDMD-N, and CD47, with GAPDH as the loading control.

Ca2+ Ion detection: Intracellular Ca2+ levels were measured via the Ca2+ ion probe Fluo-4 AM. In brief, CT26 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were plated in 6-well plates. Once the cell density reached 80 %, the cells were treated with PBS, free bufalin, CLP, or CLBRP for 4 h. Afterwards, 2 μL of a 1 mM Fluo-4 AM solution was added, and the cells were incubated for 30 min. Following incubation, the cells were washed with fresh PBS and observed via an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Na+/K+ -ATPase activity assay: CT26 cells (1 × 104 per well) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight. Upon reaching ∼80 % confluence, cells were treated with PBS, free bufalin, CLP, or CLBRP. Na+/K+-ATPase activity was then determined using a commercial assay kit, following the manufacturer's protocol.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement: The mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed via a fluorescent probe. CT26 cells were treated with PBS, free bufalin, CLP, or CLBRP. After treatment, the cells were incubated with a mitochondrial membrane potential-sensitive dye for a specified duration. The fluorescence intensity was then measured via a flow cytometer or a fluorescence microscope, providing an indication of changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA): Tumor single-cell samples were subjected to centrifugation at 1000×g. Serum was obtained from coagulated blood following centrifugation at 2000×g. Tissue samples and cell supernatants from various treatment groups were collected, and the levels of specific cytokines were quantified via ELISA kits following the manufacturer's protocol.

Generation of bone marrow-derived macrophages and macrophage polarization: Bone marrow (BM) cells were harvested from the femurs and tibias of 5–6-week-old BALB/c mice and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin–streptomycin, and M-CSF for 7 days to induce differentiation into bone marrow-derived macrophages (eBMDMs). For M2 polarization, eBMDMs were incubated with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) for 48 h. Once reaching ∼80 % confluence, cells were treated with PBS, free bufalin, CLP, or CLBRP. Following treatment, macrophage polarization was assessed by FCM, and culture supernatants were collected for cytokine analysis using ELISA.

Gene editing efficiency assessment: Genomic DNA was extracted from CT26 cells via DNAzol™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., 10503027). The target region near the CD47 locus was amplified via 2 × HyPerFUsion™ High Fidelity Master Mix (ApexBio Technology, K1039) and specific PCR primers. PCR primer sequences:

CD47-F: 5′-CTGTGGTCATCCCTTGCATC-3’;

CD47-R: 5′-GAAACACGAGTGCCAGACTCA-3’.

The amplified products were purified and subjected to T7 endonuclease I digestion (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to evaluate insertion–deletion (indel) frequencies. Digested fragments were analyzed by 2 % agarose gel electrophoresis, and indel formation efficiency was quantified using ImageJ (version 2.1.5.0). CD47 gene editing efficiency was assessed both in vitro and in vivo. Sanger sequencing was performed by General Bio Corporation.

In vivo tumor targeting and biodistribution: All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics Approval No. 202412A043). For biodistribution studies, BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 107 CT26 cells into the left flank. Once tumors reached ∼100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6) and administered EGFP-labeled CLBR or CLBRP via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence imaging was conducted at 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection. At 48 h, mice were euthanized, and major organs along with tumor tissues were harvested for ex vivo imaging.

In vivo antitumor therapy and immune cell detection: BALB/c mice bearing CT26 tumors were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6): PBS, free bufalin, CLP, CLBP, and CLBRP. Treatments were administered intravenously every three days over a 21-day period. Tumor volume and body weight were recorded throughout the treatment course. At study completion, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and imaged. Major organs—including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys—were collected for histopathological evaluation via H&E staining. Single-cell suspensions from tumor tissues and lymph nodes were prepared, and immune cell populations were analyzed using FCM with fluorescence-labeled anti-mouse antibodies targeting surface markers. In treated CT26 tumor-bearing mice, the residual subcutaneous primary tumors were surgically removed, followed by the establishment of both lung metastasis and tumor rechallenge models. Specifically, CT26 cells were intravenously injected post-surgery to induce a lung metastasis model, while additional CT26 cells were subcutaneously inoculated on the contralateral flank to establish a tumor rechallenge model, allowing evaluation of the post-treatment antimetastatic efficacy and immune memory response.

Tumor staining and immunohistochemistry: Tumor samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 2 μm slices for H&E staining. IHC analysis of tumor sections was performed for Caspase 3, CD4+, and CD8+ markers according to standard immunohistochemistry protocols. Immunofluorescence staining for Ki67, TUNEL, and CD47 in tumor slices was performed following standard immunofluorescence protocols.

Antimetastatic study: BALB/c mice bearing CT26 tumors were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6): PBS, CLP, and CLBRP. After different treatments, 100 μL of PBS containing 3 × 105 CT26 cells was injected into the tail vein to induce lung metastasis. Treatment was continued for 2 weeks in a manner similar to that described above. At the end of the pharmacodynamics experiment, the tumor and lung tissues were isolated, and the visible metastatic lung nodules were quantified.

In vivo biosafety evaluation: To assess biosafety, healthy 8-week-old female BALB/c mice were intravenously administered CLBRP. After treatment, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and blood samples were collected. Organs were processed for H&E staining to evaluate potential histopathological alterations. Serum biochemical parameters, including ALT, AST, ALP, BUN, TP, and ALB, were measured to assess liver and kidney function.

Statistical analysis: Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9.1.0. Differences between two groups were assessed via an two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's two-tailed LSD multiple-comparisons test was employed. When the effects of time and treatment were analyzed, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied. Survival analysis was conducted via the Kaplan‒Meier method, and differences were evaluated via log-rank tests. The data are shown as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical significance was defined as ∗P < 0.05 and ∗∗P < 0.01.
