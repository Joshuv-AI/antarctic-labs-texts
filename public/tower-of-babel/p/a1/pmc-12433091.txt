The COURSE trial and beyond: rethinking chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation management with biologics
Authors: Fumihiro Kashizaki, Hao Chen
Published: 2025

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the fourth leading cause of death worldwide, resulting in approximately 3.5 million deaths annually and accounting for 5% of global mortality. It also imposes a significant economic burden on healthcare systems (1).

COPD is primarily caused by cigarette smoking, but environmental pollutants, gas exposure, and genetic factors such as α1-antitrypsin deficiency also contribute (2). These factors lead to persistent airway and lung damage, characterized by airflow limitation (forced expiratory volume in one second/forced vital capacity, FEV1/FVC <0.7) and symptoms including chronic cough, sputum production, and dyspnea (2).

Managing COPD exacerbations and comorbidities—including lung cancer, interstitial pneumonia, and cardiovascular diseases—can significantly improve patient outcomes (2). Approximately 15% to 40% of COPD cases involve type 2 inflammation, which has been linked to an increased risk of exacerbations (3,4). This has sparked academic interest in applying bronchial asthma (BA) treatment strategies (5,6) to COPD management. For instance, triple inhaled therapy, comprising inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), long-acting β2-agonists (LABA), and long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA), has shown promise in reducing exacerbations (6). However, even with triple inhaled therapy, exacerbation rates decrease by only about 50% (6), highlighting the need for additional therapeutic strategies. In 2024, the BOREAS (3) and NOTUS (4) trials, alongside pooled analyses (7), demonstrated that dupilumab significantly reduced exacerbations in COPD patients receiving triple inhaled therapy (i.e., 98.2% of participants), all of whom had at least one confirmed blood eosinophil count (BEC) ≥300/µL (Table 1). These findings have fueled further interest in biologic therapies for COPD. However, interleukin-5 (IL-5) inhibitors have exhibited inconsistent results in preventing COPD exacerbations (Table 1) (8,9), casting doubt on their role in treatment. Additionally, studies indicate that solely controlling BEC may not consistently prevent exacerbations, underscoring the need for further research into COPD pathophysiology. Recent findings have heightened interest in biologics that target COPD exacerbations regardless of baseline BEC. Several promising candidates are currently under investigation, including anti-IL-33 (10), anti-ST2 (11), and anti-thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) antibodies (Table 1) (12).

Among these candidates, tezepelumab—an anti-TSLP antibody—has been approved for severe BA. A preclinical study revealed that TSLP expression is higher in the airway epithelium and bronchial smooth muscle of patients with COPD than in healthy individuals, with further upregulation in response to inflammatory stimuli (12). Based on these findings, it was expected that tezepelumab would broadly suppress exacerbations in COPD, a heterogeneous condition.

Recently, Singh et al. reported results from the phase 2a COURSE trial (NCT04039113), which evaluated the efficacy of tezepelumab in reducing COPD exacerbations (13). This study enrolled patients aged between 40 and 80 years with an FEV1 of 20–80% of the predicted normal value who were receiving triple inhaled maintenance therapy. Participants were randomized to receive either tezepelumab 420 mg (n=165) or placebo (n=168) via subcutaneous injection every four weeks for 52 weeks. The primary endpoint was the annualized rate of moderate or severe COPD exacerbations (13).

Unfortunately, the results of the tezepelumab trial did not demonstrate a significant reduction in exacerbations compared to placebo. The annualized rate of moderate or severe COPD exacerbations over 52 weeks was 1.75 with tezepelumab versus 2.11 with placebo {rate ratio: 0.83 [90% confidence interval (CI): 0.64–1.06]; P=0.10 (one-sided)}. However, a post-hoc analysis revealed favorable results in specific subgroups. Among patients with BEC ≥150/µL, the rate ratio was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.43–0.93). Moreover, in patients with both BEC ≥300/µL and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) ≥25 parts per billion (ppb), the rate ratio was 0.37 (95% CI: 0.06–0.45), suggesting a potential benefit in select populations (13).

Several factors may explain why the COURSE trial did not yield the same results as the BOREAS and NOTUS trials, underscoring the challenges in evaluating the efficacy of biologics in COPD trials. The first major factor is the nature of the trials: COURSE was a phase 2 study, whereas BOREAS and NOTUS were phase 3 trials. Moreover, COURSE enrolled fewer than half the number of patients compared to BOREAS and NOTUS. In contrast to the phase 3 BOREAS and NOTUS trials, the COURSE trial investigating tezepelumab was an exploratory phase 2 study and not powered to detect statistically significant differences in exacerbation outcomes. Rather, it was designed to identify potential efficacy signals and guide future research directions.

Second, a common consideration in clinical trials investigating the effects of biologics in COPD is the inclusion criteria and stratified analysis based on baseline BEC. BOREAS and NOTUS included patients who had a documented history of BEC ≥300/µL at least once (7), whereas COURSE stratified patients by BEC at enrollment to ensure a balanced distribution across study groups (Table 1) (13). These analyses have largely been guided by findings from severe BA trials. Variations in inclusion criteria may have led to differences in baseline eosinophil counts across these study populations.

Moreover, recent research suggests that these criteria may require refinement. Cabrera et al. evaluated BEC using the Mensil criteria and found distinct differences between BA and COPD patients (14). Inflammatory eosinophils (iEos), a proinflammatory subset characterized by Siglec-8+, CD62Llow, and CD123high expression, were found to be prevalent in BA patients (25.3%±15%) but significantly lower in COPD patients (0.7%±1.1%). Instead, COPD patients exhibited an increase in resident eosinophils (rEos), a regulatory subset defined by Siglec-8+, CD62Lhigh, and CD123low expression that is thought to play a homeostatic role in lung tissue (14). Although no direct association between dupilumab or tezepelumab and iEos or rEos has been demonstrated, differences in their mechanisms of action may have influenced the results of the trial. Further analysis revealed that iEos in BA patients had high IL-5 receptor expression, whereas rEos exhibited lower expression levels (14).

In the SPIROMICS cohort study, Martinez et al. analyzed bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid and reported that IL-5 receptor expression was lower in current smokers compared to former smokers (15). In addition to smoking, radon exposure has emerged as a potential contributor to eosinophilia (16). A study conducted on children aged 4–13 years, with persistent BA attending inner-city schools in the northeastern United States, found a positive association between radon exposure and IL-5, a key T-helper 2 (Th2) cytokine (17). Furthermore, radon exposure has been linked to increased morbidity and mortality in COPD patients (17). Thus, these findings suggest that the source of eosinophilia—whether smoking, environmental exposure, or other factors—may influence IL-5 release and warrant further investigation. Although many of these studies are small-scale and require additional validation, they highlight the need for more refined patient stratification. These studies were conducted in a steady-state condition, whereas the exacerbation state presents a different disease profile. Although BEC is widely used as a biomarker in COPD management, it does not directly reflect lung tissue eosinophils; therefore, its interpretation in predicting treatment response should be made with caution.

The third major factor is the nature of COPD exacerbations themselves, which presents a significant challenge. Although exacerbations are commonly used as primary endpoints in clinical trials, they arise from a variety of causes. Given this heterogeneity, it is unlikely that biologics are universally effective against all types of exacerbation phenotypes. Several clinical trials, including GALATHEA, TERRANOVA, and ABRA (18), have contributed to a deeper understanding of exacerbation subtypes and evaluated the effects of benralizumab on COPD exacerbations. While these trials included BA patients and were not exclusively focused on COPD, they provided some valuable insights. GALATHEA and TERRANOVA (8) stratified patients by steady-state BEC levels, while ABRA used a BEC threshold of ≥300/µL during exacerbations as an inclusion criterion. Results varied across trials, with GALATHEA and TERRANOVA failing to show consistent and significant efficacy in reducing COPD exacerbations. However, ABRA reported a greater reduction in treatment failure rates at 90 days in the benralizumab group compared to the control group. At 90 days, treatment failures occurred in 39 (74%) of 53 patients in the prednisolone group and 47 (45%) of 105 in the pooled-benralizumab group [odds ratio 0.26 (95% CI: 0.13–0.56); P<0.001]. Notably, 52% of ABRA participants had a BEC ≥600/µL, a median FeNO of 41 ppb during exacerbations, and a median of four exacerbations in the past 12 months. More importantly, the trial excluded patients with fever ≥38 °C or radiographic evidence of pneumonia, ensuring that the enrolled population primarily experienced exacerbations driven by eosinophilic inflammation. Approximately half of the participants were BA patients, while 32% had COPD and 12% had BA + COPD. These exacerbation-phase data, which include not only BEC but also exacerbation frequency and FeNO levels, suggest that benralizumab may be effective in a more precisely defined subgroup (19). Furthermore, the inclusion criterion in BOREAS and NOTUS—COPD patients who had ever recorded a BEC ≥300/µL—raises the possibility that patients experiencing mild exacerbations with transient BEC elevation were included.

Fourth, variation in the definition of former and current smokers across studies may influence treatment efficacy, as suggested in previous trials. Similarly, phase 2 trials of biologics targeting IL-33 (10) or its receptor ST2 (11), now undergoing phase 3 trials, demonstrated efficacy specifically in former smokers (risk ratio 0.66, 95% CI: 0.49–0.89; I2 =5.3%) (16), though controversially (17). Additionally, a Korean observational study suggested that current smoking reduces plasma IL-33 levels (20). Findings from the IMPACT trial, which examined triple inhaled therapy with ICS, LABA, and LAMA, further support the influence of smoking status on treatment response. Former smokers experienced a 30% reduction in COPD exacerbations, whereas current smokers only had a 14% reduction (6). These findings are consistent with those of a recent retrospective study evaluating the effects of smoking cessation in patients receiving triple inhaler therapy (21), which reported improvements in lung function following six months of smoking cessation, suggesting that the degree of benefit may vary depending on the duration of cessation. Future research may benefit from classifying patients based on BEC composition (iEos versus rEos) and smoking status (current, former, or non-smoker). Additionally, standardizing the definitions of “current smoker” and “former smoker” across clinical trials (7,8,13) may be necessary to ensure consistency in study outcomes.

A fifth factor to consider is the distinction between eosinophilic COPD (COPD-E), smoking asthma (SA), and BA + COPD, as well as the reliability of self-reported BA history. In recent clinical trials investigating biologics for COPD, past BA history, significant airway reversibility (defined as an FEV1 change of >400 mL and >15%), and BEC ≥300/µL have been used as inclusion criteria (2). However, differentiating between COPD-E, SA, and BA + COPD remains challenging, as frequently reported in the literature (2,9).

This difficulty may be attributed to the dynamic nature of disease progression. For instance, preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) has been increasingly recognized as an intermediate stage in the development of obstructive lung disease, characterized by an FEV1/FVC ratio ≥0.7 with an FEV1 <80% of the predicted value (22). Notably, 32.6% of individuals with PRISm progress to COPD within four to five years, which suggests that airflow limitation evolves and may complicate disease classification (22). As regards FEV1 (Table 1), patients enrolled in the COURSE trial had more severe COPD compared to those in the BOREAS and NOTUS trials. This difference in disease severity may also have influenced the outcomes observed in each study. Furthermore, the reliability of self-reported BA history remains uncertain. The recently published NOVELTY cohort study highlighted this issue, noting that the historical labels of “BA” and “COPD” used in clinical practice do not reliably distinguish clinically distinct populations (23). These findings emphasize the need for more precise diagnostic criteria and objective measures to improve patient classification in COPD studies, either by refining diagnostic criteria or redefining disease subtypes based on biologic response (treatable traits).

The final factor to consider is the mechanism of action of the drug. Dupilumab inhibits the IL-4 receptor, thereby blocking the physiological effects of both IL-4 and IL-13. This action may have significant implications for COPD as well. In murine models from basic research, IL-4 derived from basophils has been shown to induce the differentiation of infiltrating monocytes into matrix metalloproteinase-12 (MMP-12)-producing macrophages, leading to alveolar wall destruction and emphysema (24). Furthermore, research utilizing transgenic mouse models has demonstrated that IL-13 derived from eosinophils similarly promotes MMP-12-producing macrophages and induces emphysema (25). While the extent to which these mechanisms occur in humans remains unclear, it is possible that the clinical benefits observed with dupilumab—including improved airway smooth muscle relaxation, reduced airway remodeling, and decreased mucus plug formation—may also extend to emphysema suppression, although this hypothesis is primarily based on animal studies and requires further validation in human subjects. Conversely, research on tezepelumab is limited. However, some basic studies have reported that sputum TSLP levels correlate with both sputum eosinophil counts and pack-years of smoking, with higher TSLP levels observed in current and former smokers compared to non-smokers (26). Moreover, in recent years, there has been growing interest in patient-reported outcomes (PROMs), and a meta-analysis focusing on PROMs found that only dupilumab demonstrated a significant benefit over placebo (27). However, as Table 1 shows, there is considerable heterogeneity in patient populations and inclusion criteria across these biologics trials in COPD, underscoring the need for further research to validate these findings. Although COPD exacerbations are known to be associated with increased mortality, previous data suggest that biologics have demonstrated less pronounced effects in reducing exacerbations in COPD compared to those observed in BA.

The COURSE trial did not demonstrate a significant reduction in the annualized rate of moderate or severe COPD exacerbations with tezepelumab treatment, which was the study’s primary endpoint. However, comparisons between the COURSE, BOREAS, and NOTUS trials have highlighted important considerations not only for COPD but also for biologic trials in severe BA. Although BA has been more extensively studied than COPD, tezepelumab has demonstrated unexpected efficacy (28). Thus, ongoing trials of tezepelumab in COPD may offer valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms behind these findings. Based on the findings of the COURSE trial, it is anticipated that, despite varying FeNO values associated with smoking status, tezepelumab will be effective in patients with a history of BEC ≥300/µL and FeNO >25 ppb, although this may also depend on FEV1 and the number of exacerbations. Subsequent phase 3 trials will likely focus on identifying the specific patient population in which tezepelumab can effectively reduce COPD exacerbations.

Additionally, key questions remain regarding the role of biologics in preventing COPD exacerbations: To what extent can they reduce mortality risk? How cost-effective are they? While a retrospective study has explored these aspects for certain biologics (29), further research is necessary to fully address these critical issues.
