The role of native defects in organic-inorganic hybrid zinc halide luminescent materials
Authors: Yan Zhang, Qiwu Liu, Rongkang Wang, Xu Li, Xiaorui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrganic-inorganic hybrid metal halides have attracted considerable attention due to their structural diversity and promising optoelectronic properties. Although photoluminescence has been observed in hybrid zinc-based halides, the underlying emission mechanisms remain a topic of debate. In this study, we synthesized three phenylethylamine hybrid zinc halides, (C8H12N)2ZnX4 (X = Cl, Br, I), which feature zero-dimensional (0D) structures consisting of isolated [ZnX4]2− tetrahedral units surrounded by organic C8H12N⁺ cations. Upon photoexcitation, these compounds exhibit blue-white emission. Comprehensive experimental analysis suggests that the emission originates from intrinsic lattice defects, specifically zinc interstitials, and radiative transitions from the conduction band to deep-level states. Our findings confirm the presence of defect states in zinc-based halides and reveal that these defects simultaneously act as luminescence centers and limit the overall emission efficiency, contributing to the relatively low photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) observed in these materials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46769-3.Keywords: Organic-inorganic metal halides, Zinc-based halide, Photoluminescence, Defect emission, Deep levelSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Organic-inorganic metal halide luminescent materials have recently attracted significant attention and achieved considerable progress due to their remarkable properties, including excellent structure tunability, high carrier mobility, superior photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), and impressive electroluminescence and photoluminescence efficiencies1–4. In contrast to three-dimensional (3D) luminescent materials, low-dimensional counterparts often exhibit broader emission spectra owing to their unique structural features, leading to white-light emission5–8. Particularly noteworthy are zero-dimensional (0D) hybrid luminescent materials, which show exceptional performance and have the potential to rival traditional rare-earth-based phosphors in white light-emitting diode (WLED) applications9–12.

Numerous 0D organic-inorganic hybrid metal halides have been reported to date13–16. Initial research focused primarily on lead-based halides due to their favorable photovoltaic properties15,17–19. However, the toxicity of Pb has limited their further development. Consequently, the search for ideal Pb substitutes has centered on less toxic and stable materials with exceptional optoelectronic performance. In this context, Sn2+,18 Sb3+19,20, Cu+21,22, Bi3+23, and Zn2+24,25 have been explored as potential replacements for Pb2+. Among these, zinc-based materials have drawn considerable interest due to their low cost and environmental friendliness26–29. In 2019, a white-light emissive material (C5H7N2)2ZnBr₄ was synthesized with a PLQY of 19.18%, and its emission was attributed to excitons localized on C₅H₇N₂⁺ and self-trapping excitons (STEs)24. Subsequently, Amira Samet et al.30 reported an up-conversion material, (HQ)2[ZnCl4], demonstrating that its emission involves energy and charge transfer between inorganic ions and organic cations. Romain Gautier and co-workers31 further suggested that specific hydrogen bonds could facilitate the formation of VK centers, thereby enhancing luminescent properties, as exemplified by a series of (DMP)ZnX4 (X = Cl, Br) compounds. Following this, the photoluminescence mechanism of an organic zinc halide, [(N-AEPz)ZnCl4]Cl (N-AEPz = N-aminoethylpiperazine), was explored32, revealing that its emission arises from interactions between organic cations and inorganic clusters. Zhou et al.33 proposed that emissions in most zinc-based compounds originate from defects, although this assertion lacks substantial evidence. In earlier studies, Zeng et al.34 synthesized a series of zinc-based materials with notably low luminescence efficiency. However, the incorporation of Cu⁺ resulted in a significant improvement in their PLQY. To date, the luminescence efficiency of pristine organic zinc-based halides remains at most approximately 60%12,29,35,36, which is substantially lower than that of other organic metal halides (e.g., Sb- or Mn-based compounds can approach near-unity PLQY). Therefore, it is imperative to elucidate the underlying cause of this phenomenon.

In this study, three 0D zinc-based halides, (C8H12N)2ZnX4 (X = Cl, Br, I), were synthesized to investigate the origin of the generally low luminescence efficiency observed in hybrid zinc-based halides. Similar to most reported hybrid zinc-based halides, these three compounds exhibited relatively low PLQYs of 11.58%, 5.74%, and 2.12%, respectively. A comprehensive investigation of their luminescence mechanisms suggests that the photoluminescence of these compounds primarily arises from Zn interstitial defect states and transitions from the conduction band to deep-level defects. These findings demonstrate that the presence of such defect states significantly limits the luminescence efficiency of hybrid Zn-based halides, accounting for their generally poor performance.

Materials and methods

Reagents used

The following chemicals were used as received without further purification: Zinc chloride (ZnCl2, AR), zinc bromide (ZnBr2, 99.9%, Aladdin), zinc iodide (ZnI2, 99%, Innochem), phenylethylammonium (PEA, 98%, Innochem), hydrogen chloride (HCl, 37%), hydrogen bromide (HBr, 48%), hydroiodic acid (HI, 55–58%, Macklin) and hypophosphorous acid (H3PO2, 50% w/w).

Crystal growth

(C8H12N)2ZnCl4 single crystals were synthesized using a simple slow cooling method. First, ZnCl2 and PEA were each dissolved in 1 mL HCl. The solutions were stirred at 80 ℃ for 1 h and then mixed together. After slow cooling to room temperature, abundant white crystals were obtained. (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 single crystals were synthesized via the same method. For (C8H12N)2ZnI4, H3PO2 was added to prevent the oxidation of HI.

Halide-rich treatment

The powder samples were annealed at 80 °C for 4 h in a halide-rich atmosphere generated by thermal decomposition of excess NH4X (X = Cl, Br, I) in a sealed tube. After treatment, the samples were characterized under the same measurement conditions as the original samples.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction was conducted on a SuperNova diffractometer with graphite-monochromatized Cu Kα radiation at 294 K. Data processing was performed by CrystalClear software. The structures of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 and (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 were solved by direct methods and refined by full-matrix least-squares methods on F2 using SHELXTL software package. Non-hydrogen atoms were located from difference Fourier maps and refined with anisotropic displacement parameters. The hydrogen atom positions were placed in geometrically calculated positions and allowed to ride on the parent atoms. Crystallographic data and structure refinement are given in Tables S1, S2. The selected Zn–Cl bond lengths are given in Table S3. Single crystals of (C8H12N)2ZnI4 suitable for X‑ray diffraction could not be obtained.

Powder X-ray diffraction

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) measurements were carried out on powder samples of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4. A Rigaku MiniFlex600 system equipped with a D/tex detector and a Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation source was used for these measurements. Scans were performed in the 5–80° (2θ) range, with a step size of 0.02°.

Thermal analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) measurements were carried out on a TA Instruments SDT650 unit. Measurements were conducted using 90 µL alumina crucibles containing 10–11 mg of sample under a 100 mL/min flow of nitrogen in the 50–600 °C range with a 5 °C/min heating rate.

Optical absorption

Absorption spectra of the three single crystals were recorded on a Perkin-Elmer Lambda 900 UV-Vis-NIR spectra photometer at room temperature. BaSO4 was used as the 100% reflectance reference.

Photoluminescence

Room-temperature photoluminescence excitation (PLE) and PLQY measurements were performed on polycrystalline powder for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and (C8H12N)2ZnI4, using a HORIBA Jobin Yvon Fluorolog-3 spectrofluorometer using a xenon lamp and Quanta-φ integrating sphere. The two-curve method was employed over a range of 280–720 nm. Excitation wavelengths were set to 387 nm for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, 374 nm for (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and 372 nm for (C8H12N)2ZnI4. Finely ground powder samples were placed in the sample cell and measured under ambient conditions (room temperature, air). To ensure reproducibility, each compound was independently synthesized at least three times, and three measurements were performed on each batch. The instrument was calibrated using standard reference materials.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy

EPR spectra were recorded on a Bruker A300 spectrometer to confirm the presence of Zn interstitial defects.

Computational methods

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the projected augmented wave (PAW) method as implemented in the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) to investigate electronic band structures, defect formation energies. Spin polarized generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) parametrization for the exchange-correlation energy. The plane‑wave basis set energy cutoff was 520 eV, and a 4 * 4 * 3 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh was used. Long-range van der Waals interactions were taken into account using the Tkatchenko and Scheffler (TS) scheme. Force convergence was set to 0.01 eV Å−1. Hydrogen bonding interactions were analyzed using the NCIPLOT program to visualize noncovalent interaction regions.

Results and discussion

Crystals structures

For comparison, chlorides, bromides, and iodides were synthesized using a straightforward volatile solvent method (detailed in the Supporting Information). The crystal structures of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 and (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 were analyzed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD), with the results shown in Fig. 1 and Tables S1–S4. Both compounds crystallize in the monoclinic space group P2₁/c. Each [ZnX4]2- tetrahedron is surrounded by PEA+ cations, forming a zero-dimensional (0D) structure (Fig. 1a,b), consistent with previous reports. Notably, the amino groups in (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 are linked to the tetrahedron via hydrogen bonds N-H···Cl, with bond lengths of 2.44 and 2.49 Å and bond angles of 162.60 and 153.74°, respectively (Fig. 1c). In contrast, the hydrogen bonds in (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 are longer (2.815 and 2.960 Å), indicating weaker interactions (Fig. 1d). It is well known that hydrogen bonding can induce strong distortion of the excited state, leading to a significant pseudo-Stokes shift in the photoluminescence spectrum37. Therefore, hydrogen bonding may account for the differences in optical properties and stability between the two materials. Only polycrystalline samples could be obtained for (C8H12N)2ZnI4, as the synthesis of the iodide proved challenging. The structure of (C8H12N)2ZnI4 was confirmed through powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis and comparison with (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 and (C8H12N)2ZnBr4. As expected, some diffraction peaks shifted to smaller angles from chloride to iodide, with the main peaks resembling those of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 and (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 (Fig. 2), confirming the successful synthesis of (C8H12N)2ZnI4.

Optical properties

The photophysical properties of these three 0D materials were investigated using UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy, photoluminescence excitation (PLE), and photoluminescence (PL) emission spectra. The UV-Vis absorption spectra of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and (C8H12N)2ZnI4, depicted in Fig. 3a, are strikingly similar, indicating that halogen variation and the presence of hydrogen bonds have minimal impact on their absorption properties. The PLE and PL spectra of the three compounds, recorded at room temperature and shown in Fig. 3b, exhibit broad emission bands with peaks at 490 nm for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, 467 nm for (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and 461 nm for (C8H12N)2ZnI4. Notably, in contrast to most metal halides, the PL emissions of (C8H12N)2ZnX4 exhibit slight blue shifts when the halide is varied from Cl to Br and to I, a phenomenon also observed in copper halides38, indicating an increase in emission energy. Further insights into the recombination dynamics were obtained by measuring the PL decay curves (Fig. 3c). All decay curves were fitted with a biexponential function, as detailed in Table S5. For (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, the fast decay component (τ1) is 1.3 ns, and the slow decay component (τ2) is 6.3 ns, yielding an average lifetime (τave) of 3.1 ns. For (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4, the fast decay components are 0.9 ns and 0.58 ns, and the slow decay components are 5.5 ns and 3.8 ns, respectively, resulting in τave of 2.3 ns and 1.1 ns. It is well known that a longer recombination lifetime can increase the diffusion length, thereby improving optoelectronic performance39, suggesting that the longer PL lifetime of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 indicates superior luminescence potential. Consequently, (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 achieved a higher PLQY of 11.58%, compared to 5.74% for (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and 2.12% for (C8H12N)2ZnI4 (Fig. 3d), consistent with the observed trends in PL lifetime. Nonetheless, the PLQYs of these three zinc-based materials remain suboptimal, as is the case for most hybrid zinc-based halides.

To elucidate the origin of the weak emission, the luminescence mechanism of these three materials was further investigated. It is widely accepted that crucial features of STEs emission include a significant Stokes shift, broad emission, and a long lifetime. In this study, we observed a substantial Stokes shift of approximately 90 nm and a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 130 nm, both of which are indicative of STE emission. However, the PL decay curves of all three compounds exhibit lifetimes in the nanosecond range, whereas STE emission typically occurs on the microsecond timescale. This discrepancy suggests that the observed luminescence does not originate from STEs. Instead, hydrogen bonds in the crystal structure, which can significantly distort the excited state, may be responsible. This hypothesis is supported by the observation of a large pseudo-Stokes shift in the photoluminescence spectra. To gain further insight, temperature-dependent photoluminescence (PL) spectra of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 and (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 were recorded over the range of 80–300 K (Fig. S1). The integrated PL intensity and FWHM extracted from these data are shown in Fig. S2. The emission peak position remained nearly constant across the entire temperature range, while both the integrated intensity and FWHM exhibited only minimal variation. These characteristics are inconsistent with STE emission, which typically shows pronounced peak shifts due to strong exciton–phonon coupling, significant thermal quenching, and marked broadening with increasing temperature. Excitation-power-dependent PL measurements, shown in Fig. 4a, revealed that for (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4, the emission intensity gradually saturates with increasing excitation power, a behavior characteristic of emission from permanent defects. Interestingly, for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, the emission intensity increases linearly with excitation power, in contrast to that observed for (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4. To further probe this difference, the power-dependent PL data were analyzed using the equation I ≈ Lk, where I is the integrated PL intensity, L is the excitation power, and k is an exponent ranging from 0 to 2 that helps distinguish different emission origins. Typically, k = 2 indicates recombination between free electrons and holes, 1 < k < 2 suggests free or bound exciton decay, and k < 1 is associated with defect-related emission40,41. For (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, the calculated k value is 0.803 (Fig. S3), indicating that its emission is likely linked to native defects. To further validate the involvement of defects, all three samples were subjected to halide-rich treatment, and their PLQY and lifetimes were measured before and after treatment. After halide-rich treatment, the PLQY decreased from 11.52% to 7.82% for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, from 5.66% to 4.13% for (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and from 2.03% to 1.68% for (C8H12N)2ZnI4 (Fig. S4). Consistently, the average PL lifetimes also decreased for all samples (Table S6). The significant reduction in both PLQY and lifetime upon halide-rich treatment suggests that defect passivation reduces the density of radiative defect centers, supporting the assignment of the observed emission to defect-related recombination.

(c) Gaussian fitting curves of the emission spectra. (d) Schematic illustration of the proposed exciton dynamics for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) is a well-established technique for investigating native defects in solid-state materials. Accordingly, EPR spectra of the three compounds were measured, with the results presented in Figs. S5 and S6. The spectra exhibited a clear EPR signal for each material, supporting the hypothesis that the emission originates from intrinsic defects. The g-factor measurements for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and (C8H12N)2ZnI4 were 1.962, 1.965, and 1.971, respectively, indicating the presence of unpaired electrons. According to previous reports32,42, EPR signals with g-factors around 1.96 ± 0.02 are typically associated with Zn-related defects, including donor defects such as Zni••, Zni•, and ZniX, as well as acceptor defects such as VZn″ and VZn′. To further elucidate the nature of these defects, excitation-dependent PL measurements were performed on all samples, as shown in Figs. 4b and S7. When the excitation energy is slightly below the optimum value (λ = 387 nm, Ex = 3.2 eV), a broad blue emission centered at 490 nm is observed, indicating that blue emission can be excited even below the bandgap. As the excitation energy increases (E ≥ Ex), an additional emission peak emerges at approximately 550 nm and progressively intensifies. This behavior is also observed in (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4, where shorter-wavelength emissions can be excited at energies marginally above the optimal excitation. For detailed analysis, the emission spectra of all three compounds were fitted with Gaussian functions (Figs. 4c and S8). The fitting reveals two distinct emission bands: one in the blue region and another in the green region, which together constitute the overall emission profile. Among the identified defects, only Zni is a shallow donor, with its defect level likely situated just below the conduction band edge, as supported by numerous theoretical and experimental studies43–46. Consequently, the initial state observed in our measurements may be associated with Zn interstitials, potentially involving several lower-energy derivative levels that are either localized or coupled with other defects. This suggests that the blue emission can be attributed to transitions from Zni states (located slightly below the conduction band) to the valence band. The origin of the green emission was further investigated. Our data indicate that when the excitation wavelength is 390 nm, the green emission at 550 nm is almost absent. This indicates that the green emission band requires excitation energy exceeding the bandgap to become active. Based on these observations, we propose distinct origins for the blue and green emissions, as illustrated in Fig. 4d. The blue emission can be excited under various conditions (E < Eg, E = Eg, E > Eg), whereas the green emission is not effectively excited when E ≤ Eg. These findings indicate that the blue emission arises from Zn interstitial defect states, while the green emission originates from transitions from the conduction band to deeper energy levels.

To further validate the assignment to Zn interstitial defects, we performed DFT calculations of defect formation energies and electronic band structures. As summarized in Table S7, under Zn-rich, halide-poor conditions, donor defects (Zni••, Zni•, ZniX) exhibit lower formation energies than acceptor defects (VZn″ and VZn′) across all three compounds, confirming the thermodynamic favorability of Zn interstitials. The calculated band structures (Fig. S9) yield bandgap values consistent with experimental trends. Moreover, the Zni defect level lies approximately 0.15 eV below the conduction band minimum, with a radiative recombination energy of 2.68 eV (~462 nm), closely matching the observed blue emission at 490 nm. These computational results further support the assignment of the blue emission to Zni-related defects. From the above analysis, it is evident that defect energy levels can induce luminescence47. To further investigate the role of hydrogen bonding in the properties of these materials, reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis was employed. This analysis provided deeper insights into the noncovalent interactions within the materials under study (see Fig. 5a, b, and S10). In these analyses, regions exhibiting low electron density and low-gradient spikes near zero are observed, appearing as green areas. This indicates the presence of van der Waals interactions within the molecules studied. Notably, the appearance of low electron density at negative values and low-gradient spikes (shown as blue points in the left panels) signifies stabilizing interactions. The transition between blue and green regions likely represents hydrogen bonding interactions, specifically N-H···Cl or N-H···Br, as highlighted in the selected boxes. Interestingly, the weak interactions in (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 appear stronger than those in (C8H12N)2ZnBr4. This observation aligns with the hydrogen bond lengths obtained from single-crystal structure data. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to elucidate the impact of hydrogen bonding on the thermal stability of these materials. As shown in Fig. 5c, the onset of 5% weight loss for (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, which corresponds to its thermal decomposition temperature, occurs at 248 °C. In contrast, (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4 exhibit thermal decomposition temperatures of 117 °C and 138 °C, respectively (see Figs. 5d and S11). The thermal stability of (C8H12N)2ZnCl4 is markedly higher than that of (C8H12N)2ZnBr4 and (C8H12N)2ZnI4. While hydrogen bonding plays a significant role—as supported by DFT-calculated hydrogen bond energies correlating with decomposition temperatures—other factors may also contribute. The Zn–X bond strength decreases in the order Zn–Cl > Zn–Br > Zn–I due to decreasing electronegativity and increasing bond length, and the overall lattice energy also decreases from Cl to I. However, the dramatic drop in decomposition temperature from 248 °C (Cl) to 117 °C (Br) suggests that hydrogen bonding is particularly influential, as changes in bond strength and lattice energy alone would be expected to produce a more gradual trend. Overall, the correlation between hydrogen bond strength, structural rigidity, and PLQY across the halide series suggests that hydrogen bonding—along with halide electronic effects—is a key factor modulating the optical properties. Stronger hydrogen bonding in the chloride leads to enhanced structural rigidity, suppressing non-radiative relaxation and thus improving PLQY.

Conclusions

Three 0D crystalline materials, namely (C8H12N)2ZnCl4, (C8H12N)2ZnBr4, and (C8H12N)2ZnI4, were synthesized using a straightforward cooling crystallization method. These materials exhibited blue-white emission at 490 nm, 467 nm, and 461 nm, with corresponding PLQYs of 11.58%, 5.28%, and 2.6%, respectively. Detailed mechanistic studies, including steady-state PL spectroscopy, UV-Vis absorption, and EPR, revealed that the blue emission originates from radiative recombination at zinc interstitial defect levels, while the green emission is attributed to transitions from the conduction band to deep-level defect states. SC-XRD, TGA, and DFT calculations collectively indicated that hydrogen bonding within the crystals enhances both the optical properties and stability of these materials. The findings confirm the presence of defect states in zinc-based halides and elucidate the role of hydrogen bonds in modulating material properties. Notably, although the PLQY of zinc-based halides has been reported to reach up to ~56% in recent studies, it remains substantially lower than that of Sb- and Mn-based hybrids (which can approach unity), highlighting that intrinsic defects fundamentally limit the luminescence efficiency of zinc-based materials. These insights offer valuable guidance for future research in this area.
