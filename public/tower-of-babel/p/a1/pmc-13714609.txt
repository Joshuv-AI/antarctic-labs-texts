Safety and pharmacokinetics of mosliciguat, a new, inhaled soluble guanylate cyclase activator, in healthy adult subjects
Authors: Johannes Nagelschmitz, Soundos Saleh, Sandra Ligges, Melanie Seidel‐Greven, Eva Becker‐Pelster, Sylvia M Nikkho, Stefanie Boxnick, Wolfgang Timmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimMosliciguat (BAY 1237592), the first soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) activator in a dry powder inhaler (DPI), could potentially treat pulmonary hypertension with a more targeted delivery than systemic sGC stimulators. We report safety and tolerability of mosliciguat in healthy men.MethodsThree randomized, single‐blinded, placebo‐controlled Phase 1 studies of mosliciguat DPI: a single‐dose escalation study (SDES) of mosliciguat 60–4000 μg (n = 78), a multiple‐dose escalation study (MDES) of seven once‐daily doses of 480–2000 μg over 8 days (n = 36) and a multiple‐dose study (MDS) of mosliciguat 1000 μg for 14 days (n = 21).ResultsMost adverse events (AEs) were mild, with no serious AEs or AEs leading to discontinuation. No clinically relevant lung function impairment or change in oxygen saturation was observed. In the MDES, body plethysmography indicated reduced airway resistance 6 h after dosing. Generally, no clinically relevant effect on blood pressure was seen; however, a few events of mild‐to‐moderate orthostatic hypotension occurred with higher doses in the SDES and MDS. In the SDES, there was a small, dose‐dependent heart rate increase with mosliciguat. Increased plasma cyclic guanosine monophosphate levels confirmed target engagement by mosliciguat. Overall, the PK profile of mosliciguat was appropriate for an inhaled therapy, with low systemic exposures and an effect on airway resistance consistent with targeted inhaled lung‐selective delivery.ConclusionMosliciguat DPI was well tolerated in healthy subjects, without AEs related to bronchospasm or airway irritation, with a pharmacodynamic response indicating sGC activation and a bronchodilating action.Keywords: clinical pharmacology, drug safety, pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics

INTRODUCTION

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) encompasses a group of disorders in which loss and obstruction of the pulmonary vascular bed increase pulmonary arterial pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance, which can ultimately lead to right heart failure and death. 1 , 2 , 3 PH is divided into five groups: pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), PH due to left heart disease, PH due to lung diseases and/or hypoxia (PH‐CLD), PH due to pulmonary artery obstructions (mainly chronic thromboembolic PH [CTEPH]) and PH with unclear and/or multifactorial mechanisms. 4 , 5 Conditions underlying PH‐CLD include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and interstitial lung diseases (ILD), such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and other idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs). 5 PH associated with ILD (PH‐ILD) has high morbidity and mortality, 5 , 6 and improved therapies are needed. Despite available therapies in PAH, global mortality remains a concern: although the age‐standardized mortality rate dropped from 0.35 to 0.27 per 100 000 between 1990 and 2021, deaths rose by almost 50%, mainly because of population growth and ageing. 7 Concomitant PH in patients with chronic lung diseases is linked to higher mortality and more rapid disease progression, and treatment options are limited. 5 , 6 One‐year mortality reaches 28.0% in ILD patients with PAH compared to 5.5% in those without. 8 PH exacerbates symptoms such as dyspnoea and fatigue, 9 complicating the management of ILD.

Soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) is a signal transduction enzyme regulating numerous physiological functions, including pulmonary vascular tone. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 In response to nitric oxide (NO), sGC converts guanosine triphosphate into cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), which mediates smooth muscle relaxation. 10 sGC exists in an NO‐sensitive reduced (ferrous) form and an NO‐insensitive oxidized (ferric) form. 14 Altered NO–sGC–cGMP signalling has been implicated in the pathophysiology of PAH, CTEPH and PH‐CLD. 10 , 11 , 12 , 15 Restoration of NO–sGC–cGMP signalling represents a potential treatment modality in these conditions.

Two drug classes have been developed to increase sGC activity: sGC stimulators and sGC activators. 10 , 12 , 16 , 17 , 18 sGC stimulators, including riociguat and vericiguat, target the reduced native form of sGC, directly stimulating reduced native sGC independent of NO and increasing the sensitivity of reduced native sGC to NO. Riociguat improves clinical endpoints in PAH 19 , 20 and CTEPH 21 , 22 and is well tolerated and recommended by current guidelines. 3 Compared with placebo, vericiguat reduces mortality and hospitalization in heart failure 23 and has been added to guidelines. 24 Oxidative stress shifts intracellular sGC from the reduced native form toward the haem‐free oxidized form, which is unresponsive to NO. 25 sGC activators including mosliciguat and runcaciguat activate this form of sGC independently of NO. 11 , 25 In preclinical models, sGC activators have been shown to increase cGMP levels and produce pulmonary vasodilatation, 26 , 27 even under oxidative stress conditions, 28 , 29 and to exert antifibrotic effects in the lung. 30

Mosliciguat is the first sGC activator specifically designed as a dry powder for inhalation. Inhalation of an sGC activator aims to deliver the drug to ventilated lung regions, thereby decreasing pulmonary vasoconstriction and improving gas exchange while reducing the side effects of systemic vasodilators, such as systemic hypotension and decreased blood oxygen saturation. 31 , 32 The structure, synthesis and physicochemical properties of mosliciguat have been previously reported. 33 In animal models of PH, mosliciguat demonstrated dose‐dependent reductions in pulmonary artery pressure, without decreasing systemic arterial blood pressure (BP) or negatively affecting ventilation–perfusion mismatch. 29 Mosliciguat also resulted in dose‐dependent bronchodilation in a bronchoconstriction rat model with improvements in airway resistance and dynamic compliance, suggesting effects on large and small airways. 29

Here we report three randomized, single‐blinded, placebo‐controlled, Phase 1 trials of mosliciguat DPI in healthy subjects: a single‐dose escalation study (SDES; EudraCT 2016‐000824‐24), an 8‐day multiple‐dose escalation study (MDES; EudraCT 2018‐000274‐29) and a 14‐day multiple‐dose study (MDS; Regulatory Agency Identifier Number 140537).

The authors confirm that the principal investigator for the SDES is Stefanie Boxnick, the principal investigator for the MDES is Wolfgang Timmer, and the principal investigators for the MDS are Lu Ann L Bundrant MD, Austin, TX, USA, and Sandra Pagnussat MD, Columbia, MD, USA; the principal investigators had direct clinical responsibility for the subjects.

METHODS

Objectives

The primary objective was to investigate the safety and tolerability of inhaled mosliciguat. Safety assessments included oxygen saturation, lung function, systemic BP, and heart rate (HR). These assessments also served as potential indicators of pharmacodynamic (PD) effects; a reduction in systemic BP could have been indicative of systemic vasodilatation, whereas a reduction in oxygen saturation could have been related to vasodilatation in poorly ventilated lung regions. The secondary objectives were to investigate the plasma pharmacokinetics of mosliciguat to determine how much of the compound was recovered systemically after inhalation, and to measure biomarkers of sGC activation.

The SDES was the first study of mosliciguat in humans to evaluate the safety of this new therapeutic principle by measuring vital signs and parameters that might be affected by the mechanism of action and administration of the new drug by inhalation. Single inhaled doses, increasing stepwise, depending on safety from 60 to 4000 μg, were selected based on lung and systemic exposure, and the highest effective doses without systemic side effects observed in inhalation studies in animals. 29 Safety parameters, including HR, BP, cardiac output (CO) and oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry, were measured to confirm safety and to search for PD signals, and cGMP was assessed as a direct pharmacological effect. The changes in oxygen saturation and the extent of increases in plasma cGMP observed were unexpected from preclinical data with mosliciguat and clinical experience with riociguat. These parameters were therefore measured more intensely in the MDES, for which doses of 480 μg, 1000 μg and 2000 μg were selected as the potential therapeutic doses based on PD and safety data from the SDES. Lung function was assessed by body plethysmography as lung effects were seen in preclinical animal models.

Study design and procedures

All studies were randomized, single‐blinded, placebo‐controlled, parallel group, Phase 1 trials. The SDES was conducted at CRS Clinical Research Services Wuppertal GmbH, Wuppertal, Germany; the MDES at Inamed GmbH, Gauting, Germany (now part of NUVISAN GmbH, Neu‐Ulm, Germany); and the MDS at Pharmaceutical Product Development, LLC, PPD Phase 1 Clinic, Austin, TX, USA, and QPS MRA, South Miami, FL, USA. In all three studies, the dry powder formulation was contained in inhalation‐grade hard capsules of hydroxypropylmethylcellulose, administered with a commercially available, manually operated, breath‐activated, inhaler (Plastiape RS01 Model 7, Plastiape S.p.A., Osnago Lecco, Italy). In the SDES and MDES, ascending doses were administered to new cohorts of subjects only after positive evaluation of the safety, tolerability and exposure of the preceding dose. The SDES investigated single doses of 60–4000 μg. In the MDES, subjects inhaled doses of 480, 1000 or 2000 μg or placebo on Day 1 and Days 3–8. Subjects receiving placebo were included in each dose cohort as a control but were also pooled as a comparator for analysis. The MDS was planned to investigate inhaled mosliciguat 1000 μg once daily (Part A) and 480 μg twice daily (Part B) for 14 days. Part B has not been conducted at present. Further details are shown in Table S1.

All three studies met all local legal and regulatory requirements and were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonization guideline E6: Good Clinical Practice. The SDES was approved by the Ethikkommission der Aerztekammer Nordrhein, Tersteegenstraße 9, 40401 Duesseldorf, Germany (Approval Number 2016192). The MDES was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Bavarian State Medical Association, Mühlbaurstr. 16, 81 677 Munich, Germany (Approval Number 18037). The MDS was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Advarra®, 6100 Merriweather Drive, Suite 600, 21 044, Columbia, USA (Approval Number Pro00056248), and IntegReview Institutional Review Board, 1825 Fortview, Suite 110, 78 704, Austin, TX, USA (approval number not available). All subjects provided written, informed consent before enrolment.

Participants

Subjects were healthy males aged 18–45 years with a body mass index between 18.0 and 29.9 kg·m−2. Subjects of reproductive potential were required to use reliable contraception. Women were excluded because data from reproductive toxicity studies were not yet available and to avoid gender‐related effects on the results. Subjects with airway hyperresponsiveness were excluded.

Assessments

The SDES comprised an ambulant screening visit, an in‐house phase starting 2 days before dosing (Day −2) until Day 4 and a follow‐up visit. The MDES consisted of a screening visit, an in‐house phase comprising the pre‐dose day (Day −1) and the 8‐day treatment phase, an observation period (Days 9 and 10) and a safety follow‐up visit 7–10 days after discharge. The MDS comprised a screening visit, admission to the study site on Day −2, baseline assessments on Day −1, study drug administration on Days 1–14, post‐dosing assessments on Days 15–18 and follow‐up visits on Day 21 and 7–10 days after discharge. Additional details of the study designs, including the timings of the assessments performed, are given in Tables S2–S16.

Adverse events (AEs) were assessed from screening up to 30 days after drug administration. AEs were classified as study drug‐related according to investigator judgement. Safety assessments in all studies included: BP using an oscillometric device, 12‐lead standard electrocardiogram (ECG), pulse oximetry, spirometric measurement of forced expiratory volume in 1 s and clinical laboratory analyses.

The linkage between systemic cGMP signalling and arterial BP/vascular regulation has been described in several publications, 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 and studies of sGC stimulators have investigated plasma or urinary cGMP levels and systemic BP effects in humans and animals. 39 , 40 However, mosliciguat is an inhaled sGC activator intended to act primarily after deposition in the lung, where sGC is highly expressed, thus lowering pulmonary BP. As it was not possible to measure cGMP production in the lung, systemic cGMP was measured as a surrogate marker, even though the correlation between local and systemic levels is not known.

In the SDES, HR was recorded by ECG, whereas in the MDES and MDS, it was obtained over 1 min by Holter monitoring. Impedance cardiography to non‐invasively measure CO, stroke volume (SV) and HR was performed in the SDES and the MDES. Specific airway resistance was measured by body plethysmography in the MDES. Blood samples were collected for analysis of cGMP, plasma renin activity and noradrenaline in the MDES and MDS. Blood samples for pharmacokinetic (PK) analysis were taken in all studies at specified time points on the day or days of dosing, the three subsequent days and the follow‐up visit. Values below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) (0.2 μg·L−1) were not used in the calculation of individual PK parameters including half‐life. However, for the calculation of the summary statistics, PK values below the LLOQ were set to half of their value (0.1 μg·L−1) if at least three quarters of the concentrations at a given time point were above the LLOQ.

Orthostatic assessments

Orthostatic assessments were performed as a sensitive method for signals of functional effects on BP (which may reflect PD effects of mosliciguat). HR and BP were recorded before getting up after the subject had been in the supine position for at least 20 min. Then, the subject moved to the sitting position for 3 min. HR and BP were taken after 1, 2 and 3 min in the sitting position. Thereafter, the subject stood up for 2 min and HR and BP were taken after 1 and 2 min in the standing position. At the end of the procedure, the supine position was taken again and HR and BP were measured after 1, 3 and 5 min in the supine position. A clinically relevant orthostatic reaction like dizziness and earlier termination of the sitting or standing position was noted.

Nomenclature of targets and ligands

In the MDS, AEs occurred in 14 (82%) and 2 (50%) subjects receiving mosliciguat and placebo, respectively (Table 3). Most AEs were mild in intensity. The most common AE was contact dermatitis, reported in five subjects (24%). Medical device site dermatitis, palpitations, dry lip and increased hepatic enzymes were each reported in two subjects (10%). Four subjects (24%) in the mosliciguat group had AEs considered study drug‐related by the investigator: contact dermatitis in two subjects, medical device site dermatitis in one subject and increased hepatic enzymes in one subject. All dermatitis cases resulted from contact with ECG electrodes. The subject with increased hepatic enzymes had elevated gamma‐glutamyltransferase (GGT) at screening and showed increased GGT, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). The peak values of GGT, ALT and AST were 3.1 × upper limit of normal (ULN), 2.4 × ULN and 1.6 × ULN, respectively, all on Day 18. At follow‐up on Day 25, AST was back within normal range, ALT was 1.1 × ULN, and GGT was 2.5 × ULN. Bilirubin values were never outside the normal range, no AE‐related treatment was given, mosliciguat did not have to be interrupted, and no other AEs were reported for this subject. No serious AEs or AEs leading to discontinuation of study drug or death were reported in any study.

HR, BP and pulse oximetry

Overall, HR tended to increase in the first 24 h after dosing with mosliciguat compared with the time‐corresponding pre‐dose values and baseline in all three studies (Figures S1–S3 and the statistical analysis section in the Supporting Information). This effect became more marked at higher doses. The maximum mean (standard deviation [SD]) increases were 18.8 (14.8) bpm at 4000 μg in the SDES, 13.1 (7.1) bpm at 1000 μg on Day 8 in the MDES and 23.5 (14.9) bpm on Day 14 in the MDS. However, in the MDS, HR also tended to increase after placebo. In the SDES, this dose‐related increase in HR was also detected in the first 5 h after dosing by fitting an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA; p < .00161 for doses ≥1000 μg), which was not observed in the MDES and MDS in the first 4 h (ANCOVA: .0946 ≤ p ≤ .6918 for all doses and dosing days)—compare Table S17. The changes in HR were not considered clinically relevant, and no clinically abnormal signs, symptoms or AEs related to HR were reported in any study.

ANCOVA analyses did not indicate a treatment effect on systolic BP (SBP) or diastolic BP (DBP) in any study (Tables S18 and S19).

Slight dose‐dependent decreases in oxygen saturation in the first 24 h after dosing with mosliciguat compared with the time‐corresponding pre‐dose values and baseline were observed in the SDES (Figure S4) and MDES (Figure 2), with a tendency to return toward baseline by 24 h, especially for lower doses. The maximum mean (SD) decreases were −3.0 (2.67/2.39) percentage points at 240 and 1000 μg in the SDES, and −3.16 (3.73) percentage points in the MDES. The decreasing oxygen saturation was also detected by the ANCOVA in the SDES in the first 5 h (p ≤ .0029 for doses ≥120 μg) and in the MDES in the first 4 h (p = .0062 at 2000 μg on Day 1, and p = .0358 and p = .0001 at 1000 and 2000 μg, respectively, on Day 8; Table S20), as well as in the first 24 h (data are not shown). Orthostatic assessments

In the SDES, orthostatic events occurred in one subject each in the placebo and 2000 μg groups, and two subjects in the 3000 and 4000 μg groups. Some of these subjects had more than one orthostatic event (Table S21), resulting in five and four reactions in the 3000 μg group and the 4000 μg group, respectively. All reactions were of mild to moderate intensity. In the subject in the 2000 μg group, the reaction was described as ‘orthostatic collapse’ by the investigator; this subject therefore does not appear under ‘orthostatic hypotension’ in Table 2. In the placebo and 4000 μg groups, the maximum mean (SD) HR changes from the last supine measurement were 16.1 (7.7) and 18.5 (12.0) bpm, respectively, for seated measurements, and 30.7 (10.5) and 35.1 (20.0) bpm, respectively, for standing measurements. The largest mean changes in SBP and DBP from supine to standing were −18 and −4 mmHg, respectively, in the 4000 μg group. In the MDES, no effect of mosliciguat on orthostatic reactions was observed (data are not shown). In the MDS, orthostatic reactions were reported in one subject (25%) receiving placebo on Day 1. With mosliciguat, such reactions were reported in five subjects (29%) on Day −1, five subjects (29%) on Day 1 and one subject (6%) on Day 14. Two AEs related to orthostatic reactions, both mild in intensity, were reported.

Lung function tests

No clinically relevant changes in spirometric parameters were observed in the SDES or the MDS (data are not shown). In the MDES, body plethysmography revealed a slight decrease in total specific airway resistance across the dose groups 6 h post‐dosing (Table 4 and Figure S5). No apparent effects of mosliciguat on forced expiratory volume in 1 s, forced vital capacity, inspiratory vital capacity or expiratory vital capacity were seen in any study (data are not shown).

PK analysis

Due to potential bioanalytical constraints, available PK data in the SDES did not meet quality assurance criteria and therefore are not presented. PK data for Phase 1 studies of mosliciguat have been reported elsewhere. 42 Key PK parameters on Day 8 in the MDES and Day 14 in the MDS are shown in Table 5. In the MDES, the area under the concentration–time curve appeared to increase dose‐proportionally from 1000 to 2000 μg and to be overproportional from 480 to 1000 μg, but the latter observation may have been an artefact resulting from the plasma concentration of mosliciguat falling below the LLOQ 8–12 h after 480‐μg dosing. Maximum observed drug concentration was dose‐proportional across the three dose groups, and median values for time to maximum concentration for all doses of mosliciguat were similar (2.00–2.50 h). Mean elimination half‐life was 6–57 h with multiple dosing. In the MDS, PK parameters of mosliciguat were similar to those seen in the MDES at the same dose (1000 μg), and steady‐state mosliciguat levels were reached after 14 days. Biomarker analysis

Results for the SDES, derived from leftover PK samples, showed dose‐dependent increases in plasma cGMP at doses ≥1000 μg (Figure S6). Peak mean (±SD) values were observed at the follow‐up visit with 60 μg (5.9 ± 1.2 nmol·L−1), 120 μg (5.2 ± 1.1 nmol·L−1), 240 μg (6.1 ± 0.8 nmol·L−1) and 480 μg (5.7 ± 1.4 nmol·L−1); at 8 h with 1000 μg (9.4 ± 3.0 nmol·L−1), 3000 μg (18.4 ± 7.0 nmol·L−1) and 4000 μg (16.8 ± 4.4 nmol·L−1); and at 24 h with 2000 μg (9.5 ± 2.0 nmol·L−1). In the MDES, a dose‐dependent increase in cGMP concentration was observed starting 2 h after inhalation on Day 1, with a peak at 6 h in the 480 and 1000 μg groups and at 8 h in the 2000 μg group. The peak mean (±SD) cGMP concentration was 8.8 ± 1.4, 11.7 ± 1.9 and 16.5 ± 4.2 nmol·L−1 in the 480, 1000 and 2000 μg groups, respectively (Figure 3). On Day 8, peak cGMP was 12.0 ± 2.8, 16.7 ± 3.0 and 32.7 ± 9.5 nmol·L−1 with the 480, 1000 and 2000 μg doses at 6, 6 and 8 h after dosing, respectively (Figure 3). In the MDS, peak mean cGMP concentration was 14.9 ± 3.6 nmol·L−1 8 h post‐dose on Day 1, returning to baseline 24–48 h post‐dose. Up to Day 7, peak levels were reached 8 h after dosing and increased versus Day 1, reaching 24.5 ± 7.4 nmol·L−1 on Day 6. In all studies, the peak level of cGMP was reached at least 4 h after the C max of mosliciguat (2–2.5 h after dosing). 42 Thereafter, peak levels were stable (Figure S7). Effects on plasma renin activity and noradrenaline (see Supporting Information) were small and not considered clinically significant in any study.

DISCUSSION

Inhalation of mosliciguat, a new sGC activator, was well tolerated in healthy subjects, with no impact on lung function at doses up to 4000 μg. No serious or fatal AEs were reported. AEs were generally mild to moderate in severity, were not clearly related to the mechanism of action of mosliciguat and had resolved by study end. No subjects prematurely discontinued mosliciguat due to an AE. Orthostatic reactions during the planned standing test, of mild to moderate intensity, were reported in the SDES at the two highest doses and in the MDS. Rather than a PD effect of mosliciguat, these may reflect increased sensitivity of individual subjects to the orthostatic test. The low incidence of headache and orthostatic hypotension, as well as the absence of a clear dose dependency, precludes concluding that these events were related to the drug.

Small, dose‐dependent decreases in oxygen saturation were observed. They were not considered clinically relevant and were not associated with any AEs. It is possible that some of the variability in saturation may relate to small changes in ventilation–perfusion matching. Other studies have also reported small changes in ventilation–perfusion matching in healthy subjects, for example, in healthy volunteers receiving inhaled iloprost, possibly related to effects on gravity‐dependent pulmonary perfusion. 43

In the SDES, mosliciguat was associated with a dose‐related increase in HR in the first 5 h after dosing, which was not considered clinically relevant. This increase was recorded by HR measurement in ECG registration, which is sensitive to beat‐to‐beat variability. It may have been caused by meal intake and was within the range typically seen in first‐in‐human studies in healthy subjects. Holter monitoring over 1 min as used in the other studies is more reliable and showed lower effects on HR. Lung function test parameters were not negatively affected by the inhalation of mosliciguat; there was no evidence of bronchospasm, cough or signs of airway inflammation. No bronchopulmonary‐related AEs were reported, and the reduction in airway resistance measured by body plethysmography in the MDES suggests improvement in airflow and/or bronchodilation.

Mosliciguat is administered via inhalation, but pulmonary deposition was not quantified in these studies. Instead, systemic drug exposure (PK) was evaluated to characterize absorption into the systemic circulation. The PK results, including the long half‐life, 42 suggest that once‐daily inhalation could be suitable for clinical use, although this remains to be confirmed in clinical trials. This is supported by comparative PK data showing that inhaled mosliciguat exhibited a longer apparent terminal elimination half‐life than oral or intravenous dosing, consistent with a lung reservoir effect and gradual systemic release. 42 The PK data confirm systemic absorption of mosliciguat, but overall, systemic exposure was low. PK C max occurred approximately 2–2.5 h after the dose, 42 whereas the maximum plasma cGMP concentration appeared later. The mechanism causing the delayed cGMP effect is not fully understood, but it may indicate a long duration of action of mosliciguat after inhaled administration.

Some parameters, measured primarily as safety assessments, may also indicate PD effects of mosliciguat. The orthostatic events and the small reduction in oxygen saturation contrast with animal studies, in which inhalation of mosliciguat was associated with reduction of systemic BP only at the highest doses and did not increase ventilation–perfusion mismatch. 29 Some AEs, such as dizziness and headache, may indicate systemic vasodilatation, mainly after 2000–4000 μg doses, during this challenge. PK data indicate that inhalation of mosliciguat produces a depot effect in the lung, from which the drug is released over time. 42 The small decreases observed in oxygen saturation may be related to changes in ventilation–perfusion matching with vasodilation and increased perfusion to regions with poorer ventilation. 44 The continuous pulse oximetry in the MDES did not reveal any greater change in oxygen saturation than the intermittent monitoring in the SDES.

The decrease in airway resistance in the MDES suggests airflow improvement or bronchodilation, which has also been reported in animal studies. 29 The increases in plasma cGMP indicate engagement with the sGC–cGMP pathway by mosliciguat, 10 which could lead to activation of downstream mediators such as protein kinases. 28 , 45 , 46 , 47

Mosliciguat was developed to offer patients with PH the potential advantages of targeted inhaled therapy. 32 This route may be especially relevant for PH‐CLD, but data for PH‐targeted drugs in this population are limited. 5 The inhaled prostanoid treprostinil has been reported to improve exercise capacity in PH‐ILD. 48 The systemically administered endothelin receptor antagonist ambrisentan is contraindicated in patients with IPF, including those with PH—a placebo‐controlled trial in patients with IPF (ARTEMIS‐IPF) was terminated early when it became clear that the primary endpoint would not be met. 49 , 50 Small, open‐label studies reported haemodynamic benefits with riociguat in patients with PH‐ILD 51 and COPD, 52 but riociguat is contraindicated in PH associated with IIPs following negative results of a Phase 2 study. 53 , 54 Inhaled mosliciguat could overcome some limitations of current therapies and become a new treatment option for PH; its bronchodilating action could offer a potential advantage over sGC stimulators.

The limitations of these studies are typical of Phase 1 trials and include small sample sizes, short follow‐up, short dosing period, lack of heterogeneity in the population and all subjects being male. The quality concerns with the PK data are a limitation of the SDES.

In conclusion, dry powder inhalation of mosliciguat in single doses of 60–4000 μg, as well as multiple doses of 480–2000 μg over 8 days and 1000 μg for 14 days, was well tolerated without AEs related to bronchospasm or airway irritation. Evidence of PD effects on cGMP levels and potentially on airway resistance, oxygen saturation and HR was observed. A Phase 1b trial of mosliciguat in patients with untreated PAH or CTEPH reported increases in plasma cGMP and haemodynamic benefits, including a reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance. 55 , 56 A Phase 2 study programme (NCT06635850) is in progress to investigate the PD, pharmacological and clinical effects of mosliciguat in patients with PH‐ILD.
