The relationship between components of the biosafety incident response competence for clinical nursing staff: a network analysis
Authors: Chao Wu, Ning Zha, Kai Zhang, Shuai He, Jing He, Mimi Fu, Xinyan Zhang, Hongjuan Lang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiosafety, which pertains to the prevention of exposure to biological hazards that could pose risks to human health, ecosystems, and the environment, has become a global concern. Nowadays, biosafety threats are widespread and long-standing, posing a severe risk to all human lives. A key measure in tackling biosafety issues is to clarify the competence of clinical nursing staff in responding to biosafety incidents. Nevertheless, little network analysis research has been done on the relationships between the components of clinical nurses’ biosafety incident response competence.PurposeTo better and more accurately help clinical nurses enhance their ability to respond to biosafety incidents, this study examined the network structure of the components that make up biosafety incident response competence.DesignA network analysis.MethodsFrom September to November 2023, we invited 4600 clinical nurses to participate in our study. Eventually, 4338 nurses provided valid responses, with an effective response rate reaching 94.30% (4338/4600). The biosafety incident response competence of nursing staff was assessed using a scale developed by the research team. Network analysis was adopted for statistical analysis.ResultsIn the current network, “Master the correct collection methods of blood culture samples and nasopharyngeal swabs from patients with biological infection; P5: Understand the vaccination of biosafety protective vaccines”, “Possess the ability to assess the harm of pathogenic microorganisms”, “Master the key points of medical record management and record of patients with biological infection” and “Be familiar with the concept of antimicrobial resistance and the use of antimicrobials” have the highest expected influences. In the community of biosafety infection protection abilities, “Master the correct collection methods of blood culture samples and nasopharyngeal swabs from patients with biological infection” has the highest bridge expected influence. In the community of biosafety event monitoring and warning abilities, “Understand the main points and requirements of detection and screening of pathogenic microorganisms and drug-resistant bacteria” has the highest bridge expected influence. And in the community of biosafety knowledge preparedness, “Possess the ability to properly transport and evacuate bio-infected patients”, and “Be familiar with biosafety incidents involving paramedics that require paramedic involvement” have the highest bridge expected influence.ConclusionThe components of clinical nursing staff’s biosafety incident response competence are interconnected through complex association patterns. From a network analysis perspective, four items exhibit the highest expected influence, signifying their paramount importance within the network. Additionally, another four items possess the highest bridge expected influence, indicating their strongest connections with the other three communities. These findings provide significant implications for clinical practice, as they offer potential targets for interventions aimed at enhancing nursing staff’s ability to handle biosafety events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07438-3.Keywords: Clinical nursing staff, Network analyses, Biosafety incident response competence

Introduction

Biosafety refers to the prevention of potential risks and hazards that biological agents may pose to human health and national security. These biological agents include, but are not limited to, pathogenic microorganisms, biological weapons, and bioterrorist attacks. They may give rise to adverse consequences such as the outbreak and spread of infectious diseases and harm to human health [1, 2]. With the advancement of biotechnology and the acceleration of globalization, an increasing number of countries have recognized the significance of biosafety and integrated it into their national strategies [3, 4]. Biosafety has become a primary concern among countries worldwide.

Nurses play an important and irreplaceable role in biosafety rescue [5]. Operating at the grassroots of the disease prevention and control system yet at the vanguard of biosafety rescue, their work is intrinsically linked to patient safety and the protection of healthcare workers. The success of the overall medical security system is directly impacted by the biosafety preparedness and reaction efficiency of nurses, who work at the first level of the disease prevention and control system while on the front lines of biosafety rescue operations [6, 7]. In the context of addressing new outbreaks of infectious diseases, pathogenic microorganisms, biological weapons, bioterrorist attacks, and other such biosafety incidents [8, 9], nurses’ comprehensive coping capabilities, including preparedness, assessment, early warning, disposal, protection, and rescue, collectively constitute the nursing staff’s competence in handling biosafety incidents. This competence facilitates the treatment of infected patients and the containment of the spread of biosafety-related infections [10]. Moreover, it is capable to lessen the consequences caused by biological hazards, which include sickness, fatalities, financial damages, social unrest, and so on [11, 12]. From a theoretical perspective, the Environmental Theory by Florence Nightingale highlights the importance of a clean and safe environment in promoting health, aligning with the core principles of biosafety. In addition, self-care deficit theory implies that nurses need to be well-equipped with biosafety knowledge to ensure patients receive proper care without being exposed to additional biological risks [13].

Therefore, preventing such incidents and ensuring national security stability require a thorough awareness of and support for clinical nurses’ abilities to handle biosafety crises [14]. However, most existing studies on nurses’ responses to biosafety incidents tend to focus on public health-related infectious disease emergencies [15], particularly COVID-19 [16, 17]. Yet we recognize that biosafety is a more extensive concept, encompassing a diverse array of subjects that go beyond mere infectious disease scenarios [18, 19]. However, existing literature reviews show a complete lack of relevant research on this topic. Thus, there remains an insufficient grasp of nurses’ comprehensive competence in responding to biosafety incidents.

Yet, current research into nurses’ responses to biosafety incidents like infectious diseases and public health emergencies employs the total score methodology [10, 20, 21]. However, assessing correlations using aggregate scores obscures the associations between the various dimensions of the variables [22]. Conventional statistical approaches are unable to gauge the comparative significance of distinct interconnected nodes within a network [23]. Existing studies treat nurses’ response as an integrated whole when exploring its relationship with other variables [24, 25]. As the number of variables increases, the likelihood of false correlations among them rises. Consequently, the nature of interactions between individual items remains unclear.

Network analysis is capable of mitigating these issues through fitting data via the Gaussian graphical model [26]. It is grounded in the interplay of complex variables, which are expressed both visually and mathematically [27]. A key advantage of network analysis lies in its ability to graphically illustrate association patterns across numerous variables without depending on pre-existing assumptions regarding their interrelationships [28]. Communities consistently serve as representations of clusters of psychological factors, and the bridge centrality metric enables accurate quantification of elements critical to linking communities across the entire network [29].

Thus, our research will be expanded to focus on nurses’ overall competence in managing biosafety incidents, rather than being limited to their capabilities in addressing public health emergencies related to infectious diseases [30, 31]. In order to improve the clinical nursing staff’s capacity to manage biosafety incidents, we will use a network analysis approach to investigate the connections among the different elements of their biosafety incident response competency.

Materials and methods

Participants

Between September 2023 and November 2023, a cross-sectional survey was completed by 4338 nurses from 16 hospitals, which included those from different geographical regions such as the eastern, central, western, northern and southern parts of China. The convenience sampling approach was used to recruit the participants, and the following conditions had to be satisfied: (1) get a certificate of nursing qualification and become a clinical first-line nurse; (2) obtain informed consent and freely take part in the study. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) nurses who practice or study in the hospital; (2) Nurses who ask to take a break or who are not on duty during the investigation. The calculation showed that the sample size was at least 5 times of the number of items in the survey. Our survey contains 36 variables. Consequently, N= (29 + 7)*5 = 180 was the sample size calculation formula, indicating that a minimum number of 180 people were needed for this investigation.

Data collection

Participants were informed of the study’s aims and provided verbal consent. They additionally filled out an electronic informed consent form and all questionnaire responses were submitted anonymously. They received guarantees that the data they provided would be only used for study. As the result, 4600 clinical nurses took part in the survey. The participants were given the questionnaire in digital format. Of the 4,600 nurses involved, 163 withdrew from the study, and 96 failed to complete the questionnaire. In the end, 4338 valid questionnaires yielded an effective response rate of 94.30%.

Measurements

Our research tools were the self-designed biosafety incident response competence scale [32, 33]. It consists of 29 items across 4 dimensions, using a 5-point Likert scale. The scale had scores ranging from 0 to 145, which indicates the nurses’ proficiency in handling biosafety incidents. High scores signify a high degree of biosafety incident management proficiency. The scale has been used in earlier studies, and it can gain popularity given its good reliability and validity. The Cronbach’s alphas coefficient in this study was 0.977, and the values for each dimension ranged from 0.899 to 0.959.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.2.0. SPSS 27.0 was applied to analyze descriptive data, including sociodemographic characteristics and biosafety incident response competence. R 4.2.0 software was utilized to construct the network model, estimate bridge centrality, and identify bridge items connecting 4 dimensions of the biosafety incident response competence.

Network model

The network was constructed using the R package graph [34]. The Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) [35] and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) [36] were utilized to reduce trivial edges to a weight of zero. Spearman’s correlation was employed, with the EBIC hyperparameter set to 0.500. Nodes in the network stand for different facets of biosafety incident response competency, while lines illustrate the statistical associations between these elements. Statistical control was applied to rule out the impact of other nodes on the correlation between any pair of nodes [37, 38]. Within the community, there were 9 nodes related to biosafety event monitoring and warning skills, 8 nodes concerning biosafety incident nursing disposal, 7 nodes associated with biosafety knowledge preparedness, and 5 nodes linked to biosafety infection prevention skills. The R package was used to carry out tests for the accuracy of edge weight indices and the significance of differences [39].

Bridge centrality

Bridge centrality was calculated using the R package network tools [40]. A node’s bridge expected influence (BEI) equals the sum of its connections to all nodes in other communities [41]. The R package was employed to run stability tests and significance tests for differences in BEI indices. Stability was evaluated via the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient), which should ideally exceed 0.50 and must not fall below 0.25 [42].

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the interconnected structure of clinical nurses’ biosafety incident response competence using network analysis, identifying critical pathways and bridging components. By applying network analysis, this study seeks to uncover potential pathways through which various components of biosafety incident response competence are interrelated. The findings indicate that P4, M3, D5, and K5 possess the highest expected influence, highlighting their greatest importance within the network. Meanwhile, P4, M4, D8, and K1 exhibit the highest bridge expected influence, suggesting they maintain the strongest connections with the other three communities. It is worth noting that different items may involve both similar and unique pathways contributing to the enhancement of nurses’ ability to handle biosafety events. To a certain extent, our results supplement the growing body of research identifying key areas for nurses to improve their biosafety incident response competence.

Biosafety incident response competence consists of four factors: biosafety event monitoring and warning abilities, biosafety incident nursing disposal abilities, biosafety knowledge preparedness, and biosafety infection protection abilities. Regarding the components of biosafety event monitoring and warning abilities, this study identifies that the strongest correlation exists between M6 (“Monitoring of microbial resistance”) and M7 (“Monitoring of common symptoms in patients with biological infections”). This finding is consistent with previous research that emphasizes the vital role of antimicrobial resistance surveillance in infection control [43]. It reflects the importance of drug monitoring and management in the syndrome monitoring of patients with biological infection [44]. The other three strongest edges are between M1 “Possess the ability to assess biosafety incident level, radiation impact range, severity, and medical rescue response level” and M3 “Possess the ability to assess the harm of pathogenic microorganisms”, M5 “Ability to identify biosafety risks” and M6 “Monitoring of microbial resistance”, M4 “Understand the main points and requirements of detection and screening of pathogenic microorganisms and drug-resistant bacteria” and M5 “Ability to identify biosafety risks”. This illustrates how components of the biological infection monitoring and early warning are interdependent and mutually reinforcing. Within components of biosafety incident nursing disposal abilities, we find a strongest correlation between D6 “Possess the ability of psychological adjust and psychological care for biologically infected patients and their families affected by infectious diseases and biological warfare agents” and D7 “Possess a good ability to withstand pressure and psychological adjustment in the biosafety incident rescue” echoes studies showing that emotional support reduces anxiety in patients during outbreaks [45]. Psychological issues are more prevalent during biosafety incidents due to fear and uncertainty about the illness [46]. These two items pertain to psychological nursing, which constitutes a critical part of biosafety nursing and thus should not be overlooked [47, 48]. In the components of biosafety knowledge preparedness, the strongest correlation is observed between K2 (“Understand relevant laws and regulations”) and K3 (“Understand the types of pathogenic microorganisms and the transmission routes of different types of pathogenic microorganisms”). As for the components of biosafety infection protection abilities, this study finds that the strongest correlation exists between P1 (“Master the emergency treatment process of skin and mucous membrane exposure, respiratory mucous membrane injury, sharp instrument injury and other biosafety occupational exposure and injury”) and P2 (“Be able to properly handle blood, body fluids, secretions, excreta and biosafety-related medical waste from patients with biological infections”).

In the current network, P4 (“Master the correct collection methods of blood culture samples and nasopharyngeal swabs from patients with biological infection”), M3 (“Possess the ability to assess the harm of pathogenic microorganisms”), D5 (“Master the key points of medical record management and record of patients with biological infection”), and K5 (“Be familiar with the concept of antimicrobial resistance and the use of antimicrobials”) exhibit the highest expected influences. These four variables suggest that they could serve as effective breakthrough points for enhancing biosafety incident response competence [49, 50]. Nursing managers and nursing staff alike should attach great importance to these aspects. While enhancing their overall capabilities, they ought to emphasize these specific areas to more effectively boost their ability to handle biosafety events.

In the current network, the bridge strength centrality of nodes can help clarify the specific roles of different items across various communities. P4 (“Master the correct collection methods of blood culture samples and nasopharyngeal swabs from patients with biological infection”), M4 (“Understand the main points and requirements of detection and screening of pathogenic microorganisms and drug-resistant bacteria”), D8 (“Possess the ability to properly transport and evacuate bio-infected patients”), and K1 (“Be familiar with biosafety incidents involving paramedics that require paramedic involvement”) demonstrate the highest bridge expected influence, meaning they maintain the strongest connections with the other three communities. From a network perspective, therefore, targeting these items may be more effective in promoting the improvement of abilities in other dimensions. Hence, in addition to focusing on variables with the highest expected influence, we should also pay attention to those with the highest bridge expected influence.

Clinical nurses’ ability to respond to biosafety incidents covers a broad spectrum of areas, where specific items within these areas interact with each other. By employing network analysis, this study explored a novel approach to enhancing clinical nurses’ biosafety incident response capacity from an alternative perspective [51]. Beyond enhancing nursing staff’s ability to handle biosafety incidents, both clinical nursing personnel and nursing managers should focus on mastering several key aspects. This targeted approach will enable them to improve the overall ability level more accurately and efficiently.

Implications and significance

The findings of this study hold critical implications across clinical, educational, organizational, and research domains. Clinically, identifying key components with the highest expected influence (e.g., P4, M3, D5, K5) provides actionable targets for enhancing nurses’ biosafety incident response competence. Prioritizing training in specimen collection (P4), harm assessment of pathogens (M3), medical record management (D5), and antimicrobial resistance (K5) could directly improve patient outcomes, reduce nosocomial infections, and mitigate occupational exposure risks. Educationally, these results underscore the need to integrate high-impact competencies into nursing curricula and continuing education programs. For instance, simulations or workshops focusing on biosafety incident monitoring (M4), patient evacuation protocols (D8), and interdisciplinary collaboration (K1) could bridge gaps between theoretical knowledge and practical skills. Organizationally, healthcare institutions should leverage the bridge centrality findings (e.g., M4, D8, K1) to optimize resource allocation, refine emergency protocols, and foster interdepartmental coordination. Strengthening these “bridge” competencies ensures cohesive responses during biosafety crises, minimizing systemic vulnerabilities. From a research perspective, this study pioneers the application of network analysis in biosafety competence research, offering a methodological framework to explore complex interactions between multidimensional variables. By pinpointing critical nodes and bridges, it equips stakeholders with evidence-based strategies to fortify global health security, enhance workforce resilience, and ultimately safeguard public health in an era of escalating biological threats.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the use of self-reported questionnaires may introduce measurement biases, such as social desirability bias and recall bias. Second, the sampling method has notable constraints: Owing to budgetary restrictions and the extensive geographical spread of hospitals in China, a convenience sampling approach was adopted. This is likely to have led to uneven representation across different regions and hospital levels, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Third, there are methodological limitations inherent in the network analysis framework: The calculation of group-level between-subject effects inevitably masks individual differences in network structure and centrality metrics.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates the richness and complexity of the associations within the structure of biosafety incident response competence. Within the network, various aspects of such competence are correlated with their internal dimensions. From a network analysis perspective, in the current network, P4 (“Master the correct collection methods of blood culture samples and nasopharyngeal swabs from patients with biological infection”), M3 (“Possess the ability to assess the harm of pathogenic microorganisms”), D5 (“Master the key points of medical record management and record of patients with biological infection”), and K5 (“Be familiar with the concept of antimicrobial resistance and the use of antimicrobials”) exhibit the highest expected influence. Meanwhile, P4, M4 (“Understand the main points and requirements of detection and screening of pathogenic microorganisms and drug-resistant bacteria”), D8 (“Possess the ability to properly transport and evacuate bio-infected patients”), and K1 (“Be familiar with biosafety incidents involving paramedics that require paramedic involvement”) have the highest bridge expected influence, indicating their strongest connections with each community. These findings hold important implications for clinical practice, as they provide potential targets for interventions aimed at enhancing nursing staff’s ability to handle biosafety incidents.

These results hold significant implications for clinical practice. They offer potential targets for interventions aiming to boost nursing staff’s capacity to handle biosafety incidents. For future research, we suggest exploring effective training programs centered around the identified key competencies to enhance nurses’ biosafety incident response capabilities. Also, longitudinal studies could be conducted to track changes in response competence over time. In terms of practical applications, hospitals could integrate the key elements of biosafety incident response competence into their regular training curricula. From a policy-aking perspective, regulatory bodies may consider formulating guidelines based on our findings to standardize biosafety practices across different healthcare settings. This not only further validates our research but also positions it within the broader research agenda, highlighting its contributions to the field.
