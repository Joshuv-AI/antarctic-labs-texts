Prediction of Digestible and Metabolizable Energy in Swine Feed Using Machine Learning
Authors: Jun-Wen Yu, Shang-Hua Liu, Dong-Xin Ye, De Wu, Hao Lin, Yan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the continuous development of animal husbandry, accurately predicting the energy content of feed is crucial for optimizing formulas and improving production efficiency. This study applied AutoGluon, an automated machine learning framework, to predict digestible energy and metabolizable energy in swine feed, and compared its performance with artificial neural networks and support vector machines. Using 1341 records from the China feed database and 45 records for pregnant sows, AutoGluon achieved superior accuracy, with R 2 values of 0.940 (digestible energy) and 0.938 (metabolizable energy) on the general test data set. On the pregnant sow test set, it performed best for metabolizable energy prediction (R 2 = 0.939), while artificial neural networks showed a slight advantage for digestible energy (R 2 = 0.920). An AutoGluon-based predictor called pig energy predictor was developed for practical application, enabling rapid and precise feed energy estimation. This study demonstrates the potential of pig energy predictor for practical applications in swine nutrition and provides a tailored model for general pig populations, supporting precision feed formulation and data-driven decision-making in animal husbandry.

Introduction

Optimizing livestock feed formulations is critical to improving the efficiency and sustainability of livestock production. , As major economic livestock, pig breeding and production have a significant impact on agricultural economic development, food safety, and ecological environment protection. Digestible energy (DE) and metabolizable energy (ME) are important indicators for evaluating pig feed, directly affecting growth performance, feed efficiency, and production costs. − Accurate prediction of DE and ME can achieve more precise feed formulations, ultimately improving production results. However, traditional methods for determining DE and ME involve labor-intensive and costly in vivo experiments, making them impractical for daily feed formulation adjustments.

To address these challenges, predictive models based on the chemical composition of feed components have been developed. These models provide an economically efficient alternative for estimating DE and ME. Over the years, various regression-based methods have been employed, but they often fail to capture the complex nonlinear relationships in feed composition data. This has led to increased interest in advanced machine learning techniques that offer better performance.

In recent years, with the rapid advancement of biotechnology and information technology, machine learning models have made significant progress in predicting the energy value of pig feed. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) and support vector machines (SVMs) have been widely employed to analyze the complex nonlinear relationships within feed components. − For instance, Ahmadi and Rodehutscord analyzed 290 samples using ANN and SVM models, with 70% of the data used as the training set and 30% as the test set. Their results showed that R 2 of ANN and SVM reached 0.95 on the training set and 0.96 and 0.94 on the test set, respectively, demonstrating that both models have high prediction accuracy and strong generalization ability on this data set.

Traditional statistical methods, such as stepwise regression and multiple regression, − struggle to handle the nonlinear relationships within feed composition, often resulting in lower prediction accuracy. For example, Wang et al. analyzed the feed data from 11 pregnant sows using stepwise regression, and their results indicated that the R 2 of DE was only 0.75 during the second trimester, while in the third trimester, the R 2 values of DE and ME were 0.83 and 0.80, respectively. Moreover, the lack of test and validation sets in this study further restricted the model’s generalization capacity.

Although ANN and SVM models can address some limitations of traditional statistical approaches, they also bring challenges. For instance, ANN models require extensive hyperparameter tuning and are prone to overfitting, especially in the case of small data sets. Meanwhile, the performance of SVM models is often inferior to ANN when applied to larger data sets. In addition, most existing studies primarily focus on predicting the energy requirements of general pig populations, with relatively few studies addressing the specific energy needs of pregnant sows. Further research in this area is crucial for deeper insights.

To address these challenges, this study is the first to apply machine learning techniques to explore the specific energy requirements of pregnant sows (the overall scheme is shown in Figure ). Compared to standard growing pigs, pregnant sows have significantly different energy demands, introducing new complexities in pig feed formulation. Additionally, this study is one of the first to incorporate AutoGluon (AGN), an automated machine learning framework, into pig feed energy prediction. AGN optimizes model selection and hyperparameter adjustments automatically, reducing manual intervention and improving prediction accuracy. Its adaptability to small and complex data sets helps overcome the limitations of previous machine learning methods in pig feed energy prediction. We compared the predictive performance of three models: AGN, ANN, and SVM. This comparison enabled a systematic evaluation of model performance for feed energy prediction. To address the issue of small data sets, we expanded the original data set of pregnant sows from 23 samples to 45 samples using data augmentation techniques such as linear interpolation and noise perturbation, , To ensure the reliability of the augmented data, we conducted validation analyses, including distribution comparison via histograms and box plots, as well as statistical tests such as the Mann–Whitney U test. These analyses confirmed that the augmented data maintained a distribution consistent with the original data set, preserving key statistical characteristics and ensuring its suitability for model training. Our results demonstrate that the proposed models are not only effective in predicting the energy requirements of pregnant sows, but also generalizable to other pig populations, achieving high prediction accuracy across different data sets. Furthermore, to enhance practical applicability, we developed a prediction tool based on our model, featuring a user-friendly interface for estimating digestible and metabolizable energy in pig feed. This tool supports practical feed energy estimation and precision feed formulation.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources and Preparation

This study utilized two primary data sources: the China feed database and published literature. The China feed database is a publicly available and widely used resource containing comprehensive information on pig feed ingredient composition and nutritional energy values. From this database, we collected and analyzed 1341 data records, which included information on feed chemical composition and energy values for various pig populations.

However, data specifically related to the energy requirements of pregnant sowsespecially studies that predict DE and ME based on feed chemical compositionwere notably scarce. To address this limitation, we conducted an extensive literature search using databases such as Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. The keywords and phrases used included “pregnant sows, feed chemical composition, digestible energy, metabolizable energy.”

Despite considerable efforts, no comprehensive experimental data set was found. Ultimately, 23 data records were identified and extracted from published literature, ,, which represented the most complete and relevant data set available for pregnant sows. These studies were conducted at Sichuan Agricultural University and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), with the following ethical approvals.(1)Study on soybean meal: Approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No.: S20174302).(2)Study on cottonseed meal: Approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No.: SICAU 20210038).(3)Study on extruded full-fat soybeans: Approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No.: S20174302).

In summary, the final data set for this study consisted of 1341 records from the China feed database and 23 records from experimental studies on pregnant sows, which formed the foundation for model training and evaluation. For clarity, the 1341 records from the China feed database are hereafter referred to as the general pig data set, whereas the literature-derived pregnant sow records are referred to as the pregnant sow data set.

In the data set preparation stage, a set of seeds was used to divide the 1341 samples from the China feed database into a 75% training set (1005 data points) and a 25% test set (336 data points) to ensure the reproducibility of the experiment. To further enhance the model’s generalization ability, 5-fold cross-validation was applied to the training set. For the 23 pregnant sow samples, 11 additional data samples were generated using linear interpolation to fill gaps between data points, and another 11 samples were generated using noise perturbation to enhance data diversity (details in Section ). This expanded the pregnant sow data set to 45 samples. To maintain consistency with the original data, the newly generated data for pregnant sows were rounded to two decimal places. These 45 samples were also split into a 75% training set (33 data points) and 25% test set (12 data points) using the same random seed, with the training set subjected to 5-fold cross-validation. Variable Selection

In theory, increasing the number of input variables can improve the predictive ability of a model. However, introducing irrelevant variables may lead to overfitting or reduce predictive accuracy. − Therefore, this study conducted Spearman correlation analysis on the two data sets using JMP Pro 17 statistical analysis software. , When predicting DE and ME, input variables with a significance level of P < 0.01 were selected to eliminate redundant variables.

Subsequently, the Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV) method based on a RandomForestRegressor was applied to further optimize the selected variables. Unlike simple univariate filtering methods, RFECV considers the interdependencies among variables by iteratively removing the least important features while evaluating model performance. Since Random Forest (RF) inherently captures variable interactions, this method ensures that feature selection accounts for both direct contributions (individual variable importance) and indirect contributions (interactions between features). This step ensures that the final features maximize the predictive performance of the model and minimize the risk of overfitting.

Data Augmentation Methods

To address the small sample size of the pregnant sow data set, two data augmentation methods, noise perturbation and linear interpolation, were used. Each method generated 11 additional samples, resulting in 22 generated samples and expanding the data set from 23 to 45 samples. To reduce the risk of synthetic-data bias, the number of generated samples was kept below the number of original samples.

Noise Perturbation

The noise data generation process involves adding a certain amount of random noise to the original data to create samples that are similar to the original ones, but with slight variations. The specific steps were described as follows.(1)Randomly select an original sample. One sample was randomly selected from the 23 original data points to ensure that the new samples are based on actual data.(2)Apply noise perturbation. Random noise with a normal distribution N­(0,σ2) was added to each feature of the selected sample, where the standard deviation σ was set to 2% of the original feature value. The perturbed feature value was calculated using the following equation: 1 x′=x+ϵ where x is the original feature value, x′ is the perturbed feature value, and ϵ is a random value sampled from N­(0,(0.02·x)2). The choice of 2% as the noise level was based on practical considerations to balance data variability and stability. Excessive noise (e.g., >5%) could introduce unrealistic variations, leading to distorted feature distributions and potential model misinterpretation. Conversely, an overly small noise level (e.g., <1%) might fail to provide sufficient diversity, limiting the effectiveness of data augmentation. Empirical studies in similar machine learning applications suggest that noise levels within 1–3% are effective in enhancing model robustness without significantly altering the underlying data distribution. Therefore, 2% was chosen as an optimal trade-off to ensure data augmentation improves generalizability while maintaining consistency with real-world variations. (3)Generate new samples. The new feature values with added noise form new samples, and a total of 11 new samples were generated.

Linear Interpolation

Linear interpolation was also used to generate new samples between two original samples. The specific steps were as follows.(1)Randomly select two original samples. For each new sample, two different samples (A and B) were randomly selected from the original data set.(2)Generate an interpolation coefficient. A random interpolation coefficient α, ranging from 0 to 1, was generated to determine the position of the new sample between A and B.(3)Calculate the new sample. The generated feature values were calculated using the following linear interpolation equation: 2 xnew=α×xA+(1−α)×xB where x A and x B are the feature values of the original samples A and B, x new is the generated feature value, and α controls the new sample’s proximity to A or B.(4)Generate new samples. Repeat the above steps to generate 11 new samples, which were then added to the original data set.

Data Augmentation Validation

Visualization-Based Validation

To evaluate whether data augmentation introduced obvious distributional shifts, visualization-based validation was performed using histograms, box plots, and principal component analysis (PCA) scatter plots. , Gross energy (GE) was selected as a representative variable because it directly reflects feed energy content and is a key indicator for evaluating feed energy density.

As shown in Figure , the histogram and density curves showed similar distributional patterns between the original and augmented data sets. The box plots showed comparable medians and interquartile ranges, with only a slightly wider range in the augmented data set. The PCA scatter plot further showed that the augmented samples were generally distributed around the original samples. Together, these results suggest that the augmentation process increased sample diversity without introducing obvious distributional shifts.

Mann–Whitney U Test for Distribution Consistency

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to quantitatively assess distributional consistency between the original and augmented data sets. All examined variables showed P-values greater than 0.05, including DM (P = 0.75), GE (P = 0.98), CP (P = 0.87), EE (P = 0.86), Ash (P = 0.70), CF (P = 0.46), NDF (P = 0.47), ADF (P = 0.43), DE (P = 0.98), and ME (P = 0.96). These results indicate that no statistically significant differences were detected between the original and augmented data sets, suggesting that the augmentation process did not introduce obvious distributional bias.

Model Validation

This section primarily investigates the impact of data augmentation on model prediction performance. We conducted training and testing experiments on both the original data set (23 samples) and the augmented data set (45 samples), using two commonly employed machine learning modelsLinear Regression and Random Forest.

In evaluating model performance, we used the R 2 to measure the model’s goodness-of-fit, the Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) to assess the consistency between the predicted and true values, and the RMSE to quantify the average prediction error. Preliminary experimental results indicate that the models trained on the augmented data set outperform those trained on the original data set in all three metrics, demonstrating that data augmentation has a positive impact on improving prediction performance. For example, the Linear Regression model shows noticeable improvements in both R 2 and CCC on the augmented data, accompanied by a reduction in RMSE, while the Random Forest model exhibits similarly higher consistency and lower error.

Specifically, taking the DE target variable as an example, the Linear Regression model’s R 2 increased from 0.77 to 0.95, CCC from 0.89 to 0.98, and RMSE decreased from 0.73 to 0.63 when using the augmented data set. Similarly, for the Random Forest model, R 2 improved from 0.77 to 0.92, CCC from 0.88 to 0.95. Overview of AGN

AGN is an automated machine learning (AutoML) framework that integrates data preprocessing, model selection, hyperparameter optimization, and ensemble learning into a unified workflow. In this study, AGN was used to build regression models for DE and ME prediction from feed composition data. Its main advantage is that it can automatically compare multiple candidate algorithms and combine models through ensemble strategies, thereby reducing manual model selection and tuning. The overall workflow and AGN-based model structure used in this study are shown in Figure .

For model training, AGN was configured using the presets = “best_quality” setting and optimized using RMSE as the evaluation metric. This setting allows AGN to explore high-quality model configurations and improve predictive performance through model stacking and ensemble learning. The trained AGN models were then evaluated together with ANN and SVM models using the same train-test splits and evaluation metrics described below.

Model Construction

AGN-Fitted Optimal Model with Training Data Set

During the process of building the optimal model using the AGN framework, we utilized presets = “best_quality” for both training sets to ensure the highest possible model performance. This configuration allows AGN to run in high-quality mode, where it automatically adjusts hyperparameters and selects the optimal model architecture based on the data. The training process was conducted by minimizing the RMSE. In this mode, AGN combines the strengths of various models, including GBM, RF, CatBoost, and others, optimizing the final predictive performance through model stacking and ensemble techniques.

Additionally, we enabled GPU acceleration by setting num_gpus = 1. This significantly accelerates the training process by utilizing parallel computation on the GPU, which is particularly beneficial when working with complex models and large data sets. AGN automatically detects and leverages available GPU resources, which drastically reduces training time and enhances scalability.

This setup was particularly useful when dealing with the large-scale data set of 1005 feed samples in the China feed database, where the combination of GPU acceleration and high-quality preset settings enabled us to efficiently handle both data complexity and model optimization.

ANN Model Tuned by GridSearchCV Using Training Data Set

ANNs are models that simulate the human brain to learn and process information. ANNs consist of an input layer, hidden layers, and an output layer. Each hidden layer is made up of neurons that process inputs by applying weights, adding bias, and using activation functions. The depth and number of hidden layers depend on the complexity of the problem, impacting the network’s ability to model complex relationships. GridSearchCV is a hyperparameter tuning method that exhaustively searches all predefined parameter combinations to find the optimal configuration for the model. It evaluates each parameter combination through cross-validation to ensure that the model neither overfits nor underfits. , In this study, GridSearchCV was used to tune multiple hyperparameters of the ANN model, and the final hyperparameter configuration was selected based on minimizing the RMSE to optimize the model’s predictive performance. Since GridSearchCV does not automatically handle normalization and standardization as some frameworks do (e.g., AGN), manual preprocessing was required before training. The input data were normalized using Min-Max normalization to scale the features to a [0, 1] range, ensuring that all input variables were on the same scale. This helped stabilize model training by properly scaling step sizes and weight updates. After obtaining predictions from the model, the output values were rescaled back to the original units to enable proper evaluation and interpretation.

During the hyperparameter tuning process, GridSearchCV performed a grid search over multiple hyperparameters. First, the hidden layer configurations were tested with three structures: a single layer with 10 neurons, two layers with 10 neurons each, and three layers with 10 neurons each. The choice of 10 neurons per layer was made to balance model complexity and computational efficiency. A relatively small number of neurons helps prevent overfitting while maintaining sufficient capacity to capture essential patterns in the data. Additionally, using a fixed number of neurons across different layer depths allows for a clearer comparison of the impact of network depth on performance. Such choices are supported by findings in related studies, as discussed in the literature. Four activation functions were considered: Tan h, ReLU, Logistic, and Identity. The optimization algorithms tested were Adam and LBFGS. In addition, different L2 regularization parameters were evaluated, including 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 0.5, 0.05, and 0.005. The learning rate strategies tested were constant and adaptive, and the maximum number of iterations was set to 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000. In total, 1920 candidate ANN architectures were generated.

Through GridSearchCV, these hyperparameter combinations were evaluated through cross-validation, and the final configuration was selected based on minimizing the RMSE to ensure optimal model performance. For the China feed database, the optimal hyperparameters for the DE model were: activation function ReLU, L2 regularization parameter 0.1, hidden layer structure with three layers of 10 neurons each, learning rate strategy constant, maximum iterations 400, and optimizer LBFGS. For the ME model, the optimal hyperparameters were: activation function tanh, L2 regularization parameter 0.0001, hidden layer structure with two layers of 10 neurons each, learning rate strategy constant, maximum iterations 600, and optimizer LBFGS.

For the pregnant sow data, the optimal hyperparameters for the DE model were: activation function logistic, L2 regularization parameter 0.0001, hidden layer structure with two layers of 10 neurons each, learning rate strategy constant, maximum iterations 200, and optimizer LBFGS. For the ME model, the optimal hyperparameters were: activation function ReLU, L2 regularization parameter 0.05, hidden layer structure with three layers of 10 neurons each, learning rate strategy constant, maximum iterations 200, and optimizer LBFGS.

SVM Model Tuned by GridSearchCV Using Training Data Set

SVM is a powerful supervised learning algorithm widely used in classification and regression tasks. SVM achieves efficient classification and prediction of complex data sets by finding the optimal hyperplane to maximize the classification margin. In most cases, the SVM model is built and optimized using two key parameters: the cost parameter and the kernel parameter.

When predicting two data sets using the SVM model, GridSearchCV was employed for hyperparameter tuning. The choice of hyperparameters is crucial for optimizing model performance, and the details are as follows:

First, the SVM model tested different kernel functions, including linear, radial basis function (RBF), and sigmoid kernels. For the regularization parameter C, the search range was set to the following specific values: 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000, and 10000. For the gamma parameter, the search range included “scale”, “auto”, 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, and 1. Additionally, for the sigmoid kernel, the coef0 parameter was tested with values of 0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 0.05, and 0.01. In total, 1296 candidate SVM architectures were generated.

Comparison of Three Models

Model Performance in the General Pig Data Set

In the training set (n = 1005), the R 2 values predicted by AGN for both DE and ME were both 0.975, significantly better than those of ANN and SVM models. The R 2 values obtained by ANN for DE and ME were 0.936 and 0.935, respectively, while the performance of SVM was slightly weaker, with R 2 values of 0.922 for DE and 0.920 for ME. This highlights that AGN is more effective than ANN and SVM in capturing complex relationships in data. In terms of RMSE, AGN also demonstrated excellent performance, with values of 0.886 and 0.818 for DE and ME, respectively, while ANN had even higher RMSE values, with DE of 1.431 and ME of 1.306. SVM has the weakest performance, with RMSE of 1.579 for DE and 1.450 for ME. This further supports the conclusion that AGN was more accurate and has smaller prediction errors than ANN and SVM. Regarding CCC, AGN outperformed the other models once again, with both DE and ME reaching 0.987, indicating a high degree of consistency between predicted and actual values. ANN followed closely behind, with CCC values of 0.967 for both DE and ME, while SVM had the lowest CCC values of 0.958 for DE and ME.

On the test set (n = 336), the R 2 values of AGN decreased slightly to 0.940 for DE and 0.938 for ME prediction, but still maintained competitive performance. ANN showed a similar trend, with R 2 values of 0.941 for DE and 0.947 for ME, slightly outperforming AGN in ME prediction. SVM followed closely, with R 2 values of 0.930 for DE and 0.932 for ME, respectively. As for the RMSE of the test set, the prediction errors of AGN slightly increased, with values of 1.423 for DE and 1.332 for ME. However, the ME of ANN has been improved, with an RMSE of 1.228, outperforming AGN, while its DE prediction is still comparable to an RMSE of 1.412. SVM had the largest error, with RMSE values of 1.545 for DE and 1.391 for ME. Finally, the CCC values of AGN on the test set were 0.969 for DE and 0.967 for ME, indicating the robustness and consistency of the prediction. ANN performed similarly, achieving CCC values of 0.972 for ME and 0.969 for DE. Although SVM is slightly lower, it still maintains relatively strong performance, with CCC values of 0.962 for DE and 0.964 for ME.

Finally, AGN significantly outperformed both ANN and SVM on the training set for DE and ME predictions, demonstrating higher accuracy, lower errors, and greater consistency, as reflected by the R 2, RMSE, and CCC metrics. However, on the test set, the differences between the models diminished, and ANN even slightly outperformed AGN in ME prediction. These results suggest that although AGN had significant advantages on the training set, the generalization performance of AGN, ANN, and SVM all converged on the test set, with all models showing relatively similar abilities. Development and Application of the Pig Energy Predictor

To enhance the real-world applicability of the AGN-based model trained on the general pig data set, a lightweight and user-friendly prediction tool named pig energy predictor (PEP) was developed, as illustrated in Figure . PEP enables fast and accurate estimation of DE and ME directly within Microsoft Excel.

This figure illustrates PEP, an AGN-based predictor developed using data from general pig populations. The predictor allows users to input feed composition valuesincluding DM, CF, EE, NFE, ash, and GE (MJ/kg)to obtain direct predictions of DE and ME. The model leverages advanced machine learning techniques to capture nonlinear relationships in feed composition, providing a rapid and accurate estimation of energy values in swine feed formulations. This tool enhances practical applicability by enabling real-time feed optimization, reducing the need for costly and time-consuming in vivo experiments. The tool offers a simple, practical, and accessible solution by operating entirely within Excel without requiring additional software, enabling offline use and making it suitable for users without programming experience. By enabling real-time feed optimization, PEP reduces the reliance on expensive and time-consuming in vivo experiments, offering a plug-and-play alternative that significantly improves user convenience. This implementation demonstrates how advanced AutoML models like AGN can be translated into high-performance, easy-to-use tools that support intelligent decision-making and precision feed formulation in swine nutrition management.

In the China feed database, the feature importance analysis revealed that GE was the most crucial feature in predicting DE, with a feature importance score of 0.854. This is consistent with the role of GE, which directly reflects the energy content in the feed and is essential for digestible energy prediction. NFE and CP were also significant contributors, with feature importance scores of 0.051 and 0.048, respectively. These results highlight the importance of carbohydrates and protein in energy digestion, particularly in metabolic processes. For ME prediction, GE again emerged as the dominant feature with a score of 0.842. In addition, the importance of NFE and Ash increased slightly in the context of metabolizable energy, with scores of 0.053 and 0.028, indicating their roles in energy conversion.

In the pregnant sow data set, GE remained the most important feature for predicting DE, with a feature importance score of 0.766, significantly higher than the other features. EE had a feature importance score of 0.234, indicating its secondary role in DE prediction. This indicates that GE is still the key driver of energy demand for pregnant sows, and fat plays a crucial role as an energy reserve for fetal development. For ME prediction, GE was once again the most important feature, with a feature importance score of 0.567, indicating its dominant position in the prediction model. However, CF and Ash had feature importance scores of 0.234 and 0.199, respectively, highlighting the significant role of fiber and minerals in meeting the metabolizable energy needs of pregnant sows. Fiber helps maintain intestinal health, while ash reflects the importance of minerals in nutrition.

In summary, the feature importance analysis clearly showed that GE played a crucial role in predicting both DE and ME. However, the importance of specific features shifted significantly with the data set. For instance, in the pregnant sow data, CF and Ash became more important, reflecting the increased demand for these components under the physiological conditions of pregnancy. These findings further validate the effectiveness of the RF model for feature selection.

Model Performance and Analysis

China Feed Database Model Performance Analysis

In the training set, AGN outperformed both ANN and SVM, particularly in handling complex nonlinear relationships in feed composition data. This advantage may be attributed to its automated hyperparameter tuning and model stacking capabilities, which helped capture interactions among features in the China feed database. Although ANN and SVM were also effective, their training-set performance was generally lower than that of AGN.

In the test set, AGN’s performance slightly decreased, but it remained competitive. Interestingly, ANN slightly outperformed AGN in ME prediction, suggesting that ANN might generalize better in certain patterns in the test set, particularly where the data relationships are more straightforward. However, SVM continued to show weaker performance, which may be due to its limited capacity in modeling the nonlinear complexity of energy demand.

Overall, AGN performed well in handling the intricate feed data, but its performance convergence with ANN on the test set indicates that although AGN is powerful, the choice of model may depend on the specific characteristics of the data set.

Pregnant Sow Data Model Performance Analysis

In the training set of the pregnant sow data set, AGN outperformed both ANN and SVM in DE and ME prediction, particularly in ME prediction. The energy requirements of pregnant sows are influenced by multiple factors, such as protein and fiber intake. AGN could capture these complex interactions. Its ability to handle multidimensional nonlinear relationships resulted in higher R 2 values and lower prediction errors. In contrast, ANN struggled more in ME prediction, which may be due to its limitations in dealing with the complex interactions between features, such as fiber and Ash, which are critical for ME. Although ANN performed reasonably well in DE prediction, its weaker performance in ME prediction suggests that the model may be suitable for simple patterns. SVM performed better than ANN in ME prediction, but still lagged behind AGN. This indicates that SVM’s kernel methods were insufficient to capture the intricate relationships required to accurately predict the energy demands of pregnant sows.

On the test set, AGN showed weaker generalization in DE prediction, with slightly lower performance compared to ANN and SVM. Although AGN continued to perform well in ME prediction, its accuracy and consistency in DE prediction were relatively lower, indicating certain limitations when generalizing DE predictions across different data sets. ANN maintained stable performance in DE prediction, but performed weakly in ME prediction, suggesting that there is still room for improvement in handling more complex energy demands. SVM’s performance on the test set mirrored its results in the training set, with lower accuracy in ME prediction, but comparable to ANN in DE prediction.

Overall, AGN showed advantages in ME prediction, whereas ANN and SVM showed relatively stable performance for DE prediction. These results suggest that model performance varied by energy target and data set size.

Limitations

Limitations of Data Set Size

The China feed database contains a large amount of data, but due to differences in data sources, there may be some inconsistencies in data quality. The data may contain imbalanced feature distributions, which can affect the model’s ability to process certain features.

For the pregnant sow data set, although it was expanded using linear interpolation and noise perturbation techniques, the data set still suffers from the limitations of a small sample size. A smaller data set makes the model prone to overfitting, meaning that even if the model performs well on the training set, it may not generalize effectively to the test set.

Impact of Random Seed Settings

In small data sets (such as the pregnant sow data set), although using a random seed ensures reproducibility of the experimental results, this fixed randomness can cause the model to become overly dependent on specific training samples. The model may perform well on the small training set, but its performance may degrade when applied to more diverse data sets.

Limitations of Computational Resources for the Model

AGN uses model stacking and automated hyperparameter optimization to find the best model configuration. While this approach significantly improves model prediction accuracy and performance, it comes at a high computational cost. Especially when handling large data sets, computational resource requirements of AGN far exceed those of ANN and SVM. In contrast, ANN and SVM, with their simpler network structures, consume relatively fewer computational resources. However, in this study, the data sets used are relatively small, so this issue does not pose a significant challenge in our experiments. Nevertheless, for future applications involving large-scale data, the high computational cost of AGN should be carefully considered, particularly in scenarios demanding rapid inference and limited hardware resources. Therefore, in practical applications where data sets are large, the use of AGN may be limited by time and hardware resources, particularly in scenarios where rapid results are needed.

Limitations of the Tool

The AGN prediction tool PEP developed in this study focuses exclusively on predicting DE and ME for general pig populations based on feed chemical composition. The decision not to develop a dedicated prediction tool for pregnant sows was primarily due to the limited availability of data specific to this population. Despite augmenting the data set through data augmentation techniques, the data set for pregnant sows remained insufficient to ensure robust and generalized predictions in practical applications. Additionally, the AGN model demonstrated relatively weak performance in predicting DE specifically for pregnant sows, further constraining the feasibility of developing a specialized predictor for this subgroup. Future research efforts aimed at obtaining larger data sets or enhancing model generalization for small-sample scenarios could enable the creation of more targeted and reliable prediction tools to accommodate the individualized nutritional requirements of various pig populations.

Future Directions in Model Optimization and Application

Future research could emphasize improving the interpretability of models. Although models like AGN perform well in terms of accuracy, their “black-box” nature remains a challenge. By introducing explainable machine learning methods, such as SHAP or LIME, it will be easier to understand how specific features impact the predictions, which can aid in refining feed formulations.

In this study, AGN models have been specifically developed for general pig populations separately, enabling accurate predictions of DE and ME based solely on feed composition.

Future studies could explore multiobjective optimization models tailored to the specific needs of different production stages, such as growing pigs and pregnant sows. This would allow for the simultaneous prediction of multiple energy demands (e.g., DE, ME, and net energy) and enable personalized feeding formulations based on individual variability.

As the demand for real-time predictions in large-scale agricultural settings increases, future research could focus on improving the computational efficiency of models. Techniques, such as distributed computing, cloud-based model deployment, and model compression, could be implemented to ensure that complex models can be deployed at scale while maintaining high prediction accuracy without excessive computational costs.

Conclusion

In this study, advanced machine learning models were successfully applied to predict DE and ME in pig feed. A comparative analysis of AGN, ANN, and SVM revealed that AGN generally outperformed the other models, particularly in capturing complex nonlinear relationships within feed composition data.

AGN demonstrated robust performance in the general pig data set derived from the China feed database and showed strong ME prediction performance in the smaller pregnant sow data set, thanks to its capabilities for automatic hyperparameter tuning and model stacking. Specifically, AGN showed higher predictive accuracy and better generalization in ME prediction on the training set of the pregnant sow data set. However, AGN exhibited relatively weak generalization in DE prediction on the test set of the pregnant sow data set, indicating that there is still room for improvement in achieving more consistent DE predictions across diverse data sets. ANN maintained stable performance in DE prediction across both data sets (including the China feed database and the pregnant sow data set) in both the training and test sets but encountered limitations with the complexity of ME prediction. SVM showed overall lower predictive accuracy for both DE and ME prediction across the two data sets, particularly struggling in modeling complex nonlinear relationships. However, traditional machine learning models like ANN and SVM still have certain advantages, such as lower computational costs and simpler model structures, making them more suitable for smaller data sets or situations requiring rapid model deployment. Additionally, in some specific cases, these traditional models demonstrated better generalization on the test set, especially in environments where the data distribution is relatively simple and consistent.

The study also highlighted certain challenges associated with AGN, such as higher computational costs and complexity when handling large data sets. These factors, combined with the inherent limitations of small sample sizes, impacted the model’s generalization. Future research could focus on enhancing model interpretability and computational efficiency, exploring multiobjective optimization tailored to specific production stages, and refining models to accommodate different energy requirements across pig populations.

These findings support the practical use of machine learning for feed energy prediction and precision feed formulation in swine nutrition. The developed prediction tool allows users to estimate DE and ME conveniently without additional setup, improving the accessibility of machine-learning-based feed energy estimation.
