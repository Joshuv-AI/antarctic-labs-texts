Green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles from melon peel and mint stem: antibacterial, antioxidant, and dye degradation potential
Authors: Mariyam Nasir, Azka Naeem, Linta Zeeshan Khan, Nida Zaib, Hanzala Khan, Zainab Najam, Anum Gul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthesis of nanoparticles employing plant extracts represents a green and sustainable strategy that reduces the use of toxic chemicals and offers a cost-effective alternative to conventional synthesis methods. In this context, the present study focused on the green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) utilizing melon (Cucumis melo) peel and mint (Mentha piperita L.) stem extracts as biological reducing and stabilizing agents. Physicochemical properties of the synthesized ZnO-NPs were characterized using UV-Visible spectrophotometry, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis. Following this, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and photocatalytic activities were evaluated. The results revealed that the average size of NPs was in the range of 35.8–46.5 nm. Both melon and mint derived ZnO-NPs (Me-ZnO-NPs and Mi-ZnO-NPs) showed strong antioxidant activity up to 41% and 83.5%, respectively. When tested against Enterococcus faecalis, Mi-ZnO-NPs demonstrated concentration-dependent effect against bacterial growth while Me-ZnO-NPs showed limited antibacterial effect. Moreover, both NPs found to mediate degradation of synthetic dyes under visible light within 48 h. The degradation efficiency against methylene blue, eosin-Y, and malachite green was found to be 73.7%, 81.2%, and 49.3% (for Mi-ZnO-NPs) and 67%, 65.6%, and 49.07% (for Me-ZnO-NPs), respectively. Hence, the present study effectively utilized agro-waste for ZnO-NPs production which can be useful for diverse biomedical and environmental applications.

Introduction

Nanotechnology involves the design, characterization, and application of materials at the nanoscale (1–100 nm), where novel physicochemical and biological properties emerge as a result of size-dependent effects and a high surface-to-volume ratio1. One of the most promising groups of nanomaterials is represented by metal oxide nanoparticles, which demonstrate superior chemical reactivity, catalytic efficiency, and biological activity relative to their bulk counterparts. These NPs possess unique physicochemical characteristics that support their broad applicability across medicine, energy, environmental science, and materials engineering1,2.

Despite their advantages, the production of nanomaterials often relies on conventional approaches such as chemical reduction, hydrothermal processes, and physical vapor deposition. These methods are typically resource-intensive, requiring high temperatures, costly reagents, and toxic solvents. To overcome these limitations, green synthesis methods can be employed. Biological sources, such as plant extracts and microbial agents, are increasingly recognized for this purpose as they act as natural reducers and stabilizers3,4. Green synthesized NPs offer a cost-effective and environmentally preferable alternative, as they typically reduce the use and generation of hazardous reagents and residues. These benefits thus support their utilization in biomedical and food-related applications4,5.

Among various metal oxide NPs, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) offer distinct advantages attributed to their low toxicity, biocompatibility, and distinct bioactive properties. These characteristics have led to their application in cosmetics, rubber production, paints, sunscreens, and antimicrobial formulations, and also in emerging technologies like biosensors, LEDs, and other commercial applications6,7. ZnO-NPs show a gradual and pH-dependent solubility in biofluids due to surface hydroxylation, allowing controlled Zn²⁺ release8. Also, their surface can be readily functionalized with biomolecules, enhancing their performance for diverse biomedical applications, e.g., drug delivery, bioimaging, wound healing, tissue engineering, and others9.

Thus, present study aimed to synthesize ZnO-NPs via green synthesis approach using extracts from Cucumis melo peels and Mentha piperita L. stem. Although often discarded as agro-waste, these plant parts are abundant in biologically active phytochemicals, including terpenoids, flavonoids, and phenolics, which can function as natural reducing and capping agents in the synthesis of NPs10. The process of ZnO-NPs production using green approach mainly involves three phases: reduction, growth, and stabilization. The phytochemicals present in the plant extracts take part in the reaction mechanism of ZnO-NPs formation. These biomolecules reduce zinc salts (commonly zinc acetate or zinc nitrate) to Zn(OH)₂, which upon calcination or heating, dehydrates to crystalline ZnO-NPs11,12. C. melo (melon), is cultivated globally, with annual production exceeding 40 million tons. Its peels constitute a major portion of the total biomass waste, estimated at 8–20 million tons annually. Valorizing this biomass for NPs production can supports waste minimization and circular bioeconomy efforts13. Another important plant is M. piperita L., well known as peppermint. Mint is highly valued for various health benefits, notably antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory effects14. Although most studies have focused on peppermint leaves, the stems also contain bioactive polyphenols, such as quercetin, rosmarinic acid, and others. These compounds contribute to antioxidant properties and can facilitate NPs synthesis15.

The growing burden of antibiotic-resistant pathogens has increased interest in alternative antimicrobial strategies. Enterococcus faecalis is an opportunistic Gram-positive bacterium frequently associated with hospital acquired infections, particularly urinary tract infections, surgical wounds, and endocarditis, etc. 16. Resistance to vancomycin and other first-line antibiotics has been increasingly reported in multiple strains, making treatment more challenging17. ZnO-NPs are considered as promising candidates against multidrug-resistant organisms. They are known to exhibit antibacterial activity through disruption of bacterial membrane, interaction with intracellular components, and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)18,19.

Beyond their biological activities, ZnO-NPs have also gained attention for environmental application, particularly in the removal of synthetic dyes from wastewater. The discharge of these dyes from textile and printing industries substantially contributes to water pollution and the contamination of aquatic resources. Dyes such as methylene blue (MB), eosin yellow (EY), and malachite green (MG) are non-biodegradable and present major health hazards and ecological risks20. Traditional dye removal approaches, including adsorption, coagulation, and oxidation, often have limited efficiency or generate secondary pollutants21. Sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) is an effective reducing agent for dye degradation but exhibits poor kinetics when used without a suitable catalyst. Moreover, its use also presents safety concerns, as prolonged exposure may lead to skin burns, respiratory discomfort, and neurotoxic effects. ZnO-NPs can serve as catalytic agent by accelerating electron transfer between BH4− and dye molecules, thereby lowering the activation energy and enhancing dye degradation22.

In present study, ZnO-NPs were synthesized using melon peels (referred as Me-ZnO-NPs) and mint stems (referred as Mi-ZnO-NPs). The study is valuable as it explores under-utilized agro waste sources to produce ZnO-NPs. The literature search showed that only few studies have reported ZnO-NPs synthesis using C. melo and M. piperita, and most of the prior studies were focused on leaves or fruit pulp. Furthermore, the antibacterial activity of ZnO-NPs derived from these specific sources against E. faecalis has not been reported. Additionally, studies reporting dye degradation efficiencies of C. melo and M. piperita derived ZnO-NPs are also limited. To address these limitations, the ZnO-NPs synthesized in present work were analyzed using established characterization methods and assessed for their antioxidant, antimicrobial, and photocatalytic activities. In particular, their potential to degrade industrial dyes was explored, underscoring their significance in both environmental and biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade, having purity of 95% or more. Zinc acetate dihydrate (catalog number: 25044) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethanol (10107) was sourced from BDH Laboratory Chemicals. Ascorbic acid (1099–4425) was purchased from Daejung Chemicals. Eosin Y disodium salt (0109-100G) was procured from VWR Chemicals BDH.

Extracts preparation from C. melo peel and M. piperita L. stem

Fresh melon peels and mint stems were first washed thoroughly to remove any surface impurities. The collected plant materials were then dried at 80 °C for 24 h (melon peels) and at 60 °C for 24 h (mint stems) using a hot-air oven. Once fully dehydrated, the materials were finely ground, and 5 g of each powdered sample was transferred into separate beakers filled with 100 mL of Milli-Q water. The resulting suspensions were incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for 30 min and then allowed to cool at room temperature. In the next step, centrifugation was performed at 6000 rpm for 10 min, and the collected supernatants were further clarified by filtration through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The extracts obtained were stored at 4 °C until further processing.

Biosynthesis of ZnO-NPs

A 0.01 M solution of zinc acetate dihydrate was freshly prepared using Milli-Q water. Then, the plant extract was added to this solution in 1:11 ratio. The mixing was done separately for each extract (melon peels and mint stem) in 250 ml media bottles. A pH of 11 was achieved by adding 0.1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH). This was followed by heating the mixtures at 70 °C on a hot plate under constant stirring at 300 rpm for 3 h. Following the heating step, the solutions were centrifuged, and the resulting precipitates were carefully rinsed several times with Milli-Q water. The purified materials were then dried overnight at 60 °C and then calcined at 400 °C for 3 h, yielding the final ZnO-NPs.

Physicochemical characterization of Me-ZnO-NPs and Mi-ZnO-NPs

UV-Vis spectroscopy

The prepared ZnO-NPs from both sources were analyzed using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (EPOCH-2, BioTek Instruments). Absorbance readings were recorded in the range of 300 to 800 nm wavelength. The resulting absorption spectra was analyzed to confirm nanoparticles formation.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The surface functional groups of prepared nanoparticles were examined using a Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer. For this purpose, the nanoparticle suspensions were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min. After centrifugation, the obtained pellets were carefully washed three times with Milli-Q water (each wash at 10,000 rpm for 10 min) and once with ethanol. In the next step, the samples were oven-dried at 60 °C. Then, the purified samples were calcined at 400 °C for 3 h, yielding a fine powder suitable for FTIR analysis. The spectral data was recorded in the range of 4000 to 650 cm− 1, that corresponds to mid-infrared region.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

The average particle size and morphological properties of the prepared ZnO-NPs were examined by using a high-resolution scanning electron microscope (IT100, JEOL EDS System). For this purpose, the dried powder of the prepared nanoparticles was utilized. Elemental composition was determined by using EDX spectroscopy to detect zinc and oxygen, and any minor constituents resulting from plant-mediated NPs synthesis.

Antioxidant activity analysis

To assess the antioxidant activity, DPPH radical scavenging assay was carried out. First, the suspensions of ZnO‑NPs were prepared. For Mi‑ZnO‑NPs, dried nanoparticles were dispersed in Milli‑Q water, while for Me‑ZnO‑NPs, ethanol was used, with final concentrations adjusted to 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. For the assay, 100 µL of 0.02 mM DPPH solution was added to an equal volume of each suspension, and the mixtures were kept at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The assay was conducted in triplicate, and absorbance was recorded at 517 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Antibacterial activity analysis

The prepared ZnO-NPs were examined for their growth‑inhibitory effects against Enterococcus faecalis using a quantitative microtiter plate assay. For this, a fresh culture of E. faecalis was prepared by inoculating 1% glucose enriched Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth, which then incubated at 37 °C under constant stirring for 24 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspension was standardized to the turbidity of a 0.15 McFarland standard. All further steps were performed under aseptic conditions.

For each source, a 500 µl solution of ZnO-NPs was prepared in Milli-Q water, similarly as mentioned before in DPPH assay at the same concentrations (See Sect. 2.5). In each well of a sterile microtiter plate, 140 µl of nutrient broth, 50 µl of nanoparticle suspension, and 10 µl of the bacterial culture were added. Afterwards, the plates were subjected to incubation for 24 h at 37 °C. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Bacterial growth was assessed by recording absorbance at 600 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Photocatalytic activity analysis

To assess the photocatalytic potential, Me-ZnO-NPs and Mi-ZnO-NPs were tested through dye degradation as a model reaction. A series of experiments were performed against selected dye pollutants including methylene blue (MB), eosin yellow (EY), and malachite green (MG). Stock solutions of each dye (100 ppm) were prepared in Milli-Q water and ZnO-NPs suspensions (500 µl) were tested in the presence of NaBH4 (30 mM) serving as the reducing agent.

For MB and EY degradation experiments, a 500 µl aliquot of dye was used as control 1, and 500 µl NaBH4 (30 mM) was used as control 2. The test vials contained 500 µl of dye with 500 µl of ZnO-NPs of each source. Milli-Q water was added to each vial to adjust the final volume to 5 mL. To explore MG degradation, only the dye-only control was used, along with test vials containing dye and synthesized ZnO-NPs. The experiment was done by incubating all the samples at 37 °C under visible light. The absorbance was taken using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at the maximum absorption wavelengths of 660 nm for MB, 520 nm for EY, and 620 nm for MG. The readings were obtained at regular intervals of 0, 30, 60, and 120 min, then 24 h, and 48 h after incubation. Statistical analysis

For graphical representation of data and statistical analysis, GraphPad Prism (version-10) and IBM SPSS statistics (version-26) software were utilized. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Before analysis, the data was assessed for normal distribution and homogeneity of variance using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene statistics, respectively. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Multiple comparisons among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s post hoc test applied subsequently, based on assumption’s check.

Results and discussion

Colorimetric indication of ZnO-NPs formation

The formation of zinc oxide nanoparticles was visually confirmed by a distinct color change during the reaction process. Upon incubating the melon peel and mint stem extracts with zinc oxide (ZnO) solution under the defined conditions, the reaction mixtures gradually turned milky-white / pale-yellowish color. (See Fig. 2). The shift in color indicated the production of ZnO-NPs, consistent with prior reports using C. melo and M. piperita leaf extracts23,24.

Optical, structural, and morphological characterization of prepared ZnO-NPs

UV-Visible spectrum analysis

The bio-reduction of metal ions into ZnO-NPs metal nanoparticles was confirmed through UV-Vis spectroscopy. Both Me-ZnO-NPs and Mi-ZnO-NPs exhibited distinct absorption peaks around 300 nm, as shown in Fig. 3. These peaks are indicative of the characteristic excitonic absorbance bands associated with ZnO nanoparticles. The absorbance spectra obtained in present work corresponds to the previously reported findings, where ZnO-NPs displayed optical absorbance in the range of 280 to 320 nm 25,26. The blue shift observed in current study, compared to ZnO-NPs reported in literature (typically showing absorption maxima around 360–365 nm), indicates quantum size effects. This occurs due to nanoscale dimensions of ZnO-NPs that shift the band-edge absorption towards shorter wavelengths27,28.

FTIR analysis of ZnO-NPs

FTIR examination allowed the identification of functional groups that were present in the plant dried powder and in the NPs derived from both sources. Broad absorption bands around 3,400–3,300 cm− 1 corresponded to O–H stretching vibrations, indicating hydroxyl groups typically linked to alkaloids, as is the case with all FTIR spectra (Fig. 4). A distinct peak, which is roughly positioned at 2,920 cm− 1, is shared by the powders of C. melo peel and M. piperita stem (Fig. 4a and c), suggesting the presence of aldehydic C–H stretching. Absorption bands at 1,625 and 1,500 cm− 1 depict the C = C stretch of alkene and cyclic alkenes. The band, which was roughly 1,382 cm− 1 in Fig. 4a and c, was linked to the sulphate’s S = O stretch. A spectral band detected between 1,240 and 1,260 cm− 1 in both C. melo peel and M. piperita stem powders (Fig. 4a and c), was indicative of the amide III region, typically linked to random coil conformations in protein. Each FTIR spectrum has a peak at roughly 1,052–1,000 cm− 1 which indicates C–O stretching, related to phenolic compounds. An absorption band appearing between 500 and 749 cm− 1 in all FTIR spectra was indicative of R–CH group. These phytochemical-derived functional groups likely adsorbed onto the ZnO-NP surfaces, acting as natural surfactants and contributing to electrostatic stabilization, as reported in similar studies24,29–31.

The smaller size of prepared ZnO-NPs is consistent with the blue-shifted UV-Vis absorption, observed in present study. Next, EDX analysis revealed the elemental composition, suggesting that zinc was present in oxide form in the samples. In the case of melon-derived Me-ZnO-NPs, the EDX spectra showed characteristic peaks for zinc at approx. 1.0 eV and 8.6 eV, and for oxygen at 0.5 eV, as shown in Fig. 5b. Here, strong signals werðe detected for both zinc and oxygen, which were found to be 60.74% and 24.56%, respectively. This suggest the presence of synthesized ZnO-NPs31. The mint-derived Mi-ZnO-NPs also exhibited distinct peaks for zinc and oxygen, as depicted in Fig. 5d. The peaks were observed at the same energy levels as noted for Me-ZnO-NPs36. The prepared Mi-ZnO-NPs contained 35.96% zinc and 17.24% oxygen. The findings indicate successful green synthesis and elemental purity of ZnO-NPs from both plant sources, aligning with the characteristic energy values reported for other plant sources of NPs of the same type38.

Antioxidant activity of synthesized ZnO-NPs

To evaluate the radical scavenging capacity (RSC) of the prepared ZnO-NPs, the DPPH method was employed. DPPH, a stable nitrogen-centered free radical, undergoes a visible color shift from deep violet to pale yellow upon interaction with antioxidant molecules that donate hydrogen atoms or electrons39. This decolorization was quantified by taking absorbance reading at 517 nm.

Among the tested samples, the highest RSC was found for ascorbic acid (25 µg/mL), serving as a reference antioxidant, compared to both Me-ZnO-NP and Mi-ZnO-NP groups (p < 0.001), with RSC values of 93.04% and 95.54%, respectively. In the case of Me-ZnO-NPs, RSC values at 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL were found to be 40.18%, 36.27%, 41.01%, and 39.26%, respectively, with no significant differences observed among test concentrations as shown in Fig. 6a. This may have happened because of the turbidity increasing with the concentration rate, which could interfere with absorbance measurements.

In contrast to this, Mi-ZnO-NPs demonstrated a significant dose‑responsive enhancement in antioxidant activity. At the tested doses of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL, the RSC values were found to be 59.96%, 64.29%, 68.87%, and 83.49%, respectively. The highest concentration of Mi-ZnO-NPs i.e., 200 µg/mL demonstrated significantly greater activity than all other tested concentrations (p ≤ 0.001). Whereas at 100 µg/mL, these NPs showed better RSC compared to that of 25 µg/mL (p = 0.001), as shown in Fig. 6b. These findings align with previous work that reported a concentration-dependent improvement in antioxidant performance of ZnO-NPs. The study on comparative evaluation of M. piperita derived ZnO-NPs found that the bio-synthesized NPs displayed superior antioxidant activity compared to chemically and commercially synthesized ZnO-NPs, owing to their phytochemical composition. Their study reported 52% greater activity of mint derived ZnO-NPs than commercial ZnO-NPs at 15.6 µg/mL 24. Additionally, a previous study on C. melo derived ZnO-NPs reported a concentration dependent increase in DPPH % inhibition, with an IC50 (inhibitory concentration) value of 48.75 µg/mL 31. Overall, in present work ZnO-NPs from both sources demonstrated notable antioxidant activity, with Mi-ZnO-NPs exhibiting a significantly stronger and more concentration-dependent effect.

Antibacterial activity of ZnO-NPs against E. faecalis

The antibacterial effect of the prepared NPs was evaluated by monitoring bacterial growth inhibition of E. faecalis over the period of 24 h. Me-ZnO-NPs showed significant reduction in bacterial growth at all tested concentrations (25–200 µg/mL) compared to the untreated culture control (p ≤ 0.01 to p ≤ 0.001). Overall, the inhibitory effect of these NPs was found to be modest, and no clear concentration-dependent pattern was observed. This suggests limited antimicrobial potency of melon peels derived Me-ZnO-NPs in present work (See Fig. 7a).

Notably, the Mi-ZnO-NPs demonstrated a dose-dependent inhibitory effect. The mean absorbance value reduced to 1.190 at 200 µg/mL from 1.490 in the positive control, with significant inhibition observed at all concentrations relative to the untreated control (p ≤ 0.001). Moreover, significant differences were observed between the highest concentration of Mi-ZnO-NPs (200 µg/mL) compared with all other tested concentrations (p ≤ 0.001), as shown in Fig. 7b. The reduction in absorbance reflects a reduced bacterial cell density relative to the untreated culture-control, thereby validating the enhanced antimicrobial efficacy of Mi-ZnO-NPs. While the actual differences in absorbance values, especially for Me-ZnO-NPs, were relatively small, statistical analysis revealed that the changes were consistent and statistically significant across replicates.

The antibacterial properties of ZnO-NPs is considered to result from multiple processes, including the electrostatic interactions with bacterial cell surface, release of Zn²⁺ ions, hydrogen peroxide formation, and ROS generation40. Although, prior studies reported that E. faecalis is sensitive to ZnO-NPs, there is currently limited evidence specifically addressing the efficacy of ZnO-NPs derived from C. melo or M. piperita extracts against this pathogen41–43. However, ZnO-NPs derived from these sources have shown to be effective in inhibiting other bacterial strains, notably E. coli and S. aureus23,31,44. In present work, Mi-ZnO-NPs exerted a dose-dependent inhibitory effect against E. faecalis, while Me-ZnO-NPs were comparatively less effective.

Photocatalytic activity of synthesized ZnO-NPs

The photocatalytic efficiency of the sustainably synthesized ZnO-NPs was evaluated based on their potential to degrade synthetic dyes commonly found in textile effluents. Time-dependent degradation of MB, EY, and MG dyes were observed under visible light exposure with various reaction conditions optimized. Both nanoparticle variants demonstrated effective dye degradation over a 48 h period, as discussed in the following sections.

Degradation of methylene blue (MB) dye

The degradation profile of MB dye over time, facilitated by the plant‑mediated ZnO-NPs, is presented in Fig. 8. The dye-only control exhibited a gradual increase in degradation, reaching 63.46% after 48 h. The dye treated with NaBH₄ alone resulted in slightly lower degradation (60.43%) at 48 h, highlighting its limited catalytic efficiency under control conditions (without NPs). In contrast, ZnO-NPs derived from both C. melo (Me-ZnO-NPs) and M. piperita (Mi-ZnO-NPs) significantly enhanced dye degradation.

At 500 µL concentration, Me-ZnO-NPs achieved MB degradation up to 67%. Whereas Mi-ZnO-NPs showed superior performance with 73.7% degradation under the same conditions. Although no reports were found on MB degradation using M. piperita derived ZnO-NPs, our findings partially correspond with those of existing reports for ZnO-NPs synthesized from C. melo peel and pulp where rapid MB degradation (65–88%) was achieved within 80 min under UV source23,33. Similarly, agro-waste mediated ZnO-NPs from other green sources such as cinnamon, papaya, bay and soursop leaves extracts have previously shown to degrade MB > 60% within 80 min under different irradiation sources45–47. While our degradation levels were comparatively lower, likely due to differences in irradiation conditions as highlighted by Nhu, et al. 48, the prolonged activity observed over 48 h indicated sustained photocatalytic behavior.

EY dye removal achieved through the action of ZnO-NPs together with NaBH₄ is presented in Fig. 9. The dye-only control exhibited the highest degradation among all groups, reaching 80.17% after 48 h, indicating that EY is partially susceptible to natural decay under ambient conditions. NaBH₄ alone enhanced the degradation moderately, achieving 72.86% at 48 h, with a rapid initial reduction observed at 30 min (27.75%) followed by a gradual increase over time.

Me-ZnO-NPs (500 µL) showed a maximum degradation of 49.07% at 48 h. A notable rise in degradation occurred within the first 120 min, followed by a slower rate of change from 24 to 48 h, indicating an early catalytic effect that tapered over time. Mi-ZnO-NPs displayed a similar trend yielding the maximum degradation of 49.27% at same conditions. Although no prior studies were found to report the MG degradation efficiency of C. melo and M. piperita derived ZnO-NPs, a study conducted using Mentha arvensis showed discoloration of MG upto 41% (at lower concentration, i.e., 10% extract) and 74% (at higher concentration i.e., 20% extract), when exposed to the prepared ZnO-NPs for 2 h under UV light50.

Electron excitation in ZnO-NPs generates reactive species that break down the dye, fading its dark color to colorless form. ZnO-NPs proved to be effective photocatalysts due to their strong oxidative activity, low toxicity, and lack of harmful byproduct formation51. The findings from present study suggest that ZnO-NPs synthesized via green methods can effectively degrade toxic dye, with M. piperita derived NPs offering slightly superior catalytic performance. The improved efficiency may be linked to differences in particle morphology, surface charge, or the nature of phytochemical stabilizers from the two plant extracts.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite its strength, this study has certain limitations. The characterization part was limited to only UV-Vis spectrophotometry, FTIR and SEM-EDX analysis. Other characterization techniques such as Gas Chromatography (GC) and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis could have provided deeper insights into phytochemical composition and crystallographic properties of the synthesized NPs. Furthermore, the antibacterial assays were conducted against E. faecalis only, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the minimal inhibitory and bactericidal concentrations (MIC and MBC values) were also not estimated in this study, which can be explored in future. Next, the dye degradation was monitored only up to 48 h under visible light conditions. The complete degradation was not achieved under these experimental conditions. Further studies are required to determine more optimized parameters to achieve complete (100%) degradation, by varying exposure times and exploring different irradiation sources. Future investigations should also focus on evaluating cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, and environmental impact of these green-synthesized ZnO-NPs. Expanding microbial testing panels and applying these nanoparticles in real-world biomedical and wastewater models will be essential to validate their broader applicability.

Conclusion

The present study successfully employed plant materials typically regarded as agricultural waste for eco-friendly production of ZnO-NPs using Cucumis melo peels and Mentha piperita L. stem extracts. The study reported physicochemical characterization as well as assessment of bioactive and photocatalytic properties of these NPs. Here, ZnO-NPs derived from M. piperita showed superior antioxidant and antibacterial properties, while both nanoparticle types showed effective photocatalytic degradation of all three tested dyes under visible light conditions. The Me-ZnO-NPs and Mi-ZnO-NPs displayed notable dye removal capabilities up to 67% and 73.7% against MB, as well as 65.58% and 81.2% against EY, and 49.07% and 49.27% against MG, respectively, after 48 h of exposure. The antioxidant activity was reported up to 41% and 83.5%, using Me-ZnO-NPs and Mi-ZnO-NPs, respectively. This unveils promising functional activities of the prepared NPs. However, the present work was limited to the use of a single bacterial strain for antimicrobial testing, which restricts broader clinical relevance. Additionally, the antioxidant and dye degradation assays were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully represent their behavior in complex biological or environmental systems. Further studies are expected to explore the enhanced activity of plant-mediated ZnO-NPs in diverse applications.
