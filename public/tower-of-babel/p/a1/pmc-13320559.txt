Effect of rolling surface texture on bearing friction pairs lubrication
Authors: Xinfu Liu, Maosheng Yin, Chunhua Liu, Jinsheng Sun, Shousen Zhang, Li Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInspired by the friction-reducing structure of pangolin scales, biomimetic conical textures were designed on bearing roller-raceway surfaces to improve tribological performance and service life. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) based on hydrodynamic lubrication and cavitation theory was used to simulate the effects of cavitation, inflow direction, and texture parameters (depth, area ratio, and angle) on oil film load capacity. Femtosecond laser fabrication and oil-lubricated friction tests were conducted for validation. The results demonstrate that the load-carrying capacity of the oil film is significantly improved under cavitation conditions. An increased textured area fraction reduces the coefficient of friction, and improves the wear resistance of the contact pairs, whereas an increased texture orientation angle leads to deteriorated lubrication performance. This study provides an effective strategy for optimizing the lubrication of rolling bearing contact pairs and extending bearing service life.Subject areas: Bionics, Engineering, Fluid Dynamics, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Friction and wear, as inevitable phenomena between two contacting surfaces, directly cause sudden mechanical failures and energy waste. A survey study indicates that 30% of energy consumption in industrialized nations stems from mechanical friction losses, while severe wear significantly reduces equipment service life.1,2,3,4 Three-row cylindrical roller bearings, as a type of rolling bearing, are frequently employed in mechanisms subjected to heavy loads and low-speed rotation across diverse operating conditions—such as shield machines and single-point mooring systems—due to their ability to handle different load types through distinct raceways.5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Operating continuously under complex conditions—low speeds, high loads, and severe impacts—these bearings frequently experience failure in their rollers and raceways due to friction and lubrication issues.12,13,14 Consequently, reducing friction and lowering resistance in cylindrical roller bearings has become critical for ensuring efficient, energy-saving, safe, and high-precision equipment operation. This approach also extends rolling bearing service life and safeguards product quality.

Recent theoretical and experimental studies have confirmed that surface texturing can significantly enhance the tribological properties of friction pairs. Consequently, the rational design of surface textures on these pairs is an effective strategy for mitigating friction and preventing wear failure in roller and raceway. The widely accepted mechanisms underlying the friction-reducing effect of surface textures are as follows: (1) textures function as reservoirs for lubricant, ensuring a continuous oil supply to the contact interface under starved lubrication conditions.15 (2) They can trap wear particles, thereby reducing abrasive damage during boundary lubrication.16 (3) Through cavitation effects, textures generate additional hydrodynamic pressure, which enhances the load-carrying capacity of the lubricating film.17

A foundational theoretical model for hemispherical-textured mechanical seals was established by Etsion and Burstein in 1996 using the incompressible two-dimensional Reynolds equation and the semi-Sommerfeld boundary condition. It was concluded that texturing could lead to a 3-fold increase in service life.18 Subsequent experimental research by Shen involved laser machining piston rings with varying textures, which indicated that area density and depth exerted a more pronounced influence on friction resistance than shape.19 Through sequential quadratic programming, texture shapes were optimized, resulting in the identification of a flat-fronted “V” shape for unidirectional sliding and a trapezoidal pair for bidirectional sliding.20 Conversely, a numerical simulation by Sharma comparing protruding textured bearings with non-textured types found that texturing enhanced both load-carrying capacity and the friction coefficient, with these characteristics being sensitive to parameters like the eccentricity ratio.21

Theoretical modeling of surface textures has also been extensively studied. Numerous studies suggest that cavitation effects within the textures are a primary contributor to the generation of net bearing capacity.18,22 Owing to their relative simplicity, the semi-Sommerfeld and Reynolds cavitation boundary conditions are frequently adopted for predicting the lubrication performance of textured surfaces. However, Qiu noted that these conventional boundary conditions inaccurately predict the extent of the cavitation zone, consequently leading to an overestimation of the load capacity.23 In contrast, the Jakobsson-Floberg-Olsson (JFO) cavitation boundary condition offers a more realistic representation. This superiority was corroborated by Zhang’s experimental results, which showed that the JFO model predicts cavitation behavior in groove textures more accurately.24 Despite its accuracy, the JFO model’s application is hampered by numerical instability and convergence challenges. Advances in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) have provided alternative approaches, enabling researchers to optimize texture parameters for enhanced tribological performance through numerical simulation.25 For instance, Guo developed a numerical model for continuous groove textures and systematically analyzed the influence of geometric parameters and sliding speed, revealing optimal lubrication at lower area densities.26 Conversely, Liu’s computational study on micro-dimple textures demonstrated that the load-carrying capacity increases with higher area density.27

In recent years, advances in bionic mechanics have enabled the design of engineered surface structures that mimic—and in some cases surpass—the favorable physical properties of specific biological organisms. Inspired by soil-dwelling animals, Chen used laser cladding to replicate alternating soft-hard structures on gray cast iron (GCI), significantly improving its fatigue wear resistance.28 Similarly, Sui fabricated bionic surfaces on GCI by mimicking the cuticle layers of desert scorpions and certain shell structures.29 In another study, Chen designed irregular gourd-shaped texture parameters to maximize hydrodynamic pressure on stainless steel surfaces through laser texturing.30 Zhang, inspired by the wear-resistant skin of certain organisms, produced two types of bionic units on GCI via laser processing to enhance sliding wear resistance under lubricated conditions.31 Qin32 developed a biomimetic texturing prototype inspired by shellfish surfaces, and fabricated wave-shaped grooves on a biomimetic coating applied to TC4 alloy substrates via laser marking technology. They systematically investigated the mechanism by which the coupling effect of the biomimetic coating-surface texture regulates the tribological properties of the alloy. Their results showed that this coupled structure breaks the continuity of the coating interface, enabling wear particles generated during friction to be readily embedded within the undulating grooves. This not only inhibits three-body abrasive wear caused by the wear particles, but also enhances the solid lubrication effect at the contact interface, facilitating the formation of a continuous and complete lubricating film on the worn surface during sliding. Ultimately, this design achieves a synergistic improvement in the lubrication and anti-wear properties of the material. This study verified the wear particle accommodation effect and lubricating film formation enhancement capability of biomimetic grooved textures, providing key biomimetic design insights for the multi-groove texture structural design in the present work. With respect to the wetting property evolution of biomimetic textured surfaces, Xiao33 proposed a controllable preparation method for shark-skin-like biomimetic textures on IN718 alloy surfaces, and systematically tracked the time-dependent characteristics of surface wetting properties after texture fabrication. Their test results indicated that the wetting state of this biomimetic textured surface undergoes a significant gradient transition over time: the surface was hydrophilic with a water contact angle of only 69.72° at one week post-fabrication; it transitioned to weakly hydrophobic with the contact angle increasing to 104.7° at two weeks post-fabrication; and stabilized in the hydrophobic state with a contact angle reaching 131.56° at three weeks post-fabrication. This study revealed the long-term wetting property evolution of laser-textured biomimetic surfaces, providing important experimental evidence and performance evaluation references for the analysis of wetting and lubrication stability of textured stator rubber surfaces under long-term downhole service conditions in shale oil wells. Research and development status of surface texture

In published studies, surface texturing technology has been extensively applied to reduce friction and drag in the metal rotor-rubber stator friction pairs of screw pumps.34 However, these studies are largely confined to rigid-soft contact, face contact, and bidirectional reciprocating sliding conditions, with the reported textured structures mostly being asymmetric. By contrast, limited research has focused on texturing applied to the roller-raceway rigid line-contact friction pairs in rolling bearings, to address bearing failures caused by raceway surface wear and lubricant film instability. In this paper, through observing the rock excavation movements of pangolins, we discovered that the unique symmetrical structure of their scales effectively reduces the resistance encountered during excavation. Additionally, existing research indicates that pangolin scale-like textures can facilitate the wettability transition from hydrophilic to superhydrophobic surfaces.35 The conical microstructures on the scale surfaces drive the directional spreading of lubricating oil via capillary effects, significantly enhancing the oil affinity of the solid-liquid interface. This in turn facilitates the rapid formation, continuous maintenance, and oil loss resistance of the interfacial lubricant film. Accordingly, laser processing technology was employed to fabricate pangolin scale-mimetic textures onto bearing raceway surfaces. Single-factor analysis examined how varying area ratios, depths, configuration angles, and the presence/absence of cavitation affected the raceway’s friction coefficient and load-carrying capacity. The optimal texture parameter combination for friction reduction was selected, thereby minimizing wear in the bearing roller-raceway friction pair and extending the service life of three-row cylindrical roller bearings.

Results

Effect of texture cavitation and non-cavitation on lubrication performance of bearing roller-raceway friction pairs

Figure 2A presents the oil film pressure distribution contours for the textured surface under cavitation and non-cavitation conditions. During hydrodynamic lubrication, the relative motion drives the lubricant from right to left over the texture. The sudden expansion in the flow cross-section at the texture inlet creates a divergent wedge, leading to a sharp pressure drop and forming a negative pressure zone. Conversely, the constriction at the outlet forms a convergent wedge, generating a significant positive pressure. The net load-carrying capacity is enhanced because the positive pressure at the outlet exceeds the negative pressure at the inlet in magnitude. As observed from the pressure distribution curve along the central axis of the oil film upper wall in Figure 2B, the pressure in the negative-pressure zone at the texture inlet declines under cavitation conditions, whereas that in the positive-pressure zone at the texture outlet rises, in comparison with the pressure profile under non-cavitation conditions. This leads to a markedly higher load-carrying capacity of the oil film under cavitation conditions relative to the non-cavitation scenario. This phenomenon arises because cavitation inside the texture effectively restrains the excessive expansion of the negative-pressure zone at the outlet section of the contact region, thereby lowering the risk of tensile fracture of the oil film. Concurrently, the nucleation and collapse of cavitation bubbles generate an extra dynamic pressure effect, which supplements the oil film pressure within the contact zone. This preserves the stability of the oil film thickness and prevents film rupture caused by high-pressure thinning.

Figure 2C compares the velocity fields and vortex structures of the textured surface with and without cavitation. In the absence of cavitation, the streamlines exhibit a centrosymmetric distribution. The vortices at the texture inlet are small-scale and well-defined, indicating a stable overall flow state. Under cavitation conditions, however, the vortex at the inlet intensifies significantly, becoming both larger in scale and more disordered in morphology compared to the non-cavitation case. This difference arises primarily from the generation and collapse of cavitation bubbles. The dynamics of the bubbles disrupt the symmetrical flow field characteristic of the non-cavitating state, directly disturbing the flow distribution at the inlet region. Concurrently, the collapse of these bubbles generates localized pressure shocks, which promote the formation of intense vortices. The emergence of these strong, localized vortices increases the oil film pressure, thereby enhancing its load-carrying capacity. Therefore, the effects of cavitation will be considered in subsequent simulations.

Effect of medium flow direction on lubrication performance of bearing roller-raceway friction pairs

Under conditions where all texture parameters remain identical, this study investigates the impact of varying inflow directions on the lubrication performance of roller-and-raceway friction pairs. As shown in Figure 3A, the inflow direction aligned with the positive y axis is designated as 0° with counterclockwise rotation representing 90° and 180° flow directions. Simulations for these flow directions yielded the pressure distribution curves on the upper wall surface of the oil film, as shown in Figure 3B. The figure indicates that the normal pressure reaches its maximum at a 0° flow direction, gradually decreasing as the flow angle increases. Meanwhile, the negative pressure values exhibit little variation across the three flow directions. Figure 3C shows the relationship between the average pressure on the upper wall of the oil film and the inflow direction angle. Analysis reveals that, compared to the 0° inflow direction, the average pressure decreases by 27.8% and 42.3% for inflow angles of 90° and 180°, respectively. Figure 3D presents the pressure contours on the oil film wall for these different inflow directions. It can be observed that as the inflow angle increases, the positive pressure zone continuously contracts while the negative pressure zone expands. Consequently, the pressure differential between the positive and negative zones decreases. It is this reduction in the pressure differential that diminishes the hydrodynamic lubrication performance of the oil film, thereby lowering its average pressure. Further analysis indicates that when the inflow direction is 0°, the lubricant flows from the larger end toward the smaller end of the texture, forming a convergent wedge. This geometric feature subjects the lubricant to significant compression at the convergent outlet, generating a region of high positive pressure. Consequently, the hydrodynamic lubrication effect becomes pronounced, substantially enhancing the load-carrying capacity of the oil film and effectively reducing friction and wear. In contrast, when the inflow direction is 180°, the lubricant flows from the smaller end to the larger end, forming a divergent wedge. This configuration results in a drastically reduced positive pressure zone compared to the 0° case, which diminishes the positive-negative pressure differential and thereby weakens the hydrodynamic lubrication effect. For an inflow direction of 90°, the texture inlet and outlet exhibit a symmetrical geometry relative to the flow, which leads to a weakened hydrodynamic performance.

Figure 4 shows the three-dimensional pressure distribution of the oil film under different inflow directions. The pressure contours clearly show that as the inflow angle increases, the positive pressure zone of the oil film continuously shrinks while the negative pressure zone expands correspondingly. It is precisely the synergistic effect between the converging wedge structure (formed at an inflow direction of 0°) and the rolling entrainment effect that markedly enhances the dynamic pressure rise rate in the inlet zone. This accelerates the formation of the lubricating oil film and effectively suppresses the initial oil film rupture in the inlet zone—an issue induced by insufficient lubricant supply and the delayed establishment of dynamic pressure. Consequently, the hydrodynamic lubrication performance of the oil film is significantly enhanced, leading to a higher load-carrying capacity. For this reason, an inflow direction of 0° for the surface texture was selected in all subsequent simulations.

Effect of texture pit depth on lubrication performance of bearing roller-raceway friction pairs

The effects of texture pit depth on the lubrication performance of the roller-raceway friction pair were investigated under the following fixed parameters: a texture angle (θ) of 60°, an oil film thickness (h0) of 10 μm, a triangular base length (1) of 0.8 mm, and a d/a ratio of 1/4. Texture pits with depths of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 μm were designed for area ratios of 18%, 22%, and 27%. The simulation results, obtained using Ansys, are shown in Figure 5. Figure 5A shows the pressure distribution along the central axis of the oil film upper wall for different pit depths. Figure 5B shows the relationship between the maximum bearing pressure and the pit depth. The results in Figure 5A indicate that at a depth of 10 μm, the positive pressure peaks while the negative pressure reaches its minimum, resulting in the maximum pressure difference and thus the optimal load-carrying capacity. As shown in Figure 5B, the maximum load-bearing pressure on the upper wall of the oil film under the three area ratios exhibits a non-monotonic trend with increasing pit depth, first increasing and then decreasing. The maximum pressure is achieved at a pit depth of 10 μm, which aligns with the findings discussed earlier.

Pressure distribution curves on the upper wall surface of an oil film with varying texture pit depths

(A) Maximum bearing pressure distribution curves along the central axis of the upper wall surface under different texture pit depths.

(B) Variation in wall pressure on the oil film and texture pit depth at coverage rates of 18%, 22%, and 27% while maintaining an interfacial angle of 60°.

Further analysis of the oil film pressure contours and velocity vector diagrams, as shown in Figure 6, reveals the underlying mechanism. In Figure 6A, the area of the negative pressure zone initially increases and then decreases with increasing pit depth, while the positive pressure zone shows the opposite trend. At a pit depth of 10 μm, the negative pressure zone at the texture inlet reaches its maximum area, concurrently with the positive pressure zone at the outlet reaching its minimum. This results in the maximum pressure difference, yielding the highest load-bearing pressure and the optimal lubrication effect. The velocity vector diagram in Figure 6B indicates that at a pit depth of 5 μm, the flow field is largely laminar due to the shallow depth, resulting in an insignificant hydrodynamic effect. At a pit depth of 10 μm, vortices begin to form in the divergent zone, and the synergistic effect between hydrodynamic pressure and vortex motion is optimal. As the depth increases further, the vortex regions expand and begin to dominate the flow. The growth of these vortices increases energy dissipation, which consequently diminishes the hydrodynamic performance. Therefore, the oil film attains its maximum load-carrying capacity when the dimple depth matches the oil film thickness. From the perspective of oil film dynamic behavior, a textured dimple structure with a depth consistent with the oil film thickness can form dynamic oil reservoirs. These reservoirs continuously replenish lubricant to the contact zone during roller rolling, compensating for lateral oil leakage under high pressure and preventing oil film rupture caused by insufficient lubricant supply. This configuration also avoids the formation of vortex dead zones induced by excessive dimple depth, ensuring that lubricant can promptly participate in the dynamic maintenance of the oil film. In summary, when the pit depth h equals the oil film thickness (10 μm), the synergy between hydrodynamic lubrication and vortex effects is optimized, resulting in the maximum load-bearing capacity and the best lubrication performance.

Effect of texture area ratio on lubrication performance of bearing roller-raceway friction pairs

Under conditions where a texture angle of 60°, an oil film thickness of 10 μm, and a d/a ratio of 1/4, this study investigates the effect of varying texture area fractions on the lubrication performance of the bearing roller and raceway friction pair. This study investigates the relationship between the maximum wall pressure on the oil film and the textured area fraction for textured pit depths of 10, 20, and 30 μm. Figure 7A shows the pressure distribution curves on the upper surface of the oil film for six different area ratios (10%, 14%, 18%, 22%, 27%, and 33%) when the texture pit depth of 10 μm. It can be seen that as the area ratio increases, the amplitude of the positive pressure zone in the oil film continues to increase, while the negative pressure zone shows relatively small changes. This occurs because a higher area ratio directs more lubricant toward the converging end of the texture. The surface geometry at the convergence point significantly influences lubricant flow more than the cross-sectional shape. Consequently, textures with converging surface geometries generate higher positive pressure at the convergence end, exhibiting stronger dynamic pressure performance. This leads to an increasing pressure differential between positive and negative pressure zones, thereby continuously increasing the maximum load-bearing pressure of the oil film. Simultaneously, the elevated area fraction facilitates the formation of a continuous lubricant replenishment chain within the rolling contact zone. The synergistic lubricant retention effects of adjacent textured dimples maximally suppress the periodic thinning and fracture of the lubricating oil film. Figure 7B shows the relationship between the maximum load-bearing pressure of the oil film and the texture area fraction for different pit depths. As shown in the figure, the maximum load-bearing pressure of the oil film increases with the increase in the texture area fraction for different texture pit depths, corresponding to Figure 7A. Figure 7C shows the cavitation gas phase volume distribution at a pit depth of 10 μm for different area ratios. As the area ratio increases, the cavitation volume grows, leading to a more asymmetric fluid pressure distribution. This asymmetry induces additional load-bearing capacity in the oil film, further enhancing the dynamic pressure performance of the texture. In summary, as the area fraction Sp increases, the maximum bearing capacity on the upper surface of the oil film also increases, resulting in improved lubrication performance of the friction pair.

Effect of texture angle on lubrication performance of bearing roller-raceway friction pairings

Under conditions of a texture area ratio of 27%, an oil film thickness of 10 μm, and a d/a ratio of 1/4, this study investigates the effect of varying texture inclination angles θ on the lubrication performance of the bearing roller and raceway friction pair. This section investigates the variation trend of the average bearing pressure on the upper surface of the oil film as the angle θ changes from 40° to 80°, under texture pit depths of 10, 20, and 30 μm. Results are shown in Figure 8. Figure 8A depicts the pressure distribution curves on the upper wall surface of the oil film at angles of 40°, 50°, 60°, 70°, and 80° when the texture pit depth of 10 μm. The figure indicates that as the angle increases, the maximum pressure in the positive pressure zone gradually decreases while the negative pressure zone remains relatively stable. This phenomenon arises because, with an increase in the texture angle, the converging end transitions from steep to gentle, leading to a progressive reduction in alignment with the rolling entrainment direction. The hydrodynamic film-forming effect is consequently weakened, which exacerbates the lag in oil film formation and triggers localized oil film rupture. Meanwhile, the gradual variation of the converging end diminishes the fluid compression effect at this location, reducing the hydrodynamic pressure enhancement generated inside and around the texture. This degrades the overall hydrodynamic performance, lowers the pressure difference between the positive- and negative-pressure zones of the oil film, and ultimately reduces the average load-carrying pressure. Figure 8B shows the variation curve of the average load pressure on the upper surface of the oil film with angle for weave pit depths of 10, 20, and 30 μm. It can be seen that as the angle increases, the average load pressure of the oil film continuously decreases for different weave pit depths, consistent with the analysis and results in Figure 8A. In summary, a smaller texture angle θ results in greater load-bearing capacity on the upper surface of the oil film, indicating superior lubrication performance of the texture for the friction pair.

Bearing raceway surface texture friction test

Femtosecond laser texturing was applied to the bearing raceway surface employing an LMS12 manufacturing system, as illustrated in Figure 9A. Guided by the simulation outcomes, the texture area ratio and the orientation angle were identified as the governing parameters for surface design; their selected values are provided in Table 1. A schematic of the laser processing setup is presented in Figure 9B. The employed processing parameters were as follows: laser power, 90%; pulse frequency, 200 kHz; scanning speed, 500 mm/s; and number of passes, two. The surface topography of the fabricated specimens was characterized using a KEYENCE VK-X1050 three-dimensional confocal microscope, with representative results shown in Figure 9C. The micrographs reveal that the non-textured matrix regions maintain high flatness, indicating low surface roughness. This characteristic is anticipated to have a minimal effect on the subsequent evaluation of textured lubrication performance. The sporadic micro-pits present in these regions are ascribed to localized energy fluctuations and plasma impact effects inherent to the laser ablation process. Owing to their limited scale, sparse distribution, and non-interference with the primary functional texture and contact paths, their systematic impact on the lubrication performance and experimental findings is judged to be within acceptable limits and is consequently omitted from the analytical considerations.

Texture parameters

Friction properties were evaluated using the UMT-3 friction and wear tester, schematically illustrated in Figure 10A. A steel ball was employed as the friction head, sliding at a constant speed relative to the specimen. Testing conditions included a temperature of 25°C, a friction frequency of 2 Hz, a reciprocating stroke of 12 mm, an applied load of 15 N, and a test duration of 600 s. Figure 10C illustrates the time-dependent variation of the friction coefficient for specimens with different texture area fractions Sp and texture angles θ. The figure clearly shows that specimens with texture exhibit significantly lower friction coefficients than those without texture. Furthermore, for specimens with the same texture angle θ, the friction coefficient decreases as the texture area fraction Sp increases. For the same Sp, the friction coefficient increases with increasing angle θ. These experimental results fully correspond with the simulation results. During the initial wear stage, the significant variation in the friction coefficient is caused by incomplete contact between the steel ball surface and the textured surface. Over time, an oil film gradually forms, enabling complete contact between the steel ball surface and the textured surface. Additionally, the textured surface enhances its ability to store lubricant and trap wear debris, leading to a stabilization of the friction coefficient. Figure 10B shows the average friction coefficients under different texture parameters, providing a more intuitive illustration of texture parameter effects on friction performance. The figure indicates that the texture area fraction Sp exerts a smaller influence on the friction coefficient of the specimen compared to the texture angle θ.

Limitations of the study

Despite the certain results obtained in this study, it is necessary to acknowledge several key limitations. First, this study did not incorporate the influence of multi-physics coupling factors on the dynamic behavior of the lubricant film under extreme operating conditions; this critical gap warrants attention in future research. Second, the influence of contact angle on oil film behavior was not accounted for in this work, and thus the synergistic or antagonistic relationship between biomimetic surface texture and lubrication could not be fully elucidated. Future investigations should incorporate systematic contact angle measurement experiments to quantify the influence of surface energy on the lubrication performance of textured surfaces. Additionally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were not employed to model the complete life cycle of cavitation bubbles, which limits our understanding of the microscopic dynamics of single-bubble formation, growth, detachment, migration, and collapse. Collectively, these limitations highlight important directions for future in-depth research.

Method details

Establishment of the geometric texture model

The pangolin is named for its unique burrowing ability. Its distinctive scales significantly reduce friction and wear from the surrounding environment during tunneling. Based on the shape of pangolin scales, this paper designs a conical structure, as shown in Figure 11A. Figure 11B depicts a three-row cylindrical roller bearing (Model: 130.50.3150), whose lower axial contact pair adopts a cylindrical roller–flat raceway contact configuration. In this contact pair, only the rollers possess a cylindrical convex surface, whereas the raceway is intrinsically flat. Grid independence analysis and solver configuration

This study employs the Fluent module within Workbench 2024 R2 for mesh generation and numerical computation. A three-dimensional double-precision solver is utilized for both meshing and calculations. During mesh generation, the number of cells significantly impacts the precision and accuracy of results. Therefore, mesh independence verification is essential to determine the optimal number of cells. The maximum oil film pressure serves as the criterion for verifying mesh independence. The following mesh sizes are evaluated: 2.7×105, 4.4×105, 6.7×105, 8.2×105, 1.0×106, 1.2×106, and 1.4×106. The maximum pressure on the upper wall surface of the oil film is output for each simulation. Figure 12A illustrates the relationship between maximum oil film pressure and the number of grid cells. It is evident that beyond 1.0×106 grid cells, the curve stabilizes. Calculations confirm an error margin below 5%, demonstrating high convergence. Considering both stability and convergence, the optimal grid cell count is determined to be 1.0×106.

Technical specifications

In this study, the computational domain of the textured lubrication film was constructed using SolidWorks 2024, and numerical simulations were subsequently performed using the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) software ANSYS Fluent 2024 R2. The laminar flow model was adopted for the flow regime, with the fluid phases defined as lubricant and air. The multiphase flow mixture model was employed to simulate cavitation phenomena, and the Schnerr-Saue cavitation model was selected for this purpose.

Laser processing and sample preparation

Biomimetic textures were fabricated on the bearing raceway surfaces using an LMS12 femtosecond laser processing system with the following parameters: laser power of 90% of the rated output, pulse repetition frequency of 200 kHz, scanning speed of 500 mm/s, and 2 scanning passes.

Testing instruments

The three-dimensional surface topography of the textured samples was characterized using a VK-X1050 3D confocal laser scanning microscope. Friction and wear tests were performed under lubricated conditions using a UMT-3 reciprocating universal friction and wear tester. All tests were conducted at a room temperature of 25°C, with a reciprocating frequency of 2 Hz, a stroke length of 12 mm, a normal applied load of 15 N, and a total test duration of 600 s.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The load-carrying capacity of the lubricating film was evaluated by the dimensionless average pressure (Pz) on the upper wall, calculated according to Equation 6.
