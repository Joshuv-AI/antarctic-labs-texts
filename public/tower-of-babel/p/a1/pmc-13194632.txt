Integrative multi-omics analyses reveal the molecular mechanisms of lysozyme in alleviating hepatic lipid accumulation in post-peak laying quail
Authors: Yibin Xu, Sisi Li, Hui Zhang, Ying Yan, Yanfen Ma, Yujiao Lai, Tao Zeng, Wenwu Xu, Linlin Li, Lizhi Lu, Xin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLysozyme is a natural antimicrobial protein increasingly used as an antibiotic alternative in animal husbandry. However, its role in hepatic lipid metabolism, especially in post-peak laying poultry, is not well understood. This study employed a multi-omics approach to investigate the hepatic responses in late-laying quails supplemented with lysozyme (500 mg/kg diet) for 8 weeks compared to a control group. Lysozyme supplementation significantly increased average egg weight during week 1∼4 (P < 0.01). At the end of the trial, it significantly reduced hepatic lipid droplet area and triglyceride content (P < 0.05). Multi-omics analysis revealed that these improvements were linked to a coordinated alleviation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and reductions in lipotoxic metabolites, despite no observed effects on gut morphology. Specifically, lysozyme downregulated key ER stress-related genes, including protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha (EIF2α), and activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) (P < 0.05), as well as lipogenic genes such as fatty acid synthase (FASN) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1) (P < 0.01). It also decreased hepatic sphingoid bases and saturated fatty acids (P < 0.05), contributing to reduced lipogenesis and hepatocyte apoptosis (P < 0.05). Although 16S rRNA sequencing revealed no shifts in overall α- or β-diversity, lysozyme induced specific taxa changes which characterized by increasedabundances of Bacteroides gallinaceum and Olsenella spp., alongside reduced levels of Subdoligranulum and RF39-related bacteria. Mantel correlation analysis further indicated species-specific associations between these microbial changes and host ER stress and lipid metabolism parameters, implicating gut microbiota-mediated modulation of hepatic responses. In summary, these findings demonstrate that dietary lysozyme mitigates hepatic steatosis in post-peak laying quails through coordinated suppression of ER stress and lipogenesis, alongside concurrent specific gut microbial alteration.Keywords: Lysozyme, Laying quails, Multi-omics, Endoplasmic reticulum, Lipid metabolism

Introduction

Quails are the smallest avian species farmed worldwide for eggs and meat production since the early sexual maturity, high egg production, and efficient feed conversion, as well as strong environmental adaptability. Approximately, quails contribute 10% of the global table eggs supply, and their eggs are widely regarded as a nutritionally balanced food suitable for the human diet(Lukanov, 2019). Compared with chicken eggs, quail eggs are more nutrient-dense per gram, with higher levels of essential amino acids, minerals, vitamins, and a superior polyunsaturated-to-saturated fatty acid (PUFA/SFA) ratio. Meanwhile, quails eggs exhibit hypoallergenic properties for individuals sensitive to chicken egg albumen proteins (Tolik et al., 2014).

The liver is a central organ supporting avian egg production, serving as the primary site of lipogenesis, protein synthesis, and lipoprotein assembly. Under estrogen stimulation, the liver synthesizes vitellogenin and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) which are secreted into the bloodstream and subsequently transported to the ovary, where they contribute to follicular development and yolk formation(Mann and Mann, 2008; Eck et al., 2023). In addition, the liver also contributes to systemic immunity through the production of acute-phase proteins and cytokines and the detoxification of harmful metabolites, thereby safeguarding both maternal health and reproductive performance(Kubes and Jenne, 2018; Yang et al., 2023). Following peak production, laying birds enter a post-peak phase characterized by a gradual decline in reproductive performance accompanied by age-related metabolic adjustments. During this stage, sustained energy intake combined with reduced metabolic efficiency may predispose the liver to lipid accumulation and increased oxidative burden(Geng et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Meanwhile, liver dysfunction is often associated with alterations in the gut microbiota, impaired intestinal barrier function, and an increased risk of bacterial translocation(Gao et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023).

Lysozyme, a natural antimicrobial enzyme, has been widely studied as a feed additive in poultry production. It exerts bacteriolytic activity by hydrolyzing the peptidoglycan layer of bacterial cell walls and is considered as safety alternative to antibiotics(Ferraboschi et al., 2021). Previous research has demonstrated that dietary lysozyme supplementation can improve growth performance, modulate immune responses, and enhance antioxidant capacity in poultry and piglets(Wu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025). However, most existing studies primarily focused on gut health or general performance traits, while the direct or indirect effects of lysozyme on hepatic metabolism and reproductive efficiency in late-laying birds remains poorly characterized, especially in quail. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of dietary lysozyme supplementation on hepatic lipid deposition and laying performance in post-peak laying quail, with the aim of elucidating potential role of lysozyme in maintaining hepatic metabolic homeostasis and providing a theoretical foundation for its broader application in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Animals and diet

The animal trial was performed according to Ethics Committee of the China Jiliang University (No.2025-012) in compliance with Chinese Animal Welfare Guidelines. The 8-month-old Korean quails (a domesticated line of Coturnix japonica) with no significant difference in laying performance were randomly assigned into two group, control (CON) and lysozyme supplementation (LYZ) group. Each group with 5 replicates and 40 birds per replicates. At this age, birds had passed the peak of egg production (around 15 weeks) and were therefore considered to be in the post-peak laying stage (Narinc et al., 2013). After one week baseline period, the birds were fed either basal diet or basal diet with 500 mg/kg egg white-derived lysozyme (5000 U/mg, derived from Zhejiang King Techina Feed Co., Ltd.) for 8 weeks. The basal diet (Table 1) formulated according to the requirements of poultry (NRC, 1994). The lighting program was maintain at 16 h light and 8 h dark during trial period.

Sample collection

At the end of this experiment, the fecal samples of each replicate were pooled to form one composite biological samples per replicate. Meanwhile. one bird of approximately average weight of each replicate were randomly selected and fasted for 24 h. Blood was collected from the jugular vein and allowed to precipitated at room temperature, centrifugated for 15 min at 3000 rpm and 4°C to obtain serum samples. Following euthanasia, the right lobe of liver was separated and a segment was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and the other remaining tissue were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

H&E and Oil Red O staining

The harvested liver were dehydrated, cleared and embedded in paraffin. Then, tissues were cut into serials sections at 5 μm depth for subsequent staining with H&E and Oil Red O staining according to previous instruments(Zhang et al., 2025). Images were captured under a microscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, JP).

TUNEL staining

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay was performed using a one-step TUNEL apoptosis kit (C1088, Beyotime Biotechnology). Fixed liver sections were treated with proteinase K at 37°C for 20 minutes for antigen extraction and incubated with TdT enzyme and fluorescein-dUTP at 37°C for 1 h. Slides were stained with DAPI to visualize nuclei and photographed under a fluorescence microscope (BX-61, Olympus Corp., Center Valley, PA).

Serum and hepatic biochemical indices

Serum levels of metabolic indicators glucose (GLU), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lipid profile including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), antioxidant-related indices (malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), calcium (Ca), and phosphorus (P) were measured using spectrophotometric methods. Serum concentrations of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), estradiol (E2), and progesterone (P4) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) following the manufacturers’ instructions. All commercial kits were purchased from Beijing Huaying Biotechnology Company, Beijing, CN.

Hepatic total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) contents were determined using commercial assay kits (Beijing Huaying Biotechnology Company, Beijing, CN) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, approximately 100 mg of liver tissue was homogenized in ice-cold physiological saline and then centrifuged at 3000×g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain the supernatant. The concentrations of TC and TG were measured spectrophotometrically and expressed relative to liver tissue weight.

DNA extraction and16S amplicon analysis

Total microbial DNA was extracted from feces according to the instructions of QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, DE). The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were determined by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop® ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). The hypervariable region V3-V4 of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using the forward primer 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and the reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). After library preparation, the PCR products was mixed and resolved via 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The recovered products were purified with an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA) and quantified with a Quantus™ fluorometer (Promega, USA). A library of purified PCR products was established using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit and sequenced on the Nextseq2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

The raw sequences were merged with FLASH (v1.2.7) and quality filtered with fastp (0.19.6). The sequences were imported into QIIME2 v2022.8 for demultiplexing and construction of an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table using DADA2. Bacterial 16S ASVs were assigned a taxonomy using the SILVA database (version 138) as the reference.

Metabolomic analysis

The liver samples (100 mg) were homogenized in 800 μL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1 (v:v) containing four internal standards (0.02 mg/mL l-2-chlorophenylalanine, etc.) by the Wonbio-96c (Shanghai wanbo biotechnology co., Ltd., Shanghai, CN) frozen tissue grinder at −10 °C and 50 Hz for 6 min. Subsequent low-temperature ultrasonic extraction was performed at 5°C and 40 kHz for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was collected for ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) analysis at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Techonology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, CN).

Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA) using a gradient elution program with mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in 95:5 water:acetonitrile) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile:isopropanol:water = 47.5:47.5:5). The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min and the column temperature maintained at 40 °C. Mass spectrometry was conducted on a SCIEX Triple TOF 6600 system in both positive and negative ESI modes. Data acquisition and peak integration were performed using SCIEX OS V1.4 software. Metabolites were identified by querying the KEGG, HMDB, and LIPID MAPS databases, and pathway analysis was carried out with MetaboAnalyst 5.0.

Transcriptomic analysis

RNA-seq of liver was performed by Majorbio and Novagene on an Illumina sequencing platform. The sequencing raw data were subjected to quality control using Fastp (v0.19.5) and mapped to the reference coding gene set using Bowtie2 (v2.4.1). The clean reads were used to analyze differential expression using limma R package (v3.56.2). The screening threshold of differentially expressed genes was as follows: |log2Fold Change | ≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis based on the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database was conducted using the R packages of clusterProfile (v3.8.1).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA extraction from liver tissue (1 mL TRIzol per 50 mg of tissue) were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. After qualification and quantification, cDNA was synthesized by HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR(+ gDNA wiper)kit (R223; Vazyme, Nanjing, CN). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711; Vazyme, Nanjing, CN) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The relative mRNA expression of target genes was normalized to that of β-actin using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt (Livak, 2001). The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR (Real-time Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction) were listed in Table 2. The amplification procedure was 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, and PCR products were confirmed using melting curves.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). After confirming normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test, differences between two groups were assessed by Student's t-test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The correlations of differentially expressed genes and differentially abundant metabolites with clinical phenotypes were evaluated using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) R package (v1.73). Gene-metabolite associations were further explored with the mixOmics R package (v6.30.0). Data visualization was conducted in RStudio (version 2023.03.0; Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA), and the final figures were assembled using Adobe Illustrator (version 25.2; Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA).

Results

Effect of lysozyme supplementation on egg production of quails

During weeks 1 to 4, dietary lysozyme supplementation did not significantly affect laying rate, average daily egg weight, or unqualified egg rate (P > 0.05, Table 3). However, average egg weight was significantly increased in the lysozyme group compared with the control (P = 0.003). From weeks 5 to 8, lysozyme supplementation showed no significant effects on laying rate, average daily egg weight, average egg weight, or unqualified egg rate (P > 0.05). Meanwhile, there were no significant differences in laying rate, average daily egg weight, average egg weight, or unqualified egg rate between the two groups over the entire experimental period (weeks 1 to 8).

Effect of lysozyme supplementation on intestinal morphology and hepatic lipid accumulation

The duodenal villi of both the CON and LYZ groups were regularly arranged, and no significant differences were observed in villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), or the VH/CD ratio between the two groups (Fig. 1A and B). However, Oil Red O staining revealed extensive lipid droplet accumulation in the livers of the control group, whereas markedly fewer droplets were observed in the lysozyme group (Fig. 1C and D). Furthermore, quantitative analysis exhibited that hepatic triglyceride (TG) content was significantly reduced in the LYZ group (P = 0.028), whereas no significant difference was observed in the content of total cholesterol (TC) between groups (Fig. 1E and F).

Effect of lysozyme supplementation on liver metabolites changes of quail liver

The non-targeted metabolomic results revealed that metabolites were mainly classified as lipids and lipid-like molecules (28.28%) and Organic acids and derivatives (25.30%) (Fig. 2A). Most metabolites were shared between groups, with a total of 2352 common metabolites, comprising 1040 detected in negative ion mode and 1312 in positive ion mode. In addition, 16 unique negative-mode and 28 unique positive-mode metabolites were identified in the LYZ group, whereas the CON group showed 13 unique negative-mode and 23 unique positive-mode metabolites (Fig. 2B). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) demonstrated a clear separation between the LYZ and CON groups (R2X = 0.636, R2Y = 0.999, Q2 = 0.512), suggesting that lysozyme supplementation changed the hepatic metabolic profile (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, a total of 82 differential abundance metabolites (DAMs) were identified based on threshold of VIP > 1 and FDR < 0.05 between two groups, of which 16 were upregulated and 66 were downregulated in response to lysozyme supplementation (Fig. 2D, Table S1). Meanwhile, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that these DAMs were involved in FoxO signaling pathway, purine metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 2E). The classification of DAMs was presented in Fig. 4E, where there are 18 organic acids and derivatives and 17 organoheterocyclic compounds, collectively accounting for 42.68% of the total DAMs (Fig. 2F). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) further clustered the 82 DAMs into three distinct metabolite modules (Fig. 2G). The M_ME2 module was significantly negatively correlated with hepatic triglyceride (TG) content. A total of 13 DAMs within the M_ME2 module were selected to construct a metabolite co-expression network using Cytoscape (Fig. 2H), in which N6-Carbamoyl-l-Threonyladenosine and 7-Ethylguanine showed broad correlations with other metabolites.

Effect of lysozyme supplementation on liver transcription of quails

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effect of lysozyme supplementation on liver, transcriptomic analysis was conducted. A total of 440145392 clean reads were obtained after quality control and filtering across ten samples (five per groups). The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed an obvious separation between the CON and LYZ group, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles between treatments (Fig. 3A). In total, 8581 and 9311 genes were expressed in the LYZ and CON groups, respectively. Among these, 8289 genes were shared, while 1022 and 292 were uniquely expressed in the LYZ and CON groups (Fig. 3B). Applying the thresholds of adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1, a total of 115 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 53 upregulated and 62 downregulated in the LYZ group relative to CON group (Fig. 3C, TableS2). The KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways associated with immune system, including antigen processing and presentation, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum and intestinal immune network for IgA production (Fig. 3D).

Furthermore, WGCNA identified three gene modules, among which the G_ME1 module showed a significant positive correlation with liver TG content (Fig. 3E). To further identify key regulators within this module, a protein-protein interaction network was constructed, and cytoHubba analysis identified LRAT, HSP90B1, PDIA4, RYBP, TMEM119, NFE2L2, CALR, LOC107307278, LOC107307240, and LOC107325734 as the top 10 hub genes among the 43 genes in the G_ME1 module (Fig. 3F). These hub genes may represent critical molecular nodes through which lysozyme influences hepatic lipid metabolism and endoplasmic reticulum-associated protein processing.

Integrative analysis of metabolomic and transcriptomic data involved in lipid accumulation

To dissect the coordinated regulatory patterns between transcriptomic and metabolomic alterations in response to lysozyme supplementation, an integrative multi-omics analysis was conducted using the multi-block sPLS-DA (sparse Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis) framework implemented in the mixOmics package. This supervised multivariate approach performed simultaneous dimension reduction and feature selection across multiple omics layers, enabling identification of variables that jointly discriminate biological groups while capturing cross-omics covariance. Based on parameter tuning, the optimal numbers of selected features were determined as 20 genes and 6 metabolites on the first component, and 5 genes with 6 metabolites on the second component. These selected loading elements constituted the most discriminative multi-omics signature separating the CON and LYZ groups (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, the correlation circle plot further revealed that several ER stress-related genes including CALR, HSP90B1, HSPA5, HYOU1, PDIA4 and DAMs including heptadecasphinganine, 2-aminooctadecane-1,3-diol, diphenylphosphinic acid, and p-cresol sulfate were clustered together in the same quadrant (Fig. 4B). Specifically, group-wise comparisons revealed that lysozyme supplementation significantly reduced hepatic levels of 2-aminooctadecane-1,3-diol, diphenylphosphinic acid, heptadecasphinganine, and p-cresol sulfate (Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, expression of CALR, HSP90B1, HSPA5, HYOU1, PDIA4, and PDIA6 were also markedly reduced in the LYZ group (Fig. 4D).

Effects of lysozyme on hepatocyte apoptosis, unfolded protein response, and lipid metabolism

Compared to the control group, lysozyme supplementation for 8 weeks significantly reduced hepatocyte apoptosis, as evidenced by a decreased number of TUNEL-positive cells (Fig. 5A) and lower expression of the CASPASE3 and CASPASE9, alongside upregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2 (Fig. 5B).

Although the canonical UPR sensors including ERN1 (IRE1α) and ATF6 showed no significant change, EIF2AK3 (PERK) expression was significantly reduced in the lysozyme group, alongside marked downregulation of its downstream effectors EIF2S1 (eIF2α) and ATF4 (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, in terms of lipid metabolism, the expression of key enzymes involved in de novo lipogenesis, including ACC1 (Acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1) and FASN (Fatty acid synthase), was significantly decreased. In contrast, the expression of genes related to mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, such as PPARA (Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha) andPPARD (Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor delta), remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). These findings collectively indicate that lysozyme reduces hepatic ER stress and apoptosis while selectively inhibiting lipogenic pathways

Effect of lysozyme supplementation on fecal microbial composition and diversity of quails

As shown in Fig. 6A, the quail gut microbiota was dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes at the phylum level. Lysozyme supplementation increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes while reducing that of Firmicutes. At the species level, Bacteroides caecicola, s_unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, and s_unclassified_g_Lactobacillus were identified as dominant taxa, all of which showed increased relative abundance in response to lysozyme treatment (Fig. 6B). Besides, lysozyme supplementation had no significant effects on Chao1, Shannon, or Observed Species indices, indicating no notable changes in microbial richness or evenness (Fig. 6C). Similarly, beta diversity based on Bray-Curtis distances at ASV level revealed no significant separation between groups (Fig. 6D). LEfSe and differential abundance analysis (Fig. 6E and F) further identified s__unclassified_g__Subdoligranulum and s__uncultured_bacterium_o__RF39 as significantly enriched in the CON group, whereas s__Bacteroides_gallinaceum and s__Olsenella_sp._Marseille-P2300 were significantly enriched in the LYZ group.

Mantel correlation analysis reveals species-specific associations between gut microbiota and host multi-omics features

To comprehensively evaluate the relationships between the four differentially abundant gut bacterial species and host multi-omics indicators, we performed a Mantel test and integrated the results with a pearson correlation matrix of the host variables (Fig. 7). Among the host variables, hepatic triglyceride (TG) content and lipid area showed broad and significant positive correlations with several top-loading features from the metabolomics and transcriptomics datasets. In contrast, expression of hepatic BCL2 exhibited a consistently negative correlation with these features and lipid deposition traits. Notably, EIF2AK3 and its downstream transcription factor ATF4 showed strong positive correlations with part of the selected loading elements, consistent with an upstream regulatory role of the EIF2AK3-ATF4 axis in shaping these transcriptomic and metabolic signatures (Fig. 8).

Meanwhile, s__unclassified_g__Subdoligranulum and s__uncultured_bacterium_o__RF39 exhibited pronounced positive Mantel correlations (solid lines) with ER stress-related genes such as ATF4, HSP90B1, HSPA5, and HYOU1. These results indicated that fluctuations in two species abundances may be involved in host ER stress processes. Collectively, the combined Mantel and pearson correlation network clearly delineates species-specific microbial-host associations and highlights potential functional segregation of gut microbial members in ER stress regulation and hepatic lipid metabolism.

Discussions

Lysozyme, first discovered by Alexander Fleming, was a natural antimicrobial protein secreted by various animal tissues and widely distributed in saliva, tears, milk, and the intestinal mucosa(Fleming, 1997; Priyadarshini and Kansal, 2002; Shahmohammadi, 2018; Liu et al., 2021). Although lysozyme is well known for its bactericidal effects against Gram-positive bacteria through hydrolysis of the peptidoglycan layer, accumulating evidence has revealed additional physiological roles of lysozyme, including modulation of gut microbiota, improvement of epithelial barrier function, enhancement of antioxidant capacities and attenuation of systemic inflammation.

In laying birds, the liver is a central organ for lipid synthesis and transport besides the intestine. During the post-peak laying period, laying birds are prone to hepatic lipid accumulation and steatosis due to sustained or elevated energy intake relative to declining reproductive demands(Imtiaz et al., 2025). While the intestinal benefits of lysozyme have been well characterized, its impact on hepatic metabolism remains largely unexplored.

In the present study, lysozyme supplementation significantly increased average egg weight during 1-4 weeks (P < 0.05) and showed a trend toward improved laying rate in 5-8 weeks, suggesting a beneficial impact on production performance. The serum biochemical analysis revealed no significant changes in hepatic function markers, antioxidant levels, or sex hormone concentrations between two groups. Meanwhile, the histological analysis of the duodenum revealed dietary lysozyme supplementation had minimal impact on villus height, crypt depth, and villus-to-crypt ratio. However, Oil Red O staining and biochemical assays consistently showed reduced hepatic lipid deposition and triglyceride (TG) content in the lysozyme-treated group. Considering that previous studies in aging laying hens have shown that hepatic TG is not necessarily correlated with circulating lipid parameters(Wang et al., 2026), the lack of change in serum TG in the present study suggests that lysozyme primarily modulates hepatic lipid metabolism rather than systemic lipid levels.

Through non-targeted metabolomic analysis, a total of 82 differential metabolites (DAMs) were identified (FDR < 0.05, VIP > 1), including 16 upregulated and 66 downregulated compounds. These were mainly classified as organic acids and derivatives and organoheterocyclic compounds, jointly accounting for 42.68% of all DAMs. Notably, fatty acids such as palmitic acid (16:0), 12-Methyltridecanoic acid, and dodecanoic acid were significantly reduced in the lysozyme group, indicating adjustments in the availability of lipid synthesis substrates and membrane lipid components. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of FoxO signaling, purine metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism, pointing to alterations in energy metabolism and stress response pathways.

From a transcriptional perspective, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that lysozyme treatment led to a distinct separation between the control and lysozyme groups. A total of 115 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and enriched in pathways related to antigen presentation, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and mucosal IgA immune networks. To investigate the cross-level mechanism underlying the observed reduction in hepatic lipid deposition, we performed WGCNA separately on DEGs and DAMs with hepatic TG content. The results indicated that both datasets were partitioned into three modules. Among these, metabolite module M_ME2 was negatively correlated, whereas gene modules G_ME1 were positively correlated with hepatic TG content. In the multi-block sPLS-DA analysis, we found that the 25 DEGs and 12 DAMs with the highest loading contributions were clustered together in the third quadrant, indicating a coordinated pattern of variation across transcriptional and metabolic levels. Notably, DEGs such asCALR, HSP90B1, HSPA5, HYOU1, and PDIA4 are key regulators of ER protein folding and stress responses, while differential metabolites including heptadecasphinganine and 2-aminooctadecane-1,3-diol represent sphingoid bases. In addition, p-cresol sulfate, a microbially derived metabolite of aromatic amino acids, emerged as a toxic compound with potential implications for hepatic stress.

Recently, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that sphingoid bases and their downstream sphingolipid metabolites exert multifaceted detrimental effects on the liver. Metabolomic analyses in patients with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) have identified that increased sphingosine d18:1 [So(d18:1)] could enhance the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and promote disease progression by directly inhibiting HIF-2α transcriptional activity in liver macrophages(Xia et al., 2024). In parallel, hepatic levels of sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) are markedly elevated in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) models, which is accompanied by activation of hepatic stellate cells and liver fibrosis(Osawa et al., 2024). Meanwhile, the enrichment saturated fatty acid such as palmitic acid in liver were direct precursors for the synthesis of ceramides. The accumulation of ceramides was a well-established driver of hepatic lipotoxicity since ceramides could impair insulin sensitivity by inhibiting AKT/PKB signaling and promote hepatocyte apoptosis by inducing ER and mitochondrial stress(Wei et al., 2006; Hajduch et al., 2021; McNally et al., 2022). Although p-cresol sulfate has been extensively implicated in renal oxidative injury and inflammatory responses, its specific contribution to hepatic dysfunction remains largely unexplored.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a central organelle in lipid metabolism, coordinating the synthesis, modification, and trafficking of cholesterol, fatty acids, and triglycerides. Excess lipid burden or aberrant lipid composition disrupts ER membrane fluidity and stability, increases the folding demand of secretory clients and promotes the accumulation of misfolded proteins within the ER lumen(Basseri and Austin, 2012; Binayi et al., 2024). These alterations elicit ER stress and trigger unfolded protein response (UPR) through three canonical sensors including inositol-requiring transmembrane kinase/endoribonuclease α (IRE1α), protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor (ATF6)(Henkel and Green, 2013). Under physiological conditions, these sensors are maintained in an inactive state through their association with molecular chaperones, primarily GRP78/HSPA5/BiP. Upon ER stress, the chaperones preferentially bind to the accumulating misfolded substrates, leading to the dissociation and subsequent activation of sensors and downstream signal pathways. Although these responses are initially protective and aim to restore proteostasis, persistent ER stress could change UPR from adaptive to proapoptotic and promote inflammation and cell death via pathways such as PERK-ATF4-CHOP and IRE1-JNK and exacerbate hepatic steatosis(Cao et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2022). For instance, previous studies demonstrated that ER stress induced by long-term high-fat or high-sucrose diets accompanied with marked by elevated chaperone expression of GPR78 and a shift toward pro-apoptotic signaling via CHOP. This chronic stress disrupts metabolic homeostasis and also downregulates key proteins like NRF2 and CPT1α which ultimately leads to an exponential increase in fatty acid synthesis and oxidative damages(Flister et al., 2018; Binayi et al., 2024).

In our study, lysozyme supplementation markedly attenuated hepatic ER stress of late-laying quail, as indicated by the downregulation of multiple ER-resident chaperones and folding enzymes, including HSPA5, HSP90B1, HYOU1, CALR, PDIA4, and PDIA6. To further delineate the underlying mechanism, we examined the three canonical UPR branches and found that only the PERK (EIF2AK3) arm was significantly suppressed at the transcriptional level, while ERN1 (IRE1α) and ATF6 remained unaffected. This selective inhibition was accompanied by a marked decrease in EIF2S1 and ATF4 mRNA levels. ATF4 has been identified as a key regulator of SREBP-1c activation, an important transcription factor of hepatic lipogenesis(Fusakio et al., 2016; Yeh et al., 2017). It could directly activate the expression of ubiquitin specific peptidase 7 (USP7) and deubiquitinates SREBP1c, and increases its protein content in cell(Chen et al., 2016). Therefore, the downregulation of ATF4 may account for the observed suppression of lipogenic genes such as FASN and ACC1, despite the genes involved in β-oxidation including PPARA and PPARD remaining unchanged. This interpretation is largely consistent with prior reports in which attenuating PERK activity and ATF4 expression could mitigate ER-stress and associated lipid accumulation through exogenous interventions such as N-acetylcysteine and Hugan Qingzhi tables (Tsai et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024).

Given the mediating role of the gut microbiota along the gut-liver axis(Ding et al., 2023), we performed 16S rRNA sequencing to investigate microbial alterations between the control and lysozyme groups. Although lysozyme supplementation did not alter α-diversity or β-diversity metrics, it significantly increased the relative abundance of s__Bacteroides_gallinaceum and s__Olsenella_sp._Marseille-P2300, while decreasing s__unclassified_g__Subdoligranulum and s__uncultured_bacterium_o__RF39. Previous studies have shown that Bacteroides species are important carbohydrate fermenters capable of producing short-chain fatty acids such as acetate and propionate, which can modulate hepatic lipid metabolism by suppressing de novo lipogenesis and improving lipid turnover through the gut-liver axis(Sun et al., 2024). Members of Olsenella, belonging to the Actinobacteria phylum, have been most frequently reported as colonizers of oral cavity and are associated with endodontic infections. Recently, Olsenella spp. have exhibited positive association with patients with ulcerative colitis(Kraatz et al., 2011; Gryaznova et al., 2021). Meanwhile, Subdoligranulum genus has been linked to inducing lipid metabolic disturbances and intestinal inflammation in growing pigs with low birth weight(Huang et al., 2020). To explore the potential function of the significantly altered microbial taxa in underlying the host transcriptomic and metabolomic alterations, Mantel correlation analysis was carried out. It was demonstrated that Subdoligranulum and RF39 was positive correlations with ER stress-related genes, including ATF4, HSP90B1, HSPA5, and HYOU1. In contrast, Bacteroides_gallinaceum and Olsenella_sp._Marseille-P2300 showed strong positive associations with hepatic lipid metabolic indicators. Collectively, our results indicate that lysozyme mitigates post-peak hepatic lipid accumulation potentially through modulation of specific gut microbial taxa, with downstream effects on hepatic ER stress and sphingolipid metabolism. This mode of action is distinct from that of other natural products which primarily exert their hepatoprotective effects through activation of the bile acid-FXR axis, leading to inhibition of hepatic lipogenesis and inflammation(Wang et al., 2023).

However, several limitations should be considered in this study. Firstly, this study examined the effects of a single dose of hen egg white-derived lysozyme on post-peak laying quails, which is a commonly used commercial source and dosage level shown to be effective in poultry nutrition trials. However, different sources (e.g., recombinant microbial lysozyme) or varying supplementation levels might exert different biological effects. Systematic dose-response and source-comparison studies are therefore required to optimize application parameters and improve generalizability of the findings. Second, the true causal roles of these bacterial taxa in vivo warrant further validation through approaches such as fecal microbiota transplantation, gnotobiotic animal models, or bacterial monoculture administration, to determine whether they serve as drivers of the observed metabolic phenotypes. Finally, it remains unclear whether prolonged lysozyme exposure would sustain the observed benefits or lead to ecological shifts in the gut microbiota beyond the 8-week experimental period.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that dietary lysozyme supplementation significantly reduces hepatic lipid accumulation and apoptosis via PERK/EIF2α/ATF4 axis to alleviate ER stress in post-peak laying quails without compromising systemic homeostasis or reproductive performance. Meanwhile, lysozyme supplementation significantly increased the abundance of gut Bacteroides gallinaceum and Olsenella spp., while decreasing Subdoligranulum and RF39-related bacteria. These microbial alterations were further associated with host ER stress and lipid metabolic profiles, suggesting a potential microbiota-mediated contribution to hepatic metabolic regulation. These findings provide a novel mechanistic basis and practical reference for optimizing hepatic health in egg-laying poultry via functional feed additives.
