Distinct physiological and transcriptomic responses between tolerant and susceptible rapeseed (Brassica napus) germplasm to flooding stress
Authors: Ronghao Guo, Yongli Fu, Xing Su, Zhengda Ge, Wenxuan Chai, Yanni Zhao, Jungang Dong, Keqi Li, Weinan Sun, Zhen Huang, Chengyu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWaterlogging and submergence are major abiotic stresses impairing rapeseed development and hence evaluation of submergence tolerance ability is crucial for the development of tolerant lines through breeding approaches. This study investigated submergence impact on the survival rate, physiological responses, and transcriptomic variation of the young seedlings of 706 Brassica napus germplasm. We identified 57 highly tolerant and 109 highly susceptible accessions underwent six days submergence. Most accessions originated from the downstream of Yangtze River region had higher tolerance level. SOD enzymic activity increased both in the five representative tolerant and susceptible genotypes under submergence stress. POD activity also increased in the tolerant genotypes whereas CAT activity decreased, showing a opposite trend compared to the susceptible genotypes. The tolerant genotypes showed less chlorosis and produced less malondialdehyde than that in the susceptible group, while soluble protein content increased more than that in the susceptible genotypes. Transcriptome analysis detected 5,072 and 5,063 up-regulated genes, as well as 3,512 and 3,889 down-regulated genes in the tolerant / susceptible group in comparison to their respective control. Many genes encoding the transcription factors in AP2/ERF, bHLH, MYB, WRKY, and NAC families were also significantly regulated. The differentially expressed genes shared by the two pairs comparison were mainly enriched in such KEGG pathways as circadian rhythm, tryptophan metabolism, and porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism. The specific pathways in the tolerant group were enriched in carbon metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of amino acid, while the susceptible group was more distributed in hormone signal transduction, sucrose and starch metabolism, and vitamin B6 metabolism etc. The results uncovered distinct physiological and transcriptomic differences between tolerant and susceptible genotypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12863-026-01423-9.Keywords: Rapeseed, Submergence, Tolerance, ROS, Transcriptome, qRT-PCR

Introduction

Excessive soil water caused stress of waterlogging or even flooding / submergence to crops, leading to serious crop yield losses all over the world [1]. Waterlogging or submergence stress have adverse effects on plants development during their entire lifecycle [2–4]. Plants undergoing waterlogging will suffer from hypoxia (a condition characterized by low oxygen concentration) and subsequent oxidative stress, energy deficiency, impaired nutrient uptake by the roots, leaf chlorosis, and vulnerability to fungal diseases [5, 6]. Plants have developed diverse mechanisms to deal with waterlogging stress, including alterative respiratory pathways, antioxidant protection, plant hormone regulation, or even morphological alterations [5]. In the plant antioxidant system, superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) are the main ROS (reactive oxygen species)-scavenging enzymes and help plants effectively combat oxidative stress [5]. Their activity levels can reflect the plant condition suffering from various stresses. Malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the products of membrane lipid peroxidation, which can reflect the degree of plant membrane lipid peroxidation. Chlorophyll content can indicate the photosynthetic performance of plants and the growth status of leaves since it is the most important substance in the plant body, serving as a carrier for light energy absorption and transport, as well as an important medium for electron transfer. In addition, soluble protein content is also widely used as an important indicator of stress response. Recently, QTL mapping [7, 8] and transcriptome analysis [9–14] are frequently employed to systematically uncover the information about plants responses to various abiotic stresses including waterlogging.

The germination, growth and development of rapeseed (oilseed rape, canola), a world’s leading oil crop, are frequently destroyed by waterlogging and flooding, which leads to serious yield loss except seed quality [15]. China is one of the world’s largest rapeseed growers, whose semi-winter-type rapeseed is mainly cultivated in paddy soil in Yangtze River basin, being prone to waterlogging stress caused by soil compaction and poor drainage, and the frequent seasonal precipitation. Waterlogging damage may occur at various stages of rapeseed growth and development but seed germination and seedling establishment are more vulnerable to waterlogging stress. Waterlogging during rapeseed germination process can lead to slow or even failed germination [11, 16, 17] and waterlogging during the seedling stage can cause poor root development, incomplete leaf development, slow growth, and even death of seedlings [9–11, 18]. Waterlogging during the seedling stage not only exaggerate the cork and lignification of rapeseed roots but also has a greater impact on plant dry weight, gas exchange, leaf water potential, and root hydraulic conductivity than other growth stages [19–21]. The seedling with an undeveloped root system is most vulnerable to waterlogging / submergence.

Above all, it is of great significance to develop and grow waterlogging / submergence tolerant variety to ensure the successful cultivation. Screening for waterlogging tolerance in the field is a highly variable because the differences on temperature, pH, and nutrient supply, which are affected by soil properties [2, 4]. Many laboratory-based screenings for waterlogging use highly controlled conditions to isolate anoxia stress by removing compounding factors in the field [2]. Zou et al. [22] found that rapeseed genotypes with higher waterlogging tolerance at the seedling stage also exhibited higher tolerance at later stages. In this study, 706 rapeseed accessions (Brassica napus L.) of various origins were used as to screen submergence tolerant germplasm. Some extreme types of tolerance / susceptible genotypes were identified, and the physiological responses of the two types were compared. The transcriptome of 24 h submerged seedlings of the tolerant/susceptible groups were compared with their respective control to uncover the important genetic regulatory mechanism and biological pathways of contributing to submergence tolerance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

A total of 706 rapeseed accessions including 668 domestic accessions with known origins from 12 provinces, 30 Chinese accessions without pedigree and origin records, and eight cultivars from Canada, Australia, UK, France, and Germany (listed in Supplementary file dataset S1) were used. All the seed samples are maintained by our team. The seeds are fresh and healthy with > 97% germination rates.

Methods

Evaluation of submergence tolerance

The results of several previous studies had proven that rapeseed genotypes with higher waterlogging tolerance at the seedling stage also exhibited higher tolerance at later stages [22]. Therefore, we modified a reproducible method conducted at the young seedling stage based on the results of our pre-experiments [23] and previous literatures [22]. Firstly, the healthy seeds after surface sterilization were embedded on two-layer moistened paper in germination boxes. The boxes were placed in growth chambers at day/night temperature of 25℃/22℃, with a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. One week later, when the cotyledons of young seedlings became flat and true leaf just formed, distilled water was added to fully submerse the seedlings. After six days of stress treatment, waste water in the box was poured and the seedlings were return to above-mentioned normal condition. The survival rate (SR) of the sample with four biological replicates was recorded in three days. The resistance levels of the samples were classified as highly tolerant (SR = 100%), moderately tolerant (80%< SR<100%), weak tolerant (50%< SR ≤ 80%), susceptible (20%< SR ≤ 50%), and highly susceptible (SR ≤ 20%) as in a previous study [24].

Measurement of physiological indicators

Based on the results of above screening experiment, five resistant genotypes (including ZY267, N111038, SY1929, Dd199, and S11AF) and five susceptible genotypes (including H9958, QQF, Z532AF, Jade, and 530BCD) with steady phenotypes in our experiment field (data not showed) as well as far genetic distances between them were selected for future study. Considering that the rotted nascent seedlings of the susceptible accessions during submergence was not suitable for detecting physiological indicators, we attempted to postpone the treatment in larger seedlings. Their seed samples were sown in pots filled with matrix-vermiculite at a ratio of 3:1. When growing to three-leaf stage, the seedlings were submerged into a water tank for 5 days. The leaves of the treated plants were used to determine the physiological indicators, each with three replicates. Several physiological indicators, including chlorophyll, SOD, POD, CAT, MDA, and Soluble protein, of the ten selected germplasm after submergence treatment were extracted respectively by using suitable solvent. Chlorophyll content was estimated by Arnon formula based on the absorption at 663 nm and 645 nm. SOD activity was measured spectrophotometrically based on inhibition in the photoreduction of Nitrogen Blue Tetrazole. POD activity was detected by the absorbance at 470 nm in a reaction system containing of H2O2 and guaiacol. CAT can decompose H2O2 and its activity can be inversely measured by the fluorescence intensity of residual H2O2 in the reaction system, at an excitation/emission wavelength of 535/587 nm. The MDA content was determined by a colorimetric method after a reaction with thiobarbituric acid. The content of soluble protein was determined after Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 stain.

Transcriptome analysis

The young seedlings of five resistant and five sensitive germplasm were divided into two groups: the control group cultivated under normal conditions and the treatment group submerged in water for 24 h. Then equal amounts of young seedlings of each genotype were mixed for RNA extraction, with three biological replicates for each sample. The subsequent processes of mRNA isolation, reverse transcription of cDNA, preparation of amplicon library etc. were performed by Biomarker Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). RNA-seq was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA) following the standard Illumina PE150 protocol. The raw data were deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus with accession GSE314699 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and Genome Sequence Archive (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) with accession CRA031976. All the clean sequences (Supplementary file: Table S2) were mapped onto the ZS11.v0 rapeseed genome (https://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnIR) using HISAT2 software (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/). StringTie (https://github.com/gpertea/stringtie) was applied to assemble the mapped reads and to discovery of novel transcripts compared with the ZS11.v0 rapeseed genome. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by the criteria of Fold Change ≥ 2.0 and P value < 0.01. Venn plot analysis, Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment, gene clustering analysis, and transcription factor prediction involved in our present study were conducted on Biomarker Cloud platform (www.biocloud.net/).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA in the leaves of the tolerant / susceptible plants submerged for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h was extracted using a RNAiso Kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan) and reversely transcribed into cDNA using FastKing Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The primers for 12 randomly selected genes (Supplementary file Table S3) were designed using Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast) and rapeseed beta-actin7 served as the internal reference gene for data normalization. The experiment was conducted on a QuantStudio 7 PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) with three biological replicates for each gene.

Results

Screening results of submergence resistance

The young seedlings of rapeseed germplasm showed quite different condition after six days submergence. The tolerant accessions had green leaves and cotyledons, thick hypocotyls, and well-developed root systems (Fig. 1a). The susceptible seedlings were dead and completely rot in two days (Fig. 1b). Most weak tolerant plants showed partially dead seedlings with transparent cotyledons, hypocotyl, or root. After submergence stress, survival rates varied significantly among the 706 accessions (Table S1), with a mean of 66.34%, demonstrating substantial variation in submergence tolerance across rapeseed varieties. We identified 57 highly tolerant and 109 highly susceptible germplasm (Fig. 1c). The survival rates of rapeseed germplasm from northern provinces of China including Qinghai, Henan, and Gansu often susceptible than those originated from southern China including Zhejiang, Hunan, Sichuan and Chongqing (Table 1), in line with previous result [24]. The results indicated that most of the tolerant germplasm came from southern region with high precipitation levels and soil moisture. We selected five tolerant genotypes and five susceptible (Table 2) with stable phenotypes in field (data not showed) and far genetic distances as two presentative groups for further physiological and transcriptomic studies.

Physiological responses between the tolerant and susceptible genotypes

After submergence treatment, the average SOD enzyme activity (Fig. 2a) of the five tolerant genotypes increased to 143.57 U·g− 1·min− 1, which was 56.11% higher than the control, while the SOD activity of the susceptible genotypes increased to 124.67 U·g− 1·min− 1, only 28.32% higher than the control. The variation range of SOD activity in tolerant genotypes and susceptible groups were 45.62%~79.52% and 20.08%~35.93%, respectively (Fig. 2b). Under submergence treatment, the average POD activity increased by 14.73% in the tolerant genotypes, while it decreased by an average of 18.03% in the susceptible genotypes. Submergence treatment resulted in the CAT activity of the tolerant group decrease by 18.04% but an increase by 17.78% in the susceptible. The results showed that the ROS scavenging SOD and POD in the tolerant genotypes are activated more greatly than in the susceptible genotypes. After submergence treatment, the average MDA content of the resistant genotypes increased only by 18.21% but 90.96% in the susceptible genotypes (Fig. 2d). The variation range of MDA content in the tolerant genotypes was 11.51%~27.47% and ‘Ning11038’ showed least increase. The variation range of MDA content in the susceptible genotypes was 82.58%~102.57% and ‘H9958’ had the largest increase. The MDA content in the susceptible genotypes was significantly higher than that in the tolerant genotypes, indicating that submergence stress caused greater damage to the cell membrane of the susceptible genotypes, since the genotypes has weaker ability to remove ROS. Under submergence stress, the content of soluble protein in both genotypes increased significantly (Fig. 2e), by about 54.7% in the tolerant genotypes but only ca. 18.6% in the susceptible genotypes. This indicated that the tolerant genotypes may have stronger protein turnover under submergence stress than the susceptible genotypes. Under submergence stress, the visible changes were that the leaves of the susceptible plants were more chlorotic than the tolerant. The chlorophyll content in both groups decreased (Fig. 2f), by average 11.3% in the tolerant genotypes but 32.5% in the susceptible genotypes, indicating more serious impacts on the susceptible genotypes.

Transcriptomic comparison between tolerant and susceptible group

The results of transcriptomic comparison (Table 3) showed that there was a total of 8,584 DEGs, with 5,072 upregulated, in the comparison between the submergence tolerant group and its control (R_CK vs. R_T). Similarly, the submerged susceptible group had 8,952 DEGs, with 5,063 upregulated, compared with its control (S_CK vs. S_T). Furthermore, we found that 3,607 upregulated DEGs were shared by the tolerant and susceptible groups (Fig. 3). There were 1,423 genes only upregulated in the tolerant group but not in the susceptible group. There were 1,739 downregulated DEGs shared by both groups, of which 1,873 genes were only downregulated in the tolerant group. The statistical data intimated distinct transcriptional regulation models and different responsive patterns to submergence between the tolerant and susceptible genotypes.

Screening of the transcription factors related to submergence responses

Many transcription factors are involved in stress response, including AP2/ERF, WRKY, bHLH, NAC, and MYB, etc., which affect plant tolerance to stress by regulating the expression of stress response genes. In the all DEGs in our present transcriptome analysis, a total of 6,180 transcription factors were predicted, divided into 69 subfamilies. The three leading groups were 479, 477, and 470 members in AP2/ERF, bHLH, and MYB families (Fig. 5). The transcription factors of AP2/ERF were mainly enriched in spliceosomes, plant-pathogen interactions, and MAPK signaling pathways. There were 60 and 72 upregulated AP2/ERF genes, as well as 24 and 17 downregulated, in the tolerant and susceptible genotypes, respectively. bHLH members are mainly involved in MAPK signaling pathway, plant-pathogen interaction, and hormone signaling transduction pathway. We found 47 and 41 bHLH members upregulated, and 34 and 25 downregulated in the tolerant /susceptible genotypes, respectively. We found 29 and 39 members upregulated, and 21 and 17 downregulated in MYB transcription factors, which are mainly participate in the plant-pathogen interaction, in the tolerant/susceptible genotypes. The WRKY transcription factor, which plays an important role in plant stress resistance, mainly enriched in plant pathogen interactions, MAPK signaling pathways, and spliceosomes. There were 74 and 74 upregulated WRKY members, and 13 and 9 downregulated in the tolerant/susceptible genotypes. There were 28 and 33 upregulated genes, 11 and 9 downregulated DEGs encoding NACs in the tolerant/susceptible genotypes respectively. In addition, most DEGs in AP2/ERF, bHLH, MYB, WRKY, and NAC families was up-regulated both in the tolerant and susceptible genotypes, compared to their respective controls. A interaction network (Fig. 5) among the identified transcription factors in our transcriptome analysis was predicted based on String platform (https://cn.string-db.org/). The results showed that WRKY33, WRKY40, WRKY53, ERF5, ERF6, ERF 109 etc. played important roles in response or tolerance to submergence treatment.

Discussion

Effects of submergence on tolerant and susceptible rapeseed

Under waterlogging/submergence stress, rapeseed seedling is in a state of hypoxia, which limits its aerobic respiration and energy metabolism. Submersion or waterlogging causes hypoxia conditions in plants with a hindrance in the exchange of oxygen and respiration, disrupting the physiological and biochemical processes of plants. The phenotypic responses to submergence stress indicates that the rapeseed germplasm have a lot of differences from the tolerance types. The genetic model of waterlogging tolerance rapeseed followed a mode of ‘major genes + minor polygenes’ with a high heredity [25]. Therefore, early generation screening for waterlogging/submergence tolerance with a high heredity is feasible. Zou et al. [22] showed that the waterlogging tolerance of rapeseed in the seedling stage was consistent with that in the mature stage, and screening for waterlogging tolerance in the germination stage was more simple and effective. Therefore, the evaluation results of the submergence tolerance levels of the nascent seedling of the 706 rapeseed accessions will be useful for design of future rapeseed breeding scheme to develop variety suitable for the Yangtze River region.

Based on our present results, the ROS scavenging ability of submergence tolerance genotype was stronger than that of susceptible genotype, and the damage on tolerant genotype was smaller than that of the susceptible genotype. In our study, after submergence treatment in the three-leaf stage, both SOD and POD activities increased and the increase of SOD activity in the tolerant genotype was higher than that in the susceptible genotype, consistent with the results of Zhang et al. [26]. However, some other studies found that after waterlogging stress, the POD activity of both tolerant and susceptible genotypes decreased [27]. Slight decrease of CAT activity in our present study maybe different from the results of many previous literatures. The difference in changes in these antioxidant enzymes after waterlogging/submergence stress may be caused by different treatment conditions, sampling time (submergence / desubmergence) [28], and plant genus in various studies.

MDA is derived from membrane lipid oxidated by excessive ROS and thus MDA content is often negatively correlated with the tolerance to many stresses such as heat, salt, drought, and waterlogging. In our present study, after submergence stress, the accumulation of MDA in the tolerant rapeseed was lower than that in the susceptible rapeseed, which is consistent with other previous results. This indicates that submergence stress leads to the accumulation of a large amount of ROS in the plant body, but the tolerant genotype has a strong ability to clear reactive oxygen species, causing less cell damage than the susceptible genotype. Soluble proteins can be used as osmoregulatory substances to resist stress. In our present study, after submergence stress, the soluble protein content in the leaves of both tolerant and susceptible genotypes increased, and the increase in soluble protein content in the tolerant genotypes was significantly higher than that in susceptible genotypes, which is consistent with previous research results. The decrease amount of chlorophyll content in the tolerant genotypes was significantly lower than that in susceptible genotypes, consistent with previous results [11].

Transcriptome analysis of rapeseed response to submergence stress

Most transcriptomic comparison often use a pair of tolerant / susceptible genotypes without control of the genetic background but this design may result in too many false positive DEGs casually. We try to control of the genetic background by using the mixture of two tolerant / susceptible pools, each containing five representative genotypes. In addition, we made an attempt to screen the group-specific DEGs and the common DEGs shared by the tolerance/susceptible genotypes in comparison to their respective control. These shared pathways showed the similar trend of submergence responses of different rapeseed genotypes. The differential expression between the tolerant and susceptible genotypes may be a reflect of the physiological mechanisms in the plants adapt to submergence stress. Notwithstanding, we should acknowledge that there are too many difficulties to find the key genes and pathways determining the tolerance levels only by transcriptomic comparison. In addition, the strategy of mixing five tolerant or five susceptible genotypes into one RNA sample is not suitable for detecting genotype-specific variation.

In addition, it was found that the number of upregulated genes encoding transcription factors such as AP2/ERF, bHLH, MYB, WRKY, and NAC in the DEGs between the tolerant and susceptible genotypes and their respective controls was higher than the number of downregulated genes. These transcription factors can regulate the expression of multiple key genes, thereby affecting the growth of rapeseed seedlings and their ability to cope with submergence. For example, some AP2/ERF members can regulate numerous genes related to stress response, promoting seedlings growth and development under flooded conditions [29, 30]. Many bHLH and MYB members can participate in plant biosynthesis and signal transduction, helping to maintain good physiological balance in rapeseed. Some WRKY and NAC members can regulate cell apoptosis and redox, helping seedling effectively cope with submergence/flooding threats. Therefore, understanding the functions and roles of these transcription factors is of great significance for a deeper understanding of the growth and adaptation mechanisms of rapeseed seedlings under submergence conditions.

Conclusion

This comprehensive study of the transcriptomic and antioxidant activities of B. napus underwent submergence stress during the early seedling stage is of great reference value for plant breeders to develop submergence- and waterlogging-tolerant variety. The identified DEGs and biological pathways may act as useful biomarkers to characterize plant performance in breeding programs under submergence stress. This study not only provides technical and theoretical guidance for the screening and breeding of flood / waterlogging tolerant rapeseed germplasm, but also lays the foundation for further research on the physiological and molecular mechanism of submergence tolerance of rapeseed.
