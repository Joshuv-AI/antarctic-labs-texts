Engineering macrophages for effective and safe targeting of CD47 cancer cells in the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Fernando Torres Andón, Ailenis Rosales Sánchez, Alba Pensado-López, Rosario García-Campelo, Chris H Takimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor-associated macrophages are key myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), acting as essential orchestrators of innate and adaptive immune responses. The efficacy of current antitumoral treatments can be promoted by macrophages, thanks to their phagocytosis, tumoricidal activity, and eliciting of adaptive immunity; or restricted by their expression of inhibitory counter-receptors (such as programmed death-ligand 1 or signal regulatory protein alpha). Furthermore, the continuous recruitment of these myelomonocytic cells into tumor tissues makes them attractive candidates for cell therapy with the development of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) effector cells. This evidence highlights the strong therapeutic potential of macrophage engineering for the treatment of solid tumors. In this line of research, Du et al developed pArg1-CD47 CAR-Mφ based on intrinsic Arg1 promoter responsiveness for TME-specific activation of cytotoxicity, effectively overcoming SIRPα inhibition against CD47+cancer cells. In preclinical murine models of breast and gastric cancer, this macrophage cell therapy demonstrated significant regression of established tumors with minimal toxicity towards erythrocytes. Although translating this work from mice to humans remains a significant challenge, it provides hope for the design of myeloid cell therapies with antitumoral efficacy and safe profile for solid tumors.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are central orchestrators within the tumor microenvironment (TME), influencing cancer initiation, progression, and elimination. TAMs are major drivers of immunosuppression, causing resistance to conventional oncological treatments (chemotherapy or radiotherapy) and newer approaches (targeted therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells).1 These innate immune cells actively support tumor growth via processes like angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, proliferation, and metastasis. Conversely, properly stimulated macrophages can actively destroy tumor cells via phagocytosis and direct cytotoxicity, engaging both the innate and adaptive antitumor immune systems. In solid tumors, perturbed hematopoiesis often skews differentiation toward myelomonocytic precursors, fueling the continuous recruitment of new macrophages infiltrating the primary tumor site. These intrinsic properties—plasticity and sustained recruitment—position monocytes/macrophages as promising effector cells for cancer treatment.2

The cluster of differentiation 47/signal regulatory protein alpha (CD47/SIRPɑ) axis is a critical immune checkpoint. Binding of CD47 on cancer cells to SIRPɑ on macrophages triggers an inhibitory “don’t eat me” signal, protecting malignant cells from phagocytosis.3 Blocking this interaction in the presence of pro-phagocytic signals activates macrophage-mediated killing, enhances antigen presentation, and triggers antitumor adaptive immunity. The overexpression of CD47 on cancer cells drove the development of the first anti-CD47 monoclonal antibody, magrolimab. However, CD47 is also broadly expressed on normal cells; thus, blocking CD47 also has the potential to cause unintended effects such as anemia, thrombocytopenia, or dysregulated innate immune response. Despite initial promise in hematologic malignancies (high-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (hrMDS), acute myeloid leukemia (AML)), large-scale Phase 3 trials failed due to safety concerns, including an increased risk of serious infections, respiratory failure, and anemia caused by CD47 expression on healthy erythrocytes.4 This highlights the need for improved tissue targeting strategies to restrict the activity to the TME.

The rational engineering of CAR-Mφ leverages the inherent advantages of these innate immune cells (ie, superior infiltration and plasticity) to target the CD47/SIRPɑ axis, override checkpoint inhibition, promote phagocytosis, and effectively reprogram the TME to induce immunostimulatory antitumor immunity. This approach specifically addresses CAR-T cell limitations in the treatment of solid tumors, such as poor blood extravasation, limited infiltration, and exhaustion within the immunosuppressive TME. Pioneer work by Klichinsky et al demonstrated the viability of anti-HER2 CAR engineering using Ad5f35 adenovirus in primary human monocyte-derived macrophages, promoting effective M1-like antitumor polarization and achieving tumor suppression.5 Subsequently, these anti-HER2 CAR-Mφ were shown to sensitize solid tumors to anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) immunotherapy.6 This promising preclinical success has rapidly moved into Phase 1 clinical trials, targeting HER2 (NCT04660929), next-generation HER2 CAR-monocytes (NCT06254807), and through in vivo programming using messenger RNA (mRNA)-lipid nanoparticles to target TROP2 (NCT05969041), and EGFRvIII/GPC3 (NCT06478693). Of note, research is rapidly advancing toward in vivo CAR engineering, specifically the use of nanoparticle platforms to deliver CAR-encoding mRNA, to enable the in situ modification of the immune cells.7 Despite significant advances, consensus remains that design improvements of CAR-M are still required for enhanced efficacy and safety.

A relevant example is the pArg1-CD47 CAR-Mφ construct developed by Du et al (see figure 1).8 To optimize selective antigen-targeting, two CD47-single chain variable fragment (scFv) constructs (366 bp and 720 bp) were evaluated. These constructs showed minor differences in CD47 binding, killing and phagocytosis in vitro and exhibited similar CAR-Mφ biodistribution and antitumoral efficacy following intravenous injection in subcutaneous murine models of breast cancer. However, the shorter scFv showed preferential accumulation in the spleen over the liver, potentially enhancing systemic antitumor activity (not tested). The shorter construct was selected for further experiments, anticipating higher transfection and expression efficiency in lentiviral systems. Taking advantage of their previous work, Du et al achieved a significant improvement in antitumoral activity by integrating the α1β1-FcγRI complex into the CAR construct.8 This complex provides superior phagocytosis compared with earlier CD47 or HER2 CAR-Mφ designs based on classical 4-1BB and CD3ζ intracellular domains.9 Although these previous constructs had already demonstrated antigen-specific phagocytosis, M1 polarization, cytotoxic T-cell activation, and tumor regression in syngeneic tumor models (including humanized immune systems), the new α1β1-FcγRI domain, in CD47 breast cancer murine models, provides superior phagocytosis and drives antitumor activity characterized by the induction of apoptosis (similar to CD47 antibodies), ferroptosis, and NF-kb activation.

Other researchers created more complex CAR structures focused on disrupting the CD47/SIRPɑ axis. Chen et al engineered a new enhanced synthetic phagocytosis receptor (eSPR).10 This receptor integrates an FcRγ-driven phagocytic signaling domain and a built-in secreted CD47 blocker (soluble SIRPα decoy), allowing for antitumor activity even under low-target antigen conditions. The eSPR construct induced sustained M1-like antitumor TAM reprogramming, boosted direct tumor cell engulfment, and promoted cytotoxic T cells across tumor models, even those with heterogeneous antigen expression. By demonstrating that cross-presentation-competent eSPR macrophages achieved durable tumor-specific memory immune responses (abrogated by host immunodeficiency), this work underscored the critical role of tumor antigen capture and presentation for T-cell priming and activation. Instead of directly targeting CD47, Zhang et al engineered CAR-Mφ by fusing a humanized HER2 scFv with the phagocytosis-activating domain FcγRIIa and integrating shRNA to silence SIRPα.11 This combination of the “eat me” signal and suppression of the “don’t eat me” signal endowed CAR-shSIRPα-Mφ with increased phagocytic activity and M1-like antitumor activity, mediated by induction of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. This resulted in significant cytotoxic effects on HER2-positive tumor cells and patient-derived organoids in vitro. Notably, SIRPα knockdown in CAR-Mφ was a safer strategy than monoclonal antibody blockade, as it did not destroy red blood cells or cause neurotoxicity. Reduction of tumor growth and prolonged survival was observed, mediated by activation of innate and adaptive immunity.

The critical challenge of dose-limiting erythrocyte toxicity associated with systemic CD47 blockade was addressed by Du et al,8 by designing an in situ arginase (Arg1) promoter-regulated CAR-Mφ, as a “Trojan horse”, that remains inactivated during systemic transfusion and activates specifically only after infiltration into the TME. High Arg1 activity is characteristic of TME metabolism, observed in patients with cancer, that promotes TAMs to produce the Arg1, making its expression in macrophages a hallmark of the immunosuppressive TME.12 The genetically integrated Arg1 promoter restricted cytotoxic activation to the tumor site, as demonstrated by its expression gradually increasing after CAR-Mφ infiltration, producing negligible off-target killing. Biodistribution experiments confirmed this safety, showing lower accumulation for the pArg1-CD47 CAR-Mφ versus the constitutive CD47 CAR-Mφ in most off-target organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) and blood. Unfortunately, this enhanced safety strategy showed a minor reduction in tumor accumulation and, consequently, lower antitumoral activity in vivo. Despite improved tumor suppression with higher doses, treatment failed to completely eliminate the tumor, strongly suggesting that future clinical application will likely require combination therapy strategies that leverage complementary antitumor mechanisms.

Building on TAM reprogramming strategies, researchers are developing new CAR-Mφ therapies and testing them alone or in combination to achieve enhanced and sustained effective innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses. While the direct engineering of CD47 CAR-Mφ remains limited, combinations of other CAR-Mφ with anti-PD-1 or anti-CD47 therapies have already been explored.13 For example, Greiner et al developed CAR-Mφ targeting CSPG4 for melanoma, demonstrating high infiltration and specific phagocytosis in three-dimensional spheroid models and murine models, and antitumoral efficacy enhanced in combination with anti-CD47 antibodies.14 Zhou et al prepared CAR-Mφ using enucleated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as targeted delivery vehicles for CAR-encoding plasmid DNA.15 The enucleated-MSCs actively homed to the TME, resulting in complete tumor suppression when combined with CD47-blocking therapies in an orthotopic glioblastoma mouse model.

The clinical application of CAR-Mφ therapy still faces critical challenges, starting with limited cell numbers due to the negligible proliferative capacity of primary monocytes and macrophages in vitro and in vivo. Addressing the bottleneck of insufficient and costly peripheral blood monocyte isolation, researchers are turning to scalable stem cell sources: human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)). Abdin et al generated CAR-iMacs from these sources using bioreactor technologies,16 and confirmed their sustained pro-inflammatory properties by single-cell RNA. Similarly, Shen et al developed an efficient monolayer culture system producing macrophages from a single hPSC17 and created a second-generation anti-CD47 CAR-Mφ, which required interferon (IFN)-γ and monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) to maintain their M1-antitumoral phenotype in vivo. Cell survival is also a critical factor often unevaluated. Engineered CAR-Mφ expressing colony stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1) or interleukin-12 cytokines showed improved persistence from 2 weeks up to 1 month in the TME,18 while stimulation with IFN-γ and MPLA or adenovirus transduction demonstrated survival exceeding 60 days in tumor-free mice.17 Further research combining new signaling domains with TLRs, FcRγ, PI3K, Megf10, CD86, or with other immune checkpoints such as CD24, MHC-I, STC-1, TIM-3, SLAMF3/4 has the potential to enhance CAR-Mφ phagocytosis, antigen-dependent M1 polarization, tumor infiltration, and survival. Furthermore, combination with conventional treatments, such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy, must not be dismissed.

Current research strategies rely on immortalized cell lines (eg, murine RAW 264.7 or human THP-1) and partially immunodeficient mice, which limits our understanding of the full immune regulatory effects in humans. Thus, further focus on developing human monocyte-derived CAR-Mφ and fully characterizing their complex interplay with the host “human” immune system is required for optimizing clinical outcomes.

In the specific case of CD47 blockade, the therapeutic mechanisms extend beyond macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, including dendritic cells cross-priming and T-cell activation, which must be tested in fully immunocompetent models.3 The route of administration and dosage are also crucial factors requiring more investigation. Leveraging new nanotechnological approaches or physical stimuli (eg, advanced electroporation) to overcome the natural resistance of monocytes/macrophages to viral transfection, but also to enable in vivo engineering and/or in situ CAR generation specifically in the TME7 will be essential to bypass the toxicities associated with systemic CD47 blockade.

Ultimately, the success of CAR-Mφ cell therapies which bridge innate and adaptive immune activation necessitates a deep knowledge of the patients immune system at both systemic and tumor tissue levels, while effective clinical evaluation and translation requires appropriate infrastructure and qualified professionals. Although translating from mice to humans remains a significant challenge, this research offers hope for designing myeloid cell therapies with antitumoral efficacy and a safe profile to treat solid tumors.
