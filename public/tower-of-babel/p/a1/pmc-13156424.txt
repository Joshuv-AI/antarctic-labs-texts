Double Asymmetric Spin Echo EPI (dASE‐EPI) Enables fMRI of the Entire Rat Brain at 9.4 T
Authors: Kyle A. Johnson, Hanbing Lu, Jason W. Sidabras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeDevelopment and evaluation of a double asymmetric spin echo planar imaging (dASE‐EPI) sequence to balance sensitivity to blood oxygenation level‐dependent (BOLD) contrast with mitigation of susceptibility‐induced intravoxel spin dephasing in ultra high‐field rodent brain imaging.MethodsA dASE‐EPI pulse sequence was implemented and resting‐state BOLD fMRI was acquired in the rat brain. Data acquired with dASE‐EPI were compared to conventional gradient‐recalled EPI. Functional connectivity analyses were performed to assess detection of established networks and to evaluate signal recovery in regions affected by susceptibility (e.g., amygdala, hypothalamic nuclei).Results dASE‐EPI was found to provide BOLD sensitivity that was non‐inferior to conventional GRE‐EPI while substantially reducing susceptibility‐related signal loss. Established functional networks including the bilateral insular, bilateral sensory, and default mode network were reliably detected with dASE‐EPI. Functional connectivity of the amygdala, which was obscured in GRE‐EPI, was recoverable with the proposed sequence.ConclusionThe proposed dASE‐EPI pulse sequence is a viable solution when studying brain regions which suffer from severe susceptibility artifacts.

Introduction

Gradient‐recalled echo planar imaging (GRE‐EPI) remains the primary method for functional brain imaging using blood oxygenation level‐dependent (BOLD) contrast [1]. The extended data acquisition window inherent to EPI makes it prone to susceptibility artifacts, namely intravoxel spin dephasing and intervoxel image distortion. Such artifacts are exacerbated at the high magnetic fields [2] typically used in rodent imaging and by the small geometric size of the rodent anatomy, which leads to inherently strong local magnetic field gradients. Ventral brain regions including the amygdala, hypothalamic nuclei, ventral subiculum, and ventral tegmental area are particularly vulnerable to susceptibility artifacts due to their proximity to air cavities [3]. Since the susceptibility‐induced local field gradients are high orders in nature, existing second‐order shimming is insufficient in correcting the resultant magnetic field inhomogeneity.

Some of the ventral brain regions are of great interest in systems neuroscience and addiction neurobiology. For example, relapse to opioid use is often triggered by environmental cues associated with previous opioid use. This generates negative emotional states that are relieved by opioid taking via negative reinforcement [4]. The amygdala and hypothalamic nuclei are critical hubs of brain circuitry that regulates these emotional states [5]. The ventral subiculum and ventral tegmental area are critically involved in reward learning and memory formation, which are maladapted by extended drug exposure [6]. Limitations in current small animal EPI data acquisition techniques in these important regions have hindered functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) as a modality in basic neuroscience research.

Several methods have been proposed to mitigate susceptibility artifacts in T2*‐weighted imaging. Spin‐echo EPI (SE‐EPI) can partially recover lost signal through the use of a 180° refocusing pulse, which reverses the phase dispersion of static spins caused by local magnetic field gradients [7]. Although SE‐EPI does mitigate susceptibility‐induced signal loss, it has inherently less BOLD sensitivity compared to GRE‐EPI [8]. This is true even when ultra‐high magnetic field and macrovessel intravascular suppression characteristics are considered [9]. The averaging of multiple spin‐echoes has been proposed as a strategy to circumvent the inferior BOLD sensitivity of SE‐EPI [10]. Other strategies such as injecting susceptibility‐matched materials into the ear [3], inductively coupled ear bars [11], gradient compensation [12, 13, 14], and tailored RF pulses [15, 16] have all been proposed. These solutions have found limited use because they are either marginally effective, invasive, or technically cumbersome.

Beyond BOLD fMRI, contrast‐enhanced and arterial spin labeling fMRI (ASL fMRI) are alternative solutions to susceptibility‐induced signal loss. Contrast‐enhanced fMRI involves the injection of an exogenous contrast agent [17]. Shorter echo times are achievable with fMRI sensitivity becoming a function of contrast dose, reducing susceptibility‐induced signal loss, but this approach has the inherent limitation of administering an exogenous contrast agent at increasingly higher dosages to offset the BOLD effect at higher magnetic field strengths [18, 19]. In addition, such high contrast doses are of concern since they may introduce unintended physiological or behavioral effects. Unlike contrast‐enhanced fMRI, ASL fMRI is an endogenous contrast method that employs preparatory RF pulses to label water protons in arterial blood [20]. Because it is based on the longitudinal magnetization of water in arterial blood and is not a susceptibility effect like BOLD, a SE‐EPI readout with short echo times can be used to reduce susceptibility‐induced signal dropout; however, ASL fMRI typically has lower sensitivity than BOLD fMRI [20].

In this study, we introduce a dual asymmetric spin echo (dASE‐EPI) sequence designed to achieve an optimal balance between BOLD sensitivity and mitigation of MR signal dropout, while maintaining the high temporal resolution of conventional single‐echo, single‐shot GRE‐EPI and SE‐EPI. The sequence involves two asymmetric spin echo (ASE) EPI readouts where the collection of the center of k‐space is offset by times τ 1 and τ 2 such that they are temporally shifted relative to the center of the spin echo. When τ is small, the ASEs retain spin echo properties while providing contrast distinct from conventional SE‐EPI or GRE‐EPI. Although ASE was first introduced in the 1990s for fMRI of the human brain [21], its potentials have remained underexplored, likely due to its intrinsically low BOLD sensitivity at low magnetic field. Additionally, severe image distortions at 9.4 T make accurate registration of EPI images and subsequent group analysis of rat fMRI data a challenge. We have employed a reverse k‐space trajectory dASE‐EPI sequence to correct for image distortions [22].

We have performed resting state BOLD fMRI experiments on 8 rats using dASE‐EPI on a 9.4 T scanner and compared it with conventional GRE‐EPI to assess limits in BOLD signal detection in regions of EPI dropout. GRE‐EPI resting‐state BOLD fMRI has been extensively performed [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29] and provides a baseline to establish BOLD fMRI improvements at ultra‐high fields. The dASE‐EPI sequence demonstrated BOLD sensitivity non‐inferior to GRE‐EPI in well‐characterized cortical networks including the insular cortex, default mode network, and whisker barrel cortex. Notably, unlike GRE‐EPI, the dASE‐EPI approach enabled robust detection of network connectivity originating in the amygdala—a region typically obscured by susceptibility‐induced signal loss in GRE‐EPI.

Methods

dASE‐EPI Sequence

Figure 1 illustrates the dASE‐EPI sequence. A 90° excitation pulse is followed by a 180° refocusing pulse, producing a spin echo at time TE. Two ASE readouts whose centers of k‐space are temporally offset by times τ 1 and τ 2 relative to the center of the spin echo are collected at TEASE1 and TEASE2. By varying the temporal offsets, different spin‐echo (T2) and gradient‐echo (T2*) weightings can be achieved.

Dual asymmetric spin echo sequence diagram. A 90° excitation pulse is followed by a 180° refocusing pulse, creating a spin echo at TE. With temporal offsets τ 1 and τ 2, two asymmetric spin echoes are sampled at TEASE1 and at TEASE2. The two dashed squares indicate EPI readouts. Multiple slices are acquired during repetition time (TR), allowing for coverage of the entire rat brain.

The dASE‐EPI sequence was implemented on a Bruker 9.4 T scanner running ParaVision 7.0 software and AVANCE‐III hardware platform. Part of the hardware platform includes a BGA‐12S HP gradient insert and second order shimming coils with a maximum shim current of 10 A. For every EPI readout, a 70 μs gradient rise time with ramp sampling was used. Both forward and reverse k‐space trajectory EPI readouts were implemented. The resulting images acquired with these two k‐space trajectories have distinct, complimentary geometric distortion characteristics, which were corrected using the method described by Chang et al. [22], implemented in FSL [30].

Validation of the dASE‐EPI Method

We performed resting state BOLD fMRI experiments on rats to assess the dASE‐EPI sequence in terms of (1) the effectiveness of MR signal recovery and (2) BOLD sensitivity. A Bruker volume quadrature transmitter coil (model: MT0381) was used for RF excitation with a custom whole brain single‐channel receive only surface coil based on the work of Sidabras et al. [31]. This strongly‐coupled geometry (SCG) features a 15 × 20 mm equalization coil with a 12 × 18 mm elongated racetrack spiral geometry. This novel geometry, coupled with a low‐noise amplifier, was engineered to provide whole‐brain coverage (see Figure 2C) in small animals without sacrificing sensitivity typically found in larger coils that are required to maintain whole brain coverage.

Animal Preparation

All procedures were approved by the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) Animal Care and Use Committee. Adult rats weighing 350–450 g were anesthetized using a combination of low‐dose isoflurane and low‐dose dexmedetomidine during BOLD fMRI experiments. This anesthesia protocol has been shown to preserve neurovascular coupling [32, 33] and large‐scale brain networks [27]. Detailed procedures were described previously [34]. Briefly, rats were initially anesthetized using 2.5% isoflurane in oxygen enriched air (70% N2 + 30% O2), followed by a bolus injection of dexmedetomidine (0.015 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). Then, rats were transferred and head‐fixed onto a customized MRI‐compatible holder. Isoflurane was delivered in oxygen‐enriched air. Isoflurane concentration was gradually lowered and maintained at 0.5%–0.75% through a nose cone. Isoflurane and expired CO2 were actively scavenged through a building vacuum line. The flow rates of the inlet and outlet gases in the nose cone were carefully balanced. Continuous, subcutaneous infusion of dexmedetomidine (0.015 mg/kg/h) was delivered through an infusion pump (PHD 2000, Harvard Apparatus, South Natick, MA, USA). Multiple physiological parameters were monitored to ensure stable physiology during fMRI recording, which occurred at least 60 min after anesthesia introduction [34]. Body temperature was maintained by a temperature‐controlled water‐heating pad during imaging. Blood oxygenation and respiration rate were also monitored (Small animal Monitoring and Gating System, SA Instruments Inc., NY). The oxygen concentration in gas mixture was adjusted to ensure at least 95% SpO2.

The decussation of the anterior commissure (approximately 0.36 mm from bregma) appears dark in T2‐weighted images and can be readily identified, serving as the fiducial landmark to standardize slice localization both within and across animals.

The GRE‐EPI sequence is identical to the Bruker EPI sequence with additional options for forward (top‐down) and reverse (bottom‐up) k‐space trajectories. Second order shimming based on the phase map defined in an elliptical volume covering the entire rat brain were performed prior to EPI data acquisition.

Functional Connectivity Analysis

Geometric distortions in the EPI images were first corrected using data acquired with forward and reverse k‐space trajectories using the topup() program in FSL [30]. The two ASE images obtained within each TR were then averaged to enhance the SNR. This averaging was justified because the interval between the two ASE images was only 14–15 ms. Given the sluggish nature of the hemodynamic response, any MR signal changes due to spontaneous neuronal activity would be indistinguishable between them. Likewise, motion artifacts arising from cardiac pulsation and respiration would also be effectively identical as their periods are much longer than the interval between the two acquisitions.

The EPI time series were then subject to spatial independent component analysis (ICA) using the MELODIC package in FSL. Noise components that exhibit periodic temporal patterns resulting from respiration and cardiac pulsation were identified and removed. Images from the one rat that had the best positioning was chosen as the template. EPI images were linearly co‐registered to this template. The co‐registered EPI images from all rats were subsequently averaged to generate a group template specific to this study. The EPI images were subject to further co‐registration to the group template. Time series were subject to a second‐order detrend along with voxel time courses averaged from white matter and CSF as the nuisance signal, low‐pass filtered with a cutoff frequency of 0.1 Hz. Voxel time courses from a seed region were averaged, serving as the reference time course, which was cross‐correlated with whole brain time courses. The resulting cross‐correlation coefficient was subsequently subject to z‐transformation. Data from all animals were grouped together and a two‐tailed t‐test against zero was conducted to derive group functional connectivity maps. Multiple comparison corrections were carried out as follows: first, the 3dFWHMx function in AFNI was applied to calculate the spatial autocorrelation function (ACF) of the EPI data as a function of radius and was fit to a model of the form [35]: (1) ACF(r)=ae−r22b2++(1−a)erc

The outputs of the ACF function parameters a, b, c were then used as inputs into the 3dClustSim function to derive the cluster size for a given threshold. An uncorrected p < 0.005 with a cluster size of at least 18 was considered significant.

Results

dASE‐EPI Recovers MRI Signal in Regions With Severe Susceptibility Dropout

Figure 2A,B presents a qualitative comparison of EPI image quality with the corresponding slice coverage shown in Figure 2C. As expected, GRE‐EPI images exhibited pronounced susceptibility‐induced signal loss with large intensity voids in the ventral hippocampus, amygdala, olfactory tubercle, and cerebellum (indicated by arrows). The severe signal dropouts effectively preclude BOLD fMRI investigations of neural systems involving these regions, rendering whole‐brain rodent BOLD fMRI impractical. In contrast, a dASE‐EPI readout with a short temporal offset (Set 1: τ 1 = 2.18 ms) substantially restored signal in these areas. As an example, Figure 2D illustrates signal coverage encompassing the bilateral amygdala (outlined by the dashed lines in Figure 2A,B), where a greater proportion of voxels fall to background levels in GRE‐EPI compared to dASE‐EPI. In dASE‐EPI images acquired with longer temporal offsets (Set 1: τ 2 = 12.33 ms and Set 2: τ 1 = 6.68 ms, τ 2 = 7.83 ms), the signal dropouts were comparable to that seen in GRE‐EPI images. Both dASE‐EPI readouts collected with Set 2 parameters had GRE‐EPI like signal dropout and thus were not further explored.

Comparison of BOLD Sensitivity Between dASE‐EPI and GRE‐EPI

Since the BOLD signal may be considered as a susceptibility effect and dASE‐EPI recovers much of the MR signal loss resulting from spin dephasing in regions with severe susceptibility as shown in Figure 2, an interesting question arises: can dASE‐EPI be applied to detect resting state brain networks?

The two ASE datasets acquired 15 ms apart offer the opportunity to suppress white noise thru averaging while preserving the physiological noises (BOLD fluctuations) resulting from spontaneous neuronal activity, effectively enhancing the contrast‐to‐noise ratio (CNR). As an example, Figure 3 presents functional connectivity maps acquired using GRE‐EPI and dASE‐EPI with the seed voxels in the insular cortex. The spatial extent and the peak t‐statistics of the connectivity maps were very similar. There was no statistically significant difference between these two maps (data not shown). The same observation is drawn when we placed a seed in the whisker barrel cortex (Figure S1).

Lu et al. [27] previously reported the existence of the default mode network (DMN) using independent component analysis (ICA), which was independently replicated by others [11]. In addition to the relatively “simpler” networks described above, we performed ICA analysis. Figure S3 shows an ICA map that corresponds to the DMN. Together, these data demonstrate that dASE‐EPI achieves a BOLD sensitivity comparable to GRE‐EPI.

Discussion

This work introduces a new pulse sequence that enables BOLD fMRI of the entire rat brain. The amygdala, ventral subiculum, orbital tubercle and cerebellum suffer severe susceptibility effects, leading to signal voids in images acquired with conventional GRE‐EPI (Figure 2A). The dASE‐EPI sequence introduces two temporal offsets (τ 1, τ 2). When the temporal offsets are short, the resulting images retain spin echo like properties with gradient echo weighting, allowing for whole brain coverage while maintaining the same temporal resolution of conventional single‐echo, single‐shot GRE‐EPI and SE‐EPI acquisitions (Figure 2B). Data acquired using two parameter sets were visually compared. Representative images showing the degree of signal dropout are provided in Figure S2. The τ 1 images from Set 1 exhibited the least susceptibility‐induced signal dropout and therefore prompted the decision to use the Set 1 data for functional connectivity analysis. Furthermore, the forward and reverse k‐space trajectories allow for the correction of geometric distortions which are otherwise severe in regions with strong magnetic field inhomogeneity.

The dASE‐EPI sequence permits functional connectivity mapping of the amygdala. The amygdala is known to possess unilateral projections with the orbitofrontal cortex and cingulate cortex, devoid of bilateral amygdalar connections. Data shown in Figure 4 are consistent with the known anatomy. While receiving minimal monosynaptic projections with the sensory cortex, the amygdala is the hub where different sensory information converges and is associated with emotional significance of such sensory information [36]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of amygdala functional connectivity in rats. Future studies with a larger number of subjects could further establish the functional connectivity map of this important structure.

BOLD Sensitivity

Since BOLD signal may be considered a susceptibility effect and the dASE‐EPI sequence is less sensitive to susceptibility‐induced intravoxel spin dephasing relative to GRE‐EPI, one might expect the dASE‐EPI sequence to possess a lower BOLD sensitivity. As shown in Figure 3 and Figures S1 and S3, dASE‐EPI achieves a BOLD sensitivity non‐inferior to GRE‐EPI. There are three reasons for this. First, given the short interval between the two ASE echoes (˜15 ms), the sluggish BOLD response, and low frequency of respiration and cardiac pulsation, the two ASE echoes can be averaged, reducing white noise by a factor of 2 while preserving physiological noise, including spontaneous BOLD fluctuations resulting from ongoing neuronal activity, enhancing SNR and CNR. Second, statistically, BOLD detectability depends on CNR. The T2* values at 9.4 T are short and heterogeneous across the rat brain and sampling a range of echo times with multi‐echo acquisitions like dASE‐EPI increases BOLD CNR [37, 38, 39]. Finally, at ultra‐high magnetic fields, the intravascular contributions are greatly reduced and is coupled with intrinsic SNR and CNR improvements inherent to ultra‐high field, improving BOLD sensitivity and specificity, complementing the properties of the dASE‐EPI acquisition [40, 41].

BOLD sensitivity achieved through the dASE‐EPI acquisition can be further modulated by strategically choosing TE and τ. It has long been accepted that the appropriate echo time to maximize BOLD CNR is approximately T2* for GRE‐EPI and T2 for SE‐EPI [37]. What Boxerman et al. [8] had shown in 1995 was offsetting the collection of the center of k‐space by some time τ such that it is temporally shifted relative to the center of the spin echo shifts the change in transverse relaxation rate as a function of vessel size curve. In the limit where τ is sufficiently large, the change in relaxation rate as a function of vessel size curve approaches that of a GRE‐EPI acquisition, increasing BOLD sensitivity [8]. However, the further the k‐space center is temporally shifted relative to the center of the spin echo, the larger the susceptibility‐induced signal dropout. The choice of TE and τ will involve a delicate balance between sufficient BOLD sensitivity and tolerable susceptibility‐induced signal dropout in brain regions of interest.

Limitations

A major limitation of the dASE‐EPI sequence is gradient coil overheating. dASE‐EPI involves two EPI readouts, doubling the duration of the readout gradient, and thus doubling its thermal load. In this study, to achieve whole rat brain coverage while preventing the gradient coils from overheating, we acquired the resting‐state fMRI data with a modest in‐plane resolution (0.547 mm) and 1 mm slice thickness with 0.3 mm slice gap, 21 slices, covering 27 mm along the rostral‐caudal direction. It is conceivable that higher resolution could further reduce intravoxel spin dephasing in regions with severe susceptibility, improving SNR, but the thermal load on the gradients would need to be monitored.

This sequence should also benefit from accelerated acquisition using parallel imaging techniques. By shortening the k‐space readout duration or reducing the number of slices, thermal loads to the gradient coils can be effectively reduced, permitting higher resolution data acquisition. However, a shorter data acquisition window leads to intrinsically reduced SNR. Future studies should optimize acceleration factor, image resolution, signal recovery, SNR and BOLD CNR of dASE‐EPI.

In addition to parallel imaging acceleration, multi‐band excitation could be used to reduce the number of slice excitations per volume. This could effectively lower the slice duty factor and provide additional flexibility to reduce TE and/or acquire thinner slices. With appropriate RF hardware and software, the inclusion of this technique could further mitigate susceptibility‐induced signal dropout.

Finally, a limitation of the study was neither the dASE‐EPI nor the GRE‐EPI acquisition were rigorously optimized. The purpose of the study was to show the dASE‐EPI acquisition can be a practical solution to susceptibility‐induced signal dropout while maintaining reasonable BOLD sensitivity. Future studies are needed to adequately quantify dASE‐EPI performance relative to GRE‐EPI beyond the qualitative comparisons made herein. These future studies would include spatial and temporal resolution improvements along with a detailed investigation into the optimal echo time(s) for the GRE‐EPI and for the dASE‐EPI acquisition to maximize BOLD sensitivity at the chosen magnetic field strength.

Conclusions

In summary, BOLD fMRI with small animals at ultra‐high field presents substantial technical challenges due to strong susceptibility artifacts resulting from the anatomy of the rat brain. In the current study, we have presented dASE‐EPI as a practical solution for mitigating susceptibility artifacts caused by rat brain anatomy with reasonable BOLD sensitivity. While validated in rats, dASE‐EPI is expected to function as well in BOLD fMRI of other small mammals and non‐human primates at ultra‐high field (> 9.4 T).
