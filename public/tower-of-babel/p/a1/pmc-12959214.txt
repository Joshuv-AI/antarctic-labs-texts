Impact of immunization strategies, public health and social measures on the incidence of varicella among children aged 0–14 years in Quzhou City
Authors: Quanjun Fang, Hui Yang, Shuangqing Wang, Xiaoying Gong, Canjie Zheng, Zhiying Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluates the impact of different immunization strategies and public health and social measures (PHSMs) on varicella incidence among children in Quzhou. Data on varicella cases among children aged 0–14 y in Quzhou from 2006 to 2022 were collected. An interrupted time-series (ITS) model was used to analyze trends in monthly varicella incidence during the implementation of the single-dose and two-dose vaccination strategies and the PHSMs. ITS analysis indicated that, following the introduction of the two-dose strategy, the varicella monthly incidence rate decreased immediately by 8.193 per 100,000 (p = .029). After the implementation of PHSMs, it also decreased immediately, with a reduction of 13.518 per 100,000 (p = .006). In terms of long-term effectiveness, neither the two-dose immunization strategy nor the implementation of PHSMs produced a sustained and significant reduction in varicella incidence. The effectiveness of the two-dose strategy may have been constrained by the low second-dose VarV coverage in Quzhou, which ranged from 46.35% to 60.65%, well below the 80% threshold recommended by the World Health Organization. Although the implementation of PHSM can serve as a useful supplementary approach to preventing childhood varicella, their influence on transmission dynamics appears relatively limited.KEYWORDS: PHSMs, varicella, interrupted time series analysis, incidence, vaccination coverage, varicella vaccine

Introduction

Varicella is an acute respiratory illness caused by the varicella-zoster virus (VZV). Varicella is highly contagious, with secondary attack rates ranging from 61% to 100% among susceptible contacts.1,2 The majority of otherwise healthy children experience mild symptoms and a favorable prognosis following primary VZV infection; however, serious complications such as pneumonia, encephalopathy, and, in rare cases, death may occur.3,4 The World Health Organization recently estimated that varicella affects at least 140 million individuals globally each year, leading to approximately 4.2 million cases with serious complications and 4,200 associated deaths.5 In China, varicella is the third most commonly reported vaccine-preventable infectious disease, with approximately one million cases notified annually.6 Between 2006 and 2022, a total of 11,990 outbreaks comprising 354,082 cases were reported.7 As varicella is not officially classified as a notifiable infectious disease in China, its true burden is likely to be underestimated. This highlights the urgent need for effective preventive strategies to mitigate its substantial public health impact.

Vaccination with the live attenuated varicella vaccine (VarV) remains the most cost-effective strategy for the prevention and control of varicella. The United States began promoting the use of VarV in March 1995, and with the subsequent increase in vaccination coverage, varicella morbidity and mortality have shown a declining trend.8 Breakthrough infections can still occur in vaccinated populations, despite the reported high efficacy of the single-dose VarV.9 In contrast, a two-dose VarV vaccination programme confers nearly 95% protection against varicella of all severities and significantly reduces the incidence of breakthrough infections.5 Despite substantial scientific evidence supporting the benefits of VarV vaccination, the vaccine has not been incorporated into routine immunization programmes across most regions of China.7 VarV was introduced in Quzhou in 2002, with a single dose recommended at 12 months of age. A two-dose immunization strategy was implemented in July 2014. It is advised that varicella-susceptible children receive the first dose of VarV between 12 and 18 months of age, followed by a second dose at 3 to 4 y, with an interval of at least three months between doses. In Quzhou, VarV is not included in the National Immunization Programme (NIP) and is therefore available only through voluntary, self-funded vaccination. The incidence of varicella increased in China between 2016 and 2019.10 It is still uncertain whether the two-dose VarV immunization strategy has effectively reduced varicella incidence in Quzhou.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, public health and social measures (PHSMs) demonstrated significant efficacy in controlling varicella outbreaks.11,12 Although these interventions were initially implemented to contain COVID-19, their application has inadvertently reduced the incidence of other respiratory infections, including varicella, owing to the shared transmission routes.12 Measures such as increased social distancing, mask-wearing, and enhanced hygiene practices have led to a significant reduction in varicella cases, emphasizing their effectiveness in managing respiratory infections. In Xi’an, China, the incidence of varicella declined by 43.18% in 2020 compared to 2019, attributable to the implementation of PHSMs aimed at containing COVID-19.13 A European study suggests that infection control measures implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic may have curtailed the spread of varicella.14

The interrupted time series (ITS) design is a quasi-experimental approach that employs segmented linear regression models to analyze changes in both levels and slopes of outcome measures, thereby assessing the longitudinal effects of interventions. It has been widely used to evaluate diverse public health interventions. Previous studies on VarV effectiveness have primarily focused on changes in serum antibodies to VZV 15,16 or on assessing vaccine efficacy within specific populations.17 Fewer investigations have addressed the impact of alterations in vaccination strategies and the implementation of PHSMs on population-level morbidity. Since PHSMs were implemented to control the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 to 2022 and discontinued thereafter, the cutoff date for this study was December 2022. In this study, we employed the ITS method to compare changes in varicella incidence before and after adjustments to vaccination strategies, as well as before and after the implementation of PHSMs. This approach enabled us to investigate the effects of different vaccination strategies and PHSMs implementation on varicella incidence among children aged 0–14 y.

This study was conducted in Quzhou City, a prefecture-level city in Zhejiang Province, eastern China, with an area of 8,844 km2 and a resident population of approximately 2.2 million. Situated at the intersection of four provinces (Fujian, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, and Anhui), Quzhou is a significant transport hub. Its extensive transport network facilitates the rapid spread of infectious diseases, making it a relevant setting for this investigation.

Methods

Study design

Three distinct periods were defined according to the differing VarV immunization strategies and the implementation of PHSMs. Phase A (January 2006–June 2014) corresponded to the single-dose varicella vaccination strategy. During this period, eligible children were advised to receive one VarV dose at their own expense. Nevertheless, a small proportion received a second dose through emergency vaccination during school varicella outbreaks or through immunization programmes implemented in other provinces. Phase B (July 2014–December 2019) corresponded to the two-dose varicella vaccination strategy. During this period, eligible children were advised to receive two VarV doses at their own expense. Phase C (January 2020–December 2022) corresponded to the implementation of PHSMs aimed at controlling the spread of COVID-19. This phase was conducted alongside the continuation of the two-dose VarV vaccination strategy.

We restricted our analysis to children aged 0–14 y. This group is the primary target for varicella vaccination and is more directly influenced by both immunization strategies and school-based PHSMs.

Data sources

Data on varicella cases in Quzhou City from 2006 to 2022 were obtained from CISDCP. Population data were obtained from the Statistical Bulletin of the Quzhou Municipal Bureau of Statistics.

Data on VarV vaccination coverage were obtained from the Zhejiang Provincial Immunisation Information System, which has recorded demographic and vaccination data for all children under 15 y of age residing in Zhejiang Province since 2006. This study presents birth cohort-based vaccination coverage. VarV coverage was calculated as the cumulative number of children vaccinated with VarV by the time of enumeration (December 2022), divided by the total number of children in the corresponding birth cohort.

Cohort-based vaccination coverage and incidence correlation analysis

To further examine the association between two-dose VarV coverage and population-level disease risk, we performed an ecological correlation analysis at the birth-cohort level. We included cohorts born between 2006 and 2016, for whom complete follow-up data to 6 y of age were available.

For each cohort, we calculated cumulative varicella incidence among children aged 5–6 y using case reports from the CISDCP and population denominators from the Quzhou Statistical Bulletin. We obtained cohort-specific two-dose VarV coverage from the Zhejiang Provincial Immunisation Information System as of December 2022.

We used Pearson’s correlation coefficient to assess the strength and direction of the linear association between two-dose coverage and varicella incidence in the 5–6-y age group. This ecological analysis complements the time-series approach by providing a cohort-specific perspective on vaccine impact. However, it does not allow inference of causality at the individual level.

Data accuracy and quality control

The Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention Information System maintains data quality through a multi-tier verification mechanism. All reported cases undergo initial review by infection control physicians at the reporting institution, followed by verification by local Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) staff. Although varicella is not a nationally notifiable disease in China, Quzhou City has managed it under its Public Health Emergency Response Plan since 2006, This local regulation mandates all healthcare institutions to report varicella cases within 24 hours, establishing a de facto mandatory reporting system at the local level. Varicella reporting currently relies on clinicians’ diagnoses and represents a passive surveillance system. However, since 2010, the Quzhou Municipal Health Bureau has required all healthcare institutions to report varicella cases within 24 hours. This mandate applies to both public and private providers, standardizing the reporting process and improving the timeliness and completeness of surveillance data. Throughout the study period, clinical diagnostic criteria for varicella remained consistent with the National Varicella Diagnosis Guidelines. Laboratory confirmation by PCR was not routinely performed, as clinical diagnosis remained the predominant method. As the case definition was unchanged, this consistency did not affect the trend analysis.

Seasonal distribution-M value

The concentration M-value quantifies the intensity of seasonal variation in disease occurrence and is calculated using the following formula18: Rx=r2+r6−r8−r122+32r3+r5−r9−r11+r4−r10;Ry=r3−r5−r9+r112+32\breakr2−r6−r8+r12+r1−r7;M=Rx2+Ry2; where R represents the degree of dispersion, and r denotes the ratio of the number of cases in a given month to the total number of cases in the year. An M-value of 0 indicates that cases are evenly distributed across all 12 months of the year. A value below 0.3 suggests a relatively uniform distribution of disease onset over time. An M-value between 0.3 and <0.5 indicates moderate seasonality; between 0.5 and <0.7 or 0.7 and <0.9 reflects strong seasonality; and between 0.9 and <1 denotes strict seasonality. An M-value of 1 indicates that cases are concentrated within a single month.

Interrupted time series design

ITS analysis is a statistical modeling method used to evaluate the effects of interventions by analyzing changes in levels and trends before and after the intervention point. This approach involves collecting outcome data at multiple time points surrounding the intervention, while controlling for preexisting upward or downward trends. ITS analyses constitute a quasi-experimental design that, through fitting segmented multiple linear regressions, can provide robust estimates even in the absence of valid control groups.19 The segmented linear regression equations were established as follows:

where the dependent variable, Yt, represents the monthly incidence of varicella; Time is a continuous variable with values ranging from 0 to 204; β0 represents the incidence of varicella in January 2006, the start of the study period; β1 denotes the slope of the first segment of the regression equation, representing the trend in monthly varicella incidence during the one-dose immunization strategy. β2 indicates the change in level, reflecting the immediate effect of the two-dose immunization strategy; β3 represents the change in slope of the second segment of the regression equation, reflecting the long-term effect following implementation of the two-dose immunization strategy, while β4 denotes the change in level corresponding to the short-term effect during the period of PHSMs implementation; β5 represents the change in slope of the third segment of the regression equation, indicating the long-term effect of PHSMs implementation on the monthly incidence of varicella. In the context of this ITS analysis, the “long-term effect” is operationally defined as the change in the underlying monthly trend (slope) of varicella incidence following an intervention. It quantifies whether the intervention led to a sustained acceleration, deceleration, or reversal of the preexisting trend over the entire post-intervention period (July 2014 to December 2019 for the two-dose strategy; January 2020 to December 2022 for PHSMs). This is distinct from the “immediate effect” (or level change), which captures the one-time, step-change in incidence observed right after the intervention. Intervention is a binary variable coded as 0 for pre-intervention observations and 1 for post-intervention observations; posterior slope is a continuous variable coded as 0 for pre-intervention observations, 1 for the first post-intervention observation, 2 for the second, and so forth; and εt represents the random error, denoting the portion of variance unexplained by the regression model.20

Although coverage of the second dose of VarV increased gradually after the policy was introduced, we selected an immediate level-change model. This choice was based on the clearly defined start of the policy in July 2014 and its likely influence on vaccination behavior and public awareness. To test the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative model specifications. These included a slope-change – only model and a lagged intervention model, in which the policy effect was assumed to begin 1 y after implementation.

Model specification and seasonality adjustment

The overall M value (0.25) indicated weak seasonality. However, visual inspection showed a consistent bimodal pattern, with peaks in May–June and November–January across all study periods. This pattern likely reflects school-term – related contact dynamics. Given the potential for residual seasonal confounding in a 17-y time series, we selected a seasonally adjusted interrupted time-series model as the primary analytical approach. The model incorporated monthly dummy variables to control for fixed calendar effects and to better separate intervention effects from underlying seasonal variation. We retained a non-seasonally adjusted model as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the results.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 was used to establish a database of the Varicella incidence and to calculate the M value. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2. ITS analysis required time series to be free of autocorrelation, and the Breusch-Godfrey test was used to assess whether autocorrelation was present. For time series without autocorrelation, analysis was conducted using ordinary least squares (OLS); For time series exhibiting first-order autocorrelation, generalized least squares (GLS) was used, implemented via the Prais-Winsten method; the Newey-West method was used for analysis when time series with higher-order autocorrelation.21 For time series with higher-order autocorrelation, we applied the Newey-West heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) estimator. We set the lag length according to the rule T1/4, where T denotes the number of observations. With T = 204 months, this yielded a lag of four. This commonly used rule of thumb in econometric practice balances bias and efficiency when correcting for autocorrelation. A two-sided t-test was used to assess statistical differences in regression coefficients, with a significance level set at p < .05. To address the potential confounding effect of the 2010 reporting mandate, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an extended ITS model with three pre-intervention segments: Phase A1 (Jan 2006–Dec 2009), Phase A2 (Jan 2010–Jun 2014), followed by the original Phase B and C. This model included an additional intervention term and slope term for January 2010. Results with p-values between 0.05 and 0.10 were considered marginally significant, suggesting a potential effect that should be interpreted cautiously in light of the effect size and biological plausibility.

To assess the overall fit of our primary seasonally adjusted interrupted time-series regression model, we report the coefficient of determination (R2) and the adjusted R2. Model assumptions were evaluated through visual inspection of residual plots for homoscedasticity and normality, and the presence of autocorrelation in the residuals was formally tested using the Breusch-Godfrey test. After correcting for autocorrelation using Newey-West standard errors, a post-correction Breusch-Godfrey test was performed to confirm the adequacy of the model specification.

Model diagnostics

The final model, selected based on AIC, demonstrated a good overall fit with an R2 of 0.34 and an adjusted R2 of 0.32, indicating that approximately 32% of the variation in the seasonally adjusted incidence rate is explained by the model. Diagnostic checks confirmed that the model’s key assumptions were reasonably met. Visual inspection of the residual plots revealed no obvious patterns or funnel shapes, suggesting that the assumption of homoscedasticity (constant variance of errors) was satisfied (Supplementary Figure S1). Furthermore, the Q-Q plot indicated that the residuals were approximately normally distributed. As reported in the Methods section, significant autocorrelation was present in the original series (Breusch – Godfrey test, p < .001), which was addressed by applying Newey-West standard errors. Following this correction, a subsequent Breusch-Godfrey test applied to the model residuals showed no significant remaining autocorrelation (p = .75 > 0.05), supporting the appropriateness of our model specification.

Note on relationship to prior preprint

A preprint reporting preliminary findings on varicella incidence in Quzhou City (2006–2022) was previously posted on Research Square.22 The current manuscript differs substantially: it restricts the analysis to children aged 0–14 y (vs. all ages in the preprint), incorporates additional sensitivity analyses including seasonal adjustment via monthly dummy variables, and employs a refined ITS modeling approach that accounts for potential seasonal confounding. All statistical descriptions and epidemiological interpretations have been comprehensively updated.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics

From 2006 to 2022, Quzhou City reported a total of 8,643 cases of varicella among children aged 0–14 y, corresponding to an annual average incidence of 146.31 per 100,000. Annual incidence ranged from 35.16 per 100,000 (136 cases) in 2006 to 263.00 per 100,000 (878 cases) in 2017 (Figure 1). The mean annual reported incidence rates during the one-dose VarV immunization strategy period, the two-dose VarV immunization strategy period, and the period of PHSMs implementation were 123.97, 198.75, and 130.54 per 100,000 population, corresponding to 4011, 3334, and 1298 cases, respectively.

Number of reported varicella cases and incidence rate among children aged 0–14 y in Quzhou City, 2006–2022.

The average reported incidence rates of varicella among individuals aged 0–4, 5–9, and 10–14 y from 2006 to 2022 were 64.55, 156.40, and 212.75 per 100,000 population, respectively. The average reported incidence rates of varicella were 156.08 and 135.77 per 100,000 population for males and females, respectively. (Table 1). After implementation of the two-dose VarV policy, the age group with the highest incidence of varicella changed. During the one-dose period (2006–2014), the highest incidence was observed among children aged 5–9 y (178.64 cases per 100,000 population). During the two-dose period (2015–2019), incidence was highest among adolescents aged 10–14 y (370.42 cases per 100,000 population). This age shift is further illustrated in Figure 2.

Temporal distribution and seasonality

Among children aged 0–14 y, peak incidence was observed annually during May-June and November-January throughout the period 2006–2022, with a consistent temporal distribution evident across the subperiods 2006–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2022 (Figure 3). The overall M-value for the average monthly reported incidence of varicella from 2006 to 2022 was 0.25, with corresponding M-values of 0.21, 0.29, and 0.27 for the periods 2006–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2022, respectively. These findings suggest that varicella incidence does not exhibit a strong seasonal pattern.

VarV vaccination status

Vaccination coverage for different doses of VarV among birth cohorts from 2006 to 2021 was queried from the Zhejiang Province Immunisation Planning Information System, with coverage rates as of the end of 2022 are presented in Figure 4. Coverage of one-dose and two-dose VarV vaccinations increased progressively with each successive birth cohort. Following the introduction of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy in July 2014, a marked increase in two-dose VarV coverage was observed beginning with the 2010 birth cohort. During the period in which the two-dose strategy was implemented, full VarV coverage ranged from 46.35% to 60.65% across different birth cohorts in Quzhou.

The two-dose VarV immunization strategy was formally implemented in July 2014. Under local recommendations, children born from January 2011 onwards were eligible for a two-dose schedule, with the first dose administered at 12–18 months of age and the second at 3–4 y. Because the policy was introduced midway through 2014, children born between 2011 and 2013 may have experienced variable access to, or uptake of, the complete two-dose schedule. Association between birth-cohort vaccination coverage and varicella incidence

We observed a strong negative correlation between birth-cohort – specific two-dose VarV coverage and varicella incidence among children aged 5–6 y (r = −0.86, p < .001; Supplementary Figure S2). Birth cohorts with higher two-dose coverage consistently showed lower incidence during early childhood.

Sensitivity analysis (model without seasonal adjustment)

When we compared the seasonally adjusted and unadjusted models, the estimated immediate effects of the two-dose strategy (β2) and PHSMs (β4) remained statistically significant and similar in magnitude. In the primary, seasonally adjusted model, the two-dose strategy was associated with an immediate reduction of 8.193 cases per 100,000 population (95% CI: −15.549 to −0.836; p = .029). PHSMs were associated with a larger immediate reduction of 13.518 per 100,000 (95% CI: −23.039 to −3.998; p = .006). These estimates closely matched those from the unadjusted model (β2 = −8.342, p = .023; β4 = −13.329, p = .005), indicating that the results were robust to seasonal adjustment. Neither model showed a statistically significant long-term change in slope (all p > .10). Coefficients from the unadjusted model are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

ITS modeling of varicella incidence by age group showed that the implementation of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy was associated with immediate reductions in monthly varicella incidence rates of 3.562, 9.642, and 10.411 cases per 100,000 population for the 0–4, 5–9, and 10–14 y age groups, respectively (p = .052, 0.053, and 0.055, respectively), which may be suggestive of an effect. During this period, no statistically significant long-term effects were observed across all age groups (p ≥ .10). Following the implementation of PHSMs, immediate reductions in monthly varicella incidence were observed in the 0–4 and 5–9 y age groups, with decreases of 3.042 and 8.491 cases per 100,000 population, respectively (p = .075 and 0.051), which may indicate a potential effect. In the 10–14 y olds, the immediate decrease was more pronounced at 30.051 cases per 100,000 (p = .003), representing a statistically significant effect. However, during this period, no statistically significant long-term effects were observed across any age group (p ≥ .10) (Table 2).

To address concerns about the plausibility of an immediate level change after introduction of the two-dose VarV strategy, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative interrupted time-series specifications. First, we fitted a model that allowed only a change in slope at July 2014, without an immediate level shift. Second, we specified a lagged intervention model in which the intervention indicator was activated 12 months after policy introduction, to reflect the gradual increase in vaccination coverage. Both alternative models produced qualitatively similar results. The two-dose strategy was associated with a non-significant long-term change in trend (p > .10), supporting the robustness of our primary findings. Detailed coefficients and model fit statistics are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

We acknowledge that PHSMs varied in intensity over time. In a secondary analysis, we divided the PHSM period into two phases: a strict-measures phase (January–April 2020) and a normalized-measures phase (May 2020–December 2022). Owing to the limited number of observations during the strict phase and the similar incidence patterns observed during the normalized phase, we treated PHSMs as a single intervention period in the primary analysis to maintain model parsimony. Results from the two-phase analysis are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

When modeling PHSMs in two phases, the strict control phase (January–April 2020) was associated with a marked change in trend (β5 = −3.649 per 100,000 per month, p < .001), indicating a rapid monthly decline in incidence, rather than an immediate level change. In contrast, the transition to the normalized management phase (May 2020 onward) showed a significant immediate reduction in incidence level (β6 = −12.392 per 100,000, p = .017).

Discussion

From January 2006 to June 2014, Quzhou City implemented a single-dose VarV immunization strategy. The annual reported incidence of varicella among children aged 0–14 y was 123.97 per 100,000, slightly above the national average for China during 2005–201523 (which was 103.35 per 100,000 for the same age group). Following the introduction of a two-dose VarV immunization strategy in Quzhou City in July 2014, coverage among eligible children ranged from 46.35% to 60.65%. Between August 2014 and December 2019, varicella incidence among children aged 0–14 y showed no declining trend, with an annual average reported incidence of 198.75 per 100,000, below the national average for China during 2016–201910 (which was 235.31 per 100,000 for the same age group). In early 2020, China experienced an outbreak of novel coronavirus pneumonia. During the implementation of PHSMs to contain COVID-19 transmission, varicella incidence showed a downward trend. The annual reported incidence among children aged 0–14 y was 135.77 per 100,000. Overall, the incidence of varicella exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline between 2006 and 2022, mirroring the epidemiological trends observed in Ningbo City24 and Anhui Province.25

In the present study, prevalence was higher in males than in females, which may be attributable to poorer hygiene practices and greater socialization among males, resulting in increased exposure to disease-causing agents.26 With respect to age of onset, the highest incidence was observed primarily among children aged 5–9 and 10–14 y. This finding is consistent with other regional studies10 and indicates that children aged 5–14 y remain a priority population for varicella prevention and control. In this study, we observed a bimodal seasonal distribution of varicella incidence across three distinct periods, consistent with the national varicella epidemiological characteristics10 and aligning with the typical high incidence of respiratory-transmitted diseases during winter and spring. The seasonal increase in varicella incidence largely coincides with the period when students congregate in schools, highlighting the need for improved prevention and control of varicella outbreaks within educational settings. The seasonality of varicella incidence during both the implementation of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy and the period of PHSMs continued to exhibit a bimodal distribution, with no significant change observed – differing from the findings of other studies.14 This may be attributable to the relatively low two-dose VarV vaccination coverage among school-age children in the Quzhou area, and suggests that the implementation of PHSMs alone may be insufficient to alter the transmission dynamics of varicella.

Previous studies have demonstrated a declining trend in varicella incidence following the introduction of the VarV.27 However, the findings of the present study contrast with those of previous reports. During the period from 2006 to 2014, when the one-dose VarV immunization strategy was implemented in Quzhou City, the incidence of varicella exhibited an increasing trend. Wang Y et al. conducted a case – control study to evaluate vaccine effectiveness and failure rates during a varicella outbreak in Jiangsu Province.28 Their findings similarly indicated that a single dose of VarV was insufficient to effectively control varicella transmission. Studies have shown that the protective effect of a single dose of VarV wanes over time, with effectiveness dropping to approximately 50% three years post-vaccination.15 This suggests that a single dose does not confer sufficient population-level immunity to reduce the incidence of varicella. In some schools in China, multiple varicella outbreaks have occurred despite high coverage with a single dose of VarV.29

ITS analysis showed an immediate decrease of 8.193 cases per 100,000 population in the monthly incidence of varicella following the implementation of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy (p = .029). However, the long-term effect was not statistically significant (p = .538). By age group, the immediate effect at the time of intervention suggested a potential decrease in varicella incidence among children aged 0–4, 5–9, and 10–14 y (p = .052, 0.053, and 0.055, respectively). However, none of the long-term effects were statistically significant (p ≥ .10), indicating that the two-dose strategy did not significantly modify the original upward trend in varicella morbidity over time. Vaccination coverage data by birth cohort (Figure 3) provide key insights into this phenomenon: two-dose coverage among children born between 2006 and 2019 remains suboptimal, ranging from 46.35% to 60.65%, well below the 80% 5 threshold recommended by the World Health Organization. Our primary interrupted time-series model assumed an immediate level change at policy implementation. This assumption may not fully reflect the gradual uptake of the second vaccine dose. However, sensitivity analyses using alternative specifications, including lagged intervention and slope-change-only models, did not change the main conclusion. The two-dose strategy did not produce a sustained reduction in varicella incidence. These findings suggest that the limited long-term effectiveness is more likely to result from persistently low coverage of the second dose (46.35%–60.65%) than from model misspecification. Because each birth cohort contributes cases across multiple months and its vaccination coverage is fixed rather than time-varying, cohort-based coverage cannot be directly incorporated into monthly time-series models.

Furthermore, our ecological analysis at the birth-cohort level demonstrated a strong inverse correlation between two-dose VarV coverage and varicella incidence among 5–6-y-olds (r = −0.86, p < .001). Although ecological in nature and not indicative of individual protection, this finding reinforces the plausibility that higher population coverage is associated with reduced disease burden. Quzhou City’s persistently low two-dose vaccination coverage (46.35%–60.65%) contrasts sharply with regions of China where VarV has been incorporated into the NIP. In Shanghai and Wuxi, for example, VarV is provided free of charge, and two-dose coverage among eligible children exceeds 90%, accompanied by substantial reductions in varicella incidence.27,30 This comparison highlights that the voluntary, self-funded nature of VarV vaccination in Quzhou remains a major barrier to achieving high coverage. It also suggests that the absence of long-term effectiveness observed in our interrupted time-series analysis is primarily attributable to inadequate coverage, rather than insufficient vaccine efficacy. The VarV vaccination policy is voluntary and self-funded, which inherently limits coverage expansion. This low coverage results in an accumulation of susceptible school-aged children, thereby diminishing the vaccine’s overall protective effect. While our data did not show an overall decline in reported varicella incidence following the two-dose strategy, the potential signal of effectiveness may have been obscured by the underlying secular trend of improving case detection and reporting that began in 2010. The apparent discrepancy between the marked increase in incidence among 10–14-y-olds during the two-dose period (Table 1) and the immediate decline identified by interrupted time-series analysis can be attributed to an age shift in susceptibility. The two-dose policy effectively protected younger children who received timely vaccination, thereby reducing transmission in preschool settings and lowering incidence in the 5–9-y age group. However, suboptimal uptake of the second dose (46.35%–60.65% during 2015–2019) left a substantial cohort of partially vaccinated or unvaccinated individuals susceptible as they progressed into adolescence. The accumulation of susceptible individuals within the 10–14-y age group, combined with intensified exposure in school settings, likely contributed to increased outbreak activity and elevated incidence in this stratum. Importantly, birth cohort analysis (Supplementary Figure S2) revealed a strong negative correlation between two-dose VarV coverage and varicella incidence at ages 5–6 y (r = −0.86, p < .001), confirming vaccine effectiveness and supporting the conclusion that insufficient coverage – rather than vaccine failure – underlies the observed age shift.

From 2020 to 2022, China experienced the COVID-19 pandemic, during which the government implemented a series of PHSMs aimed at curbing the spread of the virus.31,32 Studies have demonstrated a decline in respiratory infection cases during the prevention and control of novel coronavirus outbreaks.33 The findings of this study demonstrated a significant and substantial immediate reduction in varicella incidence among children aged 10–14 y following the implementation of PHSMs. Immediate decreases were also observed in the 0–4 and 5–9 y age groups (p = .079 and 0.052), which may suggest a potential effect in these younger age groups This is consistent with the assumption that interventions such as mask use and school closures directly reduce contact rates and airborne transmission in high-risk settings.34 These results support the immediate effectiveness of school-based PHSMs in interrupting the transmission of respiratory infectious diseases such as varicella. However, the results of this study indicate that PHSMs did not induce a fundamental long-term decline in varicella incidence, with its impact on transmission dynamics being relatively limited – findings consistent with those of previous studies.27,35 In 2020, during the period of local transmission of coronavirus, approximately 80% of vaccination clinics in China suspended all services except for the administration of hepatitis B, BCG, rabies, and tetanus antitoxin vaccines. Such disruptions to immunization services may result in reduced coverage and could precipitate outbreaks of measles, poliomyelitis, and other vaccine-preventable diseases.36 In February 2020, Quzhou City suspended vaccination clinic services, resuming them in March of the same year. Thereafter, an online appointment system was introduced to limit the number of clinic visits, which reduced the accessibility of childhood immunization services. Data from the Zhejiang Province Immunization Information System indicated that, in 2020, the timely VarV vaccination rate among eligible children in the locality was only 47.62%. However, as epidemic prevention entered a ‘normalised’ phase, social contact resumed. The reopening of schools, in particular, led to a rapid rebound in varicella incidence. This explains the absence of a sustained downward trend in the long-term effectiveness of PHSMs. Moreover, the feasibility of, and strict adherence to, certain PHSMs may be constrained, particularly when young children are required to maintain continuous social distancing, given their innate social behavior. This limitation may partly account for the lack of sustained long-term impact of PHSMs on varicella incidence once normal social activities and schooling resumed. The transitory nature of these measures underscores their value as emergency adjunctive strategies. Nevertheless, they cannot substitute for the fundamental, long-term protection provided by high vaccination coverage. Sustained increases in varicella vaccination coverage remain central to the effective prevention and control of varicella.37

The sensitivity analysis that divided PHSMs into two phases suggested distinct mechanisms of effect. During the initial period of strict measures, such as lockdowns and school closures, transmission dynamics were substantially disrupted. This phase was associated with a pronounced downward trend in varicella incidence. Such a pattern is consistent with the expectation that highly restrictive interventions act primarily through gradual reductions in population-level transmission. By contrast, the transition to normalized measures, including sustained mask use and hygiene practices, was associated with a significant immediate reduction in incidence. This step change appeared to prevent a subsequent rebound and to maintain incidence at a lower endemic level. Together, these findings indicate that both the intensity and the type of PHSMs shape not only the magnitude of their effects but also their temporal expression, with interventions differing in whether they exert predominantly gradual or immediate impacts on infectious disease dynamics.

This finding underscores the limitations of relying solely on ad hoc PHSMs and emphasizes the importance of establishing sustainable prevention and control strategies grounded in high vaccination coverage.

In this study, ITS analysis employing segmented linear regression models was used to evaluate the impact of different immunization strategies and the implementation of PHSMs on varicella incidence. Compared to traditional epidemiological study designs such as cohort studies, case-control studies, and randomized controlled trials, ITS analysis offers the advantage of quantifying the effect of an intervention in situations where identifying a suitable control group is challenging.38–40 This provides a foundation for refining the VarV vaccination strategy and the effective implementation of PHSMs in varicella outbreak management. Future research could integrate monthly vaccination rates with socioeconomic variables to optimize the ITS model.

Over the 17-y study period, factors such as improvements in sanitation and environmental hygiene may have influenced varicella incidence. Whilst these factors undoubtedly changed over time, several aspects of our analysis suggest that the observed shifts at the intervention points are primarily attributable to the interventions themselves. Firstly, according to the Quzhou Statistical Yearbook, the city’s total population and demographic composition remained relatively stable throughout the study period, with no large-scale or abrupt population movements that could have significantly altered disease transmission dynamics. Secondly, the seasonal pattern of varicella remained consistent across all three phases, indicating stability in the underlying epidemiological drivers of transmission. Most importantly, incidence rates showed an immediate and pronounced decline following the introduction of the two-dose vaccination strategy, and an even more substantial drop after the implementation of PHSMs. These abrupt “level changes,” occurring precisely at the pre-specified intervention time points, are strongly suggestive of an intervention effect and are less likely to be explained solely by gradual, long-term trends such as improvements in sanitation or environmental hygiene.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Firstly, varicella is not a notifiable infectious disease in China, and the available data are derived from passive surveillance systems, the quality of which may be influenced by healthcare-seeking behavior and physician awareness. Reporting sensitivity is likely to have improved over time, particularly after the introduction of enhanced outbreak reporting in 2006. Such improvements in surveillance sensitivity may partly explain the rising incidence observed during the early phase of the study (2006–2014). However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the observed pre-2014 upward trend in reported incidence is likely a composite of a genuine increase in varicella transmission and concurrent, substantial improvements in surveillance sensitivity, particularly the sharp increase following the 2010 mandatory reporting policy. This secular trend complicates the attribution of post-2014 trends solely to the two-dose vaccination strategy. Second, our analysis did not explicitly adjust for time-varying confounders beyond overall population stability. Although Quzhou′s population size and demographic structure remained relatively stable and varicella seasonality was consistent across study periods, other unmeasured factors that evolve over time may have influenced incidence trends. These include gradual socioeconomic change, environmental or climatic variation, shifts in healthcare-seeking behavior, and the concurrent circulation of other infectious diseases. However, the abrupt level changes observed at pre-specified intervention points make it less likely that such slowly varying confounders alone account for the observed effects. While subtle demographic shifts undoubtedly occurred, the consistent seasonal patterns and the sharp, immediate drop in incidence following PHSMs – a pattern highly specific to interventions affecting social contact – suggest that the observed changes at our intervention points are more likely attributable to the interventions themselves than to underlying demographic trends. Furthermore, due to the unavailability of monthly varicella vaccination coverage data, this study relied on VarV vaccination rates by birth cohort, rather than incorporating coverage into the ITS model. This limitation may have affected the precision of evaluating the effectiveness of vaccination activities.

Conclusion

The implementation of a two-dose vaccination strategy has not produced a sustained and significant decline in varicella incidence. This outcome may be explained by the low uptake of the second dose in Quzhou, where completion rates across birth cohorts ranged from 46.35% to 60.65%—well below the World Health Organization’s recommended threshold of 80%. The implementation of PHSMs can serve as an effective complementary measure in preventing varicella; however, its impact on transmission dynamics remains relatively limited. A key priority for future varicella prevention and control in Quzhou, and potentially in similar settings, is to substantially increase coverage with the two-dose VarV schedule. Our findings, which demonstrate the limited long-term impact of the two-dose strategy under suboptimal coverage, provide local empirical support for the existing World Health Organization recommendation of achieving high (>80%) two-dose coverage to reduce varicella burden. To overcome the financial and accessibility barriers associated with the current voluntary, self-funded vaccination model in Quzhou, policy measures to improve vaccine affordability and uptake are warranted. Considering the accumulating evidence from other Chinese regions where inclusion of VarV in the National Immunization Programme (NIP) has led to high coverage and significant incidence declines,27,30 integrating VarV into the NIP could be a pivotal strategy to achieve sustainable, high population immunity. This decision, however, should be informed by comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses and broader national public health priorities.
