Independent, joint, and temporal associations of social, intellectual, and physical leisure activities with cognitive decline in older Chinese adults: a longitudinal analysis from CHARLS
Authors: Zhou Su, Mengran Liu, Jun Kuai, Zejing Xu, Xinran Bao, Bin Xu, Shuangxi Guo, Jiyu Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt remains unclear whether engaging in different types of leisure activities leads to cognitive decline and whether participation in multidomains offers additional benefits among older Chinese adults.MethodsWe analyzed 6,847 participants aged ≥ 60 years from four waves (2011–2018) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, with a mean follow-up of 6.1 years. Leisure activities were categorized as social, intellectual, or physical. Linear mixed-effects models examined associations between baseline activity and cognitive change. Random-intercept cross-lagged panel models assessed temporal directionality. Multilevel structural equation models evaluated mediation by depressive symptoms and social support.ResultsHigher baseline frequency of each activity domain was independently associated with slower cognitive decline after mutual adjustment, with the strongest association for intellectual activities (β = 0.046 points/year per additional day/week, 95% CI 0.023–0.069), followed by social (β = 0.028, 95% CI 0.011–0.045) and physical activities (β = 0.024, 95% CI 0.009–0.039). Compared with no moderate-level activity, engagement in one, two, and three domains was associated with progressively slower decline (β = 0.043, 0.078, and 0.098 points/year, respectively; all P < 0.001). Within-person increases in leisure activity predicted better subsequent cognitive performance, whereas reverse paths from cognition to later activity were weak and nonsignificant. Depressive symptoms and social support jointly accounted for 37.6%–39.0% of the observed associations in statistical mediation analyses.ConclusionsGreater leisure engagement, particularly across multiple domains, was associated with slower cognitive decline in older Chinese adults. These findings support the potential of low-cost multidomain leisure activities as promising candidate factors worthy of further investigation in future intervention studies aimed at mitigating cognitive decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08033-1.

Introduction

Dementia and age-related cognitive decline have become defining public health challenges of population aging. People living with dementia are projected to rise sharply over the coming decades, and cognitive decline in later life may be delayed through modification of behavioural, psychosocial, and social risk factors [1, 2]. In China, recent national analyses have revealed marked geographic heterogeneity in dementia prevalence across provinces, suggesting that prevention strategies must be sensitive not only to biological risk factors but also to social and behavioural contexts [3]. While this geographic variation highlights the importance of context-sensitive prevention, a detailed examination of regional differences was beyond the scope of our study, which focused on individual-level behavioural associations. Against this backdrop, identifying everyday, scalable, and culturally adaptable behaviors that may help preserve cognitive health in later life is a matter of direct relevance for clinical prevention, community health planning, and healthy aging policy.

Leisure activities are integrated into daily life, can be adjusted without specialized infrastructure, and are sustainable over a long period. Longitudinal and intervention studies suggest that leisure activity participation is associated with better cognitive abilities, reduced dementia risk, and broader health benefits in later life [4, 5]. Leisure activities can affect health through multiple interacting mechanisms, including cognitive stimulation, emotional regulation, social connection, behavior reinforcement, and physiological adaptation [6]. Meta-analysis evidence indicates that leisure activities can help build cognitive reserves and are associated with a reduced risk of future cognitive impairment [7].

Prior studies have linked late-life intellectual activities to lower incident dementia risk, reported protective associations between leisure participation and cognitive impairment, and suggested that these associations may vary according to educational background, APOE ε4 status, sex, age, and the composition of activity patterns [8–10]. However, much research focused on single activity types, global participation scores, or cross-sectional designs [11, 12]. As a result, it remains unclear whether social, intellectual, and physical leisure activities make independent contributions when considered simultaneously, whether they follow similar dose-response patterns, and whether broader multi-domain engagement confers added benefit beyond participation in any single domain. These gaps are particularly relevant in China, where leisure behavior is strongly shaped by retirement, family structure, education, rural-urban residence, and unequal access to community resources.

Although leisure participation is interpreted as protective, declining cognition may itself reduce engagement, raising the possibility of reverse causation. Disentangling stable between-person differences from within-person temporal dynamics is essential [13, 14]. Depression, social connectedness, and perceived support may be plausible intermediaries because they influence stress regulation, motivation, health behaviours, and access to cognitively enriching experiences across later life [15, 16]. Recent multidomain prevention frameworks increasingly emphasize that cognitive health is shaped by interacting lifestyle and psychosocial factors rather than by isolated behaviours [17]. However, psychosocial pathways linking leisure activity to cognitive ageing have not been sufficiently examined in Chinese cohorts. Therefore, using four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), our objectives were to present a study that investigated the independent, joint, dose-response, and temporal associations of social, intellectual, and physical leisure activities with cognitive decline in older Chinese adults and further examined whether depressive symptoms and social support mediated these associations.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data were drawn from the CHARLS, an ongoing, nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese adults aged 45 and over and their spouses. CHARLS used a multistage, stratified, probability sampling design that covered 150 counties or districts across 28 provinces and provided rich repeated measures on health, function, family structure, and social participation. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011 (Wave 1) and included 17,708 participants from 10,257 households, with follow-up surveys conducted in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), and 2018 (Wave 4).

For the present analysis, we restricted the cohort to participants aged 60 years or older at baseline (Wave 1, 2011). Eligible participants were required to have baseline information on leisure activities, cognitive function, and covariates and to contribute at least one additional cognitive assessment during follow-up. We excluded participants who reported a physician diagnosis of Alzheimer disease, dementia, brain atrophy, or another memory-related disorder at baseline; those with severe baseline cognitive impairment (global cognitive score ≤ 9), for whom self-reported leisure activity might be unreliable; and those with missing baseline exposure, outcome, or key covariate data. The final analytical sample comprised 6,847 participants. Baseline characteristics were described for the analytic cohort, whereas longitudinal models used all available repeated observations from Waves 1–4 under a missing-at-random framework. The mean follow-up was 6.1 years. To address potential bias from attrition, the longitudinal mixed-effects models used all available repeated observations under a missing-at-random framework, which does not require complete case data. Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability weighting to account for differential attrition.

Leisure activity assessment

Leisure activities were identified from repeated CHARLS items on social participation and lifestyle behavior. To preserve conceptual clarity and avoid double counting, each activity item was assigned to a single primary domain. The social domain captured interpersonal or community-oriented participation; the intellectual domain captured cognitively stimulating activities such as reading, strategy-based games (e.g., mahjong, chess, or cards), and learning or training; and the physical domain captured walking for exercise, dancing or qigong, and gardening or yard work.

Because several CHARLS items were recorded as categorical frequencies over the previous month rather than exact days per week, categorical responses were harmonized to weekly-equivalent frequencies to improve interpretability across domains. The weekly-equivalent values (0, 0.5, 2, and 5 days/week) correspond to the original CHARLS response categories: ‘never’ (0 days/week), ‘not regularly’ (≈ 1–2 times/month, i.e., 0.5 days/week), ‘almost every week’ (2 days/week), and ‘almost every day’ (5 days/week, rather than 7), chosen to approximate the central tendency of each category while enabling quantitative comparison with physical activity items that were already reported as days per week. Physical activity items were already reported as days per week (0–7). This harmonization follows established practices in CHARLS-based research [18, 19], and while introducing certain assumptions, any non-differential measurement error would likely bias our estimates toward the null. For each domain at each wave, we calculated the mean weekly frequency across the component items, yielding a domain-specific activity score expressed in days per week. Baseline (Wave 1) activity measures were used as the exposures of interest. Repeated activity measures across Waves 1–4 were used in the RI-CLPM and mediation analyses. For dose-response analyses, each domain was categorized as none (0 days/week), low (> 0 to ≤ 1 day/week), moderate (> 1 to ≤ 3 days/week), or high (> 3 days/week). Multi-domain engagement was defined as the number of domains with at least moderate participation and was classified as none, single-domain, two-domain, or three-domain engagement.

Cognitive assessment

Cognitive function was assessed at each wave using the CHARLS cognitive battery. The global cognitive score ranged from 0 to 31 and was constructed by summing orientation (year, month, day of month, day of week, and season; 0–5), calculation (serial subtraction of 7 from 100; 0–5), immediate word recall (0–10), delayed word recall (0–10), and figure drawing (0–1). Higher scores indicated better cognitive performance. All cognitive assessments were administered orally by trained interviewers in face-to-face interviews, ensuring that literacy was not a barrier to participation. Interviewers were recruited based on their proficiency in local dialects to facilitate communication with respondents. The cognitive battery—comprising orientation, verbal calculation, word recall, and figure copying tasks—was specifically selected to minimize reliance on reading or writing skills, making it suitable for use among older adults with low educational attainment.

Depression and social support

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the validated 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale short form (CESD-10). Each item was scored from 0 to 3, giving a total score from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Perceived social support was measured using CHARLS items reflecting the availability of financial support, caregiving support, and emotional support from family members. Three dimensions were assessed: (1) perceived financial support availability, (2) perceived caregiving support availability, and (3) emotional support from family members. Responses on the financial, caregiving, and emotional support items were standardized within wave, summed, and then linearly rescaled to a 0–10 index, with higher values indicating greater perceived social support. Because depressive symptoms and social support were hypothesized to lie on the pathway linking leisure activity to subsequent cognitive decline, they were not included in the primary etiologic models as adjustment variables. Instead, they were evaluated explicitly in the longitudinal mediation analyses.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori on the basis of previous literature and biological plausibility. Baseline covariates included age, sex, educational attainment (illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school, and above), marital status (married or partnered vs. unmarried, separated, widowed, or divorced), household registration (urban vs. rural), household per-capita expenditure quartile, body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), smoking status (never, former, or current), alcohol consumption (current drinker vs. not), chronic disease count (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, chronic lung disease, cancer, and arthritis; range 0–7), activities of daily living (ADL) limitations (range 0–6), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) limitations (range 0–5), and baseline global cognitive score. ADL limitations assessed difficulty with six basic self-care tasks (dressing, bathing, eating, getting in/out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling urination), while IADL limitations assessed difficulty with five more complex tasks (doing household chores, cooking, shopping, taking medication, and managing money). In the longitudinal mixed-effects models, chronic disease count, ADL, and IADL limitations were updated when repeated measurements were available; other covariates were treated as baseline characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) and categorical variables as number (percentage). To examine the independent and joint associations of baseline leisure activity with subsequent cognitive decline, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and random slopes for time since baseline (years). The interaction between time and each baseline activity measure was the primary parameter of interest, representing the between-group difference in the annual rate of change in global cognitive score according to baseline activity level. In these models, the interaction coefficient (Time × Activity) represents the difference in the annual rate of cognitive change per one-unit increase in activity frequency. Because activity was treated as a continuous variable (days/week), the implicit reference is zero activity; the positive interaction coefficient thus indicates that each additional day/week of activity is associated with a less negative (i.e., slower) annual decline. For the dose-response models, where activity was categorized, the ‘none’ group served as the explicit reference category. We included random intercepts and slopes for time to allow each participant to have their own baseline cognition and trajectory of change. The fixed effect of time represents the average decline rate, while the interaction between time and activity (the primary parameter of interest) indicates whether baseline activity predicts individual differences in this rate.

For the primary mixed-effects models, we used baseline leisure activity as the exposure of interest. This choice was made to: (a) establish temporal precedence and reduce the risk of reverse causation, as baseline activity predates the observation of subsequent cognitive decline; (b) capture habitual activity patterns that may better reflect long-term behavioral dispositions; and (c) ensure the subsequent RI-CLPM and mediation analyses, which incorporate repeated measures of activity, were designed to complement the primary models by examining within-person temporal dynamics and potential mediating pathways, respectively. These analytical components thus address different but related research questions rather than representing inconsistent exposure definitions. Age was treated as a continuous variable in all primary models to preserve statistical power and precision. Age groups (60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years) were used for descriptive purposes and for the prespecified subgroup analyses examining effect modification.

We used a sequential modeling strategy. First, separate mixed-effects models were estimated for social, intellectual, and physical activity. Second, the three domains were entered simultaneously into a mutually adjusted model to evaluate their independent contributions. Third, dose-response models were fitted using the four-level activity categories. Fourth, we examined the joint association of multi-domain leisure engagement with the rate of cognitive decline.

To clarify temporal directionality, we estimated random-intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPMs) across Waves 1–4 (2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018), with separate models for each leisure activity domain paired with global cognitive score. The RI-CLPM decomposes stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time. Each model included random intercepts, autoregressive paths, cross-lagged paths from activity at wave t to cognition at wave t + 1 and from cognition at wave t to activity at wave t + 1, and within-wave residual covariances. To obtain pooled estimates across adjacent waves, corresponding autoregressive and cross-lagged paths were constrained to equality across the three adjacent-wave transitions. RI-CLPMs were estimated in Mplus using full-information maximum likelihood to accommodate incomplete repeated measures. Model fit was evaluated with the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).

To strengthen the temporal plausibility of the mediation models, we specified the temporal ordering as follows: baseline leisure activity (Wave 1) predicted change in mediators between Waves 1 and 2, which in turn predicted change in cognitive function between Waves 2 and 4. This specification ensures that the mediators temporally precede the outcome trajectory, although we acknowledge that this temporal alignment does not fully satisfy all assumptions required for causal mediation.

We examined whether depressive symptoms and social support mediated the association between leisure activity and cognitive decline using multilevel structural equation modeling. For each activity domain, we estimated the total effect of baseline activity on the trajectory of global cognitive change, the indirect effect through depressive symptoms, the indirect effect through social support, the total indirect effect, and the remaining direct effect. Bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals were generated from 5,000 resamples, and the proportion mediated was calculated as the total indirect effect divided by the total effect. Mediation models were adjusted for the same baseline confounders used in the primary mixed-effects analyses.

To test the robustness of our findings to the harmonization of leisure activity frequencies, we conducted two additional sensitivity analyses. First, we re-estimated the mixed-effects models treating each domain’s activity level as an ordinal categorical variable (none, low, moderate, high) rather than a continuous weekly-equivalent score, with ‘none’ as the reference category. Second, we used binary indicators for any participation (vs. none) in each domain. The results of these sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using time-updated leisure activity exposures (updated at each wave) in the mixed-effects models, with the same covariate adjustment as the primary analysis. The results are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Prespecified sensitivity analyses included restriction to participants with data from all four waves, exclusion of participants with a baseline global cognitive score of 17 or lower, inverse probability weighting for attrition, replication of the longitudinal models using generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable working correlation structure, and subgroup analyses by age group, sex, and educational attainment. All analyses were conducted in Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) and Mplus 8.7 (Muthén & Muthén, Los Angeles, CA, USA). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The analytic cohort comprised 6,847 participants aged 60 years or older at baseline (Table 1). The mean age was 68.4 years (SD 6.5), 50.8% were women, 78.3% were married or partnered, and 70.6% lived in rural areas. Educational attainment was generally low, with 35.0% of participants being illiterate and a further 36.7% having only a primary school education. At baseline, the mean global cognitive score was 21.6 (SD 6.3), the mean CESD-10 score was 8.4 (SD 6.1), and the mean social support index was 5.8 (SD 2.2). Average leisure activity frequencies were 2.8 days/week for social activities, 1.6 days/week for intellectual activities, and 2.9 days/week for physical activities. Overall, 67.3% of participants engaged in at least one activity domain at a moderate-or-higher level, whereas 9.5% met this threshold in all three domains. Sample retention declined across waves: 5,642 participants (82.4%) were reassessed at Wave 2, 4,891 (71.4%) at Wave 3, and 4,203 (61.4%) at Wave 4. Compared with participants retained throughout follow-up, those lost to attrition were older, had lower baseline cognitive scores, and had lower baseline leisure activity participation. Descriptive statistics for key variables across all four waves are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

In separate mixed-effects models, higher baseline frequency of each leisure activity domain was associated with a slower subsequent decline in global cognitive score (Table 2). For each additional day/week of activity at baseline, the annual rate of cognitive decline was attenuated by 0.041 points for social activities (95% CI 0.024 to 0.058; P < 0.001), 0.057 points for intellectual activities (95% CI 0.034 to 0.080; P < 0.001), and 0.035 points for physical activities (95% CI 0.020 to 0.050; P < 0.001). After simultaneous adjustment for the three activity domains, all associations remained statistically significant, with the strongest independent association observed for intellectual activities (β = 0.046, 95% CI 0.023 to 0.069; P < 0.001), followed by social activities (β = 0.028, 95% CI 0.011 to 0.045; P = 0.002) and physical activities (β = 0.024, 95% CI 0.009 to 0.039; P = 0.003). In unadjusted models, the associations were slightly larger but directionally consistent for all three domains (Supplementary Table S4), with the attenuation after covariate adjustment indicating that a portion of the crude association was explained by sociodemographic, behavioral, and health-related factors.

A graded dose-response pattern was evident across all three domains (Table 3). For social activities, low participation was not significantly associated with the rate of cognitive decline relative to nonparticipation (β = 0.031, 95% CI -0.005 to 0.067; P = 0.091), whereas moderate and high participation were associated with progressively slower decline (β = 0.069, 95% CI 0.036 to 0.102; and β = 0.102, 95% CI 0.058 to 0.146; both P < 0.001). Intellectual activities showed a similarly monotonic pattern, with a significant association already evident at low participation (β = 0.039, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.076; P = 0.039) and stronger associations at moderate (β = 0.084, 95% CI 0.044 to 0.124; P < 0.001) and high levels (β = 0.125, 95% CI 0.069 to 0.181; P < 0.001). For physical activities, only moderate and high participation was associated with significantly slower decline (β = 0.058, 95% CI 0.027 to 0.089; and β = 0.088, 95% CI 0.047 to 0.129; both P < 0.001), whereas low participation was not (β = 0.022, 95% CI -0.013 to 0.057; P = 0.219).

Broader engagement across multiple activity domains was associated with progressively slower cognitive decline (Table 4). Compared with participants who did not engage at a moderate frequency in any activity domain, those who engaged in a single domain showed a slower annual decline in global cognitive score (β = 0.043, 95% CI 0.019 to 0.067; P < 0.001). The magnitude of the association increased further for engagement in two domains (β = 0.078, 95% CI 0.046 to 0.110; P < 0.001) and in all three domains (β = 0.098, 95% CI 0.054 to 0.142; P < 0.001), consistent with a significant linear trend (P for trend < 0.001).

In sensitivity analyses using ordinal categorical exposure variables, the dose-response pattern remained consistent, with moderate and high participation in each domain associated with progressively slower cognitive decline (Supplementary Table S1). Similarly, when using binary indicators for any participation, all three domains remained significantly associated with slower decline, with β coefficients of 0.052 for social activities, 0.068 for intellectual activities, and 0.045 for physical activities (all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S1). In a further sensitivity analysis using time-updated exposures for each leisure activity domain, the associations with cognitive decline remained statistically significant for all three domains, with somewhat attenuated effect estimates compared with the primary baseline-exposure models (social: β = 0.036, 95% CI 0.019–0.053; intellectual: β = 0.051, 95% CI 0.029–0.073; physical: β = 0.030, 95% CI 0.015–0.045; all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S2).

In RI-CLPM analyses, within-person increases in leisure activity predicted higher subsequent cognitive performance across adjacent waves (Table 5). The standardized cross-lagged path from activity to later cognition was significant for social activities (β = 0.065, 95% CI 0.031 to 0.099; P < 0.001), intellectual activities (β = 0.119, 95% CI 0.080 to 0.158; P < 0.001), and physical activities (β = 0.082, 95% CI 0.045 to 0.119; P < 0.001), with the largest prospective association observed for intellectual activities. In contrast, reverse cross-lagged paths from cognition to subsequent activity were small and not statistically significant for social activities (β = 0.021, 95% CI -0.009 to 0.051; P = 0.167), intellectual activities (β = 0.038, 95% CI -0.003 to 0.079; P = 0.071), or physical activities (β = 0.014, 95% CI -0.020 to 0.048; P = 0.419). Autoregressive coefficients were statistically significant for both activity measures and global cognitive scores in all three models, indicating moderate within-person stability over time. Model fit was consistently good, with CFI values ranging from 0.979 to 0.990, RMSEA from 0.018 to 0.022, and SRMR from 0.014 to 0.019.

Discussion

In this nationally representative longitudinal cohort of older Chinese adults, leisure engagement was associated with cognitive ageing in a patterned rather than uniform manner. The observed associations, while modest in absolute magnitude, are clinically meaningful when considered against the background annual rate of cognitive decline in this population and the potential cumulative benefits of sustained engagement over years. Social, intellectual, and physical activities each predicted a slower decline in global cognitive score, but the magnitude and threshold of association differed across domains, with intellectual activities showing the strongest independent relation after mutual adjustment.

This present study does not reduce leisure activity to a single, undifferentiated participation score. When multiple domains were modeled simultaneously, intellectual activity showed the largest independent association with slower cognitive decline, followed by social and then physical activity. Reading, strategy-based games, and learning activities repeatedly recruit attention, working memory, cognitive flexibility, planning, and memory retrieval. These repeated cognitive demands may strengthen reserve, improve neural efficiency, or enhance compensatory capacity in the face of age-related neuropathology [4, 20–22]. Prior longitudinal studies have suggested that intellectually stimulating leisure is associated with lower dementia risk or better-preserved cognition, including among the oldest-old and among populations with relatively low formal education [21, 23, 24]. At the same time, the persistence of significant associations for social and physical activities after mutual adjustment argues against the idea that later-life cognition is protected only by “mental exercise”. The findings support a complementary-mechanisms model in which different domains of leisure affect cognitive aging through partially distinct, though overlapping, pathways.

For social and physical activities, low participation was not associated with slower decline. By contrast, low levels of intellectual activity were associated with a modest benefit. Cognitively demanding activities may exert measurable effects even at lower frequency, whereas social and physical activities may require greater regularity before they translate into sustained cognitive, emotional, or physiological gains [25–28]. More importantly, the graded pattern across single-, two-, and three-domain engagement suggests that the domains are not redundant in our data. Instead, the pattern of joint associations is consistent with additive pathways that combine cognitive stimulation, social embedding, mood regulation, mobility maintenance, vascular protection, and reinforcement of other healthy behaviors. This interpretation accords with multidomain prevention frameworks and with recent intervention studies showing that bundled lifestyle strategies can improve or preserve cognition, even if effects vary by target population, adherence, and intervention intensity [29–32]. The dose-response patterns revealed domain-specific threshold effects. For physical activities, low participation was not significantly associated with cognitive decline, whereas moderate and high levels were progressively beneficial, suggesting a minimum frequency may be required for cognitive benefits. For social activities, a similar threshold pattern was observed, although the trend at low levels approached significance. In contrast, intellectual activities showed significant benefits even at low levels. These differential patterns suggest that promoting any intellectual engagement may be beneficial, while physical activity interventions should emphasize achieving a minimum frequency.

The multidomain leisure profile may confer advantages because it combines complementary forms of stimulation [31]: social activities may sustain interpersonal engagement and emotional reciprocity, intellectual activities may challenge higher-order cognition, and physical activities may support vascular, metabolic, and functional health. Older adults who maintain more diversified activity portfolios may also be more likely to preserve structured daily routines and adaptive behavioral flexibility, both of which could contribute to cognitive resilience. Because multidomain engagement may mark broader health advantage, self-efficacy, or environmental opportunity, this gradient should be viewed as supportive of cumulative benefit rather than definitive proof of synergy [17]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the gradient may reflect broader health status or a ‘healthy lifestyle’ profile. Individuals who engage in multiple domains may differ systematically from those who do not in ways partially captured by our covariates, such as health literacy, self-efficacy, or environmental resources. While our findings demonstrate a clear dose-response relationship with multi-domain engagement, we cannot definitively distinguish synergy from general health-promoting characteristics, a distinction that warrants future investigation with objective health and functional measures.

Individuals in the prodromal phase of cognitive decline often reduce participation in leisure activities before overt impairment is recognized, making it difficult to distinguish protection from selection. After separating stable between-person differences from within-person change, higher-than-usual activity at one wave predicted better cognition at the next wave across all three domains, with the strongest prospective association observed for intellectual activity. In contrast, the reverse paths from cognition to later activity were small and nonsignificant. These results do not prove causality, but they do make a purely reverse-causal explanation and may suggest that changes in leisure are not simply epiphenomenal to underlying cognitive decline [20, 23, 31]. The two- to three-year spacing of CHARLS waves may be too coarse to capture shorter feedback loops, and subtle preclinical decline could still affect activity patterns before it is adequately captured by the global cognition measure [33, 34].

Mediation analyses provide a statistical decomposition of the observed associations, suggesting that depressive symptoms and social support may account for a substantial portion of the leisure–cognition association. Across all three domains, depressive symptoms and family-based social support accounted for roughly two-fifths of the association, with depressive symptoms explaining a slightly larger proportion. One plausible pathway is that leisure activities may be associated with reduced depressive symptoms, which in turn may correlate with better cognitive outcomes, although this interpretation remains statistical rather than causal [35]. A similar statistical pathway through social support is also plausible, particularly in China, where family and community ties remain central to later-life well-being. Leisure participation was correlated with larger supportive networks, greater perceived availability of help, and better access to emotionally and cognitively enriching exchanges [36–38]. These statistical pathways do not preclude additional mechanistic contributions through cognitive reserve, cerebrovascular integrity, neuroplasticity, or reduced systemic inflammation [22, 25–27, 39].

Leisure opportunities, health literacy, retirement patterns, and access to community resources are unequally distributed across China, and prevention strategies imported from highly urbanized or resource-rich settings may not generalize well [2, 3, 40]. These findings may be especially relevant in the Chinese aging context, where a large proportion of older adults live in rural settings and have limited formal education. We recognize that the generalizability of our findings to populations outside China or with different sociodemographic profiles requires caution, as our sample was predominantly rural and had low formal education. Nevertheless, the biological and psychosocial mechanisms underlying these associations are likely to operate across diverse populations, suggesting that our findings may inform prevention strategies in similar aging populations, though cultural adaptation would be essential for direct implementation. Interventions anchored in leisure behavior could be more scalable than resource-intensive cognitive training or clinic-based programs, although this hypothesis requires direct testing in pragmatic intervention studies, particularly because their feasibility depends on being embedded in existing community and household routines. The present results are consistent with the notion that promoting moderate or higher participation across more than one activity domain could represent a pragmatic focus for future pilot studies, while also recognizing that even relatively modest intellectual engagement can be meaningful. From a public health perspective, these supports combine cognitively engaging, socially interactive, and movement-based components rather than a single behavioral prescription.

While the effect estimates appear modest in absolute terms (β = 0.024–0.046 points/year per additional day/week), their clinical relevance should be interpreted against the background of age-related cognitive decline. In our cohort, the average annual decline in global cognition was approximately 0.45 points, meaning that each additional day/week of activity was associated with a 5%–10% reduction in this annual decline. For intellectual activities at 2 days/week, this translates to a 20% relative reduction in annual decline. Over a 10-year period, such cumulative benefits could meaningfully preserve cognitive function. Moreover, engagement across all three domains yielded more than double the benefit of single-domain engagement (β = 0.098 vs. 0.043 points/year), supporting additive clinical value. Given the low cost and favorable safety profile of leisure activities, even modest effect sizes may have meaningful population health implications.

This study has several limitations. First, leisure activities were self-reported and partly harmonized from categorical monthly responses into weekly-equivalent scores, which may have introduced non-differential measurement error and argues for caution in making fine-grained threshold claims. Second, the primary mixed-effects analyses used baseline activity as the exposure, whereas the temporal and mediation models incorporated repeated measures; accordingly, the three analytic components should be interpreted as complementary rather than interchangeable. Third, although the RI-CLPM reduced concerns about reverse causation and the models adjusted for a broad range of confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—including sensory impairment, sleep, neighborhood resources, personality, and genetic susceptibility—cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, our study remains observational, and the temporal pattern observed does not definitively prove causality, as subtle preclinical cognitive changes may antedate measurable decline. Accordingly, all findings should be interpreted as longitudinal associations, and intervention studies are required for causal confirmation. Fourth, our primary mixed-effects models used baseline leisure activity as the exposure and thus did not explicitly model changes in activity participation over time. While our RI-CLPM analyses separately examined within-person fluctuations, and the sensitivity analysis using time-updated exposures yielded consistent results, we acknowledge that trajectory-based approaches could provide additional insights. Future studies may explore these alternative modelling strategies to further disentangle the effects of sustained versus changing activity patterns on cognitive ageing. Fifth, despite adjustment for a broad range of covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. The CHARLS dataset lacked measures of sensory impairments, sleep quality, physical frailty, neighborhood environmental resources, and genetic susceptibility. These factors could be associated with both activity participation and cognitive decline. The likely direction of confounding from most of these factors would be away from the null, as they tend to correlate with lower activity and faster decline. However, non-differential measurement error in our exposure measures would bias estimates toward the null, and some factors (e.g., sleep, frailty) may lie on the causal pathway rather than acting purely as confounders. Thus, the net bias is difficult to quantify precisely, and our findings should be interpreted with this residual confounding in mind. Future studies incorporating objective sensory, sleep, frailty, and genetic measures are needed to further isolate the independent contribution of leisure activities to cognitive aging. Sixth, our mediation analyses, while temporally informed by the repeated-measures structure of CHARLS, remain observational and cannot establish causal mediation. The standard assumptions required for causal mediation cannot be fully verified with our data. Additionally, time-varying confounders that affect both the mediators and cognitive trajectories may have biased our indirect effect estimates. Therefore, our mediation results should be interpreted as statistical mediation rather than as definitive mechanistic evidence. Seventh, the global cognitive score reflects the CHARLS cognitive battery and should not be equated with a clinical dementia diagnosis. Finally, attrition, survival bias, and selective non-response may have biased our estimates, particularly among older and more vulnerable participants. In our cohort, retention declined from 82.4% at Wave 2 to 61.4% at Wave 4, and participants lost to follow-up were older and had lower baseline cognition, suggesting non-random attrition that may have led to underestimation of cognitive decline. Although our mixed-effects models assume missing at random and we conducted inverse probability weighting as a sensitivity analysis, data may not be missing completely at random, and results may differ in the most vulnerable subgroups. Future work should examine activity trajectories rather than single baseline measures, distinguish specific cognitive domains, incorporate richer contextual information on activity quality and social setting, and test whether culturally adapted multidomain leisure programs can reproduce these observational associations in pragmatic intervention studies.

Conclusion

In this nationally representative longitudinal cohort of older Chinese adults, higher engagement across social, intellectual, and physical leisure activities was consistently associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories, with the strongest independent association observed for intellectual activities and incremental benefits linked to broader multi-domain participation. Temporal analyses were consistent with a predominantly activity-to-cognition direction, while depressive symptoms and family-based social support statistically accounted for a substantial, albeit incomplete, proportion of the observed associations. Together, these findings provide observational evidence that cognitively, socially, and physically engaging leisure routines are associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories. However, because our design is observational, we emphasize that these results are hypothesis-generating. Future randomized controlled trials and mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether these associations reflect causal effects and to identify the most effective activity combinations and target populations.
