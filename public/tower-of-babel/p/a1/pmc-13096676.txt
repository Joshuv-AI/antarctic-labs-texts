Examining HbA1c in Frail Older Adults: A Linked Data Cohort Study
Authors: Gideon Meyerowitz‐Katz, Julee McDonagh, Scott William, Reejamol John, Tien‐Ming Hng, Mark McLean, Mary Bul, Richard I Lindley, Caleb Ferguson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDiabetes and frailty pose a significant issue for society. Previous research has shown a mixed relationship between diabetes and frailty in older people, indicating a complex interrelationship. The aim of this study was to examine the association between routinely collected HbA1c testing data and frailty in a large cohort from Western Sydney.MethodsData from the Western Sydney Frailty Registry Study were linked to the Blacktown and Mount Druitt hospital HbA1c testing datasets. These datasets include information on patient outcomes, pathological metrics, hospitalisation indicators, and a range of morbidity and demographic information. This was then analysed in Stata using multivariable regression.ResultsDiabetes was more common in frail patients than in pre‐frail or non‐frail patients. Those with both diabetes and frailty did not have an increased risk of death or rehospitalisation in age and sex corrected analyses (OR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.36–1.21 and 0.90, 95% CI: 0.37–2.17, respectively) compared to those with frailty alone. There was a reduced risk of death associated with higher HbA1c for frail people (OR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.90).ConclusionsThere is a complex interrelationship between diabetes and frailty. For frail people with diabetes, there are important lessons for management. These include the possibility that HbA1c is not a useful metric and that overtreatment with hypoglycaemic agents must be considered.Keywords: aged, diabetes mellitus, frailty, glycated haemoglobin, multimorbidity

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a matter of global concern that represents a major personal and public health issue [1, 2]. Diabetes also represents a significant community and financial problem, associated with high health service use. The self‐reported prevalence of DM in Australia is approximately 18% for adults aged 75 years [3]; however, due to underreporting and underdiagnosis, this number is likely to be higher [4]. Proactive screening at Blacktown Hospital in Western Sydney has shown dramatically higher rates of diabetes in hospitalised adults, especially at older ages. Australian evidence has also shown rates as high as 30% in hospitalised people [5]. Diabetes mellitus is associated with increased rates of hospitalisation, disability, cognitive impairment and mortality in older adults [6, 7]. Several previous studies have suggested adults living with DM are more likely to be frail than older adults not living with DM [8, 9, 10]. In people aged greater than 65 years, frailty prevalence is estimated to be up to five‐fold higher in patients with diabetes than the general population [1].

Frailty is a clinical state of vulnerability to stressor events and cumulative decline across multiple physiological systems [11]. It contributes to functional decline and high rates of disability and is commonly associated with multimorbidity, polypharmacy, social isolation and loneliness and iatrogenesis [12, 13]. Older adults are at greater risk of becoming frail, yet younger people with chronic diseases are also susceptible [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Due to a rapidly ageing population, frailty is considered an emerging global health priority [14, 15, 17, 18, 19]. Frailty is associated with increased risk for adverse health outcomes, including disability, hospitalisation and mortality, with significantly higher healthcare utilisation [20, 21].

There remain significant research gaps in the complex relationship between frailty and diabetes [22]. These research gaps are described in a recent review as predominantly related to age group. Dysglycaemic states, as defined by abnormalities in glycated Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), may be relevant in health outcomes related to frailty and deterioration [22]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated varying results of the impact of hypo‐ or hyperglycaemia and frailty, depending on sex and relevant comorbidities, including cognitive decline and neuropathology [23, 24, 25, 26, 27].

Traditionally, clinical trials have frequently overlooked the regular evaluation of frailty, functional status and comorbidities, leading to a significant deficiency in adequately describing the older individuals participating in these studies [28, 29, 30]. In addition, frail older people are often excluded from clinical trials due to perceived challenges in recruitment and exclusion criteria that focus the population on younger, healthier individuals with limited comorbidities [29]. This has led to a paucity of evidence on diabetes in frail older people.

Blacktown and Mount Druitt hospitals are components of a medium‐sized, jointly governed facility across two campuses with a total of 550 beds and an annual patient load of approximately 100,000 in the west of Sydney, Australia. There has been an ongoing diabetes detection effort at the hospitals since 2016 that involves routine HbA1c screening for patients attending the emergency department [31]. In addition, the hospitals host a frailty clinical quality registry that includes a wealth of data on older people who have been admitted for a variety of reasons. This paper presents an analysis of these linked datasets.

Aim

To explore the association between routinely collected HbA1C and its effect on frailty and outcomes in frail older adults through linked datasets held by Blacktown and Mount Druitt hospitals.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

Human Research Ethical Clearance was obtained from the Western Sydney Local Health District Human (WSLHD) Human Research Ethics Committee for the Western Sydney Frailty Registry Study (Ref 2019/ETH12635, date: 8 January 2020).

Data were captured and stored on the New South Wales Ministry of Health Office of Health and Medical Research REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) server. All data are re‐identifiable using a unique statewide numeric code called Area Unit ID (AUID) as per the approved and published Western Sydney Clinical Frailty registry protocol [32]. Informed consent was provided by all participants in the Western Sydney Clinical Frailty Registry to conduct data linkage within the existing approved protocol and consent process. Data were linked using a combination of AUID and other identifiable information with the HbA1c testing dataset, which also contains identifiable information. These datasets are routinely used for clinical purposes. Once linked, all identifying information was removed aside from AUID.

Design

This study was a linked cohort study using two datasets that have previously been described elsewhere. The Western Sydney Clinical Frailty Registry was developed to better understand the clinical progress of frail individuals in hospital, and includes extensive information on older adults admitted to Blacktown and Mount Druitt hospitals [33]. The Western Sydney Clinical Frailty Registry is a 10‐year longitudinal cohort that includes a range of frailty assessments as well as information on admission, comorbidities and outcomes, such as rehospitalisation and death.

The Blacktown/Mount Druitt HbA1c database (Ref 2402–07 QA date: 3 June 2024) is populated by the diabetes screening program. Briefly, all patients who attend the emergency departments at Blacktown/Mount Druitt hospitals who have blood sampled also have HbA1c measured [34]. These data are then captured in a database that includes all other tests that took place at the same time as well as demographic and clinical information on the patients. The database also includes limited information on hospitalisation and area‐level indicators, including socioeconomics. The testing database includes nearly 300,000 test results since its inception in 2016, as well as approximately 180,000 individual patient records.

Participants

In this study, the first 1028 participants enrolled in the Western Sydney Clinical Frailty Registry were linked to HbA1c testing data from Blacktown and Mount Druitt hospital emergency departments. There were no specific exclusion criteria.

Data were also linked to the hospital admissions dataset to identify individuals who had a diagnosis of diabetes since 2016. All patients were followed up in these systems from 2016 to 2024, when the extractions were conducted. Patients were linked using unique ID number (AUID). As this study was exploratory, no sample size calculations were performed.

Definition of Frailty

All participants enrolled in the Western Sydney Clinical Frailty Registry have an assessment of pre‐morbid (pre‐hospital) frailty completed at baseline using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) [35]. The CFS is a 9‐point numerical scale; an individual with a score of 1–4 is classified as non‐frail, a score of 5–6 is classified as mild‐moderately frail and a score of 7–9 is classified as severely frail [35].

Variables

We reviewed diabetes rates by frailty category. To assess the effect of diabetes and frailty on health, we used all‐cause mortality within 12 months. We also assessed rates of diabetes by frailty category, defined as either a positive HbA1c test or a clinically recorded diagnosis of diabetes in the hospital records. We included the Charlson Comorbidity Index, which was calculated as part of the Frailty Registry documentation.

We additionally included history of diseases that are recorded in the Frailty Registry, pathological findings available in the HbA1c testing data, and other biochemical information available through either dataset. Finally, we reviewed medication prescription rates, which are collected based on clinical records in the Frailty Registry.

Statistical Methods

Stata Version 15 was used to analyse quantitative data. Demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by HbA1c level were summarised using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were then summarised using frequencies and percentages. Association between HbA1c and frailty and diabetes medication was analysed using logistic regression. Logistic models were adjusted for age and sex. The rate of undiagnosed diabetes based on HbA1c levels was analysed by examining the distribution of HbA1c levels in the undiagnosed diabetes group and by conducting sensitivity analyses to explore different HbA1c thresholds for identifying undiagnosed diabetes. A HbA1c of > 6.4% was used to define a likely case of diabetes in the absence of a clinical record of a diagnosis.

To address potential selection bias, all analyses were corrected for age and sex.

There were no participants missing frailty data. After linkage, all records had a diabetes status either through HbA1c results or hospital records. All patients were followed up through linkage to hospital and death records.

Results

A total of 1028 individual records were linked, with a mean age of 82 years; 60% were female (n = 603), and the mean total number of prescription medicines prescribed was 10 (Table 1). A quarter (n = 255) had a history of stroke, 22% (219) had a history of chronic pulmonary disease and 20% (n = 204) had dementia with chronic cognitive deficit. The mean modified Charlson Comorbidity Index score for this cohort was 2.2. Frailty according to the CFS indicated that 19% (n = 192) were classified as severely frail, 57% (n = 582) were classified as mild‐moderately frail and 25% (n = 254) were classified as non‐frail. There were 285 people with a diagnosis of diabetes. In addition, there were 14 (5% of all people with diabetes) individuals who had an HbA1c > 6.5% but no diagnosis of diabetes, making the overall total 299. Participants were followed up for 1 year for rehospitalisation and death recording, and until linkage was performed in September 2024 for HbA1c data.

Correcting for age and sex, the rate of diabetes increased from 34% at a clinical frailty scale category of non‐frail to 49% at the highest category of clinical frailty (p < 0.001) as illustrated in Figure 1.

Bar graph of frailty status and proportion diagnosed with diabetes.

There was no increased risk of death for those with diabetes and frailty compared to those with frailty alone. Correcting for age and sex, frail individuals with diabetes had an odds ratio of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.36–1.21) for death over 12‐month follow‐up. There was also no increase in risk of death for those with diabetes in the cohort as a whole (OR: 0.92, 95% CI: 0.67–1.27).

People with diabetes were more likely to be readmitted at least once during the 12 months than those without diabetes, but this effect was not statistically significant (OR: 1.37, 95% CI: 0.97–1.92). This effect was also not apparent when the analysis was limited to only those with frailty (OR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.37–2.17).

We found that a higher HbA1c was associated with a reduced risk of death in this cohort. Every 1% increase in HbA1c reduced the odds of death within 12 months by 42% (OR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.37–0.90) for frail people. This result held if the analysis was limited to only frail individuals with diabetes, although it was no longer statistically significant (OR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.28–1.11). Similarly, the association held for frail people with diabetes when body mass index (BMI) was added to the regression model (OR: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.39–0.95). However, taking into account hypoglycaemic medication largely eliminated this association (OR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.41–1.19). This association was not statistically significant for the entire cohort (OR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.73–1.03) and was also materially diminished.

As a sensitivity analysis, we reviewed the risk of death for individuals with a history of stroke. There was no increased risk of death for this cohort (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 0.74–1.51 for with vs. without stroke).

Discussion

Diabetes can be associated with up to a 90% increased risk of death in frail adults [6]. After examining the association between HbA1c and frailty, our study did not find that diabetes increased the risk of death. Surprisingly, we found that for frail individuals, even those who have diabetes, a higher HbA1c was associated with a lower risk of death.

There were a small number of individuals in the cohort who had a HbA1c consistent with diabetes, but no diagnosis recorded. The rate in this population is substantially lower than has been previously seen in Australian hospital populations in the past, which may be due to the extensive testing program in Blacktown hospital [36]. Despite the relatively low rate, the fact that there were any individuals in this population who were recently screened for diabetes but had not had a clinical code is concerning.

There are several plausible explanations for the reduced rate of death with higher HbA1c. The first possibility is selection bias. This cohort recruited older people who were admitted for frailty‐related disease. To be included in the cohort, and to be considered frail, individuals require one of a selection of diagnoses. This means that the risk of diabetes may have been counterbalanced in our cohort with other diseases, such as stroke and chronic kidney disease. Our sensitivity analysis provided some evidence of this phenomenon, as a history of stroke was not associated with an increased risk of death either.

It is also possible that this represents a difference in management. This cohort was taken at a time when Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP1‐RA) drugs such as semaglutide were used in this population of older people. There is now strong evidence that semaglutide reduces the risk of all‐cause mortality both for people with diabetes and those with obesity [37]. It is possible that the reduced risk of death was caused by prescription of these lifesaving medications for older people with dysglycaemia.

Another possibility speaks to possible practice changes for frail older people with diabetes. Extensive data show that although stringent blood glucose management may be beneficial for younger individuals, it carries risks particularly for frail older people [38]. A recent cohort study demonstrated a J‐shaped curve for HbA1c in older people, with those under 6% and over 8% seeing increased risks of death [39]. Some diabetes medications can cause hypoglycaemia, and there are many risks and challenges associated with diabetes treatment. These are considered worthwhile for younger people to prevent long‐term macro and microvascular complications, but in older individuals may not be beneficial. Both the American Diabetes Association [40] and the American Geriatrics Society [41] recommend adjusted HbA1c targets for older patients.

Thus, it is possible that the lower risk of death for those with higher HbA1c is reflective of the potential problem of overtreatment in frail older people with multimorbidity. It is possible that in this highly unwell cohort the risks of glycaemic management outweigh the benefits. Our sensitivity analysis including the prescription of hypoglycaemic medication into the regression model is supportive of this possibility, as the effect of higher HbA1c was no longer detectable when these medications were taken into account. This may indicate that, unlike in the general population, higher HbA1c in this context is a proxy for optimal diabetes management in this frail older group, with targets set higher to avoid complications. There is currently a large cluster‐randomised trial being run in the Netherlands aimed at assessing whether deprescribing medications may reduce complications for frail older people with diabetes [42]. In addition, new Australian guidelines have just been released for deprescribing in older people [43]. The result of the current study provides insights which may support this concept.

It is notable that in the current cohort 83% were readmitted to hospital at least once in the 12 months following their entry into the registry, and 20% died. Our data may indicate that diabetes management in hospital for frail older people is currently suboptimal and focused on reducing HbA1c rather than on the overall well‐being of older people, although such a conclusion would require further research to substantiate.

A further explanation is that these results are reflective of deficiencies in the test itself, as HbA1c results can be distorted by a variety of biological factors [44]. There are some data indicating that such factors are more common in frail older people, and these may explain the preponderance of low HbA1c results and the very high death rate in this group [45]. Thus, these results may simply be a failure of the test rather than an indication that overtreatment is an issue. This may have implications for how HbA1c is used in frail older patients and should spur basic science research into the reasons for this unexpected association.

Another particularly useful direction would be to examine a longitudinal dataset to see how changes in blood sugar over time impact this population, as that may provide useful answers about the causal mechanisms at play. Additionally, there may be scope for future randomised trials looking at the benefits of medication reviews in older people specific to diabetes medications. It may also be helpful for future research to focus on predictive modelling for this cohort to determine which biological markers are most pertinent to future diabetes‐related risk, and to see whether HbA1c is potentially less useful than other blood glucose measurements.

These data also raise the pertinent question of whether the relationship between DM and frailty is bi‐directional. Despite this, the precise biology of shared common pathophysiological underpinnings is poorly understood. Several longitudinal studies suggest that sarcopenia, a key component of frailty, may represent the pathophysiological link between frailty and DM [8, 46, 47, 48]. In community‐dwelling adults, a relative increase loss of muscle mass is often followed by increase in visceral fat, known as ‘sarcopenic obesity’ [49].

Other studies suggest inflammation and vitamin deficiency to be shared pathophysiological mechanisms, though further research is required to better understand the complexities of the relationship and whether frailty and DM are bi‐directional [50, 51, 52].

Strengths and Limitations

There are a range of limitations to this study. Although it was a large group for those with frailty, it was not sufficiently large to examine every possible subgroup in detail. The cohort with diabetes, frailty and data on blood glucose and other comorbidities, for example, was quite small. Thus, it may be that there are explanations in the dataset that we will only discover when more patients are added to the clinical frailty registry.

The study cohort was also highly selected. We discuss this as a possibility, but it also may limit generalisability. Blacktown, in western Sydney, is a large, highly multicultural area, and thus, the results may not be applicable to more monocultural areas or those in low‐income countries. In addition, the selection of participants into the frailty register may have introduced additional selection bias which is impossible to account for.

However, this was a relatively large cohort with a substantial quantity of data to examine questions. We have also included a range of subgroup and sensitivity analyses to review possible explanations of our findings. The data were high‐quality, and linkage to administrative databases ensured that there are few missing values within the cohort.

Conclusions

Diabetes is common in those admitted to geriatric medicine hospital services. There is a strong but complex association between diabetes, HbA1c and frailty. It is possible that HbA1c may not always be a useful measure of diabetes control in this frail population. In addition, some frail individuals had undiagnosed diabetes. It is important to screen for diabetes in this group, to optimise patient outcomes. It is also important to consider management changes in people with diabetes and frailty, which may include deprescription.
