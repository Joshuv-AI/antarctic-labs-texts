Coincidence of Autoimmune Diabetes Mellitus and Familial Partial Lipodystrophy
Authors: Michelle Krienke, Hartmut H J Schmidt, Janine Buettner, Susanne Tan, Isabell Schumann, Konstanze Miehle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFamilial partial lipodystrophy (FPLD) is a rare genetic disorder leading to adipose tissue deficiency/redistribution and metabolic dysregulation with insulin resistance, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. Coincidence of FPLD and autoantibody-positive type 1 diabetes has not been published yet. We identified a female patient with the initial diagnosis of autoantibody-positive type 1 diabetes who developed severe insulin resistance with daily insulin doses up to 300 IU/d, acanthosis nigricans, polycystic ovary syndrome, hyperphagia, myalgia, and dyslipidemia. Since her phenotype was suspicious for FPLD, genetic testing was initiated. It revealed a heterozygous missense variant at hot spot codon c.1444 within Lamin A/C (LMNA) (p.Arg482Trp). Insulin resistance and hyperphagia improved during treatment with the glucagon-like peptide 1-receptor agonist semaglutide in addition to insulin therapy. Thus, we report about a patient with FPLD2 and concomitant type 1 diabetes. This case demonstrates that coincidence of these 2 severe metabolic dysregulations challenges diagnosis, management, and treatment but demands awareness in our health care system for rare endocrine diseases to improve quality of care.

Introduction

Familial partial lipodystrophy (FPLD) syndromes are rare inherited diseases characterized by partial loss of subcutaneous adipose tissue (AT) and cardio-metabolic complications [1]. The most common subtype in Europe is FPLD type 2 (FPLD2) which is caused by autosomal dominantly inherited heterozygous variants in the Lamin A/C gene (LMNA, MIM*150330) [2]. Loss and dysfunction of AT in lipodystrophy (LD) provoke ectopic accumulation of lipids in other organs with consecutive lipotoxicity [3] and derangements in AT-derived proteins (adipocytokines) [4]. Both are key mechanisms for development of insulin resistance (IR) in LD, which often occurs years before development of overt diabetes mellitus [3, 5]. Associations of LD with autoimmune diseases, such as type 1 diabetes (T1D), have been described for acquired LD [6]. However, no coincidence of genetic LD subtypes with autoimmune T1D has been reported yet.

In the following report, we describe a patient with overt diabetes mellitus, positive glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD)- and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)- antibodies, and severe IR due to genetically confirmed FPLD2 indicative of a combined diabetes mellitus with autoimmune (insulin deficiency) and genetic (specifically, IR) components. Treatment with insulin was inferior to a combined glucagon-like peptide 1-receptor agonist (GLP1-RA)/insulin regimen which resulted in a distinct improvement of glycemic control as well as in marked reduction of daily insulin dose.

Case Presentation

We describe a young woman of Caucasian ancestry. First symptoms were observed at the age of 12 years with an accumulation of fat around the face and abdomen and a loss of subcutaneous AT on the extremities leading to a muscular appearance. Additionally, she was troubled by severe fatigue and myalgia. Cushing syndrome and thyroid dysfunction were suspected but could be ruled out. At the age of 17 years, she attended her general practitioner due to polydipsia and weight loss of 12 kg within 3 months. Thereupon, diabetes mellitus was diagnosed. Elevated autoantibodies against GAD could be detected. Thus, diabetes was classified as T1D and insulin treatment was immediately initiated. Concurrently, excessive hypertriglyceridemia was diagnosed.

The patient developed carpal tunnel syndrome, which worsened during follow-up. Polycystic ovary syndrome with hirsutism was diagnosed. Moreover, she described a lack of satiety leading to hyperphagia. Shortly after diagnosis of T1D, the patient required high doses of insulin (total daily dose [TDD] 300 IU) to achieve glycemic control, indicating IR. Therefore, treatment was extended by metformin and empagliflozin. However, this treatment strategy was of moderate impact. She was overweight to obese with a body mass index around 30 kg/m². Physical examination revealed a cushingoid phenotype with paucity of subcutaneous AT at buttocks and limbs, accumulation of fat at face and neck, umbilical hernia (Fig. 1), and acanthosis nigricans of neck, groin, and axillary region. In addition, metabolic dysfunction–associated liver disease (MASLD) with steatosis hepatis and hepatomegaly on ultrasound, as well as early stage of concentric cardiac hypertrophy, were detected.

Figure shows the patient at the age of 28 years illustrating both lipoatrophy and muscular hypertrophy on her limbs with prominent veins and increased accumulation of adipose tissue in her face and neck area.

Diagnostic Assessment

Based on the clinical and biochemical phenotype, FPLD was supposed. Genetic testing was performed at the age of 21 years by PCR and bidirectional sequencing of the LMNA gene (NM_170707.4). A heterozygous missense variant NM_170707.4:c.1444C>T p.(Arg482Trp) within LMNA was detected. According to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) the variant was classified as likely pathogenic (PM1, PM2_SUP, PM5, PP3) [7] and has been submitted to ClinVar (ID: NM_ 170707.4:c.1444C>T p.(Arg482Trp). Thus, diagnosis of LMNA-associated FPLD2 could be established (Fig. 2A). The pedigree of the family is depicted in Fig. 2B. The father of the index patient was phenotypically suspicious for LD due to muscle hypertrophy with lack of functionality of the limbs [8]. Genetic analysis revealed the same heterozygous variant in LMNA at codon c.1444 (c.1444C>T) in the father as in the index patient. Mother, brother, sister, half-sister, and half-brother (both with the same mother as the index patient) had no clinical signs of LD and genetic testing showed no LD-associated mutations. The patient’s father has one brother who—similarly to the paternal grandmother—shows no phenotypical signs of LD and no metabolic alterations. Both did not undergo genetic testing. The paternal grandfather died at young age from infection. He was not affected by metabolic complications.

Treatment

During pregnancy with her first child, hypertriglyceridemia was treated with diet and lifestyle modifications. Glucose metabolism was closely monitored and insulin dose was regularly adjusted if necessary. However, despite close follow-up, it was difficult to achieve good glycemic control. At 30 weeks of gestation, the patient was admitted to the hospital due to premature labor. She gave birth by cesarean delivery at 34 weeks and 5 days of gestation. The infant had a birth weight of 3500 gram. After delivery, glycemic control worsened, leading to chronic hyperglycemic decompensation and aggravation of IR with the need of an intravenous insulin infusion. Thereby, TDD could be reduced from 280 to 100 IU and glucose levels improved. Re-evaluation of autoantibody status revealed positivity for GAD- and ZnT8-antibodies. Thus, a mixed form of diabetes mellitus with insulin deficiency and severe IR was confirmed. Anthropometric and metabolic parameters during inpatient treatment and follow-up are shown in Table 1. After recompensation, treatment with the GLP1-RA semaglutide was initiated in addition to insulin, empagliflozin, and metformin.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Under treatment with semaglutide a significant and sustainable improvement of glycemic control could be achieved with decrease of glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) from 8.8% (73 mmol/mol; reference range ≤6.5% [≤48 mol/mol]) to 7.4% (57 mmol/mol) after 3 months of treatment (Fig. 3). Moreover, she described improvement of satiety. Unfortunately, semaglutide had to be discontinued after 3 months of treatment due to missing reimbursement by the patient's health insurance company. After discontinuation, glucose homeostasis worsened. HbA1c increased to 8.7% (72 mmol/mol) at 5 months and reached 10.3% (89 mmol/mol) at 18 months after withdrawal of semaglutide despite a continuous increase in insulin TDD rising to 270 IU and nearly 400 IU at 5 months and at 18 months, respectively, underlining a marked worsening of IR (Table 1, Fig. 3). Moreover, the patient described an increase in hunger during the follow-up visits, which is a typical symptom in LD.

Discussion

We report on a patient with FPLD2 who is affected by FPLD-associated IR and concomitant T1D with positive GAD- and ZnT8-autoantibodies accounting for a mixed phenotype of diabetes. The patient, carrying a disease-causing variant within the p.482 mutation hot spot locus in exon 8 of the LMNA gene, shows a typical clinical phenotype for FPLD2. It is well known that acquired LD syndromes often are associated with autoimmunity [6]. Among other autoimmune syndromes, also T1D has been described in patients with acquired LD [9, 10]. Kumar et al reported an 8-year-old boy with acquired LD-induced generalized loss of body fat and IR as well as diabetic ketoacidosis and GAD-antibodies in later life. Poor glycemic control despite high doses of insulin and metformin but some metabolic improvement after addition of pioglitazone was observed in this case [9]. Evaluating 50 patients with LD, Park et al identified 2 patients having both acquired generalized LD and T1D [10]. In one patient, LD preceded T1D, whereas in the other patient LD followed the onset of T1D. Contrary to acquired LD, an association with autoimmune conditions has not been reported in patients with genetic forms of LD before. Moreover, no coincidence with T1D has been described in patients with FPLD. Similarly to the patients with acquired LD and T1D, our patient has a double diabetes. This double diabetes has some special characteristics that distinguish it from common T1D. Despite positive autoantibodies, residual beta-cell function in our patient has been preserved for 10 years after diagnosing autoimmune diabetes (as could be seen in the course of C-peptide levels; Table 1), preventing her from developing ketoacidosis. After C-peptide dropped below the normal range our patient developed one single episode of ketoacidosis. Whereas hypoglycemia is one of the main problems in T1DM, our patient with double diabetes is much more prone to hyperglycemic exacerbation, which is due to FPLD2 associated IR. Despite insulinopenia, achievement of glycemic control has been difficult due to the severe IR with a TDD of up to 300 IU. In LD, loss of adipose tissue leads to inadequate adipose storage capacity. The situation is aggravated by excess energy intake due to leptin deficiency [11]. Since excess nutritional fat cannot be stored in regular depots, it overflows and accumulates in ectopic tissue depots, for example, the liver [12]. Thus, fatty acids and their lipotoxic products are significantly enriched in hepatocytes. This provokes mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and an increase in proinflammatory cytokines, which impairs the insulin signaling pathway and subsequently results in severe hepatic IR [13, 14]. Due to the combination of antibody positivity and insulinopenia as well as severe IR in one patient, finding the correct diabetes diagnosis and diabetes treatment is very challenging in this case.

In our patient, diagnosis of T1D was made 4 years before diagnosing FPLD2. Insulin treatment has been initiated rapidly after diagnosis. After worsening of IR, attempts to improve glycemic control by moderately potent drugs, such as metformin and empagliflozin, was of limited efficacy.

GLP1-RAs are very efficient drugs in patients with type 2 diabetes and obesity [15, 16]. Furthermore, these drugs seem to be also interesting in LD for several reasons. First, hyperphagia is a key symptom of patients with LD [11]. Paucity of subcutaneous adipose tissue in LD—especially gluteofemoral fat loss as it is typical for FPLD—alters concentration of adipose tissue–derived hormones (adipokines) such as leptin, which is a key adipokine regulator of hunger-satiety signaling. Reduced serum leptin levels in LD result in disturbed eating behavior with strong hunger feelings and a shorter duration of satiety after eating [11]. However, due to the rarity of the disease, statistical data about the prevalence of hyperphagia and absence of satiety in LD are lacking. Second, patients with FPLD2 typically exhibit central fat distribution [17]. Valerio et al have been shown that the increased visceral AT mass in FPLD is directly associated with elevated dipeptidyl peptidase-4 levels independent of the presence of diabetes [18]. Additionally, patients with FPLD2 not only develop IR but also show a strongly diminished first-phase insulin secretion [19]. In mice with generalized LD due to loss of function of seipin, treatment with the GLP1-RA liraglutide for 14 days significantly improves lipoatrophic diabetes and MASLD [20]. Due to the rarity of LD, only a few small case series reporting treatment with GLP1-RAs in patients with LD were published so far. In patients with FPLD improvement of glycemic control and IR is depicted not only in the short-term [21]. Sustained normalization of plasma glucose and HbA1c in FPLD under treatment with GLP1-RA over a period of 4 years could be shown as well [19]. Thus, GLP1-RAs are a valuable tool for treatment of FPLD-associated diabetes. Recently, several studies describe GLP1-RAs as reasonable adjunct to insulin in patients with T1D, due to their anti-inflammatory characteristics and their ability to improve immunological control. Moreover, GLP1-RAs increase glucose-dependent insulin synthesis, while decreasing glucagon levels [22]. Another treatment option for metabolic complications in LD is metreleptin, a recombinant leptin analog. The US Food and Drug Administration approved metreleptin in the United States for the treatment of complications of leptin deficiency in patients with generalized lipodystrophy only. In Europe, to the contrary, metreleptin is also approved for treatment of metabolic complications in patients with partial LD from the age of 12 years onward, when standard treatments have failed to achieve adequate metabolic control. Thus, a relevant number of patients with FPLD have no in-label indication for metreleptin. Moreover, the outcome of metreleptin treatment in partial LD is less stringent as compared to generalized LD [23, 24]. Furthermore, one has to consider the remarkable costs of metreleptin treatment as compared to other agents. Hence, metreleptin is no first-line treatment for FPLD2.

Our case underlines the potential benefits of GLP1-RA in LD with reduction of TDD of insulin needed, reduction of hyperglycemia, and amelioration of hyperphagia during treatment, as well as worsening of metabolic and clinical traits upon withdrawal of semaglutide due to rejection of reimbursement.
