Peritoneal Dissemination after Preoperative Percutaneous Transhepatic Portal Vein Embolization in Hilar Cholangiocarcinoma: A Rare but Serious Complication
Authors: Daisuke Itoh, Toshiyuki Unno, Shojiro Hata, Tomoko Takayanagi, Go Kawai, Hirokazu Yamaguchi, Go Shirota, Tomohiko Masumoto, Masaaki Akahane, Osamu Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preoperative percutaneous transhepatic portal vein embolization is used to induce future liver remnant hypertrophy before major hepatectomy in perihilar cholangiocarcinoma. We report a patient who developed peritoneal nodules along the suspected transhepatic puncture route after right-sided percutaneous transhepatic portal vein embolization. Histopathology showed adenocarcinoma consistent with the primary tumor. The spatial distribution and timing were considered more suggestive of tract-related seeding from bile leakage rather than hematogenous spread. This rare but serious complication warrants preventive strategies, including careful route planning, strict avoidance of bile contamination, minimization of puncture attempts, and secure tract closure.

Background

Surgical resection remains the most effective treatment for cholangiocarcinoma [1]. To reduce post-hepatectomy liver failure and expand resectability, preoperative portal vein embolization―including percutaneous transhepatic portal vein embolization (PTPE)―has become standard practice in appropriate candidates [2-4]. At the same time, peri-procedural pathways require further standardization (e.g., timing relative to biliary drainage and infection control), and tract-related complications, although uncommon, should be recognized [3-5]. We report a case highlighting a plausible tract-related mechanism of peritoneal dissemination after PTPE and discuss practical strategies for risk mitigation.

Case Presentation

A 68-year-old woman was referred to our hospital after abnormal liver function tests were detected during a municipal health screening. She was asymptomatic.

Her medical history included uterine fibroids, ovarian cysts, lumbar spinal canal stenosis, and dyslipidemia. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed dilatation of the right intrahepatic bile ducts. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a right-dominant perihilar cholangiocarcinoma with predominant dilatation of the right intrahepatic ducts. The main trunk of the right portal vein was narrowed, raising suspicion of tumor invasion. No suspicious lymphadenopathy, peritoneal nodules, or other distant metastases were identified on imaging (Figure 1). Lymph-node status was assessed with contrast-enhanced CT; FDG PET/CT was not performed preoperatively.

Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT. (A) Focal thickening of the hilar bile duct suggests the primary lesion (white arrow). Dilatation of the B5 duct is present (black arrow). (B) Narrowing of the main trunk of the right portal vein is suspicious for tumor invasion (arrow).

On admission, laboratory tests showed a total bilirubin level of 0.9 mg/dL (no jaundice). Hepatobiliary enzymes were elevated (aspartate aminotransferase 68 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 118 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 1,481 U/L, and gamma-glutamyl transferase 993 U/L), whereas inflammatory markers were within normal limits (C-reactive protein 0.21 mg/dL and white blood cell count 5,620/μL). The patient had no fever or abdominal pain. Thus, there were no laboratory or clinical findings suggestive of cholangitis. The diagnosis was confirmed by cytology obtained during ERCP. An endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) catheter was placed to drain the left hepatic duct. In contrast, no biliary drainage was performed for the right biliary system, because intentional non-drainage was selected to facilitate right-lobe atrophy prior to surgery.

Based on CT and ERCP findings, the patient was preoperatively diagnosed with perihilar cholangiocarcinoma classified as T3N0M0. Because no distant metastases were found, surgical resection was planned. To increase the FLR, preoperative PTPE was performed.

PTPE procedure

The procedure was performed using an ipsilateral approach with absolute ethanol and metallic coils. Under ultrasound guidance, the portal branch to segment 6 (P6)―where bile duct dilatation was minimal―was initially punctured using a 21-gauge needle, and a guidewire was inserted. However, the sheath could not be advanced, and the patient reported pain, prompting a change in the puncture site to segment 5 (P5), where bile duct dilatation was present (Figure 2). A single successful puncture allowed placement of a 5-Fr short sheath, and embolization of the right portal vein branches was performed using a 5.2-Fr balloon occlusion catheter (Selecon MP Catheter II, 9-mm balloon; Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) (Figure 3). Embolization was performed using absolute ethanol; VortX™-35 pushable fibered platinum coils (Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, MA, USA); and MReyeⓇ embolization coils (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA). Complete occlusion of the right portal vein branches was achieved, and the procedure was completed without intraprocedural complications. Upon removal of the sheath, puncture-tract embolization was performed using coils to reduce the risk of leakage along the access path. A total of 12.5 mL of absolute ethanol and seven coils were used for portal vein embolization (VortX™ 3 mm/7 mm ×4, MReyeⓇ embolization coils 8 mm × 8 cm ×3), and puncture-tract embolization was completed with three coils (VortX™ 3 mm/5 mm, 3 mm/7 mm, and 3 mm/6 mm, respectively). The post-procedural course was uneventful. CT after PTPE demonstrated atrophy of the right hepatic lobe with compensatory hypertrophy of the left lobe (Figure 4). Surgery was performed 20 days after PTPE.

Surgical findings

Adhesion between the greater omentum and the abdominal wall at the inferior aspect of the liver was observed. Upon dissection of the adhesion, a small opening was found on the hepatic surface of segment 5, with bile staining around it. Bile staining was also observed on the contralateral abdominal wall. These findings suggested bile leakage from the PTPE puncture tract, resulting in localized adhesion between the liver and abdominal wall. The small bile-stained opening on the hepatic surface of segment 5 was closed with two 3-0 absorbable Z-sutures. Intraoperative frozen section of lymph node #16b1int revealed metastatic adenocarcinoma. Consequently, bile duct resection was not performed, and the surgery was concluded. Adenocarcinoma was also confirmed by cytological analysis of obtained ascitic fluid obtained intraoperatively.

In light of these findings, peritoneal dissemination secondary to bile leakage along the PTPE puncture tract was suspected.

Postoperative course

The patient underwent chemotherapy for approximately 1 year. CT performed 1 year after chemotherapy revealed ascites and peritoneal thickening near the prior PTPE puncture site on the segment 5 liver surface, findings suggestive of peritoneal dissemination, although no discrete peritoneal nodules were identified along the hepatic surface at that site on CT (Figure 5A and B).

Ascites and peritoneal thickening are present near the prior puncture site in segment 5, without a discrete peritoneal nodule along the hepatic surface at that site (arrows). A peritoneal nodule is noted within the paracolic gutter (circle), remote from the hepatic puncture site.

In contrast, peritoneal nodules were noted within the greater omentum and the paracolic gutter, remote from the hepatic puncture site, on non-contrast CT.

Cytological analysis of ascitic fluid confirmed adenocarcinoma, leading to a diagnosis of peritoneal dissemination. Chemotherapy was discontinued, and best supportive care was initiated. The patient died one month after discontinuing chemotherapy.

Discussion

In this single-case report, we describe peritoneal dissemination that became evident after preoperative PTPE in a patient with perihilar cholangiocarcinoma. The temporal and spatial relationship to the puncture tract suggests, but does not prove, a causal link mediated by bile leakage. Alternative explanations, including pre-existing microscopic peritoneal dissemination (as suggested by the positive intraoperative ascitic cytology), cannot be excluded. Therefore, our interpretation is hypothesis-generating and should be read with caution. Nevertheless, this case highlights a potential mechanism by which PTPE-related bile leakage may contribute to peritoneal dissemination, underscoring the importance of careful procedural planning in patients with elevated biliary pressure.

Surgical resection remains the curative treatment for cholangiocarcinoma [1]. In cases requiring extensive liver resection, portal vein embolization is performed to increase FLR and prevent postoperative liver failure [6]. Preoperative portal vein embolization in cholangiocarcinoma has been associated with reduced liver failure and 90-day mortality in large-scale analyses [7].

Preoperative PVE is widely recognized as safe and beneficial in appropriate candidates, yet standardization of peri-procedural pathways (e.g., timing relative to biliary drainage and infection control) remains incomplete, particularly in perihilar disease [2-5].

PTPE requires a percutaneous transhepatic puncture; bile leakage along the tract is a recognized complication [8], and the risk may be greater when intraductal pressure is elevated along the anticipated path. Intentional non-drainage of the lobe to be embolized can facilitate atrophy but may also imply higher upstream biliary pressure, which could increase the likelihood of bile leakage if a duct is inadvertently traversed.

Biologic plausibility of tract- or peritoneal seeding has been described after other percutaneous hepatobiliary interventions, including percutaneous biliary drainage and thermal ablation [9, 10].

An alternative strategy is transileocecal portal vein embolization (TIPE), a surgically assisted approach that avoids a transhepatic puncture by obtaining portal access under laparotomy. TIPE may help circumvent trajectories that would unavoidably traverse tumor or markedly dilated ducts, but it requires general anesthesia and surgical exposure, entails a substantially greater procedural burden, and is not routinely superior to PTPE. Selection should therefore be individualized and multidisciplinary, weighing anatomical feasibility, biliary pressure context, institutional logistics, and patient comorbidity.

In the present case, whether TIPE could have prevented peritoneal dissemination cannot be excluded; however, based on the preoperative clinical context―including the absence of jaundice or cholangitis, left-system drainage via ENBD, and the minimally invasive nature of PTPE within local practice―proceeding with PTPE was a reasonable decision. At the same time, as this case suggests, when intraductal hypertension is suspected―for example, marked ductal dilatation with intentional non-drainage of the embolization side―pre-procedural planning should be particularly cautious. This includes meticulous selection of puncture tract, ensured tract closure, and early consideration of alternative approaches when a tumor-free path is not feasible.

To mitigate potential tract-related leakage or bleeding, puncture-tract closure at sheath removal is advisable; n-butyl cyanoacrylate, absorbable gelatin sponge, or coils are commonly used, and thermal ablation of the tract has also been reported in selected high-risk situations (e.g., anticoagulation, ascites) [11-13]. Notably, in the present case, puncture-tract embolization was performed with coils; however, the possibility that sealing was incomplete cannot be excluded (e.g., residual micro-channels or occult communication with a bile duct). This observation underscores that device choice and technique may influence outcomes. Accordingly, the potential utility of alternative embolic materials (such as n-butyl cyanoacrylate or absorbable gelatin sponge) and/or combination approaches (e.g., gelatin-plus-coil or glue-plus-coil) warrants further evaluation in future studies and practice refinements [11, 13].

When a tumor-free transhepatic path is not feasible, transsplenic PVE can be considered as an alternative access route in selected patients [14].

Ethical considerations and informed consent

This study is a case report, and according to the policy of our institution, ethical approval was not required. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Conclusions

This case suggests that peritoneal dissemination may occur after PTPE, potentially via bile leakage along the puncture tract in the setting of elevated biliary pressure; however, causality cannot be proven from a single case. Interventional radiologists should recognize this potential risk when planning preoperative portal vein embolization for cholangiocarcinoma. When marked ductal dilatation is present along the anticipated percutaneous route and non-drainage of the lobe to be embolized is planned, TIPE can be provided as an option in selected scenarios after multidisciplinary discussion, taking into account its substantially greater procedural burden. Further studies are needed to better define patient selection and to quantify the oncological risk associated with different portal vein embolization approaches.
