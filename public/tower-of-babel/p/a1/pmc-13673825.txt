Vaccination against Lawsonia intracellularis reduced tail-biting related behaviors in a commercial pig herd
Authors: Déborah Temple, Ivan Díaz, Eva Mainau, Rosa Centelles, Damián Escribano, César Llorente, Llorenç Badiella, Stephan von Berg, Marta Jiménez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLawsonia intracellularis is the causative agent of proliferative enteropathy, a common enteric disease in pigs that compromises intestinal integrity and may influence behavior through inflammation and immune activation. Its potential link to tail biting, an abnormal behavior that causes stress, injury and pain, remains unexplored. This study evaluated the effect of vaccination against L. intracellularis on tail-biting related behaviors, tail lesions, active behaviors, and postures in pigs reared under commercial conditions.MethodsThe study compared two groups in a commercial herd naturally and subclinically infected with L. intracellularis: pigs vaccinated against L. intracellularis at 5 weeks of age and unvaccinated controls each comprising 21 pens observed from 11 to 28 weeks of age. Pigs had intact tails. L. intracellularis DNA in feces and specific antibodies in blood were detected by qPCR and ELISA, respectively. Tail-biting related behaviors (rear-end nosing and tail manipulation) were quantified by video analysis (n = 42 pens). Tail lesions were assessed weekly and individually for each pig (n = 516 pigs) using a standardized scoring system. Active behaviors (exploration, feeding, social interactions) and postures were recorded as additional behavioral measures. Haptoglobin (Hp), C-reactive protein (CRP), total esterase activity (TEA) and adenosine deaminase (ADA) were analyzed in blood or saliva. Mortality and average daily gain (ADG) were registered. Ethical welfare management measures (temporary space increase, tail spraying, and enrichment) were applied when recurrent or repeated bleeding from the tail was observed.ResultsL. intracellularis was detected between 15 weeks of age and 24 weeks of age. During this period, vaccinated pigs exhibited lower frequency of tail-biting related behaviors, whereas tail lesion scores did not differ between groups. Unvaccinated control pigs explored enrichment more frequently and were sporadically more active and restless while vaccinated pigs spent more time at the feeder. ADA concentrations were higher in vaccinated pigs, while Hp, CRP, TEA, mortality, and ADG did not differ (p > 0.05).ConclusionsUnder natural subclinical Lawsonia intracellularis infection, vaccinated pigs demonstrated fewer tail-biting related behaviors and lower levels of active behaviors compared with unvaccinated controls. Although tail lesion rates were similar between groups, likely due to effective welfare management measures, the observed behavioral differences suggest that vaccination against L. intracellularis can enhance pig welfare even in the absence of clinical disease. Therefore, controlling subclinical enteric infections through vaccination may serve as an indirect yet practical complementary strategy to reduce tail-biting related behaviors on commercial pig farms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40813-026-00530-4.

Background

Lawsonia intracellularis is an obligate intracellular bacterium that causes proliferative enteropathy, a common enteric disease in growing and finishing pigs. The infection is characterized by hyperplasia of intestinal crypts, mucosal thickening, and inflammation. Clinical outcomes range from diarrhea and reduced feed efficiency to increased morbidity and mortality in severe cases [1, 2]. Beyond its impact on performance, L. intracellularis infection compromises gut integrity, including epithelial structure and barrier function, and may induce discomfort and behavioral changes associated with local inflammation and systemic immune activation [3, 4]. Vaccination against L. intracellularis has proven effective in reducing bacterial shedding, mortality, and clinical signs under both experimental and field conditions [5–9]. However, the potential behavioral and welfare benefits of vaccination, stemming from improved gastrointestinal (GI) health, have not been fully elucidated.

The GI tract communicates with the nervous and immune systems through bidirectional neuroimmune signaling, known as the gut-brain axis. Perturbations in this system, driven by inflammation or microbiota shifts [10], can therefore alter motivation and normal behavior. In pigs, some studies have demonstrated that health challenges can significantly impact behavior and activity patterns; specifically, animals housed under poor sanitary conditions or exposed to suboptimal health conditions may exhibit an increase in damaging behaviors, including ear and tail biting [11–14]. Similarly, pigs suffering from digestive disturbances or discomfort have been linked to restlessness and increased exploration, suggesting that abdominal discomfort may trigger negative affective states [15].

Tail biting remains one of the most persistent welfare challenges in pig production, causing stress, pain, infection risk, and significant economic losses [16, 17]. Tail biting is a multifactorial behavior, with extensive literature identifying a wide range of contributing factors, risk conditions, and mitigation strategies [18]. A widely accepted view is that tail biting represents an abnormal behavior expressed under barren housing conditions, arising from limitations on pigs’ ability to perform natural rooting, foraging, and exploratory behaviors [19]. Many stressful challenges, including environmental and management factors, social factors and individual factors, have been identified as risks for tail biting. While environmental and management risk factors have been widely investigated, the contribution of physiological or health-related drivers remains much less explored. Few studies have demonstrated a significant link between enteric health and tail-biting behavior [11]. Distinct gut microbiota compositions have been identified in biters, victims, and neutral pigs [20, 21], supporting a connection between intestinal health and tail biting behavior.

Given the widespread prevalence of L. intracellularis and its impact on gut health, it is plausible that pigs experiencing enteric inflammation may exhibit altered activity and an increased risk of redirected oral manipulation such as tail biting behavior. Consequently, improving gut integrity through vaccination could help mitigate these welfare issues. However, this potential link between enteric health, vaccination against GI disorders, and pig behavior remains untested.

To complement behavioral observations, biomarkers can be used to capture physiological processes associated with subclinical enteric challenges. Infection with Lawsonia intracellularis is associated with intestinal inflammation and immune activation; accordingly, haptoglobin (Hp) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are commonly used indicators of systemic inflammatory responses in pigs [4, 22–24]. Total esterase activity (TEA) in saliva provides a non-invasive measure of autonomic activation related to stress or pain [25, 26]. In addition, adenosine deaminase (ADA) activity reflects cell-mediated immune activation, which is considered central to protective immune response against Lawsonia intracellularis [27, 28]. Together, these biomarkers provide physiological context for behavioral outcomes by distinguishing between systemic inflammation, stress-related responses, and immune activation.

This study compared vaccinated and unvaccinated pigs on a commercial farm with subclinical Lawsonia intracellularis infection to investigate whether vaccination influences tail-biting related behaviors, active behaviors, postures, and tail lesion severity. Behavioral observations were complemented by biomarkers of inflammation, acute stress, and immune activation.

Materials and methods

Animals, housing, and general management

The study was conducted on a commercial growing-finishing pig farm located in Spain. A total of 516 pigs (Duroc x Berkshire) with intact tails were included. Pigs were individually identified at the farrowing unit within the first 7 days of age using electronic RFID (radio-frequency identification) ear tags (LeeO®, MSD Animal Health). No specific selection criteria were applied, and all animals were managed in accordance with standard farm practices. Males were surgically castrated within the first 7 days of age under analgesia, using meloxicam (Metacam®), a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug. Pigs came from 83 sows and were mixed at weaning at an average age of 26 days (± SD 3.1). All animals spent 5 weeks at the weaning unit before being transported to the finishing building, approximately 30 min away, by a commercial trailer. After allocation to their finishing pens, no mixing was performed during the study period, except when animals were moved to hospital pens if required.

The pigs were housed in a single finishing building comprising 42 study pens, with an average of 12 pigs per pen. Pens were single sex (either males or females), and the entire building was managed under an all-in, all-out production system. Average stocking density was 0.9 m² per pig when entering the finishing unit at 10 weeks of age. Pens were equipped with fully slatted concrete floors (tread area 150 mm width) and solid partitions, which prevented physical and visual contact between animals in neighboring pens. Pigs had ad libitum access to commercial dry feed for growing-finishing pigs and to water provided via nipple drinkers integrated on both sides of the trough (Maximat® Porker).

Management followed the farm’s standard practices. The building was naturally ventilated, and lighting was provided through natural daylight from windows, supplemented by artificial light in the morning (from 07:30) and in the evening (from 18:00). Environmental enrichment was provided in each pen using a rotational system of twelve different toys (e.g. EasyFix Astro 200® rubber material; rod-shaped toys). Enrichment items were exchanged twice weekly (every Mondays and Fridays) throughout the finishing period to maintain novelty and interest. Four additional pens were used as “play pens” when tail-biting outbreaks were detected, and four other pens, located in an adjacent smaller building, were used as hospital pens. Play pens and hospital pens were not included in the study. During the trial, 20 pigs were treated with amoxicillin (Amoxal®) and 9 with benzylpenicillin (Depocillin®), administered at the individual level (13 pigs in the control group and 16 in the vaccinated group). Ambient temperature and relative humidity were monitored throughout the entire period (Additional File 1, AF Fig. 1 and AF Fig. 2).

Experimental design

The finishing cycle under study extended from July to November 2024. A total of 516 pigs in the 42 pens were included in the study. At 5 weeks of age in the weaning unit, pens were randomly allocated to either the vaccination or control groups. Consequently, all pigs within a given pen were either intradermally vaccinated against Lawsonia intracellularis (0.2 mL Porcilis® Lawsonia ID, MSD Animal Health) or left unvaccinated. At entry to the finishing unit, pigs - previously identified with RFID ear tags at the farrowing unit - were individually weighed and allocated to pens according to treatment group, sex, and body weight. Each finishing pen contained pigs from a single treatment group (vaccinated or unvaccinated) and a single sex, with efforts made to ensure homogeneous weight distribution. The allocation process aimed to balance treatment groups across the facility and avoid spatial clustering (Fig. 1). All other management practices were identical across treatment groups. Vaccination was administered at the weaning unit by farm staff and a trained technician and was therefore not blinded at the time of treatment. Treatment allocation was subsequently recorded in the LeeO® management software for each individually identified pig and associated with a color code. All personnel involved in behavioral observations, tail lesion scoring, laboratory analyses, and statistical analyses were blinded to treatment allocation. Treatments were revealed only after completion of the statistical analyses.

Monitoring of Lawsonia intracellularis

L. intracellularis had been previously detected in other batches at the facility. Fecal bacterial load was monitored to identify the timing and approximate magnitude of infection rather than to serve as a primary outcome. Measurements were obtained at pen level on a relatively small sample size (10 pens per treatment group) and at a limited number of discrete time points (11, 15, 20, 24, and 28 weeks of age). Pooled fresh fecal samples were stored cooled until analysis. Bacterial load was measured by qPCR (Bactoreal® Kit Lawsonia intracellularis, Ingenetix GmbH, Vienna, Austria). Subclinical ileitis was defined by the absence of ileitis-associated clinical signs and/or mortality, as well as by the detection of the bacterium by qPCR [9, 29]. A low bacterial fecal load was considered when the quantification of genomic copies by RT-PCR (Bactoreal®, Kit Lawsonia intracellularis, Ingenetix GmbH, Vienna, Austria) [30] did not exceed 4 Log10 GE copies/µL.

To estimate the timing of the specific antibody response, longitudinal serum samples were obtained from two pigs per pen (84 pigs in total), randomly selected within each pen based on RFID identification following allocation to the finishing unit. These pigs were individually monitored throughout the study through repeated blood and saliva sampling. Blood collection was performed on the exact same dates as fecal sampling. Samples were centrifuged and stored at -20 °C until analysis. Serum antibodies were analyzed using a commercial blocking ELISA (SVANOVIR® L. intracellularis/Ileitis-Ab, INDICAL BIOSCIENCE, Sweden) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibody levels were expressed as percent inhibition (PI) of the negative control, where PI = 100 - (ODsample / ODnegative control) x 100, and OD represents the optical density measured by the ELISA reader.

Fecal consistency was assessed by visual inspection using a four-point ordinal scale (0 normal; 1 mild diarrhea; 2 moderate diarrhea; 3 severe diarrhea, watery feces and/or with incorporation of blood) to identify signs consistent with diarrhea at pen level [31].

Behavioral observations through video recordings

Weekly assessments of pig behavior were performed from 11 weeks of age (7 days after entry at the finishing unit) until 28 weeks of age (one week before the end of the finishing period) during daytime by video recording from the 42 pens included in the study. Video cameras (dome Gamma 1080p ULTRA Sony© 2.13 Mpx Exmor IMX307) were installed, and recordings were stored in four video recorders. Observations were recorded at pen level. Behavioral observations included tail-directed behaviors as the primary outcome. In addition, active behaviors (e.g., exploration) and postures were recorded as additional behavioral measures. Both scan sampling and focal sampling were used. Each one of these observational methods was done by a single observer to avoid inter-observer bias. Observers were blinded to the treatment.

Tail lesion scoring – at the farm

Tail lesions were assessed individually at arrival at the finishing building (10 weeks old) and once a week, every 7 days, until the end of the finishing period. Tail lesions were scored from inside the pen by one trained observer (Observer 3) who remained consistent throughout the study. At first, pigs were called from outside the pen and approached the evaluator voluntarily. The evaluator then entered the pen, identified each pig via RFID ear tag, and manually inspected the tail by gently holding it to allow full visualization. When necessary, pigs were briefly distracted (e.g., with a small amount of straw) to facilitate accurate assessment. Tails contaminated with fresh liquid manure were cleaned with a cloth prior to evaluation. In cases of more adherent dirt, complete cleaning was not always possible, which could limit the detection of minor lesions. Therefore, minor superficial scratches and minor scabs were not classified as tail lesions in this study. All 516 pigs from the 42 study pens were assessed for tail lesions using the scoring system presented in Table 4. This scale has been adapted from [34] and [35], combining tail damage and wound freshness scoring. To ensure intra-observer repeatability with multi-colored pig tails (white, brown, black) and tails dirty with feces, minor scratches and minor scabs on the tail were not considered. Healed, shortened tails were classified as “no clear tail lesion” (category 0), as the assessment focused on active or recent tail lesions.

Tail lesion scoring system

Daily observations of severe tail damage were controlled by the stockperson. An aluminium-based spray (Aluspray®) was applied to tails with visible fresh wounds as soon as these were detected. This product forms a protective barrier over the wound, helping to reduce contamination, limit exposure to flies, and supports healing. It may also help to reduce bleeding and decrease the attractiveness of fresh wounds to other pigs. In addition to the spray treatment, supplementary enrichment materials were provided in the pen (e.g., Easy Fix® luna). When recurrent or repeated bleeding from the tail was observed, the affected pig was separated overnight for recovery and reintroduced to its original pen in the morning. If two or more pigs in a pen presented fresh wounds with bleeding on more than two consecutive days, the affected pen was opened and all pigs were given access to a « play pen » equipped with toys and rubbing walls for 12 h, after which they were returned to their original pen. These measures allowed full control of tail biting outbreaks at pen level.

Determination of adenosine deaminase, acute phase proteins and total esterase activity concentrations

Of the 20 pens selected for qPCR of fecal samples and of the 84 randomly selected pigs used for serological detection of specific antibodies, a subset of 40 pigs (two pigs per pen) was chosen for the determination of a panel of biomarkers including haptoglobin (Hp), C-reactive protein (CRP), total esterase activity (TEA), and adenosine deaminase (ADA). Repeated measurements were taken monthly on the same selected pigs. Saliva and blood samples were collected on the same day, with saliva sampling performed prior to blood collection. Saliva was obtained by allowing each pig to actively grab and chew a Salivette® attached to a metal rod for at least 30 s. The sponges were then placed into plastic tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The recovered saliva was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis of ADA and TEA. ADA was analyzed with a commercially available spectrophotometric assay (Adenosine Deaminase assay kit, Diazyme Laboratories, Poway, CA, USA), previously validated for porcine saliva [27]. Salivary TEA was measured by a method based on the hydrolysis of the substrate 4-NA (Sigma-Aldrich Co, St Louis, Mo, USA) by salivary esterases, as previously reported in pigs [25]. Blood samples for CRP and Hp determination were collected from the jugular vein, centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 min, and the resulting serum was stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Porcine C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and Haptoglobin (Hp) concentrations were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Olympus AU2700, Olympus Diagnostica GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) previously validated in pigs [36, 37].

Performance and mortality

Body weight was measured individually with MS Pigscale for finishing pigs (MS Schippers®), synchronized with LeeO system, at entry (10 weeks old) and prior to slaughter (29 weeks old). Mortality during the growing-finishing period was recorded by the farmer as an additional outcome in the present study.

Statistical analysis

The pen was the statistical unit for all behavioral data (42 pens) and tail lesions data (42 pens). Behavioral recordings from scan sampling were presented as the prevalence of observation periods in which the behavior in question was performed. The percentage of active pigs was expressed as a proportion of the total number of pigs observed. Categories of active behaviors were expressed as a proportion of the number of active pigs. Behavioral recordings from focal sampling were presented as the number of events per 10 pigs within 30 min. Tail lesions were expressed as the number of affected pigs in each tail lesion category relative to the total number of pigs assessed per pen. The pig was the statistical unit for specific antibodies (84 pigs), inflammatory biomarkers (40 pigs), average daily gain (ADG), and mortality (number of deaths relative to the total number of pigs). ADG and mortality were calculated for each treatment group, considering all animals present in the barn (516 pigs). ADG was calculated by weighing pigs on the day of transferring to the finishing unit and the day of transferring to the slaughterhouse and dividing by the number of days spent at the finishing unit. The specific antibody variable was log-transformed.

Continuous (antibodies, biomarkers, ADG), count (behavioral variables from focal sampling), and binomial response variables (behavioral variables from scan sampling, tail lesions) were analyzed using the appropriate generalized linear mixed model. All models included pen or animal as random factors depending on the availability of individual or group measurements and considered subject-specific random intercepts and slopes. These models also included treatment group, sex, age, and their interactions as fixed effects.

All models were validated by examining the residual plots, goodness-of-fit-indices and presence of overdispersion in count data.

Statistical decisions were made based on a significance level of 0.05. No corrections for multiplicity of contrasts were applied.

Data management and analysis were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS System, SAS Institute Inc., NC, USA, 2025).

Monitoring of Lawsonia intracellularis

qPCR (Table 6) and serological data (Fig. 2) were used to monitor exposure and to define the timing of infection rather than as a primary study outcome.

All faecal samples were qPCR-negative for L. intracellularis at the beginning of the monitoring period (11 weeks of age). Shedding was detected at 15 weeks of age in two pens from each group, with low bacterial loads ranging from 0.5 to 3.0 log10 copies/µL. Of the five sampling time points, the highest bacterial load was detected at 20 weeks of age, at which point the proportion of positive pens reached 80% (8/10) in the unvaccinated control group and 50% (5/10) in the vaccinated group (Table 6). At 24 weeks of age, all animals had cleared the infection and remained negative until the end of the study (28 weeks of age).

No evidence of clinical signs compatible with severe diarrhea was detected in either the vaccinated or the unvaccinated groups during the study.

Nosing of the tail region/rear-end (referred to as “nosing tail region”) was significantly higher in unvaccinated pigs between 16 weeks of age and 25 weeks of age. Postures showed very limited differences between groups over time. During the period of Lawsonia intracellularis detection (15–20 weeks of age), unvaccinated pigs displayed a higher proportion of “standing walking” at a specific time point (week 17: unvaccinated 24% ± 8.5% vs. vaccinated 19% ± 6.6%; OR = 1.3, 95% CI [1.1–1.6]). Overall, postural measures were largely comparable between groups, with only a transient increase in standing and walking observed in unvaccinated pigs.

ADG and mortality

Average daily gain (ADG) and mortality were similar between treatment groups (Table 5). In the vaccinated group, 11 losses were attributed to respiratory disorders, 3 to severe anemia, and 6 pigs that appeared clinically healthy were found dead. In the unvaccinated group, 11 losses were attributed to respiratory disorders, 1 to severe anemia, 2 to meningitis, and 6 pigs that appeared clinically healthy were found dead without a definitive cause.

Discussion

Pigs vaccinated against Lawsonia intracellularis displayed a lower occurrence of nosing of the tail region and tail manipulation (including tail-in-mouth, TIM). However, tail lesion prevalence did not differ between treatment groups. These behavioral reductions were most evident during the period of L. intracellularis excretion, suggesting that vaccination may influence early stages of tail-directed behavior, potentially by modulating health status and associated behavioral responses. Nosing the tail region and tail-in-mouth (TIM) are considered early manifestations of tail biting, which may precede damaging tail biting but do not necessarily result in lesions [33, 38, 39]. Although TIM does not necessarily result in immediate lesions, it reflects increased oral manipulation directed towards pen mates and may indicate underlying behavioral or physiological drivers such as discomfort, stress, or exploratory motivation. The observed reduction in nosing of the tail region and tail manipulation therefore suggests that vaccination may influence early stages of tail biting behavior.

The more pronounced increase in nosing of the tail region and TIM in unvaccinated pigs suggest a link between gastrointestinal status and behavioral expression. On the performer side, pigs experiencing suboptimal health may show increased exploratory and redirected oral behavior, particularly towards the tail region [40, 41]. Infection with L. intracellularis is known to alter gut microbiota and intestinal function [42, 43], and associations between tail biting and fecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles [44] suggest a potential role of microbial metabolites in modulating behavior via the gut–brain axis. On the receiver side, infection-related changes may also modify cues emitted by affected pigs, potentially increasing investigatory attention from pen mates towards the rear region. Given pigs’ highly developed olfactory abilities, they are likely to perceive subtle health-related odor differences in pen mates [33]. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, such changes in odor or other chemical signals may contribute to the attractiveness of affected individuals and facilitate the initiation or escalation of tail-directed interactions. Notably, the same individual may act as both performer and receiver over time, reflecting the dynamic nature of these behaviors.

In addition to tail-directed behaviors, which were the primary outcomes, general active behaviors (exploration, feeding, and social interactions) and postures were also recorded as additional behavioral measures. During the period of L. intracellularis detection, unvaccinated control pigs interacted more intensively with enrichment materials at two out of six time points during L. intracellularis detection, as well as just before and after this period. Increased use of enrichment materials has been observed in pigs during early phases of behavioral unrest preceding tail biting outbreaks [40, 45]. In the present study, this may reflect increased exploratory motivation associated with subclinical gut discomfort. Differences in active behaviors were more sporadic: at certain time points within the period of infection, unvaccinated control pigs were more active and explored the pen more, while feeding/drinking and social interactions were slightly reduced. Minor postural differences were also observed with unvaccinated pigs spending more time walking at one time point. Such minor transient changes are coherent with fluctuating discomfort associated with subclinical diseases. Similar patterns have been reported in weaners under enteric or inflammatory challenges, including Salmonella infection or post-weaning diarrhea [46, 15]. These sporadic increases in activity support the hypothesis that gut discomfort may enhance exploratory and oral behaviors, particularly during early stages of infection in unvaccinated pigs. However, the more persistent increase in tail-biting related behaviors, especially nosing of the tail region, cannot be fully explained by changes in general activity, as these were sporadic and short-lived, suggesting that additional mechanisms may be involved. Pigs with higher L. intracellularis burden may attract increased investigatory attention from pen mates towards the perianal region, contributing to the development of tail-directed behaviors.

Despite increases in tail-biting related behaviors and exploration in unvaccinated pigs, tail lesion prevalence remained similar between groups. This apparent inconsistency is biologically and methodologically coherent. Tail lesions are the end-stage expression of a multifactorial process where not all tail biting events lead to a tail lesion [18, 19, 47]. Tail lesion scoring was based on direct visual inspection with manual handling of the tail; however, in some cases, soiling or limited visibility due to multicolored tails may have reduced the detection of minor lesions. Superficial scratches and small scabs were therefore not systematically recorded, which may have reduced sensitivity to subtle differences between groups. Furthermore, the welfare management protocol implemented to prevent escalation likely contributed to limiting lesion development. When early signs of tail lesions were detected, corrective interventions were promptly applied, including increased space allowance for several hours, application of aluminium-based spray to bleeding tails, and provision of supplementary enrichment material. While these interventions are consistent with good welfare practices, they may have reduced lesion incidence and severity across both treatment groups. This context emphasizes that tail lesion frequency should not be interpreted as a direct proxy of behavioral motivation when outbreak control measures are in place. It also points out that good management is a key measure to prevent tail biting damage.

Acute phase proteins, total esterase activity, and adenosine deaminase were assessed as complementary measures to behavioral outcomes. These variables provided physiological context and helped distinguish between systemic inflammation, stress- or pain-related responses, and immune activation. Acute phase proteins and total esterase activity did not differ between groups. This is consistent with the subclinical nature of the infection, where intestinal lesions may not have been sufficient to trigger a marked systemic inflammatory response. In pigs, acute phase proteins typically respond to severe or generalized inflammation, whereas mild or localized enteric disturbances often induce only limited systemic changes [22–24]. In addition, the short-lived kinetics of Hp and CRP may have obscured potential differences at the selected sampling time points [22]. TEA, a marker of autonomic activation and acute stress, also showed no differences between groups. This biomarker is more responsive to acute stressors such as pain or clinical conditions such lameness or prolapse [26] and may be less sensitive to low-grade or localized inflammation. These results suggest comparable levels of detectable systemic stress or pain between groups. In contrast, vaccinated pigs showed a transient increase in adenosine deaminase (ADA) activity at 15 weeks of age. ADA is involved in immune regulation and has been associated with T-cell activation and adaptive immune responses [48, 49]. In pigs, increased ADA levels have also been reported during inflammatory conditions such as gastrointestinal infections [50].

In the context of Lawsonia intracellularis infection, where protection relies primarily on cell-mediated immunity, this transient increase is consistent with vaccine-induced immune activation rather than disease-related inflammation [2, 51]. The absence of parallel changes in acute phase proteins supports the interpretation that ADA reflects a limited and transient immunological response rather than a generalized inflammatory state. Taken together the profile of these biomarkers suggests that vaccination primarily enhanced immune readiness (reflected by ADA) rather than suppressing a systemic inflammatory response (Hp, CRP) or acute stress linked to painful conditions (esterase). The reduction in tail-biting related behaviors in vaccinated pigs therefore appear to be linked to a better-controlled and localized enteric infection and a more balanced immune status, rather than to differences in overt systemic inflammation or physiological acute stress response.

No significant differences were observed between groups in mortality or average daily gain (ADG). This result aligns with previous studies showing variable production responses to L. intracellularis vaccination depending on disease pressure, management, and diet [2]. The lack of a performance difference reinforces that the primary benefit observed in this trial where L.intracellularis infection was subclinical with relatively low bacterial shedding was mainly behavioral rather than purely productive. Reductions in discomfort and tail-biting related behaviors have important welfare implications, even in the absence of measurable performance gains.

Conclusions

Under natural subclinical Lawsonia intracellularis infection, vaccinated pigs demonstrated fewer tail-biting related behaviors and lower levels of active behaviors compared with unvaccinated controls. Although tail lesion rates were similar between groups, likely due to effective welfare management measures, the observed behavioral differences suggest that vaccination against L. intracellularis can enhance pig welfare even in the absence of clinical disease. Therefore, controlling subclinical enteric infections through vaccination may serve as an indirect yet practical complementary strategy to reduce tail-biting behavior on commercial pig farms.
