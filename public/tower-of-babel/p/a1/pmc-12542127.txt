Transnasopharyngeal positive pressure ventilation during balloon dilation of severe subglottic stenosis in a low-weight infant: a case report
Authors: Shaochao Wang, Chao Wang, Wen Zhao, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcquired subglottic stenosis (ASGS) is a significant cause of pediatric airway obstruction and presents various anesthetic challenges due to the complexities of altered airway anatomy. While balloon dilation serves as a first-line minimally invasive intervention, shared airway management between surgeons and anesthesiologists requires meticulous coordination. This case report demonstrates the successful implementation of nasopharyngeal airway-assisted positive pressure ventilation during balloon dilation in a critically ill infant with grade III ASGS. This approach proposes a new ventilation strategy for complex airway procedures.Case Presentation A 3.2 kg, 3-month-old male infant presented with progressive respiratory failure secondary to Cotton-Myer grade III ASGS. Following multidisciplinary consensus, emergency balloon dilation was performed under general anesthesia with tracheostomy contingency planning. Initial ventilation attempts using a laryngeal mask proved inadequate, causing surgical field obstruction. Transitioning to a nasopharyngeal airway connected to the anesthesia machine in pressure-controlled ventilation mode successfully maintained adequate gas exchange (SpO₂ 95 ~ 100%).The procedure was completed successfully without hemodynamic compromise or airway reflexes, ultimately avoiding tracheostomy. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with the patient returning to pediatric respiratory intensive care unit for continued respiratory support.Conclusion Using a nasopharyngeal airway assisted positive pressure ventilation can effectively maintain oxygenation and surgical access during pediatric ASGS interventions, proposing a novel ventilation strategy for complex airway procedures. While demonstrating procedural feasibility in this critical scenario, larger-scale prospective studies are required to validate its efficacy and safety profile across varying stenosis severities.Keywords: Balloon dilation, Nasopharyngeal airway, Pediatric airway management, Acquired subglottic stenosis, Case report

Background

Acquired subglottic stenosis (ASGS), an important etiology of pediatric airway obstruction, primarily results from prolonged endotracheal intubation or iatrogenic trauma. Clinically significant stenosis (≥ 50% luminal narrowing) typically manifests with biphasic stridor, progressive respiratory distress, and potential progression to acute respiratory failure [1, 2]. While open surgical interventions (e.g., laryngotracheal reconstruction) remain the historical gold standard, these procedures carry inherent risks of surgical morbidity and extended convalescence periods [3–5]. Balloon dilation has become a minimally invasive alternative that uses controlled radial force to temporarily widen the airway through periodic expansion of the stricture. However, this technique poses specific anesthetic challenges, as it requires maintaining proper ventilation while ensuring clear access for the surgery. This approach necessitates either preserved spontaneous respiration or secured airway maintenance, imposing significant demands on perioperative ventilation management [6, 7].

Conventional airway management techniques, such as laryngeal masks or endotracheal tubes, can hinder endoscopic visualization and limit instrument maneuverability. In cases of severe stenosis, face mask ventilation often fails to ensure effective gas exchange [8]. The nasopharyngeal airway positive pressure ventilation (NPA-PPV) system provides a non-invasive way to deliver gas to the pharynx. This system has two main benefits: (1) It offers continuous positive airway pressure to improve oxygen levels, and (2) It creates enough space for endoscopic work by separating the ventilation and surgical paths. While there is growing evidence that supports the use of NPA-PPV in challenging pediatric airway situations [9], its application in ASGS balloon dilation procedures is not well-documented in current literature, and there are no established standard protocols for this situation [10].

Case presentation

The male infant, delivered as the second twin (third parity) via cesarean section at 32+1 weeks gestational age, presented with extreme prematurity (birth weight 750 g) requiring immediate postnatal intubation. Current weight 3.2 kg with multiple comorbidities: right middle bronchial stenosis, severe laryngomalacia, bilateral vocal cord paralysis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (stage II), recurrent pneumonia, secundum atrial septal defect (3.8 mm shunt), and penoscrotal hypospadias. Progressive respiratory deterioration necessitated continuous positive airway pressure(CPAP) support (FiO₂ 0.6) maintaining SpO₂ 90%, with arterial blood gas demonstrating compensated respiratory acidosis: pH 7.40, PaCO₂ 67 mmHg, PaO₂ 60 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 42.5 mmol/L, SBE + 16.7 mmol/L, Lac 0.4 mmol/L. Diagnostic bronchoscopy confirmed Cotton-Myer grade III ASGS (2 mm lumen diameter) with superimposed dynamic laryngeal collapse. Emergency balloon dilation was planned to relieve obstruction, with tracheostomy scheduled if unsuccessful. The patient had multiple prior hospitalizations involving pulmonary surfactant administration, caffeine therapy, anti-inflammatory/anti-infective regimens, blood transfusions, and neurotrophic support. Surgical history included laryngotracheal sliding plasty, multiple bronchoscopies, and anesthesia exposures. No documented allergies or genetic/familial disorders were identified.

Anesthetic and airway management considerations: First, chronic preoperative dependence on CPAP contraindicated spontaneous breathing preservation under anesthesia. Second, the presence of subglottic stenosis precluded endotracheal intubation, necessitating the use of supraglottic airway devices as alternatives (including face mask, laryngeal mask airway, nasopharyngeal airway, and high-frequency jet ventilation). Third, preparations for emergency surgical tracheostomy were mandated.

Preoperative laboratory checks (WBC 6.28 × 109/L, HGB 88 g/L, PLT 333 × 109/L, NT-proBNP 645.7 pg/ml) and imaging tests were completed. Preoperative fasting adhered to 4 h for formula and 2 h for clear fluids. The infant was transported to the operating room with CPAP support (FiO₂ 0.6), exhibiting significant respiratory distress with audible stridor and classic triad of retractions (suprasternal, supraclavicular, and intercostal retractions during inspiration). Continuous multiparameter monitoring via Mindray BeneVision N15 system tracked: hemodynamic parameters: heart rate (HR), non-invasive blood pressure (NBP), respiratory indices: Respiratory rate (RR), SpO₂, and core temperature. Baseline vital signs upon arrival: NBP 78/45 mmHg, HR 169 bpm, RR 44 bpm, SpO₂ 95%, and temperature 36.3℃. Automated 5-minute interval data recording protocols were initiated.

Preoxygenation was performed via existing CPAP circuit with 100% FiO₂ for 3 min prior to induction. Gradual intravenous administration of penehyclidine hydrochloride (0.005 mg/kg), propofol (3 mg/kg), remifentanil (2 µg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg) achieved adequate anesthesia depth. Maintenance was accomplished with propofol (10 mg∙kg−1∙h−1) and remifentanil (0.6 µg∙kg−1∙min−1). Initial facemask ventilation proved feasible; however, placement of a size 1.5 standard laryngeal mask airway (LMA, Fig. 1A②) resulted in suboptimal ventilation. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy revealed significant glottic compression (Fig. 1B), leading to inadequate gas exchange and obstructed surgical access. Downgrading to a size 1.0 LMA restored ventilation but precluded bronchoscopic passage through the lumen, hindering procedural execution. To resolve this conflict, an alternative ventilation strategy was implemented: replacement of the LMA with a size 3.5 (ID) nasopharyngeal airway (NPA, Fig. 1A①) inserted via the left nostril to supraglottic position (Fig. 1D). Pressure-controlled ventilation (PCV) parameters were set as follows: inspiratory pressure 18 ~ 20 cmH₂O, respiratory rate 30 ~ 40 bpm, I:E ratio 1:1.5, zero PEEP, and FiO₂ 0.8 ~ 1.0 (Fig. 2D). Simultaneous bronchoscopic access was achieved through the right nostril. During balloon inflation (30–60 s cycles), ventilation was paused and resumed immediately post-deflation. Peak inspiratory pressure was capped at 20 cmH₂O. Manual sealing of the contralateral naris was performed (Fig. 1C).Critical technical considerations included: (1) Nasal seal maintenance: Manual occlusion of the right naris during bronchoscope withdrawal to prevent gas leakage; (2) Flow compensation: Increased fresh gas flow (≥ 5 L/min) during instrumentation phases to sustain tidal volumes.

Fiberoptic bronchoscopy revealed a ring of scarring in the subglottic area along with vascular growth, leading to severe narrowing of the airway with a luminal diameter of approximately 2 mm (Fig. 2A). Balloon dilation was performed via the right nasal passage under direct bronchoscopic visualization.During balloon inflation cycles: Ventilation was temporarily paused (≤ 60 s); Continuous oxygen insufflation (8 L/min) maintained via NPA; NGT suction activated every 30 s to prevent gastric distension.Under direct visualization, serial balloon dilations were performed using a 6.0 mm outer-diameter balloon catheter (Fig. 2B), administered in three cycles (30–60 s each) adjusted based on real-time oxygenation. Post-dilation evaluation demonstrated significant luminal improvement (Fig. 2C), restoring near-physiologic airway dimensions without active bleeding. The 10-minute procedure maintained stable intraoperative hemodynamics and effective ventilation (Fig. 2D), with SpO₂ 95 ~ 100%. Postprocedural arterial blood gas analysis (FiO₂ 0.6) revealed: pH 7.47, PaCO₂ 51 mmHg, PaO₂ 204 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 37.1 mmol/L, SBE + 13.4 mmol/L, lac 0.4 mmol/L. No perioperative complications occurred, including laryngospasm, bronchospasm, hypoventilation, apnea, or respiratory failure. Following return of spontaneous breathing, the nasopharyngeal airway was removed, and the patient was transferred to the respiratory intensive care unit (RICU) for postoperative monitoring.

Discussion and conclusion

While nasopharyngeal PPV is documented in pediatric airway management, its application as primary ventilation during balloon dilation for Cotton-Myer grade III ASGS in a 3.2 kg infant required specific adaptations.Key adaptations include:①Ventilation-surgical isolation via dual nasal access; ② Infant-specific pressure limits (≤ 20 cmH₂O)③ Structured apnea-protocol for dilation cycles.

This report details a critically ill preterm infant (corrected gestational age: 3 months 12 days) with congenital tracheobronchomalacia and multisystem anomalies, who developed ASGS following endotracheal intubation for respiratory failure. Notably, the patient had undergone laryngotracheal sliding plasty 7 weeks prior to the current intervention and presented with postoperative restenosis localized to the proximal tracheal anastomosis (glottis-cricoid cartilage complex level). Managing perioperative care for complex airway cases requires a balance among three key priorities: ensuring airway control safety, maintaining oxygenation stability, and achieving surgical feasibility. This case is unique for three main reasons: (1) Anatomic-physiological factors: The preterm airway is narrower (~ 2 mm), more fragile, and has increased mucosal permeability. The oxygen consumption rate is higher at 6 mL/kg/min compared to 3 mL/kg/min in adults, along with a reduced functional residual capacity, which raises the risks of hypoxemia and hypoventilation [11]. (2) Pathological basis: Bronchopulmonary dysplasia decreases oxygen reserve capacity [12, 13]. (3) Recent airway reconstruction: Changes in anatomy after surgery lead to 17 ~ 23% increase in difficult airway scenarios [14, 15], significantly heightening the risks of “cannot intubate, cannot ventilate” and “cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate”.

Compared to conventional open surgical approaches, balloon dilation achieves tissue remodeling at stenotic segments through cyclic mechanical expansion, offering distinct advantages in minimally invasive intervention and accelerated recovery [16]. However, this technique necessitates specialized anesthetic ventilation strategies that preserve either spontaneous respiration or secured airway patency. Critical adaptations included:1) Supraglottic pressure control at 18–20 cmH₂O;2) Cyclical ventilation-pause protocol during dilation (≤ 60 s inflation);3) Pressure-limited PPV (≤ 20 cmH₂O) preventing barotrauma. During the procedure, intraoperative monitoring revealed stable physiological parameters, with SpO₂ levels 95 ~ 100%, PetCO₂ levels 35 ~ 45 mmHg, and hemodynamic variability staying within 20% of baseline values.

The successful implementation of this case hinged on three pivotal elements: First, the precise pharmacological regimen, which combined cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg) with a propofol-remifentanil target-controlled infusion, effectively suppressed airway reflexes and alleviated respiratory muscle fatigue. Cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg) guaranteed zero airway movement during dilation phases, while remifentanil’s rapid offset enabled prompt emergence - essential in this grade III stenosis. Second, the innovative NPA-PPV application achieved dual optimization of gas exchange and endoscopic workspace. Finally, the multidisciplinary collaboration model facilitates real-time regulation of anesthesia depth, ventilation parameters, and surgical procedures. Notably, the precise surface anesthesia of the narrow mucosal segment (1% lidocaine 1 mL) reduced cardiovascular stress responses during the balloon dilation process, which significantly enhancing intraoperative hemodynamic stability [17].

Although no major complications occurred in this case, systematic risk management remains imperative.Integrated safety measures:1)Preemptive HFNC (flow rate 2–5 L∙kg-1∙min-1) extending apnea tolerance [18, 19];2)Cerebral oximetry monitoring [20];3)Emergency cricothyrotomy/ECMO protocol [21, 22].Emerging advancements in pediatric bronchoscopy, particularly artificial intelligence-assisted decision systems, warrant focused attention [23]. Experimental convolutional neural network based algorithms for automated bronchial stenosis detection have demonstrated promising results in preclinical validation [24, 25], potentially revolutionizing preoperative assessment and personalized ventilation strategy formulation for airway stenosis patients.

Standardized anesthetic protocols for pediatric subglottic stenosis balloon dilation remain undefined, with core priorities encompassing oxygenation maintenance, complication prevention, and surgical field optimization. This study demonstrates that NPA-PPV under general anesthesia offers an innovative solution, characterized by: (1) effective airway support, (2) controlled anesthetic depth, and (3) superior surgical exposure. However, clinical use of NPA-PPV has three key limitations: (1) Aspiration risk is associated with supraglottic airway devices [26]; therefore, strict preoperative fasting and ultrasound assessment of gastric emptying are advised. (2) Gastric insufflation occurs more frequently at airway pressures above 20 cmH₂O, requiring routine gastric tube decompression [27]. (3) Anatomical limitations, such as nasopharyngeal integrity, restrict its use in patients with craniofacial anomalies like choanal atresia or severe cleft palate. As a single-center case report, these findings require validation through multicenter randomized controlled trials to establish generalizability.

NPA-PPV under general anesthesia provides effective ventilation-surgical balance in ASGS dilation for preterm infants ≤ 4 kg, contingent on precision drug titration and structured crisis protocols.
