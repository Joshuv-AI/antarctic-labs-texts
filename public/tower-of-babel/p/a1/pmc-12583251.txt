Identification of biomarkers related to the progression of cutaneous melanoma and construction of survival prognosis model
Authors: Kana Chen, Wei Ye, Longjun Chi, Shujie Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnder certain circumstances, nevi may undergo malignant transformation into skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), which is a highly aggressive and deadly malignant tumor originating from melanocytes. Early identification of important genes in the development of nevus into melanoma, timely treatment and blocking of the disease process can effectively reduce the occurrence of melanoma.MethodsSix datasets were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus with the keywords “nevus and melanoma”. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in nevu vs. normal and melanoma vs. nevus were identified. WGCNA was used to identify disease status-related modules. Mfuzz clustering algorithm was used to select DEGs with different expression modes. PPI network was used for hub gene identification, followed by prognostic model construction, and immune microenvironment analysis. Finally, the expression of crucial genes in the process of SKCM was validated by RT-qPCR.ResultsTotally, 269 overlapped DEGs were filtered in two comparison groups. After comparing with module genes obtained from WGCNA, 159 overlapped genes were screened, which were clustered into 3 expression trend clusters. Through PPI network analysis, 26 hub genes were selected and then 5 signature genes (CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10) were selected for prognostic model construction. The samples were divided into two risk groups. Thirteen immune cell types were found to have significant difference distribution between risk groups. The consistent rate of the identified crucial hub genes between RT-qPCR and bioinformatics analyses was 100%, which showed the relatively high reliability of our bioinformatic analyses.ConclusionCCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10 were identified to be candidate markers during melanoma progression. The proposed prognostic model had good predictive precision, which may be used for clinical prediction of melanoma development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03643-4.

Introduction

Nevi are benign proliferations of melanocytes in the skin and are universally present in human populations [1]. In most cases, they are harmless [2]. However, under certain circumstances, nevi may undergo malignant transformation into skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), which is a highly aggressive and deadly malignant tumor originating from melanocytes [3]. SKCM, a highly aggressive form of skin cancer, has witnessed a steadily increasing incidence globally in recent decades. According to the latest epidemiological data, the annual incidence rate of SKCM has been rising by approximately 3–5% in many developed countries [4]. Despite significant progress in surgical resection, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, the prognosis of advanced-stage patients remains dismal, with a 5 - year survival rate of less than 20% in metastatic cases [5]. The heterogeneity of melanoma, both at the inter-tumor and intra-tumor levels, poses a major challenge for precise treatment and accurate prognosis prediction.

Currently, traditional prognostic factors such as tumor thickness (Breslow depth), ulceration, mitotic rate, and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging play a crucial role in clinical decision-making [6]. However, these factors often fail to fully capture the complex biological processes underlying melanoma progression. The development of personalized medicine strategies urgently requires the identification of novel biomarkers that can more accurately reflect the biological behavior of tumors, including their invasive potential, metastatic ability, and response to therapy [7]. According to research progress, about 33% of melanomas directly progress from benign melanocytic nevi [8]. In recent years, with the development of molecular biology techniques, such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, remarkable progress has been made in the molecular genetic mechanism of melanoma [9]. It has been demonstrated that the relationship between melanocytic nevi and melanoma is influenced by a variety of factors, including genetic mutations, signaling pathway disorders, DNA damage repair defects, and immune dysregulation [8, 10–12]. For instance, BRAFV600E gene mutations and the negative feedback imbalance of the MAPK pathway play significant roles in the evolution from melanocytic nevi to melanoma [13]. In-depth study of the key genes of nevus progression into melanoma not only helps to reveal the occurrence and development mechanism of melanoma, but also provides scientific basis for the development of new diagnostic markers and treatment strategies.

Notably, most prior studies have focused on single-gene biomarkers (e.g., BRAFV600E) or isolated signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK) for melanoma prognosis, which may lack comprehensiveness in reflecting the complex tumor microenvironment [14, 15]. Additionally, existing prognostic models often show limited predictive accuracy for long-term survival (e.g., 5-year overall survival), with area under the curve (AUC) values typically below 0.7 [16]. Furthermore, the crosstalk between gene expression profiles and immune cell infiltration during melanoma progression remains poorly characterized. Therefore, in this study, multiple expression profile data of normal skin, nevus, and melanoma were used to screen important genes related to melanoma progression. After that, combined with melanoma expression data in TCGA database, a survival prognostic model was constructed based on the expression levels of important related genes. This work will provide a more comprehensive understanding of melanoma progression.

Methods

Data sources

In NCBI GEO database, all publicly uploaded expression profile data were searched with the keywords “nevus and melanoma”. The selection criteria of dataset were: (1) transcriptome expression profile data; (2) melanoma, pigmented nevus, normal control sample type; (3) human expression spectrum. Three datasets (GSE46517, GSE114445 and GSE3189) were selected (Table 1). These data were used to screen important genes associated with melanoma disease progression.

The information of the obtained three datasets

After downloading the three datasets, the genes were annotated according to the detection platform. Then, the three datasets were combined by the same gene name, followed by batch effect removal using R4.3.1 sva package version 3.38.0 [17], The combined expression level data were used in the following analysis.

Meanwhile, GSE112219 (3 nevus and 3 normal samples, platform: GPL20795HiSeq X Ten) and GSE183115 [18] (5 melanoma and 4 nevus samples, platform: GPL18990[Xcel]) were used as validation of disease progression-related gene expression levels.

In addition, SKCM gene expression data were downloaded from the UCSC Xena database, which contains 473 SKCM tumor samples. Among these samples, 459 with clinical survival prognosis were used as the training data set for the construction of the prognostic signature.

Finally, SKCM-related gene expression detection data GSE65904 [19] was downloaded, which contained 214 samples, including 210 with survival prognosis information. The detection platform was Illumina HumanHT-12 V4.0 expression beadchip. This dataset was used as a validation dataset for the construction of prognostic signature.

Screening of differentially expressed genes at different disease stages

In the combined dataset, the samples were divided into nevus (n = 39) vs. normal (n = 24), and melanoma (n = 92) vs. nevus (n = 39). Using R4.3.1 limma 3.34.7 [20], the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |Log2 fold change (FC)| >0.5 were selected as the screening thresholds. Then, we compared the DEGs sets detected in nevus vs. normal and melanoma vs. nevus. The overlapped genes of the two comparison groups were considered as the DEGs associated with the disease progress.

Screening of modules related to disease status based on WGCNA

Based on all the genes detected in the combined data set, the WGCNA version 1.61 package [21] was used to screen the modules associated with the disease state, and the association between each module and the disease state was calculated. According to the correlation between the module and the disease stage of the sample, the genes contained in the disease-related module were retained as disease-related candidate genes. These genes were then submitted for function analyses, and then compared with the DEGs, the overlapped part was retained. The steps of WGCNA algorithm included defining the adjacency function and module partition, and the screening thresholds of module partition were: the module set containing at least 200 genes, and cutHeight = 0.995.

Analysis of DEGs with different expression modes using mfuzz clustering algorithm

Based on the expression levels of DEGs shared by the three comparison groups, we used Mfuzz 2.42.0 [22] in R4.3.1 to analyze the expression pattern change trend of the candidate DEGs. Then the genes in each cluster with different trends were subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis based on DAVID 6.8 [23]. The FDR value less than 0.05 was selected as the threshold of enrichment significance.

A PPI network construction

The interaction between protein products of genes selected by Mfuzz clustering was searched in the STRING 11.0 [24] database, and Cytoscape 3.9.0 [25] was used to visualize the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. Then cytoHubba 0.1 [26], a module recognition plug-in in Cytoscape 3.9.0, was used to perform hub gene search based on four topology analysis algorithms, including MCC, MNC, EPC and DEGREE. The hub genes screened by each of the four algorithms were compared and the overlapping part was retained as important hub genes related to disease. In addition, psych package 1.7.8 [27] in R4.3.1 was used to conduct principal components analysis (PCA) based on the expression levels of hub genes.

Construction of a prognostic risk prediction model

Based on the SKCM expression profile data in the TCGA dataset, the expression levels of hub genes screened in the PPI network were extracted. Then, combined with the clinical survival and prognosis information of the samples (Table S1), the univariate Cox regression analysis in survival 2.41-1 [28] was conducted to screen the genes significantly related to disease prognosis, with p-value less than 0.05 as the threshold.

Then, for genes with significant prognostic correlation, the LASSO algorithm in lars 1.2 [29] in R4.3.1 was used for regression analysis with Log (λ) = 0.2484, and the optimized disease progression related hub genes were screened. For the optimized hub genes, their expression levels were extracted from GSE112219 and GSE183115 datasets. Then, we investigated whether the expression trend of their expression levels in different disease stages was consistent with the change trend of the combined dataset. Afterwards, the Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve method in survival 2.41-1 [28] was used to evaluate the correlation between high and low hub gene expression groups and clinical survival outcomes.

Then, according to the LASSO prognostic coefficients of each element in the optimized gene combination and the expression levels of target genes in the dataset, the following risk score (RS) prognostic model was constructed, and the RS formula was shown as follows: RS = e∑(expr×coef)/e∑(mean×coef).

Evaluation of RS prognostic risk prediction model

In TCGA training set and GSE65904 verification dataset, RS values were calculated respectively, and then the samples were divided into high and low risk groups according to the median value of RS values. The association between the grouping of high and low risk group and the actual survival prognosis was evaluated using KM curve method in survival 2.41-1 [28]. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve method in pROC 1.12.1 [30] was used to evaluate the survival state decision prediction efficiency of the model.

Construction of a nomogram model

In the TCGA training dataset, combined with the clinical information of the samples, the univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to screen for independent prognostic clinical factors, and log-rank p values less than 0.05 were selected as the threshold.

In order to further study the prognostic independence between independent prognostic clinical factors and RS factors, we combined the independent prognostic factors with the risk information identified by the prognostic prediction model to construct a nomogram model using the rms package 5.1-2 [31]. Then the C-index coefficient of the nomogram model was calculated by R4.3.1 survcomp 1.34.0 [32].

Assessment and comparison of immune distribution

CIBERSORT [33] was used to evaluate the proportion of immune cell types in TCGA tumor samples, and then the differences in the distribution of different immune cells in different risk groups were compared. Finally, the correlation between immune cells or immune checkpoints and model genes was analyzed.

Experimental validation of expression levels of the crucial genes

The expression of the identified five optimized hub genes (CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4, and SOX10) in SKCM process were verified using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The fresh-frozen SKCM, benign skin nevi, and normal skin tissues (n = 3 for each group) were obtained from Department of Plastic and Cosmetic Surgery, Ningbo NO.2 Hospital (Zhejiang, China). All providers of the tissues confirmed that the tissue specimens employed in the current study were gathered following Institutional Review Board-approved protocols from the relevant hospitals and in line with bioethical principles. This research was in accordance with the national legislation and the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ningbo NO.2 Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The obtained tissues samples were used for the isolation of total RNA by the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and then the isolated RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TAKARA, Japan). After that, RT-qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, USA) on an ABI Viia7 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The relative expression of CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4, and SOX10 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to internal GAPDH. The sequences of all primers were presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed mean ± standard deviation (SD), and GraphPad Prism v9.0 was employed for statistical analyses. For comparison of the three groups, one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was adopted. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Screening of modules related to disease status

We selected the power value when the square value of the correlation coefficient reached 0.9 for the first time, that was, power = 5 (Fig. 2A). Then, the coefficient of divergence between nodes was calculated, and the system clustering tree was obtained. The minimum number of genes in each module was set to 100, and the pruning height was set to cutHeight = 0.995. As shown in Figs. 2 and 5 modules were obtained, including brown, blue, turquoise, yellow and grey modules. After that, the correlation between modules and disease states was calculated (Fig. 2C). The modules with the correlation coefficient higher than (plus or minus) 0.3 with the three states were retained, that were brown and turquoise modules. Then, the correlation between gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) of brown and turquoise modules was respectively calculated. As shown in Fig. 2D, GS and MM of the two modules had significant positive correlation. Therefore, brown and turquoise modules were selected as disease-related modules.

The genes involved in the brown (966 genes) and turquoise (1665 genes) modules were then sent to GO and KEGG analyses. Functional analyses of the genes involved in the brown modules were significantly enriched in “cell division”, “signal transduction”, “DNA repair” and “nervous system development” of biological process (BP) of GO terms; and “cytosol”, “nucleus”, and “nucleoplasm” of cellular component (CC) of GO terms; and “protein binding”, “ATP binding”, and “DNA binding” of molecular function (MF) of GO terms, as well as KEGG pathways of “cell cycle”, “cellular senescence”, “apoptosis”, “p53 signaling pathway”, and “folate transport and metabolism” (Figure S2A). Finally, the genes involved in the brown (966 genes) and turquoise (1665 genes) modules were compared with the identified DEGs, 71 and 88 overlapped genes were found in the comparison groups of DEGs vs. WGCNA-brown and DEGs vs. WGCNA-turquoise, as well as a total of 159 overlapped genes were employed for subsequent analyses (Fig. 2E).

Analysis of DEGs with different expression modes

Mfuzz package was used to analyze the aforementioned 159 overlapped disease-related genes according to the change trend of expression patterns, and 3 expression trend clusters were obtained (Fig. 3A). Cluster 1, 2 and 3 contained 63, 34 and 62 genes, respectively. The genes contained in each cluster were performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Genes in cluster 1 were involved in 9 pathways, such as AMPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and melanogenesis; genes in cluster 2 were involved in 6 pathways, such as PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and FoxO signaling pathway; Genes in cluster 3 were enriched in 6 pathways, such as retinol metabolism, and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, metabolic pathways were associated with all three clusters.

Efficacy evaluation of the proposed RS prognostic model

In TCGA training dataset and GSE65904 validation dataset, the RS values were calculated, and the samples in the training and validation datasets were respectively divided into high risk and low risk groups. Then, the correlation between the risk scores and clinicopathological features was calculated, and we observed that risk scores were significantly associated with age, gender, pathologic_T, and tumor recurrence (Table 3). After that the KM curves showed that the samples in low risk group had better prognosis compared with the high risk group (Fig. 6A). The RS value distribution and survival time distribution of each group are shown in Fig. 6B. The ROC curves based on the proposed RS prognostic model are shown in Fig. 6C. The AUC values for 1-, 3- and 5-year in the training and validation datasets were all > 0.7. There was a significant correlation between the different risk groups predicted based on the RS prognostic model and the actual prognosis in both the TCGA and GSE65904 dataset.

Establishment of a nomogram survival model

Through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, 5 independent prognostic factors were selected, including pathologic N and T, tumor recurrence, tumor metastasis and RS model. The KM curves related to pathologic N and T, tumor recurrence, tumor metastasis are shown in Figure S3. It was found the patients with pathologic N3, or pathologic T4, or without metastasis, or with recurrence had lower overall survival time.

Then a nomogram survival model was established with these independent prognostic factors (Fig. 7A). The survival period of the samples was predicted based on the “Total points” axis in the first row. The 1-, 3- and 5-year survival rates predicted by the nomogram model and the actual survival rates was consistent (Fig. 7B). In addition, ROC curve analysis for each prognostic factor and comprehensive factor were performed, and AUC values of pathologic N, pathologic T, metastasis, recurrence, RS model and combined were 0.604, 0.665, 0.664, 0.679, 0.710, and 0.836, respectively (Fig. 7C).

RT-qPCR validation of the identified crucial hub genes

Finally, the expression of CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4, and SOX10 in SKCM process were determined by RT-qPCR. It was found that compared with the normal tissues, the expression of CCND2 was significantly up-regulated in the nevi and SKCM tissues (P < 0.05); as well as the CCND2 expression was evidently down-regulated in the SKCM tissues than that in the nevi (P < 0.01, Fig. 9A). For NFASC, its expression was significantly down-regulated in the nevi and SKCM in comparison with the normal tissues (P < 0.01), and its expression in the SKCM was evidently lower than that in the nevi (P < 0.01, Fig. 9B). For EHPA4 and ENPP2, their expression was not significantly changed between the normal and SKCM (P > 0.05); while was significantly increased in the nevi compared with the normal and SKCM tissues (P < 0.01, Fig. 99C, D). Furthermore, the expression of SOX10 was significantly up-regulated in the nevi relative to the normal (P < 0.01), and was further enhanced in the SKCM tissues (P < 0.01, Fig. 9E). The expression trend of these hub genes in clinical samples measured by RT-qPCR was consistent with that analyzed by bioinformatics, which indicated the consistent rate between RT-qPCR and bioinformatics analyses was 100%. The findings revealed the relatively high reliability of our bioinformatic analyses.

Discussion

Nevus is composed of pigmented nevus cells, which are mostly benign. Once malignant transformation occurs, it will become SKCM, which spreads quickly mainly through the lymphatic pathway and develops rapidly [3]. SKCM is one of the malignant tumors with the highest mortality among skin tumors, which is often found at an advanced stage [5]. Therefore, early identification of important genes associated with the progression of pigmentation nevus into melanoma is necessary.

In order to identify genes that influence melanoma progression, we conducted the expression patterns trend analysis for the candidate genes. Three expression trend clusters were obtained. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that metabolic pathway was involved in all three clusters, suggesting that this pathway may be closely related to the melanoma progression. Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of malignant transformation across all cancer types [34]. In melanoma, tumor cells adapt to their increased bioenergy and biosynthesis needs through metabolic reprogramming so as to maintain redox balance. Such metabolic alterations contribute to cancer progression [35]. Genes in cluster 1 were enriched in 9 pathways, such as AMPK signaling pathway. AMPK is a key serine/threonine protein kinase that provides energy homeostasis by detecting the AMP/ATP ratio in cells [36]. Once activated, AMPK can phosphorylate key enzymes and transcription factors, thereby regulating the metabolism and expression of various genes [37]. It has been reported that AMPK may play the role of friend or foe in cancer, depending on the context [38]. Genes in cluster 2 were involved in 6 pathways, such as PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. The PI3K-Akt pathway is a highly conserved central regulator of growth, proliferation, motility and metabolism, which has been found to be activated in uveal melanoma [39]. Genes in cluster 3 were enriched in Wnt signaling pathway. Wnt signaling plays a key role in the development of melanocytes and the pathogenesis of melanoma, which not only guide neural crest cells to differentiate into terminal melanocytes, but also driving the transformation and proliferation of melanoma cells through transcriptional activation of downstream targets [40, 41]. Taken together, signaling pathways of metabolic reprogramming, AMPK, PI3K-Akt, and Wnt enriched by the candidate genes may be associated with the progression of melanoma, but how these genes regulate these signaling pathways needs to be further explored.

In this study, through WGCNA, PPI network, and machine learning methods, 5 signature genes (CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10) were finally selected for prognostic model construction. ENPP2 was enriched in the metabolic pathways, and CCND2 was enriched in PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, suggesting that they may be involved in the progression of melanoma via these pathways. EPHA4 is a member of the erythropoietin-producing human hepatocellular receptors (EPHs) family of receptor tyrosine kinases, has been shown to play oncogenic and tumor-suppressive roles in many human tumors [42, 43]. EPHA4 effectively suppresses the epithelial-tomesenchymal transition and metastatic capabilities of melanoma cells by interfering with the activation of ERK [44]. A recent study demonstrated that up-regulation of EPHA4 was correlated with a shortened overall survival along with the presence of metastasis in uveal melanomas [45]. In accordance with the findings above, in our study, EPHA4 was up-regulated in nevus and down-regulated in melanoma samples. High expression of EPHA4 also indicated poor prognosis. SOX10 is a key transcription factor that regulate pigment cell formation during embryonic development, and is expressed at all stages of melanocyte differentiation [46, 47]. SOX10 is required not only for the formation but also for the maintenance of pre-melanoma lesions. Silencing of SOX10 effectively blocks tumorigenesis by human melanoma cells in vivo [48]. In our study, SOX10 was up-regulated during the disease progression, which indicated poor prognosis.

In addition, unlike previous studies relying on single-gene markers (e.g., SOX10 with AUC = 0.575 in primary cutaneous melanoma [49]) or BRAF mutation status [50], our multi-gene prognostic model demonstrated robust predictive accuracy for long-term survival, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUC values all exceeding 0.7, indicating that this model had high predictive precision [51, 52]. This superiority may stem from the integration of diverse biological pathways, including metabolism (ENPP2), cell adhesion (NFASC), and immune regulation (CCND2), which collectively reflect the complexity of melanoma progression. Notably, a recent meta-analysis showed that multi-gene models have 1.8-fold higher prognostic efficiency than single-gene markers in solid tumors [52], consistent with our findings. Furthermore, established serum biomarkers like S100B and LDH have been widely used in melanoma prognosis, but their predictive accuracy is constrained by biological variability [53]. S100B, a calcium-binding protein, exhibits sensitivity to tumor burden but lacks specificity for molecular subtypes [54]. LDH reflects systemic metabolic stress rather than tumor-intrinsic characteristics [55]. In contrast, our gene signature captures tumor-intrinsic molecular alterations, such as metabolic reprogramming (ENPP2) and immune evasion (CCND2), which may explain its superior predictive power. Moreover, the GEP model by Cirenajwis et al. primarily focuses on metastatic melanoma gene expression [19], whereas our model integrates multi-stage gene dynamics from nevus to melanoma. Additionally, our model incorporates immune cell infiltration profiles (e.g., M2 macrophages and CD8 + T cells), which were not considered in the original GEP model. A recent review highlighted that immune-related gene signatures have 1.5-2.0 fold higher prognostic efficiency than non-immune models in solid tumors [56], supporting the value of our immune-metabolic integration strategy. Taken together, it can be inferred that the 5 genes (CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10) can be used as candidate markers during the progression of melanoma; as well as the proposed RS prognostic model may be applied to predict melanoma development.

Traditional clinical parameters for melanoma prognosis, such as Breslow thickness and ulceration status, have been widely used in clinical practice [57, 58]. However, these indicators primarily reflect macroscopic tumor characteristics and lack molecular-level information. For instance, a meta-analysis showed that Breslow thickness has an average AUC of 0.65–0.70 for 5-year survival prediction [59]. Ulceration in melanoma is a significant indicator of a poorer prognosis. It is an independent factor that worsens survival rates, regardless of other factors like tumor thickness. Ulceration signifies a breakdown in the skin covering the melanoma and is associated with a higher risk of metastasis (spreading) and recurrence after treatment [60, 61]. Nevertheless, their predictive accuracy is constrained by the heterogeneity of melanoma biology. Our nomogram model, by integrating molecular signatures (RS) with traditional clinical factors (pathologic T/N, recurrence, metastasis, recurrence), achieved a significantly higher AUC of 0.836. This improvement is attributed to the complementary nature of molecular and clinical data: the RS model captures gene expression dynamics related to metabolic reprogramming (e.g., ENPP2) and immune evasion (e.g., CCND2), while clinical indicators reflect tumor morphology and progression stage. A recent study in breast cancer has shown that such integrative models can enhance prognostic accuracy by 15–20% compared to single indicators [62], which is consistent with our findings. The superior predictive performance of our model over single indicator (17.7%-38.4% AUC increase) suggests its potential to refine risk stratification for melanoma patients. Additionally, the nomogram’s C-index of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.75–0.81) surpasses that of a recent melanoma prognostic model relying solely on clinical parameters (C-index = 0.72) [63], which warranted to be further verified its clinical utility in the future.

Melanoma is considered an immunogenic cancer [11]. The immune system may play a role in controlling the growth of nevi and preventing progression to melanoma [8]. While metabolic reprogramming in melanoma has been primarily linked to glycolysis [34], our study uncovered dynamic activation of the AMPK and PI3K-Akt pathways during nevus-to-melanoma transition, providing new insights into the metabolic timing of malignant transformation. Additionally, unlike previous studies focusing on CD8 + T cells alone [64], we systematically analyzed 13 immune cell types (such as macrophage, T cell, and NK cell. In melanoma, tumor-associated macrophages), and found that high-risk patients exhibit increased M2 macrophage infiltration and reduced activated NK cells, which correlates with poor prognosis. The identified correlation between CCND2 and CD8 + T cells further bridges gene expression with immune evasion mechanisms, a dimension rarely explored in prior prognostic models. In melanoma, tumor-associated macrophages have been shown to contribute to the progression of melanoma through various mechanisms [65]. Molecules released by melanoma can recruit macrophages to the site of the melanoma. Changes in macrophage population patterns were observed during the progression of malignant melanoma [66]. M1 is a pro-inflammatory macrophage that exhibits a defensive response to tumors. M2 is an anti-inflammatory macrophage, which is beneficial to tumors. Recently, there has been evidence that in the tumor microenvironment, macrophages exhibit a predispositions to adopt a protoma phenotype (M2) at primary and/or metastatic sites in vivo through cell-to-cell interactions. In this study, the higher fraction of M2 macrophage and lower fraction of M1 macrophage in the high risk group compared with the low risk groups were consistent with a poor prognosis in the high risk group. However, this discrepancy may be explained by spatiotemporal heterogeneity in macrophage polarization within the tumor microenvironment. A recent study reported that CD8 + T cells and NK cells could down-regulate pyroptosis expression percentage, and act as a protective prognostic predictor clinically [67]. In the present study, CD8 + T cell and activated NK cell had lower fraction in the high risk group, indicating poor prognosis, which was in accordance with the findings above. As for the correlation between signature genes and immune cells needs to be further investigated.

Methodologically, our study stands out by integrating six GEO datasets with TCGA data, providing multi-cohort validation that surpasses the single-dataset limitations of most prior studies [19]. The combination of WGCNA and Mfuzz clustering allowed us to identify three distinct gene expression trajectories, revealing stage-specific metabolic pathway activation that was overlooked in conventional network analysis [68]. This integrative approach enhanced the robustness of our findings, as demonstrated by the consistent expression trends of CCND2 and SOX10 across training and validation datasets.

However, there are some limitations in our research. Firstly, our study relies on bulk tissue analysis, which limits the resolution of spatial heterogeneity. Future research should employ single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to map macrophage polarization patterns across distinct tumor regions, thereby validating spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Secondly, the small sample size may introduce potential biases, and a larger sample size should be employed in the future. Thirdly, CIBERSORT estimates immune cell proportions based on transcriptomic data, which may not fully reflect the actual scenario. Additional validation methods, such as immunohistochemistry (IHC) or multiplex flow cytometry, should be performed to confirm the infiltration of key immune cells (e.g., M2 macrophages, CD8 + T cells). Additionally, in vitro and in vivo experiments with gene knockdown/overexpression required to be further performed to elucidate the specific mechanisms of CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10 in melanoma progression, as well as the underlying involvement of the metabolic reprogramming, AMPK, PI3K-Akt, and Wnt pathways in tumor biology of melanoma.

Conclusion

In conclusion, five genes (CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10) were identified to be candidate markers during the progression of melanoma. A prognostic model was constructed based on the 5 signature genes, which had good predictive precision, and may be used for clinical prediction of melanoma development. Furthermore, our results demonstrated the critical roles of immune cells, such as T cells, macrophages, and NK cell in melanoma progression. Our study may contribute to reveal the development mechanism of melanoma, and provide scientific basis for the development of new treatment strategies with CCND2, NFASC, ENPP2, EPHA4 and SOX10 as novel potential targets.
