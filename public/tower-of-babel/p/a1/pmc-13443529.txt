Deciphering spatiotemporal rainfall dynamics and tele-connections of West Bengal using conventional and innovative approaches: evidence of rising hydro-climatic stress
Authors: Koushik Banerjee, K. G. Mandal, S. K. Purbey, Bappa Das, Shreya Nivesh, Vikas Paradkar, Ravi Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precipitation is primarily administered by the monsoon wind system across the Indian subcontinent. The inherent rainfall variability poses significant challenges in making appropriate water resources management decisions under rainfed agricultural conditions. This complexity is further exaggerated in the Indian state of West Bengal (WB) due to its exclusive geographical setting and varied climatic conditions. To decipher the long-term rainfall variability at seasonal and annual scale of WB, the spatiotemporal rainfall trends (1990–2019) have been analyzed across regional scale using conventional and innovative approaches. it reveals that the Gangetic WB (GWB) received 47–55% less rainfall 73–78% negative percent departure of rainfall (PDR) during pre-monsoon, monsoon and annually respectively as compared to ACZs of Sub-Himalayan WB (SHWB). Conversely, GWB received 5–12% more rainfall with 52% positive PDR during winter and post-monsoon seasons as compared to SHWB. Seasonal and annual rainfall in WB exhibited steady positive anomalies until 2000–2004, subsequently, there was a shift to negative anomalies and a steady decline. This variation was reaffirmed with inter-annual rainfall variability, which exhibited most consistent rainfall pattern for Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone which covers a major part of SHWB. In contrast, major variation, with deficit to large deficit rainfall was observed in GWB consisting of Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Costal Saline Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone. Seasonal/ annual rainfall trends were analysed and compared using Mann–Kendall (MK), modified MK test (mMK), Sen’s slope estimator, and innovative trend analysis (ITA) methods. The study detected significantly (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) declining rainfall trends in 78–95% of the districts in WB by ITA method, with sharpest reduction observed in Costal Saline Zone (Medinipur-East), Vindhyan Alluvial Zone (Dinajpur-Dakshin), Northern Hill Zone (Darjeeling) and Vindhyan Alluvial Zone (Dinajpur-Dakshin) during pre-monsoon, monsoon, post-monsoon and annual period respectively. In contrast, Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone showed a consistent positive rainfall trend with the district Jalpaiguri, which exhibited the sharpest increasing rainfall trends in pre-monsoon, monsoon and annual time scale. Based on the comparison of different trend analysis methods, ITA has been found the most sensitive in identifying more hidden time series rainfall trends than MK, mMK or Sen’s slope estimator. Spatio-temporal clustering of districts using ITA derived rainfall slope revealed 5 major clusters. Among those, cluster 1 (covering Vindhyan Alluvial Zone, and parts of Northern Hill Zone and Coastal Saline Zone) showed the sharpest decreasing slope followed by cluster 5 which covers largest number of districts (11) in ACZ Gangetic Alluvial Zone and part of Undulating Red and Laterite Zone. Each subsequent decade, beginning from 2000, showed decreasing pattern in monsoon rainfall with more negative rainfall anomaly which emphasizes climatic vulnerability i.e., rising hydro-climatic stress in the state. Additionally, we provided perceptions into spatio-temporal rainfall variations based on recent theories, along with potential impacts and adaptation strategies. ENSO indices, particularly Niño 3.4, ONI, MEI, and DMI, significantly influenced the seasonal rainfall variability in West Bengal, but the overall declining trend indicates that even La Niña events might not fully offset El Niño–induced drought risks. The findings of the current study will be useful in strategic planning and mitigating climate change impacts and help in understanding changes in rainfall climatology which may assist the policymakers and beneficiaries in optimizing the use of hydrological resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52010-y.

Introduction

In the twenty-first century, climate change is documented as one of the foremost global concerns due to its detrimental impact on food security, livelihoods, health and energy security1. Large scale modifications in the atmospheric processes were observed over the last century, due to rising concentration of greenhouse gases (GHGs) and land use and land cover changes (LULC) which consequence in rearrangement of the climatic pattern worldwide with emergence of new climates at the end of the twenty-first century2,3. Notably, changing patterns of rainfall as a consequence of climate change is a concern. It affects climatological, hydrological and agricultural applications, which may have widespread significance on water resources, environmental and territorial ecosystems, agriculture and food security4. Unforeseen change in seasonal and annual rainfall disturbs the spatiotemporal distribution of runoff, soil moisture content, groundwater storage, stream flow, and water quality5. Above all, rainfall is a crucial factor, which governs agricultural production. Hence, it is prudent to decipher rainfall variability and trends in connection with current climate change pattern by employing the statistical techniques.

Climate change has already influenced the global temperature pattern, and it is foreseeable that the precipitation has also experienced several changes. While, total precipitation is decreasing in some regions, precipitation intensity is observed to be increasing3. Global studies1,6 reported the mixed rainfall trends with spatial variability, whereas, regional analysis revealed both the increasing and decreasing rainfall trends across different parts of the world5. Hitherto, several studies have endeavored to find the spatio-temporal changes in precipitation in India, however, with noticeable warming, the constancy of the Indian monsoon precipitation is still an enigma. Given the country’s vastly diverse geo-climatic conditions, regional variability in rainfall is anticipated. Previous studies have already explored significant long-term rainfall changes on a regional basis7–13, a decreasing pattern of total annual precipitation particularly in central India8. Conversely, a decline in irregular rainfall events has been reported in Varanasi, India7; significant reduction in general precipitation over tropical regions has been observed since 19703, and decreasing trends in monsoon, post-monsoon and annual rainfall in Bihar11,13. Conversely, a significant upward trend in annual rainfall was observed in Vaippar Basin, Tamil Nadu, India14. Such changes in rainfall adversely affected regional crop production systems as well as food security, biodiversity, livelihood, and human health.

Though wide-scale climate analyses are necessary for ascertaining variations, micro-level observations are more operative for designing and decision-making or conclusive. In India, West Bengal (WB) is an Eastern Indian state and is lying under the lower Indo Gangetic Plains (IGP). The state is one of the climate sensitive states due to its hydro-meteorological uncertainties, dense rural population and high poverty level. Agriculturally, rice is the major crop cultivated during the kharif season (i.e., monsoon or rainy), whereas wheat is primarily grown in rabi and the amount of rainfall received during different seasons have a major bearing on the crop production13. The agrarian economy largely depends on the normal distribution of annual rainfall, more than 80% of which occurs during monsoon months (June to September). Spatiotemporal anomalies in southwest monsoon (SWM) rainfall pose serious threats to the agricultural production system in the country level. Aggarwal et al.31 reported a 23% deficit of SWM rainfall in 2010, which adversely affected kharif production. Another report warned that crop production would reduce by 31.3% with the reduction of rainfall by 2030 in India due to anomalies in south-west monsoon32. Agriculture adds 18.33% of WB’s Gross State Domestic Product (GSDP) and a prime livelihood source to 57% of its total population (Economic Review 2017–18, Govt. of WB33). The state government of WB recognizes that the changing climatic conditions are a major barrier to the unsustainable growth rate in the GDP. Transforming agriculture and its allied sectors into climate-resilient and vibrant production systems is the need of the hour. Additionally, alterations in rainfall patterns would change the pattern of stream flows and demands (particularly in the agricultural sector), the spatial and temporal distribution of runoff, and groundwater reserves. Hence, accurate information of long-term seasonal rainfall trends is necessary for sustainable use of water resources in agricultural production systems, which profoundly impact the Indian economy. Still, there are no such comprehensive studies conducted to understand and compare seasonal rainfall trends using MK, mMK, SS, and ITA, and clustering different districts based on the rainfall trends of WB. Considering the above observation and research gap, attempts have been made in this study: i to analyze distribution pattern of seasonal rainfall of 23 districts of WB using time series rainfall data (1990–2019; ii to analyze, quantify and compare seasonal and annual rainfall trends using MK, mMK, SS and ITA methods; iii) to identify optimum number of clusters and grouping different districts of WB located in different agroclmatic zones based on the seasonal and annual rainfall trends using hierarchical cluster analysis method.

Materials and methods

Study area and data collection

West Bengal (21°25′–27°13′ N and 85°50′–89°50′ E, 182 MSL), covering total geographical area of 88,752 sq. km, has an inherent climatic variability principally owing to a vast physiographic diversity extending from the eastern Himalaya in the north to the coastal regions in the south, amidst regions of plateau highlands and Ganges delta prevailing in between. Based on climatic conditions, soil characteristics, and terrain, the state is divided into two meteorological subdivisions by IMD i.e., Gangetic WB (GWB) (south to the river Ganges) and, Sub-Himalayan WB (SHWB) (north to the river Ganges). These two subdivisions cover six agro-climatic zones (ACZ) of WB as shown in Fig. 1a,b. A clear north–south orographic gradient controls the state’s rainfall circulation, with the sub-Himalayan Dooars and Darjeeling hills getting remarkably high, sometimes excessive, rainfall (up to 5,000 mm in areas like Buxa Duars) due to the orographic lifting of moist, southerly winds. On the other hand, the western plateau (Purulia, Bankura) receives lower, semi-arid conditions, frequently facing droughts24. Rainfall is the principal source of water in WB, and the major portion is received in monsoon months (70–80%) and bestow approximately 90% to the release of the rivers. The state gets rainfall due to thunderstorms or Nor’wester in the pre-monsoon season, however, tropical cyclonic activities in pre-monsoon and post-monsoon months are prevalent in the coastline24,25. The state’s climate is vastly inclined by Bay of Bengal synoptic disturbances, predominantly in the pre-monsoon (April–May) and post-monsoon (October-December) seasons, which cause heavy cyclonic storms, resulting in saline water intrusion in coastal areas. During the pre-monsoon months, the western drier districts of WB suffer from heat waves, and occasionally the maximum temperature exceeds beyond 45 °C26. Comparatively drier situations prevail during the winter months. Due to nearness to the Himalayas and occasional snowfall, the northernmost districts rarely experience extreme temperature. Around 72% of the state’s total population lives in rural areas and 96% of the total land holdings belong to small and marginal farmers with average holding size of 0.77 ha34. This specifies a high level of dependence on agriculture, reinforced by rainfall, and the rainfall extremities stress heavily on agricultural as well as other economic activities. Hence, erraticism of seasonal rainfall is highly sensitive on socio-economic growth of this region. Therefore, in the present study, we investigated district wise seasonal and annual rainfall trends of WB from 1990 to 2019. The daily gridded rainfall data generated by India Meteorological Department (IMD) at 0.25° latitude × 0.25° longitude spatial resolution was directly extracted according to the district shapefile of WB35. This high-resolution gridded dataset was developed using daily precipitation data records collected from a network of 6,955 rain gauge stations over the country by Pai et al.35. Hence the quality of the data used in this study is maintained, detail information on data generation can be obtained from Pai et al.35. The zonal-statistical average values of the bound districts region were processed for further analysis. The IMD classified four meteorological seasons in India: winter season (January–February), pre-monsoon season (March–May), monsoon season (June–September), and post-monsoon season (October–December). Distribution pattern of precipitation

The daily rainfall data of each district was accumulated to obtain total seasonal, annual and climatic rainfall. Basic statistical parameters, i.e., mean rainfall, coefficient of variation (CV), coefficient of skewness (CS), and kurtosis (CK) were determined for each season. The CV, representing data dispersion around the mean, is a useful statistic for comparing the degree of rainfall variability between time series rainfall data. Hare36 classified rainfall variability as low (CV < 20%), medium (20% < CV < 30%) and high (CV > 30%). Seasonal rainfall deviation was calculated by using the percent departure of seasonal rainfall from its long-term rainfall (mean value of 30 years) climatological mean value of 30 years of rainfall which is called percent departure of rainfall (PDR). This calculation has been made for the district level for each season. A positive PDR indicates excess (above normal) rainfall, while, and deficient (below-normal) rainfall is indicated by a negative PDR for a season. Seasonal PDR was calculated for each district using,1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{P}\mathrm{D}\mathrm{R}= (\frac{{R}_{s}-{R}_{avg}}{{R}_{avg}})*100$$\end{document}where Rs is seasonal rainfall of a particular district, and Ravg is the average climatological long-period rainfall (30 years).

Analyzing inter-annual variability of seasonal rainfall is important for understanding rainfall pattern which can aid in climatic studies, hydrological modeling and water resource management. Additionally, it is an essential factor affecting socioeconomic condition of India. Hence, long term inter-annual variability of seasonal rainfall was determined for the entire state throughout the climatological study period using the standardized anomaly index. Based on the deviation of normal rainfall, IMD classified seasonal rainfall have been used, that is five classes, viz: large excess (≥ 60%), excess (20 to 59%), normal (− 19 to 19%), deficient (− 59 to − 20%), large deficient (≤ -60%). In this study, the classification was done to envision the spatio-temporal patterns of seasonal rainfall over WB during the study period.

Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing (LOWESS) was used to discover temporal variability in district-wise seasonal and annual rainfall. Before smoothing, rainfall values were converted into seasonal anomalies by deducting the long-term mean of each season for each district, thus eliminating climatological offsets and ensuring comparability across seasons. LOWESS smoothing was accomplished using a smoothing span (f) of 0.30, implying that 30% of neighbouring observations were used in each local regression. The default tricube weighting function with local linear fitting, as implemented in the base R lowess() function, was employed. Robust reweighting was not applied, as the analysis was intended for exploratory visualization of rainfall anomaly patterns rather than for statistical inference.

Tests for homogeneity

To assess the internal consistency of the seasonal and annual rainfall time series and to evaluate their suitability for subsequent trend analysis, the Standard Normal Homogeneity Test (SNHT) was applied to district-wise rainfall data37 at the 5% significance level. The SNHT is a widely used diagnostic tool for detecting potential discontinuities in climatic time series. Although SNHT test statistics may exceed commonly cited reference38 values (e.g., T₀ ≈ 9.17), such thresholds are indicative and primarily applicable to longer climate records. Given the relatively short length of the rainfall series (n = 30), statistical significance was assessed using Monte-Carlo permutation testing rather than relying on fixed or asymptotic critical values. or each time series, the null distribution of the SNHT statistic was generated through repeated random permutations of the data, and p-values were derived empirically from this distribution. Homogeneity decisions were therefore based on the Monte-Carlo p-values, with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Trend analysis in rainfall

Mann–Kendall (MK), Modified MK (mMK) and Sen’s slope estimator

A rank-based non-parametric Mann–Kendall (MK) analysis39,40 was performed to test the significance of rainfall trend. The benefit of using non-parametric test is that it doesn’t assume the sample to follow a definite distribution and is not subjective to irregular values making it simpler to calculate and test categorical and ordinal variables. However, identifying trends in time series data is frequently subjected by the occurrence of significant serial autocorrelation41. Since hydrometeorological time series often exhibit serial dependence, the presence of lag-1 autocorrelation (r₁) was examined for each district and season prior to trend assessment. Lag-1 autocorrelation coefficients were tested for statistical significance at the 95% confidence level. For time series exhibiting no significant serial correlation, the classical Mann–Kendall test was applied. However, for series showing significant lag-1 autocorrelation, the Modified Mann–Kendall (mMK) test with variance correction, as proposed by Hamed and Ramachandra Rao42, was employed. Additionally, another non-parametric test i.e., Sen’s slope (SS) estimator was used to determine the magnitude (true slope) of the trend43. The Positive and negative SS, MK and mMK values indicate increasing and decreasing trends, respectively.

Innovative trend analysis (ITA)

The innovative trend analysis (ITA) method of Şen44, was used to identify seasonal and annual rainfall trends. The time series rainfall (1990–2019) was divided into two halves and sorted into the ascending order. The first half of the sub-series is located on the X axis, while the second half was assigned to the Y axis of a cartesian coordinate system. If the two subseries are identical, the points in the scatter plot coincide over a 1:1 line, indicating no trend in the time series; if , points in the subseries falling above or below the 1:1 line indicate positive or negative trends in the time series data44,45. When the points fall both above and below the 1:1 line indicating the non-monomeric trends in the time series, in that case, the scatter plot will take the shape of a curve. If the points are located closer to the 1:1 line, the trend slope will be weaker. The slope of ITA (ITAS), proposed by Şen46, indicates an increasing or decreasing trend if ITAS is either positive or negative, respectively. To express ITA trends in physical time units, a calibrated ITA rate was computed following the mean-shift approach. The rainfall series was divided into two equal halves, and the difference between the mean rainfall of the second (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X\overline{2 }$$\end{document}) and first halves (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X\overline{1 }$$\end{document}) was divided by the number of years per half (15 years), yielding a trend in mm yr⁻1 (ITA mm/year = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{(X\overline{2 }-X\stackrel{-}{1)}}{15}$$\end{document}. The resulting values were further scaled to mm decade⁻1 (ITA mm/decade = ITA mm/year*10) for ease of interpretation. ITA method is free from the assumptions of serial autocorrelation, normality, and length of the records making it superior to the most commonly used classical trend analysis methods like the MK or mMK, SS and LRA method of trend analysis. Hence, in the present study, ITA was analyzed using the “trendchange” package47 in R software version 4.2.2 (R Core Team 2020) with the null hypothesis (H0) of no trend in rainfall against the alternate hypothesis (H1) of the existence of a trend was tested at two different significance levels (α), i.e., α = 5% and α = 1%.

Comparison of different methods of trend analysis was done through intercomparison of the result. To interpret for multiple hypothesis testing across districts, false discovery rate (FDR) control was conducted to district-wise p-values (derived using ITA confidence interval at 95%) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (α = 0.05).

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA)

It is an essential statistical tool for grouping homogeneous variables (here districts) based on their common features. The current study considered Ward’s hierarchical algorithm48 as a dissimilarity measure using Euclidean distance, to group different district having similar trends in time series rainfall data. This method minimizes within group variability, produce clusters with the least inter variation. Since ITA-derived rainfall slopes vary considerably in scale across seasons owing to large seasonal contrasts in rainfall totals, all seasonal and annual slope variables (Xi) were standardized preceding to clustering to avoid scale bias. HCA was performed in R-studio v. 4.2.0 where library tidyverse49, factoextra50 and dendextend51 were used and code was written for clustering the variables. Number of clusters to be created depends on the level where within-group similarity is more and between-group similarity is less. Attempt have been made in this study to find optimum number of clusters to group districts under different agroclimatic zones based on the rainfall trend analysis. To perform this, different methods i.e., Elbow, Silhouette and Gap Statistics were done using function fviz_nbclust and NbClust available in R-studio v. 4.2.0. Silhouette method52 measure the quality of clustering by determining closeness of each object lies within the cluster. Elbow method use sum squared error of the distance between the points of the sample in each cluster as an indicator to drive optimum cluster number. While, Gap Statistics53 works on the principle of reference metrics that can be assimilated using the Monte Carlo sampling method and calculate square of Euclidean distance among the two measurements in each class.

Tele-connections between seasonal rainfall and large-scale climate indices using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, partial coefficient of correlation

Six climatic indices viz. Dipole Mode Index (DMI), Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), Niño 3, Niño 3.4, Niño 4, and Multivariate ENSO Index (MEI) were used to understand the teleconnection between the seasonal rainfall and oceanic climatic indices for the study region. The monthly time series datasets were acquired from NOAA Physical Sciences Laboratory (PSL), site (https://psl.noaa.gov/data/timeseries/month/). To ensure consistency with rainfall aggregation, the monthly indices were averaged to derive seasonal and annual mean values. To track ENSO (El Niño-Southern Oscillation) efficiently, NOAA employs multiple proxy indices that represent periodic fluctuations in Sea surface temperature anomaly (SSTA) and atmospheric pressure across the equatorial Pacific. The intensity of the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) is characterised by the DMI. While, ENSO phases were primarily identified using ONI, defined as a three-month progressing average of SSTA in the Niño 3.4 region (5°N–5°S and 170°W–120°W) of the equatorial Pacific Ocean.

Following NOAA convention, an El Niño (La Niña) event was identified when ONI values exceeded + 0.5 °C (− 0.5 °C) for at least five consecutive overlapping seasons. The Multivariate ENSO Index (MEI.v2) is a comprehensive index that incorporates five key variables related to ENSO: sea level pressure (SLP), SST, meridional and zonal wind components at the surface, surface air temperature, and outgoing longwave radiation (OLR) and is frequently used in combination with other indices to understand long term tele-connection of ENSO and rainfall. The Niño 3, Niño 4, and Niño 3.4 indices were calculated from the HadISST1 based on the 1981–2010 climatology period. Niño 3.4 and the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) are among the most extensively used metrics for tracking El Niño and La Niña events. The monthly anomaly index data were averaged to get values for seasonal (as per the IMD seasonal classification e.g., Winter, Pre monsoon, Monsoon, Post monsoon) and annual scale. In this study, 30 years’ time series data was divided into neutral (1990, 1993, 1994, 1996, 2001, 2004, 2005. 2006, 2009, 2013, 2014, 2017, 20018, 2019), El Niño (1991, 1992, 1995, 1997,1998, 2002,2003, 2010, 2015,2016) and La Niño (1999, 2000, 2007, 2008, 2011, 2012) phases based on the available information (https://psl.noaa.gov/enso/past_events.html) and ONI values. The number of years contributing to each ENSO phase was recorded separately for seasonal and annual analyses to ensure adequate sample representation. Subsequently, the tele-connections with large-scale climate indices and ENSO with seasonal and annual rainfall was assessed using the Pearson linear correlation coefficient (CC) at the 95% confidence level for El Niño (n = 10), La Niño (n = 6) and neutral phases (n = 14). The confidence interval (CI) of the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) among seasonal/annual rainfall and large-scale climate indices was assessed using nonparametric method proposed by Olivoto et al.54 in r studio.

Although ENSO phases (El Niño, La Niña, and Neutral) were identified based on NOAA criteria for descriptive analysis, the correlation analysis was conducted using continuous seasonal mean values of ONI and other indices rather than categorical ENSO phases. A partial correlation coefficient was conducted to detect significant teleconnections among WB’s seasonal/ annual rainfall with ENSO (ONI) and IOD (indicate intensity of IOD). ENSO and IOD events may interact with one another in relation to rainfall variability19. Pearson’s partial correlation coefficients were computed between rainfall and individual ENSO indicator (ONI), with DMI used as the regulatory variable. The analysis was done separately for winter, pre-monsoon, monsoon, post-monsoon, and annual rainfall.

Results

Rainfall homogeneity and descriptive statistics

The results indicate that the majority of district-wise rainfall series exhibit homogeneous behaviour (p > 0.05), with SNHT test statistics below the critical threshold (Table 1). In a few districts or seasons (Alipurduar and Coochbehar in winter 1994; S-Dinajpur and W-Bardhaman in pre-monsoon during 1991 and 1996 respectively; S-Dinajpur, Maldah & Murshidabad in monsoon and annual during 2008, 2000 and 2009), comparatively higher T₀ values and significant (p < 0.05) test statistics were observed, suggesting the presence of potential localized or seasonal discontinuities. However, these discontinuities lack consistent spatial coherence across neighbouring districts and are therefore interpreted as isolated or weak inhomogeneities. Isolated significant results were not interpreted as evidence of widespread inhomogeneity unless supported by coherent spatial or seasonal patterns. Season-specific inhomogeneities are plausible in monsoon-dominated climates, where rainfall variability and observation uncertainty differ substantially across seasons, making seasonal series more sensitive to shifts than annual aggregates. Investigative data analysis: the long-term pattern of rainfall at seasonal and annual scale; percent departure of rainfall (PDR), LOWESS (locally weighted scatterplot smoothing) and rainfall classification

The long-term climatic patterns were evaluated using annual and seasonal rainfall time series. As monsoon accounts for > 75% of annual precipitation concentrations, more attention was given to explore the monsoon rainfall variation over the time period. The spatio-temporal departure of seasonal and annual rainfall for different districts was measured using PDR (Fig. 2c), where positive PDR signifies above-normal rainfall, and a negative depicts below normal rainfall. In this study, PDR varied from 147.55 in Alipurduar, (AD) to a minimum of -46.31 in Purulia (PU). Analyses revealed that more than 50% of districts received below-normal rainfall during the Pre monsoon (78.26%), Monsson (73.91%) and Annual rainfall (73.91%). Conversely, around 52% districts received above-normal rainfall during winter and post-monsoon season (Fig. 2c). Spatial mapping highlighted a concentration of negative PDR of rainfall in the Gangetic WB during pre-monsoon, monsoon and annual periods indicating regional disparity and climatic vulnerability compared to Sub-Himalayan WB. However, positive PDR was observed in both winter and post-monsoon season in the Gangetic WB as well as Sub-Himalayan WB (Fig. 2c). Irrespective of different seasonal and annual rainfall variations, among different ACZ, Undulating Red and Laterite Zone and Gangetic Alluvial Zone showed more negative PDR indicating a higher occurrence of below-normal rainfall events. This insistent deficit underlines the susceptibility of these zones to climatic stress, affecting their agricultural productivity and water resource availability.

The running mean, although normally used, is not always robust to specify local variations and can significantly inclined by short-term irregularities. To address this limitation and lessen the stimulus of local variations, the annual and seasonal rainfall time series were fitted with LOWESS regression technique to identify rainfall patterns over time. This non-parametric method offers a more flexible and adaptive fit to the data, allowing for better visualization and interpretation of underlying long-term rainfall trends. The LOWESS curves along with the rainfall anomaly of WB is shown in Fig. 3a,c,e,g,i; which represent the direction and intensity of rainfall changes over the study period. During the winter season, a predominantly positive rainfall anomaly was observed during 1990–1998 (Fig. 3a). However, from the early 2000s ahead, the LOWESS curve showed gradual decreasing pattern with an increasing number more number of years showing negative rainfall anomalies between 2000 and 2018. Thereafter, a certain rise in the curve has been observed at the study period for WB. Nevertheless, positive rainfall bars were observed during 2007–2008, despite the descending trajectory during this period (Fig. 3a). The LOWESS curve for pre-monsoon rainfall showed the peaks and valley configuration reflecting oscillating nature of rainfall anomalies, occasionally dipping into the negative anomaly sharply up to 1997, and at the other times crossing into positive anomaly zone until 2001(Fig. 3c). After 2001, the curve showed a steady decline with negative anomalies till 2017, indicating a concerning downward trend in pre-monsoon rainfall across WB. For the annual rainfall, the LOWESS curve (Fig. 3i) shows a slight increasing pattern with large positive rainfall anomaly up to the end of 2000. However, this upward pattern was followed by a prolonged decline until 2012 marked by noticeable negative rainfall anomalies. Even though, a minor increase was observed during 2015–16, the overall trajectory continues downward thereafter, indicating a consistent decline in annual rainfall along with sustained negative anomalies, suggesting the worsening rainfall scenario in WB. A comparable LOWESS pattern was exhibited for monsoon rainfall (Fig. 3e), closely mirroring the annual rainfall over the given time frame. From 2000 to 2019, the monsoon curve showed steady downward pattern, however, small patches of positive rainfall anomalies particularly during 1995–1999, 2007–2008 were evident. For post-monsoon rainfall, LOWESS curve (Fig. 3g) reveals an initial increasing trend, with more number of positive rainfall anomalies up to 2005. Subsequently, a sharp decrease in the curve was observed which sustained until 2015, indicating a period of notable rainfall deficit. Although positive anomaly was observed in some patches, however, more than 50% of the time series observations exhibited negative anomalies, where different districts received deficient to large deficient rainfall especially during 2005–2018. The inter-annual variability captured by the LOWESS curve highlights the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of rainfall distribution across the state. To better understand this variation, district-wise rainfall categorization is illustrated in Fig. 3b,d,f,h,j, which provides insights into the spatial disparity and temporal shifts in seasonal and annual rainfall patterns.

The heat map illustrating districts wise rainfall categorization across years clearly depicted the patterns of large deficient to deficient winter rainfall (indicated in red to pink colour) across different districts during the years 1999, 2001–2006, 2008, 2009–2013, 2017–2018 (Fig. 3b). Conversely, years e.g., 1990, 1994, 1998, 2007, and 2019 were marked by the excess to large excess winter rainfall across several districts. Irrespective of the districts, around 53–83% of the time series received large deficient rainfall, with Kalimpong (KM) recoding the highest number of such event. On the other hand, 16–26% time series received the excess to large excess rainfall with Alipurduar (AD) registering the highest number of large excess rainfall. Across the ACZs, Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone received large deficient rainfall in 69–76% time series. While, Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Vindhyan Alluvial Zone, Costal Saline Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone received 16–18% large excess and 59–63% large deficient rainfall during winter in the time series (represented in the spatial pie chart for winter season, Fig. 3b). The spatiotemporal heat map for pre-monsoon rainfall in WB indicates that, irrespective of different districts, the state perceived an extensive range of rainfall anomalies, with approximately 47% of the time series categorized as deficient, and 17% as large excess (Fig. 3d). Among different ACZs, as indicated in the spatial pie chart, Undulating Red and Laterite Zone and Gangetic Alluvial Zone received 64–68% deficient rainfall, whereas, Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone received 62–78% large excess rainfall. The years, 2004–2008, 2015–2018, notably received deficient rainfall in 14–16 districts in pre-monsoon season. In contrast, districts such as Darjeeling, Jalpaiguri, Kalimpong, Alipurduar, Coochbehar consistently received large excess rainfall (indicated with dark green band; Fig. 3d) throughout the time series during pre-monsoon season. During monsoon season (Fig. 3f), around 31% of the total districts received normal rainfall, whereas, 44% districts received deficit rainfall during 1990–2019 time series. During monsoon season, districts under Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone received excess (12–20%) to large excess (58–88%) rainfall, in contrast, widespread deficiency was observed across Gangetic Alluvial Zone (65%), Costal Saline Zone (37%), Undulating Red and Laterite Zone (66.66%) and Vindhyan Alluvial Zone (46.66%) as shown in the spatial pie chart (Fig. 3f). At the district level, Murshidadad, Bankura, Purba-Bardhman recorded the highest number of deficient time series rainfall i.e., 83.33%- highlighting susceptibility to monsoon inconsistency and rainfall shortage. During post-monsoon season (Fig. 3h), in general, nearly 32% of districts in WB received normal rainfall, while, 19 and 13% of the total districts received excess to large excess rainfall respectively. In contrast, 31% districts received deficient rainfall during 1990–2019 time series. Among the ACZs (shown in the spatial pie chart), Undulating Red and Laterite Zone received normal rainfall in 36–46% of the time series, however, Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Vindhyan Alluvial Zone received 30 and 52% deficient rainfall during 1990–2019 time series during post-monsoon seasons. ACZ i.e., Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone received excess (16–17%) to large excess (56–83%) rainfall in annual basis (Fig. 3j). Whereas, Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Vindhyan Alluvial Zone, Costal Saline Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone received normal rainfall in 36, 40, 81 and 35% and deficient rainfall in 61, 58, 18 and 65% of the time series respectively. Importantly, the downward trend in annual rainfall was found much similar with monsoon rainfall pattern which showed a deficient rainfall from 2008 onwards and continued to the end of 2019. Overall, except winter season, most regular/consistent rainfall event (large to excess large) was observed for ACZ Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone which covers a major part of Sub-Himalayan West Bengal, however, major variation (Normal, to deficit to large deficit rainfall) was observed in Gangetic West Bengal consisting of Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Costal Saline Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone.

Rainfall trends in winter

During the winter season, rainfall trend detected by MK/mMK and SS was majorly dominated with non-significant negative values (Table 2). However, only 3 districts e.g., Jalpaiguri, Kalimpong and Alipurduar showed a significantly (p < 0.05) decreasing rainfall trends at a rate more than − 20 mm per decade (Table 2). Conversely, ITA method indicated the significant negative (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) slope in 17 districts and significant positive (p < 0.01) slopes in 3 districts (Table 3, Fig. 4a). Among the districts, Kalimpong showed the sharpest decreasing trend of rainfall of − 29.6 mm per decade (p < 0.01) during 1990–2019 as identified using ITA. Furthermore, ITA scatter plot for winter rainfall trend depicts most of time series rainfall points accumulated below the 1:1 line which affirms the existences of decreasing trends across the districts (Fig. 4a). However, some districts viz. Jhargram, Medinipur-East, Howrah, 24-Paraganas-South and Medinipur-West showed non-monotonic trend where data points are dispersed both on above and below the 1:1 line for some part of time series, indicating a complex, oscillatory rainfall behavior. The spatial distribution of winter rainfall trends, showed a large number of districts in Northern Hill Zone, Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone, Gangetic Alluvial Zone and Vindhyan Alluvial Zone showing significant negative trends (decreasing rate 4–19 mm per decade). In contrast, southern districts showed more variable non-monotonic pattern. Among all ACZ, Northern Hill Zone exhibited sharpest decreasing trend at the rate -19 mm per decade.

Rainfall trends in pre-monsoon

Rainfall trends in pre-monsoon season as detected by MK/mMK and SS methods (Table 2) revealed that most of the districts were dominated by non-significant negative or positive Z statistics, indicating weak or unclear trends. However, ITA method detected the significant (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) negative rainfall trends (Table 3, Fig. 4b) in approximately 78% of the districts, additionally, 3 districts viz. Jalpaiguri, Paschim-Bardhaman and Purulia were identified to have significant positive slope. Spatial distribution of ITA derived trend slope (Fig. 4b) showed that a large number of districts predominantly in the Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Coastal saline zone, Northern Hill Zone and Vindhyan Alluvial Zone displayed significant negative rainfall trend at the decreasing rate − 21 to − 46 mm per decade. This was further confirmed by ITA scatter plot, where most of the time series rainfall points gathered below the 1:1 line (Fig. 4b), indicating the prevalent declining trend. However, few positive slope was also detected in small patches of western and northern part of the state indicating non- monotonic trends in the scatter plot. In terms of magnitude, Medinipur-East showed the sharpest decreasing trends (p < 0.01) at a rate more than − 47 mm per decade, while, Jalpaiguri showed sharpest increasing trends (p < 0.01) at a rate more than + 31 mm per decade. Among the ACZs, Costal Saline Zone exhibited the sharpest decreasing trend at the rate of − 46 mm per decade.

Rainfall trends in monsoon

During the monsoon season, traditional methods (MK/mMK, SS) indicated the negative rainfall trends in majority of the districts, mostly non-significant (56%). (Table 2). On the other hand, ITA method identified significant (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) negative trends in 82% of districts clearly demonstrating its higher sensitivity in detecting rainfall variability. Only three districts e.g., Jalpaiguri, Jhargram and Kalimpong were identified as significant (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) positive slopes as per ITA method in monsoon rainfall in WB (Table 3, Fig. 4c). These finding underscores the limitation of traditional methods, which failed to identify significant trends in over 60% of the districts, whereas ITA effectively reveled the hidden trends. Spatial distribution of ITA slope map showed that except 3 districts with positive slope, almost all the ACZs were identified with negative rainfall slope during monsoon. Remarkably, Dinajpur-Dakshin, Maldah and Dinajpur-Uttar displayed the sharpest declines, ranging from − 204 to − 332 mm per decade. ITA scatter plot reinforced this trend, showing majority of the time series data points except Jalpaiguri, Kalimpong, Jhargram, Alipurduar and Coochbehar, concentrating below the 1:1 line for monsoon rainfall trend confirming the widespread presence of decreasing rainfall trends for all districts (Fig. 4c). Among the ACZs, Vindhyan Alluvial Zone exhibited the sharpest declining monsoon rainfall slope at the rate − 205 mm per decade, while, other ACZs (except Northern Hill Zone) showed decreasing monsoon rainfall slope at the rate − 30 to − 111 mm per decade which is very distressing across most regions of the state requiring climate adaptation plans.

Rainfall trends in post-monsoon

We have observed that a larger number of districts (87%) having significantly (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) decreasing rainfall trends in post-monsoon season were detected by ITA method (Tables 2 and 3). In contrast, MK/mMK and SS detected non-significant decreasing trend (93% of districts) (Tables 2 and 3). Among the various districts and ACZs, the sharpest decreasing rainfall rate was observed in Darjeeling (− 46 mm per decade) and Northern Hill Zone (− 40 mm per decade) respectively (Fig. 4d). Spatial map further highlighted that Northern Hill Zone, the Gangetic Alluvial Zone and Costal Saline Zone experienced decline in post-monsoon rainfall i.e., at the rate of − 27 to − 35 mm per decade rate (Table 3, Fig. 4d). ITA scatter plot reaffirms the findings where most of time series rainfall points accumulated below the 1:1 line (Fig. 4d).

Trends in annual rainfall

Annual rainfall represents the accumulated seasonal rainfall, in which monsoon contributes around 74% of the annual average rainfall. Similar to monsoonal rainfall, annual rainfall trend was observed to have decreasing rainfall tendencies over the study period detected by MK/mMK, SS and ITA methods (Tables 2, 3 and Fig. 4e). While, MK/mMK identified non-significant trends with negative Z statistics, the ITA method revealed significant negative trends (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) in 95% of the districts. Notably, the sharpest significantly (p < 0.01) positive rainfall slope (+ 275 mm per decade) was observed only in Jalpaiguri by the ITA method. In contrast, the sharpest decline in annual rainfall was recorded in Dakshin Dinajpur, with a slope of − 414 mm per decade, while among the agro-climatic zones (ACZs), the Vindhyan Alluvial Zone experienced the most pronounced decline, i.e., at a rate of − 252 mm per decade (Table 3, Fig. 4e). Other ACZs, showed decreasing annual rainfall slope at the rate − 65 to − 165 mm per decade with majority of time series rainfall points falling below the 1:1 line in ITA scatter plot further confirming widespread negative trend in annual rainfall patterns (Fig. 4e).

Comparison of results of different rainfall trend analysis methods

In this study, we analyzed 115 time series rainfall data for seasonal and annual rainfall from 23 districts of WB using MK, mM-K, SS estimator and ITA methods. Among 115 time series analyzed, only 18 (15.65%) time series showed significant trends in seasonal and annual rainfall using MK/mMK and SS (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, ITA method detected significant rainfall trends (particularly decreasing) in 103 (89.56%) of time series rainfall data. This clearly indicates that MK/mMK and SS methods failed to detect a large number of significant trends (either positive or negative) of seasonal rainfall time series which could be identified by ITA method. Additionally, all the significant rainfall trends identified either by MK/mMK and SS were also effectively identified using ITA method, besides this, ITA identified an additional 85 time series representing a 74% increase over conventional methods (Supplementary Table 3). District-wise trend significance was reviewed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. The number of districts exhibiting significant rainfall trends before and after FDR adjustment for each season is shown in Supplementary Table 4. After applying FDR correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (α = 0.05), 19, 21, 22, 20 and 21 districts retained statistical significance during winter, pre-monsoon, monsoon, post monsoon and annual period. This indicating robust field significance and minimal inflation of Type-I error. The test results from all four methods indicated that the ITA was most effective in detecting time series rainfall trends. A fundamental advantage of ITA is its capability to graphically represent full range of data on the Cartesian coordinate system (Fig. 4a–e) which aids in better visual inspection and further interpretations.

Discussion

Rainfall is a crucial component in the hydrological cycle and shifting pattern of rainfall in relation to climate change is a key concern nowadays with regard to water resource management, sustainable agricultural planning, ecosystem management and health sector2,9,55. Our study significantly detected the long-term seasonal and annual rainfall trends in different districts of WB. For this, we have performed the in depth seasonal and annual rainfall analysis using precipitation statistics, percent departure of rainfall, LOWESS long-term pattern using, precipitation categorization through heat mapping, identified rainfall trends using four different statistical methods i.e., MK, Modified MK, Sen Slope estimator and Innovative Trend Analysis and used the slope values for clustering the districts of WB.

Causes of rainfall variations, trend and tele-connections

The study detected both increasing and decreasing rainfall trends with positive and negative rainfall anomaly in seasonal and annual rainfall across different ACZs of WB. However, predominance of significant negative seasonal and annual rainfall trends was found in a greater number of ACZs covering major fraction of total districts in the study period 1990–2019 in WB. Winter rainfall, generally contribute meager percentage of annual rainfall, showed significant decreasing rainfall trends in whole state of WB, except southern parts of ACZs. Our findings are fairly analogous to earlier study of, Ghosh et al. 2018,56 reported substantial reduction in winter (15–56%) with significant negative rainfall trends and contrasting with9 who stated non-significant decreasing rainfall trends in WB. Similarly,24,25 observed decreasing rainfall trends in the northernmost parts (covering districts Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri) in winter, possibly due to leeward side of the hills usually gets a nominal quantity of rainfall and furthermore, the local-level changes in the LULC pattern to location. This decline has affected sectors such as the tea industry, with Mallik and Ghosh (2021) emphasizing its susceptibility in the Dooars region over the past two decades. However, Costal Saline Zone and southern part of Undulating Red and Laterite Zone perceived significant positive rainfall trends which might be which might be because of cyclonic activities over coastal areas triggered by the westerly wind from the Mediterranean region57. Moreover, due to the movement of the trough of withdrawing monsoon towards the Bay of Bengal in winter, coastal regions of WB receives moist air from the ocean which causes heavy rain and thundershower58. In the present study we found significantly decreasing trends in pre-monsoon rainfall across Northern Hill Zone, Vindhyan Alluvial Zone, Gangetic Alluvial Zone and Costal Saline Zone in WB. The result is quite similar to the findings of Mukhopadhyay et al.59, who identified both significant and non-significant negative trends in the maximum districts of GWB. In contrast, some districts of Undulating Red and Laterite Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone showed a comparable portion of positive pre-monsoon trend. The higher slope values of precipitation in the northern part of WB are probably due to the Nor’wester, which enters from a northwest direction in this region24,25. The explanations for declining pre-monsoon rain in WB are multifaceted and comprise both natural and human-induced influences. One prime factor is the weakening of the heat low, which is a critical features of monsoon circulation16. Under such conditions, effective rainwater management can lessen the additive crop water stress in WB during pre-monsoon season. On the other hand, an early incidence of such catastrophe may have a damaging impression on the harvesting of Rabi crops. In general, WB gets the highest contribution of rainfall during the monsoon season. Conversely, the current study exposes a decreasing trend of monsoonal precipitation, even in the high rainfall receiving zone in WB. Sharpest changes were observed in Vindhyan Alluvial Zone, Gangetic Alluvial Zone and Costal Saline Zone followed by Undulating Red and Laterite Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone. Similarly, annual rainfall pattern closely follows monsoonal rainfall trends in WB for the same time. These findings are quite comparable to earlier study of Guhathakurta and Revadekar24,25,60, and56 who found a significant decreasing monsoonal trend in both Sub-Himalayan West Bengal, and Gangetic West Bengal. Similarly, a considerable decline in early and mid-monsoonal rainfall along with the intensification of intermittent dry spells or aridity were observed in GWB (especially in Gangetic Alluvial Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone) by Ghosh et al. 2018. Meteorological drought analysis using standardized precipitation index (SPI) revealed increase in dry events and a decrease in wet and normal events, with the causes of water scarcity in Purulia, Bankura, and Midnapore districts61 of WB over multi-decadal periods (1901–2017). Regional SPI trend analyses also reflected heightened drought duration and hazard potential with greater temporal variability of rainfall during pre-monsoon, monsoon and post monsoon period in southern West Bengal61. In another study,62, identified a significant increase in both short-term and long-term dryness using rainfall dependent dryness index (SPI) during pre-monsoon and monsoon season (Jute growing season) in different districts of South West Bengal for a time span 1981–2018. Additionally,63 assessed correlation between rainfall and MODIS-derived normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) in WB during 2011–2020. The study found a positive correlation between rainfall and NDVI in the region with lower rainfall periods associated with reduced NDVI, which provides mechanistic evidence linking rainfall anomalies to NDVI response indicative of vegetation stress. Together, these findings strengthen the findings of the current study that decreasing rainfall patterns are revealing as hydrological stress at manifold levels, with prolonged dry spells and changed vegetation responses, which are pertinent to water resources and agricultural productivity. Along with rainfall trends, several studies have documented long-term changes in groundwater levels and storage across West Bengal, supporting the broader hydro-climatic stress narrative. Trend analysis of groundwater level and ground water drought using Standard Groundwater Level Index in an eastern river basin of West Bengal exhibited that nearly 60% of wells result in decreasing water levels, predominantly in post-monsoon seasons, demonstrating groundwater depletion consistent with rainfall inconsistency and anthropogenic pressures64. Similarly, multi-decadal (1971–2020) study of rainfall variability and groundwater recharge in WB exhibits significant drops in groundwater storage across physiographic provinces such as the plateau fringe (− 5.59%), uplands (− 3.39%), Non-Reclaimed Sundarbans (− 2.54%) and Upper Ganga Delta (− 1.00%) where rainfall recharge is decreased during pre-monsoon and annual period65. In south western WB, statistical analysis of groundwater depletion and drought analysis indicates a quantifiable response of aquifers to long-term rainfall shortages (Sarkar, 2023. These findings strengthen the declining rainfall trend, found the in the current study with pertinent hydrological effects, supplementing the sector-relevant metrics of hydro-climatic stress. Several prominent global climates forcing e.g., ENSO,Indian Ocean Dipole Mode and Eurasian snow cover, influence the inter-annual variability of southwest monsoon and associated with the decreasing monsoon rainfall. Amongst these, the intensity of the tropical easterly jet (TEJ is important through the southwest monsoon10. Declining strength of TEJ from 1950s was indicated by Rao et al.66, which supposed to be connected with the declining land-sea temperature difference through the pre-monsoon season18. Similarly, in previous study Rao et al.67 observed decreasing trend in the strength of TEJ in long-term waning of Asian summer monsoon. This linked to reduced moisture advection and weaker monsoon circulation over eastern India (WB) and the Bay of Bengal. Furthermore, Chung and Ramanathan15 reported reduction of summer monsoon precipitation due to weak sea surface temperature (SST) gradient owing to the intense heating of the equatorial Indian Ocean. Experimental studies of areas adjacent to WB similarly identify inclining aerosol loading over the northern Bay of Bengal and neighbouring outflow provinces, which can influence cloud radiative properties and moisture convection, thereby altering rainfall circulation68. Such aerosol and SST gradients over the North Indian Ocean control monsoon circulation and can modify the spatial dispersal of rainfall impacting eastern India69. In addition to all these processes, the study of Sarkar et al.70 detected decline of black carbon aerosols loading, predominantly at the Darjeeling Himalayan region during monsoon, which reduces the rainfall by decreasing the SST gradient17. Additionally, large-scale variability in climate drivers such as ENSO and the IOD effect Bay of Bengal SST gradients and moisture fluxes, highlighting the intricacy of monsoon modulation disturbing rainfall trends in West Bengal71. Furthermore, the human footprint in the form of widespread LULC change (deforestation, forest destruction, urban area expansion), exclusively in the northern and southern parts of WB has already augmented the average monsoonal temperature24. Regional climate model experiments, by72, showed that human induced LULC change—particularly disforestation and alteration to cropland—decreases evaporative moisture recycling and surface roughness, leading to reductions in rainfall events over the Indian monsoon region. A significant LULC transitions over multiple decades in Alluvial, red and laterite zone in WB, including decrease in vegetation cover and rises in built-up area, escorted by modifications in surface climate indicators such as NDVI and land surface temperature can affect local and regional atmospheric processes, which may be connected with observed rainfall declines in West Bengal73. These modifications may have compensated the local-level moisture supply and brought about in the significant decline of monsoonal rainfall. The falling trend of monsoon, and annual rainfall especially in the Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Northern Hill Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone, can exaggerate the climatic susceptibilities due to water insufficiency. Post-monsoonal rainfall showed increasing pattern up-to year 2002 which is quite similar to the findings reported by Ghosh et al.26,27, who analyzed the rainfall trend for 1901–2002, in addition, the current study indicated the declining pattern of rainfall trends from 2004 onwards which sustained until 2018 in post-monsoon season in WB. Similarly,9,24,25 observed significant decrease in post-monsoonal rainfall during 1901–2020 in northern parts covering SHWB. In contrast, they also detected positive rainfall trends over Gangetic West Bengal during post-monsoon indicating a monsoon rainfall shift which contradicts the findings of the current study. Although there might be some offsetting effects from other seasons, the post-monsoon rainfall in eastern India, has largely been declining over the past few decades, leading to increased drought vulnerability in certain areas74. Such discrepancies might be accredited to the variation in timescales to prepare seasonal data (like October–November vs. October–November- December for Post-monsoon), including the chosen procedures. Rainfall over WB is prejudiced by numerous interrelating processes, including cyclonic disturbances and low-pressure systems initiating over the Bay of Bengal, along with orographic lifting over northern WB connected with the Himalayan foothills. The long-term seasonal and annual rainfall series analysed in this study intrinsically include the collective influences of these processes. Consequently, any consistent increase or decrease in the occurrence, strength, or spatiotemporal effect of cyclonic disturbances or orographic rainfall during 1990–2019 is reproduced in the detected rainfall trends. The trend measures thus indicate the net hydro-climatic impact of the region instead of the stimulus of distinct weather events. The declining rainfall trend can be tele-connected in light of the ENSO–rainfall correlation results. During neutral years, the weak correlations with SST/ ENSO indices suggest that regional drivers (e.g., Bay of Bengal depressions, local convection) play a dominant role, and the observed declining rainfall may be due to a long-term dwindling of these regional systems. During El Niño years, the negative correlation with monsoon and post-monsoon rainfall shows that El Niño already suppresses rainfall, and when combined with the underlying declining trend, it exaggerates drought situations. Conversely, La Niña years typically augment rainfall, as indicated by strong negative correlations with Niño indices; however, the long-term declining trend suggests that even La Niña years now giving insufficient rainfall compared to the past. Nevertheless, tele-connections among SST and ENSO indices with seasonal rainfall were linking well with the observed rainfall anomaly, and PDR during 1990–2019 as showed in this study. This highlights that while ENSO phases modulate interannual variability, the circumstantial declining trend is gradually lowering the overall rainfall availability across all phases. This can occur due to localized climatic factors that modulate the large-scale ENSO influence, such as the region’s specific topography and microclimatic variations75. On the contrary, ENSO effects over Bangladesh are much remarkable which was reported by Ahmed et al.76 who illustrated weak tele-connections of ENSO and significant correlation of IODs with rainfall in Bangladesh, while in Saudi Arabia, both ENSO and IOD have almost equal tele-connections77. It can be concluded that ONI, Niño 3.4 and DMI are reliable predictors of winter, pre-monsoon and post-monsoon rainfall anomalies, whereas, Niño 3.4, ONI, and MEI are the strongest predictors of monsoon and annual rainfall which seems to be better correlated with seasonal rainfall. As the overall rainfall trend is declining, high rainfall in La Niña may not be sufficient enough to compensate the drought risk generated due to El Niña. These findings may have implications for the seasonal as well as annual rainfall predictions. Further research can be extended on the evaluation of the recent climate models78,79) for the region to strengthen the rainfall predictions which will help in improving the adaptation strategies to climate change.

Better performance by ITA than traditional methods in detecting rainfall trends

For identifying rainfall trend with better accuracy, the current study found that ITA outperformed in identifying significant seasonal/ annual rainfall trends as compared to other trend analysis methods i.e., MK test, mMK test, and Sen’s slope estimator. Furthermore, ITA not only identified significant trend detected by MK test, mMK test, and Sen’s slope estimator, but also detected 85 more time series (74% more) trends that could not be detected by these methods (Supplementary Table 3). Zhou et al.45 reported superiority of ITA method in identifying significant trends in solar radiation compared to linear regression and MK test in China. The better efficiency of ITA as compared to traditional non-parametric methods in identifying seasonal rainfall trends using long term time series rainfall data in Haryana, different sub-divisions of India and Tamil Nadu were reported in previous studies14,56,80. The inclination for accepting ITA over MK/ mMK or SS is due to the underestimation as detected from the significance test. The MK/ mMK test assesses trends based on the ranks of data, making it less sensitive to subtle changes or short-term trends, especially in datasets with high variability14. Additionally, ITA method is free from autocorrelation in time series data, universally pertinent, independent of distribution hypothesis, seasonal period, or the time series size. In spite of the MK test’s extensive usage in hydrological trend analysis, several researches showed that the occurrence of autocorrelation interfered the capability to detect trends using MK and Sen’s slope45. Furthermore, ITA showed many strengths as compared to traditional methods, for example, it is simple to calculate and interpret; capable of detecting non-monotonic or oscillating trends and can efficiently handle nonlinear and nonstationary data, thereby capturing complex nature of rainfall variability more accurately.

Areas sensitive to changing rainfall patterns in and prospective strategies for WB

Previously different studies29,30,81 attempted to identify sensitive areas following clustering approach. We have analyzed the data to cluster different districts of WB based on the seasonal and annual rainfall trends obtained from ITA method. The process of data mining techniques, predominantly the cluster analysis, is reasonable and compulsory for examining spatiotemporal rainfall distribution, grouping of areas based on the rainfall trends and constructing rainfall hyetograph. The cluster analysis helped in quantifying number of districts, under each agroclimatic zone, affected with decreasing rainfall trends for different seasonal and annual rainfall. Characterization of each clusters revealed that cluster 1 had sharpest decreasing rainfall trend as compared to other clusters irrespective of different seasons. The findings are important in the perspective of environmental and economic planning and water management. Praveen et al.4 reported a forecasting of seasonal rainfall using machine learning approaches, which suggest that different meteorological divisions would receive significantly decreased precipitation for the coming 15 years. Hence, if the current declining precipitation trend extends, it may exacerbate the inaccessibility of water which may lead to conditions like drought, antagonistic influences on agriculture, water supply, and energy production in the agro climatic zones of the state1. Furthermore, the decreasing precipitation trends can affect the water quality and probably concern on the drinkable water availability, insufficient surface water resources and groundwater recharge55. Based on the analysis made in this study, climatic vulnerability due to rainfall deficiency and negative rainfall trends is found to be more pronounced during winter and pre-monsoon season in SHWB than in GWB. Whereas, GWB showed a greater vulnerability during monsoon, and annual periods, with higher concentrations of rainfall deficiency and negative rainfall trends. Remarkably, both SHWB and GWB showed susceptibility to deficit rainfall during post-monsoon season, signifying extensive and seasonally variable hydro-climatic stress across the state. More interestingly, the SHWB generally receive high average rainfall, however, the current scenario indicates vulnerability of this part due to decreasing rainfall trends, which may be because of climate change. The above discussion indicated that decreasing rainfall trends are significantly prominent in all the seasons, especially in the monsoon, which contributes 75–80% of annual rainfall; moreover, these changes are well marked both in the SHWB, and GWB. Lack of area under horticulture, low coverage of central crop insurance schemes, and prevalence of small and marginal landholders with low adaptive capacity amount to major drivers of vulnerability in these regions. In such situations, declining monsoonal rainfall can accelerate the occurrence of meteorological droughts with more burden on the groundwater resource, where the problems related to groundwater withdrawal, unsustainable use of water, and depletion are widespread. Agriculture in WB is dominated by rice-based cropping systems. Rice grown in all three major seasons e.g., aus (pre-monsoon), aman (monsoon), and boro (dry winter season with irrigation facility). Among these, aman rice is the foremost crop, responsible for the major share of total production and being highly reliant on monsoon rainfall (Economic Review 2017–18, Govt. of WB,34). Other important crops comprise wheat (winter season), maize, pulses, oilseeds, jute (pre-monsoon), and potato, with varying degrees of dependence on rainfall and irrigation. The observed decreasing trend in seasonal rainfall, specifically in monsoon and pre-monsoon periods, has momentous consequences for agricultural productivity in WB. Rainfall deficits during the monsoon directly affect aman rice, which depend on timely onset and adequate distribution of rainfall for transplanting and vegetative growth, otherwise, amplified intra-seasonal variability can postponed sowing, reduce soil moisture availability, and increase the frequency of agricultural droughts. Studies have shown that rainfall variability in eastern India significantly influences rice yields, with deficits during critical growth stages leading to substantial yield reductions65,72,82. In eastern India, result revealed (for the period 1990–2017) deficit rainfall reduces rice yield by 19%82. The study further observed the overall optimal rainfall threshold nationwide is 1621 ± 34 mm beyond which rice yield declines by 6.4 kg per hectare per 100 mm of rainfall82. Additionally, rising temperature has adverse effect on the growth and developmental phases of crop, and it forces early flowering. Concurrently, increase in temperature causes reduction of crop yield due to pollen sterility and poor pollen growth during reproductive growth stage24,69,72. The livelihoods of people, mainly depending on agriculture and fishing practices, would also largely impact due to declining rainfall trends especially during monsoon and post-monsoon season83. In drought-prone districts of western WB (e.g., Purulia, Bankura), declining rainfall has already been associated with increased drought frequency and reduced crop productivity84. To address this reduced rainfall trends in the state, different water resource management strategies viz. promoting drainage basin, as well as rainwater harvesting from the belt of increasing precipitation, improved water conservation, optimal use of water resources with improved micro-irrigation systems, conservation tillage to retain soil moisture and prevent soil erosion, retaining more soil moisture by using soil mulch, growing or planting low-water requirement crops during dry seasons or in water-limited areas, appropriate crop sequence on the same piece of land (crop diversification), use of drought-tolerant crops under water-limited conditions; rainwater harvesting and using it during lean period2,55,85. These would help in strategic planning and prescriptions for kharif and rabi agriculture . Emphasis may be given to tailored agricultural activity, through agri-diversification and eco-regional farming, integrated faming system by combining livestock, agroforestry; cultivating short duration drought tolerant crops mushroom cultivation etc.- Diversified farming could be a climate resilient and livelihood option in GWB and SHWB. Therefore, the indicated vulnerable regions of WB need special attention regarding the adaptation and mitigation strategies in the future.

Conclusion

Our study delves into an in-depth assessment of the dynamics of seasonal and annual rainfall (1990–2019) across 23 districts of WB , by the highlighting spatio-temporal variations of mean rainfall, rainfall departure, standardized anomaly, rainfall categorization and analyzed seasonal/ annual rainfall trends using MK, mMK, Sen’s Slope estimator and particularly the innovative trend analysis ITA methods. The GWB, comprising of ACZs of Undulating Red and Laterite Zone, Costal Saline Zone and Gangetic Alluvial Zone received 55, 49 and 47% less rainfall with 73–78% negative PDR during pre-monsoon, monsoon and annually respectively as compared to SHWB. In contrast, GWB received 12 and 5% more rainfall with 52% positive PDR during winter and post-monsoon seasons respectively as compared to SHWB during 1990–2019. In the rainfall time series, consistent positive seasonal and annual rainfall anomaly was observed until 2000–2004, after that, concomitant negative rainfall anomaly accelerated downward trajectory of seasonal and annual rainfall WB. Each subsequent decade, beginning from 2000, showed decreasing pattern in monsoon rainfall with more negative rainfall anomaly which emphasizes climatic vulnerability during monsoon season in WB. This variation was reaffirmed with classified heat map analyzed for different districts. Except winter season, most regular/consistent rainfall event was noticed for Northern Hill Zone and Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone which covers a major part of Sub-Himalayan West Bengal, however, major variation (Normal, to deficit to large deficit rainfall) was observed in Gangetic West Bengal consisting of Gangetic Alluvial Zone, Costal Saline Zone and Undulating Red and Laterite Zone. Among 115 time series rainfall analyzed, ITA identified an additional 85 time series representing a 74% increase over conventional methods (MK, mM-K, SS estimator), underscoring its capacity to identify significant hidden rainfall trends as compared to traditional methods. The results reveled significant declining rainfall trends in 78–95% of the districts by ITA method, with the sharpest reduction observed in Costal Saline Zone (Medinipur-East), Vindhyan Alluvial Zone (Dinajpur-Dakshin), Northern Hill Zone (Darjeeling) and Vindhyan Alluvial Zone (Dinajpur-Dakshin) during pre-monsoon, monsoon, post-monsoon and annual period respectively. In contrast, Terai-Teesta Alluvial Zone showed consistent positive rainfall trends with district Jalpaiguri exhibited sharpest increasing rainfall trends in pre-monsoon, monsoon and annual time scale. Spatio-temporal clustering of districts using ITA derived rainfall slope reveled 5 major clusters, among which cluster 1 (covering Vindhyan Alluvial Zone, and parts of Northern Hill Zone and Costal Saline Zone) showed sharpest decreasing slope followed by cluster 5 which covers largest number of districts (11) in ACZ Gangetic Alluvial Zone and part of Undulating Red and Laterite Zone. In addition, we have furnished some appropriate elucidations of spatio-temporal rainfall variations considering recent theories and also provided the possible impact/ adaptation strategies of the same. Based on the analysis, it is clear that climatic vulnerability due to rainfall deficiency and negative rainfall trends is found to be more pronounced during winter and pre-monsoon season in SHWB than in GWB,. whereas, GWB showed a greater vulnerability during monsoon, and annual periods, with higher concentrations of rainfall deficiency and negative rainfall trends. Remarkably, both SHWB and GWB showed susceptibility to deficient rainfall during post-monsoon season, representing extensive and seasonally variable hydro-climatic stress across the state. ENSO indices, particularly Niño 3.4, ONI, MEI, and DMI, emerged as the most reliable predictors of rainfall variability in West Bengal, with stronger correlations for monsoon, post-monsoon, and annual rainfall, while winter and pre-monsoon rainfall also showed significant linkages under specific ENSO phases. The established connection of rainfall erraticism with ENSO indices (Niño 3.4, ONI, MEI, DMI) delivers a scientific base for promoting climate-informed agricultural advisories and water allocation policies. This would help in understanding regional climatology, hydrology and their impacts on various sectors based on water resources and taking region specific strategies to build climate resilience farming in the state. Overall, the study demonstrates a conversion toward spatially diverse but gradually vulnerable rainfall regimes in the state. Deciphering these intuitions into adaptive water governance, climate-smart agriculture, will be critical to building long-term climate resilience across both SHWB and GWB.
