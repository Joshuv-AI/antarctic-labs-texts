Selection of revision technique for suboptimal total cavopulmonary connection based on computational fluid dynamics: a case report
Authors: Satoshi Matsuo, Chiharu Ota, Hideki Ota, Yoshikatsu Saiki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnequal pulmonary blood flow in Fontan circulation is a known contributor to development of pulmonary arteriovenous fistula (PAVF). Introduction

Unequal bilateral pulmonary blood flow from the inferior vena cava (IVC) in the Fontan circulation is a known causative factor for the formation of pulmonary arteriovenous fistula (PAVF) [1]. We report a case with hemoptysis and desaturation resulting from PAVF due to unequal bilateral pulmonary blood flow following a total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) constructed using a pedicled autologous pericardial roll.

The use of a pedicled autologous pericardial conduit in the TCPC procedure has been reported to allow for growth of the conduit both in a horizontal and a longitudinal direction in proportion to body weight gain, providing an advantage in accommodating somatic growth [2, 3]. However, when the lateral tunnel created with a pedicled pericardium is anastomosed to the right side of the Glenn connection, there is a risk of hemodynamic imbalance in which IVC blood preferentially flows into the right pulmonary artery. In our present case, we revised the TCPC by employing the preoperative simulation of blood flow analysis using the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to achieve optimal Fontan circulation with resolution of PAVF [4].

Case report

The patient was a 23-year-old woman with a primary diagnosis of pulmonary atresia without heterotaxy syndrome or IVC interruption. She had previously undergone a pedicled pericardial extracardiac lateral tunnel TCPC at 4 years of age. Coil embolization for a left-sided PAVF was performed 11 years after TCPC due to progressive cyanosis.

Over the past year, she experienced worsening desaturation with an oxygen saturation of 80% and episodes of hemoptysis, despite the absence of Fontan failure symptoms such as cardiac dysfunction, hepatic dysfunction, or protein-losing enteropathy. Diagnostic imaging with 3-dimensional computed tomography (3D-CT) showed that the anastomosis site of the extracardiac lateral tunnel had deviated to the right side of the axis of the Glenn anastomosis (Fig. 1A). Cardiac catheterization revealed normal cardiac function, a central venous pressure of 11–12 mmHg, and a 1–2 mmHg pressure gradient across the extracardiac lateral tunnel to the pulmonary artery (PA). However, nearly total IVC blood flow was directed to the right PA, while the left PA received blood predominantly from the superior vena cava (SVC). A left-sided PAVF was also confirmed. The intrapulmonary right-to-left shunt rate (IPSR) was 48.4%, calculated by lung perfusion scintigraphy. There was no evidence of Osler’s disease, cirrhosis or trauma, suggesting that the left-sided PAVF was due to a lack of hepatic factors from the IVC blood flow [5]. Coil embolization alone was not considered as a definitive treatment in this case with recurrent hemoptysis and persistent desaturation. Therefore, TCPC revision was proposed to resolve the left-sided PAVF.

CFD methodology

Patient-specific vascular geometries were reconstructed from contrast-enhanced three-dimensional computed tomography data. Blood flow was modeled as an incompressible Newtonian fluid, which is a widely accepted assumption for large-vessel Fontan circulation. Blood density and viscosity were set to standard physiological values.

Rigid vessel walls with no-slip boundary conditions were assumed. At the inlet boundaries, time-averaged flow rates were prescribed based on cardiac catheterization and phase-contrast MRI data. Outlet boundary conditions were defined by flow-split conditions according to pulmonary flow distribution, rather than pressure-based lumped parameter models, given the comparative and patient-specific nature of this study.

Mesh independence was assessed by stepwise mesh refinement until further refinement resulted in negligible changes in key hemodynamic parameters, including energy loss and pulmonary flow distribution. Numerical convergence was confirmed when residuals of the continuity and momentum equations fell below predefined thresholds.

To determine the optimal revision strategy for the extracardiac conduit, three reconstructive models were selected to represent clinically feasible and anatomically distinct options with different surgical objectives and levels of invasiveness. These models were analyzed using CFD simulation technology (Fig. 1B, Additional file 1).

Pattern 1 involved left-sided patch augmentation of the extracardiac conduit, which consisted of an autologous pericardial anterior wall and the right atrial wall posteriorly. Enlargement of the conduit required partial incision of the right atrial wall under cardioplegic arrest, with the aim of increasing IVC flow to the left PA. This strategy preserved the overall conduit configuration and represented the most limited reconstructive option among the three strategies considered, as it did not involve complete conduit replacement.

Pattern 2 extended the concept of Pattern 1 by combining left-sided patch augmentation of the extracardiac conduit with additional patch enlargement along the SVC–to–right PA pathway. In addition to right atrial incision under cardioplegic arrest, this approach required patch augmentation from the SVC toward the right PA to improve superior caval flow distribution. The aim of this strategy was to achieve a more global redistribution of both inferior and superior caval flows to the pulmonary arteries while still avoiding complete conduit replacement.

Pattern 3 involved complete replacement of the extracardiac conduit using a prosthetic vascular graft. This approach aimed to correct conduit deviation and eliminate residual narrowing entirely, thereby achieving well-balanced IVC flow to both pulmonary arteries. Although this strategy required the most extensive reconstruction, it was considered the most definitive solution among the three options.

More extensive patch augmentation of the right-sided SVC-to-PA anastomosis was not pursued, as avoidance of right phrenic nerve palsy was considered a key factor for a successful TCPC revision in this reoperative setting.

Based on the CFD analysis, Pattern 3 demonstrated the most favorable hemodynamic characteristics, including reduced energy loss and shear stress in the pulmonary circulation (Table 1). Importantly, this approach also enabled complete conduit replacement without cardioplegic arrest. Taken together with the clinical and anatomical considerations, Pattern 3 was selected for surgical intervention.

During Fontan revision, cardiopulmonary bypass was established without aortic cross-clamping. The pedicled pericardial extracardiac lateral tunnel was longitudinally incised and partially detached at the IVC–pulmonary artery junction, preserving part of the posterior wall. The incision was extended beyond the most stenotic segment of the right pulmonary artery.

Based on the preoperatively measured diameter of her IVC, a TCPC conduit diameter of 22 mm was determined to be appropriate. The anastomosis between the undersurface of the pulmonary artery and a lingual-shaped prosthetic vascular graft was performed beyond the stenotic site of the pulmonary artery, thereby relieving the stenosis at the dorsal aspect of the ascending aorta. This was followed by anastomosis between the graft and the inferior vena cava. No additional branch pulmonary artery enlargement was performed.

The total operative time was 10 h and 41 min, and the duration of cardiopulmonary bypass was 5 h and 37 min.

The patient was extubated on the day of surgery. Intermittent mild hemoptysis was observed for several days postoperatively, which was managed conservatively and gradually resolved. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 14. Hemoptysis completely resolved within 3 months after surgery.

At 6 months after revision, postoperative evaluation including cardiac catheterization and 4D-flow MRI was performed. Quantitative 4D-flow MRI analysis demonstrated that SVC flow was distributed 40% to the right PA and 60% to the left PA, while IVC flow was distributed predominantly to the right PA (80%) with 20% directed to the left PA. Peripheral oxygen saturation (SaO₂) improved from approximately 80% preoperatively to 90% postoperatively. Her oxygen saturation improved up to 90% range by 6 months after the operation. Cardiac catheterization and 4D-flow magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed 6 months after the TCPC revision showed improved oxygen saturation and balanced bilateral pulmonary blood flow (Fig. 2, Additional file 2). The IPSR decreased to 17.4% at 6 months post-revision. She has remained asymptomatic, with her lowest oxygen saturation reading over five years of follow-up being 92%.

Discussion

Post-TCPC hemodynamics can now be assessed in detail using MRI [6]. Preoperative CFD simulations have emerged as valuable tools for surgical planning by predicting optimal configurations for cardiovascular reconstruction [4]. In our case, CFD enabled us to prospectively assess bilateral flow distribution and minimize energy loss.

Although 4D-flow MRI analysis and echocardiography vector flow mapping have been used to assess Fontan circulation [4], the CFD simulation offers more precise estimations of blood flow volumes and energy loss, providing more robust data for decision-making [4, 7].

Conversely, 4D-flow MRI provides direct in vivo flow measurements but is limited by spatial and temporal resolution. Therefore, in the present case, CFD simulation was not used as a stand-alone decision-making tool, but rather as an adjunctive method to compare multiple surgically feasible reconstruction options. Postoperative 4D-flow MRI was used to qualitatively and semi-quantitatively confirm the flow redistribution trends predicted by CFD, rather than for strict point-by-point validation.

Although postoperative 4D-flow MRI demonstrated a slightly lower proportion of IVC flow to the left PA than predicted by CFD (32% to the left PA and 68% to the right PA), the overall redistribution pattern was consistent with the simulation results. This difference was considered acceptable given the inherent modeling assumptions of CFD and the limited spatial resolution of 4D-flow MRI. It is important to emphasize that 4D-flow MRI and CFD serve complementary but fundamentally different roles in the evaluation of Fontan circulation. While 4D-flow MRI provides direct in vivo measurements of postoperative flow distribution, it cannot be used to prospectively compare alternative surgical reconstruction strategies.

Our present case was a particularly complex condition because the geometry of the lateral tunnel varies depending on cardiac position and patient body habitus, making it difficult to identify a hemodynamically optimized configuration using anatomical imaging alone, such as CT or MRI. CFD simulation made it possible to design a surgical reconstruction that achieved balanced IVC blood flow to both pulmonary arteries. Importantly, CFD was used to prospectively compare multiple clinically feasible reconstruction strategies under identical boundary conditions, rather than to justify a single predetermined surgical approach. In contrast, the presence of conduit deviation, conduit narrowing, and unbalanced pulmonary blood flow was already evident on conventional preoperative angiography, and CFD was not required to establish the diagnosis of hemodynamic imbalance.

The three reconstructive models were selected to represent clinically realistic options with different surgical invasiveness and objectives, ranging from limited patch augmentation to complete extracardiac conduit replacement. More extensive right-sided SVC patch enlargement was not pursued because of concerns regarding phrenic nerve injury in this reoperative Fontan patient. Other theoretical reconstruction options were not modeled, as they were considered either surgically impractical or associated with an unacceptably high risk relative to their expected hemodynamic benefit in this reoperative setting.

Special attention must be paid to the course of the phrenic nerve during TCPC revisions where a pedicled autologous pericardial conduit is involved. Phrenic nerve palsy can significantly impair Fontan circulation; hence, preoperative planning is essential to prevent nerve injury [8]. In our case, CFD simulation was used to develop a surgical approach that optimized hemodynamics and preserved phrenic nerve function, based on scientific evidence.

Additionally, the 3D models generated by the CFD simulation were effective tools for explaining the surgical plan to the patient and her family. Overall, CFD simulation is a powerful resource for evaluating flow-related parameters across various reconstruction models and selecting the most appropriate surgical strategy in a scientifically rigorous manner.

Recent advances in patient-specific computational fluid dynamics (CFD) have demonstrated its clinical utility in planning surgical strategies for Fontan circulation. In a cohort of Fontan patients, CFD-derived hepatic flow distribution predictions were shown to correlate with postoperative pulmonary flow measurements, supporting the value of CFD in predicting complex flow patterns that influence clinical outcomes [9]. Furthermore, prospective CFD modeling has been used to compare alternative surgical options and guide interventions aimed at improving balanced hepatic venous flow, highlighting its role in individualized surgical planning [10]. While imaging modalities such as 4D-flow MRI provide direct hemodynamic measurements, CFD can complement these by simulating multiple hypothetical reconstructions and estimating parameters—such as energy loss and flow redistribution—that are difficult to capture solely from imaging data. Recent reviews have also underscored the complementary nature of 4D-flow MRI and CFD, particularly for complex Fontan anatomies where intuitive assessment of flow distribution is limited [11]. In the present case, CFD was therefore used as an adjunctive tool to compare three clinically feasible revision strategies, rather than as a stand-alone decision-making method.

A limitation of this report is that detailed quantitative hemodynamic data, including SaO₂, IPSR, and pulmonary flow distribution, were available only at a single postoperative time point, precluding a formal longitudinal timeline analysis.

Conclusion

CFD simulation can serve as a useful adjunctive preoperative tool for comparing hemodynamic characteristics of different TCPC revision strategies, particularly in anatomically complex cases involving pedicled pericardial conduits.
