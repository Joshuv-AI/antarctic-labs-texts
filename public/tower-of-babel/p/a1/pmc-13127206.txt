Time to extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation flow and outcomes in paediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest: a 12-year single-centre study
Authors: Joram Nyandat, Robyn McClelland, Renee Mintak, Srinivas Murthy, Linda Edwards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Paediatric, In-hospital cardiac arrest, Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation, ECPR, ECLS, Survival, Timely initiation

Introduction

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has become a vital rescue therapy for selected patients with refractory in-hospital cardiac arrest, providing circulatory support and restoring end-organ perfusion until the reversible cause of the arrest is addressed.1, 2

Survival after paediatric ECPR has improved in experienced centres, but outcomes remain variable across institutions.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Differences in patient populations, resuscitation practices, and system-level readiness for rapid ECPR deployment may contribute to this variability. These factors can influence the duration of low-flow physiology during cardiac arrest and may contribute to cumulative ischemic injury and hinder organ recovery.9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Low-flow duration is typically measured from the start of cardiopulmonary resuscitation to the establishment of extracorporeal life support (ECLS) flow. However, the physiologic consequences of prolonged hypoperfusion may also be reflected by metabolic markers of global tissue hypoxia, such as elevated serum lactate levels before and after cannulation.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Serum lactate has been associated with survival and neurologic outcomes in both adult and paediatric ECLS populations.14, 15, 16 However, the independent and combined associations of time to ECLS flow and metabolic markers of perfusion with clinical outcomes in paediatric ECPR remain incompletely defined.

Multicentre and single-centre studies have reported variable associations between low-flow duration and survival following ECPR, suggesting that while prolonged low-flow time may contribute to worse outcomes in some settings, additional factors, including post-arrest physiology, likely play an important role in determining survival.5, 17, 18

Registry-based studies may lack granular physiologic data and detailed characterisation of institutional ECPR workflows. Single-centre analyses conducted within standardised systems of care can therefore provide additional insights into the relationship between cannulation timing, perfusion status, and survival outcomes in paediatric ECPR. However, relatively few studies have examined these associations within clearly characterised institutional ECPR programs, in which activation pathways, cannulation workflows, and post-resuscitation management practices are defined and have evolved over time.

Within the context of an institutional ECPR program that developed progressively over the study period, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the association between the time from initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation to establishment of ECLS flow and survival to hospital discharge in children with in-hospital cardiac arrest requiring ECPR. The secondary aim was to examine the association between metabolic markers of perfusion and survival outcomes. We hypothesised that shorter time to ECLS flow and lower lactate levels would be associated with improved survival.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included patients aged 0–17 years who experienced IHCA and received ECPR at BC Children’s Hospital between January 2010 and December 2022.

Patients were excluded if cardiac arrest occurred outside the hospital, if ECLS was initiated electively or semi-urgently without preceding cardiac arrest, or if key outcome or timing variables were unavailable.

Setting and institutional ECPR program

BC Children’s Hospital is a quaternary paediatric cardiac centre with an established ECPR program aligned with Extracorporeal Life Support Organisation (ELSO) guidelines.

At the institution, ECPR is considered for patients with witnessed cardiac arrest and absence of sustained return of spontaneous circulation after approximately 5–10 min of high-quality CPR when the treating team considers recovery potentially achievable.

Cannulation during ECPR is performed by paediatric cardiothoracic surgeons using either central or peripheral cannulation, depending on patient anatomy and clinical circumstances. Institutional operational targets aim to establish ECLS flow within approximately 60 min of ECPR activation.

During the study period, several system-level improvements were introduced to enhance coordination and timeliness of ECPR activation, including implementation of a formal activation protocol, a group pager notification system, and multidisciplinary simulation-based training to streamline team communication and procedural readiness. Data collection

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records and the institutional extracorporeal life support registry.

Extracted variables included demographic characteristics, underlying cardiac diagnosis, surgical status, arrest rhythm, cannulation type and site, timing of arrest, and prior exposure to extracorporeal life support during the same hospitalisation.

Laboratory variables included arterial blood gas measurements obtained prior to extracorporeal life support initiation and approximately four hours following cannulation, including lactate and pH levels.

Definition of variables

In-hospital cardiac arrest was defined as the requirement for chest compressions for pulselessness occurring within the hospital setting.

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation was defined as initiation of extracorporeal life support during ongoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation or within 20 min of return of spontaneous circulation.

The time of cardiopulmonary resuscitation initiation was defined as the documented time of chest compression initiation in the resuscitation record. When multiple time sources were available, timestamps were cross-checked against the institutional extracorporeal life support registry to ensure internal consistency.

Time to ECLS flow was defined as the interval from initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation to the first documented evidence of extracorporeal circulation, regardless of the initial flow rate achieved.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes included survival to ECLS decannulation, stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic), non-central nervous system bleeding, and ECLS duration. Neurologic outcomes beyond stroke were not consistently available in a standardised format for the full study period and were therefore not included as study outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as means ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Continuous and categorical variables were compared between survivors and non-survivors for both survival to decannulation and survival to hospital discharge. Between-group comparisons were made using the Student t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Associations between clinical variables and survival outcomes were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models. Separate models were constructed for survival to extracorporeal life support decannulation and survival to hospital discharge. Variables entered into multivariable models were selected based on prespecified clinical relevance and associations observed in univariable analyses.

Multiple linear regression was used to examine factors associated with time to extracorporeal life support flow. The assumption of linearity between continuous predictors and the log-odds of the outcome was evaluated using locally weighted smoothing (LOWESS) plots.

Collinearity among metabolic variables (initial lactate, 4-h lactate, and arterial pH) was assessed using correlation matrices and variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIFs < 5 indicating acceptable collinearity. These variables were retained in the multivariable models based on their prespecified clinical relevance, reflecting both pre-cannulation metabolic derangement and early post-cannulation recovery.

To assess potential temporal confounding, outcomes were compared between an early study era (2010–2015, n = 28) and a late study era (2016–2022, n = 51).

Missing laboratory data were addressed using a complete-case analysis for multivariable models that incorporated lactate and pH variables.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the University of British Columbia and BC Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Board (H22-02123), with waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

A total of 98 patients were identified as potential candidates for ECPR during the study period. After exclusion of patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 84 patients underwent ECPR. ECPR characteristics

The most common initial arrest rhythm during cardiac arrest was asystole or pulseless electrical activity (PEA), occurring in 34.2% (n = 27) of cases.

The median time from initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation to establishment of ECLS flow was 35 min (IQR 22–50).

Peripheral cannulation was used in 56.2% (n = 44) of patients, most commonly via the right internal jugular vein and right common carotid artery (38%, n = 30). Central cannulation through the right atrium and aorta was more frequent among decannulation survivors (38.7% vs. 5.9%). Approximately 13% (n = 10) of patients had undergone a prior ECLS run within the same hospitalisation.

Clinical outcomes

Survival to ECLS decannulation was 80.8% (n = 64), and survival to hospital discharge was 52.1% (n = 41).

Stroke events occurred in 4.7% (n = 4), and non-CNS bleeding in 6.8% (n = 5). The median duration of ECLS was 64.0 h (IQR 30.8–98.3), and the median hospital length of stay was 18 days (IQR 12–29).

Comparisons of clinical variables between survivors and non-survivors are presented in Table 1.

Factors associated with time to ECLS flow

Potential demographic, clinical and procedural factors associated with time to extracorporeal life support flow were evaluated. No variables, including age, weight, prior cardiac surgery, cannulation type or site, prior extracorporeal life support exposure, timing of arrest, or day of arrest, were significantly associated with time to ECLS flow (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1).

Potential effect modification of the association between pre-ECLS lactate and time to ECLS flow was evaluated by including an interaction term in the regression model. The interaction term between pre-ECLS lactate and time to ECLS flow was not statistically significant, indicating no evidence of effect modification.

Multivariable analysis of survival outcomes

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, shorter time to ECLS flow was independently associated with survival to ECLS decannulation (adjusted OR 0.96 per minute increase in time to ECLS flow, 95% CI 0.93–0.99, p = 0.008).

Higher initial lactate (adjusted OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.72–0.98, p = 0.028), higher 4-h lactate (adjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.67–0.97, p = 0.014), and lower initial pH (adjusted OR 1.34 per 0.1 unit decrease, 95% CI 1.04–1.86, p = 0.023) were independently associated with lower survival to ECLS decannulation.

For survival to hospital discharge, 4-h lactate was the only factor independently associated with outcome (adjusted OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.66–0.94, p = 0.007). Full regression results are presented in Table 2.

System evolution and temporal analysis

Over the study period, several institutional process changes were implemented to strengthen the ECPR program, including the introduction of a formal ECPR activation protocol, implementation of a group pager notification system to rapidly mobilise the ECPR team, and the incorporation of multidisciplinary simulation-based training (supplemental file 2).

The evolution of institutional ECPR processes and associated temporal trends in program outcomes are illustrated in Fig. 1. Over time, median time from cardiopulmonary resuscitation initiation to extracorporeal life support flow decreased, while survival to decannulation and survival to hospital discharge demonstrated gradual improvement. There were no statistically significant differences between eras in survival to ECLS decannulation (78.6% vs 82.4%, p = 0.76), survival to hospital discharge (50.0% vs 53.9%, p = 0.81), or median time to ECLS flow (37 vs 34 min, p = 0.43).

Discussion

In this single-centre retrospective study, we examined the relationship between time to initiation of ECPR and survival outcomes in paediatric IHCA. The median time from CPR to initiation of ECLS was 35 min. Survival to ECLS decannulation was relatively high, while survival to hospital discharge remained more modest. Shorter time to ECLS flow was independently associated with improved survival to decannulation but was not associated with survival to hospital discharge. In contrast, metabolic markers of global perfusion, particularly 4-h lactate levels, demonstrated a stronger association with survival to hospital discharge.

Our observed survival to discharge of 52.1% aligns with prior literature reporting paediatric ECPR survival in the range of 40–50%, with higher survival reported in specialised centres with established extracorporeal support programs.19, 20, 21 The high proportion of patients with congenital heart disease and postoperative status in our cohort may have enhanced survival through heightened monitoring, leading to earlier arrest recognition and rapid ECPR deployment. Similar patterns have been reported in other cardiac-predominant paediatric ECPR populations, where structured monitoring and rapid escalation pathways may facilitate earlier deployment of extracorporeal support.7, 11 Importantly, a substantial proportion of patients had single ventricle physiology, a group historically considered high risk, suggesting that extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation can be effectively deployed in this population when institutional systems and expertise are well established.1, 21

We found no patient- or event-level predictors of time to ECPR flow. This observation suggests that low-flow duration may be driven primarily by system-level factors rather than patient characteristics. Over the study period, our institution implemented several process improvements designed to enhance ECPR readiness, including formalised activation protocols, a group pager notification system to rapidly mobilise the ECPR team, and simulation-based multidisciplinary training. These interventions were introduced to reduce delays in cannulation and improve team coordination during cardiac arrest events. Although the present analysis was not designed to formally evaluate the causal impact of these interventions, they reflect the type of system-level optimisation increasingly emphasised in resuscitation science as a strategy to improve performance during high-acuity events.4, 10, 16 These findings reinforce the concept that ECPR performance is fundamentally a systems-of-care problem, in which team activation efficiency, predefined workflows, and multidisciplinary training may play a greater role in minimising low-flow duration than individual patient characteristics.

Although shorter time to ECLS flow was associated with survival to decannulation, this relationship did not extend to survival to hospital discharge. This finding suggests that while timely initiation of extracorporeal support may facilitate early haemodynamic stabilisation, it may not fully capture the downstream determinants of meaningful recovery. Nearly half of the patients who survived to decannulation died before discharge, highlighting the distinction between early physiologic stabilisation and sustained recovery.

In contrast, metabolic markers, particularly 4-h lactate, demonstrated a stronger and more consistent association with survival to hospital discharge. From a physiologic perspective, early lactate trajectories likely reflect both the severity of pre-arrest hypoperfusion and the adequacy of reperfusion following ECPR initiation. These findings support the concept that post-arrest physiology, rather than timing alone, may be a key determinant of ultimate outcomes. Accordingly, time to ECLS flow and early metabolic recovery should be viewed as complementary constructs that reflect different but interrelated dimensions of resuscitation effectiveness.3, 16

In multivariable analysis, persistently elevated lactate and lower initial pH were associated with reduced survival to decannulation, and 4-h lactate remained independently associated with survival to hospital discharge. From a physiologic perspective, early lactate clearance likely reflects both the adequacy of systemic perfusion achieved after ECPR initiation and the degree of reversible ischemic injury sustained during the low-flow period. As such, early metabolic trajectories may serve as a useful adjunct to traditional timing metrics for evaluating resuscitation effectiveness and guiding post-ECPR management.8, 12, 14, 15

Although our study spanned more than a decade during which institutional ECPR practices evolved, comparisons between early and later program eras did not demonstrate statistically significant differences in time to ECLS flow or survival outcomes. Several factors may explain this finding, including the relatively small sample size and limited statistical power for temporal comparisons. In addition, incremental improvements in team coordination, resuscitation quality, and post-ECLS management may be difficult to detect in retrospective analyses. Nevertheless, the longitudinal evolution of institutional protocols and training programs highlights the importance of sustained system-level development in establishing and maintaining effective ECPR programs. These observations suggest that institutional readiness, including standardised activation pathways, coordinated team communication, and simulation-based training, may be critical determinants of ECPR performance and represent modifiable system-level targets for centres seeking to improve outcomes in paediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-centre design may limit generalisability to institutions with different patient populations or ECPR infrastructures. Second, the modest sample size constrained statistical power for detecting interactions and limited the number of variables that could be included in multivariable models. Third, detailed neurologic and functional outcome measures were not consistently available across the study period, precluding assessment of long-term neurologic recovery. Fourth, documentation variability inherent in retrospective data collection may introduce minor inaccuracies in recorded timing metrics. Fifth, our primary exposure variable was time to any ECLS flow, rather than time to achieving adequate flow, which may represent a more physiologically relevant threshold for restoring systemic perfusion. Future studies should aim to capture both time-to-flow and time-to-adequate-flow to better delineate the determinants of post-ECPR outcomes. Finally, as with most observational studies of ECPR, residual confounding by indication cannot be entirely excluded, as clinical decision-making during resuscitation may influence which patients receive earlier cannulation.

Conclusion

In paediatric IHCA requiring ECPR, shorter time to ECLS flow was associated with improved survival to decannulation but not with survival to hospital discharge. However, early metabolic recovery was associated with survival to hospital discharge. These findings highlight the complementary roles of system readiness in minimising low-flow duration and early physiologic recovery in determining survival after ECPR. Future multicentre studies incorporating metabolic trajectories alongside neurologic and functional outcomes will be important for better defining the determinants of meaningful recovery after paediatric ECPR.
