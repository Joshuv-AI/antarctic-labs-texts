Isolable Cysteine Sulfenyl Iodide: Stabilization by a Molecular Cradle, Crystal Structure, and Biologically Relevant Reactivity
Authors: Shotaro Otaka, Tsukasa Sano, Shohei Sase, Satoru Kuwano, Kei Goto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cysteine sulfenyl iodides (Cys–SIs) have long been recognized as important intermediates in the oxidative modification of cysteine thiols since 1950s, implicated in pathways such as hydrolysis to cysteine sulfenic acid (Cys–SOH) and electrophilic aromatic substitution with an indole ring of tryptophan to form Cys–Trp thioether linkages. Despite their proposed significance in both biological and chemical contexts, direct examination of Cys–SIs has been precluded by their intrinsic instability. Herein, the first isolation of a small‐molecule Cys–SI, stabilized using a nanosized molecular cradle at the N‐terminus of cysteine, is reported. The structure of the isolable Cys–SI is determined by X‐ray crystallography, and its reactivity is investigated with a range of nucleophiles. Hydrolysis to Cys–SOH and reaction with an indole derivative provide direct chemical evidence for long‐standing mechanistic proposals. Furthermore, rapid formation of sulfenamides with amines and high‐yield adduct formation with dimedone, a canonical sulfenic‐acid probe, reveal that Cys–SI possesses even greater electrophilicity than Cys–SOH. These results deliver a structurally defined reference for Cys–SI and inform mechanisms of iodine‐mediated protein oxidation and peptide modification.

Introduction

Cysteine sulfenyl iodides (Cys–SIs) have long attracted attention as important intermediates in the oxidative modification. In 1955, Fraenkel‐Conrat first demonstrated that the single cysteine residue of tobacco mosaic virus coat protein is converted into a stable Cys–SI upon treatment with molecular iodine, as evidenced by reaction stoichiometry, incorporation of radioiodine, and reversibility experiments.[ 1 ] Subsequent studies in the 1960s and 1970s provided further experimental support for the formation of Cys–SI species in proteins.[ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 – 7 ] Cunningham and co‐workers showed that β‐lactoglobulin affords a comparatively stable Cys–SI after iodination,[ 2 , 3 ] while Trundle and Cunningham reported that iodination‐induced inactivation of creatine kinase is fully reversed by thiol reduction, suggesting that the initial formation of Cys–SI was followed by hydrolysis to cysteine sulfenic acid (Cys–SOH) (Figure 1A), which then underwent disulfide formation.[ 5 ] Parker and Allison examined the oxidation of the active site cysteine in glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) by iodine monochloride and obtained results, suggesting that Cys–SI is initially formed and subsequently hydrolyzed to Cys–SOH.[ 6 ] Similarly, Thomas and Aune demonstrated that, in the peroxidase‐catalyzed oxidation of protein sulfhydryls by iodine, Cys–SI acts as a crucial intermediate and hydrolyzes to yield Cys–SOH.[ 7 ] In the 1990s, Cys–SI formation was further suggested for α2‐macroglobulin oxidized by I3 –[ 8 ] and for human serum albumin treated with Pt(IV)–iodide anticancer agents,[ 9 ] both cases again pointing to subsequent hydrolysis to Cys–SOH.

B) Our previous works: stabilization of cysteine‐ or selenocysteine‐derived reactive intermediates. C) This work: isolation of Cys–SI stabilized by the molecular cradle and direct examination of the characteristic reactivities.

In addition to cysteine oxidation, Cys–SI species are proposed to be generated during the iodine‐mediated deprotection of trityl‐protected cysteine residues, a transformation known since the 1980s.[ 10 ] The electrophilic reaction of the resulting Cys–SI with an indole ring of tryptophan residues forms a thioether linkage between cysteine and tryptophan residues (Figure 1A).[ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 – 15 ] The Cys–Trp linkage formation has been applied to the nonsymmetric stapling of peptides as well as the synthesis of peptide‐derived natural products and their functionalized derivatives.

Despite their biochemical relevance, reports of Cys–SI formation have so far relied exclusively on indirect evidence; direct spectroscopic observation has remained elusive both in proteins and in small‐molecule systems. This is largely due to the intrinsic instability of sulfenyl iodides, which disproportionate rapidly to disulfides and iodine (Figure 1A).[ 16 , 17 ] In the field of organosulfur chemistry, a variety of noncysteinyl sulfenyl iodides have been synthesized since Field's seminal work,[ 18 ] by employing either kinetic[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 – 24 ] or thermodynamic[ 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 – 29 ] stabilization strategies. We have also reported the synthesis of several isolable sulfenyl iodides stabilized by a cavity‐shaped substituent,[ 20 , 22 , 23 ] including the primary‐alkyl‐substituted derivative, BpqCH2SI.[ 23 ] However, their structural and electronic properties differ from those of cysteine‐derived sulfenyl iodides. Thus, the development of a stable reference compound bearing a cysteine backbone remains essential for a deeper understanding of the chemical behavior of Cys–SI in biological systems.

As a chemical tool to stabilize such biologically relevant reactive intermediates, we have developed a nanosized molecular cradle (henceforth denoted as Bpsc) designed to accommodate a reactive amino acid residue when installed as an N‐terminal protecting group (Figure 1B).[ 30 ] Using this cradle strategy, we have succeeded in the syntheses of various cysteine‐ and selenocysteine‐derived reactive species,[ 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 – 37 ] including an isolable Cys–SOH.[ 30 , 34 ] Here, we report the first isolation of a small‐molecule Cys–SI stabilized by the molecular cradle, along with its crystal structure and characteristic reactivities (Figure 1C).

Results and Discussion

We previously reported the synthesis of noncysteinyl sulfenyl iodides by oxidation of a thiol with N‐iodosuccinimide (NIS)[ 23 ] or with iodine[ 20 , 22 ] in the presence of amine. When Cys–SH 1 bearing a Bpsc group was treated with NIS (1.5 eq) in CCl4 at room temperature, the corresponding Cys–SI 2 was formed almost quantitatively, which was isolated in 98% yield as red‐brown crystalline solids (Scheme 1A), presenting the first example of the synthesis and isolation of a small‐molecule Cys–SI. As a model reaction of iodine oxidation of protein thiols, we also examined the reaction of 1 with iodine. Upon treatment of 1 with iodine (1.1 eq) in the presence of triethylamine in CDCl3 at room temperature, the immediate formation of 2 in 97% NMR yield was observed in the 1H NMR spectrum (Scheme 1B and Figure S1, Supporting Information). It was found that 2 can be handled in air under standard laboratory conditions. Characterization of 2 was performed by NMR (1H and 13C), IR, and UV–vis spectroscopies, as well as elemental analysis. The UV–vis spectrum (CCl4, 303 K) of 2 exhibited an absorption maximum at 455 nm, which is similar to that of the BpqCH2SI (464 nm).[ 23 ]

Synthesis of Cys–SI 2. A) Reaction of Cys–SH 1 with NIS. B) Reaction of Cys–SH 1 with iodine in the presence of triethylamine.

The structure of 2 was finally established by X‐ray crystallographic analysis by using a single crystal of 2 grown in their CHCl3‐Et2O solution (Figure 2 and Figure S4, Supporting Information).[ 38 ] The cysteine unit of 2 is anchored in a large cradle produced by the Bpsc group. The sulfur–iodine bond length of 2 is 2.378(3) Å, which is similar to that of BpqCH2SI (2.3743(17) Å).[ 23 ]

Crystal structure of Cys–SI 2 (hydrogen atoms of the Bpsc group are omitted for clarity). Selected bond lengths (Å), bond angle (deg), and torsion angle (deg): S(1)–I(1) 2.378(3); C(1)–S(1) 1.783(9); C(1)–S(1)–I(1) 102.1(3); I(1)–S(1)–C(1)–C(2): 68.1(6).

Cys–SI 2 exhibited remarkable thermal stability both in the solid state and in solution. In the solid state, 2 melted at 251.5–253.0 °C. In solution, no detectable changes were observed in the 1H NMR spectrum of 2 in CDCl3 at 50 °C over 24 h (Scheme S1 and Figure S2, Supporting Information). These results indicate that the peripheral steric protection provided by the Bpsc cradle is highly effective in suppressing the disproportionation of Cys–SI.

The formation of Cys–SI in proteins has long been envisaged to be followed in situ by rapid hydrolysis to Cys–SOH.[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 – 9 ] Experimental verification of the transformation from Cys–SI to Cys–SOH, which are two sulfur species in the same oxidation state, would furnish decisive chemical evidence for the iodination‐mediated oxidation of protein thiols to protein sulfenic acids. To date, however, no direct observation of the hydrolytic conversion of a sulfenyl iodide, cysteinyl or otherwise, into its sulfenic‐acid counterpart has been reported, owing to the extreme lability of both species. Having obtained isolable Cys–SI and Cys–SOH[ 30 ] derivatives by means of the Bpsc molecular cradle, we set out to model and examine this elusive transformation. When Cys–SI 2 was treated with aqueous NaOH in THF at room temperature, the red‐brown solution turned colorless within 10 s. Work‐up and purification afforded the corresponding Cys–SOH 3 as colorless solids in 79% isolated yield (Scheme 2 ). These findings provide chemical corroboration for the long‐standing proposal that, when a cysteine residue exists in a sterically protected microenvironment free from bimolecular decomposition of highly reactive intermediates (Figure S5, Supporting Information), it can be oxidized to a sulfenic acid by iodine via hydrolysis of an initially formed sulfenyl iodide. In contrast, when 2 was treated with water in place of aqueous NaOH under otherwise identical conditions, almost no detectable change was observed in the 1H NMR spectrum (No‐D NMR) over 1 h (Scheme S2 and Figure S3, Supporting Information), indicating that basic conditions facilitate the hydrolysis of Cys–SI.

Hydrolysis of Cys–SI 2 to Cys–SOH 3 by aqueous NaOH.

It is well established that Cys–SOH reacts with amines to form the corresponding sulfenamides.[ 39 ] Given that Cys–SI has the same formal oxidation state as Cys–SOH, it is reasonable to postulate that Cys–SI would also undergo a similar transformation. Indeed, we previously demonstrated that the noncysteinyl sulfenyl iodide, BpqCH2SI, reacts with benzylamine to afford the corresponding sulfenamide.[ 23 ] However, to date, no unambiguous example of a direct reaction between Cys–SI and an amine has been reported. Treatment of Cys–SI 2 with benzylamine (4) in CH2Cl2 at room temperature resulted in the immediate formation of the corresponding sulfenamide 5, which was isolated as colorless crystalline solids in 96% yield (Scheme 3A). The reactivity of 2 toward the side chain of lysine (Lys) monohydrochloride derivative 6 was likewise examined in the presence of triethylamine, affording sulfenamide 7 rapidly in 74% isolated yield (Scheme 3B). These results suggest that iodine‐mediated oxidation of protein thiols can give rise to an S–N linkage via a sulfenyl iodide intermediate.

Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with amines to form the corresponding sulfenamides. A) Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with benzylamine (4). B) Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with lysine monohydrochloride derivative 6 in the presence of triethylamine.

In addition to oxidative modification of protein thiols, Cys–SI has also been implicated in a distinct chemical transformation that forms a Cys‐to‐Trp side‐chain thioether bridge.[ 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 – 15 ] In this process, iodine‐mediated deprotection of trityl‐protected cysteine residues transiently generates Cys–SI, whose electrophilic sulfur then undergoes aromatic substitution at the nucleophilic C‐2 position of a tryptophan indole ring to furnish the thioether linkage. This transformation has been exploited for nonsymmetric peptide stapling[ 15 ] and for the synthesis of fluorescently labeled phalloidin analogues[ 12 ] as well as amanitins[ 13 ] and related compounds. To date, however, the proposed involvement of Cys–SI in such transformations has never been experimentally confirmed, nor has the reactivity of a sulfenyl iodide toward indole derivatives been evaluated in a model study. We therefore investigated the reaction of the isolable Cys–SI 2 with skatole (8) as a model for the tryptophan side chain. When 2 was treated with 8 (10 eq) in the presence of 2,6‐lutidine at room temperature for 10 h, the corresponding thioether 9 was obtained in 93% isolated yield as colorless solids (Scheme 4 ). This result unequivocally demonstrates that Cys–SI exhibits pronounced electrophilic reactivity toward carbon‐based nucleophiles such indole rings. The concept that conversion of a cysteine residue from a highly nucleophilic Cys–SH to a highly electrophilic Cys–SI species, via trityl protection and subsequent iodination, constitutes an umpolung of chemical polarity has been proposed previously.[ 14 ] The present study provides direct experimental support for this concept by showing that Cys–SI can indeed undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution with an indole derivative in high yield.

Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with skatole (8) in the presence of 2,6‐lutidine.

To evaluate the electrophilicity of Cys–SI toward carbon‐based nucleophiles, we compared the reactivity of Cys–SI and Cys–SOH toward dimedone (10), a representative nucleophilic chemical probe widely used for trapping and detecting sulfenic acids.[ 39 , 40 – 41 ] We previously reported that the reaction of isolable Cys–SOH 3 with 10 proceeds smoothly in the presence of a tertiary amine base.[ 30 ] Specifically, treatment of 3 with 10 in C6D6 in the presence of triethylamine at room temperature afforded the corresponding adduct 11 in 80% NMR yield after 132 min (Scheme 5A). Under identical conditions, however, Cys–SI 2 reacted with 10 far more rapidly: the characteristic red‐brown color of 2 disappeared within 1 min, and NMR analysis performed 7 min after mixing revealed the formation of 11 in 98% NMR yield (Scheme 5B). Although Cys–SOH is itself recognized as a highly electrophilic intermediate, these results clearly demonstrate that Cys–SI possesses significantly greater electrophilicity toward a carbon‐based nucleophile under comparable conditions.

Reaction of Cys‐derived reactive intermediates with dimedone (10) in the presence of triethylamine. A) Reaction of Cys–SOH 3 with 10 (our previous work[ 30 ]). B) Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with 10.

Conclusion

We have achieved the first isolation of a small‐molecule cysteine sulfenyl iodide (Cys–SI) using a nanosized molecular cradle, enabling direct structural and reactivity studies of this long‐hypothesized intermediate in cysteine thiol oxidation. X‐ray crystallography confirmed its structure, and systematic reactivity assays demonstrated its pronounced electrophilic character. Hydrolysis to cysteine sulfenic acid (Cys–SOH) and electrophilic aromatic substitution with an indole derivative provided direct experimental validation of mechanistic proposals previously supported only by indirect evidence. Furthermore, efficient trapping by dimedone demonstrated that Cys–SI exhibits even greater electrophilicity than Cys–SOH, consistent with its proposed role as a transient intermediate in iodine‐mediated protein modification. These findings not only establish Cys–SI as a chemically accessible, structurally well‐defined species but also offer new insights into its potential functions in biologically relevant transformations.

Experimental Section

General

Unless otherwise stated, all reactions were carried out under argon atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques. THF (anhydrous) and Et2O (anhydrous) were purchased from Kanto Chemical and passed through a Kayama Oxygen solvent purification system prior to use. CDCl3 was passed through a small column of neutral alumina prior to use or distilled over CaCl2 and then dried over 4ÅMS. C6D6 was distilled over CaH2 and then dried over 4ÅMS. Et3N was purchased from commercial sources and distilled over KOH. Other chemicals were purchased from commercial sources and used as received. Silica gel column chromatography was performed using Kanto silica gel N60. Preparative thin layer chromatography was performed using Merck silica gel 60 PF254. NMR yields were determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy using bis(trimethylsilyl)methane (BTMSM) as an internal standard.

Synthesis of Cys–SI 2 by the Reaction with N‐Iodosuccinimide

Cys–SH 1 (400 mg, 202 μmol), prepared according to the procedure we previously reported,[ 30 ] was placed in a 25 mL Schlenk tube. CCl4 (17 mL) was added, followed by N‐iodosuccinimide (68.4 mg, 304 μmol) at ambient temperature. The mixture was stirred in the dark at ambient temperature for 30 min and then filtered through Celite. The filtrate was evaporated in vacuo and the crude product was reprecipitated from CHCl3‐CH3CN to afford Cys–SI 2 (415 mg, 197 μmol, 98% isolated yield) as red‐brown crystalline solids.

Synthesis of Cys–SI 2 by the Reaction with Iodine

Cys–SH 1 (10.1 mg, 5.10 μmol) was placed in a 5 mm o/d NMR tube equipped with a J‐young valve. After evacuation and backfilling with argon, a CDCl3 solution of BTMSM (2.77 mM, 1.44 mL, 3.98 μmol) and then a CDCl3 solution of Et3N (4.15 mM, 1.44 mL, 5.95 μmol) were added at ambient temperature. The tube was sealed and the 1H NMR spectrum was recorded. A CDCl3 solution of iodine (13.0 mM, 0.440 mL, 5.71 μmol) was then added, and the reaction was monitored by 1H NMR spectroscopy at ambient temperature. After 9 min, the signals corresponding to Cys–SH 1 had completely disappeared and those of Cys–SI 2 were observed. The NMR yield of Cys–SI 2 was determined to be 97% using BTMSM as an internal standard.

Hydrolysis of Cys–SI 2 with Aqueous NaOH

A solution of Cys–SI 2 (100 mg, 47.6 μmol) in THF (20 mL) was prepared in a 50 mL Schlenk tube, and a solution of NaOH (21.5 mg, 536 μmol) in H2O (2.0 mL) was prepared in a 10 mL J‐young tube. The aqueous solution of NaOH was added to the THF solution of 2 at ambient temperature. After stirring at ambient temperature for 10 s, the solution color changed from red‐brown to colorless, and the reaction was quenched with a 10% aqueous NH4Cl solution (1.0 mL). The organic layer was transferred to another Schlenk tube using a Teflon tube, dried over MgSO4, and filtered through Celite on a glass filter under argon. The solvent was evaporated in vacuo, and the crude product was purified by silica gel column chromatography (CHCl3/hexane = 1:2 to 1:1) to afford Cys–SOH 3 [ 30 ] (74.8 mg, 37.5 μmol, 79% isolated yield) as colorless solids.

Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with Benzylamine (4)

A solution of Cys–SI 2 (20.0 mg, 9.52 μmol) in CH2Cl2 (1.0 mL) was prepared in a 10 mL Schlenk tube, and benzylamine (4) (3.1 μL, 28 μmol) was then added at ambient temperature. After stirring at ambient temperature for 1 min, the solution color changed from red‐brown to colorless, and a 10% aqueous NH4Cl solution was added to quench the reaction. The two layers were separated, and the aqueous layer was extracted with CHCl3 3 times. The combined organic layer was washed with brine and dried over MgSO4. After evaporation in vacuo, the crude product was reprecipitated from CHCl3CH3CN to afford the corresponding sulfenamide 5 (19.0 mg, 9.10 μmol, 96% isolated yield) as colorless crystalline solids.

Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with N‐α‐Benzyloxycarbonyl‐L‐Lysine Methyl Ester Monohydrochloride (6)

A solution of N‐α‐benzyloxycarbonyl‐L‐lysine (52.1 mg, 186 μmol) in methanol (1.0 mL) was placed in a 10 mL Schlenk tube, and thionyl chloride (30 μL, 0.42 mmol) was added at 0 °C according to the reported procedure.[ 42 , 43 ] After stirring at ambient temperature for 22 h, the solvent and residual thionyl chloride were removed under reduced pressure. Diethyl ether (5 × 2 mL) was added to the resulting residue, and the solvent was again evaporated in vacuo twice. The residue thus obtained was N‐α‐benzyloxycarbonyl‐L‐lysine methyl ester monohydrochloride (6), to which CH2Cl2 (3.25 mL) and triethylamine (31.0 μL, 221 μmol) were added. An aliquot (1.00 mL) of this solution was transferred to a solution of Cys–SI 2 (40.2 mg, 19.1 μmol) in CH2Cl2 (2.5 mL) in a 10 mL Schlenk tube. After stirring at ambient temperature for 3 min, the solution color changed from red‐brown to colorless, and a 10% aqueous NH4Cl solution (10 mL) was added to quench the reaction. The layers were separated, and the aqueous layer was extracted with CH2Cl2 3 times. The combined organic layer was washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by preparative thin‐layer chromatography (SiO2, CHCl3/hexane = 3:1) to afford the corresponding sulfenamide 7 (31.9 mg, 14.1 μmol, 74% isolated yield) as colorless solids.

Reaction of Cys–SI 2 with Skatole (8)

A solution of Cys–SI 2 (10.5 mg, 5.00 μmol) in CH2Cl2 (0.50 mL) was prepared in a 10 mL Schlenk tube, and then 2,6‐lutidine (1.7 μL, 15 μmol) and skatole (8) (6.53 mg, 49.8 μmol) were added sequentially at ambient temperature. After stirring at ambient temperature for 10 h, the solution color changed from red‐brown to colorless, and a 10% aqueous NH4Cl solution (10 mL) was added to quench the reaction. The layers were separated, and the aqueous layer was extracted with CHCl3 3 times. The combined organic layer was washed with brine and dried over MgSO4. After evaporation in vacuo, the crude product was purified by preparative thin‐layer chromatography (CHCl3/hexane = 1:1) to afford the corresponding thioether 9 (9.79 mg, 4.65 μmol, 93% isolated yield) as colorless solids.

Reaction of Cys–SI 2 or Cys–SOH 3 with Dimedone (10)

Cys–SI 2 or Cys–SOH 3 (5.42 μmol) was placed in a 5 mm o/d NMR tube equipped with a J‐young valve. After evacuation and backfilling with argon, C6D6 (0.5 mL), dimedone (10) (2.3 mg, 16 μmol), and triethylamine (2.3 μL, 16 μmol) were added at ambient temperature, and the tube was sealed. The mixture was kept at ambient temperature, and 1H NMR spectra were recorded over time. Concentrations of the components were determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy using BTMSM as an internal standard. For Cys–SOH 3, 80% conversion of 3 and formation of 11 in 80% NMR yield were observed after 132 min.[ 30 ] In contrast, Cys–SI 2 underwent almost quantitative conversion to 11 in 98% NMR yield within 7 min.
