Additional Dose Reduction Potential of Vendor-Agnostic Deep Learning Model: A Phantom Study
Authors: Jisu Kim, Won Chang, Jong Hyo Kim, Chulkyun Ahn, Heejin Lee, Jungheum Cho, Yoon Jin Lee, Young Hoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the additional dose reduction potential (DRP) of a vendor-agnostic deep learning model (DLM, ClariCT.AI) applied to iterative reconstruction (IR) methods from two CT vendors.Materials and MethodsCT images of a multi-sized image quality phantom (Mercury v4.0) were obtained using two CT scanners from different vendors at six dose levels: 6%, 12%, 25%, 50%, 100%, and 200% of the reference dose (computerized tomography dose index volume; CTDIvol = 12 mGy). Images were reconstructed using three IR methods (ADMIRE, iDose4, IMR) at two strength levels and then denoised using the DLM. The detectability index (d’) was measured using a 31 cm phantom across combinations of three target sizes (10, 5, 1 mm), five contrast levels (-895 to 1,000 Hounsfield unit), and six dose levels. DRP was defined as the percentage reduction in dose at which DLM-enhanced images achieved d′ equivalent to that of reference-dose IR images.ResultsMean DRPs were 93.2% for ADMIRE, 90.0% for iDose4, and 87.2% for IMR. Higher DRPs were observed for higher-contrast targets. Improvements in CNR was also noted in DLM-applied images.ConclusionApplying a vendor-agnostic DLM to IR images enabled substantial radiation dose reduction while preserving image quality across different CT systems.

INTRODUCTION

CT is widely used as a medical imaging test for diagnosis, screening, and evaluation of treatment response (1). However, CT delivers higher ionizing radiation than other X-ray imaging techniques, increasing the risk of radiation-induced cancer (123). Efforts to reduce radiation dose while preserving image quality have led to various technical advancements. For example, iterative reconstruction (IR) reduces image noise and streak artifacts and requires a lower radiation dose than filtered back projection (FBP) (45). Owing to these advantages, IR has been installed and used in various legacy devices since its commercialization (6).

Recently, new techniques based on deep learning algorithms have been introduced in medical imaging, and CT vendors have developed their own deep learning reconstruction (DLR) techniques (789); including TrueFidelity CT (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA), Advanced Intelligent Clear-IQ Engine (AiCE, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan), and Precise Imaging (Philips, Best, the Netherlands) (101112). These DLR techniques provide superior image quality compared to traditional IR methods and allow further radiation dose reduction (131415).

However, DLR techniques are vendor-specific and are only available on some of the latest machines. To address this limitation, vendor-agnostic deep learning models (DLMs) such as ClariCT.AI (ClariPi Inc., Seoul, Korea) have been introduced (16). These models are designed to work with images from different CT systems without requiring access to raw sinogram data. The model was pretrained using large datasets of noise-added CT images, aiming to restore the original high-quality image (1718). Several phantom and clinical studies have reported favorable outcomes using this algorithm (192021). In addition, recent studies have highlighted the potential of deep-learning-based post-hoc denoising networks to reduce image noise and enhance diagnostic performance (2223).

IR has been employed in CT examinations of various anatomical regions, enabling significant dose reduction (2425). Although DLM can also be applied to FBP images for further dose reduction, it remains unclear whether DLM and IR can be used together effectively. Several studies have demonstrated the potential of combining IR with DLMs to achieve additional noise reduction and improved diagnostic performance in various clinical settings, including coronary CT angiography, abdominal CT, chest CT, pelvic CT, and facial bone CT (2627282930). However, no previous studies have compared DLM-applied images to different IR methods from different CT vendors. This study aims to evaluate the additional dose reduction potential (DRP) of ClariCT.AI when applied to IR-reconstructed images from two CT vendors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

As the primary outcome, we adopted the DRP, defined as the ratio of the radiation dose that can be further reduced by applying the DLM to IR images (31).

To evaluate the DRP of the DLM, we applied ClariCT.AI (version 1.2.2) to CT images reconstructed using three IR techniques from two vendors. Traditional image quality metrics, such as the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), are not suitable for modern CT systems equipped with nonlinear IR techniques, which result in object-dependent resolution and noise performance (323334). Therefore, a new test guideline, Task Group 233 (TG-233), was proposed by the American Association of Physicists in Medicine. This guideline recommends using the detectability index, a task-based detection performance metric that reflects clinical performance in detecting lesions (33).

DATA ACQUISITION AND CT SCANNING PROTOCOL

We used an image quality phantom (Mercury v4.0 phantom, GAMMEX Sun Nuclear Corp., Middleton, WI, USA) designed to measure the metrics recommended by the TG-233 guidelines. This cylindrical phantom has a total length of 52 cm and consists of five sections with different diameters (36, 31, 26, 21, and 16 cm), each containing five inlets with different Hounsfield unit (HU) values. The background material is ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene, which has a nominal CT value of -90 HU. The inlets are: -985 HU (air), -40 HU (polystyrene), 0 HU (solid water), 245 HU (iodine), and 910 HU (bone) at 120 kVp. The CT value differences with the background are -895, 50, 90, 335, and 1,000 HU, respectively. The contrast values mentioned in this study represent these differences.

As required by TG-233, images were acquired at various radiation doses and reconstruction strengths (33). CT images were acquired using an iCT 256 (Philips) and a SOMATOM Force (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), both equipped with vendor-specific IR methods. Scans were performed at six radiation dose levels: 6%, 12%, 25%, 50%, 100%, and 200% of the reference dose, at a tube voltage of 120 kVp (Table 1) (33). The reference dose level was set to 12 mGy, in line with the diagnostic reference level (DRL) for abdominopelvic CT with a 30–35 cm diameter in the United States (35). Results were calculated using only the 31-cm-diameter section of the phantom. To ensure reliability, 10 repeated scans were performed at each dose level, and these were averaged to produce a single image per dose level (Fig. 1B), thereby reducing image noise and enabling accurate metric calculations.

The acquired phantom images were reconstructed using three IR methods from two vendors: Advanced Modeled Iterative Reconstruction (ADMIRE, Siemens Healthineers), iDose4 (Philips), and Iterative Model Reconstruction (IMR, Philips). IR strength settings were selected according to TG-233 guideline recommendations, which suggest evaluating medium, higher-than-medium, and maximum strengths. Accordingly, we used ADMIRE levels 3 (medium) and 5 (high), iDose4 levels 4 (medium) and 6 (high), and IMR levels 1 (medium) and 3 (high) (33). The body kernel was used for reconstruction: Br40 for ADMIRE, and the B filter for iDose4 and IMR (Table 1).

We then applied the vendor-agnostic DLM (ClariCT.AI) in enhanced denoising mode, an image-based CT denoising tool based on a CNN, to the IR images. Finally, we evaluated the DRP by comparing the DLM-applied IR images to the original IR images (Fig. 1A). Fig. 2 shows sample images before and after applying the DLM.

TASK-BASED IMAGE QUALITY ASSESSMENT: DETECTABILITY INDEX, AND DOSE-REDUCTION POTENTIAL

We evaluated the performance of DLM-applied IR (D-IR: D-ADMIRE, D-iDose4, and D-IMR) by obtaining the DRP as the primary outcome. As described above, DRP was defined as the ratio of the radiation dose that could be reduced using the DLM compared with the original IR images. We compared D-IR images at each dose level with the IR images at the reference dose (35).

To calculate the DRP, we obtained the detectability index (d′, a task-based detection performance metric that varies with task, observer, and image (33). An increase in d’ indicates that the target object is easier to detect.

A task-based transfer function (TTF) and noise power spectrum (NPS) are required to determine d’ (31). The TTF represents the spatial resolution of the CT image, where the CNR should ideally reach 15 to ensure reliable TTF measurements (33). For low-contrast inlets with high image noise, this minimum CNR may not be achievable in a single slice. Therefore, multiple slices were averaged to reduce noise and increase CNR. The spatial frequency at which the TTF reached 50% was defined as TTF50% and represented system resolution under the given conditions (33). Changes in TTF50% relative to the reference dose were measured with respect to object and radiation dose. The NPS is a comprehensive metric of noise characteristics, with its curve representing the variance in image intensity over different spatial frequencies (36).

We used dedicated software (ImQuest, Duke University, Durham, NC, USA) to analyze images and derive the required metrics (37). We obtained d’ values for IR and D-IR images under various combinations of the five inlets, six radiation dose levels, and three target sizes (10, 5, 1 mm), using the designer’s profile and one phantom diameter (31 cm). Linear interpolation or extrapolation was performed between detectability values and dose levels. In this way, we determined the dose at which the d’ of D-IR equaled the d’ of IR at the reference dose.

For example, the d’ of polystyrene is 43 in the original image obtained with ADMIRE 3 at 12 mGy, and 40 when DLM is applied to ADMIRE 3 at 0.7 mGy. By performing linear interpolation and extrapolation between detectability values and dose levels, we determined that the D-ADMIRE 3 dose level achieving the same d’ as the reference dose was approximately 0.7 mGy. Using this method, we calculated the DRP as (12 mGy − 0.7 mGy)/12 mGy, which equals approximately 93%.

INCREMENTS OF CNR AND TTF

To quantify image changes after DLM application, increments in CNR and TTF were calculated. We obtained CNR and TTF from D-IR at each dose and inlet, and calculated increments relative to IR at 12 mGy (100%) as a reference. CNR and TTF were measured using ImQuest software (Duke University), which automatically derived these metrics from the phantom images. Increments in CNR and TTF were calculated by comparing DLM-applied IR with the original IR images at the same dose levels and at the reference dose of 12 mGy.

The raw data for all IR, D-IR, and FBP images at the reference dose of 12 mGy are provided in the Supplementary Material. For comparison, FBP images at the reference dose were also included, providing a baseline for evaluating the performance of both IR and D-IR images. Additionally, the Supplementary Material includes CNR, TTF, and d’ values for IR, D-IR, and FBP images at the reference dose of 12 mGy.

RESULTS

The original and DLM-applied IR images are shown in Fig. 2. Image quality improved notably after application of the DLM.

DOSE-REDUCTION POTENTIAL

The radiation dose could be reduced by approximately 90% when the DLM was applied to IR images. The mean DRPs were 93.2% (88.5%–97.4%) for D-ADMIRE, 90.0% (87.1%–93.1%) for D-iDose4, and 87.2% (81.0%–94.6%) for D-IMR. For the specific IR methods, the mean DRPs were as follows: D-ADMIRE 3, 92.9%; D-ADMIRE 5, 93.6%; D-iDose4 4, 89.8%; D-iDose4 6, 90.2%; D-IMR 1, 89.5%; and D-IMR 3, 84.9% (Table 2). Among the reconstruction types, D-ADMIRE and D-iDose4 showed similar DRP values regardless of strength, but D-IMR 3 had a lower DRP than D-IMR 1.

By inlet type, the mean DRPs were 91.2% (83.7%–98.5%) for bone (1,000 HU), 90.1% (81.0%–92.0%) for air (-895 HU), 91.2% (83.7%–96.7%) for iodine (335 HU), 89.1% (83.1%–92.0%) for water (90 HU), and 89.1% (82.3%–95.6%) for polystyrene (50 HU) (Fig. 3A). DRPs were higher in inlets with greater CT value differences (bone, iodine, and air) compared to those with smaller CT value differences (water and polystyrene) and tended to increase with higher reconstruction strength, except for IMR. However, there was no consistent trend in DRP based on target diameter (Fig. 3B).

INCREMENT OF CNR AND TTF

Compared with IR at the reference dose, the CNR increment of D-IR was generally positive, except for the lowest dose of D-IMR 3. Notably, D-IMR 3 demonstrated a narrower CNR increment range than other IR methods, with CNR values sometimes worsening at the lowest dose. Among the IR types, D-iDose4 demonstrated the most substantial CNR improvement, ranging from 200% to 350%, particularly at higher radiation doses. Interestingly, D-IMR 1 demonstrated relatively significant CNR improvement at the lowest doses (6% and 12%). Regardless of inlet type, a clear trend of increased CNR at higher doses was observed (Fig. 4A).

The increment in TTF varied depending on dose, inlet type, and IR strength. The TTF, which indicates spatial resolution, showed mixed outcomes compared with the reference dose. For inlets with large CT value differences from the background (bone and air) reconstructed with IMR, the TTF increment was consistently negative across doses, indicating degraded spatial resolution after DLM application (Fig. 4B). In contrast, other IR methods showed slight improvements in spatial resolution. For water and polystyrene inlets, which have small CT value differences from the background, negative TTF increments at lower doses indicated worse spatial resolution compared with the reference dose. Notably, in the iodine inlet, TTF increments were positive at doses above 1.5 mGy, suggesting improved spatial resolution. Additionally, a tendency toward higher spatial resolution at higher doses was observed with DLM application. Incremental changes in spatial resolution were further influenced by target diameter, reconstruction strength, and contrast difference of inlet materials.

DISCUSSION

Applying both DLR and IR simultaneously to a single image set is not feasible because each method utilizes sinogram-based raw data generated by specific CT devices. However, DLMs, which operate on reconstructed images rather than raw data, can enhance images that are already denoised by IR methods. Although DLMs have inherent limitations in artifact reduction due to their non-reliance on sinograms, combining DLM with IR methods provides modest improvements in artifact reduction. In addition, the use of vendor-agnostic DLMs can be a practical and cost-effective solution for improving image quality, particularly in facilities with older CT machines that cannot be equipped with DLR and where the latest and more expensive CT technologies are not available.

Earlier studies focused on single CT vendors, limiting the demonstration of DLM generalizability (232738). To our knowledge, this study represents an important step in testing the broader applicability of DLMs across two vendors. It was also unclear whether the DLM could improve image quality and reduce radiation dose when applied to different IR algorithm settings, as images reconstructed with different IR techniques exhibit distinct image profiles (394041). Our results demonstrated that combining DLM with IR effectively reduces radiation doses across different IR algorithms.

The lower DRP observed in the IMR algorithm compared with the other IR algorithms, even with additional DLM application, can be attributed to a complex interplay of factors. Although DLM improved the TTF for inlets with low contrast differences, the performance of IMR may have been affected by its inherently lower spatial resolution in low-contrast areas, coupled with a reduction in spatial resolution in bone structures after DLM application. This suggests that the observed results are a consequence of the mixed effects on spatial resolution in different imaging contexts with IMR. Previous research by McCollough et al. (42) indicated that a radiation dose reduction exceeding 25% can lead to lower spatial resolution in IR images than in FBP images. In our study, concerns about decreased TTF when applying DLM to ultralow-dose IR images were particularly noted with low-contrast difference inlets. We observed a reduction in spatial resolution in such cases, as well as with specific IR algorithms.

Interestingly, for low-contrast difference objects such as polystyrene inlets, we found that even at a 50% dose with additional DLM application, spatial resolution remained higher compared with reference-dose IR images reconstructed with IMR and iDose4 at higher strength settings. Furthermore, considering recent studies evaluating DLR performance in detecting liver contrast lesions at around our reference dose level, the application of DLM at the reference dose is promising (43).

Both IMR and iDose4 demonstrated improvements in spatial resolution, indicating potential benefits even at reference dose levels. The increase in CNR observed in D-IR compared with reference-dose IR generally yielded positive values, except for the lowest IMR 3 dose. This suggests that the CNR of D-IR is either superior to or comparable with that of reference-dose IR, even at the lowest dose levels. Notably, ADMIRE demonstrated the highest mean DRP among the evaluated IR methods, suggesting that its combination with DLM offers the most effective noise reduction and dose-saving capability while maintaining image quality. These findings highlight the robustness of ADMIRE compared with iDose4 and IMR in terms of dose-reduction performance.

Despite these promising results, this study had several limitations. Although DLM is considered vendor-agnostic, there remains insufficient research on its generalizability across a broader range of CT machines and clinical settings. Further studies involving more diverse CT systems and imaging protocols are needed to confirm the widespread applicability of these findings. As both pre- and post-DLM images shared identical parameters, the influence of slice thickness and collimation on the results could not be independently assessed, as we used the minimum slice thickness available for each CT machine and consistent collimation settings across all images. Additionally, the fixed reference dose of 12 mGy, based on the DRL for abdominopelvic CT, may not be optimal for all CT exams, potentially limiting the generalizability of the DRP findings to other contexts.

While we presented results for objects measuring 10, 5, and 1 mm, it is important to recognize that these findings are based on data from a 2.54 cm inlet, and the estimated values for smaller objects may not fully represent real-world outcomes. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results for smaller objects, as detection tasks involving 1 mm objects may require higher radiation doses than for larger objects. Additionally, while DRP was presented based on the detectability index, which considers TTF, the results remain estimated values and should ideally be validated through a real-world observer study to confirm their clinical relevance.

Moreover, excessive noise reduction can result in pseudo-lesions, which pose a diagnostic challenge. In some cases, artificial image enhancements or depressions caused by excessive noise reduction in single-scan images may be mistaken for real objects. In our study, pseudo-lesions were observed in unaveraged scans acquired with very low radiation doses in combination with certain IR methods, particularly in thin-slice images. However, these pseudo-lesions became less noticeable when the images were averaged, allowing us to identify them as artifacts. Further clinical validation is necessary to ensure that such artifacts do not impact diagnostic accuracy in patient imaging.

In conclusion, our study highlights the significant DRP offered by vendor-agnostic DLMs across multiple CT vendors and IR algorithms. However, further research is needed to fully understand the clinical implications and broader applicability of these findings.
