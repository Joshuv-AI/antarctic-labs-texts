Introduction

The use of physical restraint on patients’ wrists or limbs is a common but controversial practice in the intensive care unit (ICU). The primary justification for it stems from patient safety, meaning to prevent patients, especially those who are agitated or delirious, from interfering with treatment (e.g., unplanned extubation or catheter removal) [1–4]. While often seen as a necessary measure, this practice presents a significant ethical and clinical dilemma [1, 3, 4]. A growing body of evidence links physical restraint to adverse outcomes, including delirium, prolonged ventilation, physical injury, and posttraumatic stress [5–9]. It also creates moral distress for caregivers, particularly nurses, who must balance the duty to treat or protect the patient with the moral imperative to do no harm [10–12]. The use of restraint may not be as effective as presumed in preventing device removal [13, 14]. Some experts support that restraint should only be used when benefits greatly outweigh harms [4, 15–17]. However, the implementation of this rule in daily practice is inconsistent, leading to significant variations in the prevalence of restraint use [13, 18, 19]. It is important to note that much of the evidence regarding the outcomes and factors of physical restraint comes from observational studies. Interpreting this evidence requires caution due to inherent methodological limitations. Furthermore, the direction of causality is often difficult to ascertain, as relationships may be bidirectional (e.g., does restraint cause delirium, or does delirium lead to the application of restraint?).

Caregivers are increasingly questioning the rationale for physical restraint, which is further intensified by recent high-quality research. The R2D2-ICU randomized clinical trial directly compared a restrictive, low-use wrist-strap strategy to a liberal, high-use strategy in mechanically ventilated adults [20]. Contrary to what might be expected, the trial revealed no significant difference in the number of days alive without coma or delirium between the two groups at 14 days. It challenges the assumption that reducing restraint inherently improves short‑term neurological outcomes and raise a critical, unanswered question: how to balance patient safety and the broader physical and psychological harms of restrain?

Addressing this question is of direct relevance to critical care clinicians, nurses, and policymakers worldwide. Although recent reviews described the epidemiology of restraint use [13] and conflated chemical and physical restraint [17], they did not integrate the R2D2‑ICU trial within a framework that jointly considers patient neurological and psychological outcomes, family experience, and nursing moral distress—gaps this review is designed to address. By synthesizing these dimensions, this narrative review aims to inform both future research priorities and pragmatic strategies for restraint minimization across varied ICU settings.

Search strategy and research selection

The search was conducted only in PubMed on March 23rd, 2026 using (("intensive care unit*"[Title/Abstract] OR "ICU"[Title/Abstract] OR "critical care"[Title/Abstract] OR "critically ill"[Title/Abstract]) AND ("physical restraint*"[Title/Abstract] OR "wrist restraint*"[Title/Abstract] OR "limb restraint*"[Title/Abstract] OR "restraint use"[Title/Abstract] OR "patient restraint*"[Title/Abstract] OR "mechanical restraint*"[Title/Abstract])) AND (english[Language]). Original research (observational studies, RCTs), systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and key guidelines/position statements were included. A total of 412 records were yielded. Publications (especially post-2010) were prioritized. Given the narrative format and lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials, a formal systematic review with dual screening and risk-of-bias assessment was not performed.

Epidemiology and factors associated with restraint use in ICUs

The prevalence of physical restraint use in adult ICUs varies widely across studies, patient populations, and regions. It is reported to be 0% among adult ICU patients in the UK and Portugal [21], about 10% in Turkey [22] and Germany [23], about 20% in the Netherlands [24] and Spain [19], about 60% in China [25, 26], and even 100% in an Italian ICU [21]. It is important to note that aforementioned figures come from studies with differing designs and methodologies; therefore, direct numerical comparisons between them should be made with caution. Nevertheless, the reported rates collectively underscore the common use of restraint in numerous ICUs worldwide. Longitudinal data from the United States indicate a significant increase in restraint use over the past decade, rising from 36.9% in 2008–2010 to 44.0% in 2020–2022 [18]. For specific high-risk subgroups, such as mechanically ventilated patients, the prevalence rates are consistently higher. Estimates of physical restraint use range from 39% in a large retrospective analysis of electronic health records of hospitalized adults in the United States [27], to 41.5% in a prospective observational study of 787 patient-days across six Japanese ICUs [28], with 33.3%–57.1% reported in a 3-month observational study across six Jordanian ICUs [29], and approximately 53% in a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort across 51 Canadian ICUs (n = 711) [5]. A national survey of French ICUs further indicates that in the majority of units, restraint is used in over 50% of ventilated patients [30]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 39 studies involving 21,665 patients reported a pooled prevalence of physical restraint in ICUs of 41.6% (95% confidence interval [CI] 33.8–49.5%) [13]. However, the extreme statistical heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 99.61%) indicates that this summary estimate has limited interpretive value as a single, generalizable figure. Instead, it underscores the immense variability in practice, which arises from differences in study designs, measurement methods, patient populations, and cultural/legal contexts across countries. In pediatric ICUs, this practice is also prevalent. Studies have reported crude prevalence rates of 16% in a Spanish 5-center study using three weekly 24-h prevalence observations over 6 months [31]; 53% in a Japanese 26-center longitudinal point prevalence study on three study dates [32]; and 69.1% in a single-center 1-year cross-sectional study from China [33].

The use of physical restraint is not based on a single factor but is associated with a complex interplay of patient characteristics, caregiver factors, and systemic elements. Understanding this is essential for designing effective minimization strategies.

At the patient level, specific clinical conditions and treatments are strongly associated with restraint use: the use of an endotracheal tube and mechanical ventilation [25, 28, 34], and other invasive devices, such as central venous catheters, drainage tubes, and intracranial monitors [25, 33, 34]. Agitation and delirium are frequently cited as immediate reasons for restraint [5, 28, 35–37], which sometimes is to prevent injury to caregivers. However, it is paradoxical that deeply sedated or comatose patients are also at higher risk of receiving physical restraint [24, 34, 37]. The concomitant higher doses of benzodiazepine [5, 24, 37] and opioids [5], and higher Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II scores [5] were also associated with more likely use of physical restraint. In the United States, Black or African American patients are more likely to be physically restrained than White patients [18, 38, 39]. Furthermore, compared with English speakers, non-English-speaking patients are at increased risk of restraint use [3, 38, 39]. A retrospective cohort study involving 548 mechanically ventilated adults suggested that ensuring nurse–patient language concordance was associated with reduced odds of restraining mechanically ventilated patients [3]. The combination of limited medical resources and communication barriers leads to increased restraint use in pediatric ICUs [40, 41]. Critically ill children aged 1–2 years [32, 33] and adults ≥ 75 years either inside [26] or outside of ICU from acute hospitals [23] have been associated with higher levels of restraint use, possibly because of difficulty in communication [24, 42].

Caregiver factors are also associated with physical restraint use. Nurses' knowledge, attitudes, and sense of control directly influence their actions [43–45]. Knowledge about alternatives and negative attitudes toward restraint are associated with a lower intention to use them [41, 43]. Clinical experience alone does not consistently affect restraint practices, but training and education on restraint minimization are associated with better practices [43, 46]. Ethical conflict, however, can sometimes paradoxically lead to a higher intention to restrain, possibly as a defensive reaction to the stress of the decision [44].

Systemic elements also play a role. Units without clear protocols, standardized assessment tools (for pain, sedation, and delirium), or structured decision-support frameworks tend to use restraint more [40, 45, 47]. Medical resources stresses also influence clinical decisions on restraint. Lower nurse-to-patient ratios and overwhelming workloads are consistently linked to greater restraint use [21, 32, 48, 49]. Conversely, better work environments and more nursing hours per patient are associated with lower restraint rates [49] A before-and-after comparison revealed a notable increase in the use of physical restraint, which increased from 29.4% before the pandemic to 32.5% during the pandemic [38]. This increase coincided with unprecedented pressure on intensive care resources during the COVID-19 pandemic. The type of hospital (e.g., academic vs. community) can influence practice patterns [5, 18]. Finally, the lack of institutional policies, leadership support for alternative strategies, and interprofessional collaboration can sustain a high-restraint culture [50–52].

Clinical outcomes associated with physical restraint use

Physical restraint on patients may be a double-edged sword. The evidence on its effectiveness for mainly preventing pulling out tubes is mixed [53–57]. A meta-analysis of 10 studies with 2092 patients revealed that being physically restrained made a patient more than three times (odds ratio [OR] 3.10, 95% CI 2.21–4.34) more likely to experience unplanned extubation [58]. However, this association may be influenced by a common bias: restraint is typically applied to patients who are already agitated or deemed at high risk for pulling at tubes, making it difficult to separate the effect of the restraint from the patient's underlying condition. The recent R2D2-ICU trial revealed no significant difference in self-extubation rates between restrictive and liberal restraint strategies (9.2% vs. 8.5%) [20]. The adjusted relative risk (RR) was 1.11 (95% CI 0.59–2.07). While the point estimate suggests a minimal increase in risk, the upper bound of the CI does not exclude the possibility of a clinically important, up to twofold, increase in self-extubation risk with the restrictive strategy. It is also important to note that the trial was designed as a superiority study and did not pre-specify a non-inferiority margin for this outcome. Therefore, while the results are consistent with the safety of a low-restraint approach, they do not constitute definitive statistical proof of non-inferiority. Taken together, these findings suggest that a strategy aiming to minimize physical restraint can be implemented without clear evidence of increased self-extubation, challenging the assumption that its liberal use is necessary for preventing this complication. In contrast to the adult ICU setting, the risk profile in pediatric ICUs appears distinct. A retrospective study identified that the absence of physical restraint has been identified as a risk factor for unplanned extubation in pediatric patients underwent general surgical procedures with indwelling catheters [59]. Consequently, the rationale for restraint minimization may require population-specific adaptation.

Furthermore, the use of physical restraint is associated with a range of negative physical, neurological, and psychological outcomes. The physical consequences of restraint are often underrecognized. Complications, including redness, edema, impaired circulation, and skin breakdown, are common, with the incidence increasing with the duration of restraint [8, 22, 60]. Prolonged immobilization secondary to restraint contributes to ICU-acquired weakness [61]. Furthermore, restraint use may be associated with a longer duration of mechanical ventilation and extended ICU and hospital lengths of stay [7, 62].

The most concerned neurological consequence is delirium. Physical restraint is an independent risk factor for both triggering and prolonging delirium in critically ill adults and children [28, 56, 63–65]. Restraint is also a significant risk factor for subsyndromal delirium, a less severe but still clinically important condition [66]. A meta-analysis reported an OR of 11.54 (95% CI 6.66–20.01) for delirium in restrained patients [9]. Restraint may contribute to delirium through mechanisms such as sensory deprivation, sleep disruption, immobilization, and profound psychological distress. This relationship appears to be dose dependent, with a longer duration and more frequent application of restraint associated with increased delirium risk. Among 447 physically restrained ICU patients, Pan et al. reported a dose–response association between restraint exposure and incident delirium, with restraint duration ≥ 6 days (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 26.30) and multiple episodes (≥ 2 episodes, aOR 2.38–3.62) significantly associated with increased delirium risk versus shorter or single‑episode use [67]. As for global disability in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage, the continuous use of physical restraint has been associated with unfavorable neurological outcomes in one single-center retrospective observational study involving 101 adult patients [65]. Although observational evidence links physical restraint to an increased risk and severity of delirium in ICU patients, the relationship is likely bidirectional; delirium-associated agitation often precipitates restraint use, making it difficult to isolate restraint as an independent causative factor of delirium. This complexity is underscored by the R2D2-ICU trial protocol, in which the decision to apply restraint was based on the level of agitation (a Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale score ≥ + 3), not on the presence of a delirium diagnosis. In practice, restraint and delirium are intertwined in a clinical scenario where one frequently leads to the other, complicating causal interpretations. The psychological sequelae may be profound and often long-lasting. Patients and their families report experiences of fear, helplessness, anger, and a loss of dignity and control [68, 69]. For patients, being restrained can be a traumatic experience that contributes to posttraumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and depression [9, 69, 70]. A secondary analysis of a prospective cohort study involving 2764 adults from two ICUs revealed that physically restrained patients reported significantly worse symptoms of depression and a lower physical quality of life persisting for up to 1 year [70]. This distress extends to families. This emotional burden is frequently compounded by feeling shut out of the decision-making process and a lack of clear communication from the clinical team [68, 69]. Caregivers, especially nurses at the bedside, are not immune to harm. The act of applying restraint often induces significant moral distress, guilt, and emotional exhaustion [10, 12, 57, 71].

Ethical, legal, and humanistic entanglement

The use of physical restraint in the ICU presents a moral and ethical dilemma [57, 72]. A comprehensive ethical consideration of physical restraint must also account for the profound and multi-faceted vulnerability of the critically ill patient. This vulnerability is physiological (being dependent on life-support), psychological (existing in an unfamiliar and frightening environment), and social (stripped of autonomy and customary social roles). Using restraint on a person in such a state of extreme dependency carries a heightened ethical responsibility to protect, and not exploit, that vulnerability. It demands extra vigilance to ensure that restraint is never used for staff convenience or as a substitute for adequate staffing or symptom management. This foundational understanding frames all subsequent ethical dilemmas in restraint use.

It forces a direct confrontation between two fundamental principles of medical ethics: beneficence (the duty to do good and prevent harm) and autonomy (the right to self-determination) [11, 17]. Caregivers typically invoke beneficence to justify restraint, arguing that it is necessary to protect a patient from immediate self-harm and to ensure that vital treatments can be delivered. However, this act of protection comes at a high ethical cost: it overrides a patient's autonomy, especially when they are too ill to consent or object [11, 12, 57]. This conflict is further complicated by the principles of nonmaleficence (do no harm) and justice, as the harm associated with restraint is significant and may be disproportionately applied to vulnerable populations [11, 18, 39].

Nurses, who are most involved in making the decision of physical restraint and the primary executors of restraint orders, often shoulder the heaviest ethical weight of this practice [10, 16, 35, 71]. Research consistently shows that the decision to restrain a patient is a significant source of moral distress for nurses [10, 12, 35]. On one hand, nurses report feelings of guilt, powerlessness, and internal conflict, knowing that restraint causes discomfort and psychological trauma; on the other hand, they may feel that they have no safer alternatives in the context of high workload, patient acuity, and sometimes insufficient staffing [10, 48, 71].

The legal and professional rules surrounding the use of restraint differ. In some jurisdictions, the use of restraint is legally permissible under the doctrine of necessity to prevent imminent harm to the patient or others, but it must be proportionate, time-limited, and subject to regular review [16, 72, 73]. Some regions have implemented specific reporting requirements for restraint [18]. A critical legal and ethical requirement involves the patient’s family or surrogates in the decision. The use of physical restraint must be exceptional, permitted only after all other alternative strategies have been thoroughly tried, and its continued necessity would be subject to rigorous, ongoing evaluation [16, 50].

Strategies for restraint minimization

Given the possible harms associated with physical restraint, substantial efforts have been made to develop and test interventions aimed at reducing its use.

Educational interventions targeting healthcare professionals are a critical starting point. Education alone can be effective, especially when it improves knowledge about the adverse effects of restraint, trains staff in the use of validated tools to assess pain and delirium, and builds skills in managing agitation without medication [50, 51, 74, 75]. Education empowers physicians and nurses to make more informed decisions and to advocate for alternatives, and it is more effective when it is embedded within broader organizational change initiatives for ICU patients [50] and in general geriatric adults [76, 77]. Other interventions combined with staff education include the implementation of standardized assessment protocols, decision-support tools, environmental modifications, and the promotion of less restrictive alternatives such as mittens, soft braces, or enhanced observation both inside [50] and outside the ICU [76, 78, 79].

Specific non-pharmacological strategies to manage agitation are also essential. These include pain management, reorientation strategies, ensuring that hearing aids and glasses are used, sleep promotion, music therapy, and family presence at the bedside [57, 80, 81]. Ensuring effective communication with patients to address their requirements and desires, through tools such as communication boards or speaking valves, can reduce frustration and agitation, thereby decreasing the perceived need for restraint [19, 42, 57, 80].

A multidisciplinary team of caregivers, including doctors, nurses, pharmacists, and physical therapists, also helps either in the ICU [82] or in rehabilitation settings [83]. Implementation of the ABCDEF bundle (Assess pain, Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials, Choice of analgesia/sedation, Delirium assess/manage, Early mobility, Family engagement) has been associated with significant improvements in outcomes, including substantial reductions in physical restraint use, along with decreased delirium, coma, and mortality [7, 62]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomized controlled trials involving 4338 patients on guided interventions, including the ABCDEF bundle, revealed that they significantly reduced the restraint rate (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.60–0.86), incidence of delirium (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.41–0.68), and unplanned extubation rate (RR 0.36, 95% CI 0.23–0.56) [74].

Furthermore, organizational policies such as clear documentation, flexible family visitation, the use of informed consent for restraint, and the establishment of unit-based champions or committees to oversee restraint practices are important facilitators of cultural change [42, 45, 50, 51, 81, 84].

Conclusions

The use of physical restraint in the ICU remains a challenge where patient safety, ethical principles, and evidence-based medicine intersect. While restraint is used to prevent harm, it also causes harm. Although the use of physical restraint is recommended not as a routine tool but as a last-resort measure, the recent R2D2-ICU trial adds a critical nuance. Compared with a liberal strategy, a strategy of restrictive wrist restraint use did not improve the number of days free of coma or delirium [20], which challenges the straightforward assumption that simply using fewer restraint directly leads to better neurological outcomes. Importantly, the restrictive strategy also was not associated with a statistically significant increase in adverse safety events, such as self-extubation [20]. While the trial was not designed to formally prove equivalence, these safety data are consistent with the view that a strategy of restraint minimization can be implemented without incurring unacceptable harm.​ This supports the feasibility of moving away from routine restraint use.

The R2D2-ICU trial refutes the simplistic premise that “less restraint equals better short-term neurological outcomes”, confirming that restraint minimization must not be treated as an isolated target, but as part of integrated, patient-centerd ICU care. For clinical practice, the priority is no longer debating whether to restrict use, but implementing standardized, protocolized pathways: units should adopt validated delirium/pain assessment tools, embed non-pharmacological agitation management into routine workflows, and establish regular interdisciplinary review of every restraint episode to ensure it is truly a last resort. Moving forward, we urgently need large-scale, pragmatic trials testing implementation strategies tailored to regional resource contexts (e.g., low nurse-to-patient ratio settings common in low- and middle-income countries), with core outcomes including long-term PTSD incidence, functional recovery, caregiver moral distress, and family-reported decisional satisfaction. The goal is not just to reduce restraint durations, but to build a culture of safety and humanity in the ICU, one that values not only the physical health but also the psychological well-being of both patients and caregivers.
