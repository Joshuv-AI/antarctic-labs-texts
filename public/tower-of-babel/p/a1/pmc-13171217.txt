Safety Profile of Submandibular Gland Reduction and Parotid-Tail Minimal Shaving in Deep Neck Lifting: A 341-Patient East Asian Series
Authors: Ilseok Lee, Suhwan Kim, Youngjae Lee, Jaehee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep-plane facelift has become widely adopted in facial rejuvenation, and its extension into deep neck contouring has highlighted the importance of addressing submandibular and parotid fullness when these contribute to clinically evident jawline blunting. Although many studies have reported complications following deep neck lifting and submandibular gland (SMG) reduction, reported rates remain heterogeneous because of inconsistent definitions and attribution of gland-specific events. Parotid-tail minimal shaving, originally proposed by Talei, has gained attention as a conservative option for posterolateral contour refinement; however, its clinical safety profile has not been systematically evaluated, particularly in East Asian patients.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to evaluate the overall safety and procedure-specific complication profiles of SMG reduction and parotid-tail minimal shaving performed during deep neck lifting in an East Asian cohort.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included 341 consecutive East Asian patients who underwent deep neck lifting between August 2024 and July 2025. SMG reduction was performed in 240 patients, and parotid-tail minimal shaving in 81 patients; 20 patients underwent both procedures during the same operation. Gland-related complications were assessed during postoperative follow-up of up to 6 months.ResultsAmong 341 patients, hematoma was observed in 4 (1.17%), infection in 3 (0.88%), and seroma in 6 (1.76%). Transient cervical-branch weakness was observed in 5 patients (1.47%) and marginal mandibular nerve weakness in 2 patients (0.59%). SMG-related sialocele was observed in 1 patient (0.29%), whereas parotid-related sialocele was observed in 3 patients (0.88%). No permanent nerve injury was observed.ConclusionsSelective salivary-gland reduction during deep neck lifting demonstrated low complication rates in this large East Asian series. Both SMG reduction and capsule-preserving parotid-tail minimal shaving appear safe when selectively applied.Level of Evidence: 4 (Risk) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

Modern facelift philosophy treats the face and neck as a unified anatomical and aesthetic continuum; correction of the deep compartment—including the submandibular gland (SMG)—facilitates tension-free redraping of the platysma and skin to restore a smooth, uninterrupted cervicomental contour.1-4 Recent evidence indicates that SMG prominence reflects hypertrophy or pseudoptosis rather than simple descent, supporting selective debulking as an effective method of contour refinement.3,5,6 Meta-analyses further reinforce this concept, identifying the SMG as a central structure in deep neck rejuvenation with consistently low rates of hematoma, sialocele, and transient neuropraxia.7-9 Together, these findings suggest that SMG reduction, when performed with precise anatomical control, is a safe and reproducible procedure.

Talei described a deep-plane technique incorporating mastoid crevasse–based vertical suspension to enhance cervicomandibular definition. In his technique, the lateral platysma is vertically anchored to the anterior mastoid periosteum, whereas the capsule-preserving parotid tail is gently repositioned toward the mastoid to sharpen the gonial definition.10 In selected cases with parotid-tail hypertrophy, a capsule-preserving parotid-tail minimal shaving may be performed through the same deep-plane approach while adhering to defined dissection boundaries.11 Despite rising interest in parotid-tail contouring, dedicated analyses reporting procedure-specific complication rates after capsule-preserving parotid-tail minimal shaving remain limited.

Most existing evidence in deep neck rejuvenation is derived from Western populations, with limited data for East Asian patients whose mandibular morphology, skeletal buttress, and aesthetic ideals may differ.12-14 East Asian patients often present with a weaker skeletal frame, an obtuse gonial angle, and a cultural preference for a slimmer, straighter jawline, which may contribute to earlier demand for glandular contouring.

Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the safety and complication profile of selective SMG reduction and parotid-tail minimal shaving within a standardized, drainless deep neck lifting protocol in an East Asian cohort.

METHODS

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective, single-center study included 341 consecutive East Asian patients who underwent deep neck lifting with concomitant salivary-gland contouring at a private aesthetic surgery clinic in Seoul, Korea, from August 2024 to July 2025. All patients were of East Asian origin (Korea, Japan, or China) and underwent standardized deep neck procedures as part of lower-face and neck rejuvenation.

Patients were categorized according to the glandular procedure performed: partial SMG reduction alone, parotid-tail minimal shaving alone, or combined SMG reduction with parotid-tail minimal shaving. Patients with a history of previous neck or parotid surgery, congenital craniofacial asymmetry, or uncontrolled systemic disease were excluded from analysis.

Anesthesia and Setup

All procedures were performed in an outpatient operating room under propofol-based intravenous sedation. Supplemental oxygen (2-5 L/min) was administered through nasal cannula as needed. Local anesthetic solution containing epinephrine (1:100,000) was infiltrated along the submental region, mandibular border, and postauricular–mastoid area to provide analgesia and vasoconstriction. All procedures were performed by the primary surgeon with a single assistant providing retraction. Dissection and hemostasis followed a standardized operative protocol.

Surgical Technique

SMG Reduction

A 2 cm midline submental incision was made ∼1.5 cm inferior to the natural submental crease. Subplatysmal flaps were elevated to expose the lateral border of the anterior belly of the digastric muscle. An intracapsular approach was initiated along this lateral border to enter the SMG capsule.

Anterior intracapsular dissection was performed to expose the glandular parenchyma, followed by identification and preservation of the overlying facial vein. Caudal intracapsular dissection proceeded within a relatively avascular plane, allowing early mobilization of the gland.

To maintain mandibular contour continuity, a small medial remnant of SMG tissue immediately lateral to the anterior digastric muscle was intentionally preserved. Glandular resection progressed from medial to lateral, curving posteriorly toward the mylohyoid floor, where the posterolateral capsule and principal vascular pedicles were encountered and controlled.

Definitive parenchymal reduction was performed in a lobule-by-lobule fashion under direct visualization using bipolar cautery exclusively. The extent of resection was guided by a tangent plane defined by the mandibular inferior border and the anterior digastric line. Lobules located caudal to this plane were excised, whereas cranial lobules were preserved. To prevent medialization of the digastric complex, the residual superficial glandular tissue and adjacent soft tissue were stabilized to the lateral border of the anterior digastric using 3 interrupted 4-0 polydioxanone sutures. These sutures were placed for stabilization only and not for dead-space obliteration.

Parotid-Tail Minimal Shaving

Parotid-tail contouring was performed through the same lower face–neck surgical access used for facelift and platysma dissection. After elevation of the skin and platysma flaps, the mandibular inferior border, mandibular angle, and anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle were confirmed based on preoperative markings (Figure 1).

A short vertical incision was created just anterior to the SCM muscle's anterior border, beginning inferior to the mandibular border. Through this access, the platysma and parotid-tail capsule were opened together, and the parotid tail was dissected in a capsule-preserving plane. Care was taken to avoid capsular injury, because the capsule was intended for primary repair.

Parenchymal debulking was limited to the most hypertrophic portion of the parotid tail and was performed in a minimal shaving fashion. Resection was strictly confined to the triangular region bounded by the anterior border of the SCM muscle, the mandibular angle, and the mandibular inferior border. Dissection did not extend superior to the mandibular border or posterior to the mandibular angle and remained superficial to the deep lobe and main facial nerve trunk. After debulking, the parotid capsule was meticulously reapproximated using fine absorbable sutures. No formal parotidectomy was performed.

Hemostasis and Secretion Control

During SMG reduction, the principal branches of the facial artery at the posterolateral capsule were identified and ligated first, based on previously established vascular mapping. Accessory arterial branches arising from the submental system were subsequently controlled along the planned resection plane. All parenchymal dissection and hemostasis were performed using bipolar cautery only. Quilting sutures, external hemostatic net compression, and surgical drains were not used.

To minimize dead space and intraglandular pressure, a thin film of fibrin sealant (Greenplast Q; GC Pharma, Yongin, Korea; ∼0.5 mL per side, adjusted to cavity size) was applied uniformly over the resection bed. Low-dose botulinum toxin type A (BTX-A; onabotulinumtoxinA; Botox, Allergan, Irvine, CA), typically 5 U per side, was injected into the residual SMG and, when indicated, into the residual parotid tail to provide prophylactic antisecretory control. This adjunctive strategy was applied to all cases involving glandular reduction.

Postoperative Care and Outcomes

All patients were discharged on the day of surgery under a drainless outpatient protocol. A compressive neck garment was worn continuously for the first 72 h and then nightly for an additional 4 weeks. Routine follow-up visits were scheduled on postoperative Day 1, Week 1, Month 1, and Month 6, with additional visits as clinically indicated.

Postoperative complications related to glandular reduction were assessed and included hematoma requiring evacuation or aspiration, seroma, sialocele (classified as SMG-related or parotid-related based on clinical location and imaging when available), infection requiring systemic antibiotics or drainage, and lower-facial nerve dysfunction.

Nerve-related events were clinically categorized because marginal mandibular nerve (MMN)-type weakness, characterized by perioral asymmetry with impaired mentalis function, or cervical branch–related depressor anguli oris (DAO) weakness with relative preservation of MMN function. Secondary outcomes included the need for revision surgery and overall tolerability of the drainless protocol.

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Complication rates were calculated for the entire cohort and for each procedural subgroup. Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, and procedure type using 2-sided tests with a significance level of .05.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the IRB of the author's center. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for surgery, clinical photography, and the use of anonymized data for research and publication.

Between August 2024 and July 2025, a total of 341 East Asian patients underwent deep neck lifting with selective salivary-gland reduction using a standardized drainless protocol. All procedures were performed under propofol-based monitored anesthesia care with local infiltration.

Sialocele developed in 4 patients (1.17%) and demonstrated a gland-specific pattern. On a patient level, 1 patient (0.29%) developed SMG-origin sialocele and 3 patients (0.88%) developed parotid-origin sialocele. On a procedure level, SMG-origin sialocele occurred in 1 of 260 SMG resections (0.38%), whereas parotid-origin sialocele occurred in 3 of 101 parotid-tail minimal shaving procedures (2.97%). After SMG reduction, all nerve events followed a cervical-branch pattern (4 patients in the SMG-only group and 1 in the combined group). All cases represented transient neurapraxia and resolved completely with observation and supportive care, with no permanent nerve deficits or revision surgery required.

DISCUSSION

Recent Perspectives and Trends in Facelift and Deep Neck Rejuvenation

Modern rhytidectomy treats the face and neck as a single, indivisible aesthetic continuum. Achieving a natural cervicomental contour requires addressing the deep cervical compartment—notably the SMG, the digastric complex, and subplatysmal fat—before superficial redraping, thereby enabling tension-free skin repositioning and a continuous jaw–neck transition. Together, these data support the principle that precise deep-compartment correction followed by tension-free redraping underlies stable, natural outcomes.1-4

From a morphologic standpoint, clinically visible SMG prominence represents pseudoptosis—a volume-related phenomenon rather than true descent. Previous studies have demonstrated that a substantial proportion of the gland physiologically lies below the mandibular inferior border and that age-related fibro-fatty change increases its visibility, supporting the concept that apparent “sagging” reflects volume and skeletal change rather than inferior migration.3,5,6 Thus, aesthetic management should prioritize controlled intracapsular debulking (volume modulation) over attempts at “ptosis correction” or isolated platysmal tightening.

Systematic reviews indicate that SMG reduction has become a routine component of contemporary deep neck contouring and is frequently considered alongside management of the digastric complex.7 Higher-level evidence consistently demonstrates low rates of hematoma, sialocele, transient nerve symptoms, and xerostomia following aesthetic SMG surgery, supporting a favorable safety profile.8,9 Their selective management has therefore been shown to be reproducible and safe across large-scale analyses when deep neck lifting is performed with appropriate anatomic control.

East Asian Anatomical and Aesthetic Considerations

Deep neck contouring reflects both hereditary predisposition and age-related morphologic change. Su-Genyk and Quatela noted that even relatively young patients may exhibit submental fullness caused by deep-layer structures—including misplaced subplatysmal fat, hypertrophic digastric bellies, or an enlarged, inferiorly positioned SMG—rather than by superficial laxity.15 They emphasized that correction should therefore target the deep compartment, and that patients with minimal skin redundancy may achieve meaningful improvement with an isolated deep neck lift.

With aging, mandibular resorption—manifesting as ramus shortening, reduced mandibular body height, and widening of the gonial angle—weakens the bony framework supporting the cervicomental contour.15 Among East Asians, this involutional process is superimposed upon an inherently less projected skeletal configuration. Lee et al demonstrated shorter maxillomandibular dimensions compared with Caucasian cohorts, indicating a smaller and less protrusive mandible, whereas Gao et al described a more obtuse gonial angle and a posteriorly positioned chin in East Asian populations.12,13 Together, these skeletal characteristics reflect diminished bony buttressing in the cervicomental region—an important consideration in aesthetic assessment and surgical planning.

Aesthetic preferences parallel these anatomic tendencies. Rhee et al showed a consistent East Asian preference for a delicate, V-shaped lower face, a smooth mandibular line, and a short, refined chin.14 These combined anatomic and cultural characteristics help explain why younger East Asian patients often seek deep neck lifting with SMG management and may be particularly sensitive to subtle under-correction of the submandibular or digastric regions.

Zone-Based Structural Considerations and the Role of Preoperative Imaging

Bravo's cervicomandibular zoning framework—midline submental tissues (Zone I), the submandibular segment containing the SMG (Zone II), and the lateral gonial segment incorporating the parotid tail and posterior platysma (Zone III)—provides a practical foundation for understanding cervical fullness.16,17 Within this model, contour deformities rarely stem from a single structure; rather, they arise from how adjacent zones interact. Soft-tissue volume in Zone I, SMG prominence in Zone II, and parotid-tail bulk in Zone III collectively determine the apparent cervicomental contour.

Accordingly, an ideal jawline is achieved not simply through reduction of the SMG, but through an integrated correction in which the morphological transition from Zone I to Zone II and from Zone II to Zone III remains smooth and uninterrupted. In Zone I, balanced refinement of subplatysmal fat, the anterior digastric belly, and the hyoid area may be required; in Zone II, selective intracapsular SMG reduction addresses the dominant deep component; and, as Talei highlighted, the definition of Zone III—particularly the posterior mandibular angle—has become increasingly important, especially when parotid-tail fullness contributes to lateral weight and blunts gonial-line clarity. Ultimately, optimal deep neck contouring requires not only zone-specific solutions but also preservation of continuity across zones so that no abrupt step-offs occur between adjacent anatomical segments.

To support individualized planning within this zone-based framework, preoperative imaging was used as a low-burden adjunct rather than as a source of quantitative thresholds. In such cases, portable ultrasound served as a confirmatory tool to distinguish glandular bulk from overlying adiposity, rather than as a decisive metric. This approach parallels Mashkevich's method, in which ultrasound clarified deep neck structures when palpation was unreliable.18 Beyond structural identification, we adapted the measurement logic of Gelezhe et al—who quantified skin-to-gland distance on computed tomography—by translating the same concept to portable ultrasound.19 Conceptually, this captured a “skin-to-gland distance” surrogate by comparing the subcutaneous thickness over the gland with that of an adjacent gland-absent area.

These ultrasound findings were not used to determine candidacy or mandate glandular reduction. Instead, they served as a center-specific mapping tool to anticipate gland depth, to detect when deep structures were being obscured by thick subcutaneous tissue, and to clarify the dominant contributors to fullness within each zone.

Talei's Framework for Zone III Contouring and Parotid-Tail Management

The lateral deep neck becomes particularly important when addressing residual fullness after standard deep-plane elevation. Talei described the mastoid crevasse as a dense, fibrous fixation point where the platysma–SMAS complex anchors over the parotid tail between the gonial angle and the superior SCM fascia.10 Releasing this anchor is essential for true deep-plane mobility: without adequate crevasse release, the lateral platysma cannot rotate, the posterior mandibular angle remains blunt, and the jaw–neck transition fails to sharpen. Functionally, the mastoid crevasse serves as a primary fulcrum for vertical elevation of the submandibular contents and for restoring lateral cervicomandibular definition.

Within this anatomic framework, Talei emphasized that parotid-tail prominence is often the dominant contributor to Zone III bulk, even when the SMG and anterior neck have been appropriately treated. Fullness in this region can obscure the posterior mandibular angle, efface the SCM shadow, and broaden the lower neck—changes that can disproportionately affect aesthetic balance, particularly in patients who already have a wider gonial angle or a heavier lateral neck silhouette.11 To refine this zone safely, Talei advocated a capsule-preserving, minimal debulking technique restricted to the anterior and inferior interface between the SMAS and the parotid tail. This method avoids breaching the parotid capsule and remains anterior to the great auricular nerve, thereby minimizing the risks of sialocele and nerve injury while restoring a crisp posterior jawline contour.

In summary, mastoid crevasse release establishes the mechanical precondition for meaningful lateral jawline rotation, and minimal parotid-tail reduction provides selective contour enhancement when Zone III bulk persists despite proper deep-plane elevation. Together, these maneuvers form an anatomically grounded, low-risk strategy that complements SMG contouring and improves overall jaw–neck separation in appropriately selected patients.

Comparison With Established Approaches

The evolution of deep neck surgery for SMG management has involved contributions from multiple authors over time. Within the context of aesthetic facelift surgery, Bravo highlighted the role of combined SMG–parotid reduction to refine a heavy cervicomental angle.20 Mendelson and Tutino subsequently formalized a short-incision intracapsular technique emphasizing intraglandular vessel ligation and parenchymal resection.21 Auersvald et al expanded this framework with capsular repair, quilting sutures, and external hemostatic net compression to achieve drainless closure and reduce the risk of sialocele.22,23 Marten and Elyassnia concurred on the importance of deep-layer correction but cautioned against capsule oversewing and excessive diathermy, prioritizing meticulous hemostasis beneath the mandibular border.5 Similarly, Ghoraba described a Mendelson/Marten-style approach with selective drain use, whereas Bogari and Cakmak, O’Daniel and Kachare, and Weinstein and Nahai reported variations incorporating external hemostatic net compression with consistently low rates of hematoma and sialocele.24-27

Following the foundational principles of the established masters, we performed deep neck lifting with intracapsular SMG resection. However, rather than focusing solely on hemostasis around a “central intraglandular artery,” our workflow emphasizes detailed vascular mapping and vessel-first control throughout the gland. For dead-space and secretion management, quilting sutures, drains, and external hemostatic net compression were deliberately avoided; instead, the procedure was designed around meticulous ligation of the arterial and ductal pedicles that represent the principal sources of bleeding and sialocele formation. Thin-film fibrin sealant and low-dose BTX-A were used as adjuncts to reinforce this anatomic approach.

Anatomic Basis for a Vessel-First Workflow

The vascular anatomy of the SMG has been described inconsistently across previous clinical and anatomical reports. Mendelson and Tutino, Auersvald et al, and Marten and Elyassnia each emphasized the importance of identifying and ligating a central intraglandular artery, but differed regarding its precise origin.5,21-23 O’Daniel noted rare venous communications between the anterior jugular and facial veins, whereas Cakmak et al provided a comprehensive description identifying the facial artery as the dominant feeder and the submental artery as a consistent accessory source.28,29

Subsequently, Garcia-Serrano et al clarified this anatomy through detailed cadaveric analysis, refining earlier descriptions by the major surgical masters.30 They demonstrated that the principal arterial supply originates from 1 to 3 main branches of the facial artery (MBFA), whereas accessory branches typically arise from the submental arterial system. Venous drainage is dominated by the shared complex between the concomitant Wharton duct vein and the facial vein, with the submental vein serving as a frequent accessory route.

Katsumi et al further detailed mylohyoid vascular anatomy, showing that a perforating submental vein—often accompanied by a perforating submental artery—may traverse the mid-portion of the tangent plane over the mylohyoid during SMG resection; inadvertent injury can cause brisk bleeding if not controlled with precise bipolar technique.31

Finally, Xu et al illustrated a unified resin-cast model demonstrating that the artery, vein, and duct enter the gland together in a tree-like pattern.32 This configuration supports the present lobule-by-lobule bipolar technique.

Parotid Tail Management in Zone III: Anatomical Rationale and Minimal Shaving Technique

Bravo emphasized that loss of a sharp gonial angle and obscuration of the SCM shadow are among the primary contributors to a “heavy” neck contour, especially in male patients whose aesthetic ideals center on a defined mandibular angle and a visible lateral cervical line.16 Excess parotid-tail fullness can further blunt these landmarks, producing a short-necked or widened lower-face appearance. These observations are consistent with the anthropometric features of East Asian men, who often present with a naturally wider gonial angle; when combined with parotid-tail hypertrophy, posterior heaviness becomes more prominent.

Talei et al proposed a conservative solution for such cases, noting that although parotid-tail reduction is not routinely required, a very minimal superficial debulking may be performed when hypertrophy contributes to posterior jawline blunting.11 The permissible extent of resection is strictly limited: the dissection remains superficial to the parotid capsule, kept anterior to the great auricular nerve, and must avoid any excision deep to the mastoid tip to prevent facial nerve injury.

In our practice, this conservative philosophy is preserved and operationalized through a standardized minimal-resection workflow. Preoperatively, the mandibular inferior border, mandibular angle, and anterior border of the SCM are marked over the platysma to guide orientation. Dissection is then performed along the anterior SCM border with deliberate protection of the great auricular nerve, exposing the parotid tail without violating the capsule. The superficial lobule is separated from the capsule in a limited fashion, and only a shallow, surface-level shaving is performed when reduction is indicated. Resection is confined strictly to the region inferior to the mandibular border and never extends posterior to a vertical line drawn from the mandibular angle, ensuring that the operative field does not encroach upon the facial nerve trajectory.

A critical final step is meticulous capsular and platysmal repair. Failure to reconstruct the capsule increases the risk of sialocele formation; although sialocele rates did not differ significantly between SMG-only and parotid-tail procedures in this study, our clinical impression suggests that parotid-tail cases may be more prone to transient salivary leakage without precise capsular closure.

Dead Space and Secretion Control

Auersvald et al advocated capsular repair, quilting sutures, and external hemostatic net compression to minimize dead space and reduce the risk of sialocele formation.22,23,33 In contrast, consistent with Marten's principles, we avoided quilting sutures and capsular repair and instead managed dead space within a drainless pathway using 2 adjuncts: low-dose BTX-A and thin-film fibrin sealant. This strategy also reduced the likelihood of surface irregularity or palpable nodularity that can occur with capsular repair and supported preservation of a smooth, continuous cervicomental contour.

In line with Auersvald's recommendation for prophylactic BTX use, we routinely injected 5 U of BTX-A into the residual SMG remnant following intracapsular debulking; the same protocol was applied when performing parotid-tail minimal reduction.23 Clinically, this approach corresponded with the low incidence of sialocele observed in our cohort.

When sialocele occurred, management followed the therapeutic protocol described by Santos et al, consisting of additional intraglandular BTX-A (10 U), repeated aspiration, compression, and dietary sialagogue restriction.34

In our series, most sialoceles developed within 1 to 2 weeks postoperatively and were identified clinically based on localized fluctuant swelling. In most cases, clinical resolution was achieved after 2 to 3 aspiration sessions. This stepwise management strategy did not result in meaningful long-term aesthetic or functional sequelae.

Chudek et al reported that uniform fibrin coating after parotidectomy reduced fluid collections from 25.8% to 6.9% (P = .01) and eliminated the need for drains.35 A 2024 meta-analysis by Nguyen et al found modest yet consistent reductions in wound complications and length of stay, although heterogeneity and low-certainty evidence were noted, whereas Chorney and Ryan reported no significant differences.36,37 Despite these mixed findings, we applied fibrin sealant uniformly in both SMG resection and parotid-tail minimal reduction. Given its low morbidity and ease of application, fibrin sealant served as a practical adjunct to stabilize the operative cavity within a drainless protocol.

Technical Rationale for a Bipolar-Only Dissection Strategy

Recent studies have explored the use of advanced energy devices for SMG resection in deep neck surgery. Buckner and Shah compared an ultrasonic dissector with conventional electrocautery and reported no statistically significant difference in complication rates between the 2 techniques.38 Similarly, Basaran and Comert described LigaSure (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN)-assisted SMG excision, demonstrating efficient glandular resection and reliable vessel sealing; however, their series reported a notable incidence of seroma and transient lower-facial weakness.39 These findings suggest that although such devices may facilitate rapid tissue division and hemostasis, their impact on neural safety remains uncertain.

After adopting a bipolar-only approach—using gentle, surface-sweeping coagulation along the gland capsule rather than direct capsular stripping—we observed a consistent reduction in transient nerve weakness, particularly involving the cervical branch coursing beneath the platysma. We therefore consider meticulous bipolar dissection to be a key contributor to the low incidence of nerve-related complications observed in this series.

Although bipolar-only SMG resection may be perceived as less time-efficient compared with high-energy device–assisted techniques, we found that this approach reduced the incidence of unpredictable bleeding during parenchymal division. In glandular areas lacking major vascular or ductal structures, the relatively firm consistency of the SMG allowed controlled transection using bipolar cautery alone. By minimizing random bleeding and the need for repeated secondary hemostasis, operative flow became more stable; with increasing experience, unilateral SMG resection could be completed within an acceptable operative timeframe, typically on the order of 15 to 20 min, while maintaining controlled hemostasis.

Complication Profile and Nerve Safety

The incidence of transient DAO weakness in our series was lower than that reported in several external cohorts, which we attribute to meticulous bipolar dissection and avoidance of excessive surface cautery along the mandibular margin. During deep neck lifting and salivary-gland contouring, transient lower-facial weakness is not uncommon. Although numerous publications reference DAO dysfunction, many do not specify whether the deficit represents true MMN injury or temporary involvement of the cervical branch, and several reports collectively label such findings as “MMN injury” without clear clinical differentiation.

In contrast, the postoperative presentation following SMG resection in our study—preserved mentalis contraction and maintained lower-lip protrusion with isolated DAO weakness—more closely aligns with cervical-branch neurapraxia, representing a form of MMN pseudoparalysis rather than true MMN injury.40 This characteristic dissociation between DAO weakness and preserved mentalis function is illustrated in Figure 5. Consistent with this interpretation, pseudoparalysis was the predominant pattern observed when deep neck lifting included SMG resection. Anatomically, the cervical branch of the facial nerve emerges from the inferior aspect of the parotid gland and rapidly perforates the deep investing cervical fascia, after which it courses within the fibroareolar tissue adherent to the undersurface of the platysma.40 The SMG capsule, while identifiable as a discrete layer, is not an isolated membranous envelope; rather, it demonstrates continuity with the surrounding deep investing fascia through dense connective tissue attachments.6 Consequently, excessive capsular violation may transmit thermal or mechanical stress beyond the capsule itself, potentially propagating injury to adjacent neural structures coursing within the subplatysmal plane.

Based on this anatomical relationship, our technique deliberately avoids direct capsular stripping or coagulation. Instead, intracapsular dissection is performed using gentle, surface-sweeping bipolar coagulation along the gland parenchyma, with intentional preservation of the capsular–fascial interface. By minimizing capsular disruption and limiting thermal spread, this approach aims to reduce inadvertent energy transmission to the cervical branch, which may account for the low incidence and favorable recovery pattern of nerve-related complications observed in this series.

Limitations

This retrospective, single-center study is inherently limited in generalizability. However, the protocolized vessel-first, lobule-by-lobule bipolar workflow demonstrated consistent reproducibility within a drainless outpatient setting. Future prospective and multicenter studies are needed to validate cross-phenotypic applicability and to clarify the influence of skeletal support on glandular prominence.

In addition, the absence of a control or comparator group limits the ability to directly compare the safety or efficacy of this bipolar vessel-first approach with alternative dissection or energy-based techniques. Furthermore, standardized patient- or observer-reported aesthetic outcome measures were not incorporated, because the primary focus of this study was procedural safety rather than subjective contour assessment.

Procedure-specific subgroup sizes represent an additional limitation. Although SMG reduction comprised the majority of cases, parotid-tail minimal shaving was less frequent, limiting precision in complication estimates and assessment of delayed salivary sequelae. Larger parotid-focused series with longer follow-up will be necessary to refine indications and optimize capsular management strategies.

CONCLUSIONS

This study presents an anatomy-driven framework for deep neck contouring that integrates vessel-first control, lobule-based gland reduction, and adjunctive secretion management within a drainless protocol. When applied judiciously, selective SMG reduction and minimal parotid-tail shaving can be performed safely and reproducibly, contributing to improved jaw–neck definition while maintaining a low complication profile.
