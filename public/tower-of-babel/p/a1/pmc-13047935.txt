Demyelinating Leprosy Neuropathy: An Unusual and Misleading Electrophysiological Pattern
Authors: Cendrine Foucard, Marie Jachiet, Karine Viala, Thierry Maisonobe, Guillaume Fargeot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsPeripheral neuropathy, especially mononeuropathy multiplex, is a frequent manifestation of leprosy. Electrodiagnostic studies (EDX) usually show predominant axonal involvement. In this study, we report patients with prominent demyelinating abnormalities consistent with the diagnosis of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients with leprosy who underwent EDX at Pitié‐Salpêtrière University Hospital, Paris, France, between January 2014 and June 2022. We included patients exhibiting demyelinating abnormalities that fulfilled the 2021 European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS) electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP.ResultsTen patients were included. Eight patients presented with conduction blocks (CB) and temporal dispersion (TD) outside entrapment sites, primarily localized on the median and ulnar nerves in the forearm. Eight patients demonstrated delayed or absent F‐waves in nerves without segmental slowing or CB/TD. Two patients were initially diagnosed with multifocal CIDP and treated accordingly before a nerve biopsy corrected the diagnosis. Follow‐up EDX after anti‐leprosy treatment revealed partial improvement or complete resolution of CB/TD in six patients, with improvement in distal motor amplitudes in four patients.DiscussionPatients with leprosy neuropathy can exhibit demyelinating abnormalities on EDX, particularly CB/TD in the forearm, which may misleadingly suggest an immune‐mediated origin, such as multifocal CIDP. In patients from leprosy‐endemic areas with a demyelinating neuropathy fulfilling electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP, clinicians should perform a dermatological examination, repeat bacteriological and histological sampling, and consider performing a nerve biopsy if diagnostic doubt persists.

Introduction

Leprosy is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium leprae , affecting the skin, nasal mucosa, eyes, and peripheral nervous system [1, 2].

Mononeuropathy and mononeuropathy multiplex are the most common types of neuropathies, particularly in tuberculoid leprosy, with sensory symptoms linked to small fiber impairment predominating [1, 3]. The most frequently affected nerves are the ulnar, median, radial, auricular, fibular, tibial, and facial nerves [1, 2]. Other clinical presentations have also been reported, such as generalized sensorimotor polyneuropathy, autonomic neuropathy with cardiac and respiratory involvement, isolated cranial neuropathies, acute painful neuritis, and sensory neuronopathy [1]. Pure neuritic leprosy, characterized by peripheral neuropathy without skin lesions, is reported in 4%–18% of patients, often resulting in delayed diagnosis [1, 3].

Electrodiagnostic studies (EDX) are essential diagnostic tools in non‐endemic countries, particularly for pure neuritic leprosy, in patients with a high clinical suspicion of leprosy but lacking bacteriological or histological confirmation, and in cases where other neuropathy causes may coexist. EDX typically reveal mononeuropathy multiplex or asymmetric polyneuropathy, with predominant sensory nerve involvement. Sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) and distal compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes are reduced, indicating axonal loss. Prolongation of distal latencies (DLs) and segmental slowing of motor conduction velocities (MCVs) at entrapment sites—especially the ulnar nerve at the elbow—are also frequently reported [1]. Several electrophysiological and histopathological studies have documented predominant demyelinating or mixed axonal and demyelinating abnormalities, but these remain poorly characterized [4, 5, 6]. Reports of leprosy patients with neuropathy mimicking chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) or Guillain‐Barré syndrome have rarely been reported [7, 8, 9].

In this study, we report a series of 10 patients with leprosy neuropathy who exhibited unusual demyelinating abnormalities on EDX, mimicking CIDP.

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed the medical record of patients with leprosy who underwent EDX at Pitié‐Salpêtrière University Hospital, Paris, France, between January 2014 and June 2022.

Electrodiagnostic Studies (EDX)

Patients were referred to our institution for three primary reasons: First, for baseline evaluation prior to initiation of anti‐leprosy treatments in patients presenting with clinical signs suggestive of peripheral nerve involvement; second, for neurological deterioration during treatment (leprosy reactions), to confirm progression of neuropathy and guide therapeutic adjustments; and third, for a high clinical suspicion of leprosy but negative slit skin smears (SSS) and/or skin biopsy, typically corresponding to paucibacillary disease.

Standardized EDX were performed by experienced operators and included motor (median, ulnar, peroneal, tibial) and sensory (median, ulnar, radial, sural, superficial peroneal) studies. We included only patients who fulfilled the 2021 European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS) electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP [10]. Patients with demyelinating abnormalities confined to entrapment sites were excluded. Follow‐up EDX were also reviewed when available.

Leprosy Diagnosis & Classification

Leprosy diagnosis was based on the presence of characteristic skin lesions, including hypopigmented and/or erythematous hypoesthesic macules, erythema nodosum, subcutaneous nodules, and digital ulcerations, evidence of alcohol‐positive bacilli on Ziehl‐Neelsen staining of skin biopsy or SSS and/or positive Mycobacterium leprae polymerase chain reaction (PCR) on any tissue. In cases with negative bacilloscopy and PCR, the diagnosis of paucibacillary leprosy was established on the basis of a combination of epidemiological context (residence in or travel to an endemic area), clinical findings (loss of sensation in a hypopigmented or erythematous patch and/or peripheral nerve involvement), in accordance with World Health Organization recommendations, and histopathological evidence of tuberculoid granulomas. Leprosy classification was performed according to the Ridley‐Jopling (borderline tuberculoid [BT], borderline borderline [BB], borderline lepromatous [BL], lepromatous leprosy [LL]) and the WHO classifications (paucibacillary versus multibacillary) [11, 12].

Ethics

This study was approved and performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of our institutions for clinical research (IRB number: CER‐Paris‐Saclay‐2025‐76) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

One hundred and thirty patients with leprosy underwent EDX in our institution. Among these patients, we identified 10 with demyelinating neuropathy fulfilling the 2021 EAN/PNS electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP.

There were 8 men and 2 women, with a mean age of 37 years.

Before the first neurological evaluation, eight patients had already been diagnosed with leprosy (see Table 1 for leprosy classification) and were referred to our department for baseline neurological assessment due to symptoms and clinical signs suggestive of peripheral neuropathy. Two patients (patient 8 and 10) had no prior diagnosis of leprosy and were primarily referred to our institution for investigation of neuropathy. These two patients were initially diagnosed with multifocal CIDP and treated with corticosteroids alone (patient 8) or intravenous immunoglobulins (patient 10), until nerve biopsy corrected the diagnosis (see below and Text S1). Five patients experienced a Type 1 leprosy reaction before the first neurological evaluation, and two patients were already receiving anti‐leprosy treatment.

The median disease duration before the first neurological evaluation was 2.5 years [1.25–3]. Initial neurological symptoms included numbness (five patients), neuropathic pain (five patients), weakness (four patients), and paresthesias (two patients). First symptoms appeared simultaneously in the hands and feet in eight patients. At baseline, all patients exhibited sensory symptoms, affecting the extremities of all four limbs in seven patients, always in an asymmetric pattern. Five patients presented with motor deficits in a multifocal and asymmetric pattern, primarily affecting ulnar and median‐innervated muscles of the hands. No patient exhibited diffuse motor involvement, and proximal weakness was not reported.

Electrodiagnostic Studies

At baseline, all patients demonstrated asymmetric sensorimotor neuropathy with a predominant sensory component. Motor involvement was consistently multifocal and asymmetric, whereas sensory involvement was diffuse (i.e., no normal nerve) in six patients. Only three patients exhibited mononeuropathy multiplex, whereas seven patients had a diffuse asymmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy.

Eight patients had motor conduction block (CB) and/or temporal dispersion (TD) outside entrapment sites, primarily localized to the forearm segments of the median and ulnar nerves. CB/TD were bilateral in six patients, with a mean of 2.2 per patient. The two patients who did not exhibit any CB/TD were already receiving anti‐leprosy treatment prior to their first EDX. CB/TD were more frequently observed in patients with Type 1 lepra reactions (4/5) than in those without (2/5). Eight patients had absent or delayed F‐waves in nerves without significant segmental slowing or CB. Five patients exhibited prolonged DLs. All patients exhibited some degree of axonal loss, with decreased distal motor and sensory amplitudes. Nonetheless, six patients had demyelinating abnormalities in nine nerves with normal distal CMAP amplitudes, indicating pure demyelinating lesions.

Follow‐up EDX were available for eight patients, but only six patients had their baseline EDX performed prior to initiation of anti‐leprosy treatment. Among these six patients, demyelinating abnormalities were less pronounced in all on follow‐up EDX (Table S2). Notably, motor CB disappeared in three patients with no residual TD, and motor CB improved in two patients (Figure 1). One patient experienced electrophysiological deterioration of CB with severe axonal loss 1 month after starting anti‐leprosy treatment (Figure S1), while another patient developed four new CBs in the context of a Type 1 lepra reaction. Distal CMAP amplitudes improved in all patients, while SNAP amplitudes remained stable in four patients, improved in one patient and deteriorated in one patient in the context of a Type 1 lepra reaction.

Illustrative Case of Leprosy Misdiagnosis

Patient 10 was a 36‐year‐old man from Sri Lanka who has been living in France for 25 years. He presented with a three‐year history of paraesthesia in the hands and feet, without associated pain or motor weakness. EDX revealed a diffuse sensorimotor neuropathy with multiple motor CBs and TD outside entrapment sites, along with absent SNAPs in all four limbs (Table S1). Dermatological examination revealed a subtle hypopigmented macule on the left forearm. SSS were negative, and skin biopsy showed non‐specific lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, negative Ziehl‐Neelsen staining, and negative Mycobacterium leprae PCR. Leprosy was therefore not initially diagnosed, and multifocal CIDP was considered. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was normal. The patient received one course of intravenous immunoglobulin therapy (2 g/kg). Sensory symptoms improved, but several ulcerated skin lesions appeared on his hands and feet. Leprosy was reconsidered, and a nerve biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination revealed hypomyelinated fibers, massive lymphocytic infiltration, tuberculoid granulomas with epithelioid cells, and alcohol‐positive bacilli were identified in the endoneurium on Ziehl‐Neelsen staining (Figure S2). The diagnosis of leprosy was confirmed, and appropriate anti‐leprosy treatment was initiated.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we report 10 patients with leprosy presenting with demyelinating abnormalities fulfilling electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP. We showed that conduction blocks and temporal dispersion outside entrapment sites were frequent, that some patients exhibited purely demyelinating abnormalities without axonal loss on several nerves, and that partial or complete reversibility of demyelinating abnormalities frequently occurred following anti‐leprosy treatments.

Demyelinating abnormalities on EDX in leprosy patients remain poorly documented in the literature, with most published series focusing on entrapment sites, particularly the ulnar nerve at the elbow [6, 13]. In contrast, our cohort exhibited demyelinating abnormalities outside entrapment sites, notably CB/TD in the forearm segments of the ulnar and median nerves in 8 of 10 patients. This distribution supports an inflammatory pathophysiological mechanism. Only one study reported CB outside entrapment sites in 2 of 86 patients, without further characterization [14]. In our series, abnormal F‐waves in nerves without marked segmental slowing or CB were observed in eight patients, suggesting proximal demyelination. Although all patients had a mixed demyelinating and axonal neuropathy, with decreased CMAP and SNAP amplitudes, six patients exhibited demyelinating abnormalities in nine motor nerves with preserved distal CMAP amplitudes, consistent with primary and pure demyelination.

Abrupt immunological shifts in leprosy are known to trigger peri‐neural inflammatory reactions, leading either to acute demyelination with CB or to subacute demyelination‐remyelination responsible for TD [15, 16]. In line with this mechanism, we observed a higher frequency of CB/TD in patients experiencing Type 1 lepra reactions, an exacerbation of skin lesions and nerve trunks. Prominent inflammatory infiltrates were also identified in nerve biopsies of two patients (Figure S2). Six patients exhibited improvement of motor demyelinating abnormalities following initiation of anti‐leprosy therapy, including corticosteroids, which may have contributed to the resolution of inflammation. In three patients, CB completely resolved without residual TD (Figure 1). This is unusual for classical demyelination, which generally leaves residual TD, and may indicate involvement of nodal or paranodal regions, as shown in studies of teased nerve fibers [15, 16]. In contrast, sensory improvement or recovery was uncommon, suggesting earlier and more severe axonal loss in sensory than in motor nerves.

Demyelinating abnormalities outside entrapment sites typically suggest an inflammatory or immune‐mediated neuropathy. In non‐endemic countries, where inflammatory neuropathies are more prevalent than leprosy, such abnormalities may be misleading and result in a misdiagnosis of CIDP. In our series, two patients were initially diagnosed with multifocal CIDP based on compatible clinical and electrophysiological patterns and the absence of initial histopathological or bacteriological confirmation of leprosy. Consequently, both patients received unnecessary immunomodulatory treatments until nerve biopsy established the correct diagnosis. Because leprosy patients may present with demyelinating neuropathy mimicking CIDP on EDX, the diagnosis should not be excluded solely on this basis.

To avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary and potentially harmful treatments, we recommend a dermatological examination and palpation of the peripheral nerve trunks to check for hypertrophy in patients from leprosy‐endemic areas presenting with suspected CIDP, particularly multifocal forms. When conventional diagnostic samples (skin biopsy and SSS) are non‐contributory, nerve biopsy should be considered on a case‐by‐case basis. Neuroimaging, especially nerve ultrasound, is a useful tool to differentiate CIDP from leprosy by showing different patterns of nerve enlargement [17]. Unfortunately, neuroimaging was not performed in patients 8 and 10, which may have delayed diagnostic clarification. We also recommend strict adherence to CIDP clinical criteria. Although seven patients exhibited diffuse sensorimotor polyneuropathy on EDX, none fulfilled the clinical criteria for typical CIDP. Surprisingly, despite the presence of CB in eight patients, only three exhibited motor weakness, possibly due to a substantial contribution of TD in all patients [18].

Our study has several limitations. Due to the highly selected nature of patients referred to a tertiary care center, we cannot estimate the true prevalence of demyelinating neuropathy among our cohort of leprosy patients. The retrospective methodology of our study precluded precise correlations between electrophysiological and clinical features, particularly regarding the temporal relationship between motor demyelination improvements and functional recovery. By analogy with inflammatory neuropathies in which predominant demyelinating features on EDX are a favorable prognostic factor compared to predominant axonal involvement, we could hypothesize that demyelinating forms of leprosy neuropathy carry a more favorable prognosis than predominantly axonal forms. Unfortunately, comparison between the 10 patients with demyelinating neuropathy and the 120 patients with axonal neuropathy was not possible due to a lack of clinical data on the entire cohort. This should be evaluated in dedicated studies.

Conclusion

Patients with leprosy neuropathy may exhibit demyelinating abnormalities on electrodiagnostic studies, particularly conduction blocks and temporal dispersion in the forearm segments of the ulnar and median nerves that closely mimic immune‐mediated neuropathies such as multifocal CIDP. Consequently, in patients originating from endemic areas, leprosy should not be excluded solely on the basis of CIDP‐compatible electrodiagnostic findings. A thorough dermatological examination, repeated bacteriological and histological samples, and consideration of nerve biopsy are essential when diagnostic uncertainty persists.
