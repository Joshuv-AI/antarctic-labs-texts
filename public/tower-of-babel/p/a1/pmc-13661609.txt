Detailed immunophenotypic and genetic characterization of bone marrow clonal plasma cells and B-cells in early-stage monoclonal gammopathies
Authors: Oihane Pérez-Escurza, P Martijn Kolijn, Jón Þórir Óskarsson, María González-González, María Jara-Acevedo, Julio del Pozo, Quentin Lécrevisse, Juan Flores-Montero, Luzalba Sanoja-Flores, Silvia Martín, Elín Ruth Reed, Stephen Harding, Sigrún Thorsteinsdottir, Sæmundur Rögnvaldsson, Thorvardur Jon Love, Brian Durie, Anton W Langerak, Sigurdur Yngvi Kristinsson, Alberto Orfao, ECRIN-M3
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonoclonal gammopathy (MG) of undetermined significance (MGUS) is the earliest and most prevalent stage of MG that may progress to symptomatic multiple myeloma (MM) or Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia (WM) through intermediate smoldering forms of both diseases. We used next-generation flow cytometry (NGF) to identify and purify bone marrow (BM) clonal plasma cells (cPC) and B-cells/lymphoplasmacytic cells (cB-cells) from 25 asymptomatic individuals with MG diagnosed within the population-based iStopMM screening program for further molecular analysis by next-generation sequencing (NGS). IgM-MGUS cases consistently exhibited WM-like phenotypes by NGF, and MYD88 p.L265P and/or CXCR4 mutations in the coexisting cPC and cB-cells. Conversely, cPC from non-IgM MGUS and smoldering MM (SMM) displayed a MM-like phenotype associated with genetic drivers –i.e., t(11;14)–, frequently coexisting with genetically unrelated cB-cells. Interestingly, recurrent RUNX1 single nucleotide variants were detected in cPC, but not coexisting cB-cells, from WM-like cases, suggesting a role for RUNX1 in PC differentiation. In summary, NGF enabled detailed immunophenotypic characterization of BM cPC and cB-cell populations in all cases analyzed, subdivided in IgM vs non-IgM cases with WM-like or MM-like phenotypic and genetic profiles, respectively. These findings underscore the potential contribution of NGF and NGS for accurate classification or early-stage disease, beyond conventional criteria.Subject terms: Cancer genomics, Immunoproliferative disorders

Introduction

Monoclonal gammopathy (MG) of undetermined significance (MGUS) is the most prevalent clonal plasma cell (PC) neoplasm, an asymptomatic condition that precedes malignant (symptomatic) PC diseases such as multiple myeloma (MM) or Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia (WM), via the intermediate smoldering MM (SMM) and smoldering WM (SWM) stages, respectively [1–4]. Population-based screening studies revealed a frequency of MGUS of ≈3.2–5% among adults older than 50 years (y), depending on the method used to detect a serum monoclonal (M)-component, i.e., serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) followed by confirmatory immunofixation (IF) or mass spectrometry (MS) [5–8].

Among MGUS, IgM-MGUS typically progresses to SWM and WM (or non-WM non-Hodgkin lymphoma), and rarely to IgM-MM [3, 9–11]. In contrast, non-IgM MGUS and light-chain MGUS more frequently evolve to SMM and MM [4]. Since WM and MM represent disease conditions that display different clinical features and require distinct management approaches and therapeutic interventions, a clear-cut differential diagnosis between both entities already at the earliest e.g., MGUS stages becomes of utmost clinically relevance. At present, discrimination between both disease entities at the MGUS stage is not accurate and it mainly relies on the immunoglobulin (Ig) heavy-chain isotype of the secreted M-component (i.e., non-IgM vs IgM), combined with cytomorphological and/or histopathological assessment of the bone marrow (BM), including enumeration of clonal PC (cPC) alone and together with presence of ( > 10%) lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates in SWM vs MGUS, respectively, in the absence of organomegalies and organ dysfunction (i.e., CRAB criteria: hypercalcemia, renal impairment, anemia, bone disease) [4, 12].

In recent years, next-generation flow cytometry (NGF) has emerged as a highly sensitive technique for detecting clonal PC, B-lymphocytes and lymphoplasmacytic cells in patients with MG, both at diagnosis and following therapy [13, 14]. In addition, NGF has confirmed the occurrence of uniquely different immunophenotypes of cPC, clonal B-cells and/or lymphoplasmacytic cells (cB-cells) in SMM/MM (CD19−CD38lo CD45−/lo CD56−/+ CD117−/+ cPC without clonally-related B-cells) vs SWM/WM (CD19+ CD45+ CD56− CD117− cPC coexisting with clonally-related CD19+ CD20+ CD22lo CD25+ B-cells). The latter cB-cells can also be clearly differentiated from (CD5+ CD20lo CD23+) cB-cells typically found in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)-like (or other less frequently non-CLL-like) monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis (MBL) cases [14–20]. The clonal nature of phenotypically aberrant cPC and cB-cells can be further confirmed by the restricted expression of Ig light chain kappa (κ) or lambda (λ) in the cytoplasm (cy) and/or the cell surface (s) membrane of the suspicious cPC and cB-cell populations, respectively [19, 20]. Despite distinct aberrant immunophenotypic patterns have been described for cPC and/or cB-cells in SMM and MM vs SWM and WM, systematic phenotypic and molecular characterization of these populations at the precursor non-IgM MGUS and IgM-MGUS stages remains incomplete, particularly among non-IgM MGUS and IgM-MGUS cases with coexisting (genetically related and unrelated) cPC and cB-cells.

Here, we investigated the clonal relationship between coexisting cPC and cB-cells, together with the occurrence of recurrent MM- vs. WM-associated genetic alterations in BM of a subgroup of (otherwise) healthy adult donors included in the Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents MM (iStopMM) program [21], who showed the presence of a serum M-component. Our main goal was to characterize the immunophenotypic and molecular patterns of cPC and/or cB-cells and to assess the utility of NGF for a more accurate diagnostic classification of MG, already at the earliest MGUS stages identified among subjects from the general population via diagnostic screening programs. In addition, we aimed at in-depth analysis of the genetic landscape of the purified compartments of coexisting BM cPC and cB-cells to identify molecular profiles that might contribute to the differential accumulation of genetically-related cPC vs cB-cells, already at the earliest stages of WM-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

A total of 25 individuals (13 women and 12 men; median age of 68 years, ranging from 48 to 86 years) selected from a cohort of 75 individuals which had been consecutively diagnosed with a MG without prior history of a lymphoproliferative disorder, previously evaluated by next-generation flow cytometry (NGF) within the Iceland Screens, Treats, or Prevents Multiple Myeloma (iStopMM) study [21], were enrolled in this substudy. iStopMM is a nationwide, population-based screening study from Iceland in which adult ( ≥ 40 years) volunteers are being screened for serum and/or urine M-protein, with positive cases undergoing further evaluation, including PB and BM sampling. These 75 individuals correspond to participants from whom paired BM and PB samples were obtained, which were subsequently analyzed by NGF for the identification and characterization of clonal B-cell and/or PC populations between September 2019 and December 2022. From this cohort, a subgroup of 25 subjects was selected for this study according to the following criteria: (1) presence of a serum M-protein by high-sensitive capillary zone electrophoresis and/or an altered free Ig light chain (FLC)-ratio (The Binding Site, Birmingham, UK); associated with, (2) ≥ 1 clearly defined population of cPC coexisting or not with ≥ 1 population of cB-cells as identified by NGF; and, 3) availability of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-purified BM cPC and/or cB-cells for further molecular analyses. Following the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) diagnostic criteria [1], cases were classified as MGUS (n = 19) and SMM (n = 6). The remaining cases could not be included due to the absence of detectable clonal populations and the inability to reliably purify enough number of clonal B-cells due to limited sample availability after the NGF analyses and/or very low clonal B-cell/PC infiltration levels. Paired EDTA-anticoagulated bone marrow (BM) aspirated samples (2 mL) and peripheral blood (10 mL) specimens from each donor were collected in Iceland and sent to the University of Salamanca (Salamanca, Spain) for sample processing within 36 h, as described elsewhere [22, 23].

All subjects enrolled in this study provided their informed consent to participate according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Icelandic National Bioethics Committee (NCT03327597), and the Icelandic Data Protection Agency. Permission for transfer of all biological samples for this study (Registration number: 2020/252) was granted by the Spanish Ministry of Health.

Immunophenotypic studies

For immunophenotypic identification, characterization and purification of cPC and other coexisting cB-cell populations, the EuroFlow 2-tube 8-color NGF-MM minimal residual disease (MRD) antibody panel and the Lymphocyte Screening Tube (LST) were used to stain BM samples (Suppl. Table 1), as previously described [22]. Stained samples were measured in FACSCantoTM II or FACSLyricTM flow cytometers –Becton/Dickinson (BD) Biosciences, San Jose, CA–, and data was analyzed with the InfinicytTM software (v2.1.a.000; BD-Cytognos, Salamanca, Spain) as detailed elsewhere [22].

Purification of individual populations of clonal plasma cells and B-cells and extraction of genomic DNA

Populations of cPC (n = 28) and cB-cells (n = 20) were purified from BM samples, after they had been stained with a panel of antibodies directed against cell surface membrane markers, using a BD FACSAriaTM III instrument (Cell Sorting Service, University of Salamanca, Spain). A median (range) number of 60,000 (900–1,300,000 cells) cPC with a median purity of 98%, and of 40,000 (500-8,000,000 cells) cB-cells with a median purity of 99% (assessed only in samples with > 5000 cells isolated), were collected. Genomic (g) DNA was extracted from the FACS-sorted cells using the QIAamp® DNA micro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

IGHV gene sequencing and identification of recurrent MM-related and WM-associated genetic alterations

An IGHV leader-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay was used to identify IGHV sequences of purified (paired) cPC (n = 10) and cB-cells (n = 14) from a subset of 10/25 cases (7 MGUS and 3 SMM), and to establish the clonal relationship among those clonal cell populations coexisting in the same sample. For this purpose, the leader-based IGH variable (V) region clonal rearrangement NGS assay (Leader assay), and the EuroClonality-NGS Working Group standard operating procedures (SOP) were used, as described elsewhere [24]. Briefly, amplification of IGHV gene rearrangements was performed using the IGHV leader primer set, including 24 forward primers directed at the IGHV leader sequences and a single reverse primer designed against a conserved IGHJ sequence [24], for a one-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR). PCR products were then pooled in an equimolar (4 nM) solution into two separate multiplexed library pools for cPC and cB-cells, to avoid cross-contamination. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 300 pb) was performed on an Illumina MiSeqTM sequencer using the MiSeq reagent v3 kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing data were analyzed using the ArresT/Interrogate platform (EuroClonality-NGS Working Group) for accurate clonotype identification, as previously described [25]. The clonal relationship between cell populations from the same sample was established by direct comparison of the dominant clonotype sequence (i.e., identical IGHV–IGHD–IGHJ junctional regions) together with identical complementarity determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences across the different cell compartments derived from individual BM samples. Clonotypes with low read counts or below variant-allele frequency (VAF) thresholds ( < 3%) were considered non-informative and excluded from those analyses. Due to technical limitations and/or insufficient amount of gDNA, this analysis could not be performed on the remaining FACS-purified cPC (n = 18) and cB-cells (n = 6) gDNA samples.

The presence of recurrent MM- and WM-associated genetic alterations was investigated in gDNA from FACS-purified cPC (n = 23) and cB-cells (n = 16) from 25/25 study cases (19 MGUS and 6 SMM) following the EuroClonality-NGS DNA capture assay and SOP [26] for detection of chromosomal translocations/gene fusions, somatic gene mutations and IGH, IGK and IGL gene rearrangements associated with lymphoid neoplasms [26]. For library preparation and for hybridization of libraries to the EuroClonality-NDC enrichment probes, the KAPA HyperPlus kit (Roche Sequencing Solutions, Pleasanton, CA) and both the KAPA HyperCapture Reagent Kit and the KAPA HyperCapture Bead Kit (Roche Sequencing Solutions) were used, respectively. The Illumina MiSeqTM and NextSeqTM 500/550 sequencers were employed for paired-end sequencing (75 bp) performed with the 150-cycle Mid Output Kit (Illumina). NGS data from FASTQ files was processed through the EuroClonality-NDC Analysis App (Univ8 Genomics, Belfast, UK) from the Illumina BaseSpace Sequence Hub (BSSH), following the EuroClonality-NDC Assay Analysis Guide (Univ8 Genomics). Chromosomal translocations and single nucleotide variants (SNVs) involving those genes more frequently altered in MGUS, MM and WM, as well as IGH, IGK and IGL gene rearrangements, were selected following the EuroClonality-NDC Assay Univ8 Analysis Guide criteria. Sequence variants were first filtered by retaining only those supported by >5 alternate unique reads with VAF > 3%, and excluding variants classified as “complete” by the pipeline (a category encompassing non-functional, synonymous and/or highly-polymorphic variants). Subsequently, only pathogenic or likely-pathogenic variants previously reported in hematological malignancies were kept for downstream analyses, excluding benign variants or those not reported in this context.

Statistical methods

The Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess the statistical significance of differences observed between groups of individuals or cell populations for continuous (unpaired) variables. For those statistical analyses, the Statistical software Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v28.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) was used. For the identification of those immunophenotypic markers that contributed (most) to the discrimination between normal (n)PC and cPC, normal (n)B-cells and cB-cells, and between non-IgM MGUS cPC, SMM cPC and WM-like cPC, multivariate canonical analysis (CA) was employed with the InfinicytTM software (v2.1.a.000; BD-Cytognos SL.). Boxplot graphical representations were used to display the data with GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). Statistical significance was set at p-values < 0.05.

Results

Demographic, clinical and laboratory features at diagnosis

According to conventional IMWG diagnostic criteria, the study cohort comprised 25 asymptomatic individuals diagnosed with MG: 19 cases were initially classified as MGUS (8 women and 11 men; median age of 65 y, ranging from 48 to 86 y) and 6 had SMM (5 women and 1 man; median age of 82 y, ranging from 58 to 85 y). Among MGUS cases, BM biopsy systematically showed PC infiltration levels of ≤10% at diagnosis ( ≤ 5% in 7/19 and 6–10% in 9/19 cases). In the remaining 3/19 cases, BM samples were of poor quality for reliable histopathological assessment; however, complementary BM cytomorphologic and flow cytometry data showed cPC levels of 6% and 1%, respectively, whereas it could not be quantified in the third case. These results were associated with low serum IgM (n = 7), IgG (n = 8) and IgA (n = 4) M-protein concentrations –median (range): 3 g/l (0–8.9 g/l)–. In turn, the majority of patients newly diagnosed with SMM in parallel to the MGUS cases (4/6) displayed between 11 and 15% PC in their BM biopsy with > 15% BM PC levels in the other 2/6 cases, together with greater median (range) serum M-protein concentrations of 13.5 g/l (0.4–15.8 g/l) (Suppl. Table 2).

High-sensitive immunophenotypic studies and final diagnosis

NGF analyses identified coexisting cPC and cB-cells in BM of 15/25 (60%) of cases including 12/19 MGUS and 3/6 SMM patients. Among those 15 cases, a second additional cB-cell population was found in BM of 1/6 SMM patients, whereas a second cPC population together with an additional (second) cB-cell population were identified in 1/12 MGUS cases, and a second cPC population together with three different cB-cell populations were found in another MGUS case. In the remaining 7/19 MGUS and 3/6 SMM patients, a single cPC was identified in the absence of cB-cells, except for one MGUS case that showed two coexisting cPC populations (Suppl. Table 3).

Based on the above NGF immunophenotypic data, a WM-like immunophenotypic profile was identified in 7/12 MGUS (7/7 corresponding to IgM-MGUS) and 1/3 SMM (1/1 biclonal IgG/IgM-SMM) cases, as determined by the presence of (coexisting) phenotypically-related populations of cPC and cB-cells. Accordingly, all eight cases were hereafter referred to as WM-like cases (n = 8). In contrast, the remaining 17/25 individuals exhibited an immunophenotypic profile consistent with MM-like cPC alone, or cPC coexisting with ≥1 phenotypically unrelated cB-cell populations resembling low-count CLL-like or non-CLL-like MBL. Based on those NGF results, hereafter cases were regrouped as 12 non-IgM MGUS, 5 SMM and 8 WM-like MGUS cases (IgM-MGUS plus IgG/IgM SMM) (Suppl. Table 3).

Differential immunophenotypic profiles between normal and clonal plasma cells and B-cells

As expected, BM PC (both nPC and cPC) showed downregulation of CD19 and CD45, positivity for CD27, CD38, CD56 and CD138 and increased expression of total (cyIg+sIg) cyIgκ or cyIgλ together with a larger size –forward light scatter (FSC)– and internal complexity –sideward light scatter (SSC)– compared to both mature nB-cells and cB-cells (p ≤ 0.008) (Fig. 1A–C; Table 1; Suppl. Figs. 1A–C and 2A). Among PC, cPC showed reduced CD19 (p < 0.001), CD27 (p < 0.001), CD38 (p = 0.02), CD81 (p < 0.001) and higher amounts of expression of CD117 (p = 0.04) compared to nPC (Fig. 1A–C; Table 1; Suppl. Figs. 1A–C and 2A). In turn, cB-cells displayed decreased CD20 (p = 0.003), CD38 (p = 0.007) and CD81 (p < 0.001) expression levels together with a greater size (p = 0.03), internal complexity (p = 0.04) and positivity for CD5 (p < 0.001), CD27 (p = 0.001), CD45 (p = 0.001) and CD138 (p = 0.04), compared to nB-lymphocytes (Fig. 1A, B; Table 1; Suppl. Fig. 1A–C and 2A).

Clonal relationship between coexisting cPC and cB-cells

Successful NGS-based IGHV and/or IGK/IGL gene sequencing was achieved for 23/28 (82%) purified cPC populations and 16/20 (80%) cB-cell populations. Regarding cases with coexisting cPC plus cB-cell populations, complete (paired) clonality data was yielded for 12 (6 non-IgM MGUS/SMM and 6 WM-like MGUS) cases. Comparison of IGHV and/or IGK/IGL clonal rearrangements between coexisting cPC and cB-cells revealed identical clonotypes in both clonal cell populations in 6/6 WM-like MGUS cases with successful sequencing, but none of the non-IgM MGUS and SMM patients, these latter cases displaying discordant IGHV and IGK and IGL gene rearrangements in coexisting cPC and cB-cells (Table 2).

Genetic alterations in WM-like MGUS, non-IgM MGUS and SMM cases

The EuroClonality-DNA capture assay for detection of genetic alterations associated with lymphoid neoplasms was successfully performed on purified cPC and/or cB-cells of 25/25 cases, including 8 WM-like MGUS, 12 non-IgM MGUS and 5 SMM subjects (Fig. 2; Table 2; Suppl. Fig. 3). Briefly, 8/8 WM-like MGUS cases systematically showed MYD88 (7/8 cases) and/or CXCR4 (3/8 cases) mutations in both cPC and cB-cells of 5/5 and 2/2 cases investigated, respectively. In addition, RUNX1 variants (SNV plus one substitution and one indel), were a hallmark of WM-like cases, while absent in non-IgM MGUS and SMM; however, in contrast to MYD88 and CXCR4 mutations, RUNX1 mutations were systematically restricted to the cPC compartment (6/6 cases tested), while absent in the paired cB-cells. Likewise, CREBBP mutations were also found in 4/8 WM-like MGUS cases, among whom they were restricted to cPC (2/4 cases), to cB-cells (1/4 cases) or shared by both cell populations (1/4 cases). Other mutations identified in WM-like MGUS cases involved the ARID1A, TP53, PTPRD, TENT5C, FOXO1, KMT2D and ATM genes, the first three altered genes being found in the same patient, whereas the other four were detected in a single patient each. Except for the TP53 and ATM genes, those mutations were typically restricted to cB-cells (ARID1A, TENT5C, FOXO1 and KMT2D) or to cPC (PTPRD). Gene fusions derived from chromosomal translocations were detected in only 3/8 WM-like MGUS cases (38%), being restricted to the cPC compartment in 2/3 individuals. Those translocations consisted of t(2;7) and t(2;14) involving the IGKV::SLC26A3 and the LTBP1::IGH-switch genes; the latter rearrangement was also found in cB-cells of a third altered case.

In contrast to WM-like cases, the presence of t(11;14) involving the CCND1::IGH (IGH-switch, IGHD or IGHJ regions) genes was the single most frequent alteration in both non-IgM MGUS (5/12, 42%) and SMM (3/5, 60%) (Fig. 2; Suppl. Fig. 3). Additional IGH-associated translocations involving different partner genes (i.e., HCRTR2, WWOX/MAF, PAX3, and LTBP1) and a t(2;19) involving the LTBP1::TCF3 genes were identified in 3/12 non-IgM MGUS cases, rarely coexisting within the same individual across distinct clonal populations. In addition, mutations in the CREBBP and ARID1A genes or in the DIS3, ATM, and NRAS genes were detected in two non-IgM MGUS cases, respectively, whereas mutations in the histone H1-3 gene (alone or associated with mutations in the H1-2 gene) were found in another two non-IgM MGUS subjects, one also carrying t(11;14). Likewise, a single mutation at the TCF3 gene and coexistence of RUNX1 and CREBBP mutations were identified each in one SMM patient which also carried t(11;14). Except for a non-IgM MGUS case who showed two mutations in the RUNX1 gene in their cPC, associated with an additional SF3B1 mutation in the coexisting CLL-like MBL cells, no genetic alterations were identified with the gene panel used in the remaining 2/12 MGUS and 2/5 SMM individuals (Fig. 2A).

Of note, DNA copy number alterations could not be accurately assessed because of insufficient sequencing data quality, likely resulting from the limited number of purified cells and DNA input, which compromised library resolution after DNA amplification.

Disease progression

Monitoring of the serum monoclonal component and BM biopsy analysis during follow-up revealed changes in 11/25 (44%) patients after median (range) time from diagnosis of 27.5 months (2–55 months) (Fig. 3). These changes involved 5/12 (42%) non-IgM MGUS who subsequently met criteria of SMM (3/12, 25%) and MM (2/12, 17%) and 6/8 (75%) IgM-MGUS who met stable (5/6 cases) or transient (1/6 patients) criteria of SWM; subsequently, one of those cases evolved to an overt WM (Fig. 3). In addition, the one non-IgM MGUS patient with coexisting SF3B1-mutated cB-cells was subsequently diagnosed with CLL during follow-up, while two SMM were (transiently or stably) redefined as non-IgM MGUS. During the study period, four additional patients died from causes unrelated to documented disease progression (Fig. 3).

Discussion

NGF immunophenotyping has emerged as a key tool to improve the classification of early-MG, through high-sensitive identification and characterization of the underlying cPC and/or cB-cell populations [22]. While immunophenotypic profiles of these clonal cell populations are well-established in overt (symptomatic) MM and WM patients, comparative data related to the earliest disease stages, particularly non-IgM or IgM-MGUS, still remain limited. Here, we investigated the utility of NGF for the identification and characterization of BM cPC and/or cB-cells present in (otherwise healthy and asymptomatic) adults with early-stage MG identified through the population-based iStopMM screening program in Iceland [21]. In addition, we assessed the contribution of NGF to improving disease classification and validated immunophenotypic findings through NGS analysis of purified clonal cell populations, targeting recurrent genetic alterations associated with MM, WM and other related mature B-cell neoplasms [27–31].

BM cPC and/or cB-cell populations were immunophenotypically assigned to either an MM-like or WM-like disease profiles [22]. Notably, phenotypic stratification by NGF revealed that all IgM-MGUS individuals and one SMM case with biclonal IgG plus IgM serum M-components, unequivocally exhibited an WM-like immunophenotypic profile, and was more likely to be classified within the spectrum of WM-like neoplasms [16, 32]. As expected, this reassignment was supported by the demonstration of identical IGHV and IGK/IGL rearrangements between coexisting cPC and cB-cells, together with MYD88 p.L265P and/or CXCR4 gene mutations consistent with the genomic landscape of WM [27, 30, 33–37]. The reassignment of the SMM case into the WM-like group is particularly noteworthy given the distinct biological trajectories and clinical implications of both entities [38, 39]. Together, these findings underscore the relevance of the specific cell driving MG based on immunophenotypic and molecular features, rather than relying exclusively on conventional diagnostic criteria.

In contrast, cPC and (when present) coexisting cB-cells, from non-IgM MGUS/SMM cases displayed immunophenotypic profiles consistent with MM-like disease and harbored recurrent MM-related genetic alterations, most commonly IGH translocations, –i.e., t(11;14)–, without MYD88 or CXCR4 mutations [40, 41]. Importantly, in non-IgM MGUS/SMM cases where coexisting cB-cell populations were detected in addition to cPC, these typically exhibited a CLL-like (and less frequently a non-CLL-like) MBL phenotype, associated with unrelated IGHV and/or IGK/IGL gene rearrangements between cPC and cB-cells, confirming that they most likely represent independent neoplastic cell compartments, rather than belonging to a single evolving clone. Although coexistence of clonally unrelated B-cell and PC populations has been only sporadically reported [42, 43], population-based studies have shown that CLL-like MBL clones are frequently detectable in (otherwise) healthy adults using high-sensitive NGF [44, 45]. Thus, the presence of minor clonal B-cell populations in individuals with MM-like cPC on immunophenotypic grounds should not be interpreted per se, as evidence of an underlying lymphoplasmacytic disorder, but rather as a risk factor for lymphoid malignancy development [46]. Our observations are also aligned with recent clinical evidence showing that approximately 10% of MGUS cases from the iStopMM study present with multiple paraproteins, most of them being transient, and some of them displaying more than one aberrant cPC population, suggesting coexistence of biologically independent processes, rather than linear clonal evolution [47].

Interestingly, recurrent genetic alterations, including both t(11;14) in cPC (with high median VAF) from nearly half of non-IgM MGUS and more than half of SMM cases, and MYD88 p.L265P mutation (with median VAF of 44%) in both cPC and cB-lymphocytes of WM-like MGUS cases, were detected at both high frequency and high variant allele burden in early-stage disease. These findings indicate that such alterations likely arise early during clonal ontogeny and may act as initiating events driving disease development, being detectable at high VAF, not only in the terminally differentiated cPC, but also in upstream precursors such as the coexisting cB-cell populations in WM-like MGUS cases [35, 36, 48, 49]. In contrast, in one of our CXCR4-mutated WM-like cases, MYD88 p.L265P was detected exclusively in the cB-cell compartment at low VAF (i.e., 13%), while absent in the remaining CXCR4-mutated cPC fraction, potentially reflecting a MYD88 mutational burden below the detection threshold in cPC in an individual where the CXCR4 mutation could represent the driver genetic event, as discussed below [27].

Beyond those potential (recurrent) genetic drivers, we further identified a complex landscape of shared and/or cell compartment-restricted genetic alterations, even in cases with clonally related cPC and cB-cell populations. Most interestingly, recurrent RUNX1 mutations were identified exclusively in the cPC compartment of WM-like cases, while absent in their coexisting cB-cells, despite clear evidence of a common origin of both clonal cell populations. Although recurrent RUNX1 mutations were identified exclusively in the cPC compartment, and they have not been previously reported in WM and SWM, RUNX1 is known to be upregulated during B-cell differentiation toward plasmablasts and PC, its disruption delaying this transition in MM [50]. Our findings would suggest that RUNX1 mutations may promote PC differentiation from the cB-cell precursor population, thus contributing to the disease evolutionary profile, already at early stages of WM disease (i.e., IgM-MGUS with a WM-like profile) rather than clonal initiation [34, 50]. Notably, despite two translocations involved chromosome 2, only one affected the IGK locus. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a IGK gene translocation in WM-like MGUS patients, though translocations involving the IGL gene locus in chromosome 22 have been sporadically reported in WM [31].

The remaining alterations were identified as being restricted to a single clonal B-cell or PC compartment and were largely non-recurrent, reflecting i) subclonal heterogeneity in WM-like cases, and ii) the absence of clonal relationship between cPC and the coexisting (independent) cB-cell populations in non-IgM MGUS/SMM cases [35, 37]. In this regard, CXCR4, ATM and TP53 mutations were detected in both cPC and cB-cell compartments of WM-like cases [51, 52]. Of note, CXCR4 mutations, typically considered to be subclonal, and acquired after MYD88 p.L265P, were usually detected at lower frequency and VAF, consistent with prior reports, except for the one case discussed above, where CXCR4 emerged as the primary genetic event [35, 37, 53, 54]. Conversely, cPC-restricted mutations showed a more heterogeneous distribution, including PTPRD and specific IGH translocations in WM-like cases, RUNX1 in non-IgM MGUS and SMM, and CREBBP in SMM. In non-IgM MGUS cases with cPC only, mutations in ATM, NRAS, DIS3, H1-2 and/or H1-3, as well as high-risk IGH translocations, were identified. Several of these cases progressed to SMM or MM, highlighting the potential role of e.g., ATM, NRAS and DIS3 mutations as early drivers of disease evolution and genomic instability, in line with prior observations [28, 55–58]. Notably, SMM cases showed a lower mutational burden compared to MGUS, which is likely attributable to the smaller number of SMM cases in our cohort rather than reflecting true biological differences. Additionally, it is worth noting the high rate of progression from MGUS to SMM/MM or SWM/WM observed here. This may reflect a selection bias toward MGUS cases with higher levels of BM infiltration, as these patients were more likely to i) yield enough purified cPC and/or cB-cells for inclusion in this study and ii) exhibit infiltration levels closer to the diagnostic threshold for discriminating between MGUS and both SMM and SWM. Therefore, the above findings should be considered with caution. Likewise, transition to an earlier disease stage should not be directly interpreted as true biological regression, but rather a reclassification based on the evaluation of a single BM aspirated sample in the context of a low tumor burden, which may highlight the potential inaccuracy in some patients, of single histopathologic and cytomorphologic BM assessments for diagnostic purposes, underscoring the need for approaches that minimize reliance on isolated measurements and/or provide more accurate quantification of BM cPC and cB-lymphocytes. Further studies in larger patient cohorts followed for longer periods are needed to accurately establish the rate of progression of our cases compared to other patients included in the iStopMM program.

Despite the selection criteria significantly reducing sample size from the initially available cohort due to the requirement of sufficient numbers of FACS-purified clonal B-cells and PC for downstream molecular analyses, thereby introducing a degree of selection bias towards cases with higher clonal burden, all included cases originate from a population-based screening setting and therefore remain representative of the earliest stages of MG.

In summary, our results show that even with a limited sample size, NGF enabled detailed characterization of cPC and cB-cells in early-stage MG, revealing substantial biological heterogeneity even among MGUS cases identified through population-based screening. Integration of NGF, cell sorting and NGS confirms the presence of MM-like and, particularly, WM-like driver genetic alterations already at the earliest stages of the disease and reveals compartment-specific genetic complexity, any conclusion about its relative frequency requiring further studies in larger patient cohorts. Importantly, NGF-based classification refined diagnostic accuracy in around one-quarter of our cases, compared to conventional criteria alone, which supports its role as a complementary frontline diagnostic tool for MG.
