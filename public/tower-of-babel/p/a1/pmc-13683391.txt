Deep Ocean Temperature from the Stratus Ocean Reference Station (85 °W, 20 °S), 2012–2025
Authors: Yu Gao, Albert Plueddemann, Robert A Weller, Sebastien Bigorre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term deep ocean temperature monitoring is crucial for understanding the ocean’s role in climate variability and storage of heat in the deep ocean. Observation of the ocean surface is relatively accessible via both in-situ and remote sensing; however, continuous, high-temporal resolution, decade-long temperature records from abyssal depths face the technical challenges of sustained deep ocean deployments. The addition of calibrated, internally-recording temperature sensors to deep-ocean moorings not far from the sea floor provides a means of making high temporal resolution temperature observations. Quality controlling and merging records from successive mooring deployments results in a decade-long time series. Our work has been to optimize approaches for ensuring data quality and continuity in multi-year deep ocean temperature datasets. Here we show a comprehensive processing framework that yields 13 years (2012 to 2025) of continuous temperature measurements at approximately 4200 to 4500 meters depth from the Stratus Ocean Reference Station near 22 °S, 85 °W, 1500 km off the coast of Chile in the Southeast Pacific. Our framework incorporates timing checks, automated spike detection, systematic multi-sensor validation, statistical validation, human-in-the-loop quality control, and merging protocols. This framework establishes reproducible standards for processing long term oceanographic observations from multiple deployments. For the Stratus data set, the result is a unique, decade-long abyssal temperature record with quantified uncertainties that constitutes a benchmark time series for evaluating the realism of deep ocean temperature in models.

Background & Summary

Long-term continuous observation of deep ocean temperature can help understand the ocean’s role in climate and ocean heat content variability. Compared to the surface of the ocean, deep ocean observations are rare, especially decade-long continuous temperature at abyssal depth (typically defined from 3,000 to 6,500 meters). About a decade ago, the physical oceanography community recognized the need for additional temperature observations from the deep oceans to drive improvement of model representation of the downward mixing of heat and other properties into the deep ocean. Such measurements could be assimilated into ocean models and used to assess the realism of climate model outputs. Bottom mounted instrument packages and existing moorings provide opportunities to add observations of deep ocean temperature. Tracey et al.1 examined a four-year record of deep temperature observations in the Drake Passage and Meinen et al.2 reported a decade of near-bottom temperature observations in the Argentine Basin, both from pressure‐equipped inverted echo sounder (PIES) moorings. Uchida et al.3 show a 13 year record of near-bottom temperature from a CTD mounted 34 m above the seafloor.

To further increase deep temperature observations, OceanSITES4, the international collaborative effort to collect sustained time series observations at open ocean sites under the Global Ocean Observing System (GOOS), launched an effort to coordinate deployment of temperature recorders on existing deep ocean moorings. The Upper Ocean Processes Group at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) joined this effort and committed to deploy pairs of temperature recorders on Ocean Reference Stations5. A station in the North Pacific Ocean (47 °N, 160 °E) produced a decade-long continuous temperature at abyssal depth from 2010 to 20243. This paper focuses on the work at another one of these Ocean Reference Stations to obtain, quality control, document, and share deep ocean temperature time series.

The Stratus Ocean Reference Station (ORS) is located near 22 °S, 85 °W, 1,500 km west of northern Chile. Deployment of deep-ocean instruments at the Stratus site started in 2012 using two Conductivity, Temperature and Depth Instruments (CTDs) on the mooring. Sampling began on 2012-05-28 02:30:00 and data collected up to 2025-03-18 11:00:00 UTC are considered. On a roughly annual basis, a new surface mooring was deployed and the existing mooring recovered. Due to occasional mooring and instrument problems, some short gaps exist between 2012 and 2025. Overall, gaps represent only 0.07% of the data set. The longest gap is 105.5 hours (4.4 days) between 2014-03-03T12:30 and 2014-03-07T22:30, which occurs between the recovery of Stratus 12 and the deployment of Stratus 13. Temperature observations were collected using Seabird SBE-16 and SBE-37 instrument pairs mounted at a fixed depth above the sea floor. Each sensor was calibrated prior to deployment. Although the instruments record temperature, conductivity and (in some cases) pressure, only the temperature data have been processed and released to date. Temperature sensing was found to be stable, while conductivity and salinity computed from it were not stable in these deep deployments, and a means to correct them is not yet in hand.

Instrument depths range from 4191 to 4526 meters. Instrument depth is determined by subtracting the instrument height above the bottom from the water depth. Instrument height is a fixed depth of 39 m, determined from summation of the mooring components from the anchor to the load bar containing the instruments. The water depth is obtained from a shipboard fathometer and is corrected for sound speed variation. This value is unique to each deployment and is denoted as “water_depth_from_ship_corrected_m” in the metadata.

Temperature data are processed and quality-controlled using six major steps: timing checks, automated spike detection, systematic multi-sensor validation, statistical validation, expert validation, and merging protocols. The processed dataset includes temperature, latitude, longitude, instrument depth and timestamps. The dataset spans multiple mooring deployments. The moorings are deployed for year-long intervals with a standardized design, traceable sensor calibrations and overlapping “burn-in” (validation) periods to ensure data continuity and quality5. Data gaps are present between some deployments, and minor gaps may also arise during periods of instrument failure and other operational issues. The deployments were merged into a continuous time series as described in the metadata. Data gaps are filled with standard fill values and are therefore readily identifiable.

The merged Stratus time series represents the longest in-situ deep ocean temperature record in the region, with potential uses including studies of ocean heat content and validation of ocean models. The data contributes to broader observational programs such as OceanSITES and GOOS.

Methods

Instrumentation

Temperature data were collected using pairs of either SBE-16 or SBE-37 microCAT sensors6. For each deployment, a pair of identical sensors were deployed 39 meters above the mooring anchor on the sea floor. All sensors were factory-calibrated prior to deployment preparation. In preparation for deployment, all sensors underwent a laboratory burn-in period during which the instruments were run continuously to verify sensor stability, clock synchronization, and data logging functionality.

Recovered data from each instrument was processed separately prior to merging. Instrumentation was first deployed in 2012 on the 12th setting of the Stratus mooring (Stratus 12) and data was processed through the 22nd setting (Stratus 22). Data processing

Time check and truncation

We begin by checking the potential timing errors. To verify instrument clock accuracy, a timing mark was created both before deployment and after recovery. This involved briefly submerging the instruments in cold water to induce a sharp temperature change or “spike” and independently logging the start and end times of the event for comparison with the instrument’s internal timestamps. Potential timing errors are addressed by comparing the spike times in the data to the deployment and recovery instrument log. If the offset is greater than one sampling interval (30 minutes for Stratus 12 and 13, 5 minutes for Stratus 14 to 22), it indicates misalignment in time, and the data is linearly interpolated to a new time base to synchronize instruments. For the Stratus 12–22 deployments, we did not find time misalignment, so no interpolation was done.

Data set truncation is necessary to remove data prior to deployment and after recovery. After the anchor is dropped from the ship, it takes several hours to fall to the sea floor. Tracking the acoustic release just above the anchor has shown that the anchor is down and at rest within 4 hours after anchor drop. Specifically, we take the following steps for data truncation:

Spike removal

Spike removal is a standard component of oceanographic quality control where we remove the anomalous outliers in the data, since spurious values can arise from electronic noise or sensor instability and lead to misleading variability in the dataset7. We identify and remove spikes from time series data using a rolling mean and standard deviation within a 2-hour window around each data point. Data points that deviate from the rolling mean by more than 3 times the standard deviation are considered spikes and replaced with fill values.

Uncertainty estimates

To estimate temperature measurement uncertainty, we compared temperature data from co-located sensors deployed at the same depth. The difference statistics are computed after spike removal to avoid contamination by known bad points. When data from both instruments is available, we compute the mean and standard deviation of temperature for each sensor, and for the difference between sensors. Cross-sensor error is defined as one standard deviation of the difference between two records from duplicate sensors8. The cross-sensor differences are an indication of sensor precision and can also be used to inform quality control processing and relative sensor drift that may occur during deployment.

The difference statistics are stored as metadata in the NetCDF files with the following attributes for the temperature variable:

std_single_sensor: Standard deviation of the time series from a single sensor

std_single_sensor: Standard deviation of the time series from a single sensor

std_sensor_diff: Standard deviation of the difference between two co-located sensors

std_sensor_diff: Standard deviation of the difference between two co-located sensors

mean_sensor_diff: Mean of the difference between two co-located sensors.

mean_sensor_diff: Mean of the difference between two co-located sensors.

There are several sources of uncertainties in instrument depth. The synthetic mooring components may elongate during deployment due to sustained mooring tension. The anchor may tip over or sink into the seafloor. Although 84 seventeen-inch glass balls directly above the instruments provide substantial vertical tension, the mooring may tilt. Water depth from the shipboard fathometer may be in error. Each error is of order 1–2 m. If assumed independent, the overall depth uncertainty depth is about 3 m.

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) quality control

A standard “catalog” of time series plots for temperature, salinity, and pressure like Fig. 1 are created for each deployment, including both raw and quality-controlled plots for all measured variables. Human-in-the-loop (HITL) quality control involved domain expert review of these plots to determine whether additional data removal or adjustment was necessary. We assessed each time series for signs of sensor drift, sensor failure, and outliers that were not captured by automated checks. This manual inspection is particularly effective for identifying long-term drift, and the long drift after deployment in pressure and conductivity was detected in this check (not shown). Because pressure drift affects instrument depth measured by its pressure sensors, we used a fixed instrument depth based on shipboard bathymetry and mooring geometry rather than time-varying pressure measurements.

Standardized metadata

With the method described above, we developed a standardized pipeline to process the raw data and ensure consistent metadata, data quality and temporal consistency. Our metadata follow the OceanSITES data format9 and are compliant with Climate and Forecast (CF) Metadata Conventions, version 1.8 (CF-1.8). The metadata structure includes categories describing (i) deployment and platform information (site code, cruise names, deployment/recovery times, institutional affiliations), (ii) geospatial and temporal coverage (latitude, longitude, depth, start and end times, merge points), (iii) instrumentation (model, manufacturer, serial numbers, mounting, instrument depth), (iv) measured variables (sea water temperature with associated CF standard names, units, and uncertainty metrics), (v) data processing and quality control (processing version, date, conventions, quality comments), and (vi) administrative and citation information (license, acknowledgements, project website, dataset summary).

Merging deployments

The creation of continuous time series from sequential mooring deployments required careful handling of temporal overlaps and data gaps. Overlaps result from the standard practice of deploying the new mooring prior to recovery of the old mooring5. Gaps may result from sensor failures prior to mooring recovery, or inability to follow the standard practice due to weather or other constraints on field operations.

For each deployment transition, the following protocol was implemented:

Instrument Selection: When multiple instruments were used for a single deployment, we selected the instrument providing the most complete record and showing the smallest difference from the preceding and following deployment to ensure smooth transitions across deployments.

Overlap Handling: When temporal overlap existed between consecutive deployments, optimal merge points were identified by the smallest mean differences over rolling 6-hour windows. This approach ensured smooth transitions between time series while preserving the natural variability characteristics of each instrument.

Gap Management: For non-overlapping deployments, data from each instrument are joined end-to-end and we fill the gap with “NaN” to create a continuous time series. Sampling Rate: When instruments operated at different sampling frequencies, higher-frequency data were resampled to lower frequency to maintain temporal consistency across the merged dataset. Stratus 12 and 13 have a sampling interval of 30 min. Stratus 14–22, with a sampling interval of 5 min, were resampled to 30-min intervals. Data Record

This deep ocean mooring temperature dataset10 is archived at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) Data Library and Archives at https://doi.org/10.26027/DATAKX5CQK. The repository contains two files: (1) ReadMe.txt, a plain-text file (2 KB) providing basic metadata, and (2) stratus_temperature_2012_2025.nc, a NetCDF file (1.0 MB) containing deployment metadata and temperature time-series data collected from the Stratus mooring between 2012 and 2025. The NetCDF file includes variables for temperature, depth, and time, with all timestamps recorded in UTC.

Technical Validation

The six major processing steps in creating the merged temperature dataset are described above. In this section the outcome of selected assessments relating to data validation and interpretation are presented in more detail.

Dataset continuity

The complete merged temperature record (Fig. 2) illustrates the full scope of the dataset, with coverage across all deployments from 2012 to 2025. Gaps were confined to brief periods between deployments, and no prolonged gaps were observed. This continuity underscores the unique value of the record for long-term deep-ocean studies.

Complete merged temperature dataset from Stratus 12 to Stratus 22 deployments (2012–2025). The upper panels display a continuous time series of deep-sea water temperature at approximately 4200–4500 m depth. The lower panel shows the spatial displacement of each deployment relative to the original Stratus 12 location (red line) and to the immediately preceding deployment (blue line). Despite inter-deployment distances reaching several hundred kilometers in some cases, the majority of successive deployments remained within close proximity to each other.

Instrument locations for each deployment are shown in Fig. 3. Deployment of a surface mooring is a day-long process, with the ship slowly steaming towards the anchor site target. That target site is identified during a bottom survey as a large enough, relatively flat area with a depth consistent with the mooring design. At the Stratus site, the bottom topography was challenging, and subsequent mooring sites were intended to be within ~50 km of the Stratus 12 site. The goal is to deploy the new mooring close to the existing mooring to enable intercomparison and continue sampling close to the initial target for the Stratus ORS. However, strongly ridged bottom topography and variable wind, wave, and surface current conditions together with challenges to ship handling at low speed and work on deck, would lead to the decision to deploy over an area of suitable flat bottom rather than towing closer to the target site. After initial deployments, the presence of heavy fishing activity and entanglement of the mooring with fishing gear drove the decision in 2018 to a locus of anchor sites further to the south-southwest. As this work at the Stratus ORS is part of a pilot effort to obtain more observations in the context of very few extant time series of deep ocean temperature, little is known about either the frequency or wavenumber spectra of deep ocean temperature. Because the mooring array was redeployed up to 300 km from the original Stratus 12 site in subsequent years, the successive records reflect the broader deep-sea environment of the region and users should consider the spatial variability when interpreting the merged time series. Position (latitude, longitude) information is included with the merged time series.

Deployment locations for Stratus 12 through Stratus 22. Each colored marker indicates the mooring position during a given deployment. Time check

Routine clock checks during deployment and recovery (Fig. 4) revealed no measurable timing drift, confirming that instrument clocks were maintained throughout all deployments. An example of how we validate the time check is below. The recorded time of the temperature spike during deployment was 2012-05-17 15:48, while the time logged by the field team was 2012-05-17 15:48, indicating no timing offset. Similarly, during recovery, the recorded spike time was 2014-03-08 17:26, matching the logged time of 2014-03-08 17:26. This consistency confirms that the instrument clocks remained accurate throughout the deployment period.

Examples of temperature and salinity anomalies detected during deployment (top) and recovery (bottom) of Stratus 12 SBE16-1876. These short-duration spikes, lasting only a few hours, are associated with handling and stabilization of the instruments rather than environmental variability. Such periods were flagged automatically and excluded from the final dataset, ensuring that only stable, in-situ records are retained.

Error estimation

On each mooring, we deploy two instruments providing overlapping measurements that allow direct consistency checks. The paired records show a high degree of agreement, confirming that relative sensor drift and calibration offsets remained negligible. One example comparison from Stratus 21 is shown in Fig. 1, with a correlation coefficient of 0.997 for temperature.

Cross-sensor error analysis provides quantitative assessment of measurement precision and relative bias across all deployments (Table 3). Estimates below 0.002 °C indicate that they are indistinguishable within the instrument’s measurement uncertainty. The standard deviation of individual sensors ranges from 0.001 °C to 0.005 °C, while cross-sensor differences show standard deviations between 0.001 °C and 0.007 °C. Mean sensor differences are typically within ± 0.001 °C, indicating indistinguishable systematic bias between paired instruments. These statistics confirm that the temperature measurements have high precision, with uncertainties well within acceptable limits for deep ocean observations.

To assess absolute bias (calibration drift), we evaluated 13 post calibration records for 4 SBE-37s between 2013 and 2023. Accounting for the time between calibrations, they indicated a mean drift rate of ~0.01 m°C/month. This is consistent with the assessment of drifts in deep Argo temperature sensors11 and well within the manufacturer’s specification of 0.2 m°C/month. It is worth noting that the manufacturer’s accuracy specification (e.g. 0.002 °C for the SBE-37) is based on laboratory evaluations. For deep sea deployments, pressure sensitivity could be a cause for temperature bias of 0–0.002 °C per 4000 dbar12.

Together, the results of continuity, quality control, and error estimate demonstrate that the dataset is both internally consistent and robust against timing or sensor-related artifacts, providing strong evidence of calibration stability and inter-deployment coherence.

Usage Notes

This decade-long temperature record is useful for studies of deep ocean variability, heat content, and model validation. However, users should be aware of the following limitations: 1. Spatial Variability: Although deployments were generally co-located, some were separated by 50 to 300 km. Users should consider potential spatial variability when interpreting the merged time series. 2. Data Gaps: Brief gaps exist between deployments, which may affect analyses requiring continuous records. These gaps are clearly marked with fill values. 3. Salinity and Pressure: While salinity and pressure data were collected, they are not included in the public dataset due to pressure sensor drift issues.
