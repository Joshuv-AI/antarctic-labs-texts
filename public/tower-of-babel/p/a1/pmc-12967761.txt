Context features influence alcohol reward and motivation
Authors: Surya Pandey, Courtney A. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe context of alcohol consumption can influence an individual's experience and subsequent motivation, representing a point of potential clinical intervention in alcohol use disorder (AUD). Despite strong evidence for context‐dependent influences on drug‐cue reactivity, craving, seeking, and use, how multimodal contextual features interact to promote or suppress these behaviors is not well understood.MethodsIn adult male C57BL6/J mice, we conducted an unbiased alcohol conditioned place preference (CPP) procedure to determine the effects of proximal (sensory characteristics of the training apparatus) and distal (spatial characteristics of the training room) features of the training context on motivation for alcohol.ResultsWe found that alcohol place preference was uniquely influenced by proximal and distal context features. Specifically, training with alcohol, but not saline, resulted in a robust preference for a context with black walls, coarse floor, and coffee scent, as well as closest proximity to a wall of the room. Further analysis revealed a hierarchy of contextual influence, wherein the distal feature outweighed the proximal features. Context features also influenced the rate of change of alcohol‐induced locomotor activity during training which correlated with the strength of context preference at testing, consistent with the link between alcohol experience and motivation seen from rodents to human.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that both proximal and distal context features influence alcohol experience and subsequent motivation for alcohol. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for even subtle variations in training context, such as the location in a room. They further suggest that the varying motivational weight of different context levels could impact the development of AUD and be utilized to improve context‐based treatment strategy outcomes. This approach offers a simple method to investigate the mechanisms by which multimodal context features influence appraisal and motivation.

INTRODUCTION

Contextual information is robustly encoded with highly salient experiences, such as drug and alcohol use. These drug–context associations both shape the initial drug experience and influence continued use and relapse. Thus, the physical features of the environment in which a drug or alcohol is consumed are integral to motivation, use patterns, and long‐term outcomes in substance use disorders (SUD) (LeCocq et al., 2020). The influence of discrete cues such as a light or tone and the general context associated with drug and alcohol use have been characterized in human and animal models of cue and context‐induced relapse and renewal (Crombag et al., 2008; Napier et al., 2013). This has resulted in behavioral and pharmacological SUD treatment approaches like cue‐exposure therapies and reactivation–reconsolidation paradigms which utilize cues and contexts to access drug‐associated memories (LeCocq et al., 2020; Milton, 2023). Despite this well‐known phenomenon of drug–context association, it is unclear how the wide range of possible contextual features interacts with drug and alcohol reward to drive differential motivation. Understanding the principles by which multimodal features of the physical environment modify drug‐ or alcohol‐context associations could be used to maximize the efficacy of treatments (Byrne et al., 2019; Mellentin et al., 2017).

Most prior studies have utilized the sensory features of the drug use or exposure context to influence context associations and motivation (Krishnan et al., 2023; LeCocq et al., 2020). This may include introducing drug paraphernalia or discrete cues or reintroducing animals to the conditioning or self‐administration chambers. These proximal contextual features are typically uniform within an experiment and do not, therefore, evaluate the potentially varied motivational influence of different context features. Furthermore, focusing on the proximal context fails to take into account the potential influence of the larger environment in which they are located. Understanding how these two context levels influence motivation could provide a broader insight into effective ways to modify drug‐ or alcohol‐directed behaviors.

Conditioned place preference (CPP) is a widely used behavioral model in which a drug is paired with one conditioning context and a control treatment (e.g., saline) is paired with an adjacent, but distinct context. The contexts often differ in terms of their specific proximal sensory features (e.g., tactile, visual, olfactory) as well as distal spatial features (e.g., relative location in a room). Associations are formed between the drug‐paired context, such that reward and motivation for the drug can be determined later by presenting a choice between the two contexts in the absence of the drug (Cunningham et al., 2006; Krishnan et al., 2023). In the current study, we employed an unbiased alcohol CPP procedure to investigate the individual and combined impacts of proximal and distal context features on alcohol reward and motivation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 25–30 g (Jackson Laboratory) were housed in groups of four per cage in a temperature and humidity‐controlled vivarium under a 12:12 normal light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water in their home cage throughout the experiment. Data presented in this study were obtained from a total of five cohorts of mice: one cohort (n = 32) for saline CPP, three cohorts (n = 36, 12 per cohort) for alcohol CPP in the light, and one cohort (n = 20) for alcohol CPP in the dark. All experiments were conducted after 1 week of acclimation and 3 days of handling as previously described (Young et al., 2020). All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at The Herbert Wertheim UF Scripps Institute for Biomedical Innovation & Technology in accordance with National Institutes of Health guidelines. The data presented in this paper emerged from a study planned for male mice. Future experiments will be performed in females.

Conditioned place preference

Apparatus

The two outer chambers of the three‐chambered acrylic mouse CPP boxes (Med Associates) differed in three sensory features: wall color, floor texture, and scent. The central chamber (6 × 15 × 15 cm) had gray walls, a smooth gray acrylic floor, and no scent; it served as a corridor with guillotine‐style doorways to the two larger chambers (15 × 15 × 15 cm) on each side (Figure 1). One outer chamber (“Black”) had black walls, a coarse translucent plastic floor, and coffee scent. The other outer chamber (“White”) had white walls, a smooth light‐blue plastic floor, and orange scent. The scents, obtained from liquid extracts (McCormmick), were introduced by a cotton swab placed on a tray with cage bedding under the floors of the appropriate chamber. All chambers contained transparent plastic lids with small holes for air flow. Affixed to the inside of these lids were small light bulbs which remained off. We performed experiments using four CPP boxes placed on a table (5′L × 2.5′W × 3′H) that was positioned against a wall in a standard room (8.7′L × 7.6′W × 8.7′H). The boxes were placed parallel to each other with alternating orientation, such that the same number of Black and White chambers were adjacent to the wall or to the center of the room (Figure 1A). The sensory components of the chambers (wall color, floor texture, scent) are termed the “proximal context features” and the location of the chamber in the room (i.e., closest to the wall or center of the room) is termed the “distal context feature.”

Protocol

Our laboratory has routinely conducted mouse CPP experiments with various drugs of abuse (Briggs et al., 2017, 2018; Hafenbreidel et al., 2023; Young et al., 2016). In the current study, we adopted an alcohol CPP protocol based on prior studies by other groups (Cunningham & Shields, 2018a; Pati et al., 2019). The 7‐day CPP protocol comprised 1 day of pretest (PT), 5 days of training, and 1 day of test (T). All experiments were conducted in the light cycle (7 a.m.–7 p.m.).

Mice were handled for 3 days prior to the beginning of the CPP experiment, mimicking the general CPP procedure. Animals were first transported to the behavior core suite and habituated for 20 min in a holding room. They were then transported to the CPP testing room. The lighting conditions of the CPP room matched the experiments, but the apparatuses were not turned on and did not contain the olfactory cues. Once in the CPP room, animals were immediately mock injected with an empty syringe and then returned to their home cage. After injecting all four mice of a cage, animals were returned to their home room.

On each day of the CPP experiment, mice were acclimated to the holding room for 30 min prior to transport to the CPP room, where the procedure began immediately, without habituation. On the pretest day, mice were injected with saline intraperitoneally before being placed in the central corridor of the CPP apparatus. Mice were allowed to freely explore the apparatus for 30 min. Their locomotor activity and time spent in each of the three chambers were recorded by infrared beam breaks. The boxes were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol solution between runs.

The first 15 min of the pretest session was treated as habituation time and the second 15 min was used to determine any baseline chamber preference. Animals that spent 70% or more of the pretest time in one chamber were considered to have a strong bias and were removed from analysis (5 animals each from alcohol and saline CPP in the light and 1 from alcohol CPP in the dark). Notably, most of these animals (5 in alcohol CPP (light) and 3 in saline CPP) exhibited bias for Black chambers, indicating a potential tendency for the White chambers to be aversive, despite balancing of light levels. Pretest data were also used to assign each animal a drug training context (Black or White) in an unbiased, counterbalanced manner such that the average pretest time spent in the drug‐ versus the saline‐assigned chamber was approximately equal. Additionally, the number of animals assigned to the Black versus White chamber as well as to the Wall versus Center of the room was approximately counterbalanced. The chamber to be paired with alcohol was termed the conditioned stimulus chamber (CS+) and the saline‐paired chamber was the CS−.

On the training days, the doors of the central corridor were closed. In the morning sessions, mice received i.p. saline and were placed in the CS− chamber. Four hours later, they were injected with alcohol (or saline for saline CPP experiment) and placed in the opposite chamber (CS+). Alcohol training was conducted in the afternoon to prevent any carryover effect of alcohol to the saline training sessions. For the saline CPP experiment, animals were injected with saline in both sessions; the afternoon training context was considered CS+ for analysis purposes. Each training session lasted for 5 min, which captures the peak pharmacological effect of intraperitoneal ethanol in mice (Cunningham et al., 1997; Cunningham & Noble, 1992). Locomotor activity and time in each chamber were recorded by infrared beam breaks during each training session.

The 15‐min test session recapitulated the PT procedure. A preference score was calculated as the difference in time spent in the CS+ chamber and the CS− chamber. The difference in preference scores between test and pretest was then used to determine conditioned place preference.

Light and sound conditions

The alcohol and saline CPP procedures were conducted with the room light turned on or off, depending on the experiment. When lights were on, the illuminance in the CPP chambers was approximately 110 lx in the center, 310 lx in the Black chambers, and 430 lx in the White chambers. In adult male C57BL6/J mice, exposure to this range of illuminance has been shown previously to not affect memory retrieval or locomotor activity in a Y‐maze setting (Shang et al., 2023). For alcohol CPP in the dark, room light was turned off and injections were carried out in red light, but training occurred in complete darkness. Thus, mice were under red light during the first 10–40 s of being in the chambers depending on the order of injections and placement in the chamber. Illuminance in the chambers was at undetectable levels under red light. A white noise generator placed under the table with the CPP apparatuses was set at ∼65–70 dB for consistent noise levels throughout the experiment.

Drugs

For alcohol CPP, 20% v/v ethanol prepared from 200 proof (100%) stock ethanol in 0.9% saline solution was administered at a dose of 2 g/kg (12.67 mL/kg) (Groblewski et al., 2008).

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0. We compared pretest or test preference scores using one‐sample t‐test (baseline set at 0). Difference between test and pretest preference scores was analyzed using two‐tailed paired t‐test or a one‐sample t‐test (baseline set at 0). One‐way ANOVA was used to compare preference scores across groups, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test when appropriate. Cohen's d and η 2 were used to assess effect size for t‐tests and ANOVA, respectively. Locomotor activity data across 5 days of training were analyzed using two‐way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Sidak multiple comparisons test when appropriate. Comparison of locomotor activity between different groups was analyzed using mixed‐effects model followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test when appropriate. Effect sizes for these comparisons were assessed using partial η 2. Relationship between locomotor activity during training and CPP scores was assessed using Pearson's correlation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Proximal and distal context features influence motivation for alcohol

We conducted alcohol CPP training in three cohorts of mice (n = 10–12 per cohort) in CPP boxes made of two distinct training chambers separated by a central entry corridor (Figure 1A,B). In each cohort, the mean preference score at pretest (PT), defined as the mean difference in time spent in the (future) alcohol‐paired (CS+) versus saline‐paired (CS−) chamber, was close to zero, indicating an unbiased CS+ assignment. After training, place preference, defined as an increase in preference score from PT to the post‐training test (T), was present but weak across all cohorts, with high variability within cohorts (Figure S1–S1A,D,G: PT vs. T, mean ± SEM; Cohort 1: 17.45 ± 32.44 vs. 118.4 ± 36.67; Cohort 2: 1.93 ± 36.4 vs. 96.29 ± 48; Cohort 3: −6.64 ± 27.1 vs. 76.88 ± 50.8). Additionally, despite the balanced CS+ assignment, we consistently observed that mice trained with alcohol in the Black chambers (Black paired) exhibited a stronger place preference than those trained in the White chambers (White paired) (Figure S1–S1B,E,H). Because the three cohorts were trained with the exact same protocol, we combined their data to produce a large enough sample size to support a deeper analysis of this apparent context‐dependent CPP. As expected, the combined group (n = 31) displayed a statistically significant place preference (Figure 1D: n = 31, t(30) = 3.48, p = 0.001, d = 0.625). Furthermore, when the collapsed data were grouped by CS+ chamber, we found a significant preference in mice that underwent alcohol pairing with the Black, but not White, chamber (Figure 1E,F: Black: n = 16, t(15) = 3.622, p = 0.002, d = 0.905; White: n = 15, t(14) = 1.332, p = 0.204, d = 0.343), indicating that alcohol training is influenced by proximal context features.

Whereas the specific visual, tactile, and olfactory features associated with each chamber defined the proximal context, the spatial details of the testing room defined the distal context. Unexpectedly, we found that, irrespective of the proximal features, mice trained with alcohol in the chambers closer to the wall of the room (Wall‐paired) than the center of the room (Center‐paired) developed a place preference (Figure 1E,F: Wall: n = 14, t(13) = 5.705, p < 0.0001, d = 1.524; Center: n = 17, t(16) = 1.106, p = 0.285, d = 0.268). This indicates an impact of more distal features of the alcohol‐associated context.

Finally, since the proximal and distal context features are not mutually exclusive, we examined the influence of different combinations of these features. Robust CPP occurred when alcohol was paired with either the Black or White chamber when located close to the wall (Black/Wall and White/Wall), but not when located closest to the center of the room (Black/Center and White/Center) (Figure 1G,H: Black/Wall: n = 7, t(6) = 3.456, p = 0.013, d = 1.306; White/Wall: n = 7, t(6) = 5.242, p = 0.001, d = 1.981; Black/Center: n = 9, t(8) = 1.991, p = 0.082, d = 0.663; White/Center: n = 8, t(7) = 0.631, p = 0.548, d = −0.058). A one‐way ANOVA also indicated that CPP across the four possible context combinations was significantly different (Figure 1H: F (3,27) = 3.259, p = 0.036, η 2 = 0.265), with post hoc comparisons revealing a significant difference between Black/Wall and White/Center groups (p = 0.041, d = 1.477). The mean preference scores showed a gradient across these contexts (Black/Wall > White/Wall > Black/Center > White/Center) indicating a hierarchy of contextual influences whereby proximity to the wall had the strongest influence, followed by the visual, tactile, and olfactory features of the Black chamber itself.

Overall, these results indicate that specific proximal and distal features of alcohol exposure contexts together influence alcohol motivation.

Context‐dependent effects on alcohol motivation are directly linked to alcohol

In the collapsed data above, overall there was no baseline (pretest) preference for the chambers that were subsequently assigned as the CS+ (Figure S2A: Overall: n = 31, t(30) = 0.258, p = 0.797, d = 0.046), indicating that post‐training preferences are most likely due to the effects of alcohol, not an underlying context bias. However, when subdivided by proximal and distal features of the CS+ context, there was a subtle PT preference for the Black chamber in some animals (Figure S2B,C: Black: n = 16, t(15) = 2.290, p = 0.037, d = 0.572; Black/Wall: n = 7, t(6) = 0.862, p = 0.421, d = 0.325; Black/Center: n = 9, t(8) = 2.201, p = 0.058, d = 0.733). Thus, we tested the possibility that the context‐specific place preference observed after training reflected a time‐dependent strengthening of an inherent preference unrelated to alcohol exposure. To this end, we performed a CPP experiment with saline pairings in both training chambers; a large cohort of mice (n = 32) was used to ensure subtle effects, if present, would be detected.

Similar to alcohol, the saline CPP group showed no overall PT preference and a slight but non‐significant pretest preference for the Black chambers (Figure S2D,E: Overall: n = 27, t(26) = 0.689, p = 0.496, d = 0.132; Black: n = 14, t(13) = 1.852, p = 0.087, d = 0.479). Animals did show a significant PT preference for the Black chambers located towards the Wall (Figure S2F: Black/Wall: n = 9, t(8) = 3.324, p = 0.01, d = 1.107; Black/Center: n = 5, t(4) = 0.624, p = 0.566, d = −0.279). However, post training, there was no place preference in the overall group (Figure 2B: n = 27, t(26) = 1.216, p = 0.235, d = 0.233), in groups separated by the proximal or distal context features (Figure 2C,D: Black: n = 14, t(13) = 1.967, p = 0.070, d = 0.525; White: n = 13, t(12) = 0.147, p = 0.884, d = −0.041; Wall: n = 16, t(15) = 1.573, p = 0.136, d = 0.393; Center: n = 11, t(10) = 0.069, p = 0.946, d = 0.020), or groups separated by the four different combinations of proximal and distal features (Figure 2E,F: Black/Wall: n = 9, t(8) = 2.274, p = 0.052, d = 0.758; White/Wall: n = 7, t(6) = 0.286, p = 0.784, d = 0.108; Black/Center: n = 5, t(4) = 0.594, p = 0.584, d = 0.265; White/Center: n = 6, t(5) = 0.691, p = 0.520, d = 0.282). Furthermore, analysis of the net preference score between test and PT indicated no difference between groups (Figure 2F: F(3, 23) = 0.830, p = 0.490, η 2 = 0.097). These findings confirm that the context‐specific enhanced motivations observed in the alcohol CPP experiment (Figure 1E–H) reflect an interaction between alcohol and context features.

Proximal and distal context features influence alcohol motivation even under limited visibility

Central to the alcohol‐specific, context‐dependent responses in our experiments is the ability to discriminate between the contexts. Given the multimodal features of the contexts (spatial, visual, olfactory, tactile), this presumably recruits several sensory systems. Determining spatial location is particularly reliant on visual guidance. Thus, we tested whether removing visual input would disrupt the individual or combined effect of proximal and distal features and, in turn, abolish the context‐specific place preference. To this end, we performed alcohol training in complete darkness, with brief initial period (10–40 s) under red light when animals were administered saline or alcohol and placed in the chambers (Figure 3A). Under these conditions, pretest baseline preference was absent in the overall group or in groups separated by the proximal or distal CS+ context features or their combination (Figure S2G‐I). Post training, mice displayed a strong trend toward a place preference, but it was not statistically significant (Figure 3B: PT vs. T, mean ± SEM; 1.82 ± 29.56 vs. 61.82 ± 29.56, n = 19, t(18) = 2.022, p = 0.058, d = 0.463). However, when grouped by features of the CS+ context, a significant place preference was observed in groups paired with alcohol in the Black or Wall chambers, but not in the White or Center chambers, similar to the results from alcohol CPP under light (Figure 3C,D: Black: n = 9, t(8) = 2.941, p = 0.018, d = 0.980; White: n = 10, t(9) = 0.905, p = 0.388, d = 0.286, Wall: n = 12, t(11) = 3.258, p = 0.007, d = 0.940; Center: n = 7, t(6) = 0.753, p = 0.479, d = −0.284). Furthermore, when grouped by combinations of proximal and distal context features, once again, there was a hierarchy of contextual influences with the distal feature (Wall) having the predominant effect, followed by the proximal feature (Black) (Figure 3E,F: Black/Wall: n = 6, t(5) = 2.795, p = 0.038, d = 1.140; White/Wall: n = 6, t(5) = 2.122, p = 0.087, d = 0.865; Black/Center: n = 3, t(2) = 1.056, p = 0.401, d = 0.609; White/Center: n = 4, t(3) = 1.678, p = 0.192, d = −0.838). Furthermore, one‐way ANOVA suggested a strong trend for difference in CPP across the four possible context combinations (Figure 3F: F(3, 15) = 3.167, p = 0.055, η 2 = 0.387). These context‐specific place preferences under dark conditions indicate that animals maintained their ability to distinguish both proximal and distal features. The ability to distinguish the distal features was particularly unexpected as it is reliant on vision. The protocol was based on the common assumption that rodents are unable to see under red light. However, growing evidence suggests that visual capacity in rodents is conserved under red light, which can also influence physiology and behavior (Nikbakht & Diamond, 2021; Niklaus et al., 2020). The initial 10–40 s under red light may have provided sufficient time for mice to briefly detect spatial cues. The mean place preference in this experiment was relatively weaker than in the alcohol experiment under full light (Light vs. Dark, mean ± SEM; Overall (Figure 1D vs. Figure 3B): 93.2 ± 26.78 vs. 59.99 ± 29.67, Black (Figure 1F vs. Figure 3D): 135.9 ± 29.56 vs. 73.23 ± 24.9, Wall (Figure 1F vs. Figure 3D): 151.7 ± 26.59 vs. 111.6 ± 34.24, Black/Wall (Figure 1H vs. Figure 3F): 166.1 ± 48.06 vs. 89.99 ± 32.2). Thus, the interactive effect of proximal and distal context on alcohol place preference is present, but weakened, under limited visibility conditions. These results further confirm the strong influence of distal contextual features on reward motivation.

Proximal and distal context features influence locomotor activity during alcohol training

To better understand animals' response to alcohol in different contexts, we examined the above groups for their locomotor activity during the 5‐min training sessions across the five training days. Intraperitoneal alcohol exposure in mice is stimulating and rewarding in the first 5 min post‐administration and longer (Cunningham et al., 1997). Therefore, we examined locomotor activity during this period, comparing activity in the alcohol (CS+) and saline (CS−) contexts across the four groups defined by their CS+ context features. In the alcohol experiment under full light, the overall group demonstrated enhanced locomotor activity in the CS+ context compared to the CS− context (Figure S3A). Consistent with the context‐dependent strength of place preference, CS+ activity was also enhanced in the Black or Wall chambers, but not the White or Center chambers (Figure S3B), with a particularly robust effect in the Black/Wall chambers (Figure 4A: Chamber x Day, F(4, 48) = 2.004, p = 0.108, partial η 2 = 0.069; Chamber, F(1, 12) = 5.827, p = 0.032, partial η 2 = 0.161; Day, F(2.07, 24.88) = 10.62, p = 0.0004, partial η 2 = 0.282). Furthermore, net alcohol‐induced change in locomotor activity, measured as the difference between CS+ and CS− activity, was significantly different when grouped by combinations of proximal and distal features (Figure S3E). There was a significant main effect of chamber and post hoc comparisons between groups revealed enhanced alcohol‐induced activity, with strong effect size, in the Black chambers toward the Wall (B/W) compared to the other chambers (Figure S3E). These context‐specific locomotor behaviors at the time of alcohol exposure during training align with context‐specific alcohol CPP during alcohol‐free testing (Figures 1 and 4A, Figure S3). Importantly, there was a significant correlation between the preference score and the rate of change in alcohol‐induced activity (i.e. slope of the (CS+) – (CS−)) across 5 days of training in the overall group (Figure 4B: n = 31, Pearson's r = 0.526, p = 0.002). Furthermore, this relationship was particularly robust when training occurred in the Black or Black/Wall chambers (Figure S7A,B: Black, n = 16, r = 0.679, p = 0.004; Black/Wall, n = 7, r = 0.911, p = 0.004).

In the saline CPP control experiment, no such context‐specific elevated locomotor activity (Figure 4C, Figure S5B) or relationship with preference score was present (Figure 4D, Figure S8A,B). Instead, on day 1 of training, regardless of the context features, saline CPP animals exhibited a decline in activity in the CS+ chamber compared to the CS− chamber, indicating rapid habituation in the afternoon CS+ session (Figure 4C, Figure S5). Animals maintained this reduced activity level throughout the remaining training days in both CS− and CS+ chambers. This is in sharp contrast to the alcohol CPP group, where such habituation on day 1 was absent in all contexts, with elevated CS+ activity at the same level as the CS− activity (Figure 4A, Figure S3). Thus, alcohol exposure initially promoted locomotor activity in all contexts, but animals then habituated at different rates across days in a context‐dependent manner, with habituation occurring the slowest in the context that produced the highest place preference at alcohol‐free testing, that is, Black/Wall. This was further corroborated by data comparing locomotor activity between animals that received saline or alcohol in the same CS+ context type (Figure S4). When comparing the net difference in CS+ and CS− activity across the four combination groups, there was a significant main effect of context, with a strong trend and large effect size for difference between Black/Wall and White/Center chambers (Figure S5E). This suggests slower habituation in the White/Center chamber, suggesting an aversive appraisal.

Lastly, similar to full visibility conditions, training with alcohol under limited visibility also resulted in a context‐specific locomotor activity pattern with enhanced CS+ activity in the Overall, Black, and Wall groups (Figure S6A,B), as well as Black/Wall and White/Wall groups (Figure 4E: Black/Wall: Chamber x Day, F(2.724, 27.24) = 7.806, p = 0.0009, partial η 2 = 0.296; Day, F(2.724, 27.24) = 22.09, p < 0.0001, partial η 2 = 0.543; Chamber, F(1, 10) = 8.414, p = 0.015, partial η 2 = 0.691; White/Wall: Chamber x Day, F(2.436, 24.36) = 7.576, p = 0.001, partial η 2 = 0.205; Day, F(2.436, 24.36) = 30.13, p < 0.0001, partial η 2 = 0.507; Chamber, F(1, 10) = 7.891, p = 0.018, partial η 2 = 0.519). However, when comparing the net difference in activity between CS+ and CS− across the four combination groups, alcohol‐enhanced activity was not statistically different, although the activity in the White/Center group was lower than in the other three groups (Figure S6E). Furthermore, the rate of change of this net activity during training did not correlate with the degree of CPP in the overall group (Figure 4D: n = 19, r = −0.034, p = 0.888) or the context‐specific subgroups (Figure S9). Despite the context‐specific alcohol‐enhanced activity observed in both alcohol CPP in the light as well as under limited visibility, the absence of a relationship with preference score in the latter indicates that animals may have differential reward experience in full versus limited visibility conditions. Notably, the alcohol‐enhanced activity in the CS+ chamber under limited visibility was much higher than that under full visibility, despite similar levels of activity in the CS− chamber (Figure 4A vs. Figure 4E).

Together, these findings suggest that alcohol enhances locomotor activity and motivation in both a context‐specific and visibility‐dependent manner.

DISCUSSION

The physical contexts in which drugs and alcohol are experienced can serve as powerful drivers of continued use and relapse, with evidence suggesting that certain physical contexts are more conducive than others to positive drug experiences and subsequent motivational outcomes (Chen et al., 2008; Wall et al., 2001). For instance, humans report greater consumption of stimulants outside the home and heroin at home (Caprioli et al., 2009; De Pirro et al., 2018). Similarly, rats self‐administer more cocaine in novel environments and more heroin in the home cage (Caprioli et al., 2009). Thus, identifying specific context features that promote versus suppress positive drug experiences and motivation could be useful in prevention and therapeutic interventions. For alcohol, the differences in strength and the nature of alcohol‐context associations, that is, preference, no preference, or aversion, have historically been attributed to genetics, drug dose, drug exposure history, duration of training, context familiarity, and timing of context exposure relative to the drug administration (Barkley‐Levenson et al., 2015; Cunningham et al., 1992, 1997, 2006; Cunningham & Prather, 1992; Grahame et al., 2001; Groblewski et al., 2008). Effects of the physical context on reward motivation have also been recognized, but the specific features of these contexts have received limited attention (Chen et al., 2008; Doty & de Wit, 1995; Islam et al., 2022) and the effects of distal context features are rarely considered by investigators.

Here, we report a hierarchical interaction between context features and both alcohol reward experience and subsequent motivation for alcohol. Specifically, we determined that certain sensory features of the training chambers (proximal) and spatial location in the room (distal) resulted in greater alcohol‐induced locomotion during training. This behavioral response to alcohol has been shown from mice to humans to indicate a rewarding experience (Brabant et al., 2014). When later presented with an option between the various proximal/distal context combinations in an alcohol‐free state, mice showed the strongest motivation for alcohol when previously trained in chambers with black walls, a coarse floor, and coffee scent that were located closest to the wall of the room. The weakest CPP (overall negative preference score) was observed in mice that received alcohol paired with chambers located toward the center of the room and containing white walls, a smooth floor, and orange scent. This lack of preference in the White/Center chamber could not be attributed to hyperactivity, as the overall activity rates were similar across the CS+ context groups (Figure S12). Notably, our experiments included the same number of chambers with specific sensory features both toward the wall and the center of the room. Thus, our results indicate that spatial location is a key component of contextual discrimination. Additionally, we found this context‐specific place preference even under low visibility, suggesting that determination of spatial location may not rely solely on visual input. For instance, subtle gradients of olfactory and auditory information across the testing environment may contribute to the spatial distinction.

The specific influence of certain proximal and distal context features, but not others, on alcohol‐context association begs the question about the aspects of these features that determine this selective association. One possibility is that the effect of a context on drug experience can result from an animal's implicit perception of the context as positive or negative, depending on factors such as imminent threat or exploratory and social opportunities (Lebrecht et al., 2012; Lebrecht & Tarr, 2010; Moors et al., 2013). This, in turn, can influence the valence of subsequent experiences in that context. There is evidence supporting an additive effect of positive experience on drug reinforcement (Thiel et al., 2008, 2009; Watanabe, 2011, 2013) and inhibitory effects of aversive conditioned stimuli on appetitive response acquisition or expression (Bulganin et al., 2014; Dickinson & Pearce, 1977; Krank, 1985). The motivation for alcohol in Black chambers located near the Wall in our study is consistent with rodents' natural preference for dark, enclosed spaces with vertical surfaces, that is, thigmotaxis (Crawley & Goodwin, 1980; Simon et al., 1994). Our pretest data revealed a small, but statistically significant, baseline preference for Black or Black/Wall chambers (Figure S2B,F). This indicates that Black chambers and the Wall side of the room may be naturally preferred by the animals, in spite of light balancing inside the chambers, and the magnitude of this preference can vary from cohort to cohort (Figures S1–S1 and S2). Furthermore, our data suggest that even a subtle initial preference combines with alcohol's rewarding properties to produce a robust preference. A conditioned place preference does not develop in the absence of alcohol experience, even in the presence of an initial preference. Thus, implicit positive appraisal of Black chambers located near the wall of the room may have been permissive for alcohol's stimulating and rewarding effects, whereas negative appraisal of White chambers located near the center of the room likely acted as inhibitory cues, outweighing the positive effects of alcohol. Indeed, perceived safety and salience of the alcohol intake environment has been shown to influence alcohol motivation in rats (Testa et al., 2011).

It is also possible that the sensory salience produced by the features of the Dark/Coarse/Coffee and Wall chamber, compared to the White/Smooth/Orange and Center chamber, is more amenable to CS–US associations. The influence of varying stimulus salience on the rate of associative learning has been demonstrated. Using numerical simulations of discriminative training, Treviño and colleagues proposed a model that predicts interactions between varying salience and discrimination learning (Treviño et al., 2011). Additionally, a study in mice by Zhu and colleagues showed that activity evoked in the paraventricular thalamus by specific sensory stimuli determined the efficiency of associative learning and that this activity habituated differently across various olfactory stimuli (Zhu et al., 2018). They also found that stimuli‐evoked activity was influenced by affective state like thirst, alluding to a mechanism by which sensory and affective salience of CS and US influence learning and motivation.

Our data (Figure S10) also revealed a strong positive relationship (Pearson's r = 0.984, p < 0.0001) between pretest CS+ activity rate and preference score in animals conditioned with alcohol in the Black/Wall chambers, but not in other chambers. Importantly, this relationship was absent in the saline CPP control group and in the group with alcohol conditioning under the low visibility condition. Thus, if activity is a proxy for sensory salience, then our data indicate that the sensory features of the Black/Wall chambers are indeed more amenable to alcohol conditioning and subsequent expression of CPP, particularly under the full visibility condition. We found a strong negative relationship (Pearson's r = −0.943, p = 0.0004) between pretest CS+ time and preference score in animals conditioned with alcohol in the White/Center chambers, but not in other chambers (Figure S11). This relationship was also absent in the saline CPP and alcohol CPP under limited visibility. If time spent in a specific context is a proxy for motivational salience, then the features of the White/Center chambers seem less amenable to alcohol conditioning and CPP expression. Taken together, these findings suggest that the sensory and spatial features of specific contexts can induce differential sensory and motivational salience, which, in turn, determine their associative strength with alcohol. Our findings indicate stronger associative strength with sensory salience of the Black/Wall context and weaker associative strength with motivational salience of the White/Center context.

The absence of a relationship between both the pretest and conditioning parameters and the preference score in alcohol CPP under low visibility condition begs questions about the effects of illumination on associative strength. Although the magnitude of alcohol CPP is slightly lower under the low visibility condition, we did not find statistically significant differences. However, the lack of correlation between locomotion during conditioning and place preference during testing in the low visibility condition may be a result of this subtle reduction in CPP magnitude. We note that the CS+ locomotor activity during training in these two CPP conditions is also different. Activity in the CS+ is at a much higher level in the limited visibility condition, despite similar CS− activity. This alcohol‐induced enhanced activity under low light is consistent with findings of a prior study that assessed activity in an open field after acute alcohol administration under varying lighting conditions (Fukushiro et al., 2010). Together, these findings suggest that alcohol exposure in the two lighting conditions in our study induces differential motivational states, contributing to differences in the CPP magnitude and the relationships between CPP and pretest and conditioning parameters.

The dose and training duration in the current study were selected to elicit the rewarding effects of alcohol (Groblewski et al., 2008). Thus, the differences in CPP are likely due to contextual influences on alcohol reward experience. However, even with the same dose, there may be individual variability in alcohol experience (rewarding, aversive, anxiogenic, anxiolytic), and these various motivational properties of alcohol could, in turn, interact with the affective/motivational properties of different contexts. Separate behavioral tests of affective/motivational states (e.g., elevated plus maze, open field, etc.) following alcohol treatment in these animals could provide additional insight into alcohol's various effects and their interaction with context features.

Considering alcohol's anxiolytic effects, it is also possible that the lack of alcohol CPP in the White/Center chamber reflects alcohol‐mediated reduction of context aversion, rather than inhibition of reward. However, compared to the pretest baseline preference score for this chamber, we found a more negative preference score during testing, suggesting that an anxiolytic effect, if any, was weak. This negative preference score in the White/Center chamber during testing was also observed in alcohol CPP under the low visibility condition. Therefore, we propose that the aversive properties of White/Center chamber reduced CPP by suppressing the rewarding effects of alcohol. Furthermore, the enhanced negative preference scores for the White/Center chamber during testing, relative to pretest, in both full and low visibility conditions suggest that alcohol may exacerbate contextual aversion. Thus, alcohol appears to induce reward or aversion, depending upon the affective properties of the context. Such opposite effects from the same dose of alcohol have been previously reported in mice and are attributed to the timing of alcohol administration relative to CS+ exposure (i.e., preference when administered before and aversion after CS+ exposure; (Cunningham et al., 1997; Pati et al., 2019)). Our results suggest that contextual features, by virtue of their sensory salience and affective properties, can also contribute to this bidirectional alcohol response.

The interaction between proximal and distal features of a context and their combined effect on context appraisal seems to be an important aspect of associative learning and memory. In visual search experiments, learning of the local context was found to be contingent upon maintenance of its location in the global context (Bergmann & Schubö, 2021; Brady & Chun, 2007). Likewise, in a series of experiments, Cunningham and Zerizef (2014) found that alcohol CPP is weaker in a one‐chambered apparatus, where spatial cues are not relevant, and it is reduced in a two‐chambered procedure when spatial cues are made irrelevant during testing by reversing the location of CS− and CS+ tactile cues. These findings demonstrated that visual–spatial cues combined with tactile cues strengthen associative learning. Thus, both proximal and distal context features, even as subtle as the spatial location, are critical considerations in study designs involving learning and motivation.

In addition to the proximal and distal sensory features, time of day is an important contextual feature to consider in these types of experiments. Evidence from flies to monkeys indicates a circadian influence on food and drug CPP, with afternoon CS+ training (in normal light/dark cycle) leading to more robust CPP (Abarca et al., 2002; Monclaro et al., 2014). In our study, all CS+ groups underwent CS+ conditioning in the afternoon. Thus, the differential CPP expression in various CS+ context groups indicates that context features may modulate the strength of circadian influence on associative learning and CPP expression.

Our study has opened several questions for further inquiry. First, additional days of training or different doses of alcohol might be able to overcome inhibitory influences of the contexts that interfered with the alcohol reward. If the contexts resistant to CPP expression were indeed appraised as aversive, the animals may be in a motivational conflict between context aversion and alcohol reward. Aversive to appetitive counterconditioning has been shown to generate appetitive responses, even in aversive contexts in a reappraisal‐like process (Choi et al., 2019; Neta et al., 2023). Supporting this idea, Nguyen and colleagues found that repeated exposure to cocaine led rats to exhibit appetitive approach motivations when presented with a mixed‐valence visuo‐tactile cue associated with both sucrose reward and shock punishment (Nguyen et al., 2015). The potential of chronic drug exposure to attenuate or abolish specific contextual influences on drug‐related behaviors would indicate generalization of drug–context associations, which has important implications for uncontrolled drug use and rapid relapse. Second, our study identifies the context‐dependent alcohol effects in male mice. Our findings are a result of careful analysis of data from experiments designed for males and we will next investigate these ideas in females and adolescents, as different results are anticipated. Additionally, considering the differences in alcohol sensitivity and CPP expression across different strains of mice, such as DBA2/J and C57BL/6J (Cunningham & Shields, 2018a, 2018b), comparison of contextual influences on CPP across strains is warranted. The positive alcohol–context association in specific contexts observed in our study is consistent with a prior study in male DBA/2CrSlc mice which showed alcohol CPP learning in the black, but not white chamber (Shimizu et al., 2015). This was comparable to our current study in terms of the alcohol dose and the number of training sessions, but differed regarding the mouse strain, composition of the CPP apparatuses, and absence of olfactory cues. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate the influence of distal or tactile features of the conditioning context. Cunningham and Shields demonstrated that addition of visuospatial cues accelerated CPP in both DBA2/J and C57BL/6J mice, but at different rates (2018a). Thus, investigating whether these strain differences in CPP are observed when considering various proximal‐distal context features could provide insights into mechanisms of differential alcohol sensitivity. Finally, considering the differential impact of the environment on various classes of drugs, it will be important to investigate how other drugs of abuse interact with proximal and distal context features (Badiani et al., 2011).

Cue exposure therapy for SUDs has been met with limited success, particularly for AUD (Kiyak et al., 2023). A commonly accepted explanation is that these treatments occur in laboratory or clinical settings that are vastly different from the contexts in which alcohol is normally consumed. Virtual reality‐based cue‐exposure therapies have been more successful in generating positive outcomes, likely due to the immersive and more relevant nature of the context presentation (Nègre et al., 2023; Taubin et al., 2023). Similarly, ritualized context‐based approaches have led to positive psychedelic experience and therapeutic outcomes (Carhart‐Harris et al., 2018). The findings of our current study indicate that these treatment strategies could further benefit from incorporation of specific proximal and distal context features central to drug experiences. For instance, a benefit might be achieved by complementing current treatment strategies with controlled alcohol consumption in a virtual reality context appraised as neutral or aversive.
