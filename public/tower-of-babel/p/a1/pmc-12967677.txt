Microbial-induced calcite precipitation by indigenous alkaliphilic bacteria: a dual-enzyme strategy for crack-healing in cementitious materials
Authors: Mohammad Shiri, Abbas Bahari, Mohammad Mahdi Khani Sarbangholi, Nikolai IvanovichVatin, Hamid Rahmani, Amin Ebrahimpour Bozorg, Elahe Gholamian, Mobina Kordlou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) offers a sustainable strategy for extending the service life of concrete through autonomous crack healing, yet the high alkalinity of cementitious environments restricts microbial viability. In this study, more than 200 indigenous bacterial isolates collected from extreme environments across Iran were systematically screened for urease and carbonic anhydrase (CA) activities. A dual-enzyme activity index (EAI) was developed to quantitatively rank their calcification potential. Four robust spore-forming strains—Bacillus subtilis, Sporosarcina pasteurii, Bacillus sphaericus, and the environmental isolate E10.2—were identified as top candidates based on high EAI values, sporulation capacity, and survival at pH 13.5. These strains retained at least 70% of their enzymatic activity after alkaline exposure and precipitated up to 89% more CaCO3 than controls. When incorporated into mortar, bio-treated specimens reached strength levels slightly exceeding the uncracked control under the tested conditions (46.8 MPa at 28 days compared to 34.2 MPa in cracked controls). Ultrasonic pulse velocity, SEM, and XRD analyses confirmed dense CaCO3 bridging within healed cracks. This study establishes a performance-based framework for selecting dual-enzyme-producing alkaliphilic bacteria for durable, self-healing concrete.Keywords: Self-healing concrete, MICP, Urease activity, Carbonic anhydrase activity, Alkaliphilic spore-forming bacteria, Native microbial isolates

Introduction

Concrete is the most widely used construction material worldwide, yet its durability is severely compromised by the development of microcracks. Such cracks allow the ingress of aggressive agents, accelerating steel reinforcement corrosion and reducing service life. Conventional repair methods are costly, labor-intensive, and often only temporary. In recent years, microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) has emerged as a sustainable and autonomous crack-healing strategy, in which metabolically active bacteria precipitate calcium carbonate (CaCO3) within cracks, sealing them and restoring mechanical integrity (Nasser; Xu et al. 2024).

The most extensively studied bacterium for MICP is S. pasteurii, a ureolytic, spore-forming microorganism capable of surviving in alkaline environments. Since Jonkers’ pioneering studies on bacterial self-healing concrete (Jonkers et al. 2010), S. pasteurii has been regarded as the “model strain” for bio-concrete research. However, reliance on a single reference species presents limitations. Several studies report rapid declines in viability after incorporation into cementitious matrices(Ivaškė et al. 2023; A; Ivaškė et al. 2024a, b), while others note variability in calcite precipitation depending on mix design, temperature, and nutrient availability (Javeed et al. 2024). These factors raise concerns about the reproducibility and scalability of relying solely on S. pasteurii.

To address these limitations, increasing attention has been given to bacteria isolated from natural and engineered environments, including soils, saline lakes, alkaline springs, and even wastewater sludge. Such indigenous or consortium-derived strains often display superior adaptation to extreme pH, ionic stress, and desiccation—conditions similar to those in hardened concrete(Milović et al. 2024; Otiniano et al. 2024). For example, microbial consortia selected from activated sludge have been successfully applied to concrete crack-healing with promising results(Alshalif et al. 2022). Similarly, carbonate-producing bacteria isolated from local soils have shown effective CaCO3 deposition in mortar and concrete (Wei et al. 2024). These findings demonstrate that local environments can provide robust microbial candidates for MICP. Nevertheless, despite these advances, systematic frameworks for evaluating and selecting such strains remain lacking. Consistent with these observations, recent research has highlighted the successful integration of indigenous bacterial consortia into cementitious and alkali-activated systems, revealing both the potential and the variability of existing empirical strain-selection approaches (Sarkar et al. 2022, 2023). Most previous studies have relied on trial-and-error approaches: bacteria are incorporated into mortar specimens, and performance is evaluated post-factum by measuring crack closure, compressive strength, or permeability reduction. While informative, this method is time-consuming, costly, and inefficient, as many strains fail during the early alkaline challenge of cement hydration (Augusta Ivaškė et al. 2024a, b; Javeed et al. 2024; Pui Yan Wong et al. 2024; P. Y. Wong et al. 2024). A more rational strategy requires linking microbial functional traits—particularly enzyme activity and viability under simulated pore solutions—to material-level outcomes before mortar casting.

In this context, urease and carbonic anhydrase (CA) are the two most critical enzymes driving CaCO3 precipitation. Urease hydrolyzes urea to produce carbonate ions, while CA accelerates CO₂ hydration to bicarbonate, together enhancing carbonate availability for mineralization (Alshalif; Carter et al. 2023). Despite their importance, most studies have considered these enzymes independently rather than synergistically. Furthermore, few have attempted to normalize enzymatic activity to bacterial biomass or account for post-alkaline exposure activity, which is critical for survival in concrete environments.

The present study addresses these gaps by systematically screening over 200 indigenous bacterial isolates collected from diverse extreme environments across Iran. We introduce a novel dual-enzyme activity index (EAI), integrating normalized urease (UA) and carbonic anhydrase activity (CAA), to rank strain performance. In parallel, bacterial viability was assessed in simulated concrete pore solutions at pH levels of 12.5 and 13.5, which mimic the harsh cementitious environment. From this large pool, four robust spore-forming strains were selected for mortar incorporation, and their self-healing performance was evaluated by compressive strength recovery, ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV), and microstructural analyses (SEM/XRD) (Wang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2020).

By combining enzyme activity, viability, and mortar performance into a single screening framework, this work proposes the first systematic, performance-based method for selecting bacterial strains for bio-concrete applications. Beyond demonstrating the potential of indigenous alkaliphilic bacteria, our study highlights the advantages of a dual-enzyme strategy for developing durable, regionally adapted, and scalable self-healing concrete.

In this study, we introduce a novel dual-enzyme selection framework that uniquely combines urease and carbonic anhydrase (CA) activity into a single Enzyme Activity Index (EAI). This index not only quantitatively weights both enzymatic contributions but also correlates directly with crack-healing efficiency in cementitious materials. Unlike existing methods that consider urease or CA in isolation, our approach leverages the synergistic effect of both enzymes, offering a more holistic and regionally tailored screening strategy. This novel EAI formulation and our large-scale isolation from indigenous alkaliphiles provide a unique contribution to the field of microbial self-healing concrete.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

This study evaluated eight bacterial strains with known or suspected capacity for calcite precipitation, comprising both established reference organisms and newly isolated environmental candidates. The eight reference strains included Sporosarcina pasteurii (three variants: PTCC 1645 and two strains from Shahid Chamran University labeled CH1 and CH2), B. sphaericus (PTCC 1490), B. subtilis (PTCC 1204), Arthrobacter bussei (an alkaliphilic species, was obtained from two sources: one sample was purchased directly (PTCC 1852), and the other was provided by Dr. Ghezalbash), and Citrobacter freundii (two strains: CH1 and CH2). The remaining eight strains were novel environmental isolates—designated with “E-” or “R-” prefixes according to the initials of their discoverers, and “J. ” or “KH. ” for other distinct isolates—which were recovered from soil and water samples collected across diverse extreme environments in Iran, including saline lakes, hot springs, and alkaline desert soils. Preliminary identification suggested these isolates belong to calcifying species within the Bacillus and Arthrobacter genera.

Bacterial isolates were obtained from diverse extreme environments across Iran, including saline lakes, hot springs, alkaline desert soils, and saline surface waters. The saline lakes (e.g., Lake Urmia and inland basins) were characterized by high salinity (up to 10–12%) and alkaline pH (9–10). Hot spring samples were collected at temperatures of 40–55 °C with pH values of 8.5–9.5 and high concentrations of calcium and sulfate ions. Alkaline desert soils (e.g., from Yazd and Kerman provinces) exhibited pH 9–10.5, low moisture, and daily temperature fluctuations between 5 and 45 °C. In addition, saline channels and surface waters (from Khuzestan and Sistan regions) showed pH 8.5–9 and salinity up to 8%. These environments provided robust alkaliphilic and spore-forming bacteria adapted to survive under conditions analogous to cementitious matrices.

With the exception of Citrobacter (a Gram-negative strain), all isolates were Gram-positive. Prior to downstream analyses, each strain was subcultured on nutrient agar to confirm purity and then propagated in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 30 °C with shaking (150 rpm) until mid-exponential phase (OD₆₀₀ ~ 1.0). For assessing sporulation potential, cultures were grown in 2 × SG medium—composed of nutrient broth supplemented with MgSO₄, MnSO₄, CaCl₂, and agar—under static incubation at 30 °C to promote spore formation.

Cell concentrations were estimated spectrophotometrically at OD₆₀₀, with calibration to colony-forming unit (CFU) counts established through serial dilution plating. An OD₆₀₀ of 1.0 corresponded to approximately 8.6 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. This conversion was used for subsequent viability and survival calculations. All experimental procedures were conducted with a minimum of three biological replicates to ensure statistical robustness and reproducibility.

Enzyme activity assays (urease and carbonic anhydrase)

Both urease and carbonic anhydrase activities were quantified using colorimetric assays to assess each strain’s potential for CaCO3 precipitation (Fig. 1).

Urease and carbonic anhydrase activities in reference bacterial strains. This figure presents the quantitative measurement of urease (red line, left Y-axis) and carbonic anhydrase (blue line, right Y-axis) activities across various reference bacterial strains frequently utilized in microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) research, including S. pasteurii (CH1, CH2, 1645 PTCC), Arthrobacter (bussei, CH), Citrobacter (CH1, CH2), B. subtilis (1204 PTCC), and B. sphaericus (1490 PTCC). The results highlight substantial variability in both enzyme activities among strains. Notably, a reverse correlation is evident: strains exhibiting higher urease activity often display lower carbonic anhydrase activity, and vice versa.

Urease activity was measured via a phenol red-based assay. Cultures were incubated with urea and buffer solution (Tris–HCl buffer 50mM, pH 8.0), and the increase in absorbance at 560 nm (indicating NH₄⁺ release) was monitored. A standard curve was used to calculate urease activity (UA), expressed as µmol NH₄⁺/min/mL, and normalized by OD₆₀₀ to obtain activity per unit biomass (N.UA). All assays included blanks and were run in triplicate (Fig. 1).

Carbonic anhydrase activity was measured by para-nitrophenyl acetate (p-NPA) hydrolysis. Cultures were mixed with p-NPA substrate, and absorbance at 405 nm (due to p-nitrophenolate formation) was recorded. The rate of p-NP production was used to calculate CA activity (CAA), also normalized by OD₆₀₀ (N.CAA). Assays were performed in triplicate with appropriate blanks.

This composite index reflects the combined contributions of normalized urease (N.UA) and carbonic anhydrase (N.CAA) activities. A cutoff of EAI ≥ 2.4 was used to identify high-performing strains for further evaluation (Fig. 1).

The weighting coefficient for urease (0.003) and the use of the natural log of carbonic anhydrase activity were determined through statistical regression analyses correlating enzyme levels with the observed crack-healing ratio and UPV improvements. This approach allowed us to establish a quantitative relationship between the EAI and the healing efficiency, demonstrating that our index is a reliable predictor of performance.

Alkaline viability and stress survival assays

To evaluate bacterial tolerance under cement-like alkaline conditions, two simulated concrete pore solutions (SCPS) were prepared to represent different stages of cement hydration. SCPS-12.5 (pH ≈ 12.5) mimicked cured cement and contained saturated Ca(OH)₂ and CaSO₄, while SCPS-13.5 (pH ≈ 13.5) simulated the highly alkaline environment of young cement and contained NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, and CaSO₄. Both solutions were freshly prepared, equilibrated, filtered (0.45 μm), and pH-verified before use.

Cultures were grown to mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀ > 1.5) and diluted 1:10 into SCPS-12.5 or SCPS-13.5 to minimize pH drift (< 0.2 units). Each strain was exposed at 30 °C for either 10 or 100 min, simulating short- and long-term exposure during mixing and early hydration. Viability was assessed using two complementary approaches:

Qualitative regrowth test: After alkaline exposure, cells were centrifuged, washed, and inoculated into fresh LB medium. Growth (OD₆₀₀) was monitored for 20 h. Regrowth indicated survival.

To further test robustness, a two-phase stress challenge was performed. In this assay, surviving populations from the first 100-min exposure were harvested and subjected to a second 100-min exposure under the same alkaline conditions. CFUs were again determined by serial dilution and plating.

Both reference strains (B. subtilis, B. sphaericus, S. pasteurii) and selected environmental isolates (e.g., E6.2, SH4.2, R8.1) were tested. Mortar preparation and self-healing experiments

In this study, nutrients were co-introduced with the bacterial suspension during mixing to ensure the presence of urea/bicarbonate and calcium sources required for microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP). While nutrient diffusion within the hardened matrix remains limited, bacterial activity and CaCO3 formation are reactivated upon moisture ingress through cracks. The self-healing process is therefore triggered by the availability of water, which enables spore germination, enzyme reactivation, and localized calcite precipitation within the crack region.

Four bacterial strains—two ureolytic (e.g., S. pasteurii, a Bacillus sp.) and two CA-active (e.g., B. sphaericus, an environmental isolate)—were selected based on high enzyme activity. Each was paired with a tailored nutrient regimen: urea for ureolytic strains, bicarbonate for CA-based strains.

Standard 50 mm mortar cubes were cast using OPC Type I cement and natural sand (cement:sand = 1:2.75, w/c = 0.485). The mix proportions (cement:sand = 1:2.75, w/c = 0.485) followed ASTM C109 guidelines for mortar cube preparation, ensuring consistency with standard practice. For each bacterial formulation, the suspension of a single strain was adjusted to ~10⁷ CFU/mL, a concentration commonly applied in previous MICP studies to ensure sufficient calcite precipitation while maintaining mix workability. Each strain was incorporated into a separate mortar batch and evaluated individually.

For bacterial mixes, part of the mix water was replaced with bacterial culture (~10⁷ CFU/mL). Nutrients were added as follows:

Ureolytic mixes: 333 mM urea + 250 mM calcium lactate

Ureolytic mixes: 333 mM urea + 250 mM calcium lactate

CA-based mixes: 400 mM NaHCO3 + 250 mM calcium lactate

CA-based mixes: 400 mM NaHCO3 + 250 mM calcium lactate

Selected variants used 250 mM calcium nitrate instead of lactate (denoted by “-N” label).

Selected variants used 250 mM calcium nitrate instead of lactate (denoted by “-N” label).

Each formulation was coded (e.g., “S.P.U-N” = S. pasteurii with urea and calcium nitrate). A control mix with no bacteria or additives was also prepared.

Cracks were introduced in mortar cubes using two controlled methods. In the primary approach, thin steel plates (0.3 mm thickness) were inserted into the fresh mortar during casting and carefully removed after 24 h, creating pre-formed surface cracks of uniform width. In complementary experiments, a jack-breaker device was used to apply controlled mechanical stress, producing irregular cracks that more closely resemble field conditions. Both methods were applied in preliminary testing, although the majority of the self-healing analysis focused on the pre-formed cracks. Representative images from both approaches are provided in the Supplementary Material, with scale bars included. Specimens were demolded after 24 h and cured in water at room temperature for 28 days without additional nutrients.

While alkaline tolerance tests in simulated pore solutions provide a controlled assessment of bacterial resistance to high pH, it should be acknowledged that actual survival within hardened mortar also depends on the material’s pore structure and nutrient accessibility. As the matrix solidifies, reduced porosity and limited diffusion can further restrict bacterial viability and metabolic activity. Therefore, the observed survival values represent intrinsic physiological resilience under alkaline stress rather than in situ viability within a solid matrix.

Self-healing assessment

After curing, cracked surfaces were photographed to assess visual healing. Cracks were considered healed if visibly bridged or filled with white precipitate. Before-and-after images were compared to evaluate healing extent.

Spore formation assay

To evaluate spore-forming capacity, all strains were grown in 2 × SG medium (nutrient broth + essential ions, pH ~ 7.0) at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 18–24 h. After incubation, 1 mL of culture was heat-shocked at 80 °C for 60 min to kill vegetative cells. Serial dilutions (0, 1:10, 1:100) were plated on LB agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted to quantify viable spores. Reference strains (B. subtilis, B. sphaericus, S. pasteurii) served as positive controls. Environmental isolates with > 500 CFUs post-treatment were considered high-sporulating and thus likely to survive concrete environments by forming durable spores capable of initiating MICP upon activation.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) test

We employed an ultrasonic through-transmission method to quantify internal crack closure. Each cube was subjected to an ultrasonic pulse velocity measurement by placing a 54 kHz transducer on one face and a receiver on the opposite face across the crack plane. The transit time of the pulse was recorded, and the velocity (m/s) calculated. For analysis, the ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) of each cracked specimen was normalized against the average UPV of the corresponding uncracked (sound) specimens. A normalized UPV approaching 1.0 indicates that the crack no longer significantly impedes wave propagation (i.e., it has healed and the material stiffness is restored). We report the distribution of normalized UPV values for each treatment as a measure of healing efficiency (higher = better healing).

Although the 50 mm mortar cubes are smaller than the standard path length recommended for absolute UPV measurement (typically ≥ 140 mm), the technique was used here in a comparative manner. By normalizing the UPV of cracked specimens to their corresponding sound specimens of identical geometry, the influence of path length and boundary effects was minimized, allowing reliable evaluation of relative healing efficiency.

Compressive strength and healing ratio

Compressive strength was measured using a universal testing machine in accordance with ASTM C109 (loading rate ~ 0.36–0.72 MPa/s). Cracked specimens were loaded with the crack aligned parallel to the force direction to assess healing capacity. Uncracked cubes were tested similarly as baseline controls. The healing ratio was calculated as the strength of the cracked specimen divided by its uncracked counterpart (× 100%). A ratio of 100% indicated full strength recovery; values above 100% reflected improvement. Results represent the average of three replicates. Failure modes were also examined to determine whether fractures followed the original crack or new paths.

Microstructural analysis (SEM/EDX)

To confirm that crack filling resulted from microbial calcite, fragments (~ 5–10 mm) were extracted from healed areas of selected cubes (including control and top-performing urease and CA strains) after strength testing. Samples were dried at 60 °C, mounted on stubs, and sputter-coated with gold. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine surface morphology, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was performed to assess elemental composition (TESCAN VEGA, with Oxford EDX). Precipitates showing strong Ca and C signals with a Ca:C atomic ratio near 1:1, along with oxygen, were interpreted as CaCO3. In contrast, signals lacking carbon or showing silicon suggested cement hydration products. This analysis allowed biogenic calcite to be distinguished from ordinary matrix components such as Portlandite or C–S–H (Fig. 3).

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

Finally, precipitates produced by the bacteria were scraped from crack surfaces and analyzed by powder XRD (Siemens D8 Advance, Germany). The diffraction pattern of the sample was compared to the standard reference pattern for calcite (from the RRUFF mineral database). High-intensity peaks at 2θ ≈ 29.4°, 36.0°, 39.4°, 43.1°, 47.5°, and 48.5° matching the reference confirm the presence of calcite. Each bacterial treatment’s precipitate was checked to ensure the crystalline phase was calcite and not another polymorph or mineral (Fig. 4).

16S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

To identify the taxonomic affiliation of environmental isolates and verify reference strains, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed. Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial kit, and the 16S gene was amplified by PCR with universal primers (27F/1492R). PCR products (~ 1500 bp) were confirmed by gel electrophoresis, purified, and Sanger sequenced bidirectionally. Sequences (~ 1400 bp after trimming) are being submitted to GenBank.

Sequences were aligned using Clustal Omega, and pairwise identity was calculated to generate a distance matrix and identity heatmap. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Reference sequences for B. subtilis and S. pasteurii were included. Visualization was performed in CLC Genomics Workbench. Several isolates (e.g., R8.1, R4.3, KH6.1) exhibited greater than 99% identity and clustered closely, indicating a shared taxonomic classification. Chromatograms were also reviewed to confirm sequence integrity.

Results

Enzyme activity results

The enzymatic profiles of reference and environmental strains were evaluated to assess their potential for MICP. Urease and carbonic anhydrase (CA) activities were measured and compared across all strains (Figs. 5 and 6).

The results demonstrate that several environmental isolates—particularly SH4.2 and SH5.2—exhibited substantially higher carbonic anhydrase activity than both the maximum (blue dotted line) and average (red dotted line) activities observed in the reference group, which included Bacillus sp

Reference strains exhibited expected enzymatic behavior. Sporosarcina pasteurii showed the highest urease activity (~ 700 U/OD, Fig. 5), consistent with previous reports. In contrast, Bacillus sphaericus produced lower urease but showed strong CA activity (~ 25 U/OD), supporting the known enzymatic specialization among MICP-capable species (Fig. 6). B. subtilis displayed moderate levels of both enzymes, suggesting dual-pathway potential.

Environmental isolates revealed broader and, in some cases, superior enzymatic performance. Isolate KH1.1 surpassed S. pasteurii in urease activity (~ 708 U/mL), while isolates R8.1 and E6.2 also matched or exceeded reference benchmarks. CA screening identified SH4.2 and SH5.2 as top performers, with CA activities exceeding all reference strains, including B. sphaericus (Figs. 5 and 6). These isolates likely originated from CO₂-rich environments, favoring CA expression.

The observed inverse relationship between urease and CA activities suggests enzymatic specialization. Most strains favored one dominant pathway, with few—such as E6.2 and B. subtilis—showing moderate activity in both, potentially enabling flexibility under varying nutrient conditions.

The weighting scheme for the EAI was based on preliminary correlation analyses, which showed a stronger logarithmic relationship for normalized CA activity and a weaker linear contribution for urease activity. EAI values were calculated as described in Materials and Methods.

Strains with EAI ≥ 2.4 were classified as high-activity candidates. Top-performing strains included S. pasteurii, B. subtilis, B. sphaericus, and one Citrobacter isolate. Notably, B. subtilis achieved an EAI ≈ 3.3 due to balanced enzyme contributions.

While enzymatic activity is a key performance indicator, survivability under high pH is equally critical because the internal pore solution of cementitious materials typically exceeds pH 12.5. Most vegetative bacterial cells lose metabolic function or lyse under such alkaline conditions, preventing enzyme-mediated CaCO3 formation. Therefore, only strains capable of maintaining viability or forming resistant spores can sustain biomineralization and ensure long-term self-healing within concrete. Therefore, strains with high EAI were next subjected to evaluations of alkaline tolerance and spore formation before being applied in mortar.

Survivability and sporulation capacity of isolates under alkaline cement conditions

Spore-forming strains such as B. subtilis, B. sphaericus, and S. pasteurii showed strong tolerance, with only 1–2 log CFU reductions after repeated exposure. In contrast, non-sporulating strains (e.g., Citrobacter J2.2, J4.2) lost viability entirely after the second exposure (Fig. 7).

Most high-enzyme-activity strains were also found to be spore-formers, making them suitable candidates for concrete applications. Conversely, strains like J4.2, despite good enzyme output, were excluded due to poor survival and lack of sporulation (Fig. 7).

Based on both survival and enzyme performance, four robust strains were selected for mortar testing: B. subtilis (balanced enzyme profile), S. pasteurii (high urease), B. sphaericus (high CA), and one environmental Bacillus isolate. These were chosen to represent complementary calcification pathways and proceeded to the self-healing evaluation phase.

Genetic identification of isolates (16S rRNA analysis)

16S rRNA sequencing confirmed that the top-performing isolates belonged to Bacillus and Sporosarcina genera (Fig. 8). For example, KH1.1 clustered with S. pasteurii, while E10.2 grouped with B. sphaericus.

The results showed that all top-performing isolates had over 97% identity with known Bacillus or Sporosarcina species, which are common in MICP research. For example, KH1.1 (our best urease producer) grouped closely with Sporosarcina pasteurii, suggesting it may be a local variant with even higher enzyme activity. E10.2 (high CA activity) clustered with Bacillus sphaericus or B. pseudofirmus, both known for surviving in high-pH environments.Three other isolates—R8.1, R4.3, and KH6.1—were nearly identical (>99%), indicating they likely belong to the same species. Since their enzyme activities were moderate, only R8.1 was selected for further testing. In summary, the phylogenetic analysis confirms that our selected strains belong to reliable genera—Bacillus and Sporosarcina—known for their spore-forming ability and performance in self-healing concrete (Fig. 8). This supports our strategy of using survival and enzyme activity to guide strain selection rather than relying only on taxonomy.

Self-healing performance in mortar

Fifteen bacterial treatments were tested in mortar cubes with 0.3 mm pre-formed cracks. Healing was assessed over 28 days through visual inspection, ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV), and compressive strength recovery. By day 28, most bio-treated mortars showed substantial crack closure, with white crystalline deposits visibly filling the cracks. In contrast, control samples showed minimal natural healing. Particularly strong healing was observed with S. pasteurii, where cracks were fully bridged by hard, crystalline precipitate.

Urease-based treatments (with urea) produced rapid surface mineralization within 1–2 weeks but showed some irregularity. In contrast, CA-based treatments (e.g., isolate E10.2 with bicarbonate) resulted in slower yet more uniform and deeper filling by day 28.

UPV results confirmed internal crack repair, with most treated samples achieving UPV ratios of ~0.9–1.0 (vs. ~0.6 for controls). S. pasteurii, B. sphaericus, and isolate KH6.1 achieved recovery levels comparable to, or in some cases slightly exceeding, those of the uncracked control specimens.

These results support the role of microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) in restoring structural integrity and highlight differences in healing dynamics based on enzymatic pathways.

Internal crack healing assessed by UPV

UPV testing was used to assess internal crack healing. Normalized UPV ratios were calculated by dividing the UPV of each cracked specimen by the average UPV of its uncracked counterpart. A ratio near 1.0 indicated effective healing. The control group showed a lower ratio (~ 0.6), confirming minimal healing. In contrast, most bacterial treatments achieved ratios near or above 0.9, indicating substantial internal repair. S. pasteurii, B. sphaericus, and isolate KH6.1 performed best, with values approaching or slightly exceeding 1.0—suggesting not only crack filling but potential densification of the surrounding matrix. It should be emphasized that, due to the small specimen size (50 × 50 × 50 mm), UPV results are interpreted here as a comparative indicator of internal continuity recovery rather than as definitive evidence of complete crack closure or full self-healing.

B. subtilis also improved UPV but remained slightly below the top group. Results were consistent across replicates, with limited variability. These findings aligned with visual healing observations and were further supported by compressive strength recovery, presented in the following section.

Compressive strength and healing efficiency

Compressive strength was measured at 28 days for both uncracked and pre-cracked mortar cubes treated with various bacterial formulations. Most bio-concrete mixes outperformed the control (38.5 MPa), with uncracked samples incorporating isolates R8.1-L and E10.2-L reaching ~ 20% higher strength (~ 46 MPa), suggesting matrix reinforcement even before damage.

In pre-cracked specimens, significant recovery was observed. While the cracked control reached only ~ 34.2 MPa, E10.2-Lc achieved ~ 46.8 MPa, exceeding uncracked control strength (healing ratio ~ 102%). J2.2-Lc also recovered strongly (~ 44.1 MPa). This indicates that microbial calcite not only repaired the cracks but may have densified surrounding microstructures.

Some treatments (e.g., KH6.1-L, SH4.2-L) showed limited recovery, likely due to suboptimal precipitation or nutrient compatibility. Overall, targeted microbial–nutrient pairing enabled both effective healing and matrix enhancement, confirming the potential of bio–concrete strategies.

Effect of calcium source and confirmation of structural healing

Our results also showed that the type of calcium source had a clear impact on compressive strength and healing performance. For the urease-based strain S. pasteurii, the mix using calcium nitrate (S.P.-U-N) produced slightly higher strength (~47 MPa uncracked, ~46 MPa cracked healed) than the version with calcium lactate. This is likely because nitrate and urea together create a highly alkaline environment, which boosts urease activity and accelerates calcite formation. This effect can be explained by the fact that urea hydrolysis releases ammonia and carbonate, which increases the pH, while calcium nitrate provides abundant calcium ions; together, these conditions create a strongly alkaline and ion-rich environment that enhances urease activity and accelerates CaCO3 precipitation.

In contrast, the carbonic anhydrase-based strain E10.2 performed better with calcium lactate. Organic lactate may support CO₂ generation and maintain a more stable pH, helping CA activity and cell metabolism. These findings suggest that matching the nutrient source to the bacterial pathway is important:

Microstructural evidence of calcite precipitation

Crack regions in selected specimens were analyzed using SEM, EDX, and XRD to confirm the presence of biogenic calcium carbonate (CaCO3).

SEM imaging revealed clear differences between treatments. Control samples showed open cracks lined with typical cement hydration products (e.g., portlandite, ettringite), while bio-treated specimens (e.g., with S. pasteurii or isolate J2.2) exhibited dense, rhombohedral calcite crystals bridging the crack faces.

EDX spectra confirmed strong Ca and C peaks in bacterial samples, consistent with stoichiometric CaCO3. Control samples lacked carbon signals. Occasional silicon traces suggested minor interaction with cement phases.

XRD analysis identified calcite as the dominant mineral phase, with no evidence of aragonite or vaterite, confirming the stability of the precipitate.

These findings demonstrate that bacterial activity resulted in the in-situ formation of stable calcite within cracks, explaining the strength and UPV recovery observed. Performance varied by strain and nutrient regime: ureolytic strains (e.g., S. pasteurii) were most effective with urea and calcium nitrate, while CA-based strains (e.g., E10.2) performed best with sodium bicarbonate and calcium lactate.

Strains combining high enzymatic activity with strong alkaline resistance (e.g., B. sphaericus, E10.2, KH6.1) consistently produced effective healing, validating the screening approach. Overall, these microstructural results confirm that crack sealing was due to true calcite precipitation, contributing to mechanical durability and structural recovery.

Discussion

This study establishes a framework for selecting indigenous bacteria for self-healing concrete by integrating enzyme screening, viability tests, and performance validation. Our results show that while reference strains specialize in either urease or carbonic anhydrase (CA) activity, some indigenous isolates (e.g., KH1.1 for urease and E10.2 for CA) outperformed them. This highlights the value of exploring local microbial diversity rather than relying solely on standard references.

In classical S. pasteurii–based systems, crack sealing was primarily evidenced by regained liquid tightness and microscopy of sub-millimeter cracks after wet curing, whereas our approach integrates a priori dual-enzyme screening (urease + CA) with alkaline survival to pre-select strains before mortar testing (Wang et al. 2024). Recent work by Sarkar et al. (Chem. Eng. J., 481 (2024) 148,148) demonstrated that fly-ash-assisted microbial biomineralization can significantly enhance CaCO3 precipitation efficiency and sustainability in self-healing concretes, further supporting the importance of enzyme-driven indigenous bacterial systems like the dual-enzyme framework proposed here (Sarkar et al. 2024).

The Enzyme Activity Index (EAI), which combines both activities, proved effective in identifying robust candidates, as strains with higher EAI consistently showed superior healing. Importantly, survivability under highly alkaline conditions emerged as a critical determinant: spore-forming strains such as B. subtilis and S. pasteurii retained viability where others failed. This confirms that enzymatic potential alone is insufficient; integrating survival capacity into screening provides a more reliable predictor of bio-concrete performance.

The strong correlation between spore formation capacity and survival validates previous observations by Wiktor & Jonkers (2011) and emphasizes the need to integrate sporulation potential into strain selection (Wiktor & Jonkers 2011). Several promising enzyme-producing isolates (e.g., J4.2) were ultimately disqualified due to their poor survivability, highlighting that enzymatic capability alone is insufficient. Therefore, our dual-screening framework—combining enzyme output and survival—offers a more reliable predictor of performance in concrete matrices.

Visual inspection and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) testing showed that bacterial treatments led to near-complete healing of 0.3 mm cracks within 28 days. Notably, mixes with KH6.1 and S. pasteurii produced thick mineral layers visibly sealing the cracks (Fig. 11). UPV ratios close to or above 1.0 in these treatments confirm that the internal continuity of the mortar was restored or even enhanced. These findings are consistent with those of Tziviloglou et al. (2016), who reported similar improvements in wave transmission and impermeability following bacterial healing (Tziviloglou et al. 2016). Prior work often quantified healing by permeability recovery rather than wave-based indices; consistent with that literature, we interpret our UPV gains as supportive mechanical recovery and explicitly acknowledge the need for complementary permeability or micro-CT assessments in larger beams (Fig. 4). In compressive strength testing, bio-concrete specimens consistently outperformed the control group.

In the present study, the self-healing performance was systematically evaluated on preformed cracks with an initial width of approximately 0.3 mm, which represents the upper range of microcracks typically targeted by autonomous healing strategies in cementitious materials (Fig. 10). Under these conditions, near-complete crack sealing and substantial mechanical recovery were achieved within 28 days of wet curing.

It should be emphasized that cracks wider than 0.3 mm or highly irregular crack geometries were not investigated in this work. Healing efficiency in wider cracks is generally limited by nutrient diffusion, bacterial viability, and precipitation kinetics, and is therefore expected to decrease as crack width increases. Consequently, the results presented here should be interpreted as representative of early-age or service microcracks, rather than large structural cracks requiring external repair.

From a structural perspective, cracks in the range of 0.1–0.3 mm are commonly encountered in reinforced concrete due to shrinkage, thermal stresses, or service loading, and are widely recognized as critical pathways for durability-related degradation rather than immediate structural failure. The ability of the selected bacterial systems to effectively seal cracks within this range highlights their practical relevance for durability enhancement.

Future studies should extend this framework to larger and more irregular cracks, as well as to larger structural elements, using complementary techniques such as permeability measurements and micro-CT imaging to more comprehensively assess healing limits under realistic service conditions.

In compressive strength testing, bio-concrete specimens consistently outperformed the control group. The cracked specimens treated with E10.2 and J2.2 achieved substantial strength recovery, in some cases reaching or slightly surpassing the levels of the uncracked control (Fig. 9).

This suggests not only crack sealing but also densification of the surrounding matrix. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of these measurements. Although ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and compressive strength recovery provided valuable evidence of crack healing, these techniques have limitations when applied to small mortar specimens. In particular, UPV cannot by itself confirm complete crack closure, and overestimation of healing efficiency is possible. Therefore, our findings are best understood as evidence of substantial recovery of mechanical pro00perties under the tested conditions, rather than a definitive demonstration of complete self-healing.

Although ultrasonic pulse velocity and compressive strength recovery provide valuable indicators of internal continuity and mechanical restoration, they do not fully capture long-term durability performance. Key durability-related parameters such as water permeability, sorptivity, and resistance to chloride ingress were not investigated in the present study. Consequently, the durability implications of bacterial healing are inferred indirectly from mechanical recovery and microstructural evidence rather than measured explicitly. Future work should incorporate these durability metrics, preferably on larger structural specimens, to comprehensively assess transport properties and long-term resistance to aggressive environments.

Future work should include larger beam specimens (40 × 40 × 160 mm) and complementary methods such as permeability, micro-CT, or dye-penetration testing to more comprehensively evaluate crack closure and durability under realistic conditions.

The strength gains observed in uncracked specimens (up to 20% higher than control) further support the hypothesis that microbial calcite contributes to structural reinforcement even before cracks occur. Thus, healing is moisture-triggered rather than continuous, and the nutrient level provided during mixing is sufficient for this localized reactivation process.

The healing process was monitored through both visual and instrumental analyses. Crack closure was first assessed by optical observation and high-resolution photography before and after 28 days of wet curing. Internal healing was verified by ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) recovery, while the formation of CaCO3 was confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) of the healed regions. These combined techniques provided both morphological and compositional evidence of moisture-triggered microbial calcite precipitation.

Our results corroborate findings by Akindahunsi et al. (2021) and Mirshahmohammad et al. (2023), who noted strength improvements in bacterial concretes due to CaCO3 filling pores and enhancing particle bonding (Huang; Mirshahmohammad et al. 2023). However, we also identified strains (E1.2, SH4.2, WKH6.1) with strong enzyme profiles but poor field performance, reinforcing the value of a holistic selection approach.

Our experiments show that the nutrient regime significantly affects bacterial performance. Ureolytic strains (like S. pasteurii) responded better to calcium nitrate and urea, likely due to increased pH accelerating urease-driven calcite formation. In contrast, CA-based strains (e.g., E10.2) performed better with calcium lactate and bicarbonate, which provided CO₂ and maintained favorable pH conditions for CA activity. This observation suggests that nutrient-pathway alignment is essential. For urease-dominant strains, inorganic nitrogen sources enhance activity, while CA-focused strains benefit from organic carbon sources. Future MICP designs should tailor nutrient provision to the specific metabolic strengths of the selected strains.

SEM and EDX analyses confirmed that the precipitates in bio-treated cracks were indeed CaCO3. Classic rhombohedral crystals bridged crack faces, and EDX spectra revealed strong Ca and C peaks in stoichiometric ratios. XRD further confirmed the exclusive presence of calcite (no vaterite or aragonite). These observations are critical because they confirm that bacterial activity did not merely stimulate further cement hydration but instead produced a chemically distinct and durable healing phase. Moreover, failure mode analysis of strength-tested cubes showed that cracks in bio-concrete often shifted away from the original flaw, unlike controls which fractured along the same line. This qualitative evidence reinforces the mechanical data, proving that the microbial healing process restored structural integrity.

Our findings provide a compelling case for the integration of indigenous MICP bacteria into bio-concrete applications. The dual-criterion selection method (enzyme activity + alkaline tolerance) reliably identified high-performing strains, while tailored nutrient regimes enhanced their effect. Importantly, our work suggests the feasibility of using local isolates to match or exceed the performance of commercial strains, reducing reliance on imported bio-agents. This study also highlights the need for standardization in bacterial screening and performance evaluation. The EAI framework and alkaline survival assays presented here can be applied broadly to other candidate strains. Future research should explore long-term durability under field conditions, effects of cycling wet-dry environments, and the scalability of bacterial incorporation into industrial concrete mixes. Overall, this research advances the field by demonstrating that careful microbial selection and mix design can yield self-healing concrete with improved strength, sustainability, and reliability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a systematic framework for selecting and applying indigenous bacteria for microbial-induced calcite precipitation (MICP) in self-healing concrete. Screening of over 200 isolates revealed that enzymatic specialization is common, with some indigenous strains outperforming reference organisms in urease or carbonic anhydrase activity. The Enzyme Activity Index (EAI) effectively integrated both pathways, identifying robust candidates with superior biomineralization potential.

Survival assays confirmed that spore-forming strains, particularly Bacillus and Sporosarcina species, maintained high viability under simulated pore solutions, validating the importance of combining enzymatic activity with stress resistance in strain selection. Mortar experiments further showed that selected strains enabled substantial recovery of compressive strength and ultrasonic pulse velocity, indicating significant mechanical restoration and internal continuity improvement. Overall, this dual-enzyme, performance-based screening approach provides a reliable strategy for identifying regionally adapted bacteria capable of enhancing the durability of cementitious materials. Future work should focus on scaling these findings to field applications, optimizing nutrient-bacteria combinations under real construction conditions, and evaluating long-term stability and cost–benefit performance of bio-concrete systems.
