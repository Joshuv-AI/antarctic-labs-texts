Association of iron status during early pregnancy and the risk of gestational diabetes: a retrospective cohort study in Shanghai
Authors: Xiaofeng Wang, Guangsheng Liang, Lanxin Liu, Huihui Ma, Yu Meng, Shuying Wang, Mi Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between maternal iron status and the risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) remains elusive. Clearer evidence is needed to inform appropriate ferritin thresholds and iron supplementation guidelines during pregnancy.ObjectivesTo investigate whether first-trimester serum ferritin (SF) concentration is associated with subsequent GDM risk.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingA tertiary obstetrics and gynaecology hospital in Shanghai, China.ParticipantsA total of 6429 low-risk pregnant women at fewer than 14 weeks of gestation were finally included.Outcome measuresGDM was diagnosed using a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test according to the criteria established by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG). Women were stratified by SF concentration into three groups: <30, 30–70 and >70 µg/L. Logistic regression was used to calculate adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for GDM, with the 30–70 µg/L group serving as the reference.ResultsGDM was diagnosed in 1215 women (18.9%). Elevated SF (>70 µg/L) was independently associated with increased GDM risk (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.54) compared with the reference group (30–70 µg/L). Stratified analysis revealed that the association was significant only in normal-weight women (body mass index (BMI)<24 kg/m²; OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.79), while no significant association was observed in overweight or obese women, indicating effect modification by prepregnancy BMI.ConclusionsElevated first-trimester SF is independently associated with GDM risk in pregnant women at low-risk, with the association most pronounced among those with normal prepregnancy BMI.

Introduction

Iron is an essential micronutrient during pregnancy; however, its relationship with maternal health is inherently paradoxical: as both iron deficiency and excess carry distinct risks. Serum ferritin (SF) is the most widely used clinical marker of iron stores, although its levels can be confounded by infection and acute inflammation.1 2 Iron deficiency remains the most prevalent micronutrient deficiency worldwide and is associated with increased maternal morbidity and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including low birth weight, preterm delivery and intrauterine growth restriction. Iron deficiency anaemia represents its most severe manifestation.3–5 To prevent iron deficiency anaemia, the WHO recommends 30–60 mg of elemental iron daily throughout pregnancy.6 Nevertheless, accumulating evidence indicates that iron excess may be equally harmful, particularly in relation to type 2 diabetes7–9 and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).10–14

As a redox-active transition metal, iron catalyses the generation of reactive oxygen species, driving oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation and DNA damage. These processes impair pancreatic β-cell function, reduce insulin secretion and promote peripheral insulin resistance by disrupting intracellular insulin signalling.15–17 Because pregnancy inherently elevates insulin demand; any combination of compromised insulin secretion and heightened resistance threatens glucose homeostasis and may precipitate GDM.18 19 Compounding this, elevated ferritin frequently reflects a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, which further exacerbates insulin resistance.20–23

GDM is defined as glucose intolerance first recognised during pregnancy24 and is one of the most common medical complications of pregnancy globally.25–27 It is associated with a broad spectrum of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including pre-eclampsia, polyhydramnios, caesarean delivery, shoulder dystocia, birth trauma, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, jaundice and perinatal mortality.28 Beyond the index pregnancy, GDM confers long-term metabolic risks: affected mothers and their offspring face elevated rates of cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes.29 30

Despite several prospective investigations in Western and multiethnic populations, large-scale evidence from contemporary Chinese populations remains scarce, and existing studies report inconsistent findings regarding the ferritin–GDM association.11 12 31–34 Moreover, few studies have systematically examined whether prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) modifies this association, particularly in non-obese women. Drawing on detailed clinical data from a major tertiary centre in Shanghai, this retrospective cohort study aims to: (1) characterise the association between first-trimester SF and GDM risk in a large Chinese cohort; (2) assess the robustness of this association after adjustment for multiple metabolic confounders; and (3) determine whether this association is modified by prepregnancy BMI or maternal age.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the International Peace Maternity and Child Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, using data from January 2021 to December 2021. Clinical data from the first antenatal visit and 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results were retrieved from the hospital electronic medical record system. Women were excluded if they had: (1) pre-existing chronic diseases, including type 1 and type 2 diabetes, malignancies, hypertension, hypothyroidism, chronic renal failure, severe anaemia, immune disorders or acute and chronic liver diseases; (2) multiple gestations; (3) acute and chronic inflammatory or infectious diseases; (4) drug or alcohol abuse; (5) psychiatric disorders; (6) spontaneous abortions before 24 weeks of gestation; or (7) incomplete medical records, specifically missing first-trimester clinical data or OGTT results. All eligible women had a confirmed last menstrual period (LMP) and gestational age verified and adjusted by first-trimester ultrasound. A total of 6429 pregnant women were ultimately included.

Data collection

Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including maternal age, LMP, parity, educational attainment, health insurance status, employment status, prepregnancy BMI, family history of diabetes, history of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Blood samples collected at the first antenatal visit were analysed for haemoglobin (Hb), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), SF, triglyceride (TG), homocysteine (HCY), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C). These metabolic markers were recorded as part of the baseline characteristics. A second blood draw was performed at the time of the 75 g OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, including Hb, FPG, HbA1c, transferrin and plasma glucose at 1 hour and 2 hours postglucose load.

Definitions

GDM was diagnosed by 75 g OGTT at 24–28 weeks per International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria: FPG 5.1–6.9 mmol/L, 1-hour plasma glucose ≥10.0 mmol/L or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥8.5-11 mmol/L. First-trimester anaemia was defined as Hb <110 g/L. Prepregnancy BMI was calculated as weight recorded within 3 months before conception divided by height squared (kg/m2), and participants were dichotomised as normal/underweight (<24 kg/m²) or overweight/obese (≥24 kg/m²) based on the China Obesity Task Force criteria. Given the absence of pregnancy-specific ferritin reference ranges in the literature, we adopted clinically grounded cut-offs informed by physiological iron requirements: a preconceptional iron store of approximately 500 mg, corresponding to SF of 70–80 µg/L, is estimated to sustain a normal pregnancy without supplementation. Participants were therefore grouped as: Group 1 (low): SF <30 µg/L; Group 2 (normal): SF 30–70 µg/L; Group 3 (high): SF >70 µg/L.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Between-group differences in categorical variables were assessed using the χ² test, and differences in continuous variables were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (assuming normal distribution and homogeneity of variance). Pearson correlation and univariable binary logistic regression were used to characterise relationships between SF and maternal variables. Multivariable logistic regression with sequential covariate adjustment was then applied to transparently quantify the influence of successive confounder set on the ferritin–GDM association. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs were calculated. Covariates included maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, parity and family history of diabetes (online supplemental figure 1).

Effect modification by age, BMI and parity was evaluated by incorporating interaction terms between categorical SF levels and each factor into separate logistic regression models. The significance of interaction terms was assessed using likelihood ratio tests (α=0.05). If significant interactions were identified, stratified analyses were performed. Where interaction terms were significant, stratified analyses were performed by age (<25, 25–30, 30–35, ≥35 years) and BMI: (normal/underweight vs overweight/obesity), and stratum-specific ORs with 95% CIs were reported.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results. First, we excluded women with autoimmune diseases, malignancies, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis or severe metabolic disorders. Second, we re-evaluated the association using sequential covariate adjustment models with SF as the predictor and GDM as the outcome. Full results of the sensitivity analyses are provided in online supplemental tables 1–3. All statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Of the 6429 pregnant included in the final analyses, baseline characteristics stratified by SF groups are presented in table 1. Several maternal characteristics differed significantly across ferritin strata. Women in the highest ferritin group (>70 µg/L) were more frequently nulliparous (49.4% vs 6.6% in the lowest group) and more often reported a family history of diabetes (11.2% vs 7.4%). Conversely, women in the lowest ferritin group (<30 µg/L) had markedly higher rates of anaemia (13.0% vs 3.2%), higher HCY (5.32±1.34 µmol/L vs 4.93±1.23 µmol/L) and higher LDL-C (2.67±0.64 mmol/L vs 2.39±0.61 mmol/L). Maternal age, educational attainment, parity, Hb, HDL-C and PCOS history also differed significantly across groups (all p<0.05). Prepregnancy BMI, overweight/obesity prevalence, gestational age at enrolment, TG levels, health insurance status, employment status did not differ significantly (all p>0.05). This heterogeneity in sociodemographic, obstetric and metabolic profiles across ferritin strata highlights the importance of multivariable adjustment in subsequent analyses. Midpregnancy laboratory findings at 24–28 weeks are shown in table 2. Significant between-group differences were observed in glucose metabolism parameters and GDM incidence. Women with the highest first-trimester SF (>70 µg/L) had the highest 1-hour postload plasma levels (8.00±1.62 mmol/L, p<0.001) and the greatest GDM incidence (19.6%, p<0.05). Those in the lowest ferritin group (<30 µg/L) had elevated HbA1c (5.04±0.32%, p<0.05) and lower Hb (112.41 g/L, p<0.001). FPG, 2-hour postload glucose and gestational age at testing did not differ significantly across groups. Collectively, these findings suggest that elevated first-trimester ferritin is associated with impaired postload glucose metabolism and higher GDM risk by midpregnancy.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 6429 Chinese pregnant women, elevated first-trimester SF (>70 µg/L) was independently associated with increased GDM risk after adjustment for multiple confounders. The association was most pronounced in younger women and those with normal prepregnancy BMI, providing meaningful evidence to the ongoing debate on the role of iron metabolism in GDM pathogenesis.

Our findings align with a growing body of prospective evidence. One cohort study reported a 2.31-fold increased GDM risk in the highest SF group (>87.2 µg/L; 95% CI 1.30 to 4.10),11 and a large Chinese prospective cohort study found a similar elevated risk in the highest first-trimester ferritin group (OR 1.179, 95% CI 1.017 to 1.367).22 A meta-analysis of 10 observational studies further corroborates these findings, reporting a relative risk (RR) of 1.87 (95% CI 1.50 to 2.34) comparing the highest to lowest ferritin concentrations, with each 10 µg/L increment in ferritin associated with an 8% increase in GDM risk (RR 1.08, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.13).35 Intriguingly, iron deficiency anaemia has been associated with a reduced GDM risk, raising the possibility of a U-shaped relationship between iron status and glucose metabolism.36 Null findings in other studies are likely attributable to heterogeneity in sample size, study design, timing of ferritin measurement and inconsistent adjustment for inflammatory confounders.31 37 38

Despite this accumulating evidence, no validated SF threshold for GDM prediction has been established. The highest ferritin quintile across most studies falls between 38.5 and 90 µg/L, broadly consistent with replete iron stores in pregnancy.12 28 31–33 39 Our data extend this literature by demonstrating that iron stores sufficient to sustain pregnancy without supplementation (SF > 70 µg/L) are independently associated with GDM risk. This is mechanistically supported by evidence that iron supplementation (≥60 mg/day) in women with mid-range ferritin (29.6–89.6 ng/mL) confers a GDM risk comparable to that of women with high early-pregnancy ferritin (≥90.0 ng/mL),39 and that high-dose supplementation (≥110+ mg/day) independently increases GDM risk, prompting reconsideration of routine supplementation in non-anaemic women.40 This is physiologically plausible: unlike most minerals, iron lacks a regulated excretory pathway, meaning intake can progressively accumulate to potentially harmful levels.41 42 Current international guidance diverges considerably; the WHO recommends 30–60 mg elemental iron daily throughout pregnancy, while the UK guidelines reserve supplementation for women with SF <30 µg/L or those at increased risk of iron depletion.6 43 Our findings strongly support individualised supplementation strategies predicated on early-pregnancy iron status assessment.

We observed that elevated first-trimester SF (≥70 µg/L) is associated with an increased risk of GDM. However, this association was significantly modified by prepregnancy BMI and maternal age. Specifically, a significant positive association was observed in women with normal prepregnancy weight, whereas no significant association was found in overweight/obese women. Furthermore, the association became non-significant in women aged ≥35 years. The significant association observed in normal-weight women suggests that in the absence of other dominant metabolic disturbances, iron overload can directly contribute to GDM risk by exacerbating oxidative stress. Conversely, the attenuated association in overweight/obese women may occur because the proinflammatory and pro-oxidant state inherent to obesity becomes the primary driver of insulin resistance, overshadowing the independent contribution of iron. Similarly, in women aged ≥35 years, age-related declines in antioxidant capacity and accumulated oxidative damage likely establish a predominant risk pathway, within which the relative effect of high iron status is diminished.44–47 These results underscore the importance of integrating ferritin levels with maternal characteristics in GDM risk assessment and provide a rationale for targeted preventive strategies in specific subgroups.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, SF is an acute-phase reactant and may be elevated by subclinical inflammation independent of true iron stores.48 Although we excluded women with overt infections and adjusted for BMI, the absence of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or other inflammatory indices remains a significant limitation for causal interpretation. Second, the observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured dietary factors, such as heme iron intake or antioxidant consumption, genetic predispositions to iron dysregulation or subtle inflammatory states cannot be excluded. Third, reliance on SF alone as an iron biomarker is a recognised limitation; more comprehensive indices such as transferrin saturation, soluble transferrin receptor and hepcidin were unavailable in this dataset. Finally, the application of broad exclusion criteria, while necessary for confounder control, likely enriched the cohort with low-risk pregnancies, potentially limiting generalisability to women with pre-existing complications or significant comorbidities.

Conclusions

Elevated SF is independently and positively associated with GDM risk in low-risk Chinese pregnant women, with the association most pronounced among those with normal prepregnancy BMI.
