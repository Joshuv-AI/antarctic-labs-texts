Laboratory approaches in desensitization for ABO-incompatible transplantation
Authors: Kyung-Hwa Shin, Hyun-Ji Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABO-incompatible transplantation, once considered contraindicated due to the risk of hyperacute rejection, has become a viable option owing to advances in immunosuppression and desensitization methods. Accurate measurement and effective reduction of anti-ABO isoagglutinin titers are essential for the success of ABO-incompatible transplantation. This review summarizes the principles of hemagglutination and the measurement of isoagglutinin titers, highlighting variables that influence agglutination sensitivity. The clinical significance of isoagglutinin rebound and its association with rejection risk are also discussed, along with ongoing efforts toward laboratory standardization. Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) is strongly linked to high pretransplant isoagglutinin titers (≥1:32). Posttransplant isoagglutinin rebound—an important risk factor for AMR—requires careful monitoring in liver and kidney transplant recipients with high initial titers. Desensitization strategies include rituximab, plasmapheresis, intravenous immunoglobulin, bortezomib, and emerging agents such as tocilizumab and imlifidase. This review aims to enhance understanding of the immunological and laboratory aspects of ABO-incompatible transplantation.Keywords: Transplantation, Hemagglutinins, Desensitization, Graft rejection, Plasmapheresis

INTRODUCTION

In 1900, Karl Landsteiner discovered that the serum of one individual can agglutinate the red blood cells (RBCs) of another. Blood type antigens were initially classified into types A, B, and C (later renamed O), demonstrating that individuals with blood type A possess antibodies against B antigens, while those with blood type B have antibodies against A antigens. Blood type antigens are expressed not only on RBC membranes but also in various body tissues [1]. Anti-A and anti-B blood type antibodies are known as isoagglutinins, hemagglutinins, or isohemagglutinins, as they cause agglutination of blood in vitro. Blood types A and B include immunoglobulin M (IgM), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin A (IgA) anti-A and anti-B antibodies, whereas type O predominantly contains IgG. IgG antibodies are capable of migrating from tissues into the bloodstream and crossing vascular barriers; therefore, ABO isoagglutinins can lead to antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) in ABO-incompatible organ transplantation [2].

In 1933, a human kidney transplant failed due to both warm ischemia and ABO blood type incompatibility. The first successful kidney transplant occurred in 1954 between genetically identical monozygotic twins, illustrating that organ transplantation is feasible in the absence of immunological rejection. Subsequent immunological advances have greatly improved transplant outcomes [3]. ABO-incompatible transplantation refers to procedures where donors and recipients have different blood types—such as from A to B or O, from B to A or O, and from AB to A, B, or O. Distinguishing between major and minor ABO incompatibility is essential, as their immunological consequences differ significantly. Major ABO incompatibility arises when the recipient has antibodies against the ABO antigens present on the donor’s cells or tissues [2]. For example, a blood group O recipient receiving a kidney from a blood group A or B donor would possess anti-A or anti-B antibodies capable of targeting the donor organ. This form of incompatibility is especially critical in solid organ transplantation, as these antibodies pose a high risk of hyperacute rejection and poor graft survival if not adequately managed. In contrast, minor ABO incompatibility occurs when the donor’s plasma or blood products contain ABO antibodies that can react with the recipient’s ABO antigens [2,4].

ABO-incompatible transplantation, previously deemed inappropriate and high-risk due to the potential for hyperacute rejection, has evolved into an accepted therapeutic option as a result of advances in immunosuppressive protocols and desensitization strategies. Alexandre and colleagues pioneered ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation in the early 1980s using repeated plasmapheresis, splenectomy, and immunosuppression, achieving a 1-year graft survival rate of 75%. In the 2000s, the introduction of rituximab and antigen-specific immunoadsorption (IA) columns further improved safety and outcomes, allowing ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation to reach graft survival rates similar to those of ABO-compatible procedures [5,6]. In living donor liver transplantation, particularly in East Asia, ABO-incompatible procedures have become increasingly common and yield outcomes comparable to ABO-compatible transplantation [7]. In pediatric heart transplantation, the immunologic immaturity of newborns has enabled ABO-incompatible transplantation to become the standard of care for patients under two years of age, significantly reducing waitlist mortality while maintaining noninferior survival outcomes [8]. Collectively, these developments highlight the transformation of ABO-incompatible transplantation from an experimental approach to an established clinical practice across multiple organ types.

The efficacy of ABO-incompatible transplantation has also benefited from progress in laboratory testing methodologies. Accurate blood group antibody testing is essential, with minimal variability in results. Moreover, ABO isoagglutinins must be efficiently reduced through appropriate desensitization therapy. This article reviews the methods used to measure ABO isoagglutinins and the procedures employed in desensitization treatment.

PRINCIPLES AND FACTORS AFFECTING AGGLUTINATION REACTIONS

Hemagglutination is a sensitive and easily performed technique used to detect both antigens and antibodies against RBC antigens. Agglutination occurs when there is a sufficient concentration of antigen or antibody present, whereas the absence of agglutination indicates levels that are too low for detection. For isoagglutinin titration, hemagglutination is employed semiquantitatively to assess antibody reactivity; conversely, in antigen testing, it is used to evaluate antigen expression.

Agglutination reactions proceed through two main steps. The first step, sensitization, refers to the reversible physical binding of antibody molecules to antigens on the erythrocyte surface. The second step, lattice formation, involves the creation of cross-links between sensitized particles and antibodies, resulting in visible aggregation. This second phase is markedly slower than the initial sensitization step [9].

The sensitivity and specificity of agglutination reactions are influenced by several factors, which can differentially affect the sensitization and lattice formation phases. First, the surface charge of the particles influences sensitization. RBCs have a net negative surface charge, which may induce repulsion between cells and hinder antibody-antigen interactions [9]. Second, the concentration of electrolytes and the viscosity of the reaction medium impact sensitization. Notably, the sodium chloride concentration in the reaction medium can influence antibody binding to erythrocyte antigens [9]. Third, the agglutination capabilities of certain antibody classes, particularly IgM and IgG), vary considerably. IgM antibodies exhibit superior efficacy in direct agglutination due to their larger size (approximately 30 nm) and greater number of binding sites, compared to IgG antibodies (approximately 14 nm). IgG antibodies often require the use of anti-human globulin (AHG) to facilitate agglutination in indirect assays. The primary antibodies in blood groups A and B are predominantly IgM anti-A and anti-B, although IgG may also be present. In contrast, IgG is predominant in blood group O [9]. Fourth, optimal agglutination depends on achieving the appropriate antigen-to-antibody ratio [9]. Fifth, the number and spatial distribution of antigenic determinants on the erythrocyte surface affect agglutination. ABO blood group antigens (A and B) are present at approximately 1.5 million sites per RBC. If antigenic sites are deeply embedded within the membrane or present in low density, antibodies may not be able to access them for interaction. Sixth, physiological factors also influence sensitization. The optimal temperature for equilibrium in antibody-antigen reactions varies by antibody class. IgM antibodies react at lower temperatures (4 °C–22 °C), while IgG antibodies react at higher temperatures, with an optimal temperature of 37 °C. Because IgM is predominant in the anti-A and anti-B antibodies of groups A and B, testing is often conducted at room temperature. In contrast, detecting IgG antibodies may require incubation at 37 °C. The incubation time needed for optimal agglutination depends on the association and dissociation rates of each antibody. METHODS OF ABO ISOAGGLUTININ TITRATION

ABO isoagglutinin measurement involves a sequential two-fold dilution process and the observation of agglutination. The titer is defined as the highest dilution factor at which agglutination remains visible [10]. The conventional method for ABO isoagglutinin titration uses test tubes. The column agglutination technique (CAT) detects agglutination within a gel matrix. CAT can be incorporated into automated systems or assessed visually by an observer. It omits a wash step, thereby reducing the chance of antigen-antibody complex dissociation and enhancing the detection of weakly positive reactions [11]. IgM primarily agglutinates in antibody agglutination assays performed at room temperature using the tube method, whereas IgG aggregates more rapidly with CAT than with the tube method. Dithiothreitol (DTT) treatment is employed to distinguish serum antibodies into IgG and IgM. Both automated and manual measurement approaches are available [11]. In Korea, laboratories use a variety of methods, including tube-immediate spin, tube-room temperature (RT), tube-AHG, tube-AHG with DTT, CAT-RT, CAT-AHG, and CAT-AHG with DTT [12]. Youk et al. [13] introduced new techniques for quantifying isoagglutinins using the tube method, applying the complement-dependent cytotoxicity assay to assess isoagglutinin-mediated reactions with RBCs, followed by plasma hemoglobin measurement to evaluate hemolysis. The correlation between isoagglutinin titer and hemolysis was examined. The findings demonstrated that higher titers were associated with increased hemolysis percentages for both IgM and IgG. Samples with low-titer ABO isoagglutinins—specifically, IgM titers below 1:4 or IgG titers below 1:32—showed negligible hemolysis [13].

Flow cytometry has emerged as a reliable, sensitive, and quantitative method for ABO isoagglutinin titration, overcoming the limitations associated with traditional hemagglutination assays. Won and Kim [14] demonstrated that their “Flow ABO Ab” protocol offered enhanced sensitivity and reproducibility for the detection of anti-ABO IgG antibodies compared to CAT, showing strong correlation with conventional titers and improved identification of borderline antibody levels. Bürzle et al. [15] further validated a fluorescence-activated cell sorting-based method, reporting significantly lower intra-assay variability and higher sensitivity to both IgM and IgG isoagglutinins compared to the hemagglutination method described in the European Pharmacopoeia, thus supporting its application in pharmaceutical quality control. Yang et al. [16] emphasized the clinical significance of IgG subclass analysis using flow cytometry, showing that persistent IgG1 isoagglutinins constitute an immunologic risk factor for hyperacute rejection in ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation—a complication not detected by standard IgM-based protocols.

CONSIDERATIONS AND METHODOLOGICAL VARIABILITY IN ABO ISOAGGLUTININ TITRATION

In clinical laboratories in Korea, both the tube method and CAT are used for ABO isoagglutinin titration, necessitating method-specific standardization of procedures and interpretation criteria. In 2023, a total of 38,831 ABO antibody titer tests were performed for 5,658 individuals, reflecting an increase from 31,896 tests for 4,677 patients in previous years [17]. Accurate interpretation of these results requires differentiation between IgM and IgG isoagglutinins and the standardization of endpoint definitions to reduce interobserver variability. Interoperator differences—especially in pipetting, reaction grading, and dilution handling—also contribute to inconsistencies. Well-defined protocols and comprehensive operator training are therefore essential. Participation in External Quality Assessment (EQA) programs, such as those operated by the College of American Pathologists and the Korean Association of External Quality Assessment Service, plays a critical role in identifying and correcting discrepancies. Although automation may facilitate standardization, routine quality control of instruments and reagents remains indispensable.

A nationwide Korean study on the standardization of ABO antibody titration identified several key sources of interlaboratory variability, including sample type (serum versus ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid plasma), red cell concentration, dilution protocols, endpoint definitions (e.g., weak or 1+), and the use of antiglobulin reagents (monospecific versus polyspecific). In a 2012 EQA survey involving 68 laboratories in Korea, substantial heterogeneity was observed in technical procedures and reagent use. A follow-up assessment demonstrated improved standardization, particularly regarding AHG-phase testing and RT incubation. Notably, laboratories employing CAT showed significantly lower variability in reported titers compared to those using the tube method, emphasizing the importance of both procedural consistency and platform selection for achieving reproducible results [18].

However, international studies have indicated that standardization alone does not completely resolve interlaboratory variation. AuBuchon et al. [19] reported that in a global multicenter study using the tube method, standardization reduced variability only when the endpoint was defined as weak positive; in other scenarios, its effect was minimal. Bachegowda et al. [20] analyzed College of American Pathologists proficiency testing data and found that harmonized procedures did not significantly reduce variance in seven out of eight testing events—and in some cases, variance increased following standardization. Most recently, Karafin et al. [21] reviewed 5 years of antibody titration data from 1,337 laboratories and found that over 50% of results deviated beyond ±1 titer of the manufacturer’s target value. Even among laboratories using the same methodology, up to 20% exhibited significant deviations. These findings underscore the limited effectiveness of procedural harmonization alone and highlight the need for contextual interpretation when using titer values to guide clinical decision-making.

Despite these challenges, 5 years of Korean EQA data demonstrated high rates of acceptable results, ranging from 84% to 100% per session [12]. This indicates relatively consistent performance across participating institutions, yet also reinforces the need for ongoing standardization tailored to specific test platforms. Several studies have examined concordance between the tube method and CAT. Nam et al. [22] reported moderate agreement for anti-A and anti-B titers in individuals with blood groups A and B, with tube method results generally higher, whereas CAT yielded higher titers in group O. Shim et al. [23] observed a one-dilution difference in IgG titers and variable concordance for IgM depending on blood type, ranging from 55% to 100% across antigen-antibody pairs. An Italian study comparing the automated Galileo NEO system with manual CAT found agreement rates of 66.2% for IgM and 60.6% for IgG titers [11]. Another study reported that semiautomated CAT yielded the highest titers for both IgM and IgG, followed by manual tube testing, while fully automated solid-phase techniques produced the lowest results [24]. Collectively, these findings emphasize that test results are influenced not only by standardization but also by the testing platform itself, highlighting the need for platform-specific reference ranges and interpretation strategies.

There is no universally preferred method; authors express varying preferences based on clinical experience, sensitivity, reproducibility, objectivity, cost, and workflow efficiency. Nonetheless, the general trend indicates a shift toward more standardized and automated methods to mitigate the inherent variability of manual techniques.

Clinical importance of ABO isoagglutinin

ABO isoagglutinins can induce AMR in ABO-incompatible organ transplantation. To prevent the onset of AMR, isoagglutinin levels must be reduced below a critical threshold. Elevated isoagglutinin titers are recognized as a predictor of potential acute kidney injury [25]. Therefore, monitoring isoagglutinin levels in the recipient’s plasma is essential. However, titration results are subject to significant interinstitutional variability, and protocol heterogeneity limits the interpretability of absolute titer values.

Morozumi et al. [26] reported that anti-ABO antibody titers—both IgG and IgM—exceeding 1:32 immediately before transplantation predict AMR. Desensitization is required to reduce anti-ABO antibody titers to 1:16 or lower. Following transplantation, a rise in isoagglutinin levels—referred to as rebound—is commonly observed and is strongly associated with AMR [27]. In kidney transplantation, an increase in isoagglutinin titers has been shown to raise the risk of acute rejection by 2.72-fold [28]. In liver transplantation, a postoperative isoagglutinin rebound of ≥1:1024 has been identified as a critical threshold for the development of clinical AMR [26].

The threshold that defines isoagglutinin rebound varies according to organ type. In ABO-incompatible liver transplantation, a postoperative isoagglutinin titer exceeding 1:64 is classified as rebound [27]. In ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation, rebound is defined as a titer of ≥1:16 occurring 2 weeks posttransplantation in patients who had a titer of <1:16 at the time of transplantation. For patients with an initial titer of ≥1:16, any increase is considered a rebound [25,28].

Rebound occurs more frequently in patients with high initial titers; however, the incidence varies depending on the transplant center and the organ involved [25,27,29]. Lee et al. [30] reported that no rebound in isoagglutinin titers occurred following ABO-incompatible liver transplantation among patients with initial titers below 1:16 in either cohort. Additionally, no significant difference in isoagglutinin titers was observed between the rituximab plus plasmapheresis and rituximab-only groups. Similarly, no difference in overall survival was seen between the two groups. Nevertheless, in patients with initial titers above 1:16, the rituximab plus plasmapheresis group demonstrated a more pronounced rebound in isoagglutinin titers following ABO-incompatible liver transplantation [16]. In one study, 67.3% of patients with an initial titer of ≥1:128 experienced rebound—the highest reported incidence to date [29]. Furthermore, rituximab-free preconditioning regimens in kidney transplantation have been associated with a higher incidence of isoagglutinin rebound (24.1%) compared to rituximab-treated groups (5.6%). Elevated initial IgM levels—but not IgG—have also been linked to posttransplant titer rebound in kidney transplant recipients [28]. These findings underscore the importance of postoperative isoagglutinin titer monitoring.

DESENSITIZATION METHODS FOR ABO ISOAGGLUTININ TITER REDUCTION

Rituximab, plasmapheresis, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and other therapies are commonly used to remove preexisting antibodies and prevent the production of de novo antibodies by depleting B cells, although specific protocols vary among institutions and transplant organ types [31,32].

Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, is a cornerstone of desensitization and is administered pretransplant to eliminate B cells [27,29]. However, rituximab may independently increase the risk of posttransplant infections due to B cell depletion [27]. The dosage varies by organ: for liver transplantation, 300–375 mg/m2 is typically administered 2–3 weeks prior to surgery [29], while for kidney transplantation, a dose of 200 mg is commonly used [25].

Plasmapheresis is effective at removing isoagglutinins from plasma. Numerous studies support its pretransplant efficacy in lowering isoagglutinin titers in ABO-incompatible transplantation. Plasmapheresis can be categorized by the method used for blood component separation, including centrifugation-based procedures, membrane filtration techniques, and selective adsorption methods. Centrifugation separates plasma from blood cells using centrifugal force, while membrane filtration relies on physical separation based on pore size. Membrane-based techniques include subtypes such as postcentrifugal plasma filtration, double filtration plasmapheresis, and selective plasma exchange, each allowing targeted removal of specific plasma components. These approaches differ in selectivity, efficiency, and the range of compounds eliminated. In Korea, plasma filters are not currently imported, restricting options to centrifugation-based plasmapheresis. Pretransplant target titers vary across studies (e.g., <1:8, ≤1:16, ≤1:32). Plasmapheresis is less common in patients with low initial isoagglutinin titers than in those with high titers, and the number of desensitization sessions required increases proportionally with the initial antibody level [4]. Thus, accurate titration is crucial for both planning and executing desensitization in ABO-incompatible transplantation [33,34]. According to the American Society for Apheresis [35], desensitization with either therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) or IA to prepare ABO-incompatible kidney or liver transplant recipients from living donors is classified as Category I, Grade 1B for kidney and Grade 1C for liver transplantation, reflecting strong recommendations. Plasma volumes treated per session range from one to 1.5 total plasma volumes for TPE, or 1.5 to 2.5 volumes for IA, and replacement fluids generally consist of albumin or plasma, depending on institutional protocols and patient-specific factors [35]. Desensitization protocols typically involve multiple sessions performed every other day, with the total number tailored to achieve target antibody titers before transplantation [35].

The mechanisms underlying IVIG’s desensitization effects are not fully understood, but several have been proposed, including F(ab')2-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, suppression of intercellular interactions, and neutralization of cytokines and autoantibodies. IVIG may also act by saturating Fc receptors, enhancing regulatory T cell activity, disrupting immune complex binding, and modulating dendritic cell function through various immunomodulatory pathways [36]. In ABO-incompatible liver transplantation, if the isoagglutinin titer exceeds the threshold (e.g., >1:16) immediately before surgery, simultaneous splenectomy and postoperative high-dose IVIG (0.8 g/kg/day for 5 days) may be administered [29].

Bortezomib induces plasma cell apoptosis by inhibiting the 26S proteasome, thereby blocking nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) translocation to the nucleus. Because plasma cells lack CD20 expression, they are resistant to rituximab. In patients who respond poorly to rituximab, desensitization with bortezomib may be considered [27]. For liver transplant recipients with high initial isoagglutinin titers (≥1:256), bortezomib (1.3 mg/m2) may be included in the desensitization protocol—alongside rituximab and plasma exchange—to target plasma cells. Bortezomib has been shown to reduce isoagglutinin rebound and prevent clinical AMR [27].

Novel agents for desensitization in ABO-incompatible organ transplantation are under active investigation to enhance current protocols and improve efficacy. Obinutuzumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20, leads to B cell depletion and reduced antibody production. It is used similarly to rituximab but may provide more potent and sustained B cell inhibition [7]. Imlifidase, an enzyme derived from Streptococcus pyogenes, rapidly cleaves IgG antibodies and is used to quickly reduce circulating IgG before transplantation, making it particularly useful in urgent or high-risk cases [7]. Tocilizumab, a monoclonal antibody against interleukin-6 (IL-6), suppresses immune responses by inhibiting IL-6 signaling, a pathway involved in inflammation and B cell activation. It is used both for desensitization and for the prevention of chronic AMR, as well as in the treatment of graft-versus-host disease [7]. Belimumab, tabalumab, and atacicept are monoclonal antibodies that inhibit B cell-activating factor (BAFF), a cytokine essential for B cell survival and differentiation. By targeting BAFF, these agents suppress B cell function and are being studied for use in desensitization protocols and AMR prevention [7]. These novel drugs represent promising enhancements to pretransplant immunosuppressive regimens intended to improve the safety and efficacy of ABO-incompatible transplantation.

Several studies have compared combination therapy (plasmapheresis plus rituximab) versus rituximab monotherapy regarding transplant outcomes. Some report no significant difference in survival rates, while the plasmapheresis group had a higher incidence of isoagglutinin rebound [30]. In liver transplant recipients with pretransplant titers of 1:32 or lower, rituximab monotherapy achieved outcomes comparable to those observed in ABO-compatible transplantation [37].

Certain centers implement personalized preconditioning protocols based on initial antidonor ABO antibody titers to minimize immunosuppressive burden and reduce infection risk [28]. For instance, patients with low titers (≤1:8) may receive only oral immunosuppressants, those with moderate titers (1:16) may receive immunosuppressants plus plasmapheresis, and those with high titers (≥1:32) typically require a combination of immunosuppressants, plasmapheresis, and rituximab [28]. Some studies have reported no significant difference in transplant outcomes when higher target titers are accepted for ABO-incompatible transplantation compared to current thresholds for isoagglutinin reduction.

Monitoring isoagglutinin titers after transplantation is essential, and postoperative plasmapheresis is initiated if a rebound is observed [25,27,29]. In ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation, postoperative plasmapheresis is performed when rebound titers reach or exceed 1:16 [25]; for ABO-incompatible liver transplantation, the threshold is 1:32 [29]. The addition of bortezomib has been shown to significantly reduce severe isoagglutinin rebound, especially in patients with high pretransplant titers, thereby preventing clinical AMR [27].

CONCLUSION

Desensitization therapy is essential in ABO-incompatible transplantation to prevent AMR. Accurate titration of isoagglutinin levels is critical for planning and evaluating desensitization treatment. A thorough understanding of the factors influencing agglutination reactions is necessary for the accurate assessment of isoagglutinin reactivity. In addition, familiarity with the characteristics of each test method is important for establishing appropriate titer thresholds. The effectiveness of desensitization therapy should be carefully evaluated in patients with high baseline titers, given the increased risk of isoagglutinin rebound in this population.
