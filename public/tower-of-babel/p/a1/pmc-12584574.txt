Transcatheter Management of Severe Paravalvular Leak in the Inferior Vena Cava Prosthesis of the TricValve Bicaval Valve System
Authors: Marcel A Beijk, Robbert J De Winter, Berto J Bouma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present a 74‐year‐old female with a history of mechanical aortic and mitral valve implantation and non‐Hodgkin lymphoma. She presented with right sided heart failure due to severe functional tricuspid regurgitation and was treated with transcatheter TricValve bicaval system implantation. However, this was complicated by a paravalvular leak of the inferior vena cava prosthesis. Treatment of the paravalvular leakage was successful with implantation of four Amplatzer Vascular Plugs II.Keywords: paravalvular leak, tricuspid regurgitation, TricValve bicaval valve system, vascular plug

Introduction

The prevalence of moderate or severe tricuspid regurgitation increases with age and affects approximately 4% of patients aged 75 years or more [1]. In over 90% of patients the aetiology is secondary (functional) due to (1) right ventricular (RV) dilatation as a result of pressure and/or volume overload or (2) enlarged right atrium and tricuspid annulus as a result of atrial fibrillation. Secondary tricuspid regurgitation is associated with left‐sided heart disease, that is, valvular or myocardial dysfunction, in the majority of cases and is independently related to mortality [1]. In contrast, the aetiology of primary tricuspid regurgitation may be infective endocarditis (mainly due to intravenous drug abuse), myxomatous disease, carcinoid syndrome, rheumatic heart disease, or congenital valve dysplasia (i.e., Ebstein's anomaly). Finally, cardiac implantable electronic device‐lead implantation may also lead to tricuspid regurgitation in 20%−30% [2, 3].

Severe tricuspid regurgitation is associated with progressive heart failure and impaired survival [4, 5, 6]. In general, tricuspid valve interventions are underused and appropriate timing is crucial to prevent irreversible RV damage and organ failure. Surgery is the first choice of treatment in symptomatic patients with severe primary tricuspid regurgitation and with secondary tricuspid regurgitation in patients undergoing left‐sided heart surgery. Surgery for isolated secondary tricuspid regurgitation is under debate. Transcatheter tricuspid valve intervention (TTVI) is a minimally invasive option for treatment for severe tricuspid regurgitation. Currently various TTVI systems are available enabling coaptation enhancement, annuloplasty (tricuspid repair), or valve replacement (orthotopic or heterotopic). The heterotopic bicaval trancatheter TricValve system (Products & Features) was designed for the treatment of severe tricuspid regurgitation in high‐risk surgical patients.

Case Report

A 74‐year‐old woman with right sided heart failure, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class 4, due to severe functional tricuspid regurgitation (Panel A) was referred for transcatheter management. Her medical history included mechanical aortic and mitral valve implantation due to aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis 35 years prior, and non‐Hodgkin lymphoma. Grading the severity by transthoracic echocardiography showed a massive (4+) tricuspid regurgitation according to the Hahn classification as the vena contracta was 15 mm and 2‐dimensional effective regurgitant orifice area was 107 mm2 [7]. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) revealed a tricuspid annulus of 54 mm with mild billowing of the anterior leaflet and a coaptation gap width 0.8 × 1.2 cm (Panel B). The patient was discussed in the multidisciplinary valvular team wherein surgery was deemed too high risk due to her medical history. Moreover, transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair was not feasible due to the large coaptation gap, and no orthtopic transcatheter TV replacement devices were available to us at that time. She was accepted for bicaval transcatheter TricValve system implantation. The procedure was performed with implantation of a 29 mm superior vena cava (SVC) prosthesis and 41 mm inferior vena cava (IVC) prosthesis (Panel D). However, positioning of the IVC prosthesis was challenging due to angulation of the IVC to the right atrium transition (Panel C) and a paravalvular leak (PVL) was observed immediately. Moreover, a persistent high v‐wave in the IVC of 37 mmHg was observed due to the PVL, the v‐wave in the right atrium was 44 mmHg.

After TricValve implantation, the patient remained symptomatic and TOE imaging showed severe PVL of the IVC prosthesis posterior through the stent frame and anterior of the prosthesis to the hepatic vein (Panel E and F, Video S1). Guided by TOE and fluoroscopy, the PVL was crossed using an IMA guiding catheter and four Amplatzer Vascular Plug II (14, 14, 16, and 12 mm) were successfully implanted (Panel G, Videos S2 and S3), leaving a mild PVL (Panel H, Videos S4 and S5). At 1 year follow‐up, the patient is in NYHA class 2, without lower‐limb oedema.

This case shows successful transcatheter management of severe PVL in the IVC prosthesis of the TricValve bicaval valve system.

Discussion

The heterotopic trancatheter TricValve system is a dedicated caval valve implantation device comprising of two self‐expanding nitinol stents equipped with bovine pericardial leaflets. The system is available in different sizes tailored to the anatomical characteristics of the SVC and IVC. Specifically, the SVC valve is available in diameters 25, 29, and 33 mm, while the IVC valves is available in diameters 31, 35, 41, and 45 mm. Pre‐procedure computed tomography (CT) imaging is essential for accurate assessment of the caval anatomy and to determine appropriate device sizing. The valves are pre‐mounted on a 27.5 Fr delivery catheter and the Tricvalve system has a Conformité Européenne (CE) mark.

The procedure is typically performed via right femoral venous access with fluoroscopic venography employed to guide positioning of the prosthetic valves. Deployment begins with placement of the SVC valve, which is positioned such that its central portion (“belly”) resides just inferior to the innominate confluence within the SVC. Subsequently, the IVC valve is deployed with the proximal edge situated between the cavo‐atrial junction and the confluence of the supra‐hepatic vein. It is critical that the IVC valve's extension into the right atrium does not exceed 10 mm, to minimize the risk of PVL. In addition, care must be taken to avoid obstruction of hepatic vein inflow by the 20 mm skirt of the IVC valve [8].

PVL is a potential complication following TricValve implantation often due to incomplete sealing of the SVC or IVC or malposition of the valves. PVL of the valves may be less hemodynamically significant than in aortic or mitral valve procedures but can still impact clinical outcomes. Factors contributing to PVL may include anatomical variability, underexpansion of the device, or calcified or irregular landing zones. Echocardiography and CT imaging are essential for both the diagnosis and assessment of PVL severity. Management of the PVL depends on the severity, that is, conservative monitoring in mild cases to percutaneous closure or even re‐intervention in more severe PVL. In our case, the patient remained symptomatic due to severe PVL of the IVC valve, which was attributed to suboptimal prosthesis positioning secondary to complex anatomy features. When percutaneous closure is considered, the stent frame of the prosthesis can be easily crossed with guiding catheters through which vascular plugs can be delivered to reduce the PVL.

In appropriately selected patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation, bicaval valve implantation can significantly improve cardiac output and reverse RV remodeling despite anatomic ventricularization of the right atrium [9, 10]. The safety and efficacy of the TricValve system were evaluated in the TRICUS EURO Study, which enrolled 35 patients meeting the following inclusion criteria: severe symptomatic tricuspid regurgitation, left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 40%, systolic pulmonary artery pressures ≤ 65 mmHg, vena caval v‐waves ≥ 25 mmHg on right heart catheterization, and a CT scan demonstrating anatomic suitability [11]. At 1 year follow‐up, over 95% of patients had a significant improvement in NYHA functional class and quality of life as assessed by the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire by ≥ 15 points [12]. Rehospitalization rate for heart failure was 29.5%. Moreover, at 1‐year echocardiographic follow‐up, 63.8% of patients had durable absence of systolic reversal in hepatic venous flow but three cases of PVL were observed with 1 significant. Ongoing research aims to optimize patient selection, device design, and procedural technique to minimize PVL and improve long‐term outcomes after TricValve implantation.

Conclusion

PVL is a recognized complication after bicaval TricValve implantation. Patients selected for transcatheter bicaval valve implantation are deemed high‐risk for surgery and, therefore, management of PVL is preferably percutaneously. A variety of vascular plugs are available to successfully treat PVL (Figures 1 and 2).
