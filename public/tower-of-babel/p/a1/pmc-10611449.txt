Investigating community-based care service factors delaying residential care home admission of community dwelling older adults and cost consequence
Authors: Yun-Hee Jeon, Judy M Simpson, Tracy Comans, Mirim Shin, Judith Fethney, Heather McKenzie, Tonia Crawford, Catherine Lang, Maria Inacio
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine factors contributing to delaying care home admission; and compare the rates of care home admission and cost consequence between two government subsidised programmes, Veterans’ Affairs Community Nursing (VCN) and Home Care Package (HCP).MethodsOur national, population-based retrospective cohort study and cost analysis used existing, de-identified veterans’ claims databases (2010–19) and the Registry of Senior Australians Historical Cohort (2010–17), plus aggregate programme expenditure data. This involved 21,636 VCN clients (20,980 aged 65–100 years), and an age- and sex-matched HCP cohort (N = 20,980).ResultsService factors associated with lower risk of care home admission in the VCN cohort were periodic (versus continuous) service delivery (HR 0.27 [95%CI, 0.24–0.31] for ≤18 months; HR 0.89 [95%CI, 0.84–0.95] for >18 months), and majority care delivered by registered nurses (versus personal care workers) (HR 0.86 [95%CI, 0.75–0.99] for ≤18 months; HR 0.91 [95%CI, 0.85–0.98] for >18 months). In the matched cohorts, the time to care home admission for VCN clients (median 28 months, IQR 14–42) was higher than for HCP clients (14, IQR 6–27). Within 5 years of service access, 57.6% (95%CI, 56.9–58.4) of HCP clients and 26.6% (95%CI, 26.0–27.2) of VCN clients had care home admission. The estimated cost saving for VCN recipients compared to HCP recipients over 5 years for relevant government providers was over A$1 billion.ConclusionsCompared to an HCP model, individuals receiving VCN services remained at home longer, with potentially significant cost savings. This new understanding suggests timely opportunity for many countries’ efforts to enhance community-based care services.

Introduction

Population ageing is a global phenomenon. Most older people prefer to remain in their homes for as long as possible, rather than being cared for in nursing homes or care homes (care home hereafter) [1]. The longer older people live in the community the higher their quality of life and lower the cost for governments because care home is usually more costly than community-based care services [2, 3]. Delaying or preventing care home admission has long been an important government policy goal for many developed countries [4–6].

A multitude of factors contribute to care home admission. One of the most common predictors of care home admission is individuals’ loss of functional independence, usually measured in terms of the ability to perform activities of daily living (e.g. bathing, toileting and eating, medication management, and household maintenance) [7]. Loss of functional independence is commonly a result of diseases of ageing, such as dementia, the leading predictor of care home admission, along with personal factors such as living alone and social limitations [8–10].

To remain in the community, older adults experiencing frailty, functional or cognitive decline or other health challenges typically require multiple types of assistance, including medical support, personal care, domestic and social support. One of the Australian government responses to the desire of older adults to stay at home has been the emphasis on support through community-based services. Examples are the Australian Government Department of Veterans’ Affairs Community Nursing (VCN) programme for veterans [11] and the Home Care Package programme (HCP) [12] for all older Australians. Similar to community-based services by the US Certified Home Health Agencies, [13] the aim of VCN and HCP is to maintain and enhance the physical wellbeing, quality of life and independence of clients through the delivery of clinical care, personal care, and/or domestic, social and other services through additional programmes (eBackground).

Multi-factorial community-based home support programmes that include individualised interventions (e.g. nursing care, home assistance, social assistance) and case management have been found to be the most effective interventions in delaying or preventing care home admission [5]. A recent US New York State cohort study [6] demonstrated the positive impact of a community-based programme in enabling clients to remain at home longer and delaying care home admission. Others have shown that receiving more community-based services is associated with a lower risk of care home admission [8, 14, 15]. However, little is known about service and staff related factors or comparative outcomes of government supported national community-based service models. The availability of VCN and HCP models in Australia that are separately funded and administered, with similar goals and objectives of delaying or preventing care home admission, presents an opportunity for comparing their outcomes.

This paper reports aspects of a larger study investigating protective factors of the VCN programme and other related factors that contribute to avoiding early care home admission (eBackground). The paper addresses three objectives to determine: (i) VCN programme and individual factors contributing to delaying or preventing care home admission; (ii) differences in the rates of care home admission and death at home between VCN clients and HCP clients, both overall and by dementia status and (iii) differences in the costs of the VCN and HCP programmes from the government funder perspective.

Methods

A retrospective population-based cohort study was conducted using existing, de-identified claims databases from Department of Veterans’ Affairs containing VCN, Veterans’ Home Care and other health care related service data, and the Registry of Senior Australians (ROSA) Historical Cohort [16] for comparison. Cost comparison was based on aggregate programme expenditure data (eMethods Data sources).

Study population

All clients who received their first VCN programme, from January 2010 to December 2014 were included in the study and followed till December 2019 giving at least five years’ follow-up per client (N = 21,636). The mean monitoring period was 43 months (median 36, Interquartile range [IQR] 14–68). For comparison with HCP clients, VCN clients under 65 and over 100 years were excluded, leaving 20,980 clients. HCP clients were chosen from the Historical Cohort (N = 99,701) using a random selection of 20,980 individuals to match the age and sex distribution of the VCN cohort, where age is at first VCN or HCP service (eMethods).

HCP clients were the most appropriate comparison group because this programme supports older Australians to stay home as long as possible. HCP is the usual pathway for those who need home care. If they have a veteran’s Gold/White card, they typically follow a VCN pathway, considering no co-payment and other advantages. As the project was commissioned by the government department, the project team consulted the department to understand the comparability of the two national services (eBackground).

Ethical approval for the VCN cohort study was granted by the Departments of Defence and Veterans’ Affairs Human Research Ethics Committee (No. 316–21). Approvals for the ROSA Historical Cohort analysis were obtained from the University of South Australia (ID 200489) and Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (EO2018/1/418).

Main outcomes and covariates of interest

Care home admission was the main outcome of interest, defined as entering a care home permanently, excluding temporary admission for respite care. Death at home or care home was a secondary outcome and includes those who died in hospital after an acute medical episode whilst residing at home or care home.

To investigate VCN programme factors delaying or preventing care home admission, we included age at accessing VCN, receipt of Veterans’ Home Care, staff type (nurses, care workers), care type (clinical, personal, both), service pattern (periodic versus continued) and care level (frequency of visit, Table S2). Individual factors examined were age at first VCN service, geographical area, dementia status, relationship type (veteran, partner of deceased veteran), medical burden, presence of carer and living alone. All variables compared between VCN and HCP were based on the same definitions (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to describe the study cohorts and crude outcomes. Survival analyses were used to examine VCN and individual factors associated with care home entry. A multivariable Cox model revealed non-proportional hazards for some variables, so separate models were fitted for the first 18 months (period 1) and after 18 months (period 2) (eMethods Data analysis), using a Fine-Grey model to adjust for the competing risk of death. Fine-Grey models were also used to estimate the cumulative incidence of care home admission adjusted for the competing risk of death, and the cumulative incidence of death at home adjusted for the competing risk of care home admission. Subhazard ratio (HR) estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported.

VCN and HCP outcome analyses were descriptive. In these matched cohorts, competing risk models were not adjusted for any covariates.

A cost analysis was conducted from the perspective of the government funder for the care of older people to compare the costs of the two cohorts of 20,980 people receiving VCN or HCP. A four-state model was used to apply the costs, excluding health costs such as hospital and primary care, where people were assigned to one of these states: living at home, living in residential care, died at home or died in care home according to the transitions observed in the study and including estimated time in care home. Sensitivity analyses were conducted on the cost of the VCN programme, the length of stay in residential care and the distribution of HCP types (eMethods Cost Comparison, Tables S3, S4 and S5, and Figure S1).

Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 and R through RStudio.

Outcome: Risk factors of care home admission

Amongst the VCN cohort, during follow-up 47% died at home and 31% had care home admission. Having a carer was associated with a lower risk of care home admission (HR 0.87 [95%CI, 0.77–0.97], period 1). Client factors associated with higher risk of care home admission were: being a Partner of a deceased veteran (compared to Veteran) (HR 1.47 [95%CI, 1.33–1.63], period 1; HR 1.32 [95%CI, 1.24–1.41], period 2); having a higher medical burden score of 5–7 (HR 1.51 [95%CI, 1.35–1.68], period 1) or ≥ 8 (HR 1.79 [95%CI, 1.58–2.03], period 1) (compared to 0–4); accessing the VCN service when older (HR 2.29 per 10 years [95%CI 2.08, 2.51], period 1; HR 1.92 [95%CI, 1.83–2.01], period 2); having dementia (HR 2.51 [95%CI, 2.28–2.77], period 1; HR 2.48 [95%CI, 2.33–2.64], period 2); and living alone (HR 1.19 [95%CI, 1.11–1.27], period 2 (Table 2).

Outcome: Time to care home admission and death

Compared to VCN clients, HCP clients had higher unadjusted cumulative incidence of care home admission (56% versus 26%), lower mortality at home (26% versus 41%), and higher overall mortality (63% versus 56%) over the follow-up period. Having dementia was associated with higher care home admission (74% versus 56%) and lower mortality at home (17% versus 26%). Median time to care home admission for VCN clients was 28 months (IQR, 14–42) compared to 14 (IQR, 6–27) in HCP clients (Table 4).

When adjusted for competing risks, at five years after service access, 58% of HCP clients (versus 27% VCN clients) had care home admission and 26% of HCP clients (versus 41% VCN) had died at home (Table 4). Median time to care home admission for the HCP cohort was about 39 months (versus over 60 for VCN) (Figure 1). A total of 76% of HCP clients with dementia (versus 56% without) and 50% of VCN clients with dementia (versus 21% without) had care home admission; 18% of HCP clients with dementia (versus 27% without) and 28% of VCN clients with dementia (versus 45% without) had died before care home admission. Cost comparison between VCN and HCP

The base case cost analysis estimated cost savings for the cohort receiving VCN as compared to HCP recipients is over A$1 billion, with the majority of savings occurring due to lower residential care costs. The cost saving per person was over $52,000. Sensitivity analysis showed that the results remain cost saving if the VCN programme cost is increased to the same as the HCP (eMethods Cost comparison).

Discussion

We explored a range of possible protective factors to elucidate why the VCN programme is associated with later care home admission. Similar to others, we found clients less likely to enter a care home were: veterans (mainly males), those who accessed the service at an earlier age, had no dementia and fewer medical conditions, had a carer and were not living alone [8–10, 19].

VCN programme factors associated with a lower risk of care home admission were: receiving periodic services, majority of care from skilled nursing workforce, clinical care or combined clinical and personal care (versus personal care only), a higher level of personal care and a lower level of clinical care, and receiving VCN without additional Veterans’ Home Care. VCN service delivery is based on clinical need and is not always continuously required (e.g. recovery from a surgical procedure, and/or maintenance of independence). Those who receive VCN continuously may therefore be those with greater need.

Overall, clinical care delivered by a skilled nursing workforce, combined with personal care where clinically required, may play a protective role against care home admission. Two Australian studies report receiving a combination of clinical and personal care services and a higher volume or more frequent use of community-based services delay care home admission [14, 20]. Systematic reviews suggest that community-based case management by nurses improves clinical outcomes and reduces health system costs through effective control of co-morbid symptoms and provision of tailored person-centred care [21, 22].

Comparison of time to care home admission between VCN and HCP draws attention to important indicators of service impact. HCP clients stayed at home for 14 months before care home admission or death at home, whilst VCN clients remained at home for 28 months before care home admission and 16 months before death. At five years after first access to HCP, 58% of HCP clients had care home admission and 26% had died before care home admission. In the same period, only 27% of VCN clients had care home admission and 41% had died at home. Estimated cost savings over 5 years for those accessing VCN services was over A$1 billion. Clients with dementia in both programmes were much more likely to enter care homes and did so earlier than clients without dementia. Importantly, VCN clients have a lower 5-year mortality than HCP clients but a higher incidence of death at home because they tend to remain at home longer.

There are multiple/intersecting factors contributing to our results, such as VCN clients receiving the majority of care from registered nurses and also having access to uncapped clinically necessary care services, with no user fees, indicating that timely provision by registered nurses of comprehensive planned care optimises potential for VCN clients to remain at home longer. Our qualitative arm of the study with VCN clients, providers and clinicians supports this, highlighting the importance of early comprehensive clinical assessment and regular review of care plans by registered nurses [23]. Timely comprehensive assessment can delay development of disability [24] through reducing barriers for those with complex social and medical needs to present for treatment. The use of comprehensive, clinical assessment, regular care planning reviews and case management are all features of community-based care services for older people that were effective in delaying care home admission [5].

Whilst HCP provides a similar range of services, with the same goal of enabling people to remain at home, the programme does not offer an initial comprehensive nursing assessment for care planning. HCP adopts consumer co-payment and is underpinned by consumer-directed care, where consumers decide which services they want to receive within their allocated level and budget (eBackground) [4, 12, 25]. The recent national inquiry of the care of older people in Australia described the system ‘Neglect’ where many older people had to wait for too long for the HCP eligibility assessment, approval and service, leading to premature care home admission or death before help became available [26]. Another Australian study of 178,924 HCP recipients (2003–2013) found that only a third of people received HCP within 30 days of assessment, 74% within 6 months and 26% after 6 months, concluding that the longer the wait time for HCP the higher the risk of mortality or transition to care home [24]. Such delay may help explain why the HCP cohort in our study had a five-times higher rate of care home admission than the VCN cohort in the first 12 months. In 2021, the wait time for HCP seemed to peak, with some older Australians waiting 34 months for a level 4 HCP [27].

Our study results provide timely evidence for other countries providing community-based services that aim to support older people to remain at home without having to rely on institutional care until they die. No research to date has compared time to care home admission of different national community-based service programmes. This new evidence is particularly timely in the Australian context. In response to the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety (2018–21), [27] the Australian Government is undertaking major reforms to the care sector for older people, with a new proposed care at home programme to be introduced in 2024 [28]. However, the plan released for public consultation lacked the remedies required to address timely and comprehensive assessment, care planning and care managed by skilled nurses.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of our study reported in this paper include its national representativeness of the cohorts studied, large sample size and reasonable recency. This is a population-based study of two nationally implemented programmes in Australia, making the findings generalisable of the Australian population receiving HCP (over 167,000 in 2021) [29] and VCN. The study’s large sample size also allowed for precise estimates of the cohort outcomes, increasing the confidence in the cost analysis. Additionally, multiple sensitivity analyses of key parameters did not find any scenarios where the VCN intervention was not cost saving (eMethods Cost comparison).

Our study relied on mostly provider payment claims data to ascertain clinical parameters about the individuals, therefore a validated measure of dependency level was not available and the specific care level distinctions could not be compared between HCP and VCN services. Additionally, because we analysed two age and sex matched cohorts side by side due to the restrictions in integrating these unique datasets, it is possible that other differences between the cohorts could also have influenced the results.

The cost analysis took the limited perspective of the government funder of care programmes for older people. This excluded other costs that may be high such as primary care, allied health and hospital services, and which may also differ between groups and have relevance for overall government funding. For example, recent Australian studies suggest hospitalisation is higher in HCP than care home [30, 31]. Such health service costs were unavailable, limiting our capacity to assess the impact more broadly. Costs were only available at the aggregate level and could not reflect heterogeneity in cost and outcomes for the cohorts.

The VCN cost was based on one year of data, which may not be representative of usual care. This cost was applied continuously for each year the client remained at home, which likely inflated the overall cost given VCN’s periodic service option. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that increasing the cost to equivalence with HCP (almost double the estimated cost of VCN) still led to a substantial cost saving for the programme due to delayed or no care home admission (eMethods Cost comparison). For care home costs, an average of the sector was used, noting that a more granular analysis may yield different results. The use of the median length of stay underestimates the true cost as the mean is higher at 29.9 months versus 19 [32].

Our study has highlighted the importance of further research in this area to measure more detailed cost consequences as well as the performance of varying community-based service models internationally.

Conclusions

VCN clients remain at home longer and with a lower overall incidence of care home admission than HCP clients, with estimated cost savings. This new understanding suggests timely opportunity for the Australian Government’s current community-based service reform agenda and other countries’ efforts to enhance community-based services. Timely support tailored to the person’s care needs, fully based on up-to-date comprehensive assessment and suitably trained lead health professionals such as registered nurses, should be the key features of community-based services for older people if the goal is enabling them to remain at home for as long as possible.
