Efficacy and safety of twice-daily accelerated hyperfractionated re-irradiation for thoracic malignancies☆
Authors: Victoria L. Doss, Cecilia F.P.M. de Sousa, Elaina Hales, Tinker Trent, Esi Hagan, Tsion Gebre, Anas Obaideen, Meti Negassa, Dezhi Liu, Chen Hu, Akila N. Viswanathan, Heng Li, K.Ranh Voong, Xun Jia, Todd McNutt, Rachel B. Ger, Russell K. Hales
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Balancing the demands for dose-escalated re-irradiation with toxicity to organs at risk (OARs) is a challenge. Twice-daily (BID) accelerated hyperfractionated re-irradiation (re-RT) is a potential solution, yet there is no guidance for its application for thoracic malignancies.Methods:All BID thoracic re-RT cases at our institution since 2014 were reviewed. Prior radiation courses were deformably registered. Summary plan dosimetrics were extracted using per-voxel cumulative EQD2 (α/β = 3 for normal tissues). Maximum toxicity following BID re-RT was graded per CTCAE v5.0 and assessed for relationship to radiation. Local failure (LF), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) were measured and compared between subgroups based on histology, re-RT dose, and metastatic status.Results:Sixty-four BID re-RT cases delivering at least 45 Gy in 30 fractions were reviewed. Fifty-eight patients (90.6 %) received a prior definitive course of radiation. The median first course EQD2 (α/β = 10) was 60.2 Gy. Median follow-up after re-RT was 14.2 months. Median OS was 18.5 months from completion of BID re-RT, and 59.0 % of patients had sustained local control at two years. Ten patients (15.6 %) experienced grade ≥3 toxicity. There were no grade 4 toxicities. There was one grade 5 event possibly attributable to re-RT. Of 24 OAR doses exceeding American Radium Society (ARS) and American College of Radiology (ACR) cumulative dose recommendations for re-RT, one case was associated with grade ≥3 toxicity.Conclusions:Accelerated hyperfractionated BID re-RT is safe and effective for thoracic re-RT, providing durable local control and favorable survival outcomes with acceptable toxicity.

Introduction

Re-irradiation (re-RT) is increasingly utilized for patients with cancer [1–3]. Advances in cancer care leading to improvements in survival have made re-RT for the intent of durable local control an important consideration. Re-RT to the thorax is increasingly used for local progression of primary thoracic malignancies, metachronous primary thoracic malignancies, and metastatic disease. However, balancing the demands for dose-escalated curative-intent thoracic re-RT with the potential toxicity of increased cumulative dose to OARs remains a challenge. Particularly within the thorax, cumulative dose to lung volume and central structures raises concern for increased toxicity and treatment-related mortality [4,5].

There is a scarcity of high-level evidence to guide the safety and efficacy of dose-escalated thoracic re-RT. Prior studies have been mostly limited to retrospective reviews with wide ranges of re-RT doses and techniques, including large proportions of 3D-conformal palliative plans [6,7]. A review of recent thoracic re-RT studies utilizing modern RT techniques to deliver radical-intent doses to central tumors found that increased dose at the time of re-RT was correlated with improved median locoregional control and improved median overall survival [8], yet with variable toxicity reporting, including up to 14 % treatment-related mortality [9].

An international expert survey recently published recommendations to guide consideration of thoracic re-RT patient selection and dose objectives [10]. Additionally, the American Radium Society (ARS) and American College of Radiology (ACR) recently released combined recommendations for thoracic re-RT composite dose constraints [11]. However, both groups ultimately recognized that the decision for thoracic re-RT is highly individualized, and there is a pressing need for more data to guide clinical decision-making [10,11].

Accelerated hyperfractionated RT delivered in a twice daily (BID) fashion is a potential solution to achieve dose-escalation while decreasing the risk of long-term toxicity [12]. This fractionation scheme leverages differences in the radiosensitivity of cancer compared to normal tissue. The hyperfractionated dosing enhances the therapeutic ratio, reducing late toxicity in slowly proliferating normal tissues. Meanwhile the accelerated BID schedule overcomes tumor repopulation [12]. Given favorable outcomes of BID re-RT in other disease sites [13,14], the radiobiologic advantages of this schedule may provide a solution for dose-escalated re-RT for thoracic malignancies while balancing toxicity. However, while there are reports that include small cohorts of patients who received BID re-RT [15,16], there are no dedicated reports focusing on accelerated hyperfractionated BID re-RT outcomes and toxicity for thoracic malignancies.

The purpose of this study is to characterize the survival, local failure, and toxicity outcomes for patients treated with hyperfractionated accelerated BID re-RT courses to the thorax.

Methods

Patients

We screened all patients treated with BID thoracic re-RT at Johns Hopkins University between 2014 and 2024. Re-RT was defined per ESTRO/EORTC re-irradiation guidelines, and those with type 1 and type 2 re-RT courses were included [17]. Type 1 overlap in this series was further defined as overlap of 95 % isodose line between the BID re-RT course and the 95 % isodose line of any prior course. Type 2 re-RT involved a repeat course of thoracic RT with concern for cumulative toxicity to OARs despite lack of direct overlap of irradiated volumes [17]. Included patients had primary thoracic malignancies or metastatic disease to the thorax treated with a radical-intent BID re-RT course of at least 45 Gy using 150 cGy per fraction. Patients were excluded if age <18 years or incomplete RT records. The decision for treating with radical-intent BID re-RT was at the discretion of the treating radiation oncologist and considered in settings where patients had no other sites of progressive disease and in which a once-daily course of re-RT would have resulted in composite doses to normal structures exceeding ARS/ACR re-RT guidelines. This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB00461902).

A REDCap database was created and integrated with Epic (Epic Systems, Wisconsin, USA) and Mosaiq (Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden) for automated data retrieval for patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment course data, and outcomes. Medical records were retrospectively reviewed by a physician in Radiation Oncology to confirm all automated data retrieval and to review all toxicity data. Cardiovascular, gastrointestinal (GI), respiratory, and neurologic toxicity occurring at any time point following initiation of the BID re-RT course was recorded and graded per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 [18]. Toxicity events were evaluated for relationship to RT using NCI guidelines for adverse event reporting attribution scores (unrelated, unlikely, possible, probably, definite) [19]. Events with an attribution score of possible, probable, and definite were included in the toxicity analysis.

Dosimetry

The DICOM records for all prior thoracic RT courses for each patient were imported into the RayStation treatment planning system (Raysearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) and deformably registered onto the most recent CT scan. A custom Python script converted dose per voxel to equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) using α/β = 3 for normal tissues and α/β = 10 for tumor. All EQD2 calculations for BID courses were based on dose per fraction. Radiation treatment courses were then summed together for a final composite EQD2 plan from which summary plan measures were extracted.

Summary plan dosimetric measures were compared to ARS/ACR 2021 consensus dose constraints for thoracic re-RT [11]. Planning target volume (PTV) location was recorded for spatial relationship to mediastinal structures.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using descriptive statistics with median and interquartile range (IQR) describing continuous variables with non-normal distributions.

Endpoints of overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), cancer-specific survival (CSS), and local failure (LF) were measured from the end of the BID re-RT course. OS was defined as the time from completion of BID re-RT to the time of death or last follow-up. LF was defined as progression of the re-irradiated tumor within the thorax. PFS was defined as any form of progression, including progression of the re-irradiated tumor, other intrathoracic progression (including contralateral lung disease and new mediastinal lymph node disease), and distant disease progression.

OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Subgroups based on primary lung histology, BID re-RT dose 45 Gy vs > 45 Gy, and metastatic status were compared with the log-rank test and linear combination of parameters and modeled using the Cox proportional hazards method. Cumulative incidence was used to estimate LF, and subgroups (primary lung histology, BID re-RT dose 45 Gy vs >45 Gy, and metastatic status) were compared with Gray’s test and modeled with the Fine-Gray method.

All statistical analyses were done using Python version 3.11.7, except for Gray’s test and Fine-Gray models, which were done with R Studio version 4.4.3.

Results

There were 64 cases of definitive-dose thoracic BID re-RT identified for analysis. Table 1 presents patient demographics and tumor characteristics. Six patients (9.4 %) were treated for a second de-novo primary thoracic tumor, including five new small cell lung cancer (SCLC) diagnoses and one new squamous cell carcinoma (SCC-NSCLC) diagnosis. The other 58 patients (90.6 %) received BID re-RT in the setting of progression of either their primary thoracic malignancy or for metastatic disease in the thorax. The median follow-up time from completion of BID re-RT course was 14.2 months (IQR 8.9 – 21.5 months).

The number of total thoracic RT courses per patient ranged from two to five courses. Most patients (78.1 %) underwent two total courses (the initial course and one BID re-RT course). The median first course EQD2 (α/β = 10) for all patients was 60.2 Gy (IQR: 48.8 – 66 Gy). Twelve patients, all of whom had SCLC as their primary histology, underwent a first course which was delivered as 45 Gy in 30 fractions BID. Among the 52 patients who did not receive 45 Gy in 30 fractions as a first course, the median first course EQD2 (α/β = 10) was 62.0 Gy (IQR: 60–66 Gy). Fifty-eight patients (90.6 %) received at least one prior course of thoracic RT with an EQD2 (α/β = 10) of at least 60 Gy or a first course with 45 Gy in 30 fractions BID.

All patients received a radical-intent BID re-RT course delivering at least 45 Gy with 150 cGy per fraction. Fifty patients (78.1 %) were treated with 45 Gy in 30 fractions for their BID re-RT courses, and 14 received greater than 45 Gy (maximum 60 Gy in 40 fractions) for their BID re-RT course. Fifty-four BID re-RT courses (84.3 %) represented type 1 re-RT with overlap of 95 % isodose lines. Sixty-two patients (96.9 %) had at least one RT course in which the PTV overlapped with mediastinal structures, and the remaining two patients had at least one RT course in which the PTV was within 2 cm of the proximal bronchial tree. Forty-five patients (70.3 %) received concurrent systemic chemotherapy during their BID re-RT course, and one patient received concurrent pembrolizumab.

Twenty-six patients (40.6 %) experienced failure of the re-irradiated tumor. For all patients, the cumulative incidence of LF was 26.5 % at 12 months and 41.0 % at 24 months (Fig. 1). Median local failure-free survival of the re-irradiated tumor was not reached.

Table 2 gives the cumulative incidence of LF at 12 months and 24 months for all patients and for each subgroup based on primary lung histology, BID re-RT dose, and metastatic status. SCC-NSCLC was significantly associated with increased risk of LF as compared to adenocarcinoma-NSCLC (subdistribution hazard ratio (HR) 2.94, 95 % confidence interval (CI): 1.16 – 7.45; p = 0.023) and trended toward significantly increased LF versus SCLC (HR 0.273, 95 % CI: 0.074 – 1.007, p = 0.051). There were no significant differences in the cumulative incidence of LF between those receiving 45 Gy vs > 45 Gy BID re-RT dose (p = 0.200), nor based on metastatic status at the time of BID re-RT (p = 0.424), although there was a qualitative improvement in local control with higher RT doses at re-RT. Supplemental Figs. 1 and 2 give the cumulative incidence of LF specific to patients with primary lung tumors and by primary lung histology.

For the overall cohort, 45 patients (70.3 %) experienced any form of progression. Median PFS from completion of BID re-RT was 8.0 months for all patients with PFS 32.9 % at 12 months and 14.4 % at 24 months (Fig. 2A). The median PFS for those with primary lung cancers was 7.0 months, and there were no significant differences in PFS between the different primary lung histologies. Additionally, there were no significant differences in PFS based on BID re-RT dose 45 Gy vs >45 Gy, nor based on metastatic status at the time of BID re-RT (Table 3). Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4 show PFS for patients with primary lung tumors and by primary lung histology.

Of the 45 patients who progressed, eight patients (17.8 %) had progression limited to the re-irradiated tumor only, ten patients (22.2 %) had progression limited to the thorax (re-irradiated tumor progression and/or other intrathoracic progression), 12 patients (26.7 %) had distant-only (extra-thoracic) progression, and 15 patients (33.3 %) had both intrathoracic and extra-thoracic progression.

For the entire cohort, the median OS from completion of BID re-RT was 18.5 months. OS was 69.3 % at 12 months and 36.6 % at 24 months (Fig. 2B). For those with primary lung histologies, the median OS from completion of BID re-RT was 17.9 months. As compared to adenocarcinoma-NSCLC, SCLC was associated with worse survival (HR 2.87, 95 % CI: 1.10 – 7.52; p = 0.031). There were no significant differences in OS based on BID re-RT dose nor based on metastatic status. Table 3 shows median, 12-month, and 24-month PFS and OS for all patients and for each subgroup. Supplemental Figs. 5 and 6 provide OS specific to patients with primary lung tumors and by primary lung histology.

For the entire cohort, the median CSS from completion of BID re-RT was 25.9 months. CSS was 77.6 % at 12 months and 49.7 % at 24 months. At the end of follow-up, 24 patients (37.5 %) remained alive. Of these, 15 patients exhibited no evidence of disease. In contrast, 40 patients were deceased by the end of the follow-up, of whom 28 had cancer as a direct cause of death with 14 deaths directly related to intrathoracic progression.

Fifty-one patients (80 %) experienced grade 1–2 toxicity, most commonly dysphagia (G1: 28.1 %, G2: 23.4 %) and esophagitis (G1: 3.1 %, G2: 21.9 %). Ten patients (15.6 %) experienced grade 3 toxicity, totaling 15 events: five cardiovascular, six GI, and four respiratory. All grade 1–3 toxicity events are given in Supplemental Table 1. There were no grade 4 events. One grade 5 event of esophageal perforation occurred 16.9 months after completion of concurrent carboplatin and paclitaxel with the BID re-RT course with a cumulative esophagus EQD2 (α/β = 3) Dmax of 99.9 Gy. This case involved progression of the re-irradiated tumor with invasion of the esophageal wall. The event was clinically reviewed as possibly attributable to re-RT. Of the ten patients with a grade 3–5 toxicity, eight received concurrent chemotherapy: seven received concurrent carboplatin and paclitaxel, and one received concurrent carboplatin and etoposide.

Fig. 3 shows the distribution of cumulative EQD2 doses (α/β = 3) for each relevant OAR compared against the ARS/ACR recommendations for thoracic re-RT. Relevant OARs were chosen based on location of irradiated volumes (i.e., brachial plexus only relevant for 19 cases). Of 24 instances of cumulative dose exceeding that of the ARS/ACR recommendations, only one case had a grade ≥ 3 toxicity event: a grade 3 esophageal obstruction in the setting of composite esophagus Dmax of 111.7 Gy.

Discussion

Our institutional series is the first to analyze the efficacy and safety of accelerated hyperfractionated BID re-RT to the thorax, and it is also one of the largest cohorts studying thoracic re-RT. Notably, all patients in our series were treated with BID re-RT with at least 45 Gy in 30 fractions, and 90.6 % of patients had received a prior definitive-intent thoracic RT course, highlighting the ability of this dosing scheme to deliver definitive-intent doses in the setting of prior high-dose courses. Our study suggests that BID re-RT can be an effective strategy for intrathoracic local control with more than half of patients (59.0 %) having sustained local control two years after re-RT and a median OS of 18.5 months. As clinicians increasingly encounter a broad range of situations where thoracic re-RT is considered, this cohort adds accelerated hyperfractionated BID re-RT as a promising means of tumor dose-escalation while achieving safe thoracic re-RT plans with acceptable toxicity despite high-risk clinical scenarios.

Our survival outcomes align with those of prior thoracic re-RT studies. A 2022 literature review by Grambozov et al. examined 11 studies of modern, high-dose re-RT techniques, reporting a median OS of 18.1 months (range 9.3 – 25.1 months) [8,9,15,20–24]. While our cohort included patients with metastatic disease to the thorax whose overall disease trajectories may differ from those with recurrent primary thoracic disease, our results of median OS of 17.9 months for patients with primary lung cancer remain comparable to prior studies. Notably, 25.0 % of patients in our study had SCLC as their primary lung histology which was associated with worse OS, consistent with the generally poor prognosis of SCLC as compared to non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [25]. Since many prior studies either excluded or had smaller proportions of patients with SCLC, our median OS is even more favorable if analyzed exclusively in patients with NSCLC (31.9 months for adenocarcinoma-NSCLC, 16.5 months for SCC-NSCLC). Additionally, 71.9 % of our patients received concurrent systemic therapy, which may contribute to improved survival outcomes, but also highlights the feasibility of BID re-RT as a radical-intent treatment approach.

Regarding local control, our cohort exceeded that of all but one of the previously reported conventionally fractionated re-RT studies where median locoregional control ranged from 6.5 to 18.0 months over a similar follow-up period [9,15,20–24]. A 2018 report of intensity modulated proton therapy (IMPT) for thoracic re-RT by Ho et al. that included 33 patients similarly reported a median freedom from locoregional failure which was not reached and a 12-month rate of freedom from locoregional failure of 61 % [26]. Highlighting the successes of BID re-RT in our patient population, 15 patients (23.4 %) were alive without evidence of disease by the end of follow-up. Amongst patients with disease progression, 26.7 % had extra-thoracic progression only and otherwise experienced local control of intrathoracic disease. This suggests that BID re-RT may offer a favorable strategy for tumor dose-escalation and intrathoracic control. Furthermore, our findings highlight the importance of achieving durable local control for patients undergoing thoracic re-RT across a variety of clinical scenarios. Even in a study cohort where 43.8 % of patients had metastatic disease at the time of BID re-RT and most received concurrent systemic therapy, intrathoracic disease progression was a significant contributor to cancer-specific mortality with 14 patients dying from intrathoracic disease progression. This underscores the mortality risk of uncontrolled intrathoracic disease and the need for still further improvements in strategies for safe dose-escalation. While higher BID re-RT doses had lower LF rates in our study, the difference was not statistically significant. However, our lack of difference in outcomes based on BID re-RT dose may be a reflection of the lack of power to detect such a difference and warrants further investigation.

Our toxicity outcomes also align with those of prior thoracic re-RT studies. Recent conventionally-fractionated IMRT/IMPT re-RT studies report grade 3–4 toxicity rates from 0 % to 39 %, and grade 5 event rates range from 0 % to 14 % [9,15,20–24,26]. Among the ten patients who experienced at least one grade 3–5 toxicity event, only one patient had a cumulative delivered dose that exceeded the recommendations of ARS/ACR [11]. Notably, one patient experienced two grade three GI events and then experienced a grade 5 esophageal perforation. This grade 5 event was in the setting of metastatic disease involving mediastinal lymph nodes with a first course of RT with 45 Gy in 15 fractions and a BID re-RT course with 45 Gy in 30 fractions, resulting in a cumulative EQD2 esophageal Dmax of 99.9 Gy, which approaches the ARS/ACR recommendation for < 100 Gy. The patient ultimately had intrathoracic disease progression despite re-RT and concern for tumor involvement of the esophagus with esophageal perforation 16.9 months after BID re-RT.

Importantly, there were 23 cumulative doses delivered that exceeded ARS/ACR recommendations which were not associated with a grade 3–5 toxicity. Overall, the majority of composite doses to various OARs met ARS/ACR thoracic re-RT recommendations, as shown in Fig. 3. This highlights the feasibility of dose-escalated BID thoracic re-RT for achieving safe plans that meet expert-recommended thoracic re-RT constraints and allows room to further increase re-RT doses. While expert guidelines provide critical reference points, these data suggest that exceeding re-RT OAR dose recommendations may be reasonable in carefully selected situations. Additionally, 71.9 % of patients in our series received concurrent systemic therapy with their BID re-RT course which can contribute to increased toxicity. Of the ten patients with a grade 3–5 toxicity, eight received concurrent chemotherapy. The relatively low rates of grade 3–5 toxicity for the overall cohort, despite most patients receiving concurrent systemic therapy, supports the safety of BID re-RT in radical-intent treatment situations. Ultimate decisions for thoracic re-RT dose objectives should be highly personalized based on each unique clinical situation. From our analysis, accelerated hyper-fractionated BID re-RT is a safe option with a low toxicity profile that should be considered.

Our analysis is limited by several factors inherent to retrospective studies. Our local control and toxicity were acceptable, but this may have been driven by patient selection. Specifically, patients were narrowly offered re-RT if they had progression limited to the thorax and the treatment plan was deemed medically reasonable. Second, patients were treated with heterogeneous dose-fractionation schemes for prior courses. While the heterogeneity of our study population may be considered a limitation when comparing to other studies primarily limited to NSCLC, it adds a broader perspective toward understanding outcomes and toxicity for all patients who may require thoracic re-RT. Additionally, our cohort was small in size, and generalizability needs to be taken with caution. While this report is large in relation to other thoracic re-RT studies, there remains a need for prospective studies to validate our findings and to explore sub-groups that will guide patient and treatment selection. Lastly, EQD2 doses for BID courses were calculated based on dose per fraction, which assumes complete repair between fractions and results in lower EQD2 as compared to using dose per day. This assumption of complete repair between fractions is imperfect, and the true biologic impact of BID dosing schemes likely varies between different tissues and may manifest in differences in acute versus late toxicity [12]. Additionally, the summative EQD2 model does not account for time between RT courses or other factors potentially impacting toxicity. Future studies investigating recovery between treatment courses, concurrent systemic therapy toxicity, and baseline organ dysfunction may assist in creating more personalized re-RT constraints and models to predict toxicity.

Conclusion

Our analysis supports accelerated hyperfractionated BID re-RT as a safe and effective approach for thoracic re-RT, providing durable local control and favorable survival outcomes while achieving acceptable toxicity rates across a variety of high-risk clinical scenarios.
