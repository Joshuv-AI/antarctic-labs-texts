Impact of Septoplasty on Right Ventricular Mechanics Assessed by Three-dimensional Echocardiography, Myocardial Performance Index, and Strain Analysis
Authors: Cem Korucu, Arif Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chronic nasal septum deviation (NSD) may cause persistent upper airway obstruction, intermittent hypoxia, and sympathetic overactivity, potentially leading to subtle alterations in right ventricular (RV) structure and function. Advanced echocardiographic techniques – including three-dimensional RV ejection fraction (3D-RVEF), myocardial performance index (Tei index), and RV longitudinal strain – enable detection of subclinical RV dysfunction associated with chronic hypoxic states and sleep-disordered breathing. Relief of airway obstruction may be associated with changes in cardiopulmonary physiology that could influence RV functional parameters. This study aimed to evaluate changes in RV mechanics following septoplasty using comprehensive conventional and advanced echocardiographic assessment.Methods:This prospective observational study included 30 consecutive adults undergoing elective septoplasty for symptomatic NSD. Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively. Conventional RV functional parameters – tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), RV fractional area change (FAC), and pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) – were measured together with advanced indices obtained from 3D echocardiography (3DE) and speckle-tracking analysis, including 3D-RVEF, RV myocardial performance index (Tei index), and RV free-wall longitudinal strain (FWLS). Paired statistical comparisons were conducted to assess postoperative changes in accordance with contemporary echocardiographic quantification standards.Results:Septoplasty was associated with significant improvement in both conventional and advanced RV functional parameters. TAPSE increased from 21.6 ± 2.7 mm to 23.4 ± 3.1 mm (P = 0.002), RV FAC increased from 43% ± 6% to 47% ± 7% (P = 0.008), and PASP decreased from 30 ± 6 mmHg to 25 ± 5 mmHg (P = 0.001). Advanced echocardiographic analysis demonstrated a significant rise in 3D-RVEF (48% ± 5% to 53% ± 6%, P < 0.001), a reduction in Tei index (0.46 ± 0.08–0.38 ± 0.07, P = 0.002), and improvement in RV FWLS (−19.8% ± 3.2% to −23.1% ± 3.6%, P < 0.001). Left ventricular systolic function remained unchanged, and no perioperative cardiovascular complications occurred.Conclusion:Septoplasty is associated with measurable improvement in RV mechanics assessed by 3DE, myocardial performance index, and strain imaging. Relief of chronic upper airway obstruction may therefore contribute to favorable subclinical cardiopulmonary remodeling beyond symptomatic nasal improvement. These findings highlight the value of advanced echocardiographic assessment in detecting reversible RV dysfunction and warrant confirmation in larger controlled prospective studies.

INTRODUCTION

Nasal septum deviation (NSD) represents one of the most prevalent causes of chronic upper airway obstruction in adults. Although frequently considered a localized otorhinolaryngological condition, persistent nasal airflow limitation may exert systemic physiological effects. Chronic upper airway obstruction has been associated with intermittent hypoxia, increased negative intrathoracic pressure swings, sleep fragmentation, and sustained sympathetic activation. These mechanisms resemble, to a lesser extent, those described in sleep-disordered breathing and obstructive sleep apnea, conditions known to influence cardiopulmonary hemodynamics and right ventricular (RV) performance.[12]

The right ventricle is uniquely sensitive to changes in pulmonary vascular resistance and afterload. Even mild elevations in pulmonary artery pressure or chronic hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction may lead to early alterations in RV geometry and contractile function before overt pulmonary hypertension becomes clinically apparent.[3] Subclinical RV dysfunction has prognostic significance across a wide spectrum of cardiopulmonary diseases, underscoring the importance of sensitive diagnostic modalities capable of detecting early changes.[4]

Conventional echocardiographic parameters such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and RV fractional area change (FAC) are widely used and recommended for routine assessment of RV systolic function.[3] However, due to the complex crescent-shaped geometry of the right ventricle, two-dimensional measurements may underestimate global performance and may fail to detect subtle myocardial impairment.

Three-dimensional echocardiography (3DE) allows direct volumetric quantification of RV volumes and ejection fraction without relying on geometric assumptions and is increasingly recognized as the reference echocardiographic method for global RV systolic assessment.[35] Speckle-tracking-derived RV longitudinal strain, particularly RV free-wall longitudinal strain (RV-FWLS), provides a sensitive measure of myocardial deformation and has demonstrated incremental value over conventional indices in various hypoxic and pulmonary vascular states.[6] Additionally, the myocardial performance index (Tei index), integrating systolic and diastolic time intervals, reflects global ventricular function and has been validated in both right and left ventricular assessment.[7]

While septoplasty is performed primarily to alleviate nasal obstruction symptoms, emerging evidence suggests that relief of chronic upper airway obstruction may improve pulmonary hemodynamics and exercise capacity.[8] However, data regarding its impact on comprehensive RV mechanics assessed by advanced imaging techniques remains limited.

Therefore, the present prospective study aimed to evaluate the effect of septoplasty on RV structure and function using a multimodal echocardiographic approach incorporating conventional parameters, 3D RV ejection fraction (3D-RVEF), myocardial performance index, and speckle-tracking-derived strain analysis.

METHODS

This prospective, single-center, observational cohort study was conducted at the Departments of Cardiology and Aesthetic, Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery between January 2025 and December 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval number: 2025/2) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was designed and reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology recommendations.

Consecutive adult patients aged ≥18 years undergoing elective septoplasty for symptomatic NSD were prospectively enrolled. Diagnosis was confirmed by anterior rhinoscopy and nasal endoscopy. To minimize confounding effects on RV performance, patients with known structural heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, chronic lung disease, obstructive sleep apnea requiring continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, significant valvular disease, chronic kidney disease stage ≥3, or inadequate acoustic window were excluded. Patients were clinically screened for symptoms suggestive of obstructive sleep apnea (such as excessive daytime sleepiness, loud snoring, or witnessed apneas). However, formal polysomnography was not systematically performed; therefore, undiagnosed mild sleep-disordered breathing cannot be completely excluded. Baseline demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, and medication use were recorded. Blood pressure and heart rate were measured under standardized resting conditions prior to imaging.

All septoplasty procedures were performed under general anesthesia using standardized surgical techniques without additional sinonasal interventions. A follow-up evaluation was conducted 3 months postoperatively to allow resolution of acute postoperative inflammatory changes and stabilization of respiratory mechanics.

Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed within 1 week prior to surgery and repeated at 3 months using a commercially available ultrasound system equipped with second-harmonic imaging, pulsed- and continuous-wave Doppler, tissue Doppler imaging, two-dimensional speckle-tracking software, and real-time 3D capability. Patients were examined in the left lateral decubitus position during quiet respiration, and all acquisitions were electrocardiographically gated. Care was taken to optimize image quality by adjusting depth, sector width, and gain settings to ensure clear delineation of endocardial borders. Frame rates for two-dimensional imaging were maintained between 60 and 90 frames/s to ensure adequate temporal resolution for deformation analysis.

All measurements were obtained according to current recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging for cardiac chamber quantification and right heart assessment.[34] An RV-focused apical four-chamber view was specifically acquired by lateral transducer angulation to maximize visualization of the RV free wall and avoid foreshortening. All datasets were digitally stored and analyzed offline by two experienced cardiologists who were blinded to clinical data and surgical timing.

Conventional RV systolic assessment included TAPSE measured by M-mode at the lateral tricuspid annulus, ensuring alignment of the cursor with the longitudinal motion of the annulus. RV FAC was calculated from RV-focused apical views by tracing the endocardial border at end-diastole and end-systole, excluding trabeculations and papillary muscles from the cavity area. Peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity was obtained using continuous-wave Doppler, and pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) was calculated using the modified Bernoulli equation, incorporating estimated right atrial pressure derived from inferior vena cava diameter and respiratory collapsibility. Each parameter was measured over three consecutive cardiac cycles and averaged to reduce beat-to-beat variability.

For 3D RV analysis, full-volume datasets were acquired from the apical window over four to six consecutive cardiac cycles during breath-hold to minimize respiratory motion and stitching artifacts. The entire RV cavity – including inflow tract, body, apex, and outflow tract – was encompassed within the pyramidal dataset. Dedicated vendor-specific software was used for semi-automated endocardial border detection. Endocardial contours were manually initialized at end-diastole and end-systole in multiple orthogonal planes, followed by automated surface reconstruction. Manual adjustments were performed when necessary to optimize border tracking and avoid inclusion of trabecular structures within the cavity. RV end-diastolic volume, end-systolic volume, and 3D-RVEF were calculated without geometric assumptions. Speckle-tracking analysis of RV-FWLS was performed from RV-focused apical four-chamber views. The endocardial border was manually traced at end-systole, and a region of interest was automatically generated and adjusted to match myocardial thickness. Adequate tracking quality was visually confirmed for each segment; segments with suboptimal tracking were excluded. RV-FWLS was calculated as the average peak systolic longitudinal strain of the basal, mid, and apical segments of the RV free wall, excluding the interventricular septum to avoid left ventricular influence. Measurements were averaged over three cardiac cycles.

RV myocardial performance index (Tei index) was derived from pulsed-wave Doppler recordings by measuring isovolumetric contraction time, isovolumetric relaxation time, and ejection time. These intervals were averaged over three consecutive cycles to enhance reproducibility. The calculation of the myocardial performance index (MPI) is summarized in Figure 3, demonstrating the echocardiographic time intervals employed for its determination.

Reproducibility analysis was performed in a randomly selected subset of ten patients. For intraobserver variability, the same observer repeated measurements 2 weeks after the initial analysis, blinded to previous results. For interobserver variability, a second independent observer performed blinded reanalysis. Intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated for 3D-RVEF and RV-FWLS to quantify measurement agreement.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Preoperative and postoperative values were compared using paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test as appropriate. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The primary endpoint of the study was the change in 3D-RVEF, while other echocardiographic parameters were considered secondary exploratory endpoints. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for key pre–postdifferences to improve interpretation of the magnitude of change. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d.

RESULTS

A total of 30 consecutive patients undergoing elective septoplasty were prospectively enrolled and completed both baseline and 3-month follow-up evaluations. The mean age of the study population was 34.8 ± 9.6 years, and 18 patients (60%) were male. Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Baseline cardiovascular risk factors were limited; mild hypertension was present in four patients (13%), and six patients (20%) were active smokers. All participants had preserved left ventricular systolic function at baseline (mean left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]: 62% ± 4%), and no patient demonstrated structural heart disease or significant valvular abnormality.

All patients underwent septoplasty without perioperative cardiovascular complications. No subject was lost to follow-up, and all echocardiographic datasets were of adequate quality for analysis. 3D and speckle-tracking analyses were feasible in 100% of cases.

Baseline conventional echocardiographic measurements demonstrated RV systolic parameters within normal ranges but toward the lower-to-mid spectrum of reference values. At 3 months postoperatively, statistically significant improvements were observed in multiple conventional RV indices. TAPSE increased from 21.6 ± 2.7 mm preoperatively to 23.4 ± 3.1 mm postoperatively (P = 0.002). RV FAC improved from 43% ± 6% to 47% ± 7% (P = 0.008). PASP decreased significantly from 30 ± 6 mmHg to 25 ± 5 mmHg (P = 0.001), indicating a reduction in estimated RV afterload following surgical correction of nasal obstruction.

3D echocardiographic analysis demonstrated a significant increase in global RV systolic performance. 3D RVEF improved from 48% ± 5% at baseline to 53% ± 6% at 3 months (P < 0.001). The absolute mean increase of 5% points corresponded to a large effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.90), supporting the clinical relevance of the observed change. End-diastolic and end-systolic RV volumes showed a modest reduction trend postoperatively, although these changes did not reach statistical significance.

Speckle-tracking strain analysis revealed a significant enhancement in RV myocardial deformation. RV FWLS improved from − 19.8% ± 3.2% preoperatively to − 23.1% ± 3.6% postoperatively (P < 0.001). The magnitude of strain improvement reflects enhanced longitudinal contractility and is consistent with improved ventricular–vascular coupling after relief of chronic airflow limitation.

RV myocardial performance index (Tei index) demonstrated a significant reduction from 0.46 ± 0.08 to 0.38 ± 0.07 (P = 0.002), indicating improvement in combined systolic and diastolic RV performance. The decrease in Tei index was consistent across the cohort and correlated moderately with the change in PASP (r = 0.42, P = 0.02), suggesting a relationship between improved pulmonary hemodynamics and global RV function. Reproducibility analysis demonstrated excellent intraobserver and interobserver agreement for primary advanced parameters. The intraclass correlation coefficient for 3D-RVEF was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.82–0.96) for intraobserver and 0.88 (95% CI: 0.76–0.94) for interobserver variability. For RV FWLS, ICC values were 0.89 (95% CI: 0.78–0.95) and 0.86 (95% CI: 0.72–0.93), respectively, confirming high measurement reliability.

Subgroup exploratory analysis according to smoking status and mild hypertension did not reveal statistically significant interaction effects on postoperative RV improvement, although the study was not powered for subgroup comparisons.

Overall, septoplasty was associated with consistent and statistically significant improvement across conventional, volumetric, and deformation-based RV functional parameters, accompanied by a reduction in estimated pulmonary artery pressure, while left ventricular function remained stable.

DISCUSSION

In this prospective study, septoplasty was associated with significant and consistent improvement in RV mechanics assessed by a comprehensive echocardiographic approach integrating conventional parameters, 3D volumetric analysis, myocardial performance index, and speckle-tracking-derived strain imaging. Importantly, these improvements were observed in a carefully selected population without overt cardiopulmonary disease, suggesting that chronic NSD and persistent upper airway obstruction may exert subclinical yet measurable effects on RV structure and function. The parallel improvement across multiple RV indices strengthens the internal consistency of the findings and supports the biological plausibility of reversible RV remodeling following relief of chronic airflow limitation.

The pathophysiological link between upper airway obstruction and RV function was not directly evaluated in this study. However, several mechanisms have been proposed in previous literature. Chronic nasal obstruction increases upper airway resistance and may generate repetitive episodes of mild hypoxia, particularly during sleep. Even in the absence of diagnosed obstructive sleep apnea, sustained airflow limitation may contribute to subtle oxygen desaturation, sympathetic activation, and increased intrathoracic pressure swings, mechanisms well described in sleep-related breathing disorders and their cardiovascular consequences.[12] Negative intrathoracic pressure enhances venous return and transiently increases RV preload, while hypoxia-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction elevates pulmonary vascular resistance and RV afterload.[9] The right ventricle, characterized by its thin free wall and high compliance, is highly sensitive to afterload alterations, as emphasized in contemporary right heart assessment guidelines.[3] Consequently, even modest and chronic increases in pulmonary vascular tone may lead to measurable changes in RV performance before clinically overt pulmonary hypertension develops.[9]

The reduction in PASP observed after septoplasty may suggest improved pulmonary hemodynamics; however, direct measurements of oxygen saturation, sleep-related breathing parameters, or pulmonary vascular resistance were not performed in this study, and therefore, mechanistic explanations remain speculative. Although baseline PASP values were within nonsevere ranges, the postoperative decline suggests that relief of chronic airflow limitation may attenuate hypoxia-driven pulmonary vasoconstriction. Similar improvements in pulmonary artery pressure and RV indices following septoplasty have been reported in previous prospective analyses.[1011] The concept of reversible cardiopulmonary remodeling after elimination of a chronic hypoxic stimulus is well established in conditions such as sleep-disordered breathing and pulmonary vascular disease.[129] Our findings extend this paradigm to isolated nasal obstruction.

3DE played a pivotal role in detecting global RV functional improvement. Because of the complex crescentic geometry of the right ventricle, two-dimensional measurements are inherently limited by geometric assumptions and plane dependency. Current recommendations for cardiac chamber quantification highlight the incremental value of 3D imaging for more accurate volumetric assessment.[34] The significant increase in 3D-RVEF following septoplasty suggests a true improvement in global contractile performance rather than isolated longitudinal motion changes. The magnitude of change, supported by a large effect size in power analysis, indicates that the observed differences are not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful.

Speckle-tracking–derived RV FWLS offered additional mechanistic insight. Longitudinal fibers dominate RV contraction, and strain imaging enables detection of subclinical myocardial impairment before conventional indices become abnormal. Consensus documents on deformation imaging underscore the importance of standardized strain analysis for RV assessment.[56] Furthermore, RV strain has demonstrated prognostic relevance in pulmonary hypertension and related cardiopulmonary disorders.[8] The improvement in RV-FWLS observed postoperatively, therefore, likely reflects enhanced myocardial deformation capacity secondary to reduced afterload and improved ventricular–vascular coupling.

The reduction in RV myocardial performance index (Tei index) further corroborates global functional improvement. The Tei index integrates systolic and diastolic time intervals and has been validated as a noninvasive marker of combined ventricular performance.[712] Improvement in this composite parameter suggests that septoplasty may favorably influence both contraction and relaxation dynamics of the right ventricle. Taken together, the convergence of volumetric, deformation-based, and time-interval indices provides a comprehensive demonstration of improved RV mechanics.

An important observation in this study is the absence of significant change in left ventricular systolic function. This finding supports the concept that the primary physiological impact of chronic nasal obstruction is mediated through the pulmonary circulation and right heart, rather than direct systemic myocardial involvement. The selective improvement in RV parameters strengthens the mechanistic plausibility linking airway resistance, pulmonary vascular tone, and RV load, consistent with current understanding of right heart–pulmonary circulation coupling.[39]

The clinical implications of these findings merit consideration. Subclinical RV dysfunction has been associated with adverse outcomes in various cardiopulmonary conditions, even when conventional parameters remain within normal limits.[8] Early detection and potential reversibility of RV impairment are therefore clinically relevant. Although septoplasty is performed primarily for symptomatic nasal relief, our results suggest that its benefits may extend beyond local symptom control to include measurable cardiopulmonary effects. Prior clinical investigations have similarly demonstrated improvements in pulmonary hemodynamics and RV parameters after septal correction,[1011] supporting the translational relevance of our findings.

Furthermore, the study highlights the incremental value of advanced echocardiographic techniques in detecting subtle changes in RV performance. Contemporary echocardiographic guidelines emphasize comprehensive right heart evaluation, particularly in conditions affecting pulmonary hemodynamics.[34] The present findings reinforce the importance of incorporating 3D imaging and strain analysis when investigating potentially reversible cardiopulmonary interactions.

Several factors may explain the observed magnitude of improvement. Relief of mechanical airflow resistance likely reduces nocturnal hypoxic exposure and sympathetic drive,[12] normalizes intrathoracic pressure dynamics, and decreases pulmonary vascular tone.[9] Over 3 months, these physiological adjustments may translate into improved ventricular–vascular coupling and enhanced myocardial efficiency, reflected by improvements in both 3D-RVEF and RV strain.

However, baseline RV function was largely within normal ranges, and therefore, the clinical relevance of these modest shifts in otherwise healthy individuals remains uncertain. Because patients with overt cardiopulmonary disease and obstructive sleep apnea requiring CPAP therapy were excluded, the study population represents a relatively healthy and highly selected cohort. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and may not be generalizable to patients with more advanced cardiopulmonary disease.

While the present study demonstrates significant postoperative improvement, it also raises important questions regarding the long-term sustainability of these changes and their clinical significance in broader populations. It remains to be determined whether similar improvements would be observed in patients with greater hypoxic burden or early pulmonary vascular disease, and whether earlier surgical intervention might mitigate progression toward overt RV dysfunction.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center observational study without a control group, limiting causal inference. Second, the study population was relatively small and highly selected. Third, direct measurements of oxygen saturation, sleep-related breathing parameters, nasal airflow resistance, or pulmonary vascular resistance were not performed, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Finally, the follow-up period was limited to 3 months, and longer-term studies are needed to determine whether the observed changes are sustained.

In summary, the present findings support the concept that chronic NSD may exert subclinical effects on RV performance through hypoxia-mediated pulmonary vascular mechanisms and altered intrathoracic dynamics. Surgical correction via septoplasty appears to reverse these changes, as demonstrated by consistent improvement across conventional, volumetric, deformation-based, and time-interval RV indices. The integration of advanced echocardiographic techniques, as recommended in contemporary imaging guidelines,[3456] was essential in revealing these reversible alterations and underscores the value of comprehensive RV assessment in conditions traditionally considered extracardiac.

CONCLUSION

In this prospective observational study, septoplasty was associated with significant improvement in RV mechanics as assessed by a comprehensive echocardiographic evaluation incorporating conventional indices, 3D volumetric analysis, myocardial performance index, and speckle-tracking-derived strain imaging. The consistent enhancement observed across multiple RV functional parameters, together with the reduction in estimated PASP, suggests that chronic NSD may exert subclinical yet measurable effects on RV performance through potentially reversible cardiopulmonary mechanisms.

Importantly, these changes were detected in a population without overt cardiopulmonary disease, highlighting the sensitivity and incremental value of advanced echocardiographic techniques in identifying early RV dysfunction. The absence of significant changes in left ventricular systolic function further supports the concept that the primary physiological impact of chronic upper airway obstruction is mediated through the pulmonary circulation and right heart.

These findings suggest that septoplasty may be associated with modest improvements in RV functional parameters, although the clinical significance and long-term implications of these changes require confirmation in larger controlled studies with longer follow-up.
