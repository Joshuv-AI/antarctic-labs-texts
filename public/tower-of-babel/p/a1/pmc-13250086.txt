Planar, spiral, and concentric traveling waves distinguish behavioral states in human memory
Authors: Anup Das, Erfan Zabeh, Bard Ermentrout, Joshua Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A fundamental challenge in neuroscience is explaining how widespread brain regions flexibly interact to support behaviors. We hypothesize that traveling waves of oscillations are a key mechanism of neural coordination, such that they propagate across the cortex in distinctive spatial patterns that control how different regions interact. To test this hypothesis, we used direct brain recordings from humans performing multiple memory experiments and an analytical framework that flexibly measures the propagation patterns of traveling waves. We found that traveling waves propagated along the cortex in not only plane waves, but also spirals, sources and sinks, and more complex patterns. The propagation patterns of traveling waves correlated with novel aspects of behavior, with specific spatial patterns reflecting particular cortical processes and even individual remembered items. Our findings show that large-scale cortical patterns of traveling waves reveal a diverse range of spatial patterns in the brain that are relevant for neural decoding.

Introduction

Neurons in the human cortex have rich dendritic arbors that integrate diverse inputs and axons that project outputs to multiple, distributed areas1. However, recent developments in brain imaging have suggested a dynamic interplay of distributed brain regions that underlie complex human behaviors2. How do individual neurons or regions reorganize their activity so that they are selectively and dynamically linked to certain other areas even as the structure of these neurons does not change on the timescale of behavior? We hypothesize that propagating patterns of brain oscillations, or “traveling waves,” underlie this selective reorganization. Traveling waves propagate across the cortex in specific directions during behavior, so that certain spatial arrangements of neurons are active at similar times, thus flexibly and efficiently communicating between the given brain regions that are relevant for a task3. Traveling waves are present at various frequencies,4 including the theta/alpha (~4–12 Hz) range in humans5. Because propagating oscillations correlate with underlying neuronal activity,6 the spatiotemporal organization of traveling waves at each moment may indicate which brain areas are active and how they correspond to particular computational processes and memory representations in the cortex7,8. The propagation of traveling waves may therefore show how the brain’s spatial organization and connectivity flexibly adapt to task demands and behaviors.

Traveling waves are known to play a critical role for behaviors such as visual processing and spatial navigation in rodents and non-human primates,9–13 and recently in human cognition5,14–17. Traveling waves also appear in spirals during sleep in humans18. However, our current understanding of traveling waves in cognition is largely focused on planar waves, where a consistent direction of wave propagation is maintained across a large region of cortex. It is possible that other types of traveling waves with more complex spatial patterns are also relevant functionally,18–21 so this focus on plane waves in humans may have limited our understanding of the role of traveling waves and oscillations in modulating cortical representations to support behavior.

We recently found that the direction of propagation by planar traveling waves during behavior correlated with distinct memory processes3. Extending this idea, we tested here the broader idea that the spatial patterning of wave propagation changed shape according to the current behavior. We designed a flexible analytical framework for measuring general patterns of traveling wave propagation and applied this procedure to direct brain recordings from neurosurgical patients performing multiple memory experiments. Our results showed an array of complex traveling wave propagation patterns including spirals, concentric waves (sources and sinks) (also known as target waves22–24), and heterogeneous directional propagation patterns that extended beyond those seen previously in humans.

Our findings show that the human cortex exhibits complex spatial patterns during memory processing, which are evident through the propagation of oscillations (i.e., traveling waves). By demonstrating rotational, concentric, and other traveling waves that were specific to particular functional states, our findings are consistent with predictions from computational models suggesting that traveling waves would exhibit individualized patterns based on different cognitive and computational processes, as well as variations in anatomical connectivity25,26.

Results

Theta/alpha and beta frequency oscillations are widespread and organized as spatiotemporally stable traveling waves

We sought to identify brain oscillations that were spatially organized into traveling waves and test whether they reorganize into different directional patterns to distinguish separate behaviors. To flexibly detect spatial patterns of traveling waves in each subject, we first identified the frequencies where groups of contiguous electrodes showed common oscillations (Methods, Fig. 1C–E). Identifying a single common frequency is crucial because, by definition, a traveling wave involves an oscillation at a single frequency that progressively propagates across a region of cortex, thus making it possible to detect the traveling wave when it passes by these electrodes27. Overall, oscillations were most often present in the theta (~4–8 Hz), alpha (~8–12 Hz), and beta (~ 12-30 Hz) frequency bands (Fig. 1C, and Supplementary Tables 1, 2), and ~86% of all electrodes showed a narrowband oscillation in at least one of these bands. Interestingly, sometimes the same ECoG grid showed multiple peaks in the power spectrum, i.e., they had peaks at both the theta/alpha and beta frequency bands (Supplementary Tables 1, 2, also see Fig. 8), which suggests the presence of multiple overlapping oscillatory networks.

We next distinguished the patterns of oscillations that were traveling waves. Because a traveling wave involves an oscillation at a single frequency that is present simultaneously on multiple electrodes, we identified the contiguous clusters of electrodes with oscillations at the same frequency. A traveling wave requires the presence of a systematic propagation of phase across a cluster of electrodes. To test for this pattern, we filtered the signals of the electrodes in each cluster at their peak frequencies and extracted the instantaneous phase at each electrode and timepoint using the Hilbert transform (Fig. 1F). Visual observation of the propagation of the absolute phase (Hilbert-transformed phase) across time showed clear traveling waves, with phase advancing in consistent spatial patterns across neighboring electrodes (see Fig. 1H, for example).

Next, to probe this phenomenon systematically, we implemented a quantitative framework for measuring the spatial pattern of each traveling wave. Here, for each electrode in a cluster, we used a windowed circular–linear regression approach to identify the relative phases that exhibit a linear relationship with electrode locations locally27 (Methods). This regression also measured the properties of each wave’s propagation, including its phase velocity and direction at each timepoint (Fig. 1H, Methods).

Recent evidence has shown that oscillations and traveling waves in humans often appear in transient bursts, but that they nonetheless are functionally relevant28–33. We thus designed a single-trial method that identified stable periods of wave propagation (Methods, Fig. 1G) and used this procedure to identify individual epochs where waves progressed across the cortex in a consistent spatial arrangement (Methods, Fig. 1G, H, and Supplementary Videos 1-7). This procedure revealed that the stable periods of individual traveling waves lasted ~80–180 msec on average (Fig. 1G, H). These wave epochs were statistically robust (all p’s < 0.001, Methods, Fig. 1I, J, and Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables 3, 4) ruling out the possibility that these stable epochs could arise due to chance or noise, and their period of stability was shorter for oscillations at high frequencies than low (median 107 versus 134 msec, p = 0.002, Mann-Whitney U-test, Supplementary Table 5). Consistent with earlier work,5,17,27,34 the propagation speed of these traveling waves was ~0.3–3.3 m/s, and faster for oscillations at higher temporal frequencies than low (median 1.1 m/s for 12-26 Hz versus 0.5 m/s at 5-12 Hz, p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U-test;5).

Independent component analysis (ICA) reveals the most dominant traveling wave patterns

Using this approach, we observed a diverse range of robust traveling wave patterns across subjects (Figs. 2, 3). These patterns included plane waves as seen previously, but also spirals, concentric waves, and spatially heterogeneous, complex waves (for examples, see Supplementary Figs. 9–18). We also saw that, over time, the traveling waves at individual electrodes often shifted between different patterns. To quantitatively distinguish the full diversity of spatial wave patterns over time, we used an algorithm based on independent component analysis (ICA)35,36 to identify the range of spatial patterns of traveling waves that appeared on each electrode cluster (see Methods). Using this algorithm, we labeled each category of spatial wave pattern that appeared on each cluster, which we refer to as a “mode” (Fig. 2, Methods). We classified each mode according to its shape, including plane, spiral, and concentric waves, as well as other waves with complex shapes (Fig. 3). This algorithm provided a series of weights that quantify the contribution of each mode to the epoch’s current shape. We call these weights the epoch’s “activation function”, and they quantify the instantaneous magnitude and direction of a mode for each epoch. In addition to applying our methods on the experimental data, we also carried out extensive simulations to statistically validate our proposed ICA approach to confirm that it was working as expected in terms of sensitivity and specificity for extracting true traveling wave patterns and demonstrating negative results in the absence of such patterns. In particular, we carried out four different types of simulations, which validate our approach (see Methods for details) and identify robustness against noise, the ability of ICA to extract multiple types of traveling waves, and show negative results in the absence of true traveling waves (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5).

This ICA algorithm effectively quantified the spatial wave patterns that appeared visually in the recordings, as the dominant wave patterns that were evident from visual inspection generally corresponded with the modes with the strongest weights from ICA. For example, Fig. 2D shows three different wave patterns seen in the raw data, which match the corresponding activation functions for the top 6 modes shown in Fig. 2Ei, and the corresponding modes shown in Fig. 2Eii (also see Supplementary Figs. 7, 8).

Using this ICA procedure to identify the modes that were present on each cluster throughout the task, we identified a wide range of spatial traveling wave patterns across all clusters (Methods, Fig. 2, and Supplementary Figs. 9–18). Individual clusters showed a mean of 6 significant modes, indicating that there were generally multiple spatial patterns of traveling waves within individual clusters. On average, the first three modes explained ~34%, ~19%, and ~10% variance respectively, and the first six modes combined explained >80% variance (Fig. 4E). Diverse spatial patterns of traveling waves and their characteristics

To explain the diversity of the spatial wave patterns in the data, we classified each mode based on its shape into one of the following categories: “planar”, “rotational”, “concentric” (“expanding” or “contracting”), or “complex” (Methods, Fig. 3)37. Complex waves were those that showed a robust spatial propagation pattern (i.e., p’s < 0.001) but did not meet the criteria for the other categories. Some complex waves showed a combination of multiple patterns, such as separate subsets of electrodes showed planar, rotating, or expanding/contracting waves (Fig. 9, Supplementary Figs. 9-18).

One previous study showed rotational and expanding traveling waves during human sleep spindles, supporting the idea that these types of waves may support memory consolidation and plasticity18. Based on this, we hypothesized that rotational and expanding (sources) traveling waves would be more prevalent in the spatial episodic memory task which involves processing a broad range of inputs including spatial and non-spatial information.

The spatial organization of traveling waves varied across regions and temporal frequencies. For clusters with oscillations at low temporal frequencies, plane waves were most dominant (median wave strength 59% across tasks), and this pattern was present in both the spatial (χ2(3) = 165.0, p < 0.001, Fig. 4A) and verbal (χ2(3) = 288.7, p < 0.001, Fig. 4C) memory tasks. At higher frequencies, although plane waves were again most dominant overall (median wave strength 43% across tasks), there was an increased prevalence of rotational waves (median wave strength 37% across tasks), especially in the spatial memory task (χ2(3) = 162.3, p < 0.001, Fig. 4A). When we compared the strength of these different traveling wave patterns as indexed by variance explained (see Methods), we found that planar waves were strongest, followed by rotating and expanding/contracting waves (Fig. 4E, F). Complex waves, although highly statistically significant and strongly relevant to behavior (see below), were less strong and explained less variance in the raw data compared to the other wave types.

We also compared these effects between hemispheres, in light of work on lateralized oscillations in hippocampus and neocortex27,38. Although plane waves were dominant in both hemispheres, they were stronger on the right hemisphere (~64%) than the left (~42%; χ2 test p < 0.01, Fig. 4 B, D). We next compared this lateralization between the two different memory tasks. In the left hemisphere, during the spatial memory task wave patterns were preferentially rotational rather than concentric, (χ2(3) = 150.0, p < 0.001, Fig. 4B). Conversely, concentric wave patterns were more common in the verbal memory task (χ2(3) = 288.1, p < 0.001, Fig. 4D). But, in the right hemisphere, we found statistically similar patterns across both tasks. This suggests that the shape of traveling waves across the cortex during behavior reveals specific task-related neural assemblies that are lateralized across the cortex.

Given that we saw both counter-clockwise and clockwise rotational wave patterns, we examined whether the orientation of wave propagation was functionally relevant. One previous study showed that during sleep, human cortical spindles are rotational traveling waves that propagated in a temporal→parietal→frontal (TPF) direction18. Therefore, we examined whether rotating waves in our dataset had a directional preference by labeling each one’s direction (TPF or TFP). If there were multiple rotational modes in a single oscillation cluster, then we labelled each separately. We did not find a significant preference for a specific rotational direction in either task. In the spatial memory task, there were 7 TPF waves versus 9 TFP waves (p > 0.05, binomial test) and, similarly, in the verbal memory task (12 TPF versus 15 TFP, p > 0.05, binomial test; Supplementary Table 6).

We also compared the propagation of concentric traveling waves, comparing the prevalence of inward (sink) versus outward (source) propagation. In the spatial task, there were more sources compared to sinks, as 78% (7 of 9) concentric waves were sources. Inversely, in the verbal memory task, there were more sinks than sources, as 68% (15 of 22) concentric waves were sinks (Supplementary Table 6). Thus, the brain shows different types of concentric waves (χ2 test, p < 0.02) between spatial and verbal memory.

Traveling waves can distinguish both broad and specific behavioral states of human memory representations

Next, to identify the potential functional role of traveling waves, we examined how the prevalence of traveling waves with different shapes shifted between stages of memory. First, we examined encoding, retrieval, navigation, etc. in the spatial memory task. To assess statistical significance, we used multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) (Methods) to test for changes in activation functions between conditions.

Traveling waves showed different propagation patterns between stages of memory processing (Fig. 5, Supplementary Figs. 9–18, Supplementary Videos 1–4). As an example, Fig. 5A–C show a cluster of electrodes that showed three distinct modes of traveling waves that changed their propagation direction between the stages of memory. In this cluster, the plane wave pattern (mode 1) was nearly absent during navigation and distractor stages (as indicated by its low magnitude) but was strongly present in other task phases. However, its direction of propagation shifted between behaviors, with a different direction for retrieval compared to the encoding, confidence, and feedback periods. Similarly, this cluster’s mode 2 also differed in propagation direction between memory stages and had the highest wave strength during retrieval and feedback. We also examined whether the directions and strength of traveling waves shifted to represent the specific item that each subject was viewing, extending item-specific gamma oscillations seen previously39. To decode each subject’s behavior from their spatial pattern of traveling waves, we trained multilayer neural networks, with cross-validation, for classifying pairwise behavioral states. We found that behavioral states can be decoded at the individual cluster level and also at the group level (Fig. 10C, p < 0.001, one-sided sign test), for the spatial memory task. Interestingly, we found that some behavioral states can be more easily decoded than others (Fig. 10C, F). For example, Fig. 10C shows that for cluster 7, the spatial wave patterns for the encoding and distractor periods were distinct from each other and hence were more easily distinguishable compared to those from the confidence and feedback periods. Overall, the navigation and distractor states were generally decodable from other states, but not from each other, which indicates that these behaviors generally exhibit similar traveling wave patterns (Fig. 10F, G).

Similarly, we found that the identity of the specific memory item that a subject was viewing can be decoded from the shape of their traveling waves in the verbal memory task, and also at the group level (Supplementary Fig. 20A, p < 0.001, one-sided sign tests across clusters versus chance). Overall, we often found that letter “H” was the most decodable, followed by the letters “J” and “Q”, compared to the other letters (Supplementary Figs. 20D, E). These results mean that letters “H”, “J”, and “Q” often elicited distinctive patterns of traveling waves39 Visually, behavioral states that showed lower decoding accuracy had more similar wave patterns compared to the ones that had higher decoding accuracy (Fig. 10C, and Supplementary Fig. 20A), thus the results of this network decoding analysis were closely aligned with the results from the MANOVA analysis (Figs. 5, 6). This similarity between the results of these analyses was robust, as we found mostly positive correlations between the neural network decoding accuracy and the dissimilarity of traveling wave patterns (calculated as the mean Euclidean distance between the ICA weights for the given pair of behavioral states) (Fig. 10D, E, and Supplementary Figs. 20B, C).

Discussion

We used direct human brain recordings to investigate the directional propagation of traveling waves and probe their link to behavior. We found that diverse patterns of traveling waves, including rotational, concentric, and complex wave patterns, changed their shapes, direction, and strength to distinguish various behaviors. These results support the notion that the human brain exhibits complex spatiotemporal patterns of traveling waves that reflect complex cortical processes including memory and specific brain states. Notably because task-related traveling waves often show complex spatial patterns, traveling waves may therefore show how the brain supports new types of functionally relevant spatial organizations, extending beyond currently known anatomical and functional hierarchies40,41. An important area of future work will be to understand the functional relevance of the spatial pattern of traveling waves, to explain how the shape of wave propagation maps on to specific neurocomputational mechanisms29.

Roles for rotational and concentric traveling waves for human memory processing

Traveling waves have previously been seen in rodents and non-human primates,11–13,42–51 including complex patterns of traveling waves37,52–55. Rotational waves play a critical role in the prefrontal cortex of non-human primates during working memory tasks, demonstrating a dynamic modulation of wave orientation that aligns with task intervals19. In rodents, cortex-wide spiral waves not only exhibit mirrored patterns between hemispheres but also between sensory and motor cortices, reflecting the structural and functional symmetry of long-range axonal projections56. In human sleep there are also rotating waves, suggesting that repeating activations in a spiral pattern could help enhance synaptic plasticity for memory consolidation18. Here we found that, in addition to planar waves, during memory tasks the human cortex shows rotational and concentric wave patterns as well as complex spatial patterns. The diverse propagation patterns of these cognition-related traveling waves is consistent with a rich set of models, suggesting a role for spatially organized neural assemblies and oscillations in the computational processes underlying memory, cognition, and other behaviors7,8,47,57.

An especially distinctive aspect of our results concerns concentric source and sink waves. A previous study showed that source waves were common than sinks during sleep spindles in humans, indicating that sources putatively support complex cortical processes such as memory consolidation and synaptic plasticity18. Because they involve waves propagating outward from a single location, we hypothesize that the source waves we observed indicate the presence of a small local group of neuronal assemblies that dominate information flow by routing their information outward from one local area in a direction towards widespread brain areas. Given that we found an especially large prevalence of source waves in our spatial memory task, it suggests that spatial cognition involves directed information flow propagating outward from local nodes. Spatial episodic memory (navigating or recalling spatial layouts) requires broad integration of rich sensory information across multiple cortical and subcortical areas, which might favor more sources of waves since these sources involve widespread initiation of oscillatory activity to broadcast information outward across multiple networks. In comparison to the spatial task, the Sternberg verbal working memory task, which involves maintaining a few items on each trial that each come from a relatively small dictionary of possible items, primarily recruits fronto-temporal circuits rather than the larger-scale brain networks that are important for richer spatial memories. Therefore, the verbal memory task might rely more on focused convergence of waves onto sinks for more localized cortical processing. In the verbal memory task, inhibitory control is stronger to limit irrelevant spread; thus, in this context our results suggest that the oscillations we observed are relevant because they suggest that neural activity converges onto fewer stable sinks during encoding to help maintain fixed items in buffers. Our findings and these predictions regarding the functional role of traveling waves and spatial oscillation patterns in memory are, of course, predictions based on our empirical findings, but they provide useful hypothesis for computational modeling that may probe the importance of these source nodes for cortical processing and spatial cognition.

Muller et al., 2016 also found the presence of rotational waves during sleep spindles. Rotational waves such as spirals revisit the same brain areas in multiple cycles, therefore sequentially scanning distributed brain areas to help enhance synaptic plasticity for memory consolidation. We observed a higher number of rotational waves during the spatial memory task compared to the verbal memory task. Spatial episodic memory requires bidirectional loops between the cortical areas and the subcortical areas. Rotational waves naturally emerge in recurrent loop topologies where excitatory signals re-enter the system with delays. These spirals can integrate multimodal spatial signals such as landmarks, trajectories, and contexts into coherent phase patterns, therefore providing a temporal scaffold for multiplexing different feature streams around the spiral core and supporting flexible scene construction and navigation. These features might be less critical for short term verbal working memory which is more maintenance-oriented, therefore might favor more simple, stable oscillatory attractors such as planar waves with minimal dynamical complexity.

Possible mechanisms for complex patterns of traveling waves

Our findings converge well with theoretical predictions from biologically plausible neural models based on weakly coupled oscillators25,26. These models hypothesize that complex patterns of waves can be generated locally based on the initial spatial activation of neurons, where each neuron is connected to a few of its neighbors, with distance dependent axonal delays in the order of conduction along unmyelinated horizontal fibers4,10,58. These locally generated waves can propagate across widespread regions between locally connected neurons and interact with other locally generated waves, to generate complex patterns of propagating oscillations59–65.

In addition to the coupling functions, a key determinant of the shape of these wave patterns is the presence of local shifts in the frequency of local oscillations. In locally coupled oscillator networks, waves tend to propagate away from the cortical locations with the fastest intrinsic oscillation frequencies, following a gradient to the locations with the slowest oscillations66. Thus, local activations of strong or fast oscillations can have a strong influence on the global topography of these traveling waves25,26,61. Using this model, based on the spatial location of the locally generated wave sources and their relative frequencies, a wide range of stable, regular (non-random) wave patterns can be generated66. For example, a linear spatial gradient in frequencies, such as relatively higher frequency on the left side of the ECoG array compared to the right side, can lead to a planar traveling wave going from the left to right on the ECoG array67. Therefore, a presence of a planar traveling wave for a specific English letter would imply this frequency heterogeneity in the left of the ECoG array compared to the right side represents that specific English letter. Similarly, bullseyes or target waves can also be generated by frequency gradients using nearest-neighbor coupled oscillators68. For example, a source wave in an ECoG array can be generated when the frequency at the center of the array is relatively higher compared to all other places of the ECoG array, constituting a wave diverging away from the center of the array. Therefore, a presence of a source wave for specific English letter indicates relatively higher frequency at the center of the ECoG array responsible for cognitive representation for that specific letter. Similarly, a sink wave in an ECoG array can be generated when the frequency at the center of the array is lower compared to all other places of the ECoG array, constituting a wave converging to the center of the array. Therefore, a relatively lower frequency at the center of the array for a specific letter would indicate that such frequency lowering is causally responsible for cognitively representing that specific letter. Recently, in a new work, we have found the effect of the frequency heterogeneities on generating these planar and concentric traveling waves and when these waves are combined with rotational traveling waves, can produce a wide range of complex traveling waves69. More broadly, our results of distinctive spatial wave patterns for separate memory items suggest that there are spatial patterns of excitation across the cortex for separate memory items during cognition, similar to those seen in the hippocampus70.

Similarly, the presence of spiral waves is also informative about the spatial arrangement of the cortical pattern that supports distinct behavioral states. A surprising aspect of our findings is the large degree of heterogeneity we found in the directions and strengths of the task-related traveling waves, across oscillation clusters, brain regions, and subjects. This degree of variability is in line with recent advances in structural MRI studies, which have shown that inter-individual variability can have a major impact on human behavior72,73. This type of variability has also been shown in functional MRI studies, which found that inter-individual variability in the functional connectivity of task-related activations correlates with the underlying inter-individual variability in anatomical connectivity74. In the same way that individual differences in structural patterns drive large-scale differences in fMRI signals, an interesting area of future work will be to assess the degree to which broad inter-subject differences in traveling waves may be driven by small anatomical differences across individuals. Beyond anatomical differences, given the models described above, the diverse wave patterns may also be explained by individual differences in local oscillation frequency and amplitude. Thus, examining interindividual differences in oscillation frequencies is likely to be an important area of future work to explain the diverse patterns of traveling waves.

It is notable that the traveling waves that we detected often traveled through the sulci and gyri, and even across the Sylvian fissure, across distributed brain areas. For example, for mode 3 in Fig. 6A, the traveling wave appeared to propagate through the Sylvian fissure. Recent work on computational modeling of neural field theory of brain waves8,10,75,76 has shown that stable electric fields are capable of carrying information through sulci and gyri, thus providing a mechanism for ephaptic coupling of multiple, distributed brain areas. The traveling waves that we observed could putatively be a manifestation of these stable electric fields and their interactions with the sulci and gyri, which allows for ephaptic coupling between distributed brain areas4. An additional way that traveling waves could effectively propagate through sulci is by generation in subcortical areas, such as thalamus, as demonstrated by recent computational modeling work25 and experimental data in rodents56. Subcortical generation of traveling waves also hints at the possibility of three-dimensional traveling waves coordinating neural activity between the cortical and subcortical brain areas, rather than waves propagating only along the cortical surface. Recent fMRI studies using a new type of wave mode namely the geometric eigenmode have demonstrated that three dimensional thalamocortical traveling waves play a critical role in many higher order brain functions77,78. Denser sampling of depth electrodes in three-dimensions may help to better understand the full complexity of the generation and propagation of traveling waves across different shapes of cortex, perhaps answering the question about how waves propagate across sulci.

Behavioral relevance of diverse patterns of traveling waves

In general, our findings support the overall notion that the spatial organization of activity across the brain shifts with behavior, given that we found that the spatial patterning of various traveling wave patterns varied in relation to behavior. In the established hierarchy, neural activity associated with sensory processes flows “forward” towards other cortical areas, while that related to higher-order cortical processes such as memory retrieval feeds “backward” from frontal cortical areas to coordinate and reinstate neural activity in other brain regions79–81. Our earlier study3 and others14 showed a role for these forward and backward patterns, which were planar traveling waves. However, going further, our present results show an additional new hierarchy of traveling waves, by showing more complex spatial patterns such as spiral waves, source and sink waves, and more complex wave patterns, which appear to complement the feedforward-feedback cortical hierarchy. Our current work converges with a large body of recent work on traveling waves, which found that complex patterns of waves in rodents and non-human primates19,37,52,53,82 as well as in humans,18 play a critical role in cognition. Our results are also consistent with a recent fMRI study83 that found a critical role for spiral waves in the cortex during language and arithmetic tasks. Since traveling waves are known to be closely associated with spiking activity of neurons,42 their propagation may reflect packets of neuronal activity sequentially scanning distributed brain areas to transiently reorganize functional connectivity between them, to represent complex behaviors in human memory processing84,85. In this context, it suggests that planar waves may be relevant for globally routing neural spiking activity, by directionally propagating the activity to different cortical areas in a feedforward-feedback manner. In contrast, because rotational waves revisit the same brain areas in multiple cycles, it suggests that these waves may be relevant for dynamically strengthening functional connectivity between large-scale neuronal assemblies for efficient memory processing, similar to the rotational waves observed during sleep spindles18. Future studies with simultaneous single neuron and intracranial EEG recordings from neurosurgical patients performing spatial and verbal memory tasks are needed to directly examine the functional relevance of the spike-wave coupling. This will reveal how the complex spatial patterns of traveling waves modulate and are modulated by the spiking activity of the neurons.

The more complex, heterogenous patterns of waves that we observed putatively route information in more flexible ways by propagating in several directions to rapidly reorganize functional connectivity between neuronal assemblies, to distinguish both broad and specific behavioral states. Recent imaging studies in rodents86 as well as humans87 support this hypothesis by showing that the brain can show dynamic and complex patterns of functional connectivity related to various behaviors that is seemingly independent of underlying anatomical connectivity. Our findings of traveling waves that differentiate behavioral states are consistent with this proposal, by showing that complex spatial patterns underlie cortical processes supporting diverse computational processes during cognition. Thus, traveling waves may reveal new aspects of the brain’s spatial organization that were missed by previous researchers using simpler analytical approaches.

Going forward, it would be useful to map out in more detail the specific cognitive, sensory, and motor processes that underlie different types of traveling waves (e.g.,88). In new studies, this may be possible by measuring traveling waves with richer stimulus sets and in multiple different tasks, including measuring the neural activity associated with the same stimuli in memory and non-memory tasks. It would be important to identify the aspects of individual traveling waves that are related to individual stimuli versus those related to broader memory and computational states. Also, distinguishing traveling waves related to motion, perspective, environment, etc. using advanced methods such as eye-tracking is an important future area of research. It will also be interesting to see the effect of motion, environment, etc. on different task periods such encoding, retrieval, navigation, etc. and the different patterns of traveling waves associated with them.

In summary, we used human electrocorticographic recordings and multiple memory tasks to investigate the large-scale electrophysiological basis of distinct cortical representations subserving human memory processing. We showed that theta/alpha and higher frequency beta oscillations are widespread in neocortex and demonstrate distinctive spatial patterns. Using a localized circular–linear regression and then independent component analysis, we found that in addition to planar waves, rotational, concentric, and complex patterns of traveling waves are widespread in the human brain. These diverse patterns of traveling waves were able to distinguish both broad and specific behavioral states underlying human memory and, crucially, by processing the features of traveling waves with machine learning we were able to decode behavioral states at the individual subject level. Thus, the approach we presented here provide a general framework for analyzing traveling waves and association with behavior, and these methods are also applicable to other data modalities such as scalp EEG, magnetoencephalographic, optical, as well as fMRI.

Methods

Human subjects

We examined direct brain recordings from 24 patients with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy who underwent surgery for removal of their seizure onset zones. All patients consented to having their brain recordings used for research purposes and all research was approved by Institutional Review Boards. The patients who performed a treasure hunt (TH) spatial memory task (N=9, 4 females, minimum age = 20, maximum age = 57, mean age = 36.6, see below for details) were part of a larger data collection initiative that can be downloaded from the UPENN-RAM consortium (http://memory.psych.upenn.edu/RAM), shared by Kahana and colleagues89. Recordings from the patients who performed the Sternberg (ST) verbal working memory task (N=15, 7 females, minimum age = 20, maximum age = 58, mean age = 35.9, see below for details), were collected at four hospitals (Thomas Jefferson University Hospital, Philadelphia; University of Pennsylvania Hospital Philadelphia; Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, and University Hospital Freiburg). The direct recordings of these patients can be downloaded from (https://memory.psych.upenn.edu/Data)39.

Electrophysiological recordings and preprocessing

Patients were implanted with different configuration of electrodes based on their clinical needs, which included both electrocorticographic (ECoG) surface grid and strips as well as depth electrodes. In this work, we only examined the ECoG grid electrodes on the cortical surface. ECoG recordings were obtained using subdural grids (contacts placed 10 mm apart). Anatomical localization of electrode placement was accomplished by co-registering the postoperative computed CTs with the postoperative MRIs using FSL (FMRIB (Functional MRI of the Brain) Software Library), BET (Brain Extraction Tool), and FLIRT (FMRIB Linear Image Registration Tool) software packages. Preoperative MRIs were used when postoperative MRIs were not available. From these images, we identified the location of each recording contact on the CT images and computed the electrode location in standardized Talairach coordinates.

Original sampling rates of ECoG signals in the TH task were 500 Hz, 1000 Hz, and 1600 Hz. ECoG signals in the TH task were downsampled to 500 Hz, if the original sampling rate was higher, for all subsequent analysis. Original sampling rates of ECoG signals in the ST task were 400 Hz, 512 Hz, and 1000 Hz. Therefore, ECoG signals in the ST task were downsampled to 400 Hz, if the original sampling rate was higher, for all subsequent analysis. We used common average referencing (ECoG electrodes re-referenced to the average signal of all electrodes in the grid), similar to our previous studies on traveling waves5,27. Line noise (60 Hz) and its harmonics were removed from the ECoG signals. For filtering, we used a fourth order two-way zero phase lag Butterworth filter throughout the analysis.

Cognitive tasks

Treasure Hunt spatial episodic memory task

The patients performed multiple trials of a spatial memory task in a virtual reality environment38,90 on a laptop computer at the bedside. They controlled their translational and rotational movements through the virtual environment with a handheld joystick. In each task trial, subjects explored a rectangular arena on a virtual 3D beach to reach treasure chests that revealed hidden objects, with the goal of encoding the location of each item encountered (Fig. 1A). The virtual beach (100 × 70 virtual units, 1.42: 1 aspect ratio) was bounded by a wooden fence on each side. The locations of the objects changed over the trials, but the shape, size and appearance of the environment remained constant throughout the sessions. The task environment was constructed so that the subject would perceive one virtual unit as corresponding to approximately 1 foot in the real world. Subjects viewed the environment from the perspective of cycling through the environment and the elevation of their perspective was 5.6 virtual units. Each end of the environment had unique visual cues to help the subjects navigate.

Each trial of the task begins with the subject being placed on the ground at a randomly selected end of the environment. The subject initiates the trial with a button press, then navigates to a chest using a joystick. Upon arrival at the chest, the chest opens and either reveals an object, which the subject should try to remember, or is empty. The subject remains facing the open chest for 1.5 sec (encoding period) and then the object and chest disappear, which indicates that the subject should navigate (navigation period) to the next chest that has now appeared in the arena. Each trial consists of four chests; two or three (randomly selected, so that subjects could not predict whether the current target chest contained an object, which served to remove effects of expectation and to encourage subjects to always attend to their current location as they approached a chest) of the chests contain an object, and the others are empty. Each session consists of 40 trials, and in each session, subjects visit a total of 100 full chests and 60 empty chests. The chests are located pseudorandomly throughout the interior of the environment. After reaching all four chests of a trial, subjects are transported automatically so that they have an elevated 3/4 overhead perspective view of the environment at a randomly selected end of the environment. They then perform a distractor task (distractor period), a computerized version of the “shell game”, before entering the retrieval period. During recall, subjects are cued with each of the objects from the trial in a random sequence and asked to recall the location of the object. In each recall period, they first indicate their confidence (confidence period) to remember the location of the object (“high”, “medium”, or “low”). Next, they indicate the precise location of the object by placing a cross-hair at the location in the environment that corresponds to the location of the cued item. After the location of each object of the trial is indicated, the feedback stage (feedback period) of each trial begins. Subjects are shown their response for each object and given feedback on their response accuracy, following a point system where they receive greater rewards for accurate responses. A response is considered correct if it is within 13 virtual units of the true object location. Mean accuracy across subjects was ~41%.

We analyzed the 1.5-sec long trials from the encoding periods of the task. For the navigation periods, we analyzed 1.5 sec long segments approximately corresponding to the middle of the navigation trial. Similarly, for the distractor periods, we analyzed 1.5 sec long time segments approximately corresponding to the middle of the distractor trial. For the confidence periods, we analyzed 1.5 sec recording immediately following the presentation of the visual cues. For the retrieval periods, we analyzed 1.5 sec immediately prior to the retrieval of the objects. For the feedback periods, we analyzed 1.5 sec time segments immediately following the feedback.

The 9 subjects in the spatial task completed 16 sessions in total. On an average, each subject completed ~128 encoding trials, ~80 confidence trials, ~32 navigation trials, ~80 retrieval trials, ~32 distractor trials, and ~75 feedback trials, in each session of the spatial task. Such a relatively large number of trials for the different task periods ensured a high degree of statistical power to yield robust results.

Sternberg verbal working memory task

Patients performed multiple trials of a Sternberg verbal working memory task39,91. In each trial of the task, first the patients viewed a fixation cross and then they were sequentially presented with one to three English letters on the screen of a bedside laptop computer (Fig. 1B). Each letter appeared on screen for 1 sec. Patients were instructed to closely attend to each stimulus presentation and to silently hold the identity of each item in memory. After the presentation of each list, the subject viewed a probe item and was asked to respond by pressing a key to indicate whether the probe was present in the just-presented list or whether it was absent. After the key press, the computer indicated whether the response was correct, and then a new list was presented. Across all letter presentations, patients viewed an 8 or 16 letter subset. Mean accuracy across subjects was ~92%. We analyzed the 1 sec long trials from the encoding period of each letter.

The 15 subjects in the verbal task completed 32 sessions in total. Each subject completed ~72 trials of each letter in a session.

Identification of oscillations

The overall goal of our analysis framework was to identify neural oscillations that were present across the cortex, and to measure their spatial patterns of propagation across the cortex. To this end, our first goal was to identify the oscillations that were present on multiple electrodes, and then (as described in subsequent sections), to map their spatial pattern of propagation as traveling waves. We focused our analysis on oscillations in the theta/alpha and beta bands. To identify the oscillations present on multiple channels, we adopted methods similar to our previous approach5,27. This subject-specific algorithm accounts for individual differences in electrode positions and oscillation frequencies across individuals. To identify traveling waves, a single oscillation frequency per electrode group is crucial since, by definition, a traveling wave involves a single frequency and whose phase progressively propagates through a contiguous region of cortex.

In this algorithm, to identify the frequency of interest for each electrode we first used Morlet wavelets to compute the power of the neural oscillations at 200 frequencies logarithmically spaced from 3 to 40 Hz. To identify narrowband oscillations, we fit a line to each patient’s mean power spectrum (mean across trials) in log–log coordinates using robust linear regression27 (Fig. 1D). We then subtracted the actual power spectrum from the regression line. This normalized power spectrum removes the 1/f background signal and emphasizes narrowband oscillations as positive deflections (Fig. 1E). We identified narrowband peaks in the normalized power spectrum as any local maximum greater than one standard deviation above the mean (Fig. 1E). We repeated this procedure for each phase of both tasks. The mean of the peak frequencies of the electrodes of a ECoG grid was defined as the grid’s cluster frequency (CF). The mean peak frequency across all clusters was ~12 Hz. Since by definition, a traveling wave involves a single oscillation frequency, we only included, in these analyses, oscillation clusters where at least 2/3rd of the electrodes in a grid had a narrowband peak in the power spectrum. We excluded 5 patients in the TH task and 2 patients in the ST task from further analyses since these patients did not meet this criterion. This resulted in 9 patients in the TH task and 15 patients in the ST task to be included in all subsequent analyses below. Overall, ~87% of ECoG electrodes in the TH task and ~86% of ECoG electrodes in the ST task had a narrowband oscillation in one of the theta/alpha (< 12 Hz) or beta (> 12 Hz) frequency bands, indicating that an overwhelming number of ECoG electrodes show oscillatory activity.

Note that in many cases, electrodes in the same ECoG grid showed multiple peaks in the power spectrum, i.e, they had peaks at both the theta/alpha and beta frequency bands. We estimated traveling waves for each of these narrowband frequencies separately. Moreover, since all electrodes in an oscillation cluster will not have identical oscillation frequency, we also allowed for electrodes to show oscillations at nearby but nonidentical frequencies (within 5 Hz of cluster frequency). All our analysis such as traveling waves and independent component analysis below were carried out based on the individual frequency of each electrode to account for the variation of frequencies across electrodes.

Identification of traveling waves

Having identified the electrode clusters that showed oscillations, we next identified and characterized traveling waves. A traveling wave can be described as an oscillation that moves progressively across a region of cortex. Quantitatively, a traveling (phase) wave can be measured as a set of simultaneously recorded neuronal oscillations at very similar frequencies whose instantaneous phases vary systematically with the locations of the recording electrodes. To identify these traveling waves we used a localized circular-linear regression approach, assuming that the relative phases of the oscillation clusters exhibit a linear relationship with electrode locations locally27,69,92,93. This locally circular-linear fitting of phase-location can detect complex patterns4,18 of traveling waves in an oscillation cluster in addition to planar traveling waves.

To identify traveling waves from the phases of each oscillation cluster, we first measured the instantaneous phases of the signals from each electrode of a given cluster by applying a 4th-order Butterworth filter at the cluster’s oscillation frequency (bandwidth [fp×.85, fp/.85] where fp is the peak frequency). We used the Hilbert transform on each electrode’s filtered signal to extract the instantaneous phase.

Next, we used circular statistics to measure the spatial propagation of phase between neighboring electrodes in each cluster to identify traveling waves at each time point94. To simplify visualizing and interpreting the data, we first projected the 3-D Talairach coordinates for each cluster to a 2-D plane using principal component analysis (PCA).

To identify traveling waves, we used a series of two-dimensional (2-D) localized circular–linear regression that were fit to model the direction of wave propagation in a local subcluster in the 25-mm neighborhood of each electrode. The local regression determines the direction of local wave propagation in the subcluster surrounding each electrode, by measuring whether the local phase pattern varies linearly with the electrodes’ coordinates in 2-D. Thus, this regression fits a single direction of wave propagation locally around each electrode at each moment, and these measures are then subsequently combined across subclusters to identify larger-scale spatial patterns of propagation.

In this regression, for each nearby electrode, we first identified the neighboring electrodes that were located nearby, constituting a sub-cluster of the given cluster. We note that because a few of the ECoG electrodes did not have a narrowband oscillation on certain trials, to allow us to estimate the overall shape of the traveling wave across the cluster, we estimated the traveling waves for those electrodes by an extrapolation procedure where, for the given electrode, we substituted the mean of the traveling waves of all electrodes within a 25 mm radius (subcluster) of the electrode under consideration. This extrapolation step was necessary for classification of each oscillation cluster as one of the wave categories using curl and divergence analysis (rotational or expanding/contracting or complex, see section below on Identification of planar, rotational, and concentric traveling waves). This is reasonable since as mentioned above, an overwhelming number of ECoG electrodes showed oscillatory activity (~87% of ECoG electrodes in the TH task and ~86% of ECoG electrodes in the ST task had a narrowband oscillation). Nevertheless, we reran our complete series of analyses without the extrapolation step, and found that the same modes and clusters were still statistically significant in both the TH and ST tasks, and also the decoding accuracy was very similar to that without the extrapolation step, indicating that this step did not have a large effect on our decoding results.

Identification of modes using complex independent component analysis (CICA)

We next used a large-scale dimensionality reduction framework to measure the spatial patterns of wave propagation across each cluster. This framework identified the different types of spatial wave patterns that formed traveling waves at each cluster and modeled how they summed to determine the signal on each epoch. We thus applied complex ICA (CICA) to the data on each epoch. Within each stable epoch identified as described above, the direction and strength of the traveling waves remain almost consistent across time-points. Thus, we averaged the direction and strength of the traveling waves within each stable epoch to find one spatial wave pattern associated with each stable epoch, which was input to the CICA algorithm.

Next, to analyze how the spatial patterns of waves changed over time and related to behavior, we concatenated the wave patterns (i.e., direction and strength) for all stable epochs across encoding, retrieval, navigation, etc. periods into a single matrix and then passed this matrix as input to the complex version of the independent component analysis (CICA)35,36. We used this complex version of the ICA, as compared to real ICA, to incorporate the 2-D directions of the traveling waves, weighted by the strength (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \cos (\alpha )$$\end{document}ρcos(α) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \sin (\alpha )$$\end{document}ρsin(α)), defined for each electrode in the ECoG grid. We then extracted the independent activation functions (or, weights) (each activation function corresponds to one of the stable epochs) and the corresponding modes (“raw modes” in Fig. 2) as the output from the CICA35,36. Multiplication of each of these modes with the mean of the weights across all stable epochs corresponding to that specific mode results in a unique wave pattern associated with that mode (the “mean mode” in Fig. 2). At the individual epoch level, a higher CICA weight for that epoch corresponding to a specific mode indicates higher representation of that wave pattern in that specific epoch and a lower CICA weight for an epoch corresponding to a specific mode indicates lower representation of that wave pattern in that specific epoch (Fig. 2). Moreover, the higher the variance explained by a given mode, the higher will be its representation across the trials (see Fig. 4E). Generally, we observed more complex wave patterns in higher order modes (modes beyond 6), which suggests that complex wave patterns explain relatively less variance in the data compared to spirals and concentric wave types. In this way, we can extract the CICA weights for each of the encoding, retrieval, navigation, etc. periods in the TH task and each of the English letters in the ST task and directly compare them statistically; see Statistical analysis section for details.

Identification of planar, rotational, and concentric traveling waves

After extracting the mean modes from the CICA procedure above, we next sought to classify each of the mean modes into one of the following categories “planar”, “rotational”, “concentric” (“expanding” or “contracting”), or “complex” (Fig. 3, and Supplementary Figs. 9–18),37 to identify global patterns of traveling waves in particular shapes that might be meaningful for cortical processing. We will be using the terms “modes” and “wave patterns”/“spatial patterns” interchangeably throughout our manuscript. This is because each of the modes corresponded to one unique and independent spatial pattern extracted from the CICA algorithm.

For classifying each of the modes into a specific wave pattern, we carried out robust statistical tests. We constructed appropriate surrogate distributions by shuffling the coordinates of the electrodes which destroys the specific structure associated with a given wave pattern. Please note that the raw modes are not unique since the activation functions and raw modes can be scaled to an arbitrary complex factor to yield the same output. Therefore, all our statistical procedures are done on the mean modes for accurate results.

For classifying the specific shape of each wave, we created a series of scores, which identified plane waves, and rotational/concentric waves. Clusters that did not meet our criterion for planar/rotational/concentric waves were labeled as ‘complex’. For detecting planar traveling waves, we calculated the mean wave direction (weighted by the strength of the wave) of all electrodes in an oscillation cluster. We then constructed surrogate distributions by shuffling the coordinates of the electrodes and recalculating the mean wave direction for each shuffling, therefore constructing a null distribution against which the empirical data can be compared. From the surrogate distribution, the threshold that corresponded to significance at p = 0.001 was ~0.6 across all clusters. Thus, we declared a wave as a planar wave for which the mean wave direction was higher than 0.6. Visually, this procedure of threshold selection identified individual mean modes that showed clear planar waves (Fig. 3) and small changes in this threshold did not substantially change the results.

Similarly, we classified the mean modes that represented rotational and concentric traveling waves by measuring the shape of their wave propagation using the curl and divergence metrics respectively18. Curl measures rotational patterns (for example, clockwise or counter-clockwise rotation) in wave dynamics and divergence measures expanding/contracting patterns (for example, sources or sinks) in wave dynamics18. We first calculated the curl and divergence metrics for each electrode and then calculated the mean curl and divergence across all electrodes in an oscillation cluster.

Modes that were not classified as one of the planar, rotational, or concentric, were designated as complex waves. Complex waves were also stable at the individual epoch level as shown in Fig. 9 and also across epochs, as these waves were able to distinguish behavior, for example, see Supplementary Fig. 18. Even though there was no global pattern associated with these complex traveling waves, many of these complex waves showed interesting local patterns, where a subset of electrodes in an ECoG grid showed planar, rotational, or concentric waves (Fig. 9, Supplementary Figs. 9–18).

If the size of the ECoG grid is small, such as the 6×4 or 8×4 grids in our dataset, there is a possibility that the detected planar waves could represent segments of larger rotational waves. Therefore, the number of rotational waves that we have detected in our dataset could potentially be undercounted. Future studies with much larger ECoG arrays across many individuals are needed for a more accurate assessment of the number of rotational waves and their functional role during various memory tasks.

Validating CICA using simulations

In addition to statistical validation on the experimental data using shuffling, we have also carried out four types of simulations to validate our proposed CICA approach to confirm its sensitivity to extract expected wave patterns and demonstrate specificity to reject negative results in the absence of such patterns.

Simulation 1, Sensitivity test, single wave patterns: In this simulation, we test the sensitivity of our framework where we feed-in known traveling wave patterns and then test the robustness of the CICA framework against noise. We first simulated a plane wave using an 8*8 electrode grid and added Gaussian random noise to generate multiple trials (100 in total) and then passed these into our CICA algorithm to test how well our algorithm is able to estimate the wave pattern. We generated activation functions or weights from a uniform distribution. To quantify, we calculated the root-mean-squared-error (RMSE) between the ground-truth pattern and the estimated pattern by the CICA approach and we repeated this procedure from low to high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). RMSE for this simulation and all subsequent simulations below was calculated by taking the root of the sum of squared differences of the activation functions estimated by CICA and the activation functions in the original data that we simulated. This demonstrates the ability of CICA to not only reconstruct the modes present in the original data, but also the degree of representativeness of the individual modes timepoint-by-timepoint. If the activation functions estimated by CICA match the activation functions in the original data timepoint-by-timepoint, then it can be claimed that CICA has high sensitivity. Additionally, we also tested the robustness of our CICA approach using rotational wave, source wave, and complex wave patterns.

Results from this analysis are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 4A, which showed that the performance of the CICA approach improved as SNR increased, with near-perfect reconstruction of the underlying traveling wave patterns at higher SNR values. Moreover, the performance of the different wave shapes such as planar, source, rotational, and complex waves were very similar, demonstrating the ability of the CICA approach to robustly estimate a diverse range of wave patterns. Simulation 1 demonstrates the sensitivity of the CICA method for a single wave-type and CICA is able to reconstruct the waveforms for each individual timepoints, in addition to reconstructing the shape of the waveform, i.e., the mode.

Simulation 2, Sensitivity test, multiple wave patterns: In this simulation, we test the sensitivity of our framework for identifying and distinguishing combinations of traveling wave patterns, i.e., multiple modes, in the presence of noise. For this, we simulated planar+source, planar+rotational, planar+complex, and planar+source+rotational wave patterns. We generated the activation functions for each type of wave shape from a uniform distribution. We again added Gaussian random noise to generate multiple trials and then passed these into our CICA algorithm to test how well our algorithm is able to separate multiple wave patterns. To quantify, we calculated the root-mean-squared-error between the ground-truth pattern and the estimated pattern by the CICA approach for all wave shapes and we repeated this procedure from low to high SNR.

Results from this analysis are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 4B, which showed that the performance of the CICA approach improved as SNR increased, similar to results from Simulation 1, with near-perfect reconstruction of the underlying traveling wave patterns at higher SNR values, demonstrating the ability of the CICA approach to robustly estimate the underlying wave patterns when multiple traveling waves are simultaneously present. For example, if the degree of representativeness of the rotational wave-type is high at a specific time-point, then CICA is accurately able to reconstruct this waveform at that individual time-point, and therefore the RMSE is close to zero at reasonably high SNR in our simulations.

Simulation 3, Specificity test: We also tested the specificity of the CICA method in the presence of spatially autocorrelated signal whose spatial autocorrelation matches to those in the spatial and verbal experimental data. We considered a rotational wave. Note that these rotational waves were ubiquitous in our experimental data and the spatial autocorrelation of the rotational wave matches to those in the experimental data. We also created spatially circularly shifted versions of the original rotational wave: vertically shifted, horizontally shifted and both vertically and horizontally shifted versions (Supplementary Fig. 5). Note that the spatial autocorrelation of all these circularly shifted waves is the same as the original rotational wave, although the specific structure is now destroyed. We separately gave these circularly shifted waves and also the original rotational wave as inputs to the CICA method. For the circularly shifted waves, we created 500 permutations of the circular shift and for each permutation, we generated 50 trials by adding Gaussian random noise to the traveling waves and passing them to the CICA method as input. This analysis revealed that CICA cannot reconstruct the original rotational wave anymore after using the circularly shifted versions. The error magnitude when the original wave is given as input to the CICA method is significantly lower than the distribution of the error magnitudes when the circularly shifted waves are given as input to CICA (p < 0.001). This analysis strongly demonstrates the ability of CICA to show negative results in the absence of any specific shape of wave patterns.

Simulation 4, Joining tests of sensitivity and specificity: This test assesses the joint ability of our framework to distinguish individual trials that showed real versus no waves, within an individual dataset. For this, we simulated rotational waves and planar+source+rotational wave patterns. The performance of other wave patterns such as planar waves and source waves was similar to the rotational wave pattern and hence omitted. For the input, half of the trials consisted of the given wave pattern (rotational or planar+source+rotational) and the other half consisted of no wave (zero signal, corresponds to zero weights or activation functions). We then calculated the detection percentage of our CICA approach, i.e., we calculated the percentage of trials our CICA approach is able to correctly identify the presence or absence of the wave patterns and we repeated this procedure from low to high SNR.

This analysis revealed that the CICA correctly estimates both the presence and absence of wave patterns (Supplementary Fig. 4C). For the rotational wave shape, CICA is able to achieve near-perfect detection accuracy at > ~ 15 dB SNR and for the planar+source+rotational case, CICA is able to achieve near-perfect detection accuracy at > ~ 20 dB SNR, for both the sensitivity and specificity tests, demonstrating the ability of the CICA to extract the expected wave patterns and also demonstrate negative results in the absence of such patterns. Simulation 4 demonstrates both the sensitivity and specificity of the CICA method. Simulation 4 demonstrates that the time-points where there was a wave present, CICA is able to accurately reconstruct both the shape and the degree of representativeness of that waveform at that individual time-point. On the other hand, CICA is also able to estimate the value of zero activation function with high accuracy at a given time-point where there was no wave, indicating that CICA can produce null result when there are no waves. Furthermore, in the case where we simulated multiple waveforms in equal contribution such as the “planar+source+rotational” case, the CICA method was able to reconstruct all of these three waveforms in equal contribution. Therefore, Simulation 4 strongly demonstrates both the sensitivity and specificity of the CICA method.

We would like to clarify that the individual time-points in our simulations correspond to the individual stable epochs in the experimental data. The shape of the traveling waves changes from one stable epoch to the next stable epoch, therefore the wave shapes also change from one task (for example, encoding) to the other (for example, retrieval). The CICA method is able to capture the variability of the changes in the wave shapes by using the activation functions whose value dynamically changes across different stable epochs and task periods, indicating the sensitivity and specificity of the CICA method. Similarly, in the simulations, the CICA method is able to capture the presence or absence of waveforms or the degree of representativeness of the waveforms by estimating the dynamic or time-varying activation functions. Since, in our simulations, we simulated planar, spiral, and concentric waves, which were ubiquitous in our experimental data and whose spatial autocorrelation is highly similar to the experimental data, our simulations therefore very strongly support the claims in our experimental data as well.

Statistical analysis of the relation between traveling waves and behavior

Identifying significant modes in CICA

To estimate the number of significant modes in the CICA output for each cluster, we compared the variance explained for each mode with the theoretical variance threshold 100/n, where n is the number of electrodes in an ECoG grid. This theoretical variance corresponds to the variance of each mode if the total variance (100%) is equally distributed among all modes. Because of the spatial structure of traveling waves, some modes will explain more variance compared to the other modes in the empirical data (Fig. 4E). We additionally shuffled the electrodes in each cluster and recalculated the variance distribution across modes and confirmed that the shuffled variance for all modes converged to the theoretical variance threshold of 100/n.

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA)

To identify how traveling wave patterns related to behavior, we directly compared the weights estimated from the CICA procedure above between encoding, retrieval, navigation, etc. periods in the TH task and between the English letters in the ST task, for each mode using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). MANOVA was used because the weights were complex. We statistically distinguished weights corresponding to different behavioral states using the following model: Real + Imag ~ States, where Real and Imag are the real and imaginary parts of the weights respectively and States are encoding, retrieval, navigation, etc. in the TH task or letters “B”, “G”, etc. in the ST task. We used this model for each mode and applied FDR-corrections for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05) across all modes and oscillation clusters. Statistical significance from the MANOVA would indicate that traveling waves shift their direction and/or strength to form distinct directional patterns which can distinguish different behavioral states. We designated an oscillation cluster to be significant if at least one of the modes from the CICA for that cluster showed statistical significance in MANOVA. Moreover, we also repeated our MANOVA analysis by shuffling the labels of the different behavioral states such as encoding, retrieval, etc. and built a histogram from these shuffles (f-statistics) against which we compared the empirical data. This analysis revealed that all our MANOVA results were still statistically significant (p’s < 0.05).

Robustness of traveling waves at the individual epoch level

We conducted surrogate analysis to test the significance of the estimated stable epochs (see Identification of stable epochs section above) and whether the observed stable epochs are beyond chance levels. For each time-point, we shuffled the trial labels so that the temporal contiguity within a given trial is destroyed and also the electrodes, so that the spatial topography for the corresponding behavioral state is destroyed, and then ran the stable epoch analysis using identical methodology as above. In this way, we built a surrogate distribution by aggregating all time-points corresponding to these shuffled stable epochs against which we then compared the aggregated time-points from the empirical stable epochs (p < 0.05).

Additionally, we also carried out statistical tests based on spatial autocorrelation-preserving surrogates to ensure that the traveling waves corresponding to the different behavioral states are statistically robust. To do this, we circularly rotated the coordinates of the electrodes for each stable epoch and passed these shuffled wave patterns into our CICA approach and subjected the corresponding results to MANOVA to estimate the f-statistics for these shuffled wave patterns. Surrogates tests are powerful non-parametric tests that are adaptive to the data in consideration, and highly robust to the specific distribution of the data, therefore reducing Type I and Type II statistical errors usually found in parametric tests. F-statistics from empirical data are then compared to this histogram of f-statistics and then significance is assessed based on whether the empirical data is outside 95% (p < 0.05), 99% (p < 0.01), or 99.9% (p < 0.001), of the histogram. Note that this shuffling preserves the spatial autocorrelation of the wave patterns, but destroys the specific structure associated with them, therefore destroying the systematic relationship between the wave direction and task state. We then constructed the histogram of the f-statistics of these surrogates and then compared the empirically estimated f-statistics to this surrogate distribution. This analysis revealed that our results were highly significant across all clusters (all p’s < 0.001).

Assessing group-level significance

For assessing statistical significance for the group level results related to fraction of wave strength and fraction of significance (Figs. 4, 7–9, Supplementary Fig. 19), we used chi-squared tests with FDR-corrections for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05) across frequencies (theta/alpha and beta) and hemispheres (left and right).

Control analysis to assess the effects of oscillation frequency on behavior

To rule out the possibility that the behavioral results could be driven by changes in spectral content (e.g., oscillation frequency), we ran the CICA analysis on the frequency contents of each episode. Specifically, we estimated the peak frequency for each trial and electrode separately, and then passed these measures to the same CICA and MANOVA analysis as in our wave analysis approach. However, this analysis revealed that the spectral contents do not differ between any of the task periods in the spatial task or any of the letters in the verbal task (all p’s > 0.05), indicating that the effects that we observed cannot be explained by differences in spectral contents, rather they are related to the traveling waves.

We also carried out additional analysis by passing the peak frequencies during different tasks to train our neural network decoder to distinguish pairwise behavioral states. As before, we used our shuffling analysis procedure by shuffling the labels of the behavioral states such as encoding, retrieval, etc. and then running the same decoding analysis on this shuffled data to generate a distribution of shuffled data against which the actual decoding performance can then be compared. However, we could not decode the behavioral states by using the peak frequencies, neither in the spatial task nor in the verbal task (all p’s > 0.05). This suggests that the decoding performance is driven by the different wave patterns that we observed across behaviors, and not driven by a difference in peak frequencies across different task conditions. These new results reinforce the fact that the effects that we observed cannot be explained by differences in peak frequencies, rather they are related to the traveling waves.

Control analysis to assess the effects of filtering

Since our oscillation detection algorithm is based on identifying peaks in the power spectrum which is averaged across trials, we carried out a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) analysis to ensure that these oscillations are actually present at the individual stable epoch level. Specifically, we concatenated all the filtered recordings at the oscillation frequency and raw recordings across all the stable epochs for each cluster and then calculated the Pearson correlation between the filtered and raw data. This analysis revealed that the correlation values are statistically significant across all clusters (median Pearson r ~ 0.6, all p’s < 0.001). These high correlation coefficients indicate that the narrowband filtered signals are capturing a large amount of signal in the raw recording, showing that the filtering procedure is capturing a dominant amount of variance in the recordings.

Moreover, we also ran our SNR analysis for the different wave modes such as planar, rotational, concentric, and complex, to examine whether the SNR differs for the different wave modes. We first classified each stable epoch as one of the modes (planar/rotational/concentric/complex) based on the magnitude of the activation function, i.e., a stable epoch was classified as a planar wave if the magnitude of the activation function corresponding to the planar mode was the highest, etc. We then concatenated the filtered and raw recordings across all the stable epochs for each mode and calculated the Pearson correlations between the filtered and raw data for each mode. We then compared the correlations for the pairwise modes (planar vs. rotational, planar vs. concentric, rotational vs. concentric, etc.) for each oscillation cluster. This analysis revealed that the correlation values for the pairwise modes do not differ from each other in any cluster (all p’s > 0.05, Mann-Whitney U-test). This suggests that the different wave patterns that we observed in our data are not driven by a difference in SNR.

To rule out the possibility that the effects could be driven by changes in SNR, we calculated the Pearson correlation between the filtered and raw data for each of the behavioral states separately, both for the spatial memory and verbal memory tasks. We then used these correlation values in our neural network decoding analysis to distinguish pairwise behavioral states. As before, we used our shuffling analysis procedure by shuffling the labels of the behavioral states such as encoding, retrieval, etc. and then running the same decoding analysis on this shuffled data to generate a distribution of shuffled data against which the actual decoding performance can then be compared. However, we could not decode pairwise behavioral states by using these correlation values (all p’s > 0.05). This suggests that the decoding performance is driven by the different wave patterns that we observed across behaviors, and not driven by a difference in SNR across different task conditions.

Decoding analysis of the relation between traveling waves and behavior

Our final goal was to test whether we could decode the broad and specific behavioral states from the diverse traveling wave patterns in our datasets. Decoding behavioral states in our datasets is a multiclass classification problem. We converted this problem into a series of binary classification tasks, as these can be solved straightforwardly with various multivariate algorithms. We trained multilayer neural networks,96 with cross-validation, for classifying pairwise behavioral states (for example, encoding versus retrieval, letter “D” versus letter “J”, etc.), separately for the spatial and verbal memory tasks, training a neural network classifier for each oscillation cluster separately. We used a PyTorch-based four-layer neural network for this binary classification problem97. We used the extracted weights from the CICA procedure as features for training our neural network classifiers. We observed that increasing the number of modes for training increased the network test decoding accuracy (Fig. 10B). Therefore, we included all the modes for classifying the behavioral states. Also please note that the higher order modes (modes beyond 6) were mostly complex wave shapes and, these higher order modes progressively contributed less to the test decoding accuracy (Fig. 10B), indicating lack of specific wave structure in these higher order modes compared to the lower order modes (modes lower than 6).

The multilayer network architecture comprised an input layer, two hidden layers (32 and 16 neurons respectively), and an output layer with a sigmoid activation function, similar to the neural network architectures previously used for classifying behavioral states96. The inputs for all our decoding analysis were the activation functions or the weights since for a given mode, these were changing across different task periods. Please note that for a grid with “N” number of electrodes and hence “N” number of dimensions in CICA, a 2N dimensional neural network was trained (the factor 2 comes from the fact that the weights are complex numbers and hence real and imaginary parts were considered separately). ReLU activation functions were applied to the hidden layers to introduce nonlinearity and improve generalization98. The network architectures were kept the same across the spatial and verbal memory datasets, and individual models were trained for each oscillation cluster separately to optimize neural network hyperparameters. To further enhance generalization, dropout regularization was implemented after each hidden layer99. For model optimization, we used Binary Cross Entropy as the loss function, ideal for binary classification tasks,100 and the Adam optimizer during training97.

To ensure robust optimization and generalization, we employed cross-validation for iterative training over multiple epochs. For each pair of behavioral states, we first rebalanced the data such that we have an equal number of epochs per behavioral state101. We used a five-fold cross-validation technique in which the network model was fitted on 80% of the data and then its performance was tested on the remaining 20%. We used a subsampling procedure where each epoch was randomly assigned to a particular fold, subject to the constraint that all behavioral states are evenly represented,101 and then averaged the network decoding accuracies across folds.

To access group level statistical significance for the decoding accuracy results, we used one-sided sign tests versus chance (p < 0.05). Moreover, we also carried out additional statistical analysis by shuffling the labels of the behavioral states such as encoding, retrieval, etc. and then running the same decoding analysis on this shuffled data to generate a distribution of shuffled data against which the actual decoding performance can then be compared. This analysis revealed that our decoding results are statistically significant across all clusters (all p’s < 0.05), suggesting that structure of wave activity is the source of the classification accuracy.

Please note that all our traveling waves analysis were carried out for each cluster and subject separately since each cluster showed specific patterns of wave strength and directions, which differed across clusters and subjects. Due to the variability of the strength and directions of the traveling waves, our decoder was also run in each cluster separately rather than collapsing across subjects (Fig. 10, Supplementary Fig. 20). Group level results are finally shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 19 to compare the wave-strength for different types of waves and task periods.
