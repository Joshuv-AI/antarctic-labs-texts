Lenvatinib enhances the response of biliary tract cancer to anti-HER2 therapy
Authors: Huishan Sun, Shuofeng Li, Zhe Zhu, Guanhua Yu, Ziyue Huang, Nan Zhang, Shi Feng, Yiran Li, Cong Ning, Ziyu Xun, Jingnan Xue, Dandan Li, Longhao Zhang, Mingjian Piao, Chengjie Li, Jiongyuan Li, Boyu Sun, Xiaobo Yang, Hanping Wang, Haitao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is frequently overexpressed or amplified in biliary tract cancer (BTC). Although NCCN clinical practice guidelines recommend HER2-targeted agents as subsequent-line therapy, drug resistance often restricts the clinical benefits of existing regimens. Here, we demonstrate that HER2 inhibitors have heterogeneous effects on the proliferation of BTC cells. Further bioinformatic analysis and functional experimental validation revealed that HER2 inhibitors significantly activated fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signalling pathway in HER2high BTC. Notably, the combination of lenvatinib and a HER2 inhibitor exerted potent antiproliferative effects on HER2high BTC models both in vitro and in vivo. In the clinical cohort, the combination therapy achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 58.1% and a disease control rate (DCR) of 86.0%. The median progression-free survival (mPFS) was 11.27 months, and the median overall survival (mOS) reached 19.50 months. For patients with HER2high expression, the ORR and mOS were 71.4% and 27.67 months, respectively. The overall safety profile was manageable, with no treatment-related deaths observed. These findings demonstrate that the activation of FGFR signalling confers resistance to HER2 inhibitors in HER2high BTC. The combination of a HER2 inhibitor with lenvatinib, particularly when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors, has exhibited both promising antitumour responses and good tolerability in patients with advanced BTC.

Introduction

Biliary tract cancer (BTC) encompasses a group of highly heterogeneous epithelial malignancies of the biliary tract, including intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ECC) and gallbladder carcinoma (GBC).1 Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with 5-year survival rates of only 3.0% for patients with metastatic disease.2 Currently, gemcitabine–cisplatin combined with durvalumab or pembrolizumab is recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines as a preferred first-line regimen for patients with advanced BTC, whereas the folinic acid, fluorouracil and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) regimen is recommended as a preferred subsequent-line therapy.3 Nevertheless, the addition of durvalumab or pembrolizumab to gemcitabine–cisplatin resulted in a median overall survival (mOS) and median progression-free survival (mPFS) of only 12.7–12.9 months and 6.5–7.2 months, respectively.3–6 The mOS for subsequent-line FOLFOX therapy was only 6.2 months.3,7 Therefore, more effective therapeutic strategies are urgently required to address the poor prognosis of patients with advanced BTC.

In recent years, precision targeted therapies based on molecular subtypes have demonstrated promising efficacy in treating advanced BTC.8–11 Among these, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), with its gene amplification and protein overexpression, has emerged as a druggable therapeutic target. HER2 is also a clinically significant biomarker with both prognostic and predicative value in advanced BTC.12 Compared with HER2-negative BTC, HER2-positive BTC is associated with shorter OS and significantly shorter first-line progression-free survival.12 The promising clinical efficacy of HER2-targeted therapies (such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, pyrotinib and disitamab vedotin) has been observed in various tumours, particularly breast cancer13 and gastric cancer.14 Previous studies have shown that HER2 mutation, amplification or overexpression can be observed in 3.7–4.8% of ICCs, 17.4–18.5% of ECCs and 19.1–31.3% of GBCs.15,16 Lee et al. also verified that anti-HER2 therapy significantly improved OS among HER2-positive BTC patients.12 Current NCCN guidelines primarily recommend HER2-targeted regimens, such as zanidatamab, trastuzumab deruxtecan, trastuzumab plus tucatinib and trastuzumab plus pertuzumab, as subsequent-line treatment regimens for HER2-positive tumours.3 These regimens have shown only modest efficacy, with mPFS values ranging from 4.0–5.5 months and mOS values ranging from 10.9–15.5 months.17–20

Based on the current therapeutic status of the four HER2-targeted therapies recommended by the NCCN guidelines, an increasing number of clinical trials combining HER2-targeted therapy with other regimens, especially chemotherapy, have been conducted to explore improvements in the clinical efficacy of patients with advanced BTC. Trastuzumab plus FOLFOX chemotherapy as a subsequent-line therapy resulted in a 29.4% objective response rate (ORR) and a 5.1-month mPFS in HER2-positive BTC patients in the KCSG HB19-14 study.21 Another multicentre, phase II study (TAB) evaluated the clinical activity of trastuzumab combined with gemcitabine–cisplatin chemotherapy in treatment-naive patients with HER2-positive BTC.22 The disease control rate (DCR) of this combination therapy was 80%, and the mPFS was 7.0 months.22 In the TreeTopp study, varlitinib plus capecitabine achieved an ORR of 9.4% and mPFS of 2.83 months in previously treated advanced BTC patients.23 Although these combination therapies can benefit a few BTC patients, the clinical efficacy of these current regimens is still moderate, and new translational research is needed to improve the clinical efficacy of HER2-targeted therapy.

Lenvatinib, a multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor, selectively inhibits VEGFR 1-3, FGFR1-4, PDGFRα, KIT and RET.24 Wang et al. confirmed that lenvatinib has promising clinical activity against advanced BTC and has an acceptable safety profile.25 Jin et al. reported that EGFR inhibition is synthetically lethal when it is combined with lenvatinib in liver cancer.26 Crosstalk among VEGFR, EGFR and their downstream signal transduction pathways results in the formation of an intricate interaction network that limits the response of cancer cells to antitumour drugs.27 It has also been reported that lenvatinib or HER2 inhibitors can overcome resistance to anti-PD1 therapy.28,29 HER2-targeted therapy plus lenvatinib may be a potential combination therapy to effectively induce tumour cell death. In line with this phenomenon, we previously observed one ICC patient in the Peking Union Medical College Hospital clinical cohort whose HER2 immunohistochemical (IHC) score was 3+ and whose disease progressed after pyrotinib treatment.30 He subsequently received lenvatinib plus pyrotinib and pembrolizumab combination treatment.30 After 6 months of combination therapy, he achieved a PR, with a PFS of 17 months.30 Our study also verified that the FGFR/MAPK signalling pathway was activated after HER2 inhibitor treatment both in vivo and in vitro. Therefore, exploring the interaction between HER2 inhibitors and lenvatinib is urgently needed to enable more patients with advanced BTC to benefit from this combination therapy.

In this study, we verified that HER2 inhibitors could induce FGFR signalling pathway activation in HER2high BTC. We further verified that lenvatinib plus anti-HER2 inhibitors could significantly inhibit HER2high BTC tumour cell growth. Notably, this combination therapy strongly blocked FGFR/MAPK signalling pathway activation. Finally, we analyzed an observational clinical cohort to assess the clinical feasibility of a HER2-targeted combination regimen in patients with advanced BTC.

Results

Heterogeneous effects of HER2 inhibitors on BTC cell proliferation

First, we analysed HER2 expression across a panel of human BTC cell lines and identified several cell lines with high HER2 expression, among which SNU1196 and GBC-SD cells presented the highest levels (Fig. 1a). To investigate whether HER2 is required for BTC cell proliferation, we knocked down HER2 expression in SNU1196 and GBC-SD cells using two different siRNAs. Cell proliferation was significantly slowed after HER2 was knocked down in both cell lines (Fig. S1). We then selected several HER2 inhibitors, including pyrotinib,31 disitamab vedotin,32 trastuzumab,18 afatinib33 and CP-724714,34 all of which have documented clinical efficacy in solid tumours. The growth rate of the two cell lines significantly decreased when the cells were treated with increasing concentrations of these inhibitors (Fig. 1b–f). Long-term colony formation assays also revealed that HER2 inhibition using different inhibitors had different antitumour effects on BTC cells (Fig. 1g–j). Anti-HER2 treatment potentially induces FGFR signalling pathway activation

The results of clinical trials revealed that the ORR of patients receiving anti-HER2 therapies recommended by the NCCN guidelines was 23–56.3%.17–20 Therefore, identifying drugs that can significantly increase sensitivity to current anti-HER2 drugs is highly important. We performed long-term culture of SNU1196 and GBC-SD cells treated with pyrotinib, CP-724714, afatinib, or trastuzumab. After almost 2 weeks of culture, we harvested these cells and performed RNA sequencing. We first analysed the targets of FDA-approved drugs for BTC patients (Fig. 2a). The heatmap results revealed that the mRNA levels of some targets summarised in Fig. 2a were dysregulated (Fig. 2b). In SNU1196 cells, FGFR2, and FGFR3 were highly expressed in the HER2 inhibitor treatment group. In GBC-SD cells, FGFR3 and FGFR1 mRNA levels tended to be higher in afatinib or trastuzumab treatment groups. We subsequently conducted a qPCR assay to validate the expression of FGFRs in the anti-HER2 drug treatment groups. The results revealed that both FGFR3 and FGFR2 were significantly upregulated across most drug treatment groups in SNU1196 cells (Fig. 2c and Fig. S2a–c) and GBC-SD cells (Fig. 2d and Fig. S2d–f). Notably, trastuzumab treatment had no significant effect on FGFR2 mRNA expression in GBC-SD cells (Fig. S2f). Accordingly, FGFR3 and FGFR2 protein levels were significantly greater in the HER2 inhibitor-treated groups (pyrotinib or disitamab vedotin) than in the control group (Fig. 2e–f). Moreover, we assessed FGFR phosphorylation levels at Tyr653/654. Lenvatinib sensitises BTC to anti-HER2 treatment

Lenvatinib exerts its therapeutic effect, in part, by targeting FGFRs. Lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab is recommended for BTC patients in the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines. Therefore, we investigated whether lenvatinib synergised with HER2 inhibitors in BTC cells highly expressing HER2. Compared with the single-drug or control treatments, the combination of lenvatinib with pyrotinib (Fig. 3a, c, d), disitamab vedotin (Fig. 3b, e, f), CP-724714 (Fig. S3a, S4a, b), afatinib (Fig. S3b, S4c, d), or trastuzumab (Fig. S3c, S4e, f) significantly inhibited BTC cell growth regardless of assay duration. The results of apoptosis assays demonstrated that the lenvatinib plus HER2 inhibitors synergistically induced apoptosis in both GBC-SD and HCCC-9810 cells and was significantly more effective than any single agent alone (Fig. 3g, h, Fig. S5). Lenvatinib in combination with pyrotinib or disitamab vedotin also significantly suppressed tumour growth in mice bearing GBC-SD xenografts, whereas single-drug treatments had minimal effects (Fig. 3i–k). IHC staining revealed that the expression of a proliferative marker (Ki-67) and microvessel density (CD31) significantly decreased after combination treatment (Fig. S7a, c, d). The body weights of the mice in the different groups did not differ, indicating that the combination treatment was well tolerated (Fig. S7e). These results demonstrate that lenvatinib synergises with HER2-targeted inhibitors in HER2high BTC.

Anti-HER2 activity activates the FGFR–MAPK pathway

To clarify the molecular mechanism through which FGFR signalling activation impairs the sensitivity of HER2high BTC cells to anti-HER2 agents, we reanalysed the genes that were differentially expressed between anti-HER2–treated cells and control cells. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that genes related to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, the MAPK signalling pathway and the JAK–STAT signalling pathway were significantly enriched (Figs. S8–9). Further gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) suggested that pyrotinib treatment markedly induced the dysregulation of gene sets related to the ERK1/ERK2 cascade and MAPK signalling pathway (Fig. 4a). Previous studies have suggested that the MAPK/ERK pathway is an important downstream effector of FGFR.35,36 Thus, the FGFR–MAPK pathway may function as a survival mechanism for HER2high BTC cells under anti-HER2 therapy. Here, we observed that the combination of lenvatinib and HER2 inhibitors blocked FGFR phosphorylation (Fig. 4b–e). Despite the inhibition of ERK1/2 and MEK1/2 phosphorylation by pyrotinib or disitamab vedotin single-agent therapy, the combination therapy resulted in more profound suppression (Fig. 4b–e). Similarly, the combination therapy also strongly inhibited AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 4b–e). Similar findings were also validated in GBC-SD xenograft mouse models (Fig. 4f–g). IHC staining further demonstrated that compared with pyrotinib or disitamab vedotin single-agent treatment, combination treatment suppressed the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 (Fig. 4h, Fig. S7b). Overall analysis of clinical response to combination therapy

A total of 388 advanced BTC patients were screened for eligibility, 43 of whom were received HER2 inhibitors plus lenvatinib (cohort 1, n = 12) or HER2 inhibitors plus lenvatinib and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (cohort 2, n = 31) (Fig. 5a). Table 1 summarises the baseline clinicopathologic characteristics of the enrolled patients. Among them, 28 (65.1%) patients were aged 60 years or older, 23 (53.5%) patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score of 0, and 39 (90.7%) patients had Child‒Pugh class A disease. Elevated CA19-9 levels (≥200 U/mL) were noted in 24 (55.8%) patients, and elevated CEA levels (≥5 ng/mL) were noted in 28 (65.1%) patients. Seventeen (39.5%) patients had a median-to-high pathological grade, and 29 (67.4%) patients had metastatic BTC. On the basis of HER2 expression, 21 patients (48.8%) had high HER2 expression. Cohorts 1 and 2 showed no significant differences in baseline characteristics. All patients underwent imaging evaluations before the follow-up cut-off date of September 30, 2025. The clinical response and safety profiles of the enroled patients were assessed in the entire study population as well as in the subgroups. The mPFS and mOS for all patients were 11.27 and 19.50 months, respectively (Fig. 5b). The 12-month and 24-month PFS rates were 45.7% and 7.4%, respectively, whereas the 12-month and 24-month OS rates were 69.4% and 38.0%, respectively. The ORR and DCR were 58.1% and 86.0%, respectively (Table 2). The results of the treatment regimen-based analyses revealed that patients in cohort 1 had an mPFS of 6.74 months and an mOS of 17.43 months (Fig. 5c). In contrast, patients in cohort 2 experienced an mPFS of 12.90 months and an mOS of 20.43 months (Fig. 5c). Discussion

Patients with BTC typically have a poor prognosis and fewer therapeutic options, especially when the disease is advanced. Previous studies have demonstrated that compared with HER2-negative BTC, HER2-positive BTC is associated with an even poorer prognosis.12 Currently, NCCN guidelines recommend HER2 overexpression and/or amplification as therapeutic targets in advanced BTC.3 However, HER2-directed monotherapies or combinations with chemotherapy have historically yielded only modest survival benefits.17–21 Therefore, the need for novel treatment options for HER2high BTC patients remains unmet. In our study, through in vitro cell experiments and in vivo xenograft models, we indicated that FGFR signalling pathway activation confers a poor response in HER2high BTC cells to anti-HER2 monotherapy. Notably, lenvatinib, which targets multiple targets, including FGFRs, combined with a HER2-targeting agent can significantly inhibit the growth of HER2high BTC. Our clinical study confirmed that lenvatinib plus an anti-HER2 drug (pyrotinib or disitamab vedotin) could significantly improve the survival of HER2high BTC patients. These findings suggest that simultaneous inhibition of HER2 and FGFR may be a promising therapeutic approach to increase the clinical benefit for HER2high BTC patients.

FGFR gene amplification or fusion aberrantly activates FGFR downstream signalling pathways, which then induce cancer progression.37 FGFR2 gene fusion is among the most common alterations in BTC, accounting for approximately 14% of cases.38 We verified that the expression of FGFRs, notably FGFR2 and FGFR3, increased upon treatment with HER2 inhibitors (pyrotinib, disitamab vedotin, afatinib, CP-724714 and trastuzumab) in HER2high BTC. Moreover, FGFR phosphorylation was enhanced after HER2 inhibitor treatment. It has been reported that tumour-associated fibroblasts secrete high levels of FGF5 to induce FGFR2 activation, which subsequently transactivates HER2, leading to resistance to HER2-targeted therapies in breast cancer.39 Moreover, FGFR4 inhibition can increase anti-HER2 therapy susceptibility in resistant breast cancer.40 The combination of FGFR and targeted HER2 TKIs can enhance cell growth inhibition and induce apoptosis in HER2-amplified breast cancer.41 Although clinical samples were lacking to confirm the role of FGFRs, multiple studies collectively indicate that FGFR signalling may enable HER2high BTC cells to be insensitive to HER2-targeted therapy.

The MAPK and AKT signalling pathways are key downstream effectors of the tyrosine kinase activities of FGFR37 and HER2.42 A series of studies have elucidated the mechanism underlying the feedback reactivation of the MAPK pathway following FGFR inhibition26 or HER2 inhibition.43 This mechanism can help explain why monotherapy shows limited efficacy in certain patients. The KEGG and GSEA results of our study revealed significant dysregulation of the MAPK pathway upon HER2 inhibition, accompanied by sustained ERK1/2, MEK1/2 and AKT phosphorylation activation under anti-HER2 monotherapy. Only the combination of lenvatinib with pyrotinib or disitamab vedotin effectively blocked MAPK pathway feedback activation and inhibited tumour growth in all the tested cell and animal models. Moreover, HER2-targeted agents combined with lenvatinib (±ICIs) demonstrated considerable efficacy in advanced HER2-expressing (high or low) BTC patients, with an mOS of 19.50 months, which is longer than that of patients receiving the HER2-targeted regimens recommended by the NCCN guidelines.17–20

The addition of ICIs to HER2-targeted agents and lenvatinib conferred additional survival benefits compared with HER2-targeted agents plus lenvatinib. The combination of lenvatinib and pembrolizumab is also recommended for patients with BTC in the CSCO guidelines. Recent studies have also demonstrated that lenvatinib remodels the BTC tumour microenvironment by promoting the infiltration of CD8+PD-1hi effector T cells into tumour cell nests.44 Our study also provides clinical evidence supporting the synergistic potential of these drugs. Most notably, the subgroup of patients with high HER2 expression who received first-line HER2 inhibitors plus lenvatinib and ICIs achieved unprecedented outcomes: an ORR and DCR of 100%, an mPFS of 18.87 months, and an mOS of 44.47 months. Furthermore, during subsequent-line treatment, patients with high HER2 expression treated with HER2 inhibitors plus lenvatinib and ICIs also achieved longer survival, with an mPFS of 19.50 months and an mOS of 23.80 months. These outcomes are better than those in patients with low HER2 expression. This triplet therapy can enable successful conversion to R0 resection in initially unresectable patients. This underscores the potential of triple therapy as both a palliative option and a potent conversion strategy to enable curative surgery. Although limited by sample size, these exploratory findings suggest that incorporating ICIs in combination with HER2-targeted agents and lenvatinib may improve prognosis in HER2-expressing (high or low) BTC, especially in patients with high HER2 expression.

For advanced BTC, the NCCN and CSCO clinical practice guidelines recommend HER2-targeted regimens in the subsequent-line setting, yet no first-line HER2-targeted therapy exists. The standard regimen of chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy provides only limited clinical benefit for advanced BTC patients.3 Our study demonstrated that a HER2 inhibitor plus lenvatinib with or without ICIs achieved encouraging antitumour activity and significant survival benefits in both first line and subsequent-line settings. Furthermore, our study indicates that HER2low BTC patients achieved longer mOS from this triple therapy than NCCN guideline-recommended standard therapy both in first-line (17.9 months vs. 12.7–12.9 months) and subsequent-line settings (8.83 months vs. 6.2 months).3 Collectively, these findings provide support for this triple therapy as a novel therapeutic paradigm that benefits advanced BTC patients irrespective of HER2 expression levels.

The combination regimen exhibited a favourable and manageable safety profile. Although 62.8% of patients experienced grade 3–4 AEs, no treatment-related deaths (grade 5) occurred. Common AEs included fatigue, diarrhoea, decreased appetite and hypertension. Notably, grade 3–4 AEs were not significantly greater in cohort 2 than in cohort 1 (p = 0.484), supporting the tolerability of the triplet regimen.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the antitumour activity of lenvatinib combined with anti-HER2 drugs was validated only in a xenograft mouse model, as a BTC PDX mouse model with high HER2 expression was lacking to verify this phenomenon. Most importantly, this study is limited by its single-centre design, the restricted cohort of Chinese patients, and the lack of a control group, which is inherent to its single-arm nature. Without comparator data, a robust assessment of the regimen’s efficacy versus standard care (chemotherapy ± ICIs) or a single HER2-targeted agent is not possible. Therefore, these findings warrant confirmation in future multicentre, randomised controlled trials. Given that BTC encompasses ICC, ECC and GBC, subtype-specific investigations are warranted in the future.

Overall, our study demonstrated that FGFR/MAPK signalling is involved in resistance to HER2-targeted agents in HER2high BTC (Fig. S10). The combination of a HER2 inhibitor and lenvatinib, particularly with the addition of ICIs, exhibited encouraging antitumour effects and a controllable safety profile in patients with advanced BTC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

RBE, HCCC-9810 and GBC-SD cells were purchased from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resources (Beijing, China). The SNU1196 cell line was obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea), and the HuCCT1 cell line was purchased from the Health Science Research Resources Bank (Osaka, Japan). RBE, HCCC-9810, SNU1196, HuCCT1 and GBC-SD cells were cultured in standard RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The PUMC-ICC1 and PUMC-ICC3 cell lines were isolated from fresh human ICC tumour samples and cultured for more than 3 months at the Cell Resource Center of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. PUMC-ICC1 cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with insulin transferrin selenium (ITS-G; Gibco). PUMC-ICC3 cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with Y-27632 (Stemcell Technologies, Canada) and ITS-G. Unless otherwise noted, we supplemented all the cell media with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin G and streptomycin (Gibco, USA). All the cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C and were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and authenticated by STR profiling.

siRNA

Two different siRNAs targeting HER2 were obtained from GenePharma Company (Suzhou, China). The following siRNA sequences were used: HER2 #1, 5′-GCUCUUUGAGGACAACUAUTT-3′; HER2 #2, 5′-GCAUUACCAGUGCCAAUATT-3′; and negative control, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′. siRNA transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

Western blot

The cells were subsequently washed with PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor (Roche) for 30 min on ice. After the protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay kit (Solarbio), the lysates were boiled, separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Afterwards, the membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and incubated with different primary antibodies. After the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies, a chemiluminescence substrate (ECL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added, and signals were recorded using a ChemiDoc Touch system (Bio-Rad).

Quantitative PCR analysis

We extracted total RNA using a MolPure® TRIeasy Plus Total RNA Kit (19211ES60; Yeasen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell proliferation assay

BTC tumour cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 2 × 104–5 × 104 cells per well. After 24 h, the cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of drugs, and the medium was replaced every 2 days. Cell numbers were quantified every 2 days using a Count Star automated cell counter (ALIT Life Science, China).

Long-term colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 103–5 × 103 cells per well and then cultured in medium supplemented with various drugs for 9–14 days. The culture medium was changed every 3 days. The cells were fixed with methanol for 30 min, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. The stained cells were subsequently washed and photographed. ImageJ software was used to calculate the cell confluence.

Apoptosis and cell cycle analysis

Briefly, cells were seeded into 12-well plates and treated with different drug combinations. Cell apoptosis assay was conducted by harvesting BTC cells and staining them with 300 μL of Annexin V-FITC/PI staining buffer (Vazyme, China). For cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed with 70% cold ethanol for 24 h after harvest and stained with 500 μL of propidium iodide/RNase staining solution (MCE). Finally, the cells were acquired on Attune Nxt 3L-BRV cytometer (Invitrogen), and FlowJo software (version 10) was used to analyse the flow cytometry results.

RNA sequencing

BTC cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of drugs for 14 days. Total RNA was subsequently extracted using a MolPure® TRIeasy Plus Total RNA Kit (19211ES, YEASEN) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing was performed by Novogene (Beijing, China). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.20.0). GSEA (version 4.3.3) software was used to determine a priori defined sets of genes with statistically significant, concordant differences between different groups. KEGG analysis was conducted to determine dysregulated pathways after treatment with different HER2 inhibitors.

IHC staining

Briefly, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections from xenografted mouse tumours were incubated with Ki-67 (ab16667; Abcam) antibody, CD31 (ab281583; Abcam) antibody, or p-ERK1/2 (4370; Cell Signalling Technology) antibody. ImageJ software was used to quantify the percentage of positively stained cells in each group. The tumour microvessel density was assessed by CD31 staining to evaluate angiogenesis.

Mouse xenografts

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (approval number XHDW-2025-142). Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). GBC-SD cells (5 × 106 cells per mouse) in 100 μL of Matrigel/100 μL of PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice (n = 6 per group). After tumour establishment, the mice were randomly allocated to the control groups and treatment groups: pyrotinib (10 mg/kg orally by gavage), disitamab vedotin (10 mg/kg intravenously once weekly), lenvatinib (10 mg/kg orally by gavage), or combinations of pyrotinib plus lenvatinib or disitamab vedotin plus lenvatinib. For the combination groups, each drug was given at the same dose and schedule as monotherapy. Disitamab vedotin was kindly provided by RemeGen Co., Ltd. (Yantai, China). The body weight and tumour size of each mouse were monitored every 2–3 days. The tumour volume was calculated using the following formula: tumour volume = (width2 × length)/2. Approximately 3 weeks after treatment, all the mice were euthanized. Tumours were harvested for further analysis.

Clinical trial design and participants

BTC patients in this study were enroled from a registered clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov, Identifier: NCT03892577). This prospective, observational real-world study included patients with hepatobiliary tumours, including patients with advanced BTC who had available HER2 testing results. The present analysis included 43 patients with advanced BTC and available HER2 testing results who participated in this study between October 2020 and May 2025.

The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) histologically or cytologically confirmed unresectable locally advanced or metastatic BTC, including ICC, ECC and GBC; (3) HER2 status assessment by IHC, fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH), or next-generation sequencing (NGS); (4) life expectancy ≥3 months; (5) an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1; (6) the presence of ≥1 measurable lesion according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST version 1.1) criteria45; and (7) adequate cardiopulmonary, haematological, renal and liver function. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a histological or cytological diagnosis of combined hepatocellular cholangiocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, sarcoma, or neuroendocrine tumours; (2) previous treatment with HER2-targeting agents; (3) pregnancy (positive pregnancy test before medication); (4) a history of other active malignancies within 5 years prior to screening; (5) unhealed severe wounds, active ulcers, or untreated fractures; (6) active autoimmune disease or a history of autoimmune disease; (7) the presence of clinically significant gastrointestinal disorders; and (8) the presence of clinically significant cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases.

HER2 expression in BTC tumours was evaluated by IHC, FISH, or NGS. High HER2 expression was defined as IHC 3+ or IHC 2+ and positive for FISH, a HER2 copy number >6.0, or NGS evidence of HER2 copy number gain, and low HER2 expression was defined as IHC/FISH 2+/-, 1+/+, 1+/-, or 0/+.3,18,20

Procedures

Patients were divided into two treatment cohorts: cohort 1 received a combination of a HER2 inhibitor and lenvatinib, while cohort 2 received a triple regimen of a HER2 inhibitor, lenvatinib and ICIs. The HER2 inhibitors used in the present study included disitamab vedotin and pyrotinib. Disitamab vedotin was intravenously infused at a starting dose of 1.5 mg/kg, followed by 2.0 mg/kg administered every 3 weeks. Pyrotinib was administered orally at a constant dose of 400 mg. Both drugs were given following confirmation of HER2 status by IHC, ISH, or NGS. Lenvatinib was administered orally once daily at a weight-based dose of 12 mg for patients weighing ≥60 kg and 8 mg for those <60 kg. ICIs were administered intravenously every 3 weeks. Dose schedules followed the specifications for each individual agent. For each patient, we documented treatment timelines, drug dosages, cycle numbers, laboratory parameters, imaging evaluations and AEs. Treatment responses were assessed by CT or MRI at baseline and every 6–8 weeks after each treatment cycle. Independent radiologists assessed treatment response according to RECIST v1.1. AEs were rigorously analysed using electronic medical records and follow-up documentation, with severity classified in accordance with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5.0).46 Management strategies for all AEs were determined by clinicians based on a comprehensive evaluation of three factors: each patient’s physiological status, AE severity and treatment response.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the ORR, defined as the proportion of patients who achieved either a complete response (CR) or a partial response (PR) according to RECIST version 1.1. Secondary endpoints included the DCR, PFS, OS and safety. DCR was defined as the proportion of patients who achieved CR, PR, or stable disease (SD) according to RECIST v1.1. OS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to death from any cause or the last known follow-up, whereas PFS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to radiologically confirmed disease progression or death.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics are summarised as the means with corresponding standard deviations (means ± SDs) for continuous variables or as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Data from in vitro and in vivo experiments are expressed as the means ± standard errors of the mean (SEMs). Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan‒Meier method, with intergroup comparisons assessed by log-rank tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.0 software or GraphPad Prism 8.0. Significance levels are shown as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and ns nonsignificant.
