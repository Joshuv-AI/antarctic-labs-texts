Cardiac point-of-care ultrasound detection of regional wall motion abnormalities optimizes clinical assessment in patients with acute myocardial infarction in the emergency department
Authors: Chuwen Tang, Jinhui Tang, Zhen Liu, Rui Gong, Hongyan Wei, Jinli Liao, Qingli Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate assessment of the patient’s condition in the emergency department (ED) is critical for individuals with acute myocardial infarction (AMI). In this study, point-of-care ultrasound (PoCUS) was utilized to evaluate ventricular wall motion in AMI patients in the ED, aiming to determine its clinical value in early risk stratification and condition assessment.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted by systematically collecting clinical data from patients who presented to the ED, were ultimately diagnosed with AMI, and underwent cardiac PoCUS examinations.ResultsA total of 143 AMI patients were enrolled in this study. Among them, 108 patients were identified as having regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMA). The proportions of patients with complete occlusion of the culprit artery, culprit artery thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) flow ≤ 2, and culprit artery TIMI flow ≤ 1 were significantly higher in the RWMA group compared to the non-RWMA group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that complete occlusion of the culprit artery and culprit artery located in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) were independent risk factors for RWMA. Patients with RWMA exhibited a markedly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and were at an elevated risk for the occurrence of new-onset arrhythmias and acute heart failure.ConclusionsRMWA was closely correlated with both the severity of coronary artery lesions and the incidence of adverse complications in AMI patients. Cardiac PoCUS assessment in the ED helps optimize clinical evaluation and management of AMI patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01567-y.Keywords: Point-of-care ultrasound, Regional wall motion abnormalities, Acute myocardial infarction, Risk factors, Disease severity assessment, Adverse complications

Introduction

Regional wall motion abnormalities (RWMA) is a hallmark of ischemic myocardial injury. Evidence from prior studies indicates that RWMA can be reliably detected by ultrasound after coronary blood flow occlusion [1, 2]. Notably, in some cases, ischemic RWMA may manifest before the onset of clinical symptoms and electrocardiogram (ECG) changes associated with coronary artery ischemia [3]. In cases where patients present with atypical clinical presentations or ambiguous ECG findings that cannot definitively confirm whether the underlying pathophysiology is AMI, further characterization through imaging studies, including evaluation of ventricular wall motion via ultrasound, may provide additional insights and aid in accurate diagnosis. Although ST-segment elevation (STE) has been used synonymously with acute coronary occlusion (ACO), current STE criteria miss nearly one-third of ACO cases and result in a substantial number of false catheterization laboratory activations [4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that cardiac PoCUS can identify occlusive myocardial infarction (OMI) earlier than standard evaluation and may expedite definitive management [5]. Clinically, it is imperative to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of patients suspected of having AMI for the accurate formulation of appropriate management plans. In the ED, cardiac PoCUS may provide critical imaging evidence to assist in the diagnosis and treatment planning for AMI. With the continuous advancement of ultrasound technology and the growing diagnostic proficiency among ED physicians, PoCUS has emerged as an increasingly acknowledged and indispensable diagnostic and evaluation tool for assessing cardiovascular emergencies in most hospitals.

Most previous cardiovascular ultrasound studies on AMI were conducted in the inpatient department and after the implementation of reperfusion therapy [6–9], which suggests that such studies may not have fully evaluated the important role of PoCUS during the early stage of AMI management. Thus, in this study, PoCUS examinations were conducted on patients in the ED who were suspected of having AMI, prior to undergoing reperfusion therapy, to evaluate their ventricular wall motion characteristics. Based on their clinical data, we investigated the influencing factors associated with RWMA and further assessed the potential value of cardiac PoCUS-based detection of RWMA in the initial assessment of AMI patients.

Materials and methods

Participant enrollment

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted in a tertiary general hospital. Approval from the Institutional Review Board was obtained from the study institution. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was waived (Ethical Approval No.: 2022 - LHQRMYY - KYLL − 003). The study population consisted of adult patients with chest pain who presented to the Chest Pain Center in the emergency department of our hospital between May 1, 2018, and May 31, 2022. The inclusion criteria were patients who met the diagnostic criteria for acute myocardial infarction according to the fourth edition of the Global Uniform Definition of Myocardial Infarction, published by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) in 2018 [10], and who underwent complete cardiac PoCUS examinations in the ED before receiving reperfusion therapy. Furthermore, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Being under the age of 18. (2) Existence or inability to rule out diseases that may cause abnormal ventricular wall motion, such as myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, old myocardial infarction, or complete left bundle branch block. (3) Cardiac PoCUS observations being compromised by external factors such as pneumothorax, fluid pneumothorax, or chest trauma. (4) The patient refused further diagnosis and treatment, including coronary angiography.

Data collection

Electrocardiogram (ECG) and cardiac PoCUS examinations were promptly performed by emergency physicians. Meanwhile, nurses obtained blood samples from the chest pain patients for laboratory testing. All PoCUS examinations were conducted and interpreted by three senior emergency physicians who had received standardized training (having completed the same cardiac PoCUS training program) and undergone unified calibration before the study. A uniform scanning protocol, standard imagig views, and consistent diagnostic criteria were strictly applied to minimize operator-dependent bias. Ventricular wall motion was evaluated using cardiac PoCUS through three steps: (1) Parasternal long-axis view (PLAX): to evaluate left ventricular (LV) anterior wall, interventricular septum, posterior wall, and mitral valve motion; (2) Parasternal short-axis view (PSAX): acquired at the mid-papillary muscle level to assess regional wall motion of the anterior, septal, inferior, and lateral LV walls; (3) Apical four-chamber view (A4C): to visualize wall motion of the apical, septal, lateral, anterior, and inferior LV segments. Wall motion score index (WMSI) [11]: Each segment of ventricular wall movement is scored. The left ventricle is divided into 17 segments, with WMSI = the sum of each segment score/17. The following scoring system is recommended: (1) normal or hyperkinetic, (2) hypokinetic (reduced thickening), (3) akinetic (absent or negligible thickening, e.g., scar), and (4) dyskinetic (systolic thinning or stretching, e.g., aneurysm). The Simpson method was uniformly adopted for measurement of LVEF.

The relevant clinical data were collected, including the general conditions of the patients, medical history, and related examination results (including electrocardiogram, high-sensitive cardiac troponin (hs-cTnI) (Pathfast POCT, detection range 0–50 ng/mL), coronary angiography results, effects of interventional treatment, myocardial enzymes, and blood biochemical test results, etc.). Myocardial enzymes and NT-proBNP were measured using the electrochemiluminescence method (Roche Company). The detection ranges are as follows: CK-MB 0–300 ng/mL, cTnT 0–10 ng/mL, and NT-proBNP 0–30,000 pg/mL. The relevant influencing factors of AMI include smoking history, obesity status, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Dyslipidemia specifically refers to the abnormal increase in the levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), or low-density lipoprotein (LDL). A body mass index (BMI) greater than 28 is defined as obesity. The onset time of AMI is calculated as the time from the new onset or recurrence/aggravation of chest pain symptoms to the time of undergoing cardiac PoCUS examination in the emergency department. A positive result of hs-cTnI is defined as ≥ 0.02 ng/mL. Electrolyte abnormalities refer to abnormalities of sodium, potassium, and calcium ions that have an impact on cardiac electrophysiology.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using the SPSS 23.0 statistical software package. The quantitative variables were expressed as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), and the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison between groups. The categorical variables were presented as frequency with percentage, and the differences between groups were compared using the chi-square test or chi-square test with Yates’ correction, as appropriate. First, univariate analysis was performed to identify which clinical variables were potentially associated with RWMA or adverse complications. Subsequently, variables with P < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression analysis. To assess the clinical predictive values of WMSI, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn and the areas under the curves (AUCs) were calculated. The optimum cut-off values were defined based on their maximum Youden index. Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to examine the correlation between WMSI score and clinical characteristics. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 143 patients diagnosed with AMI and who underwent cardiac PoCUS examinations were included in this study. The patients’ ages ranged from 27 to 88 years (median, IQR: 55 years, 49–66). Among these patients, 122 (85.32%) were male and 21 (14.69%) were female. Of the AMI patients, 97 (67.83%) cases were classified as STEMI while 46 (32.17%) cases were categorized as NSTEMI. Furthermore, ED cardiac PoCUS identified RWMA in 108 (75.54%) patients. The mean emergency HEART score for the cohort was 7.07 ± 1.34 points. In terms of comorbidities, hypertension was present in 87 (60.84%) patients, diabetes in 45 (31.47%) patients, dyslipidemia in 125 (87.41%) patients, obesity in 5 (3.50%) patients, and a history of smoking in 94 (65.74%) patients. During the diagnostic and therapeutic period, acute heart failure occurred in 38 (26.57%) patients, and new-onset arrhythmias developed in 51 (35.66%) patients. The patients’ demographic data and baseline clinical characteristics were summarized and shown in the Supplementary Table-1.

RWMA is related to the severity of coronary artery lesions in patients with AMI

As shown in Table 1, the proportion of patients with STEMI in the RWMA group (74.07%) was significantly higher than that in the group without RWMA (48.57%), with a P value of 0.005. The detection rates of RWMA in STEMI and NSTEMI were 82.47% (82/97) and 60.87% (28/46), respectively. There was no significant correlation between RWMA and whether the first detection of hs-cTnI in patients was positive (P = 0.758). Meanwhile, there was also no significant association between RWMA and the onset time of patients (whthin 2/4/6 hours) (all P values > 0.05). The above results indicate that for medium- and high-risk chest pain patients with a negative first detection of hs-cTnI, detecting the presence of RWMA may be helpful in assisting the diagnosis of AMI. In addition, RWMA can be detected in the early stage of the patient’s onset and does not show obvious time dependence, which provides potential value for the early identification of AMI.

Compared with the non-RWMA group, the proportions of complete occlusion of the culprit artery, culprit artery TIMI flow ≤ 2, and TIMI ≤ 1 in the RWMA group were significantly higher (all P values < 0.05). In addition, the proportion of the LAD artery as the culprit vessel in the RWMA group was significantly higher than that in non-RWMA group (53.70% vs. 25.71%, P = 0.004). However, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of culprit left circumflex (LCX) artery, culprit right coronary artery (RCA), multiple coronary artery lesions, and collateral circulation formation (all P values > 0.05). Moreover, no significant differences were observed between the groups regarding clinical factors such as age, gender, smoking history, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, dyslipidemia, and electrolyte disorders (all P values > 0.05).

Complete occlusion of the culprit artery and culprit LAD artery were the independent risk factors for RWMA in AMI patients

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that complete occlusion of the culprit artery and culprit LAD artery were the independent risk factors for RWMA in AMI patients. The ORs were 5.610 (P < 0.01) and 3.624 (P = 0.017), respectively. However, STEMI, culprit artery TIMI flow ≤ 2, culprit artery TIMI flow ≤ 1, and hypertension did not emerge as independent risk factors for RWMA, as illustrated in Fig. 1A.

As shown in Fig. 1B, a and b, the ROC curves demonstrate that WMSI has moderate discriminatory ability for predicting complete occlusion of the culprit artery or the culprit LAD. The optimal WMSI threshold for predicting complete occlusion of the culprit artery was 1.059, yielding a sensitivity of 46.9% and a specificity of 87.2%, with an AUC of 0.703 (95% CI: 0.610–0.797). The AUC of WMSI for predicting the culprit LAD artery was 0.688 (95% CI: 0.601–0.775), and the threshold value was 1.147 (sensitivity of 67.5%, specificity of 61.8%).

The peak levels of CK-MB, cTnT, and NT-proBNP levels in AMI patients with RWMA were significantly elevated, and the ED-LVEF value was markedly reduced

As shown in Table 2, the ED-LVEF value of AMI patients with RWMA was significantly lower compared to those without RWMA. The peak CK-MB, peak cTnT, and peak NT-proBNP levels were significantly higher in the group with RWMA than in the non-RWMA group (all P values < 0.01). Additionally, the first hs-cTnI value of AMI patients with RWMA was also higher than that of the group without RWMA, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05).

AMI patients with RWMA had a higher risk of developing new-onset arrhythmias and AHF

As shown in Table 1, AMI patients with RWMA were more likely to develop new-onset arrhythmias and AHF during the diagnosis and treatment compared with the group without RWMA (all P values < 0.05). The multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that the occurrence of RWMA was an independent risk factor for AHF or new-onset arrhythmias in patients with AMI, with odds ratios (ORs) of 4.464 (95% CI: 1.298–15.354) and 3.186 (95% CI: 1.210–8.386) respectively, as presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. The WMSI score in the AHF group was markedly higher than that in the non-AHF group (P < 0.001); however, no significant difference was observed in WMSI scores between the new-onset arrhythmia and non-new-onset arrhythmia groups (P = 0.383), as shown in Table 3. Furthermore, WMSI demonstrated a moderate predictive efficacy in predicting whether AMI patients would develop AHF. The optimal threshold for predicting AHF was 1.206, yielding an AUC of 0.726 (95% CI: 0.628–0.824), with a sensitivity of 73.7% and a specificity of 62.9%, as illustrated in Fig. 1B, c.

Discussion

Biomarkers such as CK-MB, cTnT, and NT-proBNP can assist in the diagnosis of AMI and the assessment of patients’ prognosis. There is a certain correlation between higher peak concentrations of these biomarkers and poor prognosis of coronary patients [12–14]. Our study found that the peak values of myocardial markers CK-MB and cTnT in AMI patients with RWMA were significantly higher than those in patients without RWMA. WMSI was significantly positively correlated with them. The ED-LVEF value in the RWMA group was significantly lower than that in the non-RWMA group, and WMSI was significantly negatively correlated with the ED-LVEF value of AMI patients. In addition, this study found that patients in the RWMA group were more likely to develop new-onset arrhythmia and AHF, and the WMSI had clinical application value in predicting the occurrence of heart failure. Previous studies have also pointed out that LVEF and WMSI have a certain predictive efficiency on the occurrence of adverse cardiac events [6, 15]. Therefore, our findings suggest that AMI patients with RWMA may have a more severe clinical condition and a worse prognosis. These patients require early intervention and intensified monitoring. The use of cardiac PoCUS for detecting RWMA is beneficial for emergency physicians to accurately assess the condition of AMI patients and subsequently take appropriate treatment measures.

In patients with NSTEMI, the ECG often shows ST-segment depression, T-wave inversion, or transient ST-segment elevation; some exhibit nonspecific findings, and a subset may even have a completely normal initial ECG. The absence of electrocardiographic evidence of ischemia does not exclude ACS [16]. The underlying mechanisms for this phenomenon are as follows: when the myocardial ischemia is located in the posterior wall or right ventricle, the corresponding areas are not covered by the conventional 12 - lead ECG; the electrical activity abnormalities caused by micro - focal myocardial infarction are too weak to be captured by the conventional ECG; in patients with multi - vessel coronary artery disease, the electrical vectors generated by myocardial ischemia in different regions cancel each other out, resulting in a “false - negative” ECG finding. It is worth noting that, as the ECG of these patients fails to reflect the actual damage to the myocardial structure, detecting RWMA by PoCUS serves as a crucial supplementary diagnostic method. PoCUS, combined with clinical symptoms and high-sensitivity troponin testing, can improve early NSTEMI diagnosis and reduce missed diagnoses.

Occlusive thrombosis leads to complete ischemia and subsequent necrosis in the area supplied by the occlusive artery [17]. In the reperfusion era, STEMI is commonly used as a synonym for acute complete coronary occlusion (ACO). However, previous studies have shown that approximately 25.5%-39% of NSTEMI patients also suffer from ACO [18, 19]. Evaluating whether AMI patients with RWMA through ultrasound examination may aid in the identification of these patients [5]. In this study, we found that the prevalence of RWMA in ACO patients was significantly higher than that in non-ACO patients, and complete occlusion of culprit artery was one of the independent influencing factors for RWMA. The coronary angiography revealed that the LAD artery was the most frequent culprit artery in both STEMI (54.8%) and NSTEMI patients (40.6%). However, the LCX artery was more frequently involved in NSTEMI patients (28.1%) compared to STEMI patients (4.7%; P < 0.001) [20]. Our study demonstrated that the higher WMSI score has moderate predictive value for complete occlusion of the culprit artery or the LAD being the culprit vessel. Furthermore, the LAD being the culprit vessel was identified as an independent risk factor for RWMA. This may be attributed to the fact that the anterior wall is predominantly supplied by the LAD, whereas other ventricular walls receive a combined blood supply from the LAD and LCX, or the LCX and RCA. Compared with patients with LCX and RCA culprit lesions, patients with LAD culprit lesions tend to have a larger infarction area. Thus, these results imply that the occurrence of RWMA is associated with the degree of coronary occlusion, myocardial damage, and the site of the infarction determined by the culprit vessel. Nevertheless, we would like to indicate that cardiac PoCUS remains valuable in RCA and LCX-related AMI. It can detect inferior, posterior, or lateral RWMA suggestive of RCA/LCX involvement, evaluate right ventricular function, and detect complications.

Previous studies on the impact of coronary artery occlusion on ventricular wall motion have found that RWMA was observed by ultrasound examination were 12 ± 5 s and 19 ± 8 s, respectively. It progressed within 1 h, but there were no significant changes in the range and amplitude of ventricular wall motion abnormalities in the following 6 h [1, 2]. Racz et al. [21] reported that the severity of abnormal ventricular wall movement did not correlate significantly with the time elapsed after the onset of chest pain symptoms in AMI patients. Additionally, rapid reperfusion therapy can significantly improve the recovery of abnormal myocardial motion in patients with AMI [17]. Our study also found that the occurrence of RWMA in AMI patients was not related to the time elapsed since the onset of clinical symptoms. These findings indicate that RWMA can occur shortly after coronary artery occlusion. Compared with conventional serum myocardial markers, the detection of RWMA in the ED does not exhibit significant temporal dependence, making it a valuable tool for the early detection and treatment of AMI patients, thus improving the prognosis of patients. AMI patients may only need to complete one cardiac PoCUS assessment during emergency visits, rather than frequent dynamic reexaminations like electrocardiograms and myocardial enzymes. To some extent, this also means that cardiac PoCUS may not impose a significant additional economic burden on these patients.

There may be gender-specific differences in the underlying pathophysiology of AMI, accompanied by variations in symptom presentation [22, 23]. Cumulative evidence indicates that advanced age, smoking, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerotic diseases are significant risk factors for the development of AMI [24–26]. Electrolyte imbalances can disrupt myocardial action potential propagation, yet research on their impact on ventricular wall motion remains limited. Shiraz et al. [27] reported that electrolyte imbalances (mainly potassium and sodium) can affect the distribution and occurrence frequency of RWMA in STEMI patients. However, our research results found that these comorbid factors were not independent risk factors for RWMA in patients with AMI. Meanwhile, it was observed that electrolyte imbalances had no pronounced influence on the emergence of RWMA. Thus, our study demonstrated that the incidence of RWMA in AMI patients is closely linked to coronary artery occlusion, rather than being significantly affected by comorbidity factors. This highlights the broad applicability of PoCUS for detecting RWMA and underscores its importance in evaluating conditions and developing treatment strategies for AMI patients. Of course, larger-scale studies need to be carried out to further verify the above conclusions in the future work.

There are limitations in our study. Firstly, it is a single-center, retrospective cohort study, which may impose constraints on the efficiency of statistical analyses and potentially increase the risk of bias in the research findings. Secondly, PoCUS examination depends on the operator. In this study, no formal assessment was conducted on the inter - observer or intra - observer variability. However, we carried out unified training and adopted the same operating procedures and diagnostic criteria to reduce such biases. Thirdly, as all study participants were recruited from a single medical institution, this may limit the generalizability and external validity of our findings. Nonetheless, our study has preliminarily revealed the necessity of using PoCUS in the emergency department to rapidly assess whether there is RWMA in medium- and high-risk chest pain patients or in AMI patients. This may be conducive to a more accurate assessment of the condition of AMI patients. In the future, multi-center, large-sample, and prospective cohort studies (with inter- and intra-observer variability analyses) may be needed to further verify its clinical application value in the management of AMI patients.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that RWMA is closely associated with the severity of coronary artery lesions and the development of adverse complications in patients with AMI. These findings underscore the substantial clinical utility of cardiac PoCUS in detecting RWMA for evaluating AMI patients in the ED and support more informed decision – making, including earlier reperfusion therapy, complication assessment, risk stratification, and adjustment of monitoring intensity.
