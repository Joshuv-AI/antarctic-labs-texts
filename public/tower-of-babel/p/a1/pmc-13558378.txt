Palliative Care Co-management at a Liver Transplant Center: A Mixed Methods Study
Authors: Lisa X. Deng, Lisa Catalli, Sara Miller, Courtney Sherman, Jennifer C. Lai, Kara E. Bischoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsDespite significant morbidity and mortality, patients with cirrhosis are infrequently referred to palliative care (PC). We developed a hepatology-PC collaborative care program to improve access to PC and quality of care for patients with cirrhosis.MethodsThis mixed methods cohort study included adult patients with cirrhosis who were referred to outpatient PC between 2021 and 2023. Rates of advance care planning (ACP) and health care utilization six months before and after PC program initiation were compared. Clinicians participated in a focus group and completed a survey to provide feedback on the program.ResultsA total of 41 patients (44% female, median age 61) were seen by both hepatology and PC teams. A PC physician, nurse, social worker, and/or spiritual care provider addressed non-pain symptoms (81%), ACP (68%), mood (63%), pain (59%), family/caregiver support (34%), social needs (24%), and spiritual concerns (15%). With PC, patients had increased ACP note completion (34% vs. 95%, p < 0.001), surrogate decision maker identification (46% vs. 83%, p < 0.001), and code status documentation (64% vs. 78%, p = 0.03). There were no differences in rates of non-elective hospitalizations or emergency department visits from six months before to six months after PC initiation. In a survey of clinicians who participated in the program (n = 11), all clinicians recommended continuing the co-management program and for other health systems to develop similar collaborations.ConclusionsHepatology-PC co-management is feasible and positively received by hepatology and PC clinicians alike. Outpatient PC addresses needs in multiple domains for patients and caregivers and is associated with increased rates of ACP.

Introduction

Cirrhosis is a progressive, debilitating disease with variable trajectory, and liver transplantation is an option for only a minority of patients. Patients with cirrhosis frequently experience significant symptom burden, psychosocial distress, and complex medical decision-making [1]. High health care utilization is common across the disease course, including at the end of life [2].

Palliative care—distinct from hospice—is interdisciplinary, holistic care focused on improving quality of life of patients with serious illness and their families across the disease continuum. Emerging evidence demonstrates that palliative care can improve symptom burden, advance care planning (ACP), and health care resource utilization in patients with cirrhosis [3–8]. Accordingly, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases practice guidance recommends considering palliative care at all stages of liver disease, including patients awaiting liver transplantation [9]. Despite these recommendations, timely referral of patients with cirrhosis to palliative care is lacking [10, 11].

Outpatient palliative care is particularly well positioned to partner with hepatology to support patients and families throughout the trajectory of cirrhosis. Hepatologists frequently identify time constraints as a major barrier to addressing palliative care needs particularly in the outpatient setting [12, 13]. Palliative care provides an interdisciplinary approach to support the multidimensional needs of both patients and families—particularly psychosocial needs—that may exceed the bandwidth of hepatology teams, while also bringing expertise in complex symptom management. Longitudinal relationships further facilitate iterative ACP with health status changes. However, there is limited data on outpatient hepatology and palliative care co-management models and their impact on patient outcomes and clinician experience. We describe the implementation and outcomes of a palliative care co-management program at a liver transplant center.

Methods

We conducted a mixed methods cohort study comprised of adult patients with cirrhosis who were co-managed by hepatology and outpatient palliative care through a collaborative care program at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF). Patients with cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma who were seen by both hepatology and palliative care teams for at least one visit between 6/1/2021 and 7/30/2023 were included in the study cohort. The UCSF institutional review board waived the requirement for informed consent because this program was undertaken primarily for quality improvement purposes.

The co-management program was developed by hepatology and palliative care clinicians through an iterative process in response to the unmet needs of patients with cirrhosis and historically low rates of referral to outpatient palliative care. Hepatology and palliative care clinicians first jointly established guidelines for outpatient palliative care referral: patients with cirrhosis and/or hepatocellular carcinoma and (1) uncontrolled physical or psychological symptoms, (2) need for additional help to make decisions about their care, and/or (3) exceptional psychosocial or spiritual distress in patient or family caregivers. Systematic screening of general and transplant hepatology clinics was then performed to identify potential candidates for palliative care. The four hepatology clinicians who participated in the collaboration received weekly lists of scheduled clinic patients who might benefit from palliative care. However, the decision to refer was ultimately up to hepatology providers’ discretion, taking into account patient preferences gleaned from discussion with the patient.

Patients referred to palliative care were seen by the UCSF Outpatient Palliative Care Service (OPCS) and received usual care in accordance with established OPCS practices. The OPCS is dedicated to patients with non-cancer serious illnesses and is comprised of palliative care physicians, a registered nurse, social workers, and a spiritual care provider. During clinic visits, patients and family members are seen by multiple members of the interdisciplinary OPCS team to address concerns such as symptoms, ACP/goals of care needs, and/or psychosocial or spiritual concerns. The follow up interval typically ranges from every four to twelve weeks, depending on the acuity of needs. Between visits, palliative care clinicians often communicate with patients/families (by phone or secure messaging) to support ongoing care, such as following up symptoms or assisting with care coordination. The palliative care social workers and spiritual care provider also frequently meet individually with patients or family members between scheduled team visits.

A cornerstone of the collaborative care model was frequent communication between hepatology and palliative teams about patient care, including through messages in the electronic health record (EHR) and routing of electronic notes after encounters. Joint conferences were also held to review the referral process and identify barriers to referral, such as discussing patients who met screening criteria but were not referred. Select shared patient cases were also discussed to address clinical issues through multidisciplinary discussion.

Demographic and clinical characteristics, number and characteristics of palliative care visits, and ACP documentation were abstracted from the EHR. Non-elective hospitalizations (excluding hospitalization for liver transplant), 30-day hospital readmissions, and emergency department visits 6 months before and 6 months after palliative care initiation were also abstracted from the EHR.

A focus group with involved clinicians was conducted to understand experiences with the collaboration and to identify opportunities for improvement. Semi-structured questions were adapted from previously published work in patients with interstitial lung disease and refined by the authors through consensus process [14]. The focus group was conducted virtually, recorded with permission, and transcribed verbatim for analysis. Clinicians also completed a survey about their satisfaction with the co-management model.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were performed to examine the distribution of clinical and demographic characteristics of the cohort. Rates of ACP and health care utilization before and after palliative care co-management program initiation were compared using Wilcoxon rank sum tests. An alpha of < 0.05 was utilized to determine statistical significance. Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for MAC, version 27.

Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize clinician responses to the survey questions. The focus group transcript was analyzed using Rapid Qualitative Analysis to identify themes and immediate action items for improving the co-management model [15–17]. Representative quotes were selected for each theme. Qualitative data was used to contextualize and interpret quantitative results related to clinician experience and observed outcomes.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 44 patients referred from general and transplant hepatology clinics were seen for at least one visit in the palliative care clinic during the study period. Three patients were excluded due to lack of cirrhosis diagnosis. Of the 41 patients included in the analyses, 18 (44%) were female and median (interquartile range [IQR]) age was 61 (52–70) years (Table 1). The median (IQR) time from palliative care referral to chart review or patient death was 16 (8–27) months. At the time of palliative care referral, the median (IQR) MELD 3.0 was 17 (12–22), and 31 (76%) patients were Child Pugh Class B or C. The most common etiologies of cirrhosis were alcohol related liver disease (27%), Hepatitis C (20%), and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (17%). Eight (20%) patients had a diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma. Regarding complications from cirrhosis, 28 (68%) had ascites, 33 (81%) had hepatic encephalopathy, and 17 (42%) had a history of variceal bleeding at the time of palliative care referral. Almost half of the sample (n = 17, 42%) were listed for liver transplant. Palliative Care Visits

The median (IQR) time from palliative care referral to first palliative care clinic appointment was 32 (22–52) days. Patients had engaged in median (IQR) 3 (2–8) palliative care clinic visits at the time of chart review, with almost half (n = 19, 46%) still being followed by palliative care. Only one patient was lost to follow up and was later identified as deceased. Most patients (90%) received outpatient palliative care exclusively via video telemedicine. Patients were most often referred to palliative care for non-pain symptoms (59%), mood symptoms (51%), and ACP (27%) (Table 2). However, the palliative care team often identified other needs in addition to the referral reason(s). A palliative care physician, nurse, social worker, and/or spiritual care provider addressed non-pain symptoms (for 81%), ACP (68%), mood symptoms (63%), pain (59%), family/caregiver needs (34%), social/practical needs (24%), and spiritual concerns (15%) (Table 2). The palliative care social worker provided counseling sessions to seven patients (17%) who had significant mood symptoms. ACP and Health Care Utilization

From the time of palliative care referral to the time of chart review, patients had increased rates of having an ACP note completion in the EHR (34% vs. 95%, p < 0.001), surrogate decision maker identification (46% vs. 83%, p < 0.001), and code status documentation (64% vs. 78%, p = 0.03) (Fig. 1). The proportion of patients who expressed a desire to limit life-sustaining treatments (were not full code) increased from 0 (0%) to 8 (20%) (p = 0.008). The number of patients who completed Physician Orders for Life Sustaining Treatment increased from 1 (2%) to 6 (15%), although this was not statistically significant (p = 0.06). The number of patients who had an advance directive on file increased from 6 (15%) to 7 (17%) (p = 1.0). Palliative care visits were directly linked to completion of new ACP documentation for 96% of patients, surrogate decision maker identification (87%), and code status documentation (33%). There were no differences in rates of non-elective hospitalizations (0.17/month/patient vs. 0.17, p = 0.89) or emergency department visits (0 vs. 0, p = 0.15) in the six months before compared to the six months after palliative care initiation. Of the 17 patients listed for transplant at the time of palliative care referral, 8 (48%) received liver transplantation. Nearly one-third (n = 13, 32%) of the cohort died during the study period. Of the patients who died, the median time from palliative care referral to patient death was 6 (IQR 3 – 9) months. Almost all patients (n = 11/12, 92%) were hospitalized within the last 30 days of life. A total of 7 (58%) patients utilized hospice and died at home, with median (IQR) length of stay of 7 (3–16) days. The remaining 5 patients (42%) died in the hospital. The date and location of death for one patient was not available in the EHR.

Clinician Focus Group

A total of 11 hepatology and palliative care clinicians participated in the focus group assessing experiences with the collaboration (Table 3). Participants expressed consensus that patients benefited from hepatology and palliative care co-management. In particular, clinicians highlighted that the interdisciplinary palliative care team helped to address complex psychosocial needs—a gap frequently unmet in the hepatology practice due to limited social work resources. For example, one hepatologist commented, “I feel like for some patients, especially those that are really struggling…, I don’t really have the training to do that, as much as I would like to be able to do everything for them. So it’s been really nice thinking, There’s this extra thing [palliative care] that I think would really help you. It would help your family navigate through all of this and process it and what it means for you.” Some patients developed such strong relationships with the palliative care team that they contacted palliative care first for symptoms or hospitalizations, which was sometimes appropriate and helpful but other times complicated care for those listed for transplant because hepatology providers wanted to know about those issues. In response, the teams created guidelines to determine which team should be contacted for specific issues and designed a workflow to efficiently route messages to the appropriate team member. Clinicians noted that patients experienced a high symptom burden and palliative care helped to support these patients, though limited evidence-based treatment options in cirrhosis exist at this time. Pain management was identified as particularly challenging, especially given transplant-related restrictions on opioid use and the risk of adverse effects of opioids in cirrhosis. For example, one clinician commented, “She has severe muscle cramps and abdominal pain…. When she was taking opioids she was getting more encephalopathic…. [Palliative care] did a great job with finding solutions for her and making [the patient] feel like we were entertaining all kinds of options to support her and manage her symptoms.” While a palliative care clinician mentioned, “There’s a huge symptom burden in people with cirrhosis and there’s a paucity of… research on what to do about it.”

For liver transplant candidates, clinicians were of the opinion that palliative care visits provided a setting apart from the transplant clinic where patients could voice emotional distress and concerns without fear of jeopardizing their candidacy. A clinician commented, “Both patients and caregivers are reticent to express some of that distress [to their hepatologist] because they don’t want it to be interpreted as ‘This person can’t manage a transplant or be listed’—the consequences of that feel very different in hepatology visits than they do with us… One of the ways in which we add to the care for the patient and their family is giving space… that feels safe because it’s not tied to transplant candidacy.” Another clinician highlighted the value of referring patients for ACP, explaining that “if [the liver transplant] doesn’t happen, [I want] it to not come as a shock and for there to be preparation and conversation.”

Clinicians emphasized that the co-management program was helpful to them as well as to patients and care partners, “Palliative care is… not just the extra layer of support for the patient and the caregiver. It’s the extra layer of support for me as a provider. I actually find that the biggest impetus to bring in palliative care is actually when I need help.” Clinicians also emphasized that providing optimal co-management remains a learning process for both teams, for example saying, “Open lines of communication are really key because it can be difficult to figure out, in particular with this patient population, exactly what are the issues that hepatology manages and what are the issues that palliative care manages… There can be complex symptom management with an evolving evidence base.”

In the clinician survey, on a scale of 0 (least) to 10 (most), clinicians reported high personal satisfaction with the co-management model with median (IQR) score of 9 (7–10). Clinicians also reported high amount of patient benefit with median (IQR) score of 9 (7–10). They rated the collaboration a success with median (IQR) score of 9 (7–9), and all clinicians recommended continuing the co-management program.

Discussion

In this mixed methods study, we found that palliative care co-management addressed a broad spectrum of patient and caregiver needs, with positive impact on rates of ACP among patients with cirrhosis. The co-management model was feasible to implement, even in the liver transplant setting, and was positively received by hepatology and palliative care clinicians alike.

Most patients referred to outpatient palliative care in this study had Child Pugh Class B or C cirrhosis. Nearly one-third of the sample died during the study period, underscoring the poor prognosis associated with cirrhosis. It is worth noting that a subset of patients with compensated cirrhosis were also referred and almost half of the patients were listed for liver transplant at the time of palliative care referral. This suggests that, contrary to common perceptions, palliative care is not only appropriate for patients at the end of life but can also offer benefits throughout the disease course, regardless of transplant listing status [9]. Almost all palliative care visits occurred virtually, underscoring the role of video-based telemedicine in improving access to palliative care and facilitating close follow-up through mitigating travel barriers related to debilitating symptoms, geographic distance, and financial constraints [18].

Outpatient palliative care addressed a wide spectrum of symptoms in the cohort including pain and non-pain symptoms. Patients with cirrhosis experience a substantial symptom burden, and emerging evidence suggests that palliative care interventions can improve symptom management in this population [1, 4, 7, 8, 19]. In particular, more than half of patients experienced mood symptoms, corroborating prior data demonstrating high prevalence of anxiety and depression among patients with cirrhosis and its negative impact on health-related quality of life [20]. The palliative care social worker conducted counseling sessions with patients with severe mood symptoms, highlighting a benefit of the interdisciplinary palliative care team to support mental health and coping needs. Furthermore, hepatology clinicians emphasized the benefit patients received from the palliative care social worker to address complex psychosocial needs, a resource which was lacking within the hepatology clinic.

With palliative care co-management, virtually all patients in the study engaged in ACP discussions. Prior studies have shown that patients with cirrhosis rarely engage in timely ACP and frequently have limited understanding of their prognosis, contributing to greater use of life-sustaining treatments and low rates of hospice enrollment [21, 22]. These challenges are also evident among patients awaiting liver transplantation, where focus on curative therapy may delay conversations about goals of care [23]. Our findings highlight the potential of proactive ACP within a co-management model, which may help overcome barriers such as limited time during hepatology clinic visits and discomfort with these discussions [13]. Initiating ACP earlier in the outpatient trajectory may better prepare patients and families for the uncertainty of liver disease progression, while ensuring that treatment decisions remain aligned with their values and preferences.

Previous studies have shown that outpatient palliative care referral can improve health care utilization among patients with cirrhosis [24–26]. This was not observed in our study. However, more than half of patients in the study were listed for transplant, and in clinical practice, these patients are commonly referred to the emergency department for symptoms by the hepatology team. Additionally, due to small sample size, we were likely underpowered to find any significant differences in health care utilization.

The focus group identified many strengths of the collaboration with benefits for patients and clinicians alike. Co-management enhanced care for patients with cirrhosis and benefited hepatology clinicians by addressing complex symptom, psychosocial, and ACP needs that are often unmet in the hepatology setting due to limitations in resources, time, or expertise. Clinicians also described co-management as a bidirectional learning process that requires time to refine and optimize. This is a key insight as both hepatologists and palliative care clinicians report gaps in training and knowledge related to each other’s domains, highlighting the need for cross-disciplinary education to improve quality of care [12]. Opportunities for improvement include communicating explicitly about prognosis to ensure a shared understanding across teams, clarifying roles and responsibilities within the co-management framework, and instituting regular case conferences to promote practice evolution. Clinicians highlighted the paucity of data in guiding safe and effective symptom management for patients with cirrhosis and the need for further research to develop evidence-based strategies in this patient population.

While we explored a co-management model between hepatology and palliative care clinicians, a recent randomized controlled trial showed that palliative care provided through monthly visits with trained hepatologists (who completed a 12-week online course) was non-inferior to care delivered by palliative care specialists [7, 27]. These findings are encouraging regarding the ability of hepatology clinicians to learn and apply palliative care skills. At the same time, given the limited supply of hepatologists and their time constraints, it may not be feasible for all hepatologists to engage in palliative care training or conduct frequent, extended visits. A hepatology-palliative care co-management model such as the one we describe may remain preferable in many care settings and warrants further exploration. Even in settings where the hepatologist provides palliative care, collaboration with specialty palliative care remains important for patients with complex needs or those who would benefit from interdisciplinary support.

Limitations of the study include small sample size and single center design, which may affect generalizability. The cohort is heterogeneous with possible selection bias and potentially unmeasured confounders. We did not conduct patient interviews or assess changes in symptom burden associated with palliative care initiation. The study was likely underpowered to detect significant differences in certain outcome variables such as healthcare utilization or POLST completion. Despite these limitations, these findings provide a real-world, pragmatic perspective on how palliative care can be integrated into clinical care for patients with cirrhosis. We demonstrated that a hepatology and palliative care co-management model was feasible, improved ACP rates, and was well received by clinicians. Future, larger, multicenter studies should examine the impact of palliative care interventions on patient reported outcomes and health care utilization among patients with cirrhosis.
