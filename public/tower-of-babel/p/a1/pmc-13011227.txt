Keeping the Air On: Energy Security and Social Work in European Home Respiratory Therapy
Authors: Eva María Gómez Pedrajas, Johanny Vargas Rodriguez, Salvador Díaz Lobato
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Energy poverty is increasingly recognised in Europe as a social determinant of health.1 The European Commission understands it as the inability of households to access essential energy services required for adequate heating, cooling, lighting, and a dignified standard of living.2 Yet a critical facet remains underappreciated: the effect of energy poverty on electricity-dependent home medical care, particularly in respiratory disease.

Across Europe, the shift towards home-based respiratory care has expanded the number of patients reliant on electrically powered devices – for example, oxygen concentrators, non-invasive ventilation, CPAP, cough-assist devices, and telemonitoring systems – whose safe and effective operation presupposes reliable, affordable electricity. In clinical follow-up, a non-trivial subset of vulnerable patients report markedly reducing use – or even discontinuing – prescribed therapy due to perceived electricity costs, fear of bill increases, and/or substandard home electrical installations. For clinically fragile patients with prolonged daily prescriptions (e.g., 16 h/day), these barriers can translate into sustained underuse and lost therapeutic effect.3

Framing this as “non-adherence” risks individualising what is, in essence, a structural constraint.4 Adherence in electricity-dependent respiratory therapies is partly determined by household economics and infrastructure. Recognising energy poverty as a structural determinant reframes the clinical task: beyond education and motivation, teams must systematically detect and address energy barriers.

We propose integrating an “energy vulnerability radar” into home respiratory care pathways, with healthcare social work playing a central role. This approach would include three main actions. First, brief screening to identify energy insecurity, arrears on energy bills, and electrical safety issues. Second, rapid-response measures that allow temporary adjustments to the prescribed therapy when needed – for example, short-term switches to cylinder oxygen, battery-backed systems, or reduced-power modes when clinically acceptable. Third, activation of a structured social-work pathway to coordinate community resources and secure patient entitlements. Depending on the country, this may involve priority service registers, disconnection moratoria for medically dependent patients, mediation for unpaid bills, or targeted tariff protections (such as Spain's bono social eléctrico). Ultimately, the aim is to ensure that a clinically appropriate prescription becomes a treatment that patients can realistically maintain at home.

Both within the internal organisation of clinical services and across the broader healthcare system, services should incorporate energy-aware discharge checklists; procure energy-efficient devices with transparent lifetime electricity costs; and report adherence and outcomes stratified by energy vulnerability. Data-sharing agreements with utilities and social services – within General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)-compliant frameworks – can enable proactive safeguarding (e.g., alerts for medically dependent households facing supply interruptions).

The proposed measures would entail minimal additional costs for healthcare services, particularly when linked to the use of energy-efficient devices and existing social-support structures. Furthermore, several countries – such as the United Kingdom and France – already operate protection schemes for electricity-dependent patients that offer practical reference models.

In conclusion, energy poverty functions as a structural determinant of adherence and continuity in home respiratory care. Systematic screening and integrated clinical–social responses – anchored by healthcare social work – can prevent avoidable interruptions, reduce inequities, and ensure that indicated therapies remain effective and safe where patients live.
