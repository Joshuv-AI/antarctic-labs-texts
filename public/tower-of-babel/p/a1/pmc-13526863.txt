Drought influence on carbon assimilation and water use efficiency in Mediterranean ecosystems
Authors: Odunayo David Adeniyi, Manuela Balzarolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increase of aridity in Mediterranean region limits the ecosystem ability in sequestering carbon and efficiency in using water. Using 20 years (2000–2020) of satellite-derived observations of gross primary productivity (GPP), canopy greenness (NDVI), crop water stress (CWSI), evapotranspiration (ET), and water-use efficiency (WUE), combined with the Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), we aimed to (1) identify the earliest drought-sensitive indicators and ecosystem-specific response times, (2) quantify cumulative sensitivities across vegetation types to establish resilience benchmarks, and (3) characterize the temporal propagation pathways through which thermal and physiological stress (CWSI) co-varies with changes in GPP, water flux, and ecosystem efficiency across vegetation types. CWSI is most sensitive satellite-based drought indicator, responding within 0–1 month across most biomes and detecting physiological stress before declines in GPP or NDVI. Croplands and grasslands showed the strongest and fastest responses (|R_max| up to 0.38 with lags of 1–3 months), whereas forests displayed weaker but delayed responses (lags of 4–8 months). The analysis of cumulative sensitivities revealed prolonged drought integration in semi-arid shrublands (6–10 months), intermediate durations in croplands and grasslands (2–4 months), and shorter durations in forests (1–3 months). These results provide an empirical basis for integrating CWSI into regional drought monitoring frameworks and inform adaptive management strategies for Mediterranean ecosystems under future climate extremes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54809-1.Keywords: Water use efficiency, Gross primary productivity, Normalized difference vegetation index, Evapotranspiration, Crop Water Stress Index, Lagged effect, Cumulative effectSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The Mediterranean region is one of the most sensitive regions to climate change globally1. With the ongoing decrease in precipitation and the increase of temperatures accompanied by an increase in potential evapotranspiration, the Mediterranean region is becoming progressively arid1,2. The increase of aridity together with the intensification and frequency of extreme heat waves and droughts strongly alters the structure and function of many ecosystems and their services. The lack of water limits the ecosystem ability in sequestering carbon, reduce soil fertility and crop production leading to economic loss3,4. Thus, an accurate assessment of ecosystem processes (e.g., photosynthesis, transpiration, water use efficiency) and their responses to drought is essential for building adequate ecosystem management and adaptation plans in Mediterranean region5–7.

The ecosystem response and resilience to disturbances (i.e., drought, heat waves) depends on how past conditions affect the current and future ecosystem functions (‘legacy effect’). Due to the complexity of the ecosystem response to disturbances, global ecological models face persistent challenges in capturing changes in ecosystem processes to quantify mass and energy fluxes8,9(e.g., Gross Primary Productivity—GPP; Actual evapotranspiration—ET), especially during drought6,10. Under projected climate change, drought events are expected to become more frequent, severe, and prolonged across many regions, driven by rising temperatures, shifts in precipitation regimes, and increased atmospheric evaporative demand11. Hence, the improvement of observation-based methods of assessing the impact of drought on ecosystem processes (e.g., GPP, transpiration, water use efficiency) is a major research challenge in ecosystem ecology and climate adaptation12,13. This limitation of process-based models highlights the need for observation-driven characterizations of drought propagation across carbon–water pathways, particularly at regional scales where ecosystem heterogeneity amplifies uncertainty.

Recent advances in remote sensing have enhanced the description of ecosystem processes and carbon uptake14, and the understanding of drought effects on ecosystem functions8. Remote sensing observation can provide spatially continuous information on ecosystem greenness, land surface temperature, and atmospheric water demand, making them particularly suited for characterizing drought propagation across heterogeneous landscapes. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI15 captures changes in canopy greenness and structural biomass, providing a proxy for the structural dimension of drought impact on vegetation. It reflects how drought progressively reduces photosynthetically active leaf area and canopy cover across ecosystem types. The Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI16 is derived from canopy–air temperature differences and is used to monitor plant physiological stress, enabling early identification of water limitation. It can be estimated from thermal infrared satellite observations, such as those provided by Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) at regional scale17. CWSI directly reflects stomatal regulation and canopy energy balance, it captures physiological water stress that may not yet be expressed in structural or productivity indicators. The ET integrates soil and plant water transfer to the atmosphere, linking vegetation processes to hydrological fluxes18. It captures the hydrological dimension of drought by quantifying how water deficits progressively constrain the transfer of water from the land surface to the atmosphere, bridging plant physiology and catchment-scale water balance. GPP quantifies carbon fixation through photosynthesis, estimated as the product of absorbed photosynthetically active radiation and light use efficiency19. It can be derived from satellite observations by integrating MODIS surface reflectance and vegetation indices with eddy-covariance measurements from FLUXNET using machine-learning approaches (e.g., FLUXCOM)20. GPP captures the carbon uptake dimension of drought, reflecting how water deficits suppress photosynthetic activity across vegetation types and timescales. The Water Use Efficiency is defined as the ratio of carbon gain to water lost (GPP/ET) and indicates the capacity of an ecosystem to maintain productivity under water limitation21. WUE bridges the carbon and hydrological components of the ecosystem response, serving as an integrative metric of drought-induced carbon-water decoupling. Shifts in WUE under drought therefore reflect the net outcome of simultaneous changes in carbon assimilation and water use across the carbon-water cascade.

Meteorological drought conditions can be quantified using the Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), which accounts for both precipitation deficits and atmospheric evaporative demand across multiple accumulation timescales. Two complementary analytical frameworks are used to characterize ecosystem drought responses. Lagged correlation analysis identifies the temporal delay between a drought event and its expression in ecosystem functioning, where short lags indicate fast-responding systems and long lags reflect hydraulic buffering or structural resilience22. Cumulative correlation analysis identifies the moisture deficit accumulation window that most strongly predicts current ecosystem state, capturing legacy effects and ecological memory, where long cumulative windows indicate systems where drought impacts persist and compound over time23. Together these frameworks address distinct but related dimensions of ecosystem vulnerability: how quickly drought affects ecosystem function, and how long drought must persist for maximum impact.

Research has shown that drought responses in ecosystem physiology and canopy greenness are tightly coupled but can differ in their sensitivity and timing, with physiological indicators such as gas exchange and water status often responding at different timescales than structural indicators such as greenness or biomass24. Global analyses using MODIS and Solar-Induced Fluorescence (SIF) data report reductions in GPP of 10–30% during droughts25,26, with NDVI typically lagging by 1–3 months in semi-arid regions27,28. Decline in ET commonly reflect soil moisture limitations, while WUE may increase under mild drought but declines sharply during prolonged or severe stress events29,30. In Mediterranean systems, regional studies reveal ecosystem-specific sensitivities: croplands can exhibit partial resilience through irrigation31, whereas forests and shrublands experience longer recovery periods linked to hydraulic constraints and shallow soils32. The CWSI has demonstrated value for early stress detection, frequently responding several weeks before NDVI33,34. Moreover, cumulative drought indices such as the SPEI capture delayed or legacy effects on GPP and WUE that can persist for several months35–37.

Despite these advances, important knowledge gaps remain. Most studies assess individual drought-sensitive variables independently, limiting understanding of how structural, physiological, hydrological, and functional responses interact to shape ecosystem resilience. Physiological stress indicators such as CWSI are rarely analyzed together with GPP, ET, and WUE at regional scale, despite their strong theoretical linkage through stomatal regulation and canopy energy balance38. Moreover, the timing (lagged responses) and duration (cumulative effects) of drought impacts are poorly quantified across Mediterranean vegetation types, particularly using harmonized satellite datasets and ecosystem-specific growing seasons. As a result, the mechanisms by which drought propagates across carbon–water pathways, that is from canopy temperature to transpiration, carbon uptake, and efficiency, remain insufficiently characterized for this climate-vulnerable biome.

This study addresses these gaps by assessing Mediterranean ecosystem productivity, water flux, and efficiency under drought using two decades (2000–2020) of high-resolution (0.05°) satellite observations in conjunction with the SPEI. We quantify the responses of GPP, NDVI, CWSI, ET, and WUE to varying drought severities and durations, evaluate their lagged and cumulative interactions, and characterizing the full carbon–water propagation cascade across different ecosystem types. Specifically, we aim to (1) identify the earliest drought-sensitive indicators and ecosystem-specific response times, (2) quantify cumulative sensitivities across vegetation types to establish resilience benchmarks, and (3) to characterize the temporal propagation pathways through which CWSI co-varies with changes in GPP, water flux, and ecosystem efficiency, and (4) to assess the sequence and timing of drought-driven carbon–water cascade across Mediterranean vegetation types. By integrating multiple indicators across Mediterranean ecosystems, this study provides an empirical framework for identifying early drought signals and quantifying resilience, thereby improving predictive capacity for ecosystem management under future climate extremes. Findings contribute to global efforts to developing adaptive strategies that sustain carbon–water balance, ecosystem services, and resilience in one of the world’s most climate-vulnerable regions.

Results

Drought response across Mediterranean ecosystems

Across Mediterranean ecosystems, standardized anomalies (z-scores) of carbon and water-related variables showed systematic differences between drought and non-drought periods during 2000–2020 (Table 1). GPP anomalies during drought were positive across all ecosystems (0.154–0.568), indicating values above the long-term ecosystem mean, while anomalies during non-drought periods were generally close to zero or slightly negative. As a result, the anomaly difference between normal and drought periods (i.e., Drought − Normal) were consistently negative, ranging from − 0.17 in cropland/natural mosaic (CNM) to − 0.61 in evergreen needleleaf forest (ENF) and evergreen broadleaf forest (EBF), reflecting lower productivity during drought relative to non-drought conditions. NDVI responses to drought varied among ecosystems. Positive NDVI anomalies during drought were observed in deciduous broadleaf forest (DBF; 0.12), mixed forest (MF; 0.23), woody savanna (WS; 0.40), and savanna (SAV; 0.20), whereas negative anomalies characterized closed shrubland (CS; −0.43), open shrubland (OS; −0.33), and cropland (CRP; −0.35). Minimum drought NDVI anomalies reached − 2.53 in CNM, indicating pronounced reductions in canopy greenness during extreme drought conditions. CWSI anomalies during drought were positive in most ecosystems (0.07–0.60), indicating elevated thermal and physiological stress relative to the long-term mean, except for DBF (− 0.06), MF (− 0.15), and WS (− 0.04), where drought anomalies were slightly negative. ET anomalies during drought were positive in most ecosystems (0.18–0.61), while OS was the only ecosystem exhibiting a negative drought ET anomaly (− 0.43). WUE drought-period anomalies were consistently positive across all ecosystem types (0.24–0.98), indicating that WUE exceeded its long-term growing-season mean during drought conditions, likely reflecting temporary increases in carbon gain per unit water lost under mild to moderate stress. Correlations with SPEI at the 1-month timescale were negative for GPP, ET, and WUE across all ecosystems (− 0.14 to − 0.45), indicating reductions in productivity, water flux, and efficiency with drought severity. NDVI–SPEI correlations ranged from moderately negative in DBF (− 0.16) to strongly positive in CS (0.33), highlighting ecosystem-specific greenness responses to metrological drought. CWSI–SPEI correlations were weakly negative (− 0.36 to − 0.03) except in ENF, where a slight positive correlation was observed (0.02). Together, these results demonstrate clear and spatially coherent drought–normal contrasts across the Mediterranean ecosystems, with pronounced ecosystem-dependent variability in z-score of productivity, greenness, thermal stress, evapotranspiration, and water-use efficiency during drought periods.

Lagged effects of drought on GPP, NDVI, CWSI, ET, and WUE

Drought response timing revealed a clear biome hierarchy across the Mediterranean: open herbaceous ecosystems responded most rapidly and strongly, while forests exhibited delayed but spatially widespread responses. Overall, drought impacts were strongest for NDVI (mean |R_max| = 0.34; mean lag = 3.2 months), ET (|R_max| = 0.33; lag = 3.3 months), and GPP (|R_max| = 0.33; lag = 4.4 months), followed by CWSI (|R_max| = 0.33; lag = 1.7 months) and WUE (|R_max| = 0.27; lag = 3.0 months) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1). Median |R_max| values converged near 0.32–0.33 for GPP, NDVI, ET, and CWSI, with slightly weaker responses for WUE.

Spatially, shorter response times and higher |R_max| values dominated the western Mediterranean, including coastal Iberia and western France, while lower correlations and longer lags were concentrated in the eastern Mediterranean. The central Mediterranean exhibited transitional behavior with mixed lag structures across vegetation types (Fig. 1). These patterns indicate that drought exerts strong and geographically coherent controls on ecosystem functioning across Mediterranean landscapes, with response timescales that differ systematically among vegetation types and functional indicators. Across the region, statistically significant lagged correlations were found in a large proportion of vegetated pixels, ranging from 61.7% for GPP to 81.8% for CWSI, demonstrating widespread drought sensitivity across the Mediterranean landscape (Supplementary Table S1).

At the ecosystem level, a systematic pattern emerges. Ecosystems with shorter lag times tend to show stronger correlations (higher |R_max|), while those with longer lags show weaker responses. This reflects two distinct ecological clusters, fast-responding, drought-sensitive systems (croplands, grasslands) at short lags of 1–3 months with high |R_max|, and slow-responding, hydraulically buffered systems (forests) at longer lags of 5–7 months with lower |R_max|. Shrublands and savannas displayed intermediate magnitudes and timing. These ecosystem-specific relationships between drought-response strength and lag duration are summarized in the lag–|R_max| space (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S1).

The distribution of lag months per ecosystem further resolved indicator-specific timing differences (Fig. 3). For GPP, deciduous forests showed the longest delays, with DBF and MF peaking predominantly at 8 months (57% and 44% of pixels respectively), while croplands showed a distinct 1-month peak (approximately 50%). Evergreen forests peaked at 5–6 months, with ENF at 5 months (24.6%) and EBF at 6 months (34.5%). For NDVI, early responses dominated herbaceous systems, with SAV (63.8%), GRA (53.8%), OS (≈ 32%), and CRP (≈ 47%) peaked at 1 month, while forests showed mixed peaks at 0–3 months. CWSI showed the shortest and most consistent lags, with most ecosystem types peaking at 0–1 month, including ENF, EBF, and OS, while CS was dominated by lag 0 (68%). ET followed a pattern, similar to GPP in forests, peaking at 5–8 months, but resembled NDVI in shrublands and grasslands, peaking at 0–3 months, and at 1–2 months in croplands. WUE showed fast responses across all ecosystem types, with forests peaking at 0–2 months, and grasslands, savannas, and croplands clustering within 0–3 months (full distributions in Supplementary Table S1).

Cumulative effect of drought on GPP, NDVI, CWSI, ET, and WUE

Cumulative drought sensitivity revealed a systematic pattern across Mediterranean ecosystems: semi-arid shrublands integrated drought stress over the longest time windows, while forests responded over short cumulative durations, and croplands and grasslands showed intermediate but strong responses. Across all ecosystems, NDVI and CWSI showed the strongest cumulative sensitivity (mean |R_max_cum| ≈ 0.35), though NDVI responded over slightly longer durations (~ 4.5 months) than CWSI (~ 3 months) (Supplementary Table S2). GPP and ET exhibited intermediate cumulative responses (mean |R_max_cum| ≈ 0.30–0.31) with average durations of ~ 4.8 months, whereas WUE showed weaker sensitivity (0.26).

Spatially, the strongest and most persistent cumulative effects concentrated in the southwestern, southern, and eastern Mediterranean (Fig. 4). GPP showed high |R_max_cum| values (> 0.30) and long cumulative durations of 6–12 months across the Iberian Peninsula and North Africa, corresponding to and predominantly negative correlations in forested regions (up to 98%) and moderate significant-pixel frequencies (≈ 52–66%). NDVI showed extensive cumulative responses across southwestern Europe, northwest Africa, Turkey, and the eastern Mediterranean, with strong correlations (> 0.40–0.60), cumulative durations of 3–7 months, and high significant-pixel frequencies (59–84%). CWSI exhibited the most spatially continuous sensitivity, with |R_max_cum| >0.40 dominating North Africa, southern Spain, and Turkey, very high significant-pixel rates (74–95%), short cumulative durations (2–4 months), and nearly universally negative correlations (> 90%). ET displayed strong cumulative responses along the southern Mediterranean rim, with |R_max_cum| values of 0.30–0.45, cumulative durations of 4–7 months. WUE showed more heterogeneous spatial patterns, with moderate cumulative effects (> 0.25) across southern Spain, northern Morocco, Algeria, and southern Italy, characterized by predominantly positive correlations in agricultural and grassland ecosystems (60–90%) and negative correlations in forested and shrubland systems (55–99%) (Fig. 5).

At the ecosystem-level, the cumulative durations distribution further resolved these contrasts (Fig. 6). For GPP, most forest ecosystems peaked at short cumulative durations (1–3 months), with MF showing an overwhelmingly 1-month response (84%), while semi-arid shrublands showed pronounced long-duration integration, with OS and CS peaking at 8–10 months (≈ 87–95% of pixels). Grasslands and savannas showed substantial peaks at 6–8 months, croplands displayed were dominated by 2–3-month durations (≈ 65%). For NDVI, responses were concentrated at intermediate durations of 2–4 months across most vegetation types, while OS and WS shifted toward longer-duration of 6–7 months. CWSI showed the fastest and most consistent cumulative responses, with nearly all ecosystems peaking at 1–3 months, including strong concentrations at month 2 for EBF (73%), GRA (51%), CRP (47%), and SAV (47%). ET showed a bimodal structure, with forests and woodlands peaking at 1–2 months and OS and CS peaking at 8–9 months (≈ 45% and 65% respectively), while grasslands and croplands retained early-duration maxima of 1–3 months despite broad distribution. WUE displayed mixed structures, with forests dominated by short responses of 1–3 months, and semi-arid ecosystems split between immediate and intermediate durations.

Ecosystem-level cumulative correlations reinforced this hierarchy (Fig. 6). Forests displayed relatively low cumulative correlations (|R_max_cum| ≈ 0.18–0.30) with short-to-intermediate durations of 1–6 months, dominated by negative correlations. Shrublands and savannas showed stronger correlations, with OS reaching |R_max_cum| up to 0.47 (NDVI) and cumulative durations of 7–9 months. Grasslands and croplands exhibited the strongest cumulative responses overall, with croplands showing the highest |R_max_cum| values across all variables and predominantly positive correlations for GPP and NDVI (> 93%) (full distributions in Supplementary Table S2).

Cumulative and lagged effects of water stress on GPP, NDVI, ET, and WUE

Cumulative and lagged correlations between CWSI and ecosystem indicators revealed consistent and spatially widespread water stress responses across Mediterranean ecosystems, with clear differences in response magnitude and timing among vegetation types (Figs. 7 and 8; Supplementary Tables S3–S4).

Scatter plot of mean cumulative duration (months) vs. mean maximum cumulative correlation (R_max_cum) between CWSI and indicators (GPP, NDVI, ET, WUE) across Mediterranean ecosystems (ENF, EBF, DBF, MF, CS, OS, WS, SAV, GRA, CRP, CNM) during 2000–2020.

Cumulative CWSI effects were strong across all ecosystems, with significant pixel coverage of 82.6–90.1% (Supplementary Figure S1). Regional mean cumulative correlations were highest for NDVI (|R_max_cum| = 0.63;), followed by GPP (0.58), ET (0.53), and WUE (0.41), with maximum values reaching 0.96, 0.95, 0.94, and 0.94 respectively. Mean cumulative durations were generally short (2.8–3.1 months), though ecosystem-specific integration windows showed substantial variability (Fig. 8). Croplands and grasslands exhibited the strongest cumulative sensitivities, with |R_max_cum| up to 0.70 for GPP, 0.74 for NDVI, and 0.63 for ET, over cumulative durations of 1.9–3.7 months. Forest ecosystems showed moderate cumulative responses (|R_max_cum| = 0.41–0.51) with durations of 2–5 months. Semi-arid shrublands displayed the longest integration periods, with CS showing cumulative durations of 6.3–6.7 months for NDVI and GPP, and OS exhibiting 5.5–5.8-month durations. Negative cumulative correlations were predominant in shrublands, where NDVI and GPP exceeded 90% negative correlations (Supplementary Fig. S2). WUE showed weaker and more variable cumulative responses, with short durations in CNM and CRP (1.9–2.8 months) but longer durations in CS and ENF (5.0 and 4.1 months respectively).

Lagged effects were more spatially more extensive than cumulative effects, with significant pixel coverage of 89.5–92.3% (Supplementary Figure S3, S4). Mean lagged correlations were highest for GPP (|R_max_lag| = 0.59), followed by NDVI (0.58), ET (0.56), and WUE (0.42), with maximum values reaching 0.96 for GPP, NDVI, and ET, and 0.92 for WUE (Fig. 7). Lag maxima occurred at longer delays than cumulative durations, with regional means of 3.7 months for GPP, 2.4 months for NDVI, 4.3 months for ET, and 4.8 months for WUE. Croplands and grasslands again showed the strongest lagged responses, with |R_max-lag| up to 0.71 for NDVI, 0.68 for GPP, and 0.62 for ET at short lags of 1.3–3.8 months, and NDVI frequently peaking at lag 0 in croplands (up to 80% of pixels). Forests ecosystems showed moderate lagged correlations (|R_max-lag| = 0.33–0.55) with longer lag maxima of 4–5 months, particularly in ENF and MF. Shrublands showed delayed lagged peaks, with CS and OS reaching 5–6 months for GPP and ET. WUE lagged responses were weaker overall, with shorter windows in CRP and CNM (2.2–4.8 months) and longer lags in forests and OS (4.2–5.5 months).

Comparing the two frameworks, croplands and grasslands consistently showed short cumulative windows and short lag delays, reflecting rapid and strong physiological coupling between canopy water stress and carbon-water indicators. Shrublands exhibited long cumulative windows and delayed lag maxima, indicating stress accumulation and persistent legacy effects. Forest showed moderate response magnitude with the longest lag delays across indicator. ET displayed both early and delayed peaks depending on ecosystem type, while WUE showed the weakest and most variable responses across both frameworks. Together, these patterns confirm clear and systematic contrasts in the timing and persistence of water stress propagation among Mediterranean ecosystem types.

Discussion

Drought lagged responses across ecosystems

Drought influences the carbon–water coupling by propagating physiological water stress through thermal, photosynthetic, structural, and hydrological pathways. The short lag times of CWSI across most ecosystem types, particularly CRP and GRA when correlated with the SPEI at 1-month timescale (Supplementary Table S1), are consistent with the near-instantaneous coupling of canopy temperature and vapor pressure deficit with stomatal closure under water-limited conditions39–43. This immediacy is particularly pronounced in CRP and GRA, where shallow rooting depth, high evaporative demand, and rapid canopy thermal response amplify stress signals44–47. The high prevalence of zero-lag correlations in CS and CNM further supports CWSI’s suitability for real-time physiological drought monitoring in water-limited environments.

The anomaly-based contrasts between drought and normal conditions provide essential context for these lagged responses (Table 1). Positive drought anomalies for GPP and ET across most ecosystems indicate that Mediterranean droughts often co-occur with anomalously warm, high-radiation periods, which can temporarily sustain carbon uptake and evaporative demand even as soil moisture becomes limiting. In contrast, strongly negative NDVI anomalies in shrublands and croplands, together with widespread WUE increases, are consistent with drought-induced reductions in transpiration and temporarily increases in water-use efficiency, a pattern demonstrated in controlled studies on seed plants under water stress48, and with ecosystem-scale reductions in ET under water deficit as documented in flux tower and satellite-based analyses49. These differing anomaly signatures foreshadow the ecosystem-specific lagged and cumulative propagation patterns observed in subsequent analyses, demonstrating how drought effects cascade across interconnected components of the carbon–water cycle.

Forest ecosystems exhibited longer lag responses of GPP and ET to both SPEI and CWSI, with lag maxima in ENF and EBF often exceed 4 months (Supplementary Tables S1, S4). These delayed responses are consistent with the buffering capacity attributed to deep rooting systems, hydraulic redistribution, and internal water storage in experimental and modelling studies50–53, though these processes were not directly measured in the present analysis. They likely reflect the capacity of forest canopies to maintain carbon assimilation during early drought stages, followed by more pronounced declines during prolonged water deficits54. Shrublands, particularly CS and OS, exhibited intermediate-to-long lags for GPP and ET (Supplementary Tables S1, S4), consistent with sclerophyllous leaf morphology, low hydraulic conductivity, and conservative water-use strategies55,56. Such lag heterogeneity across ecosystems highlights differential drought resilience mechanisms, from rapid physiological sensitivity in herbaceous vegetation to delayed yet sustained responses in woody systems.

Cumulative drought effects

Cumulative drought effects reveal additional dimensions of ecological memory and stress integration. OS and CRP exhibited the strongest cumulative correlations, with OS displaying long integration periods of up to 8.4 months for GPP and CRP showing short but intense responses of 1.9–3.5 months for GPP and NDVI. The persistence of cumulative effects in OS is consistent with slow hydraulic recovery and limited soil-water retention typical of Mediterranean shrublands23,51,57, while the rapid CRP response likely reflects acute carbon–water decoupling under high evaporative demand and irrigation dependence58,59. Forest ecosystems, especially DBF, showed shorter cumulative windows of 1.9–2.2 months, reflecting phenological constraints and reduced resilience under prolonged deficits, whereas ENF and EBF exhibited moderate but sustained responses linked to their hydraulic regulation capacity50–53. Across all ecosystems, significant-pixel fractions were exceptionally high, reaching up to 96% in shrublands and croplands, surpassing typical global semi-arid values23,60,61. This spatial coherence suggests that Mediterranean ecosystems exhibit strong regional synchrony in their drought responses, likely driven by shared climatic forcing and convergent stress pathways.

Drought indicators and ecosystem functioning

Joint evaluation of GPP, NDVI, ET, WUE, and CWSI revealed tightly coupled carbon–water dynamics under drought. Strong negative correlations between CWSI and GPP/NDVI (Supplementary Tables S3–S4) are consistent with water stress suppressing photosynthetic activity and canopy greenness through stomatal regulation62. The consistently stronger correlations under CWSI-based analysis compared to SPEI-based analysis reflect CWSI’s physiological grounding and its capacity to detect drought propagation at sub-monthly scales. WUE responses were mixed: positive anomalies and correlations in CRP and ENF suggest transient optimization of carbon gain under moderate stress61,63,64, whereas negative responses in CS and CNM reflect severe stress conditions where both carbon uptake and transpiration decline simultaneously58,59. These contrasting WUE behaviors reinforce that drought-induced changes in carbon–water coupling are highly context-dependent, shaped by rooting depth, phenology, and canopy structure.

Cross-indicator patterns suggest a coherent drought-propagation sequence in which water stress first manifests through canopy temperature (CWSI), then constrains carbon assimilation (GPP), followed by structural canopy decline (NDVI), and eventually alters water-use strategies (WUE). ET responses, which combine biophysical and atmospheric controls, exhibited both rapid and delayed components depending on ecosystem type, bridging immediate physiological adjustments with longer-term water limitations. Together, these linkages suggest that drought reshapes carbon–water coupling through both fast physiological pathways and slow ecological memory effects across multiple timescales. Spatially, southern Mediterranean agro-ecosystems showed rapid and strong drought responses, eastern shrublands showed prolonged cumulative integration, and northern forests exhibited delayed but buffered responses paralleling the region’s hydroclimatic gradient, from arid southern basins to more mesic northern forests.

The correspondence between CWSI, GPP, and NDVI dynamics supports the integration of CWSI into multi-indicator drought monitoring frameworks. Short CWSI lags in CRP and GRA (< 3 months) indicate that CWSI-based alerts could provide operationally relevant lead time for water management decisions in these ecosystems. Long cumulative windows in shrublands and forests (4–8 months) suggest that sustained monitoring over multi-month periods is necessary to capture the full drought exposure of woody vegetation. The specific design and effectiveness of management interventions based on these signals require dedicated field studies and management scenario analyses, which are beyond the scope of the present work.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The SPEI dataset operates at 0.5° spatial resolution, which is coarser than the 0.05° MODIS-derived variables, and may smooth over fine-scale hydroclimatic variability, particularly in topographically complex landscapes. CWSI from MODIS LST are sensitive to emissivity assumptions, atmospheric corrections, and within-pixel canopy heterogeneity65, and lack of widespread field validation across the Mediterranean limits assessment of sensor-specific biases. The cropland class includes both rainfed and irrigated systems, and irrigation may partially decouple cropland drought responses from meteorological SPEI signals, potentially attenuating measured drought sensitivity for this class. The static land cover approach, while consistent with comparable large-scale studies, does not account for land-cover transitions that may alter local drought sensitivities over the 20-year study period. Finally, the observational correlation-based design does not allow causal inference, and the mechanistic interpretations offered here remain plausible but untested hypotheses to be addressed in future process-based analyses. Future work should prioritize higher-resolution drought characterization, integration of eddy-covariance flux tower data for validation, sub-classification of croplands by irrigation status, and process-based or causal inference approaches to test the mechanistic hypotheses generated by this empirical analysis. Extending this framework to other semi-arid biomes will further improve understanding of drought-induced carbon–water decoupling globally.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive empirical assessment of drought propagation across Mediterranean ecosystems by jointly evaluating lagged responses, cumulative integration, and cross-indicator linkages among physiological and biophysical variables. Lagged and cumulative correlation analyses reveal clear biome-specific drought transmission pathways: rapid sensitivity in croplands and grasslands, prolonged stress accumulation in shrublands, and delayed but buffered responses in forests, reflecting differences in vegetation structure, rooting depth, and ecohydrological strategies. Cross-indicator patterns suggest a coherent propagation sequence in which thermal stress as captured by CWSI co-varies with subsequent reductions in carbon assimilation (GPP), structural canopy decline (NDVI), and shifts in water-use strategies (WUE), consistent with drought affecting carbon–water coupling across multiple timescales. CWSI showed strong correlations with GPP, NDVI, ET, and WUE, often exceeding those of SPEI, supporting its value as a physiologically grounded indicator within multi-variable drought monitoring frameworks. Collectively, these findings show that drought induces both immediate and persistent shifts in the balance between carbon assimilation and water use, with distinct temporal signatures across Mediterranean ecosystem types. To our knowledge, this is the first regional study to integrate CWSI alongside GPP, NDVI, ET, and WUE in a harmonized lagged and cumulative drought analysis across all major Mediterranean ecosystem types, providing an empirical framework for ecosystem-specific drought propagation signatures. These results support the integration of CWSI into regional drought monitoring systems and contribute to the development of evidence-based adaptation strategies for one of the world’s most climate-vulnerable regions.

Methods

Study region

This study focuses on the Mediterranean region, defined by a bounding box spanning longitudes −10–40°E and 30–45°N (Fig. 9), following the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) delineation for the Mediterranean basin66. The region’s high climatic variability, frequent droughts, and diverse species composition and land management practices make it an ideal area for evaluating drought impacts on ecosystem functionality. Land cover classes present in the selected region were classified using the MODIS MCD12C1.061 Land Cover Type product at 0.05° Climate Modelling Grid (CMG) product67, based on the International Geosphere–Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification. Eleven Mediterranean-relevant vegetation classes were retained: Evergreen Needleleaf Forest (ENF), Evergreen Broadleaf Forest (EBF), Deciduous Broadleaf Forest (DBF), Mixed Forest (MF), Closed Shrublands (CS), Open Shrublands (OS), Woody Savannas (WS), Savannas (SAV), Grasslands (GRA), Croplands (CRP), and Cropland/Natural Mosaic (CNM). Due to data availability, the 2001 land cover map was used for 2000. Annual land cover maps (2000–2020) were clipped to the study area, reprojected to the reference grid (EPSG:4326), and filtered to retain the 11 IGBP classes. A static land cover map was created by computing the mode of annual classifications per pixel to mask non-vegetated cover areas and exclude them (Fig. 9). The modal class approach assigns each pixel its dominant long-term vegetation type, minimizing misclassification from inter-annual land cover fluctuations and classification noise in MODIS annual products67,68. This approach prioritizes stability over dynamic tracking of land-cover change, which falls outside the scope of this study.

Identification of drought periods

Drought period were identified using the Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), which quantifies drought intensity by balancing precipitation and potential evapotranspiration over multiple timescales69. Positive SPEI values (> 0) indicate wet conditions, while negative values (< 0) denote water deficits. Monthly SPEI data at 0.5o resolution were obtained from the SPEIbase v2.10 dataset70. The SPEI dataset was bilinearly resampled from its native 0.5° resolution to the common 0.05° analysis grid, so that each vegetation pixel was assigned the SPEI value corresponding to its geographic location. SPEI data, calculated at 1- to 12-month timescales, were resample and reprojected to the reference grid (EPSG:4326) using bilinear interpolation, with invalid values (− 9999, 9999, 1e30) masked as NaN. Monthly SPEI values were spatially averaged over the study area to generate a regional time series (January 2000–December 2020).

Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) served as a proxy for vegetation greenness, status and growing, and it is commonly used to capture plant development and phenology15. Monthly NDVI were retrieved from the MOD13C1.061 Terra Vegetation Indices product at 0.05° resolution71. Cloud-free NDVI composites were used to describe seasonal and interannual vegetation dynamics. Growing seasons were defined for each IGBP class (Supplementary Table S5, Figures S5–S15), to focus analyses on growing season months, minimizing noise from dormant seasons72.

Crop water stress index (CWSI)

The Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI) assesses the vegetation water stress by comparing canopy temperature to the air temperature, reflecting physiological responses to water availability16,17. High CWSI values indicate elevated stress due to reduced transpiration and increased canopy temperature. CWSI was used to assess drought-induced stress and its interactions with GPP, NDVI, ET, and WUE. Monthly land surface temperature (LST) data were obtained from the MOD21C3.061 Terra LST and 3-Band Emissivity Monthly L3 Global 0.05° CMG product73, while 2-meter air temperature data were sourced from the ERA5-Land monthly reanalysis dataset74,75. The CWSI calculation, following the methodology of Jackson et al.16,17 is described in the supplementary material.

Actual evapotranspiration (ET)

Actual evapotranspiration (ET) represents the water flux component of ecosystem functionality. ET is primarily driven by atmospheric evaporative demand, including net radiation, temperature, and vapor pressure deficit, and is constrained under drought conditions when soil moisture limits transpiration through stomatal regulation76. As a result, ET integrates both atmospheric demand and soil water availability, making it a sensitive indicator of drought-induced plant water stress Monthly ET data. were obtained from the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model (GLEAM) v3.6a dataset77,78 at its native resolution of 0.25°, and subsequently resampled to a common 0.05° grid for analysis. GLEAM estimates actual evaporation using a Priestley–Taylor formulation for potential evaporation based on surface net radiation and temperature, which is then constrained by an evaporative stress factor derived from microwave vegetation optical depth and root-zone soil moisture estimates. GLEAM v3.6a spans the period 1980–2021 and uses MSWX radiation and air temperature forcing alongside ESA-CCI soil moisture77.

Gross primary productivity (GPP)

Monthly Gross Primary Productivity (hereafter named as GPP) was derived from the Global MODIS and FLUXNET-derived Daily Gross Primary Production, Version 2 dataset (MODIS-GPP-FLUXNET v2), produced by the NASA Carbon Monitoring System and distributed via NASA Earth data. The dataset integrates MODIS reflectance and vegetation indices, FLUXNET tower-based GPP from eddy covariance sites and machine learning upscaling using ensemble tree-based models to produce globally consistent GPP estimates at 0.05° resolution, corrected for sensor, climate, and biome-specific biases79,80. Monthly means were computed from daily data, and unrealistic values (e.g., < 0 or > 200 gC m⁻² d⁻¹) were masked.

Lagged and cumulative correlation analyses

The lagged and cumulative correlation analyses together provide a comprehensive understanding of ecosystem responses to drought. While the lagged correlation approach identifies the timing of ecosystem responses, that is, how quickly variables such as GPP, NDVI, ET, or WUE react to changes in drought conditions, the cumulative correlation approach captures the duration-dependent effects, revealing how prolonged water deficits integrate over time to influence ecosystem function.
