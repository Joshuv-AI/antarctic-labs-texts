Health status characteristics and aging determinants of centenarians in Colombia: a real-life expanded cohort profile description
Authors: Ivan David Lozada-Martinez, Sandra Milena Castelblanco-Toro, Juan Carlos Salazar-Uribe, Vanessa García Rudas, María José Diaz Gutiérrez, Ledys Daniela Montaño Vega, Juan-Manuel Anaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study of centenarians from a biopsychosocial perspective would allow for understanding the complex interactions among biological, psychological, and social factors, and their impact on defining a healthy aging phenotype in settings with specific conditions. The aim of this study was to describe the baseline characteristics of a centenarian cohort across demographics, clinical, functional, cognitive, and biological measures.MethodsThis report outlines the Colombian Centenarian Project and the variables used to assess their overall clinical status (including prior chronic diseases) and specific conditions (such as cognitive status and quality of life), as well as the biological variables assessed.ResultsA total of 160 centenarians were included, with a median age of 101 years; 72% were women. Most participants (77%) were from low or very low socioeconomic backgrounds, and only 5% had completed at least secondary education. Neurological disorder was the most prevalent chronic disease (24%), followed by arterial hypertension (22%) and cardiovascular disease (17%). A total of 56 centenarians (35%) were free of any age-related chronic disease. Frailty/pre-frailty and sarcopenia were highly prevalent, affecting 98% and 75% of participants, respectively. A favorable self-perceived health status was reported by 62%, and 86% exhibited positive satisfaction with life. 47% had no dementia/questionable dementia, whereas 45% of centenarians were independent or mildly dependent.ConclusionsWe present a real-life baseline study on centenarians. Our findings contribute to understanding extreme longevity and may facilitate future studies, progress in healthcare, lifestyle decisions, and societal policies that benefit long-lived people.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07198-z.Keywords: Centenarians, Longevity, Aged, Health status, Health status indicators

Introduction

Currently, the world is undergoing a demographic transition characterized by accelerated population aging and increased life expectancy [1]. This has notably raised the proportion of older persons and long-lived individuals globally, and it is expected that this trend will continue in the coming decades [2]. This scenario poses a challenge for both healthcare systems and society due to heterogeneity in adaptive capacity and responses for building a longevity society to address needs of the older persons with pathological aging [3]. This would require healthcare attention for chronic diseases, disability, dependence, and loss of autonomy [3, 4].

Centenarians, those with a chronological age of 100 years or older, represent the most successful model of biological aging in humans [5]. The term healthy aging phenotype is understood not as the absence of disease, but as a multidimensional expression of maintaining the functional ability, preserved intrinsic capacity, adaptability, and subjective well-being in extreme longevity [1, 6]. In centenarians, this phenotype encompasses the balance between physical and cognitive function, emotional resilience, low prevalence of age-related chronic diseases, social engagement, and self-perceived quality of life, despite the presence of chronic conditions or frailty indicators [7–10]. Within this framework, intrinsic capacity is defined as the composite of all the physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw upon, reflecting their underlying biological and neurofunctional reserves [6]. This conceptualization aligns with contemporary geroscience models that view longevity as a dynamic outcome of biological, psychological, and social adaptation rather than the mere avoidance of morbidity [11].

It is important to note that, in this study, the term healthy aging is used exclusively as a scientific construct to describe a measurable health and functional phenotype observed in centenarians, without implying normative or moral judgments about aging trajectories. The terminology follows its established use in gerontological and geroscience research to facilitate comparability across studies [6–10], while fully acknowledging that diverse forms of aging can be adaptive and meaningful.

The study of centenarians has enabled the identification and understanding of potential determinants of healthy aging and extreme longevity [12], mechanisms that delay biological aging [13], and possible interventions to enhance the functional and intrinsic capacities of older persons [14]. The main determinants include genetics and environment factors, which vary among populations [14, 15]. In this framework, the most relevant determinants can be grouped into biological (e.g., genetic and metabolic), psychological (e.g., cognition, mood, resilience), social (e.g., education, family support, and community integration), functional (e.g., mobility and autonomy), and environmental (e.g., living conditions and access to healthcare) domains [14, 15], which together shape the multidimensional aging phenotype [14, 15].

For this reason, previously published centenarian cohorts have found similarities in the health phenotypes, but differences in associated protective factors [9, 16–20]. Then, approaching the study of centenarians from a biopsychosocial perspective would allow for understanding the complex interactions among biological, psychological, and social factors, and their impact on defining a healthy aging phenotype [14, 15].

The biopsychosocial model, originally proposed by Engel [21] and later expanded within gerontological sciences, conceptualizes health and aging as the result of continuous interactions among biological, psychological, and social systems [21]. This framework departs from reductionist biomedical views by integrating molecular, behavioral, cognitive, and environmental determinants across the life course [21]. In geroscience, it provides a comprehensive lens through which resilience, functionality, and well-being can be interpreted as emergent properties of these multidimensional interactions [21], particularly relevant in centenarians, whose longevity often reflects adaptive balance rather than disease absence [20].

Colombia offers a uniquely informative context to study extreme longevity. As part of Latin America, a region marked by pronounced social, cultural, and ethnic heterogeneity and persistent health inequities [22–24], determinants of aging likely operate differently than in high-income settings. Within Colombia, structural inequality remains high (e.g., Gini index ≈ 54.8 in 2022), with downstream effects on education, income security, and access to age-appropriate healthcare across the life course [22–24]. These macro-level conditions intersect with proximal, biopsychosocial factors (family structure, caregiving networks, and community support) that may shape resilience and health trajectories into extreme old age [21].

Moreover, many Colombian centenarians have lived through periods of profound social and economic adversity, including early-life hardship and population-level instability, which may have shaped resilience and survival mechanisms across the life course [10]. Despite the global relevance of centenarian research, active cohorts in Latin America are scarce, with most detailed profiles concentrated outside the region (e.g., the Nicoya peninsula in Costa Rica) [9].

Colombia lacks comprehensive, real-life cohort descriptions of centenarians that integrate clinical, functional, cognitive, psychosocial, and biochemical domains. To date, only preliminary or localized reports on Colombian centenarians have been published [25–27]. No comprehensive cohort profile integrating biopsychosocial and biochemical dimensions has been documented nationally. This evidence gap prevents the identification of context-specific aging determinants and limits the comparability of Colombian data with international centenarian cohorts.

Generating such evidence is essential to (a) delineate context-specific determinants of aging; (b) inform geriatric guidelines adapted to extreme longevity; and (c) guide public policy for older persons, recognized as a protected population under national law, toward measurable, equity-oriented outcomes. The present study addresses this gap by describing Colombian centenarian profiles across eight domains within a national cohort study (Colombian Centenarian Project [CCP]), thereby providing a regionally grounded benchmark for comparative aging research.

This study was reported according to STROBE guidelines [28].

Methods

Study design

Cross-sectional analysis derived from the CCP [10]. This is a nationwide cohort study aimed at identifying and characterizing centenarians across Colombia.

Setting and participants

Because the number of centenarians in Colombia is small and not officially recorded in national demographic registries, no formal sample size calculation was feasible. Instead, a simple random sampling strategy was implemented using verified lists from a national health insurance company and state health office databases, located in six states of Colombia (Figure S1).

Centenarians were eligible if they: (a) had a confirmed chronological age of 100 years or older based on official identification or state records; (b) were registered in the national health insurance or state health office databases; (c) resided permanently in any Colombian state; and (d) provided written informed consent personally or through a legal representative.

No formal exclusion criteria were established to preserve the ecological and real-life representativeness of the cohort. However, individuals were excluded in practice if their age could not be verified, if they declined to participate, or if no legally authorized caregiver was available to provide consent or assist with the structured interview. This pragmatic definition ensures that the sample reflects the true heterogeneity of centenarians living in community and institutional settings across Colombia. Those who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled up to December 2024.

A total of 330 potential centenarians were initially identified through national and regional health registries. After verification of eligibility, 160 individuals were enrolled and completed the baseline assessment, representing a robust and diverse sample of confirmed centenarians across the country. The main reasons for non-enrollment included difficulties in verifying chronological age, inability to contact potential participants, or lack of an available legal caregiver to provide informed consent. These factors are inherent challenges in real-world centenarian recruitment rather than systematic losses, and the achieved sample ensures substantial ecological and geographic representativeness of the population.

Participants were assessed either at their homes or, when feasible, at outpatient centers specifically equipped for geriatric evaluation and primary care of older persons. These centers are part of community-based health facilities within the Colombian public and private health system and are not specialized tertiary geriatric units. They provide general medical, nursing, and functional assessment services adapted to older adults, ensuring accessibility for those with mobility limitations (Figure S2).

Variables

Given the aim of exploring general and domain-specific characteristics, the outcomes, whether categorical or quantitative, were derived from the scales used to determine the centenarian’s health phenotype.

Data measurement

Using a semi-structured questionnaire designed by a multidisciplinary team, data were collected during a medical interview alongside the centenarian’s caregiver. All assessments were performed by a team of trained physicians and nurses who underwent standardized training sessions to ensure inter-rater consistency administering questionnaires and performing physical measurements. Data were entered into a centralized database with double verification to minimize transcription errors. Missing data were rare (< 5% across variables) and were handled by complete-case analysis without imputation, as the objective of this study was purely descriptive. Continuous quality control was maintained throughout data collection to ensure methodological consistency across sites.

To describe the multidimensional and multidirectional process of aging in centenarians, the questionnaire included variables assessing eight domains: sociodemographic characteristics, clinical status, nutritional status, functional status, cognitive status, lifestyle, prosociality, and biochemical parameters. To achieve this, previously validated scales and tools used whit older persons in Colombia [23] and other centenarian cohorts were employed [8, 9, 17]. All instruments and scales used in this study are freely available for research and clinical use, without licensing restrictions. Each tool was applied according to its validated Spanish version or regional adaptation.

Each domain was selected to represent a key determinant of aging within the biopsychosocial framework: sociodemographic and clinical variables capture structural and health-related determinants; functional and cognitive assessments reflect intrinsic capacity; nutritional and biochemical parameters indicate physiological resilience; and psychosocial, sleep, and prosociality measures address emotional well-being and social adaptation in extreme longevity [29] (Table S1).

To assess clinical status, vital signs, personal history, frailty using Fried’s phenotype criteria [30], sarcopenia using the SARC-F scale [30], self-perceived health [31], quality of life using the WHOQOL scale (The World Health Organization Quality of Life) [32], general signs and symptoms, and dentition were evaluated. To assess nutritional status, the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) scale [33] and Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) [34] were used. The Barthel Index [35], Lawton and Brody [35], Downton [36], and Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [37] scales were used to determine functional status. The Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) [38, 39], and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [40] were used to evaluate cognitive status, accompanied by the Yesavage scale [41] and GAI-SF (Geriatric Anxiety Inventory - Short Form) [42] to inquire about depression and anxiety, respectively.

To explore lifestyles, sleep quality was assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [43], smoking history, alcohol consumption, physical activity [9], and Satisfaction With Life Scales (SWLS) to assess life satisfaction [44]. Finally, prosociality was explored, inquiring about the perception of caregiving, loneliness, social interaction, and contact [29], and history of any type of violence. All centenarians were assessed by a single team of researchers to ensure data homogeneity, objectivity, and quality.

Biochemical parameters of interest in the health status of extreme longevity were studied, including lipid metabolism, bone metabolism, renal profile, thyroid profile, and inflammatory markers, among others (reference ranges in Table S2) [45–47].

For the assessment of arterial hypertension, given the absence of centenarian-specific clinical practice guidelines in Colombia, we reconsidered the cut-off points recommended by the Heart Foundation (Australia) [48], Hypertension Canada (2018) [49], and NICE (UK, 2019) [50]. These guidelines indicate that, in frail and high-risk populations aged 80 years or older, hypertension may be considered clinically relevant at values starting from 160/100 mmHg. Given the absence of specific clinical practice guidelines for centenarian populations, these recommendations were adopted, as other clinical consensus statements propose more restrictive thresholds that could lead to a high prevalence of false-positive cases.

The absence of age-related chronic diseases was defined as the absence of any of the following chronic conditions: arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes, obesity, autoimmunity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, neurological diseases, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, dyslipidemia, thyroid diseases, chronic kidney disease, or bone diseases [1].

All instruments and scales used in this study are freely available for research and clinical use, without licensing restrictions.

Statistical methods

The normality of quantitative variables was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and median (interquartile, IQR) for skewed variables. Qualitative variables were summarized using frequency and percentages. The statistical analysis was conducted using the R software (Version 4.3.1) (https://www.r-project.org/).

Ethical statement

This study was performed in compliance with Act 008430/1993 of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Colombia, which classified it as minimal-risk research. The ethics committee of the Universidad de la Costa approved this study. Informed consents were obtained from all the participants and their legal guardians.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 160 centenarians were included (median age, 101 years; IQR 3). Most participants were women (72%, n = 115), 80.4% (n = 127) resided in urban areas, and 77.2% (n = 122/160) belonged to low or very low socioeconomic strata. Only 5% (n = 8) had completed at least secondary education (Table 1).

Clinical profile, frailty, and sarcopenia

Median systolic and diastolic blood pressure during the medical consultation was 130 (IQR 46.5) and 70 (IQR 18) mmHg, respectively. Median grip strength was 8.3 kg (IQR 6.42), and median body mass index was 18.79 kg/m² (IQR 5.9).

The most prevalent chronic conditions were neurological disease (24%, n = 39), arterial hypertension (22%, n = 35), and cardiovascular disease (17%, n = 28). Notably, 35% (n = 56) reported no chronic diseases. Polypharmacy was observed in 23% (n = 36), and 15% (n = 24) reported previous COVID-19 infection.

Frailty was highly prevalent, affecting 77% (n = 117/152) of participants, while 21% (n = 32/152) were classified as pre-frail and only 2% (n = 3/152) as robust. Sarcopenia was identified in 75% (n = 120/160) (Table 2).

Quality of life and psychological well-being

Favorable self-rated health (excellent or good) was reported by 62% (n = 95/154). Positive self-perception of memory, vision, and hearing was reported by 56%, 39%, and 36% of participants, respectively.

Overall psychological well-being was high: 80% (n = 123/154) reported enjoying life, 89% (n = 137/154) reported having a clear sense of purpose, and 86% (n = 126/160) expressed satisfaction and self-realization. The median quality-of-life score was 51.31 (IQR 19.3) (Table 6). Sleep quality was generally preserved, with 82% (n = 130/158) reporting no insomnia (Table 7).

Discussion

This study aimed to describe the multidimensional determinants of health and aging determinants among centenarians in Colombia, integrating sociodemographic, clinical, functional, cognitive, psychosocial, and biochemical domains. As a descriptive cohort profile, no inferential analyses or hypothesis testing were performed; instead, the objective was to portray the real-life phenotype of extreme longevity within a highly heterogeneous and unequal Latin American context.

Contemporary models of aging recognize that the aging phenotype results from the dynamic interaction of biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors operating at multiple levels throughout the life course [51]. However, considering the interindividual variability present during the dynamic aging process [52], it is not possible to generalize or extrapolate the biological or statistical influence of aging determinants over the life course, risk, or protection from illness in populations with different social, cultural, economic, and environmental traits [52]. Centenarians, as an extreme phenotype of longevity and being the age group with the longest exposure to specific conditions for aging determinants in their own environment, provide valuable and precise information on the impact of certain factors on the exposure-effect relationship during aging in the community [19].

The findings reveal a distinct social and health landscape among Colombian centenarians: most participants were women, lived in urban areas, and belonged to low or very low socioeconomic strata, with limited access to education and formal retirement benefits. Despite these structural inequities and high prevalence of frailty and chronic disease, the majority reported favorable self-perceived health and life satisfaction, reflecting strong resilience and psychosocial adaptation. These results provide a baseline characterization of the Colombian centenarian phenotype and illustrate the interplay between vulnerability and well-being in extreme old age [25].

The absence of formal education, low or very low socioeconomic status, economic vulnerability and dependency, elementary and informal occupations, lack of retirement plans, and notable difficulties in meeting basic needs, including food security and social protection, were predominant in this sample. Colombia is one of the most socially and economically unequal countries [53]. These findings indicate that centenarians constitute one of the population groups with the highest levels of social inequity and vulnerability, even though older persons are considered subjects of special protection under Colombian law and by the Inter-American Commission for the Protection of the Human Rights of Older Persons [54]. This underscores public policy objectives and goals for older persons to develop evidence-based action plans that have a real impact on the most important and violated determinants in this population.

The prevalence of arterial hypertension diagnosis, neurological diseases, and cardiovascular diseases was high compared to other centenarian cohorts [9, 17, 55]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they may partly reflect overdiagnosis or differences in diagnostic thresholds applied to individuals of extreme longevity. We observed a discrepancy in the previous diagnosis of some chronic diseases (e.g., hypertension, whose prevalence was lower in consultation and careful review of clinical charts). To date, no clinical practice guidelines specifically tailored for centenarians exist, and current diagnostic criteria are often extrapolated from younger elderly populations [56]. However, this study adopted flexible cut-off scores due to the very high prevalence of pre-frailty/frailty, as suggested by several previous clinical consensus statements on arterial hypertension [48–50]. This lack of adapted parameters may also contribute to the observed polypharmacy rate (23%), underscoring the need for more context-sensitive diagnostic and therapeutic frameworks for the oldest-old [57].

We observed that 35% of centenarians presented no aging-related chronic disease, an intriguing finding that supports the formulation of future hypotheses regarding the biological resilience and resistance of this population group to the pathophysiological mechanisms traditionally associated with the onset of such conditions. The lack of specific clinical evidence and randomized controlled trials in centenarians represents a significant barrier to the implementation of evidence-based geriatric care, as it often omits some characteristics or predictors commonly observed in this population [58], that are crucial for maintaining disability-free life expectancy or quality of life. For example, independence and functional status in extreme longevity are stronger predictors of survival than chronic diseases [59]. Therefore, the pharmacological intensification and polypharmacy for chronic diseases may not be as important in most cases and could pose more risk than benefit [58].

The distribution of frailty in this cohort highlights the substantial burden of vulnerability among Colombian centenarians, with 77% classified as frail, sarcopenia (75%), malnutrition or malnutrition risk (77%), dependency (55% [moderate, severe, or total]), inadequate physical performance (87%), and high fall risk (55%). This pattern is consistent with previous studies and the expected accumulation of physiological deficits at extreme ages [60–62], yet it also underscores the heterogeneity of health states that persists even beyond 100 years [63]. From a clinical perspective, the predominance of frailty, sarcopenia, and functional dependence indicates a high demand for individualized, function-oriented care, careful medication management, and proactive prevention of adverse outcomes such as falls, disability, and hospitalization [62]. From a public health standpoint, these findings emphasize the need to strengthen geriatric services, long-term care infrastructure, and community-based support systems tailored to the oldest-old, particularly in low- and middle-income settings where formal care resources remain limited. At the same time, the presence of pre-frail and robust individuals, albeit small, suggests a potential window for targeted interventions aimed at preserving functional capacity and delaying further decline, even at extreme longevity.

Dementia prevalence (33% [moderate or severe according to CDR-SB]) and insomnia (18%) were among the most notable health-related outcomes observed in this cohort [64]. The low frequency of regular physical activity and the presence of lifetime exposure to smoking and alcohol use further reflect cumulative lifestyle risks [11]. These findings likely result from decades of exposure to social, economic, and environmental determinants that influence active and healthy aging trajectories [14]. Overall, centenarians represent a highly vulnerable population with complex health needs, underscoring the importance of developing integrative, multidomain interventions to preserve intrinsic capacity and optimize quality of life in extreme old age [14, 15, 20].

Despite the prevalence of frailty and chronic diseases, 62% of centenarians reported a favorable self-perception of health, and 41% expressed a positive sense of purpose in life. More than 90% perceived the care they received as good, mainly provided by family members (85%), which highlights the central role of kinship-based caregiving in supporting longevity in Colombia. These findings reflect the strong psychosocial resilience and prosociality of this population, suggesting that emotional and social resources may act as protective mechanisms that buffer the impact of physical decline in extreme old age [14, 19, 51].

These adaptive patterns align with the selection, optimization, and compensation model proposed by Baltes & Baltes [65], which posits that healthy aging results from the dynamic capacity to select attainable goals, optimize available resources, and compensate for functional losses [65]. This theoretical basis helps explain how Colombian centenarians maintain autonomy and well-being despite biological and social constraints [65].

One of the most intriguing findings concerns the biochemical and immunological parameters observed in Colombian centenarians. Consistent with European and Asian cohorts, hematological and metabolic markers in this population were generally homogeneous and remained within or near normal reference ranges [66]. Particular markers of inflammation, including C-reactive protein, ESR, ferritin, or lymphocyte counts, exhibited only slight rises, suggesting that inflammaging is not a hallmark of this population (Table 9).

Despite the expected physiological decline associated with aging and the hypothesis that centenarians may experience delayed biological aging [13], the majority of hematological parameters in this population remained within normal limits. This could suggest preservation of multisystem organ function despite advanced chronological age [12]. Further in-depth studies are needed to assess the association between the variability of hematological parameters across different centenarian phenotypes and health scenarios, and their implications for health outcomes and survival [12]. Such investigations are crucial for informing healthcare decision-making and interventions in centenarians, as biochemical parameters serve as a direct measure of the body’s capacity to respond to disease [12].

The descriptive nature of the analysis, as well as the bias introduced by the non-specificity of existing diagnoses in medical records derived from routine health care for the centenarian are shortcomings of this study that confound the actual prevalence of chronic diseases. Also, a potential limitation of this study is that several of the instruments used, such as the SARC-F or the ISI, are screening rather than diagnostic tools. However, these instruments are internationally validated, widely used in geriatric and centenarian research, and offer robust construct validity for population-based assessments. Their use was intentional, as they provide feasible, standardized, and comparable measures across domains while minimizing participant burden. This study underscores the lack of validated, centenarian-specific cut-off values for key measures, including blood pressure, cognitive tests, and functional assessments, and highlights the need for future research to establish thresholds tailored to this population. In addition, our findings should be interpreted in light of survival bias, as centenarians represent a highly selected subgroup with greater biological and social resilience, which may lead to an underestimation of the true burden of disease and functional impairment in the original population. As strengths, it should be noted that this is the first comprehensive real-life cohort description of Colombian centenarians. Data presented correspond to centenarians from a geographic area characterized by racial and cultural mixing [25, 66], providing valuable information for subsequent analyses.

Conclusions

This study presents baseline characteristics of a Colombian centenarian cohort across demographics, clinical, functional, cognitive, and biological measures. These data characterize the population, guide hypothesis generation, identify key covariates, and serve as the reference for future analyses.
