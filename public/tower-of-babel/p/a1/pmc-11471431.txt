Risks of segmentectomy for interstitial pneumonia with diffuse pulmonary ossification
Authors: Karin Shimada, Satoshi Takamori, Marina Nakatsuka, Makoto Endoh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 84‐year‐old man with a history of progressive interstitial pneumonia presented to our department with lung cancer (cT2aN0M0‐IB) in right S6. Moreover, computed tomography revealed progressive diffuse pulmonary ossification in the bilateral lower pulmonary lobes. S6 segmentectomy was performed via video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery. It was difficult to divide the intersegmental plane using a stapler because of severe fibrosis and pulmonary ossification with bone marrow formation. Pulmonary ossification may be an important finding for surgical planning because of severe fibrosis or inflammation associated with severe lung condition. We suggest that the surgical indications and approaches for such cases should be reconsidered because pulmonary ossification can be associated with severe lung conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Pulmonary ossification is a rare condition characterized by diffuse ectopic ossification of the pulmonary parenchyma. 1 Pulmonary ossification is often associated with lung diseases such as interstitial pneumonia and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). 2 , 3 Moreover, it may be associated with a high rate of lung cancer, which often requires surgery. However, there are no surgical reports of lung cancer in patients with pulmonary ossification. Herein, we report a patient with interstitial pneumonia and pulmonary ossification of the dendritic type who underwent segmentectomy for lung cancer.

CASE REPORT

An 84‐year‐old man presented to our department with an abnormal shadow on computed tomography (CT). He had a history of acute exacerbation of interstitial pneumonia (3 years ago) and left‐sided lung cancer, for which wedge resection was performed 9 years ago (squamous cell carcinoma, pT1aN0M0‐IA1). CT revealed lung cancer in the right S6 segment (Figure 1a) and diffuse pulmonary calcifications in the bilateral lower lobes (Figure 1b). Interstitial pneumonia and diffuse pulmonary calcification had progressed gradually (Figure 2).The patient's laboratory data showed Lactate Dehydrogenase 185 U/L, Krebs von den Lungen‐6 917 U/mL, surfactant protein D 90.7 ng/mL. We considered radiation therapy, surgery, and best supportive care as treatment options. These treatments carry a risk of acute exacerbation of interstitial pneumonia. Radiation therapy is associated with a higher regional recurrence rate than surgery. Based on the preferences of the patient and his family, we decided to perform surgery. The patient's cardiopulmonary function was compromised by the right lower lobectomy [ejection fraction, 45%; forced expiratory volume in 1 second, 2.06 L (94.9% of predicted); vital capacity, 2.73 L (93.2% of predicted); diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (76.3% of predicted)]. We suspected lung cancer (cT2aN0M0‐IB) and planned to perform S6 segmentectomy via video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) because the cardiopulmonary function was not feasible for lobectomy. First, we divided A6 using a stapler, followed by the division of V6; B6 was divided using a stapler. Intraoperative interlobar lymph node (no. 11) metastasis was absent; therefore, mediastinal lymph node dissection was omitted. We attempted to divide the intersegmental plane using a stapler while ensuring a sufficient resection margin; however, the lung parenchyma was stiff and could not be fully compressed because of severe fibrosis and pulmonary ossification. We tried to divide it from different directions using a stapler, but it was difficult. Eight staplers (Powered ECHELON FLEX™; Ethicon Endo‐Surgery, Cincinnati, OH, USA) with black cartridges were used to divide the intersegmental plane. We used six 45‐mm and two 60‐mm staplers. The Echelon Endopatch SLR (ESLR, Ethicon, Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA) was also used. Compression of the lung parenchyma was achieved with a 60‐mm stapler with a larger aperture, since it was difficult to accomplish it with a 45‐mm stapler. We performed S6 segmentectomy via VATS (Supplemental file, Video S1). The operative time was 124 min, and blood loss was minimal. The postoperative course was uneventful; the drain was removed on postoperative day 1, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2. The pathological findings revealed squamous cell carcinoma (pT2aN0M0‐IB, margin about 2 cm). In addition, severe fibrosis and multiple ossified areas with bone marrow formation were observed (Figure 3). At the 6‐month follow‐up after surgery, the patient was alive and recurrence‐free.

DISCUSSION

Pulmonary ossification is a rare condition, characterized by ectopic ossification of lung tissue, with a reported incidence of 0.16–0.4% from previous autopsy cases. 2 , 3 CT shows diffuse and small bilateral peripheral calcified nodules, mainly involving the lung bases. 4 , 5 It is classified into two types, nodular and dendritic, which differ in the type of calcification, background disease, and presence or absence of myelopoiesis. The dendriform type is a consequence of chronic inflammation, and ossification occurs in and replaces the alveolar septae. Therefore, the dendritic type is associated with lung diseases, such as COPD and interstitial pneumonia, and bone marrow formation. Lara et al. reported that pulmonary fibrosis was the most common cause of pulmonary ossification as the dendritic type, followed closely by established COPD. 2 Moreover, there are report that diffuse dendriform ossification was identified in 6.7% with usual interstitial pneumonia on review of CT and histopathologic specimens. 6 In this case, our patient presented with progressive interstitial pneumonia and calcification characteristic of pulmonary ossification. The pathological findings also revealed myelopoiesis. Therefore, we diagnosed the pulmonary ossification as the dendritic type.

Sonokawa et al. reported a case of diffuse pulmonary ossification that required emergency treatment for intrathoracic bleeding, suggesting that idiopathic bleeding occurred during pulmonary ossification due to bone marrow formation. 7 Pulmonary ossification occurs in chronically injured lungs under conditions of low pH and low oxygen tension. 8 The occurrence of pulmonary ossification indicates severe lung disease. It also represents a risk of bleeding or other problems, with or without surgery. Although this patient's postoperative course was uneventful, careful follow‐up is necessary because the ossified lung parenchyma is still at risk for bleeding and pulmonary laceration.

Interstitial pneumonia is often associated with lung cancer, and surgery may be required. Additionally, pulmonary ossification is associated with such serious lung conditions as interstitial pneumonia and COPD. Pulmonary ossification on preoperative CT may be an important finding for surgical planning because severe fibrosis or inflammation is associated with severe lung condition. In the present case, dividing the lung parenchyma was difficult because it was stiff and could not be fully compressed using a stapler. Moreover, the potential impact of increased resistance to incision by the buttress materials could not be ruled out. Although we used it in this case, one should be aware that buttress materials may increase resistance. The indications for surgery and approaches that avoid deep‐wedge resection or segmentectomy such as S9 or/and S10 segmentectomy should be carefully considered in these cases. If the cardiopulmonary function is acceptable, lobectomy with minimal parenchyma resection should be considered. Radiotherapy yields the same results with respect to overall survival but is inferior with regard to the regional recurrence rate compared with surgery. Meanwhile, surgery is associated with high perioperative risks, however, regional control is advantageous. 9 In this case, we thoroughly discussed the treatment options with the patient and his family and chose surgery.

CONCLUSION

Herein, we reported a surgical case of lung cancer complicated by interstitial pneumonia with pulmonary ossification. Pulmonary ossification might reveal severe lung disease. Therefore, when surgery for pulmonary ossification is necessary, it may be important to reconsider surgical indications and approaches.
