Association between hypothyroidism and nephrotic syndrome: a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Can Liu, Jiaxuan Shen, Zhaoran Ding, Shuwen Duan, Enlai Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a close clinical association between hypothyroidism and nephrotic syndrome (NS) was close, but whether there is genetic causality between the two is not known.ObjectiveUsing pooled data from a genome-wide association study (GWAS), the association between hypothyroidism and NS was explored via Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.MethodsSingle-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with hypothyroidism (or NS) were screened as genetic instrumental variables (IVs) from pooled GWAS data, and inverse-variance weighting (IVW) was used for the main analysis to estimate causal effects, with MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode used as complementary methods. Sensitivity analyses, including Cochran’s Q test, MR-Egger intercept, MR-PRESSO and leave-one-out, were also conducted to assess the robustness of the results.ResultsGenetically predicted hypothyroidism was positively associated with the risk of developing NS (IVW: OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.07–1.30, p = 0.00; MR-Egger: OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.10–1.68, p = 0.01), and the MR-Egger intercept (intercept = −0.02, p = 0.14), MR-PRESSO test (p = 0.14), Cochran’s Q test (p = 0.15) and leave-one-out test results supported the robustness of the results. Genetically predicted NS status might not be associated with an increased risk of developing hypothyroidism (IVW: OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03, p = 0.08; MR-Egger: OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.98–1.04, p = 0.43), and the MR-Egger intercept (intercept < 0.01, p = 0.69), MR-PRESSO test (p = 0.64), Cochran’s Q test (p = 0.61) and leave-one-out test results supported the robustness of the results.ConclusionHypothyroidism status could increase the risk of developing NS.Keywords: hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, Mendelian randomization, genome-wide association study

Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome (NS) is a group of glomerular diseases, that are characterized mainly by edema, massive proteinuria, hypoproteinemia and hyperlipidemia. Among them, proteinuria is a characteristic manifestation of the disease and a trigger for hypoproteinemia and other conditions and renal pathological damage [1]. The components of proteinuria in NS patients include blood albumin and high-molecular-weight proteins (e.g. thyroxine-binding globulin and thyroid hormones). Persistently high levels of proteinuria might lead to subclinical hypothyroidism, which manifests itself as clinical hypothyroidism when there is an insufficient reserve of thyroid function.

Elevated serum thyroid-stimulating hormone levels and decreased levels of free thyroxine and triiodothyronine characterize the clinical syndrome of hypothyroidism. Among the total European population, the incidence of hypothyroidism is 226.2 (222.26–230.17) cases per 100,000 people per year [2], which is approximately 0.22%. An Iranian study comparing thyroid hormone levels between children with nephropathy and healthy controls revealed that elevated TSH levels were more common in children with nephropathy (34.2% vs. 10.8%) [3].

Several studies have confirmed the strong association between proteinuria and the risk of developing hypothyroidism [4, 5]. Persistent proteinuria without remission was highly likely to lead to abnormal thyroid hormone levels. Clinical studies have confirmed that hypothyroidism often occurs in patients with NS [6–11]. Hypothyroidism is also associated with abnormal renal function, dyslipidemia, and deterioration of renal pathology [12, 13]. In this study, we systematically investigated the genetically predicted causal association between hypothyroidism and NS.

Methods

Data source and study design

Data on hypothyroidism and NS were obtained from Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) data [14, 15] (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets). Pooled statistics included 30,155 patients with hypothyroidism and 379,986 control participants without hypothyroidism (ID: ebi-a-GCST90018862), as well as 775 patients with NS and 475,255 non-NS control participants (ID: ebi-a-GCST90018884). Both samples were derived from the study of Sakaue S et al. [16], who conducted a meta-analysis of phenotypic GWASs from the Japan Biobank, UK Biobank, and FinnGen and statistically disaggregated the phenomenological summary statistic matrices to create new summary data on the genetic information typing of similar diseases. The NS and hypothyroidism data in this paper were the new pooled data formed in Sakaue S’s study, in which the NS data were taken mainly from N14_NEPHROTICSYND in FinnGen for European populations, and the hypothyroidism data were taken mainly from E04 Other hypothyroidism and E06.3 Autoimmune thyroiditis in the UK Biobank for European populations. The demographic data for the included participants are summarized in Table 1. The calculations of the overlap rates for the two samples are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) studies were used to assess the causal relationship between genetic susceptibility to hypothyroidism (or NS) and the risk of developing NS (or hypothyroidism). An overview of the study design is provided in Figure 1. The SNPs were used as instrumental variables (IVs), and the whole process conformed to the three main assumptions of classical MR analysis [17]: the assumption of correlation (assumption 1), the assumption of independence (assumption 2), and the assumption of exclusivity (assumption 3). Ethical approval was received for the original studies.

Flowchart of the study design for MR studies.

(A) Flowchart of the design of the MR study to assess the genetically predicted causal effects of hypothyroidism status on the risk of developing NS; (B) Flowchart of the design of the MR study to assess the genetically predicted causal effects of NS status on the risk of developing hypothyroidism. MR is based on three assumptions: 1) the correlation assumption that IVs are strongly correlated with exposure; 2) the independence assumption that IVs are independent of any confounding factors; and 3). the exclusivity assumption that IVs affect outcomes only through exposure and not in any other way.

Statistical analysis

In our study, inverse-variance weighting (IVW) was used as the primary method, with MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode as supplemental analyses to estimate the causal effect of exposure on outcomes. IVW was the primary analytical tool used to estimate the underlying causality because it provides the most accurate results when all selected SNPs are valid IVs [18]. MR-Egger and weighted median were used to improve IVW estimates, as they provide more reliable, albeit less efficient (wider CI), estimates across a wider range of cases [19]. For weighted median analysis, at least 50% of the weights contributed by the genetic variance must be valid before causality can be consistently estimated [20]. Weighted mode is used to cluster SNPs into a subset on the basis of the similarity of causal effects, thus estimating the causal effect of the subset with the highest number of SNPs [21].

Heterogeneity, pleiotropy and sensitivity analysis

Heterogeneity tests were used to assess differences between individual IVs. Heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran’s Q test [22], and the MR-PRESSO package was further used to detect outlier SNPs when heterogeneity was suggested via Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.05); outlier SNPs were rejected and then the MR analysis was performed again. An alternative solution to the presence of heterogeneity is to choose a random effects model to estimate the MR effect size [23]. Pleiotropy testing is used to assess the presence of directional pleiotropy among multiple IVs, usually represented by the intercept term of the MR-Egger method [19]; A funnel plot is used to assess possible directional pleiotropy, similar to the method used in meta-analysis to assess publication bias. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was used to calculate MR results by removing the remaining IVs one by one, and the results of the analysis are presented in a forest plot. All the statistical analyses were performed via the TwoSampleMR [15] and MR-PRESSO [24] R packages.

Results

Forward two-sample MR: Genetically predicted causal effect of hypothyroidism status on the risk of developing NS

We included 75 independent (r2 < 0.001 and a distance of 10000 kb) SNPs strongly associated (p < 5 × 10−8) with hypothyroidism. MR analysis with NS resulted in the loss of three SNPs, of which one (rs11406335) was a SNP for an incompatible allele and two (rs1065386, rs2412976) were SNPs that were palindromic with intermediate allele frequencies. A total of 71 SNPs were used for MR analysis, and Cochran’s Q test revealed heterogeneity (p < 0.01) (Supplementary Table 2). One anomalous SNP (rs9273371, p < 0.0144) was excluded with the MR-PRESSO outlier test (Supplementary Table 3); thus, the remaining 71 SNPs were rerun for MR. The average F statistic value for hypothyroidism in NS patients was 84.58, with all F statistic values greater than 10 (Table 2).

Overall, there was indeed a positive correlation between genetically predicted hypothyroidism status and the risk of developing NS. The IVW results suggested that genetically predicted hypothyroidism status might increase the risk of developing NS (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.07–1.30; p = 0.00), which was consistent with the results of the MR-Egger method (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.10–1.68; p = 0.01). Although the weighted median (p = 0.07) and weighted mode (p = 0.36) methods did not support the results, the directions of the betas for both tests were consistent (Figure 2), so the IVW result was prioritized. The scatter plot of SNP effect sizes for hypothyroidism on NS is shown in Figure 3A-1. The MR-Egger intercept (intercept = −0.02, p = 0.14) and MR-PRESSO global test (p = 0.14) indicated that the presence of directional pleiotropy would not distort the result (Table 3, Figure 3A-1). Cochran’s Q test (p = 0.15) indicated that there was no heterogeneity among individual SNPs (Table 3, Figure 3B-1). The leave-one-out test revealed that no single SNP had an effect on the overall causality estimate (Figure 3C-1).

Reverse two-sample MR: Genetically predicted causal effect of NS status on the risk of developing hypothyroidism

We included 36 independent (r2 < 0.001 and a distance of 10000 kb) SNPs associated (p < 1 × 10−5) with NS in the MR analysis, and IVW indicated that genetically predicted NS status might increase the risk of developing hypothyroidism (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13; p = 0.02). Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.01) revealed heterogeneity (Supplementary Table 2), and 2 abnormal SNPs (rs1265889, p < 0.0072; rs17843611, p < 0.0072) were excluded via the MR-PRESSO outlier test (Supplementary Table 3). thus, the MR analysis was performed again with the remaining 34 SNPs. The average F statistic for the genetically predicted causal effect of NS status on the risk of developing hypothyroidism was 21.28, with all F statistic values greater than 10 (Table 2).

The IVW results revealed no association between genetically predicted NS status and the risk of developing hypothyroidism (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03; p = 0.08), which was consistent with the MR-Egger (p = 0.43), weighted median (p = 0.26), and weighted mode (p = 0.75) (all p > 0.05) results (Figure 2). The MR-Egger intercept (intercept < 0.01, p = 0.69) and MR-PRESSO global test (p = 0.64) indicated that the presence of directional pleiotropy would not distort this result (Table 3, Figure 3A-2). Cochran’s Q test (p = 0.61) indicated that there was no heterogeneity among individual SNPs (Table 3, Figure 3B-2). Leave-one-out analyses revealed that the included SNPs did not significantly affect the results, indicating that the results of the MR analyses were reliable (Figure 3C-2).

Discussion

In this research, we explored the genetic causality between hypothyroidism and NS via a two-sample MR method, which revealed that hypothyroidism was a risk factor for the development of NS (IVW: OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.07–1.30; p = 0.00) and that patients with hypothyroidism were at increased risk of developing NS. The results of the present study are consistent with the clinical epidemiological evidence that reported an increased incidence of NS in patients with hypothyroidism. In a study involving renal biopsy, Singh U et al. reported the presence of renal parenchymal lesions in all patients who with hypothyroidism of 6 months to 14 years [27]. A Nigerian cross-sectional study reported similar results and revealed a 2-fold increased risk of developing hypothyroidism in patients with steroid-resistant NS compared with patients with steroid-sensitive NS (OR = 2.901, 95% CI: 1.831–4.012; p = 0.038) [7]. A retrospective study by Gu et al. reported that the presence of hypothyroidism was associated with failure of NS remission (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.07–3.40; p = 0.029) by assessing urinary protein and serum positivity for the circulating anti-phospholipase A2 receptor [28]. Similarly, Li et al. in a systematic retrospective analysis, reported that hypothyroidism predicted higher levels of urinary proteins and LDL and lower levels of blood albumin (all p < 0.001) and was more prevalent in patients with membranous nephropathy (46.7%) [29]. The current study [30–32] revealed that the mechanisms by which hypothyroidism leas to NS, including antigenic cross-reactivity, lipid nephrotoxicity, and the effects of thyroid hormones on the glomerular filtration rate as well as sodium and water homeostasis, are complex and varied. In the context of the present study, the antigenic cross-reactivity theory [30] and the lipid nephrotoxicity theory [31] were considered. The theory of antigenic cross-reactivity suggests that thyroglobulin deposited in the glomerular basement membrane cross-reacts with in situ immune complexes, altering the structure of the basement membrane and the function of podocytes, activating the complement system to exacerbate the inflammatory process, and damaging renal tissue [30]. The theory of lipid nephrotoxicity describes an indirect mechanism in which low thyroid hormone levels in hypothyroidism lead to reduced lipolysis and lipid deposition that damage renal tissues directly or affect renal function indirectly through a combination of oxidative stress, epigenetic modifications, and other effects [31]. These two theories might explain the close association between hypothyroidism status and NS-related kidney damage.

Two-sample MR revealed that genetically predicted NS status did not increase the risk of developing hypothyroidism (IVW: OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03; p = 0.08). The results of the present study were inconsistent with the clinical epidemiological evidence reporting an increased incidence of hypothyroidism in patients with NS. A cross-sectional study at the National Referral Hospital in Mulago, Kampala, Uganda, reported a 23% prevalence of hypothyroidism among children and adolescents with NS attending the clinic and that low serum albumin was associated with the risk of hypothyroidism (OR = 35.80, 95% CI: 5.97–214.69; p < 0.001) [10]. Similar results were reported in a retrospective U.S. cohort study in which proteinuria status in patients with NS was associated with an excessive TSH concentration (> 10 mIU/L: OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.05–2.84; p = 0.008) [5]. Similar results were reported by Li et al. in a systematic retrospective analysis in which high levels of proteinuria were associated with an increased risk of developing hypothyroidism (OR = 1.137, 95% CI: 1.02–1.27; p = 0.021), and high levels of albumin were associated with a decreased risk of developing hypothyroidism (OR = 0.862, 95% CI: 0.8–0.93; p = 0.000) [29]. On the basis of these analyses, possible potential mechanisms between genetically predicated NS status and the risk of developing hypothyroidism include the axial feedback [33] theory and the colloid osmolality [34] theory. Axial feedback theory suggests that thyroid hormones are lost with the loss of hormone-binding proteins, leading to a decrease in the serum levels of TT3 and TT4, which in turn stimulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis through negative feedback, that leads to abnormally elevated serum levels of TSH, which triggers hypothyroidism [33]. Colloid osmolality theory suggests that human plasma colloid osmolality is derived mainly from albumin, and a large amount of plasma protein leakage into the urine induces hypoproteinemia, leading to a decrease in plasma colloid osmolality, followed by gastrointestinal mucosal congestion and edema, which affects the absorption of proteins [34]. Protein and iodine are raw materials for the synthesis of thyroid hormones, and restriction of both high-protein and iodized salt intake in patients with NS could lead to a decrease in thyroid hormone synthesis and trigger hypothyroidism. These two theories suggest that proteinuria and a low-protein state exacerbate fluctuations in the thyroid hormone profile from the level of hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid negative feedback regulation and insufficient raw material synthesis. These two theories might account for the fact that genetically predicted NS status was not associated with the risk of developing hypothyroidism.

Strengths and weaknesses

MR has obvious advantages over traditional epidemiologic methods in that it involves the use of randomly assigned genotypes in nature as IVs to infer the effects of exposure factors on outcomes, largely reducing the effects of reverse causality and confounding factors. However, several limitations exist. First, the present study did not classify NS status by pathologic typing or etiology to explore the causal associations between hypothyroidism and different pathological types of NS or NS induced by different factors. Datasets of NS with different causes and pathological types should be included in future analyses to improve the precision of the conclusions. Second, the pooled data used in the present study were derived from European populations, and the conclusions obtained herein are not generalizable without additional validation. Furthermore, the small number of databases used in this study and the lack of compatibility might introduce some bias to the MR analysis. Finally, in this study, we only explored the relationship between hypothyroidism and NS from a genetic point of view, but its specific mechanism of action still needs to be further verified by more studies.

Conclusion

The close physiological and clinical relationships between hypothyroidism and renal disease are well known. Hypothyroidism can damage the kidneys by affecting renal hemodynamics and water and electrolyte metabolism and by altering the glomerular structure; the reduction in endogenous thyroid hormones due to proteinuria is considered to be an important cause of hypothyroidism [32]. Our study demonstrated that genetically predicted hypothyroidism status was associated with the risk of developing NS, suggesting that the development of hypothyroidism might play a role in the development of NS. Our study failed to demonstrate an association between genetically predicted NS status and the risk of developing hypothyroidism, which seems to be in line with the current understanding that NS is triggered by hypothyroidism.
