Diffuse Alveolar Hemorrhage as a Rare Initial Presentation of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus in Early Pregnancy: A Case Report
Authors: Simran Rauniyar, Sonam Dhenga, Milan Pokhrel, Sameer Basnyat, Shailesh Kurmi, Jyoti Kalwary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiffuse alveolar hemorrhage is a rare but fatal manifestation of SLE, typically reported in well‐established diagnoses of SLE. DAH itself as a presenting symptom is quite rare. In this case, we present a 24‐year‐old pregnant female at 10 weeks of gestation without a history of SLE presenting with DAH.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease mostly affecting females of childbearing age involving any organ causing a wide range of symptoms from mild skin rash or arthralgia to diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) [1, 2]. DAH is typically found in established diagnoses of SLE and is a rare initial presentation of SLE. It is a fatal complication with mortality as high as up to 50% but fortunately, it is rare occurring only in about 2 to 5% of cases [3].

The pathophysiology of DAH is poorly understood but has been suggested to be pathogenetically similar to lupus nephritis. It has been postulated that there is an immune complex deposition and the induction of apoptosis causing capillary endothelial damage and inflammation that would result in local or diffuse bleeding from those capillaries [4, 5]. Pregnancy is a proinflammatory state due to hormonal changes in estradiol, progesterone, and prolactin; it may trigger or worsen symptoms. An individual may first experience symptoms during pregnancy or postpartum, or they may end up with a lupus crisis as their initial manifestation in an emergency setting [1, 6]. In this case report, we describe a 24‐year‐old pregnant female at 10 weeks of gestation with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage as the first presentation of SLE.

Case History/ Examination

A 24‐year‐old Gravida 2 Para 0 Abortion 1 (G2P0A1) female at 10 weeks of gestation, who is a non‐smoker and without any comorbid conditions, presented to the medicine department of our hospital with complaints of shortness of breath for 4 months and cough with blood‐mixed sputum for 2 months. Upon questioning, she also gave a history of bilateral knee pain and generalized body weakness. She did not have any previous history of similar illness as well as no history of rheumatological disease in her family members.

Her general physical examination revealed pallor. Vital signs revealed a body temperature of 100°F, pulse rate of 108 beats per minute, blood pressure of 90/60 mm of Hg, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 90%. Crepitations were heard over bilateral lung fields on auscultation. Other systemic examination findings were unremarkable.

She was admitted to the medical ICU, and initial stabilization was done. One pint of packed red blood cells was infused. After initial stabilization, she was shifted to the general ward, where further investigations were planned. USG obstetric scan showed early intrauterine pregnancy of 13 weeks gestation and a normal maternal scan. Blood and urine cultures were negative. Her serology was non‐reactive. Sputum profiles, including gene X‐pert, were also negative. Echo revealed mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%. Bronchoscopy revealed a few blood‐tinged secretions coming from the posterior segment of the left lower lobe (Figure 2). Bronchoalveolar Lavage (BAL) revealed progressively hemorrhagic samples (Figure 3) and BAL cytopathology showed hemosiderin‐laden macrophages and was negative for malignant cells.

The patient's history, clinical examination, and above investigations raised suspicion of rheumatological disease. Thus, a thorough rheumatological workup revealed positive ANA, anti‐dsDNA, and p‐ANCA. ESR was elevated, and serum C3 complement was decreased with normal C4 levels. The panels of the anticardiolipin antibodies, c‐ANCA, anti‐GBM antibodies, and APLA (antiphospholipid antibodies) were all within normal limits. Eventually, the patient was diagnosed with DAH secondary to SLE. She was treated with prednisolone and hydroxychloroquine. Rituximab 1 g in 500 mL normal saline was infused, and the second dose was planned after 2 weeks. Her symptoms gradually improved. She was admitted for a total duration of 21 days. At discharge, she was hemodynamically stable with normal vital signs. She was discharged on hydroxychloroquine and prednisolone. The patient was advised to follow up regularly, but due to a poor financial background and living very far from our center, the patient could not come for follow‐up visits. A screening echocardiogram was performed at 28 weeks, which revealed normal results. She delivered a 2.6 kg female baby at 38 weeks of gestation via normal vaginal delivery without any complications.

Conclusion and Results

While SLE in a female of childbearing age is fairly common, initial presentation as DAH is rare. In conclusion, this report emphasizes the need to consider DAH as a potential complication in pregnant women with SLE, even in the absence of severe nephritis. Prompt identification, strong clinical suspicion, and timely initiation of treatment with glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive agents are essential for managing DAH in these patients. A collaborative approach involving rheumatologists, obstetricians, and other specialists is crucial to ensure comprehensive care and improve patient outcomes. Further research and clinical trials are needed to enhance our understanding of DAH in pregnancy and refine management strategies.

Discussion

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) predominantly affects women of reproductive age, with hormonal fluctuations during pregnancy and childbirth acting as key triggers or exacerbating factors for the condition [6, 7]. Its presence poses a risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes, which can be divided into maternal complications (such as lupus flares, kidney damage, preeclampsia, hypertension, and venous thromboembolism) and fetal‐neonatal complications (including miscarriage, preterm birth, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), congenital heart block (CHB), and neonatal lupus (NL)) [7]. A study among 223 Chinese mothers with SLE that presented to the hospital from 2013 to 2017 shows 30.4% to be symptomatic for the first time during pregnancy or puerperium, and of these, 72.1% were diagnosed during the 1st and 2nd trimesters [8].

DAH is a rare but potentially life‐threatening complication of SLE with exceedingly uncommon occurrences during pregnancy and only a few cases are reported [9]. Usually, DAH in SLE is associated with heightened disease activity, particularly in patients with active class III or IV lupus nephritis [9]. In contrast to these typical presentations, our patient developed DAH as the initial presentation without any evidence of active class III or IV lupus nephritis, highlighting an atypical and noteworthy case.

The exact cause of DAH remains unclear, but it is thought to involve immune complex‐induced alveolar capillaritis or non‐inflammatory hemorrhage leading to red blood cell leakage, with antiphospholipid antibodies potentially playing a role in some cases [2]. The “classic triad” of hemoptysis, a rapid drop in hemoglobin, and new diffuse infiltrates on chest imaging suggest DAH, but more common symptoms like hypoxemia, dyspnea, and fever can mimic conditions such as pneumonia or pulmonary edema [2, 9]. In some cases and as noted in our patient, alveolar hemorrhage is sufficient to cause anemia; therefore, in anemia of unknown origin, DAH should be excluded [5]. Diagnosis of DAH is based on flexible bronchoscopy with sequential BAL, which exhibits progressively hemorrhagic samples and hemosiderin‐laden macrophages upon Prussian blue staining as present in our case [9]. Due to limited evidence and the unavailability of universal guidelines for managing DAH, especially in pregnant patients, early diagnosis and treatment with pulse methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide are key, while rituximab is used in some cases and ventilatory support and blood transfusion are offered as necessary [9]. Favorable outcomes of rituximab as initial therapy in addition to the traditional management have been reported with successful treatment of some recurrent cases of DAH [4]. However, they do not mention its role in pregnancy. Plasma exchange has been reported successfully as an adjunctive treatment of DAH in lupus by a study [3]. In contrast, others show that it has better outcomes for the management of DAH due to small vessel vasculitis rather than SLE [4].

In pregnancy, non‐fluorinated oral glucocorticoids like prednisone are metabolized by the placenta, minimizing fetal exposure [10]. High‐dose intravenous methylprednisolone (250–1000 mg/day for 3 days) is used for severe, life‐threatening SLE manifestations [10]. Dexamethasone and betamethasone, due to their longer duration and ability to cross the placenta, expose the fetus to higher levels and should only be used for fetal lung maturity or when preterm birth is expected [10]. Antimalarial agents—Hydroxychloroquine and chloroquine used to treat SLE have proven to prevent disease flares and long‐term survival and reduce the risk of preeclampsia and preterm birth in pregnant women with SLE [10]. Although these drugs cross the placenta, they are not considered to have notable toxic effects, congenital malformations, or cardiac conduction disturbances on the fetus [10].

Owing to their potential teratogenic effects, immunosuppressants—Methotrexate, Cyclophosphamide, Thalidomide, Mycophenolate mofetil are—contraindicated during pregnancy and are to be stopped 3 months to 6 weeks before conception [10]. Biologic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic agents (bDMARDs) are more effective than conventional synthetic DMARDs in achieving disease remission and preventing flare‐ups, raising interest in their compatibility during pregnancy [4, 10]. Monoclonal antibodies are transferred across the placenta after the 16th week via Fc receptor‐mediated transport, increasing until delivery [4, 10]. The first trimester lacks significant placental transfer, reducing teratogenic risks; however, the long‐term effects on neonatal health are not fully understood, as pregnancy is excluded from clinical trials [4, 10].

It is known that SLE in pregnancy with concurrent thrombocytopenia, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, lupus nephritis, antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), and anti‐Ro/La antibodies heightens the rate of maternal complications like preeclampsia and abnormal placentation [11, 12]. Adverse neonatal outcomes are fetal growth abnormalities, neonatal lupus, preterm birth due to hypertension, placental insufficiency (PI), higher incidence of lower segment caesarean section deliveries, small for gestational age babies, and pregnancy loss [11, 12]. This has been linked to abnormal complement activation and an increase in C3 in the initial weeks of pregnancy in women with SLE [12]. Neonatal lupus is an autoimmune disorder caused by transplacental transfer of maternal anti‐Ro/SS‐A and anti‐La/SS‐B antibodies, with cutaneous involvement in 4%–16% and cardiac manifestations in about 2% of cases [11, 12]. Similarly, fetal growth restriction is seen in 20%–30% of the cases, contrasting with the 10% seen in the general population, while approximately 20% of pregnant women with SLE will have miscarriage or stillbirth [11, 12]. Screening echocardiogram is advised at 16 to 28 weeks of gestation to timely diagnose AV blocks, which carry a 17.5% mortality rate [11]. Cautious use of medications is to be made to control SLE flares with minimum fetal effects; hydroxychloroquine has been shown to be effective in preventing cardiac neonatal lupus, preterm deliveries, and intrauterine growth restriction [11]. No features of complications were present, and the baby was healthy in our case.
