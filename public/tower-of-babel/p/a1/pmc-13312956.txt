Genome Scanning Reveals the Genetic Basis of a Color Pattern Morphotype in an Island Population of the European Adder (Vipera berus)
Authors: Nina Casillas, Bent Petersen, Sarah L F Martin, Kjeld Henrik Ophus, Henrik Bringsøe, Jaelle C Brealey, Vanessa C Bieker, Jaime Morin-Lagos, Dag Dolmen, Thomas Madsen, Rasmus Nielsen, Morten E Allentoft, José Cerca, Michael D Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColor polymorphism is an important trait due to its role in defense strategies, feeding habits, environmental responses such as temperature regulation, and overall fitness. While the genetic basis of color polymorphism is well understood in mammals, it remains relatively understudied in nonavian reptiles. Vipera berus, the European adder, is the most widely distributed and northernmost terrestrial snake in the world. Similar to other European vipers, V. berus exhibits a range of dorsal color patterns spanning from zig-zag to complete melanism. However, a unique longitudinal dorsal stripe pattern was recently discovered in the isolated Gossa Island (∼45 km2) population of Norway, where ∼5% of snakes exhibit this distinct pattern instead of the common dorsal zig-zag pattern. In this study, we investigated the genetic basis of the unique phenotype observed on Gossa by combining low-depth whole-genome shotgun sequencing and pairwise FST genome scanning. We discovered a moderately sized, significantly differentiated genomic region containing five associated genes, including the premelanosome gene (PMEL) that encodes a well-known transmembrane glycoprotein that is a key component of melanosome organelles. We implicate a functionally relevant variant in the encoded premelanosome protein. Our results provide insight into the evolution and genetic basis of pigmentation of squamate reptiles using the unbiased approach of genome scanning, a method that has rarely been used in this context.

We provide strong evidence from population genomics that the genetic control of stripe pigmentation in the European adder (Vipera berus) is the PMEL gene that encodes the premelanosome protein. This is only the second time this gene has been implicated in stripe pigmentation in a snake, so the work suggests PMEL may play a universal role in determining squamate skin stripes. The PMEL gene is evolutionarily conserved and important in vertebrate development, but its entire range of functions is still unknown. Thus, this research provides evidence for further work toward PMEL's full functional characterization.

Introduction

The investigation of intraspecific melanin-based polymorphism has captured the interest of researchers across various taxa, as it offers insights into sexual selection, predator–prey interaction, thermoregulation, ecological niches, pattern formation, and mimicry (Murray and Myerscough 1991; Roulin 2004; McKinnon and Pierotti 2010; Murray 2011). Snakes, in particular, have garnered interest due to their remarkable diversity of pattern and color variations that are unparalleled among vertebrates (Allen et al. 2013). While color polymorphism in mammals and birds has been the focus of numerous studies—largely centered on melanocyte biology—our understanding remains incomplete and is well resolved only in a handful of model species. Investigations into the genetic basis of color polymorphism in reptiles have been comparatively limited.

The primary protein targets implicated in studies of nonavian reptile color polymorphism have been pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), agouti-signaling protein (ASIP), premelanosome protein (SILV/PMEL), the tyrosinase gene family, and the melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) (Rosenblum et al. 2004; Corso et al. 2012; Cox et al. 2013; Ducrest et al. 2014; Brown et al. 2022). Although melanocytes do contribute to pattern formation, in reptiles color patterning is more complex due to the presence within the dermis of additional pigmentation cells called chromatophores (Alibardi 2013; Ullate-Agote et al. 2020; Tang et al. 2023). In many reptiles (including most snakes and lizards), chromatophore stratification begins with melanophores at the base of the dermis, producing black pigment patterns (Kuriyama et al. 2020). Ascending from the melanophores are the iridophores, erythrophores, and xanthophores, which are located in the upper layers of the dermis and produce light-scattering effects and red/yellow pigments. These chromatophores are derived from neural crest cells, which migrate in developing embryos and give rise to a vast range of cells in addition to chromatophores (Tang et al. 2023). Reptile pattern diversity is the result of the distribution and layering of chromatophores and melanocytes in the dermis and epidermis (Alibardi 2013; Saenko et al. 2013; Ullate-Agote et al. 2020). While the vertebrate model organism zebrafish has played a significant role in advancing understanding of chromatophores and pattern formation, there has been only limited research on the complex color and pattern morphology of reptiles (Kuriyama et al. 2020).

The European adder (Vipera berus Linnaeus, 1758; family Viperidae) is the most widely distributed terrestrial snake in the world, with its range stretching from Sakhalin Island in the Far East of Russia to Scotland (Lourdais et al. 2013; Horreo and Fitze 2018) and from Northern Greece to above the Arctic Circle (Andersson 2003; Ursenbacher et al. 2006). Due to its northern distribution, in most parts of its range the adder lives at low altitudes, but in, eg southeastern Europe with warmer climatic conditions, V. berus is restricted to high altitudes (1,000 to 2,600 m above sea level; Ursenbacher et al. 2006). Despite the European adder's wide range, by far the most common skin pattern is a dorsal zig-zag (Fig. 1a). In some parts of its range (such as Scandinavia), however, melanism is common, with a population frequency ranging from 10% to >80% (Madsen et al. 2022). The zig-zag pattern had been suggested to be an example of aposematic coloration (Wüster et al. 2004), disruptive coloration (Valkonen et al. 2011; Bauwens and Claus 2019), and resulting in a flicker-fusion effect (Shine and Madsen 1994). Vipera berus is one of the few snakes that show sexual dichromatism from birth, and this dichromatism is maintained in adults, where males have a black zig-zag pattern with white, gray, or light blue coloration while females often have a dark brown zig-zag pattern and light brown body color (Andrén and Nilson 1981; Cui et al. 2016; Madsen et al. 2022).

Both V. berus morphs can be observed on the Norwegian island of Gossa. a) A male (individual M2) and female (individual F1) with the zig-zag dorsal pattern. b) A male (individual M4) and female (individual F4) with the comparatively rare dorsal stripe phenotype. Photos © 2017 by K. H. Ophus.

Apart from melanism, deviations from the zig-zag pattern morphology are considered rare for the European adder, and a study conducted in south-east England between 2005 and 2020 observed only three adders to have a straight dorsal line (Hills 2020). Although this striped phenotype is rare for V. berus, it is common in V. seoanei (Lataste, 1879; 4 Mya divergence from V. berus; Martínez-Freiría et al. 2017; Šmíd and Tolley 2019) and in Vipera aspis (Linnaeus, 1758; 15 Mya divergence from V. berus; Ducrest et al. 2014; Šmíd and Tolley 2019). Similar to Swedish V. berus, melanism in V. aspis is quite common (Ducrest et al. 2014). In an analysis of the POMC and the melanocortin-1-receptor (MC1R) genes, a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) within the 3′-untranslated region (3′UTR) of the POMC gene was discovered to be correlated with the dorsal color polymorphism in V. aspis (Ducrest et al. 2014). Since POMC is a precursor to several hormones, and consequently responsible for several vital functions in the body, it is highly conserved across multicellular animals (Cai and Hruby 2016; Dores et al. 2016).

On the island of Gossa off the west coast of Central Norway (Møre og Romsdal county), a population of V. berus was recorded displaying the striped phenotype (Fig. 1b) in ∼5% of individuals during preliminary field observations in 2017 (2 striped of ca. 42 live individuals) and 2020 (5 striped of 92 live individuals). Gossa, also called Gossen, is ∼45 km2 in area and covered in boreal heathland, moorland, and marsh (Thorsnæs 2024). Several locations have rocky outcrops with crevices that go deep into the earth (K. H. Ophus, personal report). The landscape is speckled with numerous ponds and streams providing habitat for frogs (Rana temporaria), which constitute the main diet of the Gossa adder population (K. H. Ophus, personal report).

Given the limited research on reptile color polymorphism in regards to pigmentation genes outside of melanocytes, this study aimed to determine the genomic basis of the rare striped phenotype that is common in the Gossa population. Our work uses whole-genome sequencing and genome scanning of 76 individuals from the Gossa population, including 19 with a striped phenotype, to further our understanding of pattern development and polymorphism in wild snake populations.

Results

Bioinformatic Processing and Analysis of Population Structure

The 76 combined samples (both field collected and from a natural history collection) were sequenced to a mean genomic depth of 3.4× (minimum: 0.2×, maximum: 12.1×) after mapping against the V. berus reference nuclear genome and removing reads with low-quality alignments (Table S1). After processing the dataset with ANGSD and removing repeat regions, we were left with 1,796,192 variant positions used in the subsequent genome-wide association study (GWAS) analyses. Further filtering, by pruning the variants in high linkage disequilibrium (LD), reduced the dataset to 373,558 variants used for inferring the overall population structure via a principal component analysis (PCA). In the PCA, the top four eigenvectors were displayed to visualize the population structure within Gossa (Fig. 2). Notably, the first four principal components explain 18% of the variation, and the structuring of genetic variation is not associated with the phenotypes of interest. The kinship analysis revealed that 19 of the individuals were related with rxy > 0.3, including the expected close kinship between mothers and their offspring and siblings within the same brood (Table S2). Selecting a single representative of each kin group resulted in a set of 57 unrelated individuals used in a subset of the subsequent analyses.

Population genetic structure of V. berus individuals (n = 76) on the island of Gossa. Displayed are the first four principal components, which account for ∼18% of genetic variation within the Gossa population.

F ST Analyses

The pairwise FST analysis based on the site frequency spectra of the two color morph cohorts including all 76 sequenced individuals resulted in a global weighted FST value of 0.0020. The sliding window analysis considered 123,909 10 kb windows, 124 of which were outliers in the 99.9% quantile (Fig. 3). To identify genomic regions with exceptionally strong genetic differentiation, the outliers were further filtered to include only those with Z-scores >12, resulting in a list of 19 windows (Table 1). Liftover of these windows to a newly available, improved, haplotype-resolved V. berus genome assembly (rVipBer3.hap1.1; Owens et al. 2025) revealed that 90% (17 of 19) of these regions align to a single 250 kb locus of chromosome 2 (Table 1). A second FST analysis of the two color morph cohorts using genomes from the 57 unrelated individuals resulted in largely similar results, but with a smaller global weighted FST value of 0.0017, yielding 25 outlier windows (Table 1). These align to a 1 Mb region of chromosome 2 that completely encompasses the 250 kb region identified in the analysis with all the individuals, as well as smaller, 7 to 20 kb regions on chromosomes 1 and 2. There was considerable overlap between the outlier windows identified in the two FST analyses (Table 1).

GWAS Analysis

The GWAS analysis including related individuals yielded an overall low inflation factor of 1.092 (Fig. S1) and revealed 76 SNPs with highly significant (P < 5e−8) associated with the striped phenotype (Fig. S2). The most significant of these mostly fall within the same genomic windows revealed as extreme outliers in the FST analysis (Table S3). Another GWAS considering only unrelated individuals also yielded a low inflation factor of 1.087 (Fig. S3) and revealed 58 SNPs with highly significant (P < 5e−8) associations with the striped phenotype (Table S4, Fig. S4).

Ab Initio Genome Annotation

After an initial run of RepeatMasker and RepeatModeler, a second run of RepeatMasker and RepeatModeler was performed using the generated model as a custom library input. This second run resulted in masking 491,372,682 bp (32.1%) of the genome assembly. This custom repeat library, as well as the genome assembly sequence, a Boa constrictor transcriptome, protein homology evidence from Squamata, and several run iterations using MAKER2, SNAP, AUGUSTUS, and BUSCO, resulted in a final ab initio annotation containing 7,948 gene models. Owing to the highly fragmented nature of the genome assembly, the BUSCO analysis of 3,354 total BUSCO groups revealed 930 (27.7%) complete BUSCOs, 913 complete and single-copy BUSCOs (27.2%), 17 complete and duplicated BUSCOs (0.5%), 521 fragmented BUSCOs (15.5%), and 1,903 missing BUSCOs (56.8%).

Homology-Based Gene Prediction and Annotation in Outlier Windows

The homology-based prediction of V. berus genes in identified highly differentiated (Z > 12) outlier genomic windows produced the full-length position, sequence, and exon structure of PMEL (premelanosome: 713 aa, 12 exons), DGKA (diacylglycerol kinase alpha: 745 aa, 23 exons), RAB5B (Ras-related protein Rab-5B: 214 aa, 5 exons), DDN (dendrin: 659 aa, 2 exons), MYL6 (myosin light polypeptide 6: 151 aa, 5 exons). Highly differentiated outlier windows from both FST analyses (all sequenced individuals or only unrelated individuals) and containing highly significant SNPs from both GWAS analyses overlapped the genes RAB5B, PMEL, and DGKA. The gene DDN was contained in only those outlier windows from the FST analysis with all sequenced individuals, and MYL6 was contained in only those outlier windows from the FST analysis with only unrelated individuals (Table 1). Notably, all four of these genes were determined to fall within the same ∼1 Mb locus of chromosome 2. Together with the clustering of highly differentiated windows, this indicates that the genomic signal underlying the striped phenotype is concentrated within a single locus, rather than distributed across multiple independent regions. The remaining highly differentiated (Z > 12) outlier genomic windows contained no annotated genes.

Variant Effect Analysis and Inheritance Patterns

The analysis of gene variant effects within the predicted PMEL, DGKA, RAB5B, MYL6, and DDN genes revealed that the Gossa population of V. berus carries no variants causing nonsynonymous substitutions (missense variants) within predicted exons of DGKA, RAB5B, or MYL6. However, one missense variant (Gly206Ser) was detected in PMEL exon 5, which is within the core amyloid fragment domain (Table 2, Fig. 4). Discussion

Reptile pattern polymorphism and the mechanisms driving pattern variation are under-studied topics, as the majority of pigmentation research in this group is limited to genes specific to melanocytes (Rosenblum et al. 2004; Corso et al. 2012; Cox et al. 2013; Kuriyama et al. 2020). With the pattern variation of adders in the Gossa population being so rare outside of Gossa, determining the genomic basis is significant, not only for the Gossa population, but also for all reptiles that exhibit polymorphism in color and pattern. Unlike most previous studies that targeted known pigmentation genes, we took an unbiased approach by scanning the whole genome for associations in a population that displays no overall genetic structure associated with pigmentation phenotype, finding a single genomic region with significant genetic differentiation containing several genes that are strong candidates for playing roles in the Gossa stripe morph phenotype. Our discovery that PMEL likely plays a role in V. berus pattern polymorphism broadens our understanding of the genetic components at the basis of these polymorphisms in snakes.

Most noteworthy is the identification of PMEL in our genome scanning analyses. This gene encodes a type I transmembrane glycoprotein called the “Pre-melanosomal protein,” which is localized to the melanosome and plays a role in eumelanin production, although the gene is also expressed in the outermost layer of the retina and the basal ganglia (Hodges et al. 2023) and may also have other functions (Brunberg et al. 2006; Fowler et al. 2006; Chrystal et al. 2021). Because an aspect of pattern formation depends on the ability of pigment to bind to vesicles in the melanocytes and chromatophores, PMEL plays a vital role in the development of melanosomes, and therefore color pattern morphology, by creating tight parallel sheets of fibrils for melanin to synthesize and adhere to in stages III and IV of melanosome biogenesis (Watt et al. 2013; Bissig et al. 2016). These tight fibrils give the melanosomes their ellipsoidal shape, characteristic of the darkly pigmented eumelanin. Pheomelanin-producing melanocytes (responsible for red and yellow pigments in skin) lack this ellipsoidal shape and are instead spherical, as was observed in PMEL knock-out mice (Hellström et al. 2011).

PMEL is involved in numerous pigmentation disorders in a variety of species such as chickens, horses, cattle, dogs, mice, and zebrafish (Drögemüller et al. 2010; Watt et al. 2011; Claerhout et al. 2018), caused by both loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutations (Chrystal et al. 2021). Our association of the V. berus stripe morph phenotype with a single amino acid substitution at the border between the PMEL transmembrane region and the cytoplasmic domain is interesting, since mutations in or near the transmembrane domain have already been identified in pigmentation disorders in both chickens (Dominant White; Karlsson et al. 2009) and horses (Silver; Brunberg et al. 2006). Previous work has suggested that mutations in PMEL's well-conserved transmembrane domain (Brunberg et al. 2006) produce divergent forms of amyloid fibrils that reduce melanocyte pigmentation and melanocytes that are depleted of melanosomes (Brumbaugh 1971) and may even be toxic to the melanocytes (Watt et al. 2011).

PMEL was very recently reported as the gene underlying the Terrazzo morph phenotype in the corn snake (Pantherophis guttatus) system (Tzika et al. 2024). In that work, a 3 bp polymorphism in the PMEL gene sequence was shown to be responsible for the Terrazzo skin pattern, which is characterized by stripes, in contrast to the blotched pattern of the wild type. This multinucleotide polymorphism introduces a premature stop codon that produces a truncated premelanosome protein product lacking the Kringle-like domain, the transmembrane helix, and the cytoplasmic domain at carboxyl-terminal end, which ultimately results in the protein's reduced expression. That work also used gene-editing and single-cell transcriptomics to provide strong evidence that PMEL is involved in both chromatophore patterning during corn snake embryonic development and melanophore melanogenesis (Tzika et al. 2024).

We determined that the V. berus PMEL protein product is 713 aa in length, as in the P. guttatus genome. We hypothesize that underlying the V. berus striped phenotype is a missense variant within the 713-aa PMEL protein. This Gly206Ser amino acid substitution falls specifically within PMEL's core amyloid-forming (CAF) domain. Such amyloids play a critical role in melanosomes by providing a scaffold for melanin deposition, which is essential for pigmentation (Yanagisawa et al. 2025). A previous study of the human PMEL protein identified a different A Gly > Ser variant, 27 residues downstream and also in the CAF domain, which was found to alter amyloid formation by changing the protein's β-sheet organization and thereby enhancing polymerization capacity (Yanagisawa et al. 2025).

Beyond PMEL, several additional genes (DGKA, RAB5B, DDN, and MYL6) were identified within highly differentiated outlier windows. However, our results strongly suggest that these genes do not represent independent loci underlying the striped phenotype. Instead, liftover of outlier windows to the improved, haplotype-resolved genome assembly revealed that the vast majority of differentiated regions, including all candidate genes, fall within a single ∼250 kb to 1 Mb locus on chromosome 2. This physical clustering indicates that the observed signals likely reflect a single underlying genomic region rather than multiple independent genomic targets. In this context, the most parsimonious explanation is that the missense variant identified in PMEL represents the primary causal mutation underlying the striped phenotype, while the signals observed in DGKA, RAB5B, DDN, and MYL6 are due to physical linkage and genetic hitchhiking within the same genomic region. Such clustering of functionally related genes within a genomic region is not unexpected, particularly for genes involved in pigmentation or cellular processes associated with melanogenesis.

DGKA encodes an enzyme that phosphorylates diacylglycerol to phosphatidic acid and is involved in lipid-mediated signaling pathways that can influence melanogenesis (Kawaguchi et al. 2012; Sakane et al. 2021). RAB5B is an early endosomal Rab GTPase and regulator of endocytic trafficking, its silencing impairs melanocore uptake by keratinocytes (Correia et al. 2018). MYL6 encodes a component of the myosin motor complex involved in muscle and nonmuscle cytoskeletal function. MYL6 is expressed in smooth muscle and various nonmuscle tissues, where it contributes to actin-based contractility and cellular motion (Heissler and Sellers 2014). Similarly, DDN encodes a cytoskeletal/scaffold protein predominantly expressed in neurons and in the kidney (Herb et al. 1997). Although actin–myosin machinery is important for pigment cell processes (Ripoll et al. 2018), we are not aware of any experimental evidence specifically linking MYL6 or DDN to melanocyte function, pigmentation processes, or skin patterning in snakes or other vertebrates.

Although these additional genes have functions that could plausibly relate to pigmentation biology or chromatophore dynamics, we find no direct evidence from our data supporting a causal role for them in the striped phenotype. However, no missense variants were detected in DGKA, RAB5B, or MYL6, and although several missense variants were identified in DDN, there is currently no clear evidence linking this gene to pigmentation or pattern formation. Taken together, these observations support a model in which these genes represent linked passengers within the same genomic region rather than independent contributors to phenotype determination.

We cannot exclude the possibility that regulatory variants within this locus influence the expression of multiple genes; however, the convergence of FST outliers, GWAS signals, and a clear functional candidate mutation within PMEL strongly supports a model in which a single major-effect locus underlies the striped phenotype. Although DGKA occurs nearby PMEL in the corn snake genome, the amino acid variants in the corn snake Terrazzo morph individuals were not predicted to produce changes in the domain structure of the DGKA protein product (Tzika et al. 2024). Similarly, the V. berus genomes sequenced in this study contained no missense variants in the DGKA gene, nor in the MYL6, or RAB5B genes. Still there may be noncoding variants outside the gene regions that affect the expression of these genes and thus play some meaningful role in the striped phenotype. Future studies incorporating transcriptomic data and improved genome annotation will be valuable for disentangling potential regulatory interactions within this region and confirming the extent to which linked genes contribute, if at all, to phenotypic variation.

In an exploratory analysis of color polymorphism in the closely related asp viper (V. aspis), two genes (POMC and MC1R) were screened to test for genotype–phenotype correlation (Ducrest et al. 2014). Both genes are related to melanin-specific pigmentation; however, POMC also contributes to several other functions and hormones (Cai and Hruby 2016). Although there is evidence that the POMC gene is involved in pattern variation in V. aspis (Ducrest et al. 2014), unfortunately the location/sequence for the POMC gene could not be recovered from the genome annotation of V. berus and, therefore, could not be tested for associations with the striped phenotype in the Gossa population. The annotation used in this study resulted in only 7,948 gene models, which falls well short of the estimated 21,194 predicted protein-coding genes found in Deinagkistrodon acutus (Günther, 1888), another member of the family Viperidae (Yin et al. 2016). Future work is needed to determine whether POMC also plays a role in color polymorphism in V. berus.

In many snake species, longitudinal stripes assist in a rapid-escape strategy by disrupting the predator's ability to maintain a focal point on the body when the snake escapes (Jackson et al. 1976; Madsen et al. 2022). Though the striped phenotype is perhaps more conspicuous in closed habitats, it is more likely to have a benefit in open and wetland habitats (Allen et al. 2013), such as are common on Gossa. This is supported by striped individuals of the harmless viperine snake (Natrix maura, Linnaeus, 1758) having been shown to flee from approaching predators at further distances than zig-zag individuals (Santos et al. 2018). In that same study of N. maura, striped individuals were almost exclusively found in more open habitats, while the zig-zag counterparts were found in all habitats (Santos et al. 2018). Thus, it is possible that the V. berus population on Gossa face similar selective pressure since Gossa is largely covered in heathland, moorland and marsh. Therefore, it would be interesting for future studies to search for further evidence of selective sweeps in the aforementioned genomic regions, especially those containing PMEL, DGKA, and RAB5B.

However, a recent study (Madsen et al. 2022) suggested that when zig-zag adders are moving, the pattern creates a flicker-fusion effect (Shine and Madsen 1994). This effect makes it harder for a visual hunting predator to focus on a reference point or track the moving adder because the dorsal zig-zag blurs into a dorsal stripe that appears to stay stationary while the snake moves away. From this view, the striped pattern on some of Gossa adders most likely provides just as effective an antipredator effect as does the zig-zag morph. It should be noted that the geographical origin of the striped morph of V. berus on the island is not known. There exists a photo of an approximately striped adder specimen from the mainland ∼2 km away, and an observation has been made of a striped adder on a neighboring island further away (D. Dolmen, personal report).

Although closely related snakes have evolved the striped and melanistic phenotypes in addition to the zig-zag phenotype, they appear to differ in the specific genetic mechanisms used to achieve this variation. In this study, we discovered a single, moderately sized, gene-containing genomic region with significant association with the striped phenotype on Gossa. The ability of V. berus to adapt color patterns to local environments is highly beneficial and as a result, the species has evolved phenotypes similar to other species within Vipera. We provide the second report of PMEL as a molecular control of pattern variation in snakes, here determining the dorsal pattern of the unique stripe morphs observed at high frequency in Gossa's V. berus population. Further genomic resources for this organism will be highly beneficial for further contextualizing our findings and for exploring the extreme outlier windows we identify with an improved genome annotation based on mRNA evidence from the taxon of interest.

Materials and Methods

Sample Acquisition

During the field seasons of 2017 to 2021, V. berus snakes were captured on the island of Gossa, Norway under permits 2017/8183, 2,638 from the Norwegian Environment Agency, granted to KHO. Ideal locations on the island were targeted, which included south-facing rocky outcrops, where V. berus naturally hibernates. After capture, each snake was photographed (Figs. S5 to S16), individually identified using their head scalation pattern, marked with water-based, nontoxic marker to avoid recaptures, then released. When possible, the individual's sex and capture location were recorded (Table S1). DNA samples were collected using buccal swabs (Boca Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol, which were then placed into a collection tube with a silica capsule. Additionally, ventral scale clippings were collected from each individual and stored in 95% ethanol. Further skin tissue samples were taken from four individuals collected in 2016 to 2017 and stored in ethanol in the vertebrate collections at the NTNU University Museum (Hårsaker 2025; Table S1). To further explore the basis of the striped morph, during the 2017 fieldwork, two pregnant striped adders (F1S and F2S) were collected and maintained under observation and care in captivity (terraria) from August 2017 to April 2018. A total of 14 neonates were born in captivity in August 2017 (7 in each brood). Within the brood of individual F1S, 5 out of 7 (71%) neonates were striped (Fig. S11). In contrast, within the brood of individual F2S all 7 individuals were zig-zag morphs (Fig. S12). Shed skin was collected from each of the neonates and then subjected to DNA extraction and genomic sequencing. A total of 76 snakes were sequenced, 57 having the zig-zag phenotype (36 males and 21 females) and 19 having the striped phenotype (10 males and 9 females; Table S1).

DNA Extraction, Library Preparation, and Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from buccal swabs, scale clippings, and museum skin samples with the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue extraction kit following the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted DNA was sheared using the Covaris ME220 or Bioruptor Pico sonication instruments with a target fragmentation length of 300 bp. Double-stranded DNA genomic libraries were constructed using blunt-end adapters (Carøe et al. 2018). High-throughput sequencing was performed initially on the Illumina MiniSeq platform in 75 bp paired-end format, then at the Norwegian National Sequencing Centre on the Illumina HiSeq × platform in 150-bp paired-end format, and at the Novogene Europe sequencing facility on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in 150 bp paired-end format.

Ab initio Genome Annotation and Repeat Masking

Because no gene annotation is available for the V. berus reference genome (Vber.be_1.0), we used an ab initio approach to obtain an annotation of the masked genome using MAKER v3.01.03, SNAP and Augustus (Korf 2004; Stanke et al. 2006; Cantarel et al. 2008). Prior to the ab initio genome annotation, repeats and low-complexity regions were masked from the genome assembly using RepeatMasker v4.1.2-p1 (Smit et al. 2013–2015) to apply the taxon-specific “common viper” subset of the curated Dfam/Repbase repeat database. About 3.2% of the genome assembly (49,031,054 bp) was masked in this initial process. Specific repetitive elements remaining in the masked genome were predicted de novo using RepeatModeler v2.0.2a (Flynn et al. 2020). Subsequently, a second round of RepeatMasker was performed on the previously masked genome, using the model generated from RepeatModeler as a custom library input. This custom repeat library and the annotated repeat regions of the V. berus genome assembly were extracted for later use.

Genome annotation was performed using the genome annotation pipeline MAKER2 v2.31.9 (Holt and Yandell 2011) in an iterative process. For the first round of annotation, the genome assembly file, the custom repeat library from the previously described repeat masking process, a B. constrictor transcriptome (Card et al. 2019) used as alternative expressed-sequence tag (EST) evidence, and protein homology evidence from 2,689 unique experimentally verified protein sequences from Squamata (lizards and snakes) [TaxID:8509] were used as the input files to the annotation pipeline. The custom repeat library was used to soft mask the repetitive regions. Additional regions with low complexity were soft masked using RepeatMasker. Gene predictors SNAP v2013-11-29 (Korf 2004) and AUGUSTUS v3.3.1 (Stanke et al. 2006) were trained by performing iterative runs of MAKER2 as recommended by the authors (Cantarel et al. 2008). As the first round of annotation was based on the alignment of the EST evidence to the genome, the est2genome option in the MAKER2 control file was set to allow MAKER2 to infer gene models directly from the EST evidence. The protein2genome option was also enabled to allow MAKER2 to infer predictions from protein homology. After the completion of the first round of annotations, gene models were retained and used to train SNAP to obtain a SNAP HMM file. We then trained AUGUSTUS using BUSCO v5.1.3 (Manni et al. 2021). First, training sequences were identified using the gene models predicted by MAKER2 from the first run by excising regions with mRNA annotations and 1,000 bp on either side. These were used to run BUSCO using the “tetrapoda_odb10' set of conserved genes. After training both SNAP and Augustus, MAKER2 was run again, with SNAP hmm and Augustus files. A total of three rounds of BUSCO/Augustus retrainings and four MAKER2 iterations were performed.

Bioinformatic Data Processing, Read Mapping, and Variant Discovery

The pipeline Paleomix v1.3.6 was selected for bioinformatic processing of the raw genomic sequencing data due to its efficiency in handling datasets that originate from a combination of modern and degraded samples (Schubert et al. 2014). Initially, AdapterRemoval v2.3.2 (Schubert et al. 2016) was used to trim adapters, as low-quality bases, and ambiguous bases from the 3′ end of sequence reads, as well as to merge overlapping read pairs. The trimmed sequences were then mapped against an available highly fragmented V. berus reference genome assembly (Vber.be_1.0; NCBI accession code GCA_000800605.1) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) v0.7.16a (Li and Durbin 2010) with the backtrack algorithm, allowing for a minimum map quality of 30. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) duplicates were removed using Picard Tools v2.9.1 (Broad Institute 2020). Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK) v3.7 (McKenna et al. 2010) was used to perform local sequence alignment around indels. Mean sequencing depth was assessed at all sites in the reference genome using samtools v1.16.1 (Li et al. 2009).

The software package ANGSD v0.934 (Korneliussen et al. 2014) was used to parse the BAM files for variant discovery and genotype likelihood (GL) estimation after filtering the mapped genomic sequences, requiring a minimum phred-scaled base quality 20 (-minQ 20) and a sequence mapping quality of 25 (-minMapQ 25). Although typically related individuals should be removed from population genetic analyses, we took a less conservative approach, electing to include both the neonates and their mothers so as to increase the number of striped individuals in our analysis. PCR duplicates and mapped sequences with multiple best hits were removed (-remove_bads -uniqueOnly). ANGSD was used to calculate GLs using the algorithm from GATK (McKenna et al. 2010), infer and output the major and minor alleles at each variant site (-doMajorMinor 1 -doGeno 1), require a minimum SNP P-value of 1e−6 (-SNP_pval), and calculate genotype posterior probability using the allele frequency as a prior (-doPost 1). For each sample, a minimum of two high-quality mapped reads was required to estimate the GL at a particular site (-setMinDepthInd 2). Only sites that were covered by at least two high-quality mapping sequences in at least half the individuals (-minInd 38) and only genotypes with a 95% posterior probability (-postCutoff 0.95) were included in downstream analyses. Finally, ANGSD was used to generate a plink-compatible output file (-doPlink). Previously identified V. berus genome regions containing transposable elements and repeats were also removed from all downstream analyses. This process resulted in the GLs at what we hereafter refer to as the “final dataset of filtered SNP sites.”

Principal Components Analysis of Population Structure

PLINK v1.90 (Purcell et al. 2007) was used to calculate pairwise LD within the final dataset of filtered SNP sites with the (-r2) command. To allow unrecognized chromosome codes (-allow-extra-chr) was included, as well as (-allow-no-sex), which keeps the phenotype values of missing-sex samples. A threshold of 0 was used for the analysis (-ld-window-r2 0) to visualize the decay of r2 in LD. The average LD distances were plotted in a 100 kb window (-ld-window-kb 100) with a window limit of 100 (-ld-window 100) to limit the variant count. From this plot, the threshold for pruning was determined (Fig. S17). PLINK was then used to establish an independent pairwise cut-off at r2 = 0.4, using 25 kb windows and a step size of 5 kb. To estimate the covariance matrix and allele frequencies, the variants remaining after pruning were analyzed by the software PCAngsd v1.03 (Meisner and Albrechtsen 2018, 2019). PCAngsd uses the GLs from heterogeneous populations to infer population structure using PCA of low-depth genomic sequencing data. The covariance matrix was then imported into R v4.0.5 to calculate the eigenvectors and eigenvalues using the eigen function included in the base R. A PCA plot was created with ggplot2 v3.3.5 (Wickham 2016) using the eigenvectors.

Kinship Analysis

To estimate kinship between individuals, angsd was run exactly as before except that only sites with a minor allele frequency of at least 5% (-domaf 1 -minmaf 0.05) were utilized. The software ngsRelate v2 (Korneliussen and Moltke 2015) was used to estimate pairwise relatedness rxy (Hedrick and Lacy 2015) among the 76 individuals. For sets of individuals with rxy > 0.3, only the individual with the highest mean sequencing depth was selected, resulting in a set of 57 genomes from unrelated individuals, of which 14 were striped and 43 were zig-zag.

Genome Scanning Analysis

An FST-based genome scanning approach was selected in order to identify genomic regions that are highly differentiated in a comparison of striped versus zig-zag individuals. The analysis was first conducted using the entire set of all sequenced individuals and then a second time using a subset of only unrelated individuals. To estimate the folded site frequency spectrum (SFS) from the striped and unstriped cohorts, first the site allele frequency (saf) likelihoods were calculated using the GLs from the final dataset of filtered SNP sites using realSFS in ANGSD v0.934 (Korneliussen et al. 2014) with the flag -doSaf 1. The V. berus reference genome sequence was used in place of the ancestral state (assigned using the flag -anc), thus using the reference allele as a proxy to define a consistent orientation of variants in the folded SFS. The GATK model was implemented using the -GL 2 option and the quality threshold was set using -minMapQ 25 to ignore reads with a mapping quality score below 25. Further filtering was applied, requiring a minimum quality score of 20 using -minQ 20, removing duplicate reads (-remove_bads 1) and discarding sites with multiple best hits (-uniqueOnly 1). Then, using the striped and unstriped site allele frequencies, the 2D SFS was calculated. The -fold option was included to estimate the folded SFS since no ancestral state was used. The FST index was calculated from the allele frequencies from both striped and unstriped sub-populations as well as the folded 2D SFS using the Hudson estimation method (-whichFST 1) in realSFS. The realSFS FST statistics were then used to calculate weighted FST values for each genomic window. Using the realSFS stat2 function, FST was calculated in nonoverlapping sliding windows of length 10 kb (-win 10000 and -step 10000). The results from the FST analysis were refined by correcting negative FST values to zero and removing windows with fewer than 100 variants. To identify outliers and potential genes of interest, Z-scores were calculated and ranked using the quantile function in base R. The extreme outlier windows (Z-scores >12) were extracted from the V. berus reference genome. The genome annotation was used to identify genes positioned within outlier regions.

GWAS Analysis

To complement the FST-based genome scanning, a GWAS analysis approach was also implemented, again comparing the striped cohort versus the zig-zag cohort within the set of all sequenced individuals. For this, GLs from the final dataset of filtered SNP sites were utilized, including both LD-pruned and unpruned SNP data. Genotype probabilities were imputed from the likelihoods with Beagle v.3.3.2 (Browning et al. 2018) and converted to genotype dosages. GWAS was performed with GEMMA (Zhou and Stephens 2012). A centered genotype relatedness matrix was included in the GEMMA model to account for the high relatedness among individuals in the population. The genomic inflation factor (λ) was calculated and results were visualized with Q–Q and Manhattan plots, generated with the qqman package in R (Turner 2018). The GWAS analysis was performed again with the set of unrelated individuals.

Homology-Based Gene Prediction and Annotation in Outlier Windows

As particular genes within outlier windows became of interest, we determined many of these to be only partially annotated by the BUSCO/Augustus/MAKER2 annotations. Thus Exonerate v2.4.0 protein2genome algorithm was used to predict the location and full extent of protein-coding genes within the identified outliers windows of interest, using homology evidence from all RefSeq protein sequences from P. guttatus (NCBI RefSeq accession GCF_029531705.1). Within these outlier windows, V. berus gene annotations from Exonerate were further considered if they matched with >85% identity and >90% similarity along the entire length of a P. guttatus gene. The exon structures and amino acid sequences of the genes by Exonerate were confirmed by extracting the nucleotide sequence of each exon using the getfasta algorithm of BEDTools (Quinlan and Hall 2010) and then using using the translate algorithm of SeqKit (Shen et al. 2016) to obtain the amino acid sequence. In each case, the amino acid sequence was then compared to that of the corresponding P. guttatus RefSeq protein.

Variant Effect Analysis

To explore the potential effects of variants within the genes of interest identified in the extreme outlier windows, the HaplotypeCaller algorithm within the Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK) v4.3 (McKenna et al. 2010) was used to create sample-specific GVCF files. Then GATK GenotypeGVCFs was run using default settings (eg –sample-ploidy 2, –heterozygosity 0.001, –indel-heterozygosity 1.25e-4, –heterozygosity-stdev 0.01, –standard-min-confidence-threshold-for-calling 30.0, –max-alternate-alleles 6, and –max-genotype-count 1024) to produce a multisample VCF file containing genomic variants and the genotypes for each sample. The software snpEff v5.2c (Cingolani et al. 2012) was used to annotate genomic variants within the outlier regions annotated as genes. Only the three predicted full-length genes (PMEL, DGKA, DDN) predicted to overlap with these windows were considered, and only missense variants were analyzed. VCFtools v0.1.17 (Danecek et al. 2011) was used to filter out individual genotypes supported by fewer than 2 sequencing reads or a Phred-scaled genotype quality <15. The InterProScan (Jones et al. 2014) website was used to identify and assign functional domains within the predicted amino acid sequence of the V. berus PMEL gene.

Liftover to Improved Genome Assembly

To better understand the proximity of the genomic regions identified in this study, scaffolds of the highly fragmented V. berus reference genome assembly (Vber.be_1.0) were aligned to haplotype 1 of an improved, haplotype-resolved assembly (rVipBer3.hap1.1; Owens et al. 2025) that became available late in the life of the present project. This improved assembly lacks a gene annotation at present. To achieve the liftover, MUMmer v4.0.1 (Marçais et al. 2018) was used with the options –maxmatch -l 100 -c 500 to identify all maximal exact matches between the reference and query sequences with a minimum seed length of 100 bp and requiring matches to form alignment clusters of at least 500 bp. The delta-filter algorithm −1 option was used to retain only the best mutually consistent set of alignments such that each region participates in at most one alignment.
