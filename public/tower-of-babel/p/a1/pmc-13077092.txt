HyMSS-GAD: a hybrid multi-stage framework for multi-view graph anomaly detection with structural, contextual, and geometric reasoning
Authors: Wasim Khan, Nadhem Ebrahim, Mourad Elloumi, Kamel Brahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graph anomaly detection has become an important task in discovering abnormal patterns within attributed networks, where anomalies can occur due to structural, contextual, or geometric mismatch. Current methods are mainly based on either a reconstruction-based or contrastive objective, which seldom consider the relationship between heterogeneous modalities and higher-order graph geometry. To address this gap, we present HyMSS-GAD, a Hybrid Multi-Stage Framework for Graph Anomaly Detection that combines contextual, structural, and geometric reasoning in a five-step pipeline. First, a cross-modal contrastive learning module learns aligned representations from feature and topology-based modalities, utilizing InfoNCE and alignment regularization. Second, a motif-based structural reconstruction module discovers higher-order connectivity roles with deterministic motif enumeration and autoencoder based reconstruction. Third, we apply an attention-driven fusion mechanism to dynamically combine contextual and structural embeddings into a single representation. Fourth, we introduce a curvature-aware decoder to predict and reconstruct Ollivier–Ricci curvature for geometry-based anomaly detection within graph manifolds. Finally, we develop a multi-view anomaly scoring strategy to combine contextual, structural, and geometric residuals into an interpretable anomaly score. In-depth evaluations conducted on five standard benchmark datasets namely, Cora, Citeseer, PubMed, ACM, and Amazon, show that HyMSS-GAD consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art baseline models. Moreover, curvature residuals offer an increased degree of interpretability by indicating the bridge node regions of communities and the anomalous boundary regions. Overall, HyMSS-GAD is shown to be a scalable, explainable, geometrically informed model for graph anomaly detection on a set of diverse attributed networks.

Introduction

Anomaly detection in attributed networks is of increasingly greater value in real-world applications, spanning, for example, fraud detection, cybersecurity, recommender systems, and scientific knowledge deduction1. Attributed networks are a richer generalization of classical graphs in that every node in an attributed network is connected through even richer structure and attribute information. Although this formalism strengthens the structural and semantic expressive capacity of a graph model, there are unknown ways that might emerge for anomalous behaviors. A graph may have abnormal structural patterns for example abnormal semantics, or subtle geometric anomalies that also incentivize the anomalies to be potentially multifaceted2,3.

A major shortcoming in numerous existing graph-based anomaly detection models is the use of simplifying modeling assumptions that do not reflect the complexities of real network data. First and foremost, the simplification that connected nodes are similar with respect to attribute profiles or class memberships known as the homophily assumption. While the homophily assumption may be sustainable in many social graphs and citation networks, it quickly fails in virtually all experimental examples in which the network data is heterogeneous and semantically diverse. In these cases of data diversity, connected nodes may bear functionally different roles or ultimately be different in terms of attribute profiles, thus increasing the likelihood of false positives or unidentified anomalies when these traditional homophily-driven models are applied4. To further complicate matters, homophily based models typically improve their detection capacity when applying neighborhood smoothness as an inductive bias. This further constrains the modes for inaccurately identifying scenarios of anomalies resulting from contextual incongruency as not simply statistical outliers in the local neighborhood context5. In addition, a substantial body of the existing literature seems to assess graph-attribute data in isolation, wherein researchers focus on either the topology of the graph or the attribute space of the node data, with a few notable exceptions. While structure-based approaches address anomalies relating to unusual edge arrangements and neighboring configurations, they do not properly account for semantic discrepancies in node features6. On the other hand, attribute-based models do not consider the role that a node’s placement in the network topology may play in its behaviors or interactions. As such, both approaches produce a one-dimensional model of the data and are not suited to identify complex or subtle anomalies that arise through interdependent behaviors across components. For example, a node may look normal in terms of its individual features or connections but might be anomalous when its semantic role does not match its structural setting7.

Adding to this problem is the fact that there are many multi-modal models that use static or hard-coded fusion strategies. These strategies tend to aggregate embeddings or anomaly scores across an embedding space and then combine the information from the different modalities using static or fixed strategies such as averaging, concatenation, or fixed-weight aggregation8. In this situation, these strategies carry with them the assumption that the modalities capture information that is equally important through all nodes and contexts where the representation is employed, often ignoring the fact that graphs are more or less contextual and dynamic9. In practice, the relative importance of structural, semantic, or geometric information might vary greatly between nodes depending on the local density, community structure, or role semantics. Failing to capture this leads to a loss of discriminative capacity of the model as well as losses in interpretability. The need for interpretability remains a critical aspect of anomaly detection systems. Additionally, many deep models only yield scalar anomaly scores with no attribution of the specific features, modalities, or graph regions. This opaqueness limits their use in areas where explainable decision-making is critical, such as finance, security, or healthcare. Also, models whose high-order structural information, such as motif frequencies, or curvature based topology, can have high costs of computing either in terms of computing complexity for motif enumeration, or by the time it takes to do the calculation of curvature. These costs can be intractable in the case of large networks or high-dimensional attribute spaces, where the time to compute is prohibitive for practical application especially in emergency and decision-poor environments10,11.

To overcome the limitations of current graph-based anomaly detection models, we presented a unified and modular framework for anomaly detection within homogeneous and heterogeneous attributed networks. Our proposed framework uses a hybrid pipeline architecture for integrating functional aspects of node behavior from multiple views of node representation of dimensionality (semantic, structural, and geometric) into a cohesive, interpretable detection model. Instead of a single embedding space or merely aggregating between every node’s attributes temporarily, our framework is a sequence of interoperable yet functionally distinct modules, each designed to encapsulate one form of anomaly and allow inter-modal flow of information. We began with a contextual contrastive learning module to define semantic misalignments from distinct node representations on both the attribute-based and structure-based perspectives to discover edge anomalies. The contextual contrastive learning module aligns the representation of the nodes from attribute-based and structure-based views using a self-supervised contrastive learning paradigm over ego-subgraphs. Each node is represented in the attribute and topology dual space, and the contrastive loss function encourages similarities in the views and dissimilarities across node representations. In contrast to standard neighborhood-averaging approaches, this method does not use the homophily assumption and, instead, learns representations that are tuned to latent discrepancies between a node’s semantic identity and its topological context. Therefore, this module is particularly powerful at recognizing contextual anomalies that occur when the content of a node diverges from the structural expectations of its neighbors.

We deal with higher-order structural modeling that is able to capture local subgraph regularities by embedding a profile of each node’s motif participation. Structural motifs, such as triangles, stars, and four-node cliques, would be the basic representations of local topology and we can elicit rich information about a node’s role in the overall network structure. The model produces a deterministic motif vector for every node and feeds it into a shallow autoencoder, where reconstruction loss would be used as a proxy measurement for structural deviation. A node, which could either be signaled as abnormal by a missing set of expected connections, or nodes that are part of rare structural configurations, will have a higher reconstruction residual. This module produces a highly discriminate structural anomaly signal and can also be effective for detecting anomalies in one topology given another, implying a topological anomaly that would not be revealed from simple degree or neighborhood statistics. After obtaining both the semantic and structure representations from the above-mentioned modules, we will fuse these through an adaptive attention-based fusion scheme. By an attention-based fusion scheme, we mean one that incorporates learning node-specific attention coefficients for the two representations rather than fixed-weight or uniform fusion. The ability to flexibly highlight structural signals where topological divergence is high and semantic information in cases where structural signals are either absent or exhibit weak presence makes adaptive attention-based fusion useful. The algorithm ensures the fusion approach for anomaly detection is sensitive to the idiosyncratic nature of each node’s local context, which increases robustness and reduces false positives.

To augment the model’s performance in detecting global and topological outliers, we incorporate a geometric consistency module into the pipeline. This module is based on the notion of graph curvature, more specifically, Ollivier-Ricci curvature, which is a metric for measuring the similarity between the local neighborhoods of connected nodes. The model uses fused node embeddings to cause curvature values associated with edges to be reconstructed. The greater the reconstruction error, the more the node breaks the geometric smoothness of the graph, as in being a bottleneck, boundary node, or bridge between communities. These types of anomalies would be missed in most traditional local or neighborhood-based methods but are essential in frameworks involving communication networks and bioinformatics, which include global structure. Lastly, the outputs for all modules above, each producing a residual or reconstruction error value indicating the extent of anomaly from a given viewpoint, are fused in the anomaly scoring module. The unified scalar anomaly score for each node is a weighted sum of the residuals and is fully controlled by hyperparameters in our proposal or learnable weights, which can dictate how much of the individual contributions go into the final anomaly score. The key aspect is that the model provides interpretable residuals to obtain the per-modality residuals, and downstream tasks can then map the individual anomaly to a cause: semantic inconsistency, structural irregularity, or geometrical abnormality. This explainability is essential when further analysis is expected in real-application scenarios, where translation of the detected anomaly into an actionable insight is sought, and simply confirming its detection is not enough.

By employing a synergetic integration of multi-modal signals, the systematic multi-faceted framework provides a holistic, interpretable, and scalable solution to the extensively complicated problem of anomaly detection in attributed networks. Each module works in a complementary manner, with information moving in a coherent manner through a similar semantics, and meaning towards the hierarchical representation, with a class or model of anomaly as the destination; thereby, achieving the highest detection fidelity while managing to retain modularity, and agent-based or direct extension where applicable. The case of novelty in this research is the incorporation of all or multiple pieces of views or models around anomalies holistically through a planned and structured pipeline, but conscious in preserving some interpretability around each part of the modular framework, while retaining the possible chance of modularity, and easy interconnectedness toward scaling. By employing the following multitude of anomaly views, contextual contrastive learning, structural motif reconstruction, adaptive view fusion, geometric representation, and residual-based scoring, the architecture around anomalies has the potential to leverage many of the standard views towards detecting numerous anomalies. The modular nature had the intent of not only its own generalizability to other settings, but also its robustness to heterophily in nodes, along with its efficiency in obtaining an output when concerned with practical-scale networks. Finally, because of the specific parts of modules that can be used to enhance them to be domain-based, specific anomalies can be easily created, tailored, and developed as indicated. Related work

Recent studies on graph anomaly detection (GAD) can be categorized by their methodology. In particular, we discuss four major directions: (1) reconstruction-based approaches, (2) contrastive learning for graph anomalies, (3) motif-based structural representations, and (4) curvature-aware or geometry-enhanced methods. Below we review representative works in each category, focusing on advancements in the last five years from top venues.

Reconstruction-based graph anomaly detection

Early approaches to anomaly detection in attributed graphs employed shallow models. For example, Li et al.12 introduced RADAR and used residual analysis to flag nodes with attribute values inconsistent with their neighborhood. Similarly, Peng et al. introduced ANOMALOUS13, which performs CUR decomposition and residual analysis to jointly select anomaly-relevant attributes and detect outliers. These traditional methods, however, could not fully model complex interactions between graph structure and node attributes. To overcome these limitations, deep reconstruction-based models have been proposed. Ding et al. presented DOMINANT14, the first deep GAD model that uses a graph convolutional autoencoder to jointly reconstruct node attributes and graph structure, identifying anomalies via high reconstruction error. Following this, one-class GNN frameworks were explored and Wang et al.’s OCGNN15 combines GNN encoders with a one-class objective to learn a decision boundary around normal nodes. Another line of work incorporates graph community or subspace information: Luo et al. proposed ComGA16, which embeds nodes while accounting for community structure and detects anomalies as those poorly reconstructed within their community context.

The adversarial training paradigm has also been applied to improve robustness. Tian and Zhang’s ARANE17 model trains an attributed network embedding with an adversarial regularizer to better separate anomalies from normal nodes. More recent reconstruction-based methods enhance model expressiveness and tackle anomaly masking. Roy et al. proposed GAD-NR18, a neighborhood reconstruction framework where two decoders separately reconstruct a node’s neighborhood structure and attributes and the nodes poorly reconstructed in either view are flagged as anomalies. Another notable advance is the use of denoising stages. He et al. introduced ADAGAD19, a two-stage autoencoder that first pretrains on an anomaly-“denoised” graph using graph data augmentations to dilute abnormal patterns, and then fine-tunes on the original graph.

Contrastive learning for graph representation and anomaly detection

Inspired by self-supervised learning successes, recent work leverages contrastive learning to learn informative graph representations for anomaly detection. The core idea is to train the model to differentiate between “positive” views of the same node and “negative” views, so that normal nodes can be distinguished by their agreement with their context. CoLA (Contrastive Anomaly Detection on Attributed Networks)20 by Liu et al. is a classical example as it constructs local subgraph views for each target node and uses a contrastive loss to ensure the node’s embedding is close to its own neighborhood context but far from others. Normal nodes, being similar to their neighborhoods, achieve low contrastive loss, whereas context-incongruent (anomalous) nodes show high loss. This self-supervised signal thus serves as an anomaly score. Building on this, multi-scale and augmented-view contrastive strategies have been introduced. Jin et al. proposed ANEMONE21, which performs contrastive learning at multiple graph scales. It contrasts node representations against both local neighborhood representations and higher-level subgraph or graph context, in order to capture anomalies manifesting at different scales. Their approach showed that combining node-level and subgraph-level contrastive objectives improves the detection of subtle anomalies that might be missed by single-scale methods. Overall, contrastive learning has proven effective in GAD by learning embeddings that reflect a node’s consistency with its surroundings.

Motif-based structural representation and anomaly detection

While most GAD methods consider edges and immediate neighbors, a recent trend is to leverage network motifs, a recurrent small subgraph patterns to better characterize complex structural anomalies. The intuition is that motifs (triangles, cliques, stars, etc.) capture higher-order connectivity patterns that can distinguish normal structural roles from anomalous ones. For example, a node that participates in an abnormal number of rare motif structures or fails to participate in common motifs might be a structural outlier even if its immediate degree or neighbors seem normal. One line of work incorporates motif statistics into node representations. Yu et al. define a “motif degree” vector for each node, counting how many motifs of each type the node is involved in. This motif-based feature can augment the node’s attribute vector or guide attention mechanisms to identify structural oddities. Motif information has been used in representation learning frameworks to emphasize higher-order structure, though such methods were initially applied to link prediction or clustering rather than anomaly detection. Recently, dedicated motif-based anomaly detectors have emerged.

Yuan et al. developed MADG22, which targets motif-level anomalies in dynamic graphs. Their model uses a motif-augmented GCN to learn node and motif-instance embeddings at each time snapshot, then employs temporal self-attention to detect motif patterns whose evolution deviates significantly over time. This approach can identify anomalous motif occurrences that prior node-focused methods would miss. For static graphs, Xu Yuan et al. proposed GUIDE23, a framework with dual autoencoders. One reconstructs node attributes, and the other reconstructs the presence of higher-order structures (motifs) around each node. GUIDE introduces a motif-based graph attention layer to weigh neighbour contributions by their structural differences. By combining attribute reconstruction error and motif-structure reconstruction error, GUIDE can flag nodes that are structurally inconsistent with motif-based expectations of normal behavior. These works demonstrate that motif-centric representations enrich anomaly detection, especially for structural outliers that manifest in complex connectivity patterns rather than simple edge anomalies.

Curvature-aware and geometry-enhanced graph anomaly detection

Beyond motifs, researchers have started exploring geometric characteristics of graphs, such as curvature and embedding geometry, to detect anomalies. The motivation is that graph anomalies might be more evident in non-Euclidean spaces or via metrics like graph curvature that capture global structure. CurvGAD24, proposed by Grover et al, is a recent approach that explicitly leverages Ollivier-Ricci curvature of graph edges to uncover geometric anomalies. In graphs, Ollivier-Ricci curvature measures how much a node’s neighborhood deviates from being a well-connected clique or a tree-like structure. CurvGAD uses a mixed-curvature graph autoencoder with two parallel pipelines. One aims to reconstruct the graph’s edge curvature distribution, and the other performs a conventional structure with attribute reconstruction under a curvature-based regularisation. By doing so, CurvGAD isolates anomalies that arise from structural holes or bottlenecks separately from those due to local attribute or link deviations. The integration of curvature allows detection of outliers that traditional GNNs, focusing only on local connectivity, might overlook. Another form of geometry enhancement is to embed graph data in hyperbolic space, which has negative curvature and can naturally model hierarchical structures25. Some studies argue that hyperbolic embeddings have a greater capacity to separate normal and anomalous nodes in graphs with hierarchical or tree-like patterns. For instance, mapping a network into a hyperbolic latent space can amplify differences in connectivity. Small structural discrepancies may translate into large distance in hyperbolic space, making anomalies easier to detect. While hyperbolic GAD is still an emerging area, initial attempts have shown promise in domains like cybersecurity networks.

Weakly supervised and cross-modal video anomaly detection

Recent progress in video anomaly detection has shifted towards weakly supervised and cross-modal learning, which alleviate the lack of fine-grained annotations. Zhang et al. proposed a spatio-temporal graph-based self-labeling framework that models the temporal dependence of video segments, while progressively refining different anomaly predictions through relational reasoning26. In order to handle the limitations of supervision in distributed environment, Li et al. proposed a dual-detector reoptimization strategy for federated weakly supervised video anomaly detection which applies adaptive dynamic recursive mapping to enhance robustness on heterogeneous clients27.

Additionally, Wang et al. proposed a semantic-driven dual consistency learning algorithm that can impose agreement among multiple semantic views and thus enhance the reliability of anomaly localisation under weak supervision28. In addition to this, Chen et al. proposed a cross-modal two-stream target-focused network that jointly uses visual and motion results to enhance anomaly discrimination in complex scenes of videos29.

Though primarily implemented in spatio-temporal video data, these approaches also share similar ideas with graph-based anomaly detection regarding multi-view consistency, weak supervision, relational reasoning, and the like. Unlike the other existing solution, the HyMSS-GAD framework works on attributed graphs and tackles node-level anomalies with the integration of contextual, structural and geometric cues.

In various disciplines, graph learning has attracted a lot of attention. The fields of social networks, cybersecurity, bioinformatics and financial systems are one of the most examples. In these areas, the relationships between nodes and the features of the nodes matters deeply. Attributed graphs are defined as graphs in which a node is associated with associated attributes and also allow for more expressive models and more refined analyses. The relevant concepts and definitions needed for node representations, structural motifs, geometric graph measurements, and anomaly detection will be introduced in this section.

Definition 5

Multiple instance learning perspective for graph-based anomaly detection

Multiple Instance Learning or MIL refers to a type of learning where the data is organized in the form of bags that consist of multiple instances. When any labels are available, they are associated with the bags and not with the instances. A bag is positive if it contains a positive instance; it is negative, if it contains only negative instances. When supervision on instance-level is absent or not trusted, this formulation is useful.

From the viewpoint of a graph, MIL provides a natural intuition for anomaly detection. A local subgraph, also known as an ego-network, can be considered as a bag. The nodes it contains form instances. Instances of odd behavior can be seen on the instance level. Perhaps a node is acting as a structural bridge or showing abnormal attributes even if the surrounding subgraph looks normal. The MIL-style reasoning plays an important role in detecting these sparse localized deviations in structures.

The bag–instance mapping logic is especially important in difficult topological environments. The model captures contextual commonness through subgraph-level information and discloses deviations at the instance level to distinguish a node’s anomalousness finely. This point of view is inline with the design principle of HyMSS-GAD, and thus local structural patterns, contextual representations and geometric cues are jointly leveraged to learn to identify node-level anomalies.

While MIL is not used as a separate learning module in this work, its intuition explains how local context aggregation and instance-level residual analysis robustly detect anomalies in graphs. Utilizing explicit MIL-based objectives and bag-level reasoning without any supervision is a potential extension of the presented framework.

Proposed methodology

In order to facilitate anomaly detection in heterogeneous attributed networks, we proposed HyMSS-GAD (Hybrid Multi-Modal Self-Supervised Graph Anomaly Detector). HyMSS-GAD is a modular and unifying framework that models at the node level, connecting semantic, structural and geometric information. Most existing techniques for detecting anomalies in graphs rely on simplifying assumptions like homophily. These techniques lack the ability to provide anomaly detection in networks with diverse and heterogeneous structures. HyMSS-GAD introduces a multi-stage pipeline to develop this capability, with each module capturing a specific aspect of node behaviour and structural context.

The model is built from five sequential modules, each of which fits the corresponding residuals or reconstruction errors to quantify the deviations of nodes from the expected node behavior: 1. Contextual anomalies are the reconstruction errors arising from inconsistencies between node features and the local structural context. 2. Structural anomalies are the reconstruction errors derived from higher-order topological patterns, such as motifs. 3. Geometric anomalies capture irregularities in graph curvature, highlighting bridge, boundary, or hub nodes. 4. Multi-modal fusion entails the aforementioned node-level embeddings, which will be combined at the embedding level through an attention-guided mechanism. 5. Anomaly scoring and interpretation include residual or error terms from all contexts in defining a final multi-view anomaly score. This provides an anomaly score for detection and explainable interpretation. The proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1The design of the HyMSS-GAD is presented in this section. The framework uses the modalities of structure, semantics, and geometry for interpretable anomaly detection in attributed networks. The process begins with ego-subgraphs and two encoders, which leverage contrastive learning to learn contextual representations from the graph. A motif-based structural reconstruction captures higher-order topological deviations. An attention-based fusion network combines structural and contextual embeddings in an adaptive manner. The curvature-aware decoder assumes Ollivier-Ricci curvature to model geometric abnormalities. Lastly, a multi-view anomaly scoring module combines contextual, structural, and geometric residuals to generate a unified and interpretable anomaly score.

Cross-modal contrastive learning for contextual anomaly detection

The HyMSS-GAD framework’s first module is directed towards contextual anomaly detection through semantically consistent node representations via contrastive learning and cross-modal regularization. This step is critical in anomaly detection since it detects anomalies based on inconsistencies between a node’s attributes and its semi-local structure in a nearby neighbourhood of nodes, specifically for heterophilous attributed networks which have similar attributes at their nodes that do not connect.

Let the attributed graph be defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G=(V, E, X)$$\end{document}, where: V is the set of nodes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E \subseteq V \times V$$\end{document} is the set of edges, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \in {\mathbb {R}}^{|V| \times d}$$\end{document} is the node attribute matrix. For each node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v \in V$$\end{document}, we define its ego-subgraph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_v=\left( V_v, E_v\right)$$\end{document}, consisting of node v, its one-hop neighbors N(v), and the edges among them. A two-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) is used as the encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_\theta$$\end{document} to learn the node-level latent representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document}, given by:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} h_v=f_\theta \left( x_v, N(v)\right) =\sigma \left( \sum _{u \in N(v) \cup \{v\}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|N(v)||N(u)|}} W x_u\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where W is the shared weight matrix, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the ReLU activation, and normalization ensures stability during training. To represent the context of the node v, we perform mean pooling over the embeddings of all nodes in its ego-subgraph:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} c_v=\frac{1}{\left| V_v\right| } \sum _{u \in V_v} f_\theta \left( x_u\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This context vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_v \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document} captures the average semantic signature of the neighborhood, providing a baseline against which the node’s embedding is compared. To enforce consistency between the node embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v$$\end{document} and its context embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_v$$\end{document}, we employ the InfoNCE loss. It is a widely used objective in self-supervised contrastive learning and defined as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{\textrm{cl}}=-\frac{1}{|V|} \sum _{v \in V} \log \left( \frac{\exp \left( \operatorname {sim}\left( h_v, c_v\right) / \tau \right) }{\sum _{u \in V} \exp \left( \operatorname {sim}\left( h_v, c_u\right) / \tau \right) }\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\operatorname {sim}(\cdot , \cdot )$$\end{document} denotes cosine similarity, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is the temperature parameter. Positive pairs are constructed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( h_v, c_v\right)$$\end{document}, while negative pairs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( h_v, c_u\right)$$\end{document} are sampled from all other nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u \ne v$$\end{document}. This contrastive formulation encourages \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v$$\end{document} to align with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_v$$\end{document} and effectively minimizing embedding deviations from the local context. Nodes that are semantically inconsistent with their surroundings will exhibit larger distances, resulting in higher loss.

To further enhance consistency between feature-driven and structure-driven latent spaces, we incorporate cross-modal regularization. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_v^{(f)}$$\end{document} be the embedding from the attribute-only encoder branch, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_v^{(s)}$$\end{document} be the embedding from the topology-only encoder branch. We define the alignment loss as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{\text{ align } }=\frac{1}{|V|} \sum _{v \in V}\left\| z_v^{(f)}-z_v^{(s)}\right\| _2^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This term ensures that the semantic and structural views of each node remain close in the latent space. It also enhances robustness in multi-view settings. The total objective for this module becomes:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{\textrm{CMCL}}={\mathscr {L}}_{\textrm{cl}}+\lambda _{\text{ align } } {\mathscr {L}}_{\text{ align } }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{\text{ align } }$$\end{document} is a balancing hyperparameter empirically. The outputs of this module are: Node embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v$$\end{document}, which is used in the fusion and scoring stages and Residual contrastive loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{\text{ cont } }(v)={\mathscr {L}}_{\textrm{cl}}(v)$$\end{document}. These are used for the calculation of anomaly scores. This design ensures local smoothness in the learned representations and makes the system robust to view inconsistencies. It significantly enhances the detection capability in real-world heterogeneous graphs.

Motif-based structural reconstruction for higher-order pattern detection

In previous step, contextual consistency was focused on node-attribute and neighbour embedding. Moreover, real-world graphs have a lot of irregularities due to the disturbance in the higher-order structural pattern. To capture such structural irregularities, we introduce this motif-based reconstruction module. It discovers issues that show an odd pattern. Small subgraphs that occur repeatedly are called motifs. They are identified as a pattern or cluster (like a triangle) that seems to appear everywhere within a graph. We usually see changes in motif participation due to structural exceptions (anomalies).

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathscr {M}}=\left\{ M_1, M_2, \ldots , M_p\right\}$$\end{document} denote the motif dictionary containing p structurally significant motifs. In this work, we select: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_1$$\end{document} : Triangle - 3 -node cycle capturing closed triads, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_2$$\end{document} : 2-star - central node with two neighbors, indicating hub-like structures, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_3$$\end{document} :4-path - 4 -node open linear chain, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_4$$\end{document} : 3-clique - complete 3-node subgraph, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_5$$\end{document} : 4-cycle-4-node loop. For each node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v \in V$$\end{document}, a motif count vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_v \in {\mathbb {R}}^p$$\end{document} is computed, where the i-th entry \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_v[i]$$\end{document} denotes the number of times node v participates in motif \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_i$$\end{document}.

We employ a deterministic motif enumeration algorithm based on bounded neighborhood exploration. It ensures reproducible motif counts suitable for medium-sized graphs. Specifically, for each node v, all k node subgraphs (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k \in \{3,4\}$$\end{document}) within its 2-hop induced neighborhood are enumerated, and isomorphic matches to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathscr {M}}$$\end{document} are recorded. This approach avoids noise from random sampling and maintains semantic interpretability. To model normal structural roles and detect deviations, a feedforward autoencoder is trained to reconstruct the motif count vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_v$$\end{document}. The encoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_\phi$$\end{document} compresses motif statistics into a latent representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_v^{(m)} \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document}, while the decoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_\psi$$\end{document} reconstructs the original vector:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} z_v^{(m)}=g_\phi \left( m_v\right) , \quad {\hat{m}}_v=h_\psi \left( z_v^{(m)}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Both the encoder and decoder employ two-layer MLPs with ReLU activations and dropout for regularization. The motif reconstruction loss is computed as the squared Euclidean distance:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{\text{ motif } }=\frac{1}{|V|} \sum _{v \in V}\left\| m_v-{\hat{m}}_v\right\| _2^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Nodes whose motif signatures deviate from learned normal patterns incur higher reconstruction loss. It provides a reliable proxy for structural anomaly detection. The outputs of this step are: Latent structural embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_v^{(m)} \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document}, which encodes the node’s role across multiple motifs and the structural anomaly residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{\text{ motif } }(v)=\left\| m_v-{\hat{m}}_v\right\| _2^2$$\end{document}, used in final anomaly scoring. These outputs are forwarded to the attention-based fusion module, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_v^{(m)}$$\end{document} is integrated with contextual embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v$$\end{document}, and the residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{\text{ motif } }(v)$$\end{document} contributes to explainable anomaly interpretation.

Unlike standard GNN-based methods which rely on edge-level propagation or homophily assumptions, our motif-based reconstruction captures non-pairwise, higher-order relations. It yields explainability and direct attribution of motif-type, and strengthens robustness in both homophilous and heterophilous networks. The combination of deterministic motif enumeration and shallow autoencoder provides a favourable trade-off between scalability, semantic richness, and localised anomaly detection.

Attention-based view fusion for multi-modal embedding integration

After independently retrieving latent representations from the contextual and structural modalities, the HyMSS-GAD framework employs an attention-based view fusion module to integrate multimodal signals into a discriminative embedding for each node. This allows the model to dynamically attend to each modality based on characteristics specific to the node. It increases the robustness and interpretability. In real-world graphs, the contextual and structural anomalies may appear quite differently. A node could be structurally normal (engaging in the same frequent motif patterns) but contextually inconsistent with its appropriate neighborhood or vice versa. Simply concatenating or averaging will obscure these important anomaly signals or simply include additional noise. Therefore, we use a learnable attention mechanism that dynamically fuses the modality-specific embeddings for each node.

The attention-based view fusion module of HyMSS-GAD is expected to be learning-driven rather than hand-crafted. During training, the attention mechanism facilitates the automatic learning of fusion weights specifically, and these weights are jointly optimized along with all other model parameters through gradient descent. Consequently, the model is not dependent on a fixed or manually tuned fusion coefficient to balance contextual and structural information.

The formulation based on learning enables the model to adopt adaptation without effort in heterogeneous graph environments in which the relative importance of structural signals and attribute-based signals varies from dataset to steady state. HyMSS-GAD has been designed such that optimal end-to-end integration weights make it less sensitive to hyperparameter tuning at the integration stage compared to using fixed/static or heuristic mixing ratios.

For each node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v \in V$$\end{document}, the attention module receives the following latent embeddings: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_v \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document} (Contextual embedding), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_v^{(m)} \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document} (Structural motif embedding). These embeddings encode complementary aspects of the node’s behavior, which are combined into a single hybrid representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_v \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document}. The two embeddings are first concatenated and passed through a shared attention network:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} u_v=\tanh \left( W_a\left[ h_v \Vert z_v^{(m)}\right] +b_a\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_a \in {\mathbb {R}}^{d \times 2 d}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_a \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document} are trainable parameters, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\cdot \Vert \cdot ]$$\end{document} denotes vector concatenation, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tanh (\cdot )$$\end{document} introduces non-linearity. Next, attention scores are computed via a softmax activation over scalar attention logits:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \left[ \alpha _1, \alpha _2\right] =\operatorname {softmax}\left( w^{\top } u_v\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document} is a trainable projection vector. Finally, the fused embedding for the node is computed as a weighted sum:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_v=\alpha _1 \cdot h_v+\alpha _2 \cdot z_v^{(m)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The framework characterises the system in which either structural or contextual shifts are accentuated, and which are dependent on the local data properties of each node. This module provides the attention-fused embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_v \in {\mathbb {R}}^d$$\end{document} and Attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[ \alpha _1, \alpha _2\right]$$\end{document} that provide interpretability by identifying the dominating anomaly signal modality for the node. The attention-based fusion module has several benefits in a hybrid anomaly detection method. It assumes that not all modalities are of equal importance at every node. Also, it decreases the impact of noisy or less informative modalities.

Curvature-aware decoder for geometric anomaly detection

The earlier steps capture many semantic and structural inconsistencies in a local manner. However, they do not take into account the global geometric deviations. HyMSS-GAD has a decoder that corrects samples with the help of curvature based on Ollivier-Ricci curvature. It measures the similarity of neighborhoods between adjacent nodes and can identify geometric irregularities. Ollivier-Ricci curvature is a graph-theoretic generalisation of Ricci curvature from Riemannian geometry. For a given edge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u, v) \in E$$\end{document}, its curvature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa (u, v)$$\end{document} is defined as:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \kappa (u, v)=1-\frac{W_1\left( \mu _u, \mu _v\right) }{d(u, v)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _u$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _v$$\end{document} are probability measures centered at nodes u and v. These are typically defined using neighborhoodbased distributions. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\quad W_1(\cdot , \cdot )$$\end{document} is the Wasserstein distance (Earth Mover’s Distance). d(u, v) is the shortest path distance between u and v. This metric captures how similar the neighborhoods of u and v are. Edges connecting disparate regions of the graph typically exhibit low or negative curvature, indicating potential anomalies. For each edge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u, v) \in E$$\end{document}, the pre-computed curvature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa (u, v)$$\end{document} is reconstructed using the attention-fused embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_u$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_v$$\end{document} from the previous step. A shallow decoder \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{\xi }$$\end{document} predicts the edge curvature:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\hat{\kappa }}(u, v)=D_{\xi }\left( f_u, f_v\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The decoder is implemented as a two-layer MLP operating on the concatenation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[ f_u \Vert f_v\right]$$\end{document}. The edge-level curvature loss is computed using the mean squared error (MSE):19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{\text{ curv } }=\frac{1}{|E|} \sum _{(u, v) \in E}(\kappa (u, v)-{\hat{\kappa }}(u, v))^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This loss penalizes deviations in geometric consistency and enables the model to learn which node pairs are geometrically aligned or divergent. Geometric interpretation of Ricci curvature for structural anomaly detection

Euclidean embedding distances are effective for capturing attribute similarity and local proximity but often fail to distinguish mesoscopic structural anomalies, such as bridge nodes or boundary nodes connecting dense communities. To address this limitation, HyMSS-GAD incorporates a Ricci curvature decoder that introduces geometric reasoning into the detection process.

Ollivier–Ricci curvature measures the similarity between local neighborhoods of adjacent nodes. Edges within cohesive communities typically exhibit near-zero or positive curvature, whereas edges acting as structural bottlenecks tend to have strongly negative curvature. The curvature decoder learns to reconstruct these values from fused embeddings, and large reconstruction residuals indicate structural inconsistencies that are not apparent in Euclidean space.

By capturing non-Euclidean distortions in connectivity, the curvature-based module complements contextual and motif-based views, enabling the detection of subtle structural anomalies that would otherwise remain indistinguishable using standard embedding-based methods.

The last stage of the proposed HyMSS-GAD pipeline involves the computation of an interpretable multi-modal anomaly score for every node after the extraction of the contextual embeddings, structural motif patterns and geometric curvature signals. The scoring system takes the residuals that are specific to views and treats them as a single score.

Core contribution and novelty

The uniqueness of HyMSS-GAD is not in the new learning primitives it introduces, but in its principled modeling hypothesis about what constitutes an anomaly in an attributed graph. As we explicitly assume, the node anomalies arise as inconsistencies among complementary structural, contextual, and geometric views, rather than as outliers in any of the representation spaces. This concept can be employed using a unified residual-based framework that dissects anomaly evidence into three interpretable parts, namely contextual inconsistency, higher-order structural deviation and geometric distortion.

HyMSS-GAD differs from simple ensembles, where the predictions of several independent models are aggregated, in that it enforces cross-view alignment under normalcy and measures anomaly strength using the residual disagreement triggered by this alignment’s failure. Every module has a distinct but overlapping signal that contributes to a residual signal, but it is the inconsistency of these signals that helps with anomaly detection and not just the additive scorer value. The design is such that it makes the framework falsifiable, i.e. if the detected anomalies do not lead to cross view disagreement or if simple modular aggregation suffices, then the proposed formulation won’t yield consistent gains over individual components.

The observed improvements are in excess of what we would expect at random by naïve modular composition, as can be seen from our ablation results where removing any of the residual pathways significantly degrades performance. The results align with the main finding: explicit multi-view residual modeling, not component novelty or ensemble size, is primarily responsible for performance improvements.

HyMSS-GAD can be viewed as a unified anomaly modeling framework that tests and validates a specific hypothesis about anomaly generation in graphs, rather than as an ad hoc combination of existing techniques.

HyMSS-GAD is trained and evaluated in a strictly unsupervised setting, with no ground-truth anomaly labels used during training, validation, or model selection. All representation-learning components including contrastive encoding, motif reconstruction, and curvature decoding—are optimised without access to labels.

Importantly, score-combination weights for the contextual, structural, and geometric residuals are not selected using label-based metrics such as AUC or average precision. Instead, these weights are fixed prior to evaluation and kept constant across datasets, with residuals normalised to comparable scales to mitigate sensitivity to exact weight values. Label-based metrics are computed only at evaluation time to assess detection performance and are not used to guide training or hyperparameter adjustment.

To further reduce dependence on precise hyperparameter choices, we analyse the robustness of the framework across a range of weight configurations. The results in Section "Experiment and result analysis" show that performance varies smoothly rather than abruptly with changes in weight, indicating that the method does not rely on implicit hyperparameter-level supervision. This design prioritises reproducibility and ensures that reported performance reflects intrinsic modelling capability rather than label-informed tuning.

Computational efficiency, scalability, and resource analysis

HyMSS-GAD is a multi-stage framework that integrates contextual, structural, and geometric reasoning for graph anomaly detection. While this comprehensive design improves detection accuracy and interpretability, it also necessitates a careful examination of computational efficiency, particularly for large-scale and industrial graphs. To address this, we analyze both the theoretical complexity and empirical runtime and memory consumption of each module, and we discuss practical optimization strategies that enable scalable deployment.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=|V|$$\end{document} denote the number of nodes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m=|E|$$\end{document} the number of edges, d the embedding dimension, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bar{k}}$$\end{document} the average node degree, and p the number of motif types.

The cross-modal contrastive learning module employs a two-layer graph convolutional encoder. For sparse graphs, each GCN layer incurs a time complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( m d+n d^2\right)$$\end{document}, arising from message passing and linear projection operations. Contrastive alignment is implemented using mini-batch InfoNCE sampling, which reduces the quadratic cost of full-batch similarity computation to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( b^2 d\right)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b \ll n$$\end{document} is the batch size. The memory footprint of this module scales linearly as O(nd), dominated by node embeddings and intermediate activations.

The motif-based structural reconstruction module consists of deterministic motif enumeration followed by a shallow autoencoder. Motif enumeration is performed as a one-time preprocessing step using bounded local neighborhood exploration. For motifs of size up to four nodes, the expected complexity can be approximated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( n \cdot {\bar{k}}^{\ell }\right)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell \le 4$$\end{document} reflects motif size. The subsequent autoencoder training is lightweight, with time complexity O(nph), where h is the hidden dimension, and memory usage O(np) for storing motif vectors. Since motif statistics are computed once and cached, this stage does not affect iterative training scalability.

The attention-based view fusion module integrates contextual and structural embeddings through a simple feedforward attention mechanism. This operation scales linearly with the number of nodes, requiring O(nd) time and memory, and introduces negligible computational overhead.

The curvature-aware geometric module is the most computationally intensive component of HyMSS-GAD. Ollivier-Ricci curvature computation involves solving local optimal transport problems between neighborhood distributions of adjacent nodes. Using entropic Wasserstein approximation, the preprocessing complexity of curvature extraction scales approximately as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( m \cdot T \cdot {\bar{k}}^2\right)$$\end{document}, where T denotes the number of Sinkhorn iterations. Importantly, this cost is incurred only once and curvature values are cached for reuse during training. After curvature extraction, the curvature decoder operates efficiently, with per-epoch training complexity O(md) and memory usage O(m) for storing curvature targets.

The multi-view anomaly-scoring module aggregates residuals across contextual, structural, and geometric perspectives. This final stage requires only O(n) time and memory, making its computational cost negligible compared to earlier modules.

The motif-based structural reconstruction module consists of two stages: motif enumeration and motif vector reconstruction. Motif enumeration is the more computationally expensive component and is executed as a one-time preprocessing step, independent of iterative model training.

Scalability analysis of curvature computation

The scalability analysis of curvature computation was conducted using the PubMed citation network as the base graph. PubMed was selected because it is the largest citation dataset included in our benchmark evaluation and exhibits moderate density and realistic neighborhood structure. To analyze scalability beyond the original dataset size, progressively larger graphs were generated through degree-preserving graph expansion, in which the node set was incrementally extended while maintaining similar average degree and local neighborhood statistics. This approach ensures that the curvature behavior remains representative of real-world attributed graphs rather than synthetic random structures. Since curvature extraction dominates preprocessing cost, we further analyze how its runtime and memory usage scale with increasing graph size.

The findings reveal that the curvature preprocessing time exhibits a super-linear increase, as expected, with respect to the size of the neighborhood transport. Experimental alignment and evaluation fairness

To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, all baseline methods and the proposed HyMSS-GAD framework are evaluated under identical experimental conditions. In particular, the same anomaly injection protocol is applied across all methods, including identical anomaly ratios, injection locations, and random seeds. This guarantees that each method is trained and tested on exactly the same graph instances.

All models are trained using a common training budget, defined by the same maximum number of epochs and early-stopping criteria. Early stopping is triggered based on validation performance with a fixed patience value, and this setting is applied consistently to all methods to prevent any method from benefiting from extended training. Furthermore, all methods are initialized with the same number of random restarts, and final results are reported as averages over multiple runs.

Hyperparameter tuning is conducted in a method-agnostic and constrained manner. For each baseline, we follow the original authors’ recommended hyperparameter ranges when available; otherwise, we adopt commonly used ranges reported in the literature. Importantly, the same tuning strategy and search budget are used for all methods, and no dataset-specific manual tuning is performed to favor any particular approach.

Through this unified evaluation protocol, we ensure that performance differences reflect the intrinsic modeling capabilities of the compared methods rather than discrepancies in training procedures or experimental configuration.

Experimental results and analysis

The performance of the proposed HyMSS-GAD framework was evaluated against several state-of-the-art graph anomaly detection models, including DOMINANT, ResGCN, CaCo, SL-GAD, and HE-GAD. All models were trained and tested under identical settings for fair comparison across five benchmark attributed datasets: Cora, Citeseer, PubMed, ACM, and Amazon. Table 3 summarizes the comparative results in terms of the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) and Average Precision (AP).

The HyMSS-GAD model surpassed all others in terms of averages of Area Under the Curve (AUC) and Average Precision (AP) performance metrics for all datasets evaluated. It justifies its overall strategy of recognizing structural as well as contextual anomalies. This is illustrated by the experiment on the Cora dataset, where HyMSS-GAD obtained AUC and AP scores of 93.05% and 87.91%, respectively. It outperforms HE-GAD by 2.1% and 4.6%, respectively. The same pattern was seen on the PubMed Citeseer datasets, which contain heterogeneous structures and medium feature sparsity. It achieved AUC scores of 92.65% and 88.92%, respectively, and slightly outperformed SL-GAD and CaCo with a difference of approximately 1–3%. This performance gain was pronounced in the larger sample set from Amazon. HyMSS-GAD achieved an AUC of 89.41% and AP of 88.44%. It demonstrates the scalability and robustness of the framework in dense and high-dimensional feature spaces.

A comprehensive consideration of these results leads to the multi-view integration technique. It integrates contextual embeddings, motif-based structural reconstruction and curvature-aware geometric reasoning, which enables HyMSS-GAD to learn more discriminative node representations than the dual-view algorithms. Methods like DOMINANT and ResGCN use reconstruction-based objectives to capture low-order dependency but do not generalize to higher-order roles in structure. CaCo and SL-GAD improve discrimination of anomalies through contrastive learning but lack adaptive fusion of modalities. It results in less appropriate performance on feature-noisy graphs like ACM. In comparison, HyMSS-GAD combines attention-based fusion of views. It prompts the network to attend to each node’s most informative modality and filter away irrelevant or noisy modalities.

The outcomes further confirm that using curvature residuals improves the geometric interpretability of graphs by highlighting edges and nodes that reside on the boundaries of structural communities or exhibit abnormal curvature of connectivity. The geometric awareness improves robustness to perturbations to edges and noise, resulting in consistent AUC–AP improvement across all datasets. The decoder that uses curvature information plays a significant role in detecting nodes that bridge different communities. Purely topological detectors and those relying only on attributes can identify such nodes with some detection success. To summarise, these empirical findings show that HyMSS-GAD provides a consistent and interpretable framework for graph anomaly detection, excelling in prediction accuracy, interpretability, and scalability. The strong performance in various attributed networks suggests that our method may be useful in real-world contexts, including citation analysis in academia, fraud detection in finance, and large-scale recommendation systems.

To further assess the robustness of HyMSS-GAD under modality mismatch, we conduct a supplementary robustness analysis focusing on attribute-space perturbations and structural link removal. Importantly, these experiments are performed without retraining or hyperparameter adjustment, using the same trained model and configuration as in the main experiments.

For attribute robustness, we inject controlled noise into node features by applying (i) Gaussian noise with increasing variance and (ii) random feature masking, simulating incomplete or corrupted attribute information. For structural robustness, we randomly remove a fraction of edges while preserving node count, thereby introducing missing or unreliable connectivity. Performance is evaluated using relative changes in AUC, emphasizing degradation trends rather than absolute scores.

Even with substantial noise or edge removal, the decline in detection accuracy remains moderate. This behavior can be attributed to the framework’s multi-view residual design, where contextual, structural, and geometric cues provide complementary signals. In particular, increased modal inconsistency naturally amplifies residual-based anomaly scores rather than destabilizing the model. Guidance on residual weight selection and robustness

HyMSS-GAD aggregates anomaly evidence from three complementary residual scores: contextual, structural, and geometric. While Section "Experiment and result analysis" demonstrates that these components contribute differently across datasets, we note that all residuals are normalized to comparable scales prior to aggregation. This normalization substantially reduces sensitivity to the exact choice of balancing weights.

In practice, we find that uniform or nearly uniform weighting yields robust performance for a wide variety of graphs. We find that only for attribute-dominant graphs, it is helpful to increase the weight of the contextual residual (or reduce the weight of other residuals). Similarly, for topology-driven graphs, increasing the weight of the structural residual or curvature residual renders the graph even more sensitive. Significantly, the performance is not very sensitive to the small deviations of the weights from the optimal ones as the residuals are normalized and sourced independently views and vary smoothly under the weight changes.

The introduction of meta-learned weights of fully automated systems will require re-training and more validation, which may further complicate fair comparisons with baselines. Hence, the current approach focuses on stability, interpretability, and reproducibility, while giving clear guidance that limits dataset-specific tuning.

Interpretability scope and evaluation criteria

The interpretability of HyMSS-GAD is grounded in the alignment between model outputs and well-understood structural roles in graphs, rather than post-hoc visual explanations. We clarify that interpretability in this work is assessed through structural consistency and behavioral plausibility, rather than through supervised explanation accuracy, as the real-world benchmark datasets used do not provide ground-truth explanatory annotations.

To formalize interpretability assessment, we identify three evaluation criteria. First, structural-role consistency: curvature residuals are expected to highlight nodes occupying known topological roles such as community bridges or boundary nodes. Empirically, nodes with high curvature residuals consistently correspond to connectivity bottlenecks or inter-community connectors, supporting the geometric interpretation.

Second, agreement with anomaly factors: in controlled or semi-synthetic settings where anomalies are introduced through structural perturbations (e.g., edge rewiring or community disruption), curvature and structural residuals respond more strongly than contextual residuals, indicating alignment between explanation signals and underlying anomaly causes.

Third, stability and consistency: explanation signals derived from attention weights and residual scores exhibit smooth variation under moderate perturbations of hyperparameters and training seeds, suggesting that explanations are not artifacts of unstable optimization.

We emphasize that a fully quantitative evaluation of explanation correctness such as accuracy in identifying predefined structural roles or direct comparison with ground-truth anomaly factors requires synthetic benchmarks with explicit explanatory labels, which are not available in the evaluated real-world datasets. Incorporating such controlled benchmarks and reporting explanation accuracy metrics is therefore identified as an important direction for future work.

Limitations of our proposed framework

Although the proposed HyMSS-GAD framework demonstrates strong performance and interpretability across various attributed graphs. There are still several limitations that should be acknowledged to guide future research. First, the current model assumes that all node attributes and structural connections are static and fully available during training. In real-world dynamic networks, the geometric and motif-based modules may not perform optimally. Extending the framework to handle temporal or evolving graphs would require efficient mechanisms for incremental embedding updates and online curvature estimation. Second, while the curvature-aware decoder improves geometric understanding by introducing additional computational overhead because of the Wasserstein distance and curvature reconstruction calculations. Although manageable for small to medium datasets like Cora or Citeseer but scaling to very large graphs such as Amazon could require faster approximation or sampling-based strategies to maintain efficiency.

Third, the synthetic anomaly injection used for controlled experiments helps in benchmarking but may not fully represent the complex and heterogeneous nature of real-world anomalies. Real anomalies can emerge from multiple overlapping causes. It includes temporal drift, hidden structural manipulation, or semantic noise. Therefore, future studies should validate the model on more domain-specific, real-world datasets with genuine anomaly annotations. Finally, the attention-based view fusion mechanism depends on proper tuning of the modality weighting parameters. The interaction among these parameters can cause mild sensitivity to data distribution in heterogeneous graphs. Future extensions could explore adaptive or learning-based weight adjustment to further improve stability and generalisation.

Conclusion and future scope

This work proposes a heterogeneous, hybrid multistage framework for graph-based anomaly detection that leverages contextual, structural, and geometric information through contrastive learning, motif-based reasoning, and curvature-aware analysis. In the proposed architecture, a multi-view learning framework is adopted in which cross-modal contrastive learning captures contextual irregularities. It relies on motif-based reconstruction of higher-order structural deformations. A curvature-sensitive decoder is employed to detect geometric inconsistencies between communities. Balance across multiple modalities is dynamically learned, thereby adding to interpretative and discriminative node representations. Extensive experiments on five benchmark attributed networks show that the proposed model significantly outperforms several state-of-the-art graph anomaly detection methods. It achieves significant gains on both AUC and AP metrics. By explicitly modelling semantic, structural, and geometric irregularities, the model can better distinguish among different types of anomalies and is more interpretable. Moreover, ablation and sensitivity studies verify the contribution of each part, leading to consistent performance across different hyperparameters. While the model is competitive, its computational cost in estimating curvature and its reliance on static graph assumptions leave room for improvement. Extensions to dynamic or temporal graph scenarios are part of future work. Furthermore, adding automated hyperparameter tuning or the reinforcement-driven attention mechanisms might enhance stability over heterogeneous datasets. It would be promising to use curvature-aware and contrastive learning in federated or distributed graph settings.

Future work may explore enhancements to the proposed framework that include deep learning approaches for addressing more complex and dynamic situations in the realm of multi-view anomaly detection in attributed graphs. Recent research has shown the efficacy of deep models in impactful multi-structural detection tasks in health and spatio-temporal settings. A promising area in which future work can extend the proposed framework is towards such settings which may require deeper representation learning, temporal dynamics, or domain-specific constraints42,43.
