Comparable biochemical responses of three Scenedesmus species to NiO and ZnO nanoparticles exposure
Authors: Samaneh Torbati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractToday, nanoparticles (NPs) have become intensively used in different fields and subsequently can be also transported to the environment. Investigating the potential toxicity of nanoparticles on algae species supply unique information about ecosystem conditions and promote the safe development of innovative nanotechnology. In the present study, the toxicity of two metal oxide nanoparticles (NiO-NP and ZnO-NP) in Scenedesmus obtusus, S. bernardii and S. obliquus was evaluated. The high dose exposure to the NPs inhibited the algae growth and contents of Chlorophyll a and b. Moreover, lipid peroxidation in three algal species was significantly increased in response to the toxic effects of the NPs. In contrast the total phenol contents of three algal species reduced after treatment with the NPs. Fatty acid profiles changed in the algae species and amount of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) in S. obliquus and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) content in S. bernardii increased. Based on the PUFAs/SFAs ratio, the Ʃn-3/Ʃn-6 ratio, and the cetane number (CN), S. obtusus and S. bernardii appear to be more suitable for nutraceutical production, whereas S. obliquus is more suitable for biodiesel production following the nanoparticles treatment. Considering the rate of ion release from the NPs, there is a close relationship between the toxicity caused by the NPs themselves and the ions released from them.Keywords: Biochemical and physiological responses, Fatty acids profile, Green microalgae, Metal oxide nanoparticles, Nanoecotoxicity, ScenedesmusSubject terms: Plant sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Outstanding properties of different nanoparticles (NPs), such as high specific surface area, catalytic ability and their thermal, chemical and photo-stability, along with their widespread utilization in different areas have led to their inevitably entrance to the environment and generation a new threat for the environment stability1. Today, the compounds were intensively used in biology, electrochemistry, pharmacology and production of sensors, catalysts and photoelectron devices. Despite the unique and numerous applications of the nanomaterials, their presence in the environment may cause hazardous biological impacts. Direct interactions of NPs with cell surface, dissolution of toxic elements from them and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are three main events causing oxidative stress in biological systems2,3. Subsequently, adverse implications arise on gene expression, metabolisms, photosynthesis, nitrogen fixation and growth4,5 and may change the concentration of macromolecules and metabolites, including fatty acids, amino acids and C and N contents in cells1. For instance, the treatment of Anabaena by TiO2-NP changed the ratio of C: N, C: P and N: P at very low concentration6. Cell membrane damage, organelle disruption and high production of ROS were identified as the main causes of ZnO-NP on Chlorella vulgaris7. However, there are no clear guidelines for determination of toxicity level of different nonomaterials in biological systems5.

Algae, as primary producers in aquatic ecosystems, play an important role in maintaining the balance of ecosystem energy flow and material circulation. Therefore, a complete understanding of the biological effects of NPs on algae as well as the underlying toxicity mechanisms are of vital importance8. Microalgae, with their unique characteristics including simple growth conditions, short life cycle and sensitivity to different classes of pollutants have been of much interest and are widely used in bioassay toxicity testing of aquatic pollutants9. Senedesmus sp. is known as a microalgae genus having good bioremediation properties and the valuable harvested biomass with high lipid content10. Its high potential in the remediation of sulfate, phosphate, ammonium, heavy metals and many antibiotics has been well confirmed10–12. Moreover, the successful use of the microalgae in biofuel production, aquaculture and biosensor manufacturing has also been reported13–15. However, despite the mentioned extensive researches about the microalgae, there is still no complete comparison about the various responses of the algae species to exposure to different types of metal oxide nanoparticles.

Specific objectives of the present study are to deliberate the toxicological impacts of ZnO-NP and NiO-NP (as two most used NPs utilized in several fields) on three Scenedesmus species (Scenedesmus obliquus, S. bernardii and S. obtusus), as well as to screen some biochemical and physiological responses shown by the algae to the existence of the two metal oxide NPs. The responses provide a better understanding of toxicity mechanisms and help to the sustainable development of nanotechnology in the future.

Results and discussion

Characterization of NPs

SEM images of NiO-NP and ZnO -NP were shown in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. Their average sizes calculated from SEM views were in the range of 10–20 nm and 60–80 nm for NiO-NP and ZnO-NP, respectively (Fig. 1c and d). Nanoparticles tend to form aggregates in aqueous media and their behaviors in the solutions can be evaluated using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Aqueous suspensions of NiO-NPs and ZnO-NPs analyzed by DLS showed a high degree of agglomeration, with measured sizes of 226.2 nm and 114.8 nm, respectively (Fig. 1e and f), which were considerably larger than the sizes observed in SEM images. Based on the obtained hydrodynamic sizes, these aggregated NPs tend to adsorb onto the algal cell wall, which can alter light absorption, cause shading, or induce oxidative stress in the algae cell2 and their internalization to the cells is improbable. The ζ potentials were − 0.5 mV for NiO-NP (Fig. 1g) and 0.7 mV for ZnO-NP (Fig. 1h), indicating their negative and positive surface charges, respectively.

Effect of nanoparticles on cell growth

Manufactured nanomaterials would induce adverse biological impacts, varying with algae species and the type, concentration and exposure time of the nanomaterials. These negative effects and implications on algae occur by affecting their metabolism, photosynthesis, gene expression, nitrogen fixation and etc16. Twenty days of treatment with 50 and 100 mg/L of NPs significantly reduced the growth of S. obtusus and S. bernardii (Fig. 2a–d). In contrast, different concentrations of NiO-NPs had no significant effect on the growth of S. obliquus (Fig. 2e). However, exposure to high concentrations of ZnO-NPs resulted in a marked reduction in algal growth (Fig. 2f). The negative effects of various nanomaterials (such as carbon nanotubes and nanoparticles) on algae growth and different sensitivity of algae species to such materials have also been reported in previous studies1. MgO and SiO2 nanoparticles were found to be toxic to S. obliquus and Pseudokirchneriella subcapitata even at low (0.8 mg/L) and high concentrations (100 mg/L), respectively17–19. Moreover, Nanochloropsis sp. and Isochrysis sp. were sensitive to Fe2O3-NP even at low concentrations20. These various responses of algal species to the materials might be a species-specific behavior and/or related to chemical characteristics of the medium, surface chemistry, shape and size of the nanomaterials and also their direct interactions and penetrations as well as ions released from nanomaterials to the cells18,21. The toxicity of NPs in green microalgae involves multiple mechanisms, including the release of dissolved ions, the generation of ROS leading to lipid peroxidation and membrane damage, the physical blocking or shading of cells, and the overwhelming of antioxidant defense systems22. The cell wall of microalgae is the primary site of NP interaction and a key factor in determining NP internalization. It generally consists of a porous matrix, with pore channels averaging 20–200 nm in diameter in unicellular green microalgae, allowing NPs to pass through and reach the plasma membrane. Additionally, it has been suggested that contact with nanoparticles can induce the formation of new pores in the membrane through lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing its permeability23. Table 1, shows the amounts of free ionic Ni and Zn (mg/L) released from different concentrations of NiO-NP and ZnO-NP, respectively, in control samples (the culture medium without algae) and in the media containing three algae species. Although, the chemical characteristics of the aquatic environment may alter the bioavailability of the ions, it may also explain the toxicity mechanisms of these nanoparticles in the algae. Our results indicate a low release rate of Ni ions from NiO-NPs considering their total mass in the media (Table 1). Such low solubility of NiO-NPs was also reported when they were suspended in other culture media24,25. In contrast, the amount of Zn ions released from ZnO-NPs was more than that of NiO-NPs (Table 1). Some ZnO-NPs toxicity studies on different organisms, including human26, Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis27, and Lemna minor28, confirmed that dissolved Zn plays a central role in causing ZnO-NP toxicity. However, there are also reports that confirm the minor role of Zn2+ in causing ZnO-NP toxicity, for instance in C. vulgaris and Dunaliella tertiolecta29,30. Thus, the released metal ions from NPs should not be considered as the sole reason for NPs negative effects and there is a close relationship between the toxicity of NPs and their dissolved portion31,32. According to the quantities of ions released from the nanoparticles, some degrees of the ions’ removal have occurred in the solutions. Meanwhile, S. obliquus showed good potential in the removal of Ni ions when compared to the other algae and control sample (Table 1). Despite the high removal of Ni ions from S. obliquus solution, the algae growth was not significantly affected after 20 days treatment with different concentrations of NiO-NP (Fig. 2e). Nickel ions can be adsorbed by functional groups of the algal cell wall (carboxyl, phosphate, hydroxyl and sulfate). Moreover, its intracellular chelation and sequestration or storage in vacuole and oxidative stress defense are some other strategies in response to the absorbed Ni2+ in green microalgae33,34.

Effect of nanoparticles on pigments content

Chlorophyll content have been used as one of the main indicators of stress conditions on algae4 and its depletion can lead to the reduction of the photosynthetic rate when followed by the decrement in the growth rate of the given organism35. Seven days treatment of three algae species by 10, 50 and 100 mg/L of the nanoparticles led to the significantly reduction of chlorophyll (Chl) a and b contents, as compared to the control experiment (Fig. 4a–f, except Chl b content in S. bernardii). It was in agreement with the results of several studies that confirm excess metallic NPs lead bleaching photosynthetic pigments in organisms such as S. bijugus and marine diatoms36–38. Although, some negative impacts of NPs such as thylakoids disorder and membrane damages were apparent in algae’s photosynthesis system and attachment of NPs to the cell surface causing light-shading effects that can inhibit photosynthesis2, but there is still no conclusive evidence explaining how NPs directly disrupt chlorophyll39.

Treatment of S. bernardii with 50 and 100 mg/L of both nanoparticles significantly decreased the total caratenoids content (Fig. 4c and d). Notably reduction of the carotenoids content of S. obtusus and S. obliquus was also obtained after their treatment with high concentration of NiO-NP and ZnO-NP, respectively (Fig. 4a and f). Carotenoids possess antioxidant properties and enhance the stability of the photosynthetic apparatus by scavenging ROS under stress conditions. However, under severe or prolonged stress, their levels may decline due to inhibition of carotenoid-synthesizing enzymes, excessive ROS production, and direct oxidation of carotenoids3.

Antioxidants assays

NPs existence could affect the metabolic processes in algae by inducing ROS production and causing oxidative stress2. Algae have evolved enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidants for tolerating and coping the stress conditions40. Enzymes like superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT) and some compounds like ascorbic acid (AsA), glutathione, carotenoids, phenols and flavonoids can be mentioned as the antioxidants2. Table 2 provides the SOD activity and the amount of two antioxidants, total phenol and flavonoids, following the contaminants exposure in the three examined Scenedesmus species. Accordingly, SOD activity was significantly enhanced after 7 days treatment by 10, 50 and 100 mg/L of both examined NPs. In agreement, augmentation of SOD activity were reported after treatments of different concentrations of nonomaterials such as carbon nanotubes, Fe2O3 nanoparticles and ZnO nanoparticles in S. obliquus and C. vulgaris7,18. SOD, as a front line of defense mechanisms, is involved in inactivation of ROSs produced during different stress conditions and its activity closely related to exposure time and concentration of the pollutants3. Concentration-dependent increase in SOD activity has been revealed under exposure of Cu, Pb and Cd in C. vulgaris and Cu and Zn in S. vacuolatus41,42. However, the SOD activity of S. obliquus treated with 100 mg/L of ZnO-NP was significantly lower than that observed in the 50 mg/L, indicating a hormesis response. At high concentrations, the stress can be severe, leading to enzyme inhibition or denatured. Similarly, an enhancement of SOD activity at low nanoparticle doses, followed by inhibition at higher doses has been reported in S. obliquus treated with varying concentrations of MgO-NP and with co-exposure to Cu and nano Al2O318,43.

Microalgae are an important source of polyphenol compounds that can act as radical scavengers because of their structural properties and ability to transfer electron and/ or chelation of metals. However, there are limited researches on the role of the compounds in algae responses to stress situations3,44. According to Table 2, treatment of the NPs for 7 days decreased the amounts of total phenol compared to the control samples of three algal species. Moreover, treatment of the three concentration of NiO-NP and 50 and 100 mg/L of ZnO-NP significantly decreased the flavanoids content in S. obliquus. Raise of phenolic compounds as protective mechanism against toxicity of heavy metals was reported in Pb and Zn treatment of S. obliquus and Dunaniella tertiolecta12,45. Perhaps, these three examined algal species used other antioxidative processes to manage the stress condition. It is while, treatments of 50 and 100 mg/L of both examined NPs in S. obtusus and 10 mg/L of the NPs in S. bernardii was lead to the significantly enhancement of flavonoids content (Table 2). It was reported that the treatment of C. sorokiniana and S. acuminatus by Zn was also lead to augmentation of flavonoids content46.

Fatty acid profiles

Microalgae are more suitable than plants for mass culturing and also oil based on them have important advantages over oleaginous crops. They grow fast, do not require soil fertility and fresh water and tolerate extreme weather conditions47. Although most microalgae can grow under extreme conditions, factors such as nutrient availability, light intensity, temperature, and exposure to stressors can significantly affect their growth rate, cellular composition, and lipid content. During oxidative stress, algae often show changes in both the types and amounts of fatty acids, typically with increased saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids (SFAs and MUFAs, respectively) and decreased polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). This shift in fatty acids profile is often associated with the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms in algae and serves as a protective strategy, reducing susceptibility to lipid peroxidation that can damage cell membranes48. The diversity of fatty acid derivatives in microalgae, along with their structures and degree of saturation, are key factors determining their suitability for various applications, including biodiesel production and nutraceuticals49. The ratio of PUFA/SFA, Ʃn-3/ Ʃn-6 ratio and cetane number (CN) are three main indices use to evaluate algal oils and determine their suitability for various applications50,51. Algal oil is considered suitable for nutraceutical applications when the PUFA/SFA ratio exceeds 1.5 and the Σn-3/Σn-6 ratio is greater than 151,52. According to obtained results, 7 days treatment of S. obtusus and S. bernardii with 100 mg/L of NiO-NPs and ZnO-NPs led to an increase in the PUFA/SFA and Σn-3/Σn-6 ratios, making these two species more suitable for nutraceutical production following the nanoparticles treatment (Table 3). The high α-linolenic acid (ALA; >24%) content observed in these two species after nanoparticle treatment further confirms their nutritional value53 (Table 3). Consistent with these findings, Scenedesmus species have been reported to contain a high ALA content (~ 22% of total fatty acids), highlighting their potential as valuable sources of ALA for food and feed applications54. Treatment with both NPs in S. obliquus notably increased the levels of MUFAs and the combined SFAs + MUFAs. The CNs of its fatty acids after exposure to NiO-NPs and ZnO-NPs were 56.4 and 56.1, respectively (Table 3). Algal oils with a CN greater than 51 are considered suitable for biodiesel production based on the EU standard (EN 14214)55. The role of nanoparticles in alteration the lipid metabolism and enhancement of lipid content in some microalgae was confirmed. For instance, increase of concentrations of MgO, Fe2O3 and Ag nanoparticles raised lipid content and its productivity of S. obliquus and C. vulgaris18,56. It has been also confirmed that the availability of some nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorous and carbon concentration can make a proper condition in synergistic effect with metal ions for accumulation of some SFAs47.

Effect on the malondialdehyde content

In addition to increase ROS production causing membrane peroxidation in algal cells by NPs, their interaction with the cells caused damage the membranes and the subcellular structures and also increase membrane porosity2. Malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the produced lipid oxidation compounds during stress conditions and its content is directly correlated with peroxidation of PUFAs, ROS accumulation and subsequent oxidative damage1. Treatment of three microalgae with 50 and 100 mg/L of NPs led to a statistically significant rise of the MDA content in the algae compared to the control samples (Fig. 5) and just treatment of S. obtusus with different concentrations of NiO-NPs had no notably effect in MDA content of the algae (Fig. 5a). The enhancement of PUFAs levels together with the increase in MDA content after treatment of S. bernardii with high concentrations of the NPs suggests that the algae actively synthesized PUFAs (possibly as a stress adaptation to maintain membrane fluidity) while simultaneously undergoing peroxidation of a portion of these PUFAs, leading to elevated MDA (Fig. 5b). The reduction of PUFAs, the concomitant increase in SFAs + MUFAs, the decline in antioxidant compounds such as carotenoids and phenols, and the elevated MDA content observed after treatment of S. obliquus with 100 mg of NPs collectively confirm the occurrence of lipid peroxidation damage (Fig. 5c). Although SOD enzyme activity increased following the treatment, this response was insufficient to counteract the detrimental effects of the high nanoparticle concentrations.

Contents of MDA (µM/ mL) in (a) S. obtusus, (b) S. bernardii, (c) S. obliquus exposed to different concentrations of NiO-NP and ZnO-NP (Mean ± SD, n = 3, Different letters assigned to each nanoparticle in different parts of the figure indicate statistically significant differences (p < 0.05)).

The obtained result could be a sign of lipid peroxidation during the treatment of three algae species with high concentrations of the pollutants. Similar results were detected after exposure of some microalgae with different nanoparticles. MDA content was raised in C. vulgaris. and S. obliquus after exposure to NiO-NPs and TiO2-NPs, respectively57,58. According to the aforementioned results, following treatment with the NPs, the amount of some antioxidant compounds such as phenolic compounds and carotenoids decrease, and this could be one of the possible influencing factors in increasing lipid peroxidation59.

Methods

Nanoparticles

NiO-NP and ZnO-NP were supplied from Iranian Nanomaterials Pioneers Company (Iran). The nanoparticles were directly suspended and dispersed by probe sonicator (60 W, 20 kHz) for 15 min to produce three different concentrations (10, 50 and 100 mg L− 1). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) was carried out by a Mira microscope (Mira3, Tescan, Czech Republic) to obtain the morphology of NPs. Their diameters were measured on the SEM images by Digimizer software (version 4.1.1.0). The hydrodynamic sizes of the NPs in suspension and zeta potential were determined using a Horiba SZ-100 (Japan).

Algae cultivation and treatments

S. obliquus, S. bernardii and S. obtusus (numbers: INA15020, ABDf21126 and ABDf21132, respectively) were obtained from Iranian National Algae Culture Collection (INACC) and cultivated in 100 mL liquid mineral Bold’s Basal Medium (BBM) using 250 mL flasks60. The flasks were at 21 ± 3 °C, illuminated with cool white fluorescent tubes at a continuous light intensity of 60 µmol photon m− 2 s− 1 (24 h photoperiod) and aerated with filtered air continuously. The microalgae were harvested every seven days. The flasks containing specific numbers of the algae and culture medium were exposed to 10, 50 and 100 mg/L of both NPs, during the experiment.

Measurement of the growth rate and pigments content

Pre-cultures of the three examined algae at the exponential phase were used for growth assays. The microalgae inoculated into the fresh culture media containing different concentrations of NiO-NP and ZnO-NP. The treatment lasted for 20 days and the algal growth was monitored daily (initial cell density: 1.2 ± 0.4 × 106 cells/mL) by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 690 nm (spectrophotometer, Techcomp S 1020, China).

To determine the contents of Chl a, b, as well as total carotenoid, 2 mL of each microalgae culture was centrifuged and after decanting the supernatant, the residue was blended with methanol 100% and then incubated for 24 h without light. Finally, the contents were determined spectrophotometrically using the equations described by Lichtenthaler61.

Antioxidants assays

To determine the changes in SOD activity in the microalgae, all algal species were treated with different concentrations of NiO-NP and ZnO-NP in the nutrient solution. After homogenizing of the cells in a buffer solution containing 0.2% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), they were centrifuged at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were applied to assess the activity of SOD. The SOD activity was acquired by determining the inhibition of the photochemical reduction rate of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) through the algal extract. The reaction mixture containing potassium phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.8), NBT, riboflavin and a suitable aliquot of enzyme extracts was illuminated for 15 min at light intensity of 5000 lx. The absorbance was measured at 560 nm and the enzyme activity was indicated by U/mL of extracts62. Bradford assay was applied for protein content measurement63.

The content of the total phenols was calculated by a spectrometric method described by Singleton64. A proper amount of the algae extracts was mixed with distilled water and the Folin-Ciocalteu’s reagent. Then, Na2CO3 (20%) was added and After 2 h incubation of the samples at the ambient temperature, the absorbance was determined at 760 nm. Their contents were expressed as µg of gallic acid equivalent (GAE) / mL of solutions. Flavonoids contents were measured according to the aluminum chloride colorimetric method65. Quercetin was applied as a reference compound. Suitable amount of the algae methanolic extracts was mixed with 2% AlCl3 methanol solution. The absorbance of the mixture was determined at 420 nm and expressed as µg quercetin equivalent (QE) / mL of solution.

Determination of total fatty acid profiles

According to the method described by Christie66, 1 ml of 2.5% H2SO4 (v/v) in methanol was added to 0.1 g of the algae samples and after heating in 80 °C for 1 h, cooled to ambient temperature. Thereafter, 500 µL of n-hexane followed by 1.5 mL of 0.9% (w/v) aqueous NaCl were added. After vortexing and centrifuging (4000 rpm, 5 min), phases were separated. Some of the upper phase was transferred to an injection vial67. Concentrated extract was injected into an Agilent 7890 A GC apparatus, equipped with J&W DB225. Fatty acids were identified by comparison of retention times to standards. Each fatty acid’s amounts in the samples were determined with addition of internal standards. The cetane number was calculated based on the weighted average method55.

MDA content

Proper amounts of fresh algae samples were ground in trichloroacetic acid (TCA; 0.1% (w/v)). After adding thiobarbituric acid (TBA; 0.5% (w/v)) in 20% (w/v) TCA, the solutions were heated at 95 °C for 30 min and then, refrigerated rapidly. The supernatants absorbance were determined at 532 nm and 600 nm and MDA contents of the algae were expressed as nmol MDA / mg protein68.

Determination of Ni and Zn ions’ dissolution

Dissolution of Ni and Zn ions from NiO-NP and ZnO-NP suspensions was determined to evaluate their involvement causing the nanoparticles’ toxicity. The prepared NiO-NP and ZnO-NP suspensions at different concentrations were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min and after filtration; the ions’ concentrations were measured by atomic absorption spectrometry (novAA 400, Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analysis was performed with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey-Kramer, multiple comparisons test (data with three replicates; using GraphPad Software, Inc. USA).

Conclusion

The exposure effects of NiO-NP and ZnO-NP suspensions with three algal species caused the deterioration of several cellular characteristics. 20 days treatment of high doses of both NPs, led to reduction of the algae growth. Considering the amount of ions released from NPs and their some biochemical effects on the microalgae, it is likely that these ions are not the only reason for the negative effects of NPs treatment and there is a close relationship between the toxicity caused by the NPs themselves and the ions released from them. Microscopic images confirm the surface adsorption of NPs, their entry into the cells, and their negative effects on intracellular structures. Moreover, NP treatment increased SOD activity and MDA content in all three species, while the levels of total carotenoids and total phenols decreased. The fatty acid profiles of the three algae species were also altered following exposure to both NPs. As a result, S. obtusus and S. bernardii became more suitable for biodiesel production, whereas NP treatment made S. obliquus more suitable for nutrient production. The results of the present study will be helpful for the risk assessments of metal oxide NPs and offer new insights into their comparative toxicological effects on the genus Scenedesmus.
