Intracoronary Full Physiology and Imaging Unveiling a Rare Cause of MINOCA Presenting as Ventricular Fibrillation
Authors: Maria Federica Crociani, Carlo Di Mario, Niccolò Ciardetti, Giulia Nardi, Silvia Maiani, Francesca Di Muro, Matteo Orlandi, Lucrezia Biagiotti, Francesco Meucci, Alessio Mattesini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNonobstructive coronary artery disease is diagnostically challenging, especially in patients with recurrent chest pain and inconclusive imaging. Timeline of the Case

Ventricular fibrillation is the leading cause of sudden cardiac death worldwide. Although most cases are triggered by acute coronary syndromes (ACS) related to obstructive coronary artery disease, a subset of patients experience ventricular fibrillation in the absence of angiographic lesions. In these cases, functional coronary abnormalities or anatomic anomalies such as myocardial bridging (MB), coronary vasospasm, and microvascular dysfunction become critical diagnostic considerations. Advanced intracoronary imaging and physiology testing are essential in uncovering such causes and guiding targeted treatment.

Case Presentation

A 57-year-old man with cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidemia, former smoker) presented with acute chest pain. Electrocardiogram (ECG) by emergency services revealed ST-segment elevation in the anteroseptal leads and reciprocal depression inferiorly. He received aspirin and heparin before his arrival at the hospital but developed ventricular fibrillation en route to the catheterization laboratory. He was successfully defibrillated, and the postresuscitation ECG showed new right bundle branch block, negative T waves in the anteroseptal and lateral leads, and persistent ST-segment depression (Figure 1). Emergent coronary angiography revealed normal epicardial coronaries with right dominance. He was transferred to the intensive care unit.

Past Medical History

A detailed medical history uncovered a decade-long course of recurrent angina dating back to 2013, initially attributed to coronary vasospasm based on symptoms and radial artery spasm observed during a normal coronary angiogram. Noninvasive investigations, including stress echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, showed septal ischemic late gadolinium enhancement and apical hypokinesia, raising suspicion for ischemic insult.

The patient was managed empirically over several years with calcium-channel blockers and nitrates, with intermittent symptom control.

In June 2019, the patient reported another episode of typical chest pain radiating to the left arm, with no significant troponin elevation. A coronary computed tomography angiography was therefore performed, which confirmed normal coronary arteries and a calcium score of 0. The treatment regimen was further adjusted, replacing verapamil with diltiazem 120 mg twice daily and adding ranolazine 375 mg twice daily, while maintaining oral nitrates.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's young age, the chronicity of his symptoms, the presence of septal late gadolinium enhancement on magnetic resonance imaging (presumed to be secondary to vasospasm), the actual apical hypokinesia on the echocardiogram, the ECG alterations seen, and the troponin rise (165 pg/mL at peak), we performed a comprehensive invasive evaluation. This included intracoronary imaging with optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the left coronary artery to search for potentially unstable plaques that could explain an angiographically silent ACS. If imaging proved negative, a full physiological assessment was planned to confirm vasospasm and evaluate for additional coronary functional abnormalities.

Management

We then proceeded as follows:

•OCT pullbacks of the left anterior descending artery and left circumflex artery showed diffuse atherosclerosis with a predominant lipid component, without features of instability. A nonsignificant caliber reduction in the mid left anterior descending artery with a homogeneous band of intermediate light intensity surrounding the vessel wall was observed, consistent with MB (Figures 2 and 3).

Optical coherence tomography revealed diffuse, noncritical atherosclerosis with a predominantly lipid-rich component and presence of the so-called “half-moon sign” in the midvessel segment, consistent with a myocardial bridge. Intracoronary physiological assessment demonstrated the hemodynamic significance of the bridge, with normalization of the RFR after intravenous beta-blocker administration (RFR: 0.96 vs 0.86 after beta-blocker administration, FFR: 0.94, CFR: 2.2, IMR: 25). Outcome and Follow-Up

The final diagnosis was myocardial ischemia due to MB and structural microvascular dysfunction. Consequently, the patient's treatment was revised by discontinuing both nitrates and diltiazem, increasing the dose of ranolazine from 375 to 500 mg twice daily, and introducing metoprolol at 100 mg twice daily. An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was placed for secondary prevention. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5. At the 6-month follow-up, he was completely asymptomatic, with a normal 12-lead ECG and normal echocardiographic findings.

Discussion

This case demonstrates how a comprehensive assessment using intracoronary imaging and physiology is crucial for the accurate diagnosis and management of nonobstructive coronary artery disease, as emphasized in the 2023 and 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of ACS and chronic coronary syndromes.1,2

The lack of a precise pathophysiological characterization over such a prolonged period (more than 10 years of misdiagnosis) led to the prescription of empirical medical therapy that was ultimately inadequate. Conversely, nitrates may have exacerbated symptoms owing to their enhancing effect on MB.

The “full physiology” approach enabled the stepwise identification of 2 coexisting proischemic conditions: structural microvascular dysfunction and ischemic MB. This facilitated the initiation of a tailored and specific medical regimen,3,4 which resulted in satisfactory symptom reduction at the 6-month follow-up.

Although the methodology and diagnostic criteria for the assessment of epicardial vasospasm and microvascular dysfunction are now well standardized,2,3 there is a lack of shared guidelines on how and when during the procedure to invasively evaluate MB as well as avoiding pharmacological interactions with agents used to assess vasospasm and microvascular function. What is known is that FFR does not appear to be the most suitable index in this context, given the dynamic nature of flow obstruction caused by MB. In small patient series, it has been observed that diastolic FFR (dFFR), measured after dobutamine administration with a significance cutoff of 0.76, correlates more directly with symptoms and intravascular ultrasound–identified MB. This index avoids the misleading effect of negative intracoronary pressure gradients measured throughout the entire cardiac cycle.5,6 Given the limited availability of dFFR, alternative nonhyperemic indices, such as instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR) and RFR, have been adopted. In a very recent cohort of 64 patients, both indices demonstrated good correlation with dFFR after dobutamine infusion, with RFR showing superior diagnostic accuracy.7

We chose to evaluate the MB using RFR, a nonhyperemic index that accounts for the entire cardiac cycle, as the final step of the procedure. This followed a sequential exclusion process: The MB was assessed only after the effects of acetylcholine, nitrates, and regadenoson had subsided. Given the short half-life of these agents, we considered the baseline altered RFR to be reliable, as well as its normalization after intravenous metoprolol administration.

Although MB is a relatively rare cause of fatal cardiac events and is often asymptomatic, it was found to be an independent determinant of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmia in patients with ICD.8 To prevent sudden cardiac death, an ICD should be considered.

In addition, we decided to perform OCT not only to exclude potential underlying causes of ACS,2 but also to provide detailed anatomical characterization of the MB in the left anterior descending artery, which could be valuable for potential future planning of a revascularization strategy.9

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of performing a comprehensive diagnostic work-up in patients with recurrent chest pain and inconclusive noninvasive findings.
