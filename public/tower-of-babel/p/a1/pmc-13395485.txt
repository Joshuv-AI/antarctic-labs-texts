Barium Where It Does Not Belong: Retrograde Filling of the Biliary Tree
Authors: Trent Walradt, Michael J. Senter-Zapata, Jonah Cohen
Published: 2026

CASE REPORT

A 60-year-old man with metastatic mucinous appendiceal carcinoma complicated by biliary obstruction, status postendoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with placement of a 10 mm × 6 cm covered metal biliary stent 2 months prior, presented with nausea and vomiting and was found to have gastric outlet obstruction. An upper gastrointestinal series with barium was performed to evaluate for multifocal obstruction and assist with procedural planning. During the examination, barium refluxed retrograde into the intrahepatic biliary tree through the indwelling metal stent (Figure 1).

(A) Upper gastrointestinal barium series demonstrating retrograde reflux of barium contrast through an indwelling metal biliary stent with opacification of the intrahepatic biliary tree. (B) Endoscopic image obtained at the time of initial ERCP demonstrating placement of a 10 mm × 6 cm covered metal biliary stent across the ampulla. (C) Endoscopic image obtained during repeat ERCP at presentation demonstrating a patent covered metal biliary stent traversing the ampulla. (D) Abdominal radiograph obtained after ERCP showing clearance of biliary contrast material and interval placement of a lumen-apposing metal stent in the left upper quadrant. ERCP, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography.

The patient subsequently underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with balloon sweep and biliary irrigation to clear retained contrast material, following by endoscopic ultrasound-guided gastrojejunostomy for definitive management of gastric outlet obstruction (Figure 1).

Reflux of orally administered barium into the biliary system is a rare but potentially serious complication of fluoroscopic studies. Retained barium may act as a nidus for hepatolithiasis, leading to obstructive jaundice and cholangitis.1 Reported predisposing factors include biliary-enteric fistulas, prior choledochoenterostomy, small bowel obstruction, and presence of biliary stents.2 While barium sulfate provides superior mucosal detail, water-soluble iodinated contrast agents are rapidly absorbed and considered safer within the biliary tree.3 In patients with biliary stents or suspected biliary-enteric communication, the use of water-soluble contrast should be considered to mitigate the risk of contrast retention and biliary complications.
