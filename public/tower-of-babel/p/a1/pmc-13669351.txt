Endothelial extracellular vesicles preserve vascular smooth muscle cell identity but do not reverse endothelial senescence
Authors: Úrsula Zúñiga-Cuevas, Vania Otárola-Urrutia, Leslye Venegas-Zamora, Magda C Díaz-Vesga, Camila López-Torres, Javiera Favi-Coloma, Constanza Rimassa-Taré, Ximena Calle-Chalco, Andrés Ramírez-Reyes, Alejandra Hernández, Danica Jiménez-Gallegos, Sebastián Urquiza-Zurich, Mayarling F Troncoso, Ignacio Norambuena-Soto, Mario Chiong, Valentina Parra, Sergio Lavandero, Jaime A Riquelme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVascular aging is characterized by endothelial senescence and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypic switching, yet the role of endothelial extracellular vesicles (EVs) in these processes remains unclear. We show that EVs from non-senescent endothelial cells prevent PDGF-BB–induced VSMC dedifferentiation, preserving contractile markers and limiting migration. In endothelial cells, EVs protected against TNF-α–induced eNOS downregulation but failed to reverse inflammatory and mitochondrial features of senescence after short-term exposure, highlighting a context-dependent protective role.Subject terms: Cell biology, Physiology

Introduction

The vascular wall relies on coordinated interactions between endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) to maintain vascular tone, barrier integrity, and homeostasis. Endothelial cells regulate vascular constriction and dilation, hemostasis, inflammation, and angiogenesis, while VSMCs provide structural support and contractility. Dysfunction in either cell type contributes to cardiovascular diseases such as arterial and pulmonary hypertension and atherosclerosis, whose incidence increases with age due to reduced vascular adaptability to changes in blood flow1–6.

Age-related endothelial dysfunction may occur alone or alongside other cardiovascular risk factors. Endothelial senescence is characterized by impaired regenerative capacity, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, barrier disruption, defective angiogenesis, and acquisition of a pro-inflammatory secretory phenotype, collectively promoting vascular stiffening and chronic inflammation4,5. Concurrently, VSMCs undergo phenotypic switching from a contractile to a synthetic state, losing contractile proteins while gaining proliferative and migratory capacity. This process is a major driver of vascular remodeling and neointima formation6.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) mediate intercellular communication within the vascular wall by transferring bioactive molecules, including microRNAs, proteins, and lipids7. Endothelial-derived EVs influence both VSMC behavior and endothelial homeostasis8–11, although their production and effects depend on the physiological state of donor and target cells12,13. Despite their recognized role in vascular regulation14, whether EVs modulate endothelial senescence and VSMC dedifferentiation remains unclear.

Here, we investigated the effects of EVs derived from non-senescent endothelial cells on VSMC phenotype and endothelial senescence.

To assess the role of endothelial EVs in vascular disorders, we used three approaches to simulate vascular dysfunction: VSMC dedifferentiation induced by platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB)6,15, replicative senescence in endothelial cells4,13, and a dysfunctional state of endothelial cells by reducing the protein content of eNOS with a pro-inflammatory stimulus12. To address this, we first isolated EVs from non-senescent HUVECs after 24 h using size-exclusion chromatography. Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis confirmed isolation, showing 7.1 × 10⁸ ± 3 × 10⁸ particles/mL and a modal size of 122.6 ± 16 nm (Fig. 1A). Electron microscopy revealed the typical cup-shaped morphology (Fig. 1B), and Western blot determined protein content of EV markers (CD9, Alix, and Flotillin) with GM130 as a negative control, confirmed by comparison with whole cell lysates. ELISA further confirmed the detection of CD81 (another EV marker) and ACE2 (a protein present in endothelial cells) (Fig. 1C).

Next, we examined whether these endothelial EVs could exert paracrine protection against VSMC phenotypic switching, a primary driver of vascular remodeling and neointima formation. We utilized A7r5 rat thoracic aorta cells, a robust and standard model for assessing smooth muscle dedifferentiation15. To artificially induce this synthetic, dedifferentiated state, VSMCs were stimulated with Platelet-Derived Growth Factor-BB (PDGF-BB) (20 ng/mL) for 24 h. Phenotypic switching is functionally characterized by increased cellular migration and the loss of contractile proteins6,15. Indeed, PDGF-BB stimulation markedly increased VSMC migration, as evidenced by a narrower gap in the wound healing assay after 24 h. Strikingly, a 30 min pre-treatment with endothelial EVs significantly prevented this hyper-migratory response (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, EV exposure prevented the loss of classical contractile markers α-SMA, SM22, and calponin induced by 48 h of PDGF stimulation(Fig. 1E–G). Given the central role of VSMC phenotypic switching in vascular remodeling and diseases such as atherosclerosis and pulmonary arterial hypertension16, these findings support the concept that endothelial EVs act as critical paracrine regulators that preserve VSMC identity and limit pathogenic dedifferentiation, consistent with prior reports describing EV-mediated regulation of VSMC behavior8,9.

Then, we tested endothelial cell dysfunction by inducing replicative senescence in HUVECs. The senescence model was confirmed by increased β-galactosidase activity (Fig. 2A), elevated p16 and p21, reduced eNOS (Fig. 2B–D), enlarged morphology (Fig. 2E), higher mRNA levels of H2AX, mtOGG, p65, and IL-6, and lower eNOS (Fig. 2F–J). Mitochondrial transcripts (mt Policistronic-RNA, 16S, ATP8, ND6, ND3) were also increased (Fig. 2K–O). However, we did not determine telomere length (a known driver of replicative senescence), which is a limitation of our study. Given the stability of the senescent state, we assessed whether short-term EV exposure could modulate selected senescence-associated outputs rather than reverse senescence per se. Immunofluorescence showed more numerous, smaller mitochondria, indicating fragmentation. However, EV treatment for 24 h did not reverse this effect (Fig. 3A). Moreover, regarding pro-inflammatory markers, EV treatment for 24 h did not reduce p65 or IL-6 mRNA expression, nor soluble IL-6 protein levels (Fig. 3B–D). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a marked accumulation of total and nuclear p65 in senescent cells, consistent with NF-κB activation. However, EV treatment did not significantly alter p65 abundance or its subcellular distribution, as confirmed by an unchanged Manders coefficient, indicating no effect on nuclear-to-cytoplasmic localization (Fig. 3E).

Our results suggest that short-term EV exposure is insufficient to override established SASP outputs, characteristic of endothelial senescence. This is consistent with evidence that the SASP is sustained by persistent DNA damage responses and mitochondrial dysfunction17–21, processes resistant to extracellular modulation. Moreover, EVs themselves contribute to SASP reinforcement by propagating inflammatory signals via NF-κB activation22,23, and EVs from senescent VSMCs can further amplify immune cell cytokine production24. Our findings do not exclude the possibility that prolonged or repeated EV exposure, or EVs derived from distinct physiological sources, could influence other aspects of senescent cell biology. Accordingly, plasma EVs from young mice have been reported to extend the lifespan and enhance mitochondrial metabolism of aged mice25, suggesting a protective effect of EVs in the context of aging, and the efficacy of these nanovesicles may also depend on the target cells or tissue.

Finally, we used a third approach of vascular dysfunction in non-senescent endothelial cells. TNF-α was used to induce a pro-inflammatory state in endothelial cells. HUVECs were treated with TNF-α for 48 h, reducing eNOS protein levels, whereas EV pre-treatment for 30 min prevented this effect (Fig. 3F), suggesting that EVs are more effective at preventing inflammatory stress–induced dysfunction than reversing established senescence. However, further studies should confirm this finding by evaluating eNOS activity and other pro-inflammatory markers associated with TNF-α stimulation.

While our study suggests the protective effects of endothelial cell-derived EVs against vascular cell dysfunction, the underlying mechanisms remain uncharacterized. Addressing this key limitation and fully understanding these effects requires further elucidating EV membrane and cargo composition. EVs facilitate paracrine communication by delivering proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (e.g., miRNAs and lncRNAs) that alter recipient cell phenotypes26. Notably, specific miRNAs protect vascular cells27. Examples include hsa-miR-126-3p and hsa-miR-126-5p in VSMCs28–30, hsa-miR-126 and hsa-miR-126-5p in endothelial cells, and hsa-miR-10a in monocytes31,32. Consequently, endothelial cell-derived EVs offer a promising delivery system that can be modified or enriched with protective small molecules, miRNAs, or other non-coding RNAs, for therapeutic applications.

In conclusion, intercellular communication within the vascular wall is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of how aging-related dysfunction emerges and propagates. Here, we show that endothelial EVs preserve vascular homeostasis by limiting VSMC dedifferentiation and protecting healthy endothelial cells from pro-inflammatory stimuli, but are ineffective against the pro-inflammatory state of senescent endothelial cells (Fig. 3G). It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our experimental design. Utilizing isolated, cross-species in vitro models -human HUVECs and rat A7r5 cells- allowed us to perform targeted screening of distinct vascular aging and vascular dysfunction hallmarks. However, these simplified models cannot fully replicate the complex, bidirectional, and continuous paracrine signaling present within an intact vascular wall. Future studies employing co-culture systems or in vivo models will be necessary to capture these complex dynamics. Nevertheless, our current data underscore endothelial EVs as preventive, rather than restorative, regulators of vascular aging and highlight the importance of temporal context when considering EV-based strategies for age-related cardiovascular disease.

Methods

Reagents

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; C2519A) and EGM-2 medium (CC-3162) were obtained from Lonza. A7R5 vascular smooth muscle cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with fetal bovine serum, penicillin, and streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). PDGF-BB was purchased from Millipore Sigma (521225). Accutase (A6964-100ML), trypsin, Rhodamine Phalloidin, Hoechst, Triton X-100, paraformaldehyde, and bovine serum albumin were from Sigma-Aldrich. EV isolation was performed using qEV size-exclusion chromatography columns (IZON). Antibodies used included anti-eNOS (BD 610297), anti-p21 (Abcam ab109520), anti-p16 (Abcam ab108349), anti-α-SMA (Abcam ab7817), anti-SM22 (Abcam ab141062), anti-calponin (Abcam ab46794), anti-CD9 (Cell Signaling D801A), anti-Flotillin-1 (Cell Signaling CD2V7J), anti-Alix (Cell Signaling 3A9), anti-GM130 (Cell Signaling D6B1), GAPDH (Cell signaling-14C10) and anti-β-tubulin (Sigma T0198), with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies from Calbiochem. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining was performed using a commercial kit (Cell Signaling, #9860). MitoTracker Orange FM, Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies, and ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. ELISA kits for human IL-6 (D6050, R&D Systems), CD81 (CSB-EL004960HU, Cusabio), and ACE2 (ab235649, Abcam) were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; C2519A, Lonza) were cultured in EGM-2 medium (CC-3162, Lonza) and passaged with Accutase (A6964-100ML, Sigma). Experiments were performed at 80–90% confluence using control cells (passages 3–12) and senescent cells (passages 18–22). Cells were seeded in 12 and 24-well plates, 35–100 mm dishes, or T75 flasks as required. A7R5 vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) from rat thoracic aorta were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 mg/mL), and passaged with trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich). Both cell types were maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Cells were stimulated with PDGF-BB (20 ng/mL; 521225, Millipore Sigma), 0.1% PBS-BSA, or 1 × 10⁸ EV particles/mL, depending on the experiment.

Isolation and characterization of endothelial EVs

For EVs isolation, early-passage HUVECs were cultured in three T75 flasks per condition, with 10 mL of medium per flask, until reaching approximately 80-90% confluence. Medium was replaced with 10 mL of culture medium supplemented with EV-depleted serum. After 24 hours, EVs were isolated from the medium using size exclusion chromatography with qEV columns (IZON) as described previously12. EVs were visualized using a Talos F200C G2 transmission electron microscope after uranyl staining, whereas size and concentration were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300) as previously described12.

Wound healing assay

Migration was evaluated using a wound healing assay as previously described15. Briefly, cells were cultured in 35-mm plates in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS until reaching approximately 90% confluence. Then, the medium was changed to DMEM with 2% FBS to synchronize the cell metabolism. After 24 h of medium change, cells were removed by gently and quickly scratching the plate using a 200 μL tip. Then, images were taken immediately and named as time 0 h, followed by EV treatment for 30 min, and then with PDGF-BB (a known inductor of VSMC dedifferentiation) and 10 μM of Bromodeoxyuridine to inhibit proliferation. After 24 h, images were taken and named as time 24 h. The percentage of wound healing was quantified by the formula [(cell-free area at 0 h)-(cell-free area at 24 h)/cell-free area at 0 h] x 100.

Western Blot

Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE as described16 and transferred to PVDF membranes by wet electroblotting (400 mA, 90 min). Membranes were blocked with 1× TBS-T containing 5% milk for 1 hour, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: mouse anti-eNOS (BD 610297, 1:1000), rabbit anti-p21 (Abcam ab109520, 1:1000), rabbit anti-p16 (Abcam ab108349, 1:1000), mouse anti-β-tubulin (Sigma T0198, 1:5000), rabbit anti-calponin (Abcam ab46794, 1:5000), mouse anti-α-SMA (Abcam ab7817, 1:20000), rabbit anti-SM22 (Abcam ab141062, 1:5000), mouse anti-CD9 (Cell Signaling D801A, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Flotillin-1 (Cell Signaling CD2V7J, 1:1000), rabbit anti-GM130 (Cell Signaling D6B1, 1:1000), and mouse anti-Alix (Cell Signaling 3A9, 1:1000). After three washes with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with Anti-IgG mouse or Anti IgG rabbit-HRP secondary antibodies (Calbiochem 402335 and 401315, 1:5000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Detection was performed using EZ-ECL on a Licor system. Band intensity was quantified with Fiji (ImageJ) and normalized to β-tubulin or GAPDH, except for EV surface markers.

β-Galactosidase staining

The senescent phenotype of late-passage cells was determined using β-galactosidase staining assays, employing the Senescence-Associated β-galactosidase Staining Kit (#9860, Cell Signaling), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were observed under a light microscope (Leica LM500) at 10x magnification to capture images. Images were analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Determination of cellular area and perimeter

Control and senescent cells were seeded on coverslips in 12-well plates. After 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS 1X, and blocked with 3% BSA/PBS for 1 hour. Cells were incubated with Rhodamine Phalloidin (1:300) for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (1:500, 2 min), and coverslips were mounted with antifade medium (DAKO). Images were obtained using a ZOE™ fluorescence microscope (BioRad). Cell area and perimeter were quantified with Fiji (ImageJ) by averaging 100 cells per condition.

Analysis of mitochondrial network and immunofluorescence

Cells were incubated with 500 nM MitoTracker Orange FM for 30 min at 37 °C, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS), and blocked (5% BSA in PBS). Primary antibodies (anti-p65, mtHSP70) were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488 and 647 secondary antibodies for 1 hour, respectively. Samples were mounted with ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant. Confocal images (Zeiss LSM-5, 63×) were obtained as previously described33 and deconvolved and 3D-reconstructed in ImageJ. Mitochondrial number and volume were quantified with the 3D Object Counter plugin, while p65 fluorescence intensity was analyzed in the equatorial plane of cells using the Analyze Particles plugin.

RT-qPCR

The real-time PCR experiments were performed using SYBR green from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA) as previously described. Accordingly, the data from each transcript were normalized to 18S using the 2ΔΔCt method34. The primers used in this study were the following: eNOS GTGATGGCGAAGCGAGTGAA (Forward) and AACTCTTGTGCTGTTCCGGC (Reverse), IL-6 AGACAGCCACTCACCTCTTCAG (Forward) and TTCTGCCAGTGCCTCTTTGCTG (Reverse), p65 TGAACCGAAACTCTGGCAGCTG (Forward)

and CATCAGCTTGCGAAAAGGAGCC (Reverse), mitochondrial PoliCistronic-RNA CCCACCCAAGAACAGGGTTT (Forward) and AATGAGGAGTAGGAGGTTGGC (Reverse), 16S GCCTTCCCCCGTAAATGATA (Forward)TTATGCGATTACCGGGCTCT (Reverse), ATP8 ACTACCACCTACCTCCCTCAC (Forward) GGATTGTGGGGGCAATGAATG (Reverse), ND6 TGGGGTTAGCGATGGAGGTAGG (Forward AATAGGATCCTCCCGAATCAAC (Reverse), ND3 ATCAACACCCTCCTAGCCTTAC (Forward) CTATGTAGCCGTTGAGTTGTGG (Reverse), H2A.X TCCCTTCCAGCAAACTCAAC (Forward) AACTCCCCAATGCCTAAGGT (Reverse), mitoOGG CCTCAGGTGATTTGTATGCTC (Forward) GAACCTGTTCTAAAACACTGC (Reverse).

ELISA

ELISA kits were used to determine IL-6, ACE2 and CD81 (IL-6: Quantikine ELISA Human IL-6, D6050, R&D Systems; CD81: ELISA Human CD81 antigen, CSB-EL004960HU, Cusabio and Human ACE2, ab235649, ABCAM), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We detected IL-6 from the supernatant of senescent or non-senescent endothelial cells, whereas CD81 and ACE2 were detected in endothelial EVs.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. A normality test was performed for all data, and a two-tailed t-test was conducted (for data with only two experimental conditions) or a one or Two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (for data with more than two experimental groups). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Significant differences were considered as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001 versus controls.
