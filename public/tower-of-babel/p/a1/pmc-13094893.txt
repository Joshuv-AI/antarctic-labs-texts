The mediator effect of emotion regulation strategies in relation between motivation and dysregulation emotion: A structural equation modeling approach
Authors: Hamid Zarrabi, Ahmad Ashouri, Komeil Zahedi Tajrishi, Amir Hossein Jalali Nadoushan, Hojjatollah Farahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The high prevalence of trauma exposure and subsequent negative consequences for both survivors and society as a whole emphasize the need for secondary prevention of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). This research aimed to investigate the mediator effect of emotion regulation strategies in the relation between motivation and dysregulation of emotion.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A structural equation modeling (SEM) approach was used in this study. The design of the present study was descriptive and correlational using structural equation modeling. The population included all of Tehran’s citizens in 2024. In total, 300 people were selected based on the SEM approach. Finally, the data were collected and analyzed by Pearson correlation and structural equation modeling using SPSS software (version 19) and AMOS software (version 20).RESULTS:The structural equation parameters for the modified research model were acceptable (X2 = 67.92; df = 40; X2/df = 1.70; P = 0.004; GFA = 0.95; AGFI = 0.92; CFI = 0.96; RMSEA = 0.05). Also, all direct effects between the research variables were significant.CONCLUSION:The results of this study showed that the model fits the data. These results highlighted the importance of emotion regulation strategies for motivation and dysregulation of emotion in PTSD patients. Therefore, the awareness of teachers, parents, adolescents, counselors, psychologists, and other professionals to promote motivation and emotion regulation can be helpful.

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), also known as the “stressor criterion,” has been a major source of debate ever since PTSD was added to the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders (DSM) in 1980.[1] Psychological trauma can lead to significant distress, functional impairment, and the development of trauma-related psychological symptoms. Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one well known reaction to trauma that includes symptoms ranging from re-experiencing and avoidance to arousal and reactivity.[2] PTSD commonly co-occurs with a range of psychopathology, and the presence of comorbid disorders can be related to greater severity of symptoms and worse outcomes. Importantly, two different diagnostic definitions of PTSD exist in the leading disease classification systems: the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder[3] and the 11th version of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11; World Health Organization (WHO). The DSM-5 includes a more expanded definition of PTSD that includes symptoms related to negative cognitions and mood,[4] whereas the ICD-11 includes a narrower definition of PTSD reflecting six symptoms from the re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal clusters only.[5]

Due to the high comorbidity between different disorders, the DSM-based diagnostic system has been criticized, and several theoretical models have attempted to explain these comorbidities in the diagnostic system.[34] Thus, the existence of higher-level factors in these disorders has been emphasized and investigated to explain the causes of comorbidity.[6] For example, in Clark and Watson’s triadic model, negative affectivity or neuroticism is a nonspecific distress factor that is common to both depressive and anxiety disorders and is considered a predisposing factor for both disorders.[7] Each of these two disorders also has its own specific components, such that depression is specifically related to low positive affect and anxiety is uniquely related to automatic arousal.[3]

Following the presentation of this model, Watson presented the quantitative hierarchy model, stating the limitations of this model and based on the research presented in the field of comorbidity and common underlying factors.[8] According to this model, the existence of a high correlation between depressed/sad mood (the core and prominent element of mood disorders) and anxious/fearful mood (a feature of anxiety disorders) has important structural implications for DSM-5, in that they indicate that these disorders should be placed in a separate general category called “emotional disorders”.[9] This general category includes three subgroups of disorders, namely bipolar disorders (bipolar I, bipolar II, cyclical), anxiety disorders (major depression, depressive mood,[2] generalized anxiety disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder) and fear disorders (panic attacks, agoraphobia, social anxiety, specific phobia).[1011]

In this regard, there is a growing interest in understanding the role of emotions and emotion regulation as common components and how this knowledge can contribute to the generation of therapeutic targets, especially for treatment-resistant cases. The most important models that currently explain the role of emotion dysregulation in various disorders are Taheri's et al.[12] (2023) emotion regulation model and Nolen-Hoeksema's et al.[13] (1991) and Mansouri's et al.[14] (2012) emotion dysregulation model. some study showed that emotion dysregulation and emotion regulation treatment model was initially developed with an emphasis on generalized anxiety disorder,[1315] but was finally revised in 2013, and this time, given the high comorbidity of generalized anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder, it considered both of these disorders and paid special attention to their comorbidity.[16]

A review of studies in the field of emotion regulation for posttraumatic stress disorder indicates that the current conceptual models are not designed based on a unique model of posttraumatic stress disorder.[17] It seems that conceptual models should be examined to conceptualize emotion regulation in this group of patients and that the proposed model can be used in affected populations.[12] Given the limitations in the lack of fitted models in emotion regulation, it is necessary to address conceptual models and fit the model based on the current research literature and previous model [Figure 1].

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The design of the present study was descriptive and correlational using structural equation modeling.

Study participants and sampling

The statistical population of the study included people living in Tehran in 2023–2024, who were between the ages of 20 and 59. Since 20 cases are required for each factor (latent variable),[18] in general, at least 300 people are selected as samples by purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria included PTSD according to the diagnostic criteria of DSM-5-TR and in the presence of other psychiatric diagnoses, the diagnosis of PTSD must be clearly stated as the main diagnosis, Age between 18 and 55 years and Minimum education was grade 9. Exclusion criteria included Severe psychiatric disorders that are a barrier to treatment for PTSD (i.e., psychotic disorders and bipolar disorders with psychotic features), Severe levels of personality disorders that cause serious communication problems (including schizotypal, schizoid, paranoid, borderline, antisocial, and avoidant personality disorders), Suffering from alcohol or substance use disorders, Severe physical illnesses such as cancer and HIV-positive patients whose physical illness is a priority for treatment, starting treatment with psychiatric medications in the last 3 months or changing the dosage of medications during this period, given that the therapeutic properties of psychiatric medications used for this disorder usually stabilize by 12 weeks, Starting other psychotherapy at the same time, Missing two therapy sessions during the treatment process.

Data collection tool and technique

Rumination Response Scale: This scale was developed by Nolen-Hoeksima and Morrow in 1991. It is a self-report instrument, has 22 questions and is scored on a 4-point scale from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always). In addition, it includes three subscales: distraction, rumination, and rumination. This scale has shown an internal reliability of 0.89 and a satisfactory 5-month test-retest reliability[13] In its Persian version, internal reliability using Cronbach’s alpha has been reported to be 0.88 to 0.92, indicating that this scale has good internal reliability. Its intraclass correlation has also been obtained as 0.75.[14]

Pennsylvania State Worry Questionnaire: This questionnaire was developed by Meyer et al. in 1990.[15] It is a 16-item self-report questionnaire that measures intense, excessive, and uncontrollable worry. This questionnaire is used as a screening tool for generalized anxiety disorder. The response scale to the questions is 5-point Likert. Each question is scored from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (very true). The specifications of the original version of the questionnaire are as follows: the internal consistency coefficient for generalized anxiety disorder was reported to be 0.86 and the test-retest reliability with a 4-week interval was 0.77. The concurrent validity with the Beck test was reported to be satisfactory (0.49). This questionnaire has a two-factor structure, the general worry factor consists of 11 questions and the lack of worry factor consists of 5 questions. The psychometric properties of the Iranian version are as follows: the internal consistency coefficient was 0.88 and the test-retest coefficient was 0.79. The correlation of the total score of the questionnaire with the score of the trait-state questionnaire (STAIT) was 0.68 and with the total score of the Beck Depression Inventory was 0.49, indicating good convergent validity.[161718]

Emotional Intensity Scale: This scale was developed by Larsen et al. in 1986. It is a 40-item self-report scale that assesses individual differences in emotional intensity. Alpha coefficients ranging from 0.90 to 0.94 have been reported for this instrument in 4 different samples. Larsen et al.[19] reported Cronbach’s alpha, parent report, and two-week reliability of the scale as 0.81, 0.50, and 0.61, respectively. They also reported friend report of mood as 0.41. In Iran, Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was reported as 0.83. Also, friend report of mood and parent report of mood and two-week reliability of the scale were obtained as 0.39, 0.47, and 0.56, respectively.[20] To measure the intensity of negative emotion as one of the components of the latent variable of motivation, the Negative Emotion Intensity subscale (11 questions) was used in this study.

Difficulty in Emotion Regulation Scale: This scale was developed by Gratz and Romer in 2004. It is a 36-item self-report scale that assesses individuals’ emotion regulation patterns and is made up of 6 subscales. These subscales include: denial of emotional responses, difficulty in performing goal-directed behaviors, impulse control problems, lack of awareness of emotions, limitations in accessing emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity. The psychometric characteristics of the original version of this scale are as follows: This scale has good internal consistency (0.93) and the reliability of its subscales using Cronbach’s alpha is more than 0.80, which is very good for each subscale.[18] The psychometric characteristics of its Persian version are as follows: Its reliability was obtained using two methods, Cronbach’s alpha and split-half, respectively, 0.86 and 0.80. To determine the validity of the aforementioned scale, its score was correlated with the score of the Zuckerman Emotion Seeking Questionnaire, and it was found that there was a positive and significant correlation between them (n = 59, r = 0.26, P < 0.043). This indicates that the difficulty in emotion regulation scale has the necessary validity.[21] The acceptance subscale was used to assess the acceptance (allowing) emotion regulation strategy.

Attention Control Scale: This scale was first developed and used by Driberry and Reid in 2002.[22] It is a 20-item scale. In a factor analysis conducted by Driberry and Reid, three factors were identified: (1) attention switching, (2) attention focus, and (3) ability to flexibly control thought. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.88 for this scale. Driberry and Reid also showed that attention control is inversely related to state anxiety. In another study, Cronbach’s alpha for the total and the subscales of focus and attention switching were calculated and reported as 0.84, 0.82, and 0.68, respectively.[23]

Experiences questionnaire

This questionnaire was developed by Fresco et al.[24] It is a 20-item self-report questionnaire that measures distancing and non-identification with negative thinking content, which is a process of change in MBCT. Items are rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (never) to 5 (all the time). This questionnaire includes two factors, distancing and rumination, whose internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha has been reported as 0.83 and 0.70, respectively. In addition, this questionnaire has reported acceptable convergent and divergent correlations in general and clinical population samples. The psychometric properties of the Persian version of this questionnaire in the Iranian sample are as follows: The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the distancing subscale was 0.82. Also, a negative relationship was found between the distancing subscale and symptoms of depression, rumination, depression, experiential avoidance, and emotion regulation.[25] In this study, the distancing subscale (11 questions) was used to assess the distancing emotion regulation strategy.

The emotion regulation questionnaire

This is a self-report questionnaire consisting of 10 items. This questionnaire was developed by Gross and John in 2003 and measures two emotion regulation strategies: suppression and reappraisal. The reappraisal subscale consists of 6 items (e.g., I change my emotions by changing the way I think about the situation) and the suppression subscale consists of 4 items (e.g., I do not express negative emotions when I experience them). Participants are asked to rate their responses on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). This questionnaire has shown good internal reliability (reappraisal: 0.79 and suppression: 0.73) as well as good convergent validity.[25] In the Italian version of this questionnaire, internal consistency was obtained for the reappraisal and suppression subscales as 0.84 and 0.72, respectively. Also, the 2-month test-retest reliability of the two subscales was reported as 0.67 and 0.71, respectively.[26] In Iran, Soleimani and Habibi reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the cognitive reappraisal subscale as 0.71 and expressive suppression as 0.75.[27]

Ethical consideration

This study is part of a larger research project. The ethics committee of University of Medical Sciences approved all study protocols (IR.IUMS.REC.1402.983). We conducted the research adhering to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis method

The data were analyzed by Pearson correlation and structural equation modeling using SPSS software (version 19) and AMOS software (version 20).

Results

A study conducted in Tehran in 2024 involved 300 participants. The participants had an average age of 28.69 years, with a standard deviation of 7.80 years. The gender distribution showed that females made up the majority at 84.66% of participants, while males constituted 15.34%. Regarding education levels, 45.66% of participants held bachelor’s degrees, 39.33% had master’s degrees, 6.66% held PhDs, and 8.33% had other educational qualifications. the skewness and kurtosis values for the research variables are between +1 and –1, indicating that the research data are normal.

The skewness and kurtosis values for the research variables are between +1 and –1, which indicates the univariate normality of the research data. In the present study, skewness and kurtosis indices were used instead of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, because the sensitivity of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to type I error (false rejection of the null hypothesis) increases with increasing sample size. No missing or outlier data were found in this study. In addition, the Mardia statistic is not presented in this study due to the limitation of SPSS software. The value of the Durbin-Watson statistic was obtained between 2.5 and 1.5, which indicated the independence of the residuals. Also, the tolerance statistic values of the predictor variables were greater than 0.40 and the variance inflation index (VIF) values were less than the worrying value of 2.50, which indicates multiple noncollinearity. The initial analysis of the structural equation model showed mixed results regarding model fit indices. While some parameters were satisfactory (GFA = 0.92, CFI = 0.90), the RMSEA value of 0.09 exceeded the acceptable threshold, and the Chi-square ratio (X2/df = 2.71, P < 0.001) indicated room for improvement. The model demonstrated that the combination of motivational factors (including negative emotion intensity, reward motivation, and safety motivation) and emotion regulation strategies (comprising body awareness, attention control, allowing, distancing, and reappraisal) explained 72% of the variance in emotion dysregulation symptoms (worry, rumination, and subclinical PTSD). The analysis revealed significant direct relationships among all studied variables [Table 2]. The findings demonstrated that emotion regulation strategies (which included body awareness, attention control, allowing, distancing, and reappraisal) mediated the relationship between motivation (comprising negative emotion intensity, reward motivation, and safety motivation) and the severity of emotion dysregulation (measured through worry, rumination, and subclinical PTSD). This mediation effect was statistically significant (Estimate = -0.39, SE = 0.083, CI = [-0.54, -0.27], P = 0.006). The refined model showed that motivational factors and emotion regulation strategies together accounted for 75% of the variation in emotion dysregulation severity [Figure 2].

Discussion

The findings of this study provide compelling evidence for the mediating role of emotion regulation strategies in the relationship between motivation and emotional dysregulation, particularly in the context of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). This research makes several significant contributions to our understanding of PTSD and emotional regulation mechanisms. First, it establishes a clear empirical link between motivational factors and emotional dysregulation, with emotion regulation strategies serving as a crucial intermediate mechanism. This finding has important implications for therapeutic interventions, suggesting that strengthening emotion regulation capabilities could help mitigate the impact of motivational deficits on emotional dysregulation in trauma survivors. The results underscore the importance of adopting an integrated approach to PTSD treatment that considers the interplay between motivation, emotion regulation strategies, and emotional dysregulation. Clinicians and mental health professionals should consider incorporating targeted interventions that enhance both motivational aspects and emotion regulation skills in their treatment protocols. This dual focus could potentially improve treatment outcomes and help prevent the development of chronic PTSD symptoms.

Furthermore, these findings have broader implications for various stakeholders, including educators, parents, counselors, and psychologists. The development of preventive programs and interventions that focus on strengthening emotion regulation strategies could be particularly beneficial in reducing the risk of PTSD and improving overall psychological resilience. Training programs for professionals working with trauma-exposed individuals should emphasize the importance of understanding and addressing these interconnected psychological mechanisms. Future research should extend these findings by examining potential moderating variables and investigating the effectiveness of specific interventions targeting the identified pathways. Longitudinal studies would be valuable in establishing the temporal stability of these relationships and understanding how they might evolve over time in PTSD patients.

Generally, this study highlights the critical mediating role of emotion regulation strategies in the link between motivation and emotional dysregulation. Enhancing motivation and adaptive emotion regulation skills can serve as effective strategies for mitigating emotional dysregulation and improving overall psychological health. Future research should explore longitudinal effects and intervention-based approaches to further validate these findings.

Limitation and recommendations

Given that the study was conducted solely in Tehran, the generalizability of findings to other cultural contexts and geographical locations may be limited. Cultural differences in emotion regulation strategies and motivational factors could influence the observed relationships. The cross-sectional nature of the research design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between motivation, emotion regulation strategies, and emotional dysregulation. The reliance on self-report measures may introduce common method bias and social desirability effects, potentially affecting the validity of the reported relationships between variables. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to better understand the temporal dynamics and causal relationships between motivation, emotion regulation strategies, and emotional dysregulation in PTSD patients. Researchers should consider incorporating multiple assessment methods, including behavioral measures and clinical interviews, to complement self-report data and strengthen the validity of findings. Finally, Cross-cultural validation studies are needed to examine whether the mediating role of emotion regulation strategies remains consistent across different cultural contexts and populations.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical mediating role of emotion regulation strategies in the link between motivation and emotional dysregulation. Enhancing motivation and adaptive emotion regulation skills can serve as effective strategies for mitigating emotional dysregulation and improving overall psychological health. Future research should explore longitudinal effects and intervention-based approaches to further validate these findings.
