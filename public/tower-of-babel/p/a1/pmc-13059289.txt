Predictive value of optic nerve sheath diameters on cerebral performance category scale in patients with impaired consciousness in the emergency department: a prospective pilot study
Authors: Yuwei Wang, Shurong Xu, Sa Wang, Zuojia Wu, Danping Yan, Yajie Liu, Meiling Wang, Shuaishuai Zhou, Bingxiang Wang, Qingfeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe value of optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) in emergency departments remains unclear. The aim of this study is to investigate the predictive value of ONSD in discharge cerebral performance category (CPC) in emergency department patients with impaired consciousness.MethodsThis is a prospective pilot study. Emergency department patients with impaired consciousness were prospectively included in a tertiary hospital in China in January 2025. Demographical data, medical data, and ONSD were prospectively collected by two researchers. Patients were divided into the good CPC group and the poor CPC group according to the CPC scale at discharge. Logistic regression was conducted to investigate the predictive value of ONSD in discharge CPC.ResultsA total of 58 patients were included in this study. 20(33.9%) patients in the good CPC group. The ONSD was significantly different between groups (good CPC group: 5.44 ± 0.46 vs. poor CPC group: 5.89 ± 0.73, P = 0.016). After adjustment for blood glucose and sodium, ONSD remained a significant predictor of discharge CPC, with consistent associations observed across per 1 mm, per 0.5 mm, and per 0.1 mm scaling (OR: 3.065, 95%CI: 1.042–9.014, P = 0.042; OR: 1.751, 95%CI: 1.021–3.002, P = 0.042; OR: 1.119, 95%CI: 1.004–1.246, P = 0.042). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further demonstrated a moderate discriminative ability of ONSD for poor neurological outcome, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.704 (95% CI: 0.571–0.837). The optimal cut-off value of ONSD was 5.67 mm.ConclusionONSD is a predictive factor for discharge neurological function in emergency department patients with impaired consciousness. Further studies are needed to clarify the predictive value of ONSD.

Background

Approximately 5%–10% of emergency department (ED) visits are attributable to impaired consciousness, and among these patients [1, 2], 30%–70% of survivors are discharged with poor neurological outcomes [3–6]. Among them, approximately 61.2% were outside the category of neurology or neurosurgery [4].

Previous studies have demonstrated that intracranial pressure (ICP) is an important predictor of neurological outcomes in neurology and neurosurgery patients. However, due to its invasiveness and procedural complexity, ICP monitoring is rarely applied in patients with impaired consciousness without cerebral infarction or intracerebral hemorrhage. Few studies have examined changes in ICP in this population or evaluated its prognostic value for neurological outcomes. The optic nerve sheath is a continuation of the intracranial dura mater and contains the subarachnoid space, thereby communicating with the intracranial compartment posterior to the globe. Recent studies have demonstrated that the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) increases in response to elevated ICP [7]. The correlation between ONSD and ICP was first elucidated by Hansen et al. in 1996, establishing ONSD as a potential noninvasive surrogate for indirect ICP assessment [8]. Subsequent studies further identified the prognostic value of ONSD for neurological outcomes in patients with cerebral infarction and intracerebral hemorrhage. Samantha et al. suggested that ONSD could serve as a promising alternative metric for ICP assessment in the ED, given the high patient turnover and fast-paced clinical environment [9]. However, their research described only 5 ED patients and lacked statistical analyses. Nevertheless, evidence suggests that, following standardized training, emergency nurses can reliably perform ONSD measurements, supporting its practicality in the ED setting [10]. Although the predictive value of ONSD for discharge neurological outcomes in patients with impaired consciousness without cerebral infarction or hemorrhage remains unclear, the feasibility of ONSD assessment and its close physiological association with ICP provide rationale for further investigation.

Monitoring ICP in these patients may be important for improving neurological outcomes. However, due to ethical concerns and procedural constraints, routine ICP measurement in the ED is difficult to implement. Owing to its simplicity and noninvasive nature, ONSD measurement is easier to perform than direct ICP monitoring in the ED and has been shown to correlate positively with ICP. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate ONSD measurements in patients with impaired consciousness in the ED, to evaluate their association with neurological outcomes, and to explore the feasibility and applicability of ONSD assessment in the ED setting.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective pilot study. Patients admitted to the ED with impaired consciousness at a tertiary hospital in Zhejiang Province in January 2025 were consecutively recruited. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; and (2) Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score < 15 or Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score ≠ 0. Exclusion criteria included: (1) cerebral infarction or a history of cerebral infarction; (2) any type of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) or a history of ICH; (3) ocular conditions that could interfere with ONSD measurement (e.g., ocular trauma, optic nerve tumors, glaucoma); (4) severe behavioral agitation precluding cooperation with ONSD measurement; (5) cervical injury or other conditions preventing tolerance of a 30° head-of-bed elevation; and (6) incomplete medical records. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical data

Demographic and clinical data, including height, weight, sex, age, heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, GCS score, and RASS score, were collected at patient admission. For patients without endotracheal intubation, the level of consciousness was assessed using the GCS. For intubated patients or those who had received sedation prior to arrival, the level of consciousness was assessed using the RASS at admission. Additional clinical data, including medical history, blood glucose, serum sodium, sedation status at admission, advanced airway management, length of stay (LOS), and discharge Cerebral Performance Category (CPC), were extracted from the electronic medical record system. CPC at hospital discharge was used as a short-term functional outcome measure because longer-term neurological follow-up was not uniformly available for this cohort. Patients were dichotomized into good neurological outcome (GNO, CPC ≤ 2) and poor neurological outcome (PNO, CPC > 2).

ONSD data

ONSD measurements were obtained within 30 min of patient arrival by two trained operators experienced in point-of-care ocular ultrasonography, both of whom were blinded to patients’ clinical information. Both operators were familiar with standardized ONSD measurement protocols and had prior experience with ocular point-of-care ultrasound examinations. Standardized measurement protocols were used to minimize inter-operator variability, which was further assessed using ICC. All ONSD measurements were performed in accordance with the Optic Nerve Sheath Diameter Point-of-Care Ultrasonography Quality Criteria Checklist (ONSD POCUS QCC) [29]. The mean of ONSD in each side was calculated by another researcher blinded to the patients’ conditions. Measurements were performed using an AIX8 ultrasound system equipped with a 7.5-MHz linear probe. The specific steps of ONSD measurements were as following: investigator lifted head-of-bed 30° and the transparent dressing was covered on eyelids before measurements. After applied sufficient ultrasound gel to the measurement area, the probe would be placed on the transparent dressing and avoid put pressure on eyeballs by supporting the hands on the patient’s forehead or cheek. For each eye, two measurements were obtained: one in the transverse plane and one in the sagittal plane, both measured 3 mm posterior to the retina (Fig. 1). The mean value of the transverse and sagittal measurements was calculated for each eye. Subsequently, the average of the measurements from the two operators was used as the final ONSD value for statistical analysis.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables that were normally distributed were presented as mean ± standard deviation. For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range were used. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. The Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, depending on the data distribution. The comparison of categorical variables was performed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with P < 0.1 were entered into binary logistic regression after collinearity diagnosis. Inter-observer reliability of ONSD measurements was evaluated using the ICC. ICC values were calculated based on a two-way mixed-effects model with a consistency definition to assess agreement between the two independent operators for transverse and sagittal measurements of both eyes. Considering that the sample size was estimated to be 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables, a maximum of three variables were included. The predictive value of ONSD for neurological outcome was then analyzed using binary logistic regression across per 1 mm, per 0.5 mm and per 0.1 mm scale after adjusting for age, gender, and BMI. In addition, a second multivariable model (Model 2) was constructed in which ONSD at the three different scales was entered together with blood glucose and serum sodium levels using the Enter method to further evaluate the robustness of the association. ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative ability of ONSD for discharge neurological outcome. The AUC was calculated, and the optimal cut-off value of ONSD was determined by maximizing the Youden index. Variables with a variance inflation factor > 10 and tolerance < 0.2 were considered to exhibit high multicollinearity. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 58 patients with impaired consciousness were included in this study (Fig. 2), of whom 42 (72.4%) were male, with a median age of 51.50(35.75, 58.75) years. Good neurological outcomes were observed in 20 patients (33.90%), while poor neurological outcomes were noted in 38 patients (65.52%). Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of ONSD, blood glucose levels, length of hospital stay, admitting department, and presence of advanced airway management (P < 0.05) (Table 1). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values demonstrated good agreement between the two operators for transverse and sagittal measurements of both eyes (ICC range: 0.790 ~ 0.861).

Considering that the number of positive samples should be 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables, ONSD, blood glucose, and the presence of advanced airway management were selected for collinearity analysis. The results showed that all variables had tolerance values > 0.2 and variance inflation factors < 0.5, indicating no collinearity between the variables (Table 2). In logistic regression adjusted for age, gender, and BMI, each 1 mm, 0.5 mm, and 0.1 mm increase in ONSD was associated with a 4.432-fold, 2.105-fold, and 1.161-fold increase in the probability of poor neurological outcomes. Discussion

Previous studies have shown that patients presenting to the emergency department have a high prevalence of poor CPC at hospital discharge. Elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) has been shown to be closely associated with poor CPC outcomes. However, due to the potential risks of harm and infection associated with ICP measurement, as well as ethical concerns, it is challenging to implement such measurements in non-stroke and non-intracerebral hemorrhage patients. Therefore, ONSD, which is easier to assess and closely correlated with ICP, has emerged as a clinically valuable surrogate in the emergency resuscitation setting. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between ONSD and discharge CPC in patients with impaired consciousness who do not have stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage in the emergency department. The results of this study showed that the ONSD and blood glucose levels in the good CPC group were significantly lower than those in the poor CPC group, while the length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the good CPC group. Additionally, significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of the number of advanced airway interventions. ONSD was significantly associated with discharge CPC after being adjusted for age, gender, and BMI. In addition, ONSD remained independently associated with discharge CPC across all three scaling approaches when included simultaneously in the logistic regression model using the Enter method.

Although several large-scale multicenter studies have demonstrated an association between elevated admission blood glucose levels and favorable Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) outcomes in cardiac arrest patients [11, 12], it should be emphasized that substantial pathophysiological distinctions exist between cardiac arrest populations and the cohort of patients with impaired consciousness examined in this investigation. Our findings suggest that higher admission glucose levels in patients with impaired consciousness may primarily reflect a more pronounced systemic inflammatory response and heightened stress state [13], rather than serving as a neuroprotective indicator as observed in cardiac arrest scenarios. In critically ill patients, elevated blood glucose levels are often associated with heightened sympathetic nervous system activity and the increased release of inflammatory mediators, which drive glucose utilization. This reflects the severity of the disease under stress-induced conditions [14, 15]. Michael et al. [16] noted that non-traumatic patients with elevated blood glucose levels at emergency department admission had an in-hospital mortality rate 2.5–2.9 times higher than the control group. This finding is consistent with the results of the present study, where patients in the poor CPC group had significantly higher blood glucose levels upon admission compared to those in the good CPC group. Additionally, our study also found a significant difference in the proportion of patients who required advanced airway management between the two groups. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that mechanical ventilation is associated with greater disease severity and a higher incidence of adverse outcomes [17, 18]. Generally, patients with worse prognoses tend to have longer hospital stays, but our study found the opposite. On the one hand, this may be attributable to the small sample size, which may limit the representativeness of the study population. On the other hand, patients in the poor CPC group may have had extremely poor baseline conditions, and many were advised to return to local hospitals for continued treatment after three days of admission.

The optic nerve sheath is contiguous with the intracranial subarachnoid space posterior to the globe, allowing communication with the intracranial compartment and exhibiting a positive correlation with ICP. Current research on the predictive value of ONSD for neurological functional outcomes has predominantly focused on survivors of cardiopulmonary resuscitation, cerebral infarction, and intracerebral hemorrhage patients, demonstrating varying degrees of prognostic utility [19–21], which is broadly consistent with the observations of the present study. Kendir et al. [22] highlighted that pediatric diabetic ketoacidosis-induced coma may concurrently elevate ICP and ONSD, and similar findings were corroborated by Nihan et al. [23] in 2021. Furthermore, Wang et al. [24] demonstrated in rabbit models of sepsis-associated encephalopathy that increased ONSD correlates with cerebral injury severity. Collectively, these findings suggest a potential pathophysiological link between metabolic disorder–induced coma and dynamic ICP fluctuations. In emergency settings, however, clinicians face challenges in performing routine ICP monitoring for all comatose patients due to its inherent risks (e.g., invasive procedures and technical complexity). Consequently, ONSD measurement has increasingly been explored as a noninvasive, bedside-accessible alternative in clinical practice. Recently, Yic et al. [25] provided a standardized and reproducible ultrasonographic protocol for ONSD measurement and further proposed a probabilistic approach to estimate intracranial hypertension using logistic regression models, thereby strengthening the methodological foundation for bedside ONSD assessment. In our study, receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated a moderate discriminative ability of ONSD for discharge neurological outcomes. Notably, the optimal cut-off value derived from our cohort (5.67 mm) was close to the threshold of 5.7 mm reported by Yic et al. [25], suggesting potential consistency across different clinical settings. Samantha et al. [9] further explored this paradigm in a small case series (n = 5), proposing ONSD as a potential surrogate marker for ICP estimation in critical care settings. Supporting this, Colak et al. [26] conducted a comparative study of 74 hepatic encephalopathy patients versus controls, revealing that the patient cohort exhibited significantly larger ONSD measurements (p < 0.01), thereby supporting its potential diagnostic relevance in metabolic encephalopathy.

While ONSD measurements exhibit substantial interindividual variability, with studies identifying age and BMI as key confounding factors [27, 28], our analysis showed that ONSD remained independently associated with discharge CPC after adjustment for age, sex, BMI. Despite the limited sample size, these findings suggest that: (1) ONSD may have potential utility as a noninvasive surrogate for ICP assessment in emergency settings; and (2) patients with non-cerebrovascular impaired consciousness (e.g., metabolic or infectious etiologies) may exhibit ICP alterations that are detectable by ONSD, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation using multimodal assessment approaches.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as a single-center prospective pilot study with a small sample size (n = 58), the findings may have limited generalizability to broader populations with impaired consciousness. Nevertheless, our data provide preliminary support for the potential clinical relevance of ONSD measurement in non-cerebrovascular impaired consciousness patients managed in the emergency department. Second, the restricted collection of laboratory parameters beyond serum sodium and blood glucose introduces uncertainty regarding potential residual confounding, as baseline characteristics may not be fully matched between groups across unmeasured variables (e.g., inflammatory markers or endocrine profiles). Finally, the limited sample size required the use of parsimonious multivariable logistic regression models, thereby precluding the inclusion of sedation-related consciousness assessments and limiting our ability to formally evaluate their interaction with discharge CPC in this cohort. Although the cut-off value observed in our cohort was consistent with prior reports, it should be interpreted cautiously and requires external validation in larger, independent populations. Larger-scale studies incorporating comprehensive biomarker panels, advanced multivariate analyses, and standardized medium- and long-term neurological follow-up are required to delineate ONSD’s prognostic capacity. In particular, the prognostic value of ONSD beyond short-term outcomes warrants further investigation, as medium- and long-term neurological outcomes are influenced by numerous intervening factors after initial emergency department assessment.

Conclusion

Our study found the potential association between the admission ONSD and discharge CPC in patients with impaired consciousness in the emergency department who do not have cerebral infarction or hemorrhage. We further suggest that ONSD measurement may have potential clinical applicability in the emergency department setting. However, larger multicenter studies are needed to further clarify the prognostic value and underlying mechanisms of ONSD in relation to CPC.
