Mitochondria-targeted CORM-401 nanoparticles inhibit A549 cells by triggering cuproptosis
Authors: Li Shao, Zhicheng Song, Xiaoli Gong, Jianjun Ma, Shujing Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn this study, mitochondrial-targeted nanoparticles containing CORM-401 were designed by using TPP and HA (named as CORM-TPP@HA). The effects of these nanoparticles on cuproptosis in A549 cells were observed to confirm their function.MethodsFirst, CORM-TPP micelles were formed by using CORM-401 and TPP-SH. Then, the micelles were encapsulated with HA to form nanoparticles (CORM-TPP@HA). The morphology, size distribution, stability, and release of CO from nanoparticles were analyzed to confirm their characterization. Meanwhile, the cellular uptake of nanoparticles was observed using a confocal microscope. The effects of the nanoparticles on cell viability, mitochondrial ROS, intracellular copper, and cuproptosis-related proteins in A549 cells were measured. Additionally, the impacts of nanoparticles on tumor growth and cuproptosis-related factors in tumor tissue were measured in vivo.ResultsThe designed nanoparticles maintained stable formation, effectively preventing premature CO release from CORM-401. Furthermore, the nanoparticles were able to be taken up by A549 cells. Moreover, treatment with the designed nanoparticles induced cuproptosis by increasing mitochondrial ROS, intracellular copper, and cuproptosis-related proteins in A549 cells. In line with the in vitro results, treatment with nanoparticles suppressed tumor growth and elevated the cuproptosis-related proteins in tumor tissues.ConclusionThe synthesized CORM-TPP@HA nanoparticles suppressed A549 cells via regulating mitochondrial cuproptosis, providing a signaling mechanism of CO in NSCLC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01149-7.Keywords: Carbon monoxide, Cuproptosis, Nanoparticles, CORM-401, NSCLC

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is one of the most common cancers in the world, accounting for 80–85% of all lung cancers [1]. Various treatments have been applied to NSCLC, including surgery, radio-chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted approaches. Meanwhile, gaseous molecules, such as nitric oxide (NO) and carbon monoxide (CO), are gaining growing interest as signaling molecules. They show promise as target molecules for the treatment of various diseases, including cancers, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related diseases, and inflammatory diseases [2–4].

CO is a relatively stable molecule that primarily targets metals and metalloproteins [5]. Due to its high affinity for heme-iron, CO hinders the transport of physiological gases in the bloodstream. Moreover, by binding to cytochrome c oxidase and cytochrome P450, CO inhibits cellular respiration, eventually leading to tissue hypoxia and cell death [6]. Therefore, the CO-based therapy is a promising strategy for cancer. Carbon monoxide-releasing molecules (CORMs) are efficient CO donors that can stably store and subsequently release their CO payload in response to specific triggers, such as solvent, light, temperature, and ligand substitution [7]. In fact, CORMs have been reported to have therapeutic potential in different types of cancers, including breast cancer [8], prostate cancer [9], and NSCLC [10]. As a stable, water-soluble CORM, CORM-401 has been successfully used to release CO in tumor tissues through multifunctional nanocarriers, thereby reducing transition-metal toxicity, prolonging in vivo circulation time, and achieving localized release [11, 12]. However, exogenous nanodrugs are easily identified and cleared by mononuclear phagocytes, which limits their effectiveness in cancer treatment. Therefore, achieving tumor-targeted delivery of CO prodrugs and controlled release of CO within tumors by multifunctional nanomaterials is an important strategy.

Lipophilic ligand triphenylphosphonium (TPP) has been used to construct a system for delivering nano-prodrugs into the mitochondria of cancer cells [12–14]. Meanwhile, the hyaluronic acid (HA) protective shell, which can be degraded by the high-abundance hyaluronidase in tumors, has been proven to be an effective way to improve the efficiency of mitochondria-targeted drug delivery [12]. Cuproptosis is a type of metabolic cell death that mainly relies on accumulating intracellular copper and facilitates the aggregation of lipoylated mitochondrial enzymes, demonstrating significant potential for cancer treatment [15–17]. In the field of lung cancer, numerous studies indicate that lung cancer cells with increased copper levels are more sensitive to cuproptosis, presenting a potential therapeutic target [18, 19]. In this study, we designed mitochondrial-targeted nanoparticles containing CORM-401 by using TPP and HA (named as CORM-TPP@HA). We hypothesized that, after the digestion of the outer HA shell, the nanoparticles would accumulate in the mitochondria of NSCLC cells under the action of exposed TPP and induce cell cuproptosis.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of CORM-TPP@HA

First, TPP-SH was synthesized by an esterification reaction between TPP-COOH and H2N-EtSH (H2N-C2H4-SH, cysteamine). In brief, TPP-COOH (1 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous DMF (10 mL), purged with argon for 10 min. Under argon and dark conditions, the solution was cooled to 0 °C, and then incubated with EDC·HCl (1.2 mmol), NHS (1.2 mmol), and DIPEA (2 mmol) for 30 min. Subsequently, deoxygenated cysteamine (1.5 mmol) was added dropwise, stirred under argon in the dark for 16 h at room temperature. DMF was removed by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure. The residue was dissolved in anhydrous DCM (20 mL), washed with saturated NaHCO₃ solution and brine (3× each). It was then dried over anhydrous Na₂SO₄ and concentrated to obtain the crude product. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography on high-purity silica gel using gradient elution (DCM/MeOH = 10:1 → 5:1). Target fractions were collected and concentrated to afford TPP-SH as a white solid.

Second, the TPP-SH conjugated with CORM-401 to form CORM-TPP in an equimolar ratio by the ligand-exchange response [20]. In brief, A 2 mM anhydrous ethanol solution of TPP-SH was slowly added drop-by-drop to an equal-volume 2 mM anhydrous ethanol solution of CORM-401. The resultant mixture was stirred under an argon atmosphere in the dark at room temperature for 2 h to complete the Mo-S covalent coordination and generate the oligomeric precursor. Subsequently, three times the volume of sterile, deoxygenated ultrapure water was added dropwise, and the mixture was left to stand at room temperature for 1 h. This led to spontaneous assembly, forming a homogeneous CORM-TPP nano-suspension. The suspension was then dialyzed against PBS solution in the dark for 24 h using a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 3500 Da. The PBS solution was replaced every 6 h to remove residual ethanol and unreacted small molecules. After centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C) and washing with water, the CORM-TPP micelles were collected and dispersed in water. Finally, 3 mg of HA dissolved in water was added to the above-mentioned micelle solution and mixed overnight at 37 °C.

Characterization of nanoparticles

The morphology of the nanoparticles was observed under a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Tecnai G2 11, FEI, USA). Their size distribution, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were analyzed using an electrophoretic light scattering analyzer (Model ELSZ-2000ZS, Otsuka, Japan). The spectral analysis of nanoparticles was carried out using an FTIR spectral analyzer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 FTIR Spectrometer).

The stability of nanoparticles was observed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solutions (pH 7.4 and 6.8) at 37 °C over 7 days using DLS. Meanwhile, the stability of nanoparticles in serum at 37 °C was observed over 7 days. The released CO concentration under H2O2 (5 µM) was monitored over time using a TRIlyzer mBA-3000 instrument (Taiyo, Japan) [21, 22].

Cell culture

STR-identified A549 cells (#CL-0016, Pricella, China) were cultured in Ham’s F-12 K medium containing 10% FBS and 1%P/S at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The frequency of medium replacement is 2–3 times per week. The cells were harvested to perform experiments when the density attained 70–80%.

Evaluation of cellular uptake of the nanoparticles

The nanoparticles were resuspended in PBS and labeled with Alexa Fluor 647(#P0180, Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 4 °C. In brief, 10 mg nanoparticles were resuspended in 100 mL PBS, and incubated with 1 mL 3-Aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) for 6 h at 60 °C. Secondly, the solutions were centrifuged (8000 rpm, 15 min) and washed three times with PBS. After vacuum drying, the nanoparticles were resuspended in PBS buffer, and the concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL. The amino-modified nanoparticle solution was incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 solution at a ratio ranging from 1:10 to 1:20 for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, with the pH maintained at 8.3–8.5. Then, the mixture was transferred into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 10 kDa. It was then immersed in 500 mL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4) for 24 h at 4 °C in the dark. The buffer was replaced every 6 h to remove unbound AF647.

A549 cells were labeled with PKH67 (#Mini67, Sigma) for 15 min at 37 °C. In brief, the trypsinized cells were washed twice with PBS and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with diluted PKH67 solution for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. Complete medium supplemented with FBS was added and mixed for 1 min to neutralize unbound dyes and terminate the staining procedure. After washing three times, the labeled cells were cultured with Alexa Fluor 647-labeled nanoparticles for 6 h and 24 h. After washing, cells were harvested and observed under a confocal microscope (#LSM800, Zeiss, Germany). The fluorescence signal was analyzed using fluorescence spectrometry (ThermoFisher Scientific, China) to calculate the proportion of exosomes taken up by cells.

Cell treatment

The A549 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated in fresh medium containing different doses of nanoparticles (5, 10, 20, 40 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 24 h.

Cell viability

Cell viability was measured using a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, #C0038, Beyotime, China). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates. The cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were incubated with 10 µL CCK-8 solution for 2 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Labsystems Inc., Pennsylvania, USA).

Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Mitochondrial ROS were measured using a MitoSOX Red kit (#HY-D1055, MedChemExpress, China). In brief, the cells (5 × 104) were incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX Red solution for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C. After centrifugation (800 ×g, 10 min) and washing twice with PBS, the cells were resuspended in 2 mL PBS and observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Cuproptosis-related proteins

Protein expression was measured by western blotting [23]. Cell proteins were extracted using a RIPA lysis buffer, separated by gel electrophoresis, transferred to membranes, and blocked with 5% milk for 1.5 h at 37 °C. The blocked membranes were incubated with specific antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBST twice, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody at 37 °C for 120 min. The details regarding the antibodies are presented in Table 1. After washing with PBST twice, the blots were visualized using a chemiluminescence reagent kit. The gray values of blots were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Intracellular copper detection

Intracellular copper was measured by CuprosGreen working solution (#C557, Dojindo, China). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates. The cells (2 × 104/well) were washed twice with serum-free medium, and then incubated with medium containing 200 µmol/L CuCl2 for 15 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After washing with PBS containing 200 µmol/L EDTA-2Na two times, the cells were incubated with 5 µmol/L CuprosGreen working solution for 3 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The results were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Animals

Twenty-four BALB/c nude mice (a 1:1 sex ratio), 4–6 weeks old and weighing 12–18 g, were purchased from Shandong Pengyue Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Jinan, China), and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water for 7 days.

Animal treatment

The mice were administered with 100 µL of PBS containing 5 × 10⁵ A549 cells into the flank. When the tumor reached 50 mm³, the mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 6): control group, 5 µg/g nanoparticles group, 10 µg/g nanoparticles group, and 20 µg/g nanoparticles group. In the control group, mice were injected with 100 µL of PBS twice a week via the tail vein. In the nanoparticle groups, mice were injected with different doses of nanoparticles resuspended in 100 µL PBS twice a week via the tail vein. Body weight was measured twice a week.

Sample collection

Tumor volumes, calculated as [(length × width2)/2], were measured weekly. At the end of the 4 weeks, the mice were sacrificed by injecting 100 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium. Subsequently, the tumors were excised, weighed, and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

Immunohistochemistry

According to the reported immunohistochemistry procedure [24], the tumor Sect. (5 μm) were deparaffinized with xylene twice, hydrated with gradient ethanol (100%, 90%, 85%, 80%), subjected to antigen retrieval with Proteinase K for 6 min and 3% H2O2 for 15 min at 37 °C, and then incubated with specific antibodies (Table 1) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS three times, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody for 90 min at 37 °C. After washing with PBS twice, the sections were stained with diaminobenzidine solution and hematoxylin solution for 30s at room temperature. Finally, the sections were dehydrated with xylene twice, cleared with gradient ethanol (75%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100%), and mounted with neutral gum. The images were collected using an optical microscope (#BX53M, Olympus, China), and the positive expression was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normal distribution, and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance, followed by post-hoc Tukey’s test, was used to compare significance among multiple groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of nanoparticles

Both types of nanoparticles, CORM-TPP and CORM-TPP@HA, exhibited a spherical shape (Fig. 1A). The diameter of CORM-TPP nanoparticles ranged from 100 to 400 nm, while that of CORM-TPP@HA nanoparticles ranged from 150 nm to 500 nm (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, the PDI and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were analyzed, and the results showed that there was no significant difference in the PDI, while the zeta potential of CORM-TPP@HA nanoparticles declined significantly compared with that of CORM-TPP nanoparticles (Fig. 1C). The results of FTIR (Fig. 1D) showed that the CORM-TPP@HA nanoparticles exhibited distinct absorptions at 1650 cm⁻¹ (amide I), 1610 cm⁻¹ (-COO⁻), and 1550 cm⁻¹ (amide II), which are characteristic peaks of HA. The C ≡ O stretching vibration in the range of 2100 –2000 cm⁻¹ (characteristic of CORM) and the characteristic peak of benzene ring monosubstitution in the range of 750 –690 cm⁻¹ (fingerprint region of TPP) remained intact, indicating that the HA coating did not disrupt the core structure of CORM-TPP. Furthermore, the size stability of nanoparticles was measured in PBS solutions (pH 7.4 and 6.8) and in serum for 7 days (Fig. 1E), and the results showed that nanoparticles showed good stability in a physiological environment. The released CO concentration from the nanoparticles under H2O2 was measured (Fig. 1F), and a gradual increase in CO levels was observed over time. These results suggested that nanoparticles maintain stable nanoparticle formation, effectively protecting CORM-401 from premature CO release.

Cellular uptake of nanoparticles

In cellular uptake experiments, nanoparticles were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 (red) and cultured with A549 cells that were labeled with PKH67 (green). From the confocal fluorescence images (Fig. 2A), cellular uptake of nanoparticles was observed after 6 h and further augmented after 24 h. The uptake ratio was analyzed (Fig. 2B), and the results showed that the nanoparticles were clearly taken up by cells over time (P < 0.05).

Nanoparticles suppressed A549 cell viability and elevated mitochondrial ROS

To evaluate the effects of nanoparticles on A549 cell viability (Fig. 3A) and mitochondrial ROS (Fig. 3B and C), A549 cells were treated with a series of nanoparticles (5, 10, 20, 40 µg/mL). The results showed that the nanoparticles significantly suppressed cell viability and elevated mitochondrial ROS in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.05).

Nanoparticles elevated intracellular copper and cuproptosis-related proteins

Furthermore, cuproptosis-related proteins (ATOX1, ATP7A, and COX17) were measured to analyze the effects of nanoparticles on cuproptosis in A549 cells. The results of western blotting were shown in Fig. 4A, and the relative expressions of the above proteins were significantly elevated with the increasing dose of nanoparticles (Fig. 4B and D, P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the levels of copper in cells were observed by CuprosGreen (Fig. 4E). The levels of copper were clearly upregulated with the increasing dose of nanoparticles (P < 0.05).

Nanoparticles suppressed tumor growth

To evaluate the effects of nanoparticles in vivo, a xenograft model in BALB/c nude mice was established by injection of A549 cells. The xenograft models were respectively injected with a series of nanoparticles (5, 10, 20 µg/g). The body weight of control mice remained stagnant, whereas treatment with nanoparticles led to an increase in body weight as the doses increased (Fig. 5A). The tumor growth curve (Fig. 5B), tumor images (Fig. 5C), tumor weight (Fig. 5D), and Ki-67 expression (Fig. 5E) were measured. The results showed that treatment with nanoparticles significantly suppressed tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner by reducing both tumor size and tumor weight (P < 0.05). The treatment with nanoparticles also significantly reduced the expression of Ki-67 in tumor tissues compared with the control group (P < 0.05), suggesting that nanoparticle treatment inhibited the proliferation of tumor cells, thereby suppressing tumor growth.

Discussion

Although it has been reported that SMA/CORM2 inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer cells [25], few studies have documented the therapeutic efficacy of nanoparticles in NSCLC, with respect to cuproptosis. By leveraging multifunctional nanomaterials, the tumor-targeted delivery of CORMs via intelligent nano-medicines is a crucial strategy [26]. In this study, the synthesized nanoparticles could induce mitochondrial cuproptosis by releasing CO in A549 cells.

The positively charged TPP ion has been well-validated as an excellent mitochondria-targeting moiety due to its electrostatic and hydrophobic attractions to the mitochondrial membrane [27]. In this study, the sulfur atom (S) of TPP-SH, acting as a strong nucleophile and soft base, attacks the axial nitrogen-containing ligand in CORM-401, resulting in an axial ligand-exchange reaction. It has been reported that in the presence of ROS, CORM-401 can rapidly release CO [28]. The released CO enhances the second phase of mitochondrial respiration in tumor cells, thus leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, the generation of ROS, and ultimately resulting in tumor cell apoptosis [11]. In this study, we utilized the TPP and HA as the protective shell to enhance the efficiency of drug delivery.

Cell apoptosis is regulated by multiple genes and enzymes, with the activation of cysteine-aspartic acid proteases (caspases) at its core. Its pathways are mainly divided into the extrinsic and intrinsic [29]. The extrinsic pathway is initiated by the binding of death receptors to ligands. In the intrinsic pathway, changes in mitochondrial permeability lead to the release of cytochrome C, which in turn activates the caspase cascade reaction, ultimately resulting in apoptotic characteristic changes such as nuclear DNA fragmentation. Cuproptosis is independent of caspase activation, with mitochondrial metabolic disorder at its core. On one hand, an excess of copper ions binds to lipoylated proteins in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, inducing abnormal protein aggregation and triggering cytotoxicity. On the other hand, ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) reduces Cu²⁺ to the more toxic Cu⁺, inhibiting the synthesis of iron-sulfur cluster proteins and disrupting the mitochondrial respiratory chain [30]. Mitochondria are the primary cellular targets of CO, as the heme proteins in mitochondria readily bind to CO [31]. CO inhibits the activity of cytochrome c oxidase (COX) in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, interfering with the process of oxidative phosphorylation and rendering mitochondria unable to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) normally. Meanwhile, after CO binds to COX, electrons accumulate at complex III, thereby inducing mitochondria to generate ROS. Therefore, the CO-induced mitochondrial dysfunction is closely associated with the cuproptosis.

In research on lung cancer [32], researchers have noticed that the serum copper ion levels in tumor patients are significantly higher than those in normal patients. Moreover, lung cancer patients with a poorer clinical stage have higher serum copper ion concentrations, which are also associated with a poorer clinical prognosis. ATPases, including ATP7A and ATP7B, are related to the extracellular excretion of copper. In the presence of ATP, ATPases export copper bound to metal-binding sites [33]. Specifically, the cytoplasmic copper-ion chaperones ATOX1 bind copper via two cysteine residues and transport it to the metal-binding sites of ATP7B for further export [30]. Additionally, the copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS) directly interacts with copper ions and transports them to superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) [30]. Furthermore, the cytochrome c oxidase copper chaperone (COX17) carries copper ions from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial intermembrane space and further delivers copper to the cysteine residues of cytochrome c oxidase 1 during its synthesis, forming disulfide bonds with them [30, 33]. This study demonstrated that the designed nanoparticles increased the mitochondrial ROS, intracellular copper, and the expressions of ATP7A, ATOX1, and COX17 in A549 cells, thereby leading to cell death. However, the apparent band sizes (ATOX1 and COX17) shown in the study do not match this expected molecular weight (approximately 7 kDa). The most common and primary reason might be that its inherent physicochemical properties (such as high isoelectric point (pI) and compact structure) lead to abnormal SDS binding and mobility. Alternatively, the binding with metal ions (copper) significantly alters its behavior in the gel.

However, there is a great deal of work remaining to be accomplished regarding the application of nanoparticles in NSCLC. Specifically, the safety of nanoparticles will be the focus of subsequent-stage experiments. It is necessary to continuously monitor the systemic toxicity of nanoparticles in the body, particularly in organs such as the heart, liver, and kidneys. Furthermore, further investigation into the effects of nanoparticles on normal cell lines is required. This will contribute to clarifying the core advantages of the nanoparticle system, specifically selective targeting and therapeutic safety. Moreover, the combined application of nanoparticles with other chemotherapeutic drugs requires studying their therapeutic effects in NSCLC.

Conclusion

In this study, CORM-TPP@HA nanoparticles were synthesized, and it was demonstrated that these nanoparticles inhibited the proliferation of A549 cells via regulating mitochondrial cuproptosis. This finding provides a signaling mechanism of CO in the NSCLC treatment.
