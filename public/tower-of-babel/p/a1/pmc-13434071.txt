Relationships between functional performance, body composition, and biochemical markers of inflammation in young men who play team sports
Authors: Patrycja Widłak, Marzena Malara, Anna Kuk, Anna Kopiczko, Piotr Bartnicki, Aspasia Dania
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMovement quality and postural control are crucial for performance and injury prevention in team-sport athletes. Although FMS and YBT are commonly used, their ability to predict injury risk is limited when used alone. Therefore, integrating functional assessment with body composition and biochemical markers may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of athletes’ functional status. The study aimed to investigate the relationships among functional performance, body composition, and chosen biochemical markers in young men’s team-sport athletes. The study included 48 young Polish professional athletes competing in team sports (football, futsal, handball, volleyball, rugby), who were divided into a lower-limb-dominant group (group 1, n = 34) and an upper-limb-dominant group (group 2, n = 14). Body composition, functional performance (FMS, Y Balance Test), training characteristics, and blood biomarkers for stress, muscle damage, and inflammation were assessed. Selected anthropometric, functional, and biochemical parameters were compared between the groups. Significant differences were observed mainly in body composition—group 2 had a higher BMI, percentage of body fat, lean body mass, and total body water content (p ≤ 0.05; Hedges’ coefficient g = 0.64–0.89). Training experience was significantly longer in group 1, whilst no differences were found in age or height. Functional performance assessed using the FMS and YBT did not differ significantly between the groups. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in biochemical markers, including cortisol, CK, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β. ANCOVA analysis showed that body weight had a significant effect on cortisol levels (F = 6.22, p = 0.02) and CRP (F = 58.45, p < 0.001), whilst training experience significantly affected CK activity (F = 4.63, p = 0.04). Despite differences in body composition between the groups, functional movement quality, dynamic balance, and biochemical markers were comparable. However, body weight and training experience significantly influenced selected biochemical indicators, suggesting their role in shaping physiological responses to training.Keywords: Functional movement, Team sports, Body composition, Biochemical markers, Injury preventionSubject terms: Biomarkers, Health care, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

The quality of basic movement patterns and postural control are crucial for the functional fitness of team sports players. Correct movement patterns enable effective force transfer, joint stabilisation, and reduce the risk of overload resulting from repetitive and asymmetrical training loads1,2. Disorders revealed by functional tests, such as the Functional Movement Screen (FMS) or the Y Balance Test (YBT), may reflect limitations in range of motion, core stability deficits, and lower-limb asymmetries, potentially affecting movement quality and training safety3–5.

As the above parameters are considered key to playing team sports, modern coaching approaches aim to incorporate game-based training methods to enable players to practise in conditions that foster the development of tactical awareness based on their individual sporting abilities6,7. The main objective is to improve players’ performance in matches by ensuring the quality of their movements and preventing injuries.

Despite the widespread use of functional tests, their predictive value for injuries in isolation remains a subject of debate. Interpreting results without considering additional factors such as body composition, training loads, or the overall condition of the body resulting from lifestyle can lead to oversimplified conclusions. Therefore, there is a growing need for a holistic approach in which functional assessment takes into account both the motor skills and physiological conditions of the athlete. Tests such as FMS and YBT, although easy to use and widely used, show high variability in sensitivity and specificity in predicting injuries in different groups of athletes. Meta-analysis findings suggest that multifactorial models that integrate both modifiable factors (e.g., training load, physical fitness) and non-modifiable factors (e.g., injury history, age, gender) are more effective in predicting injury risk8–10 .

Body composition (BIA), including body fat, lean body mass, and visceral fat, can significantly modify movement quality and dynamic stability. Unfavorable Fat-Free Mass (FFM) and Fat Mass (FM) ratios can affect movement mechanics, increase the load on musculoskeletal structures, and limit stabilization abilities, even in competitive athletes11,12. At the same time, insufficient energy supply negatively affects regeneration and adaptation processes, increases the risk of injuries and diseases, and, as a consequence, may lead to a decrease in athletic performance. Low energy availability (LEA) tends to correlate with, among other things, reduced bone mineral density and decreased resting metabolic rate, which may further impair the functioning of the musculoskeletal system and training safety. More and more studies point to links between body composition and functional test results, but these relationships still require further clarification13–16.

In addition, biochemical markers reflecting training load, fatigue, and inflammatory processes may affect neuromuscular control and recovery capacity. Elevated concentrations of creatine kinase (CK), cortisol, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., Interleukin-6, TNF-α) may co-occur with reduced functional performance and an increased risk of overload, constituting a ‘hidden’ component of functionality. Monitoring these markers may be useful in the early detection of excessive training load and chronic fatigue before symptoms of decreased performance appear17,18. High concentrations of creatine kinase (CK) and pro-inflammatory cytokines correlate with impaired muscle regeneration and impaired motor coordination, which may increase the risk of overload injuries19.

Furthermore, hormonal changes, such as chronically elevated cortisol levels, are associated with a decline in muscle mass and neuromuscular function, which affects athletic performance and training adaptation20. For this reason, regular monitoring of biochemical markers of stress and inflammatory processes may be a key element in preventing injuries and optimising recovery processes in athletes. It may also provide coaches with information enabling them to adjust training intensity and demands so that athletes’ tactical and technical development does not come at the expense of their functional stability and recovery.

The above aspects provide grounds for the validity of a comprehensive analysis of team sports players through the simultaneous assessment of movement patterns, dynamic balance, body composition, training loads, and chosen biochemical markers. This approach will allow us to determine how physiological and biomarkers of stress, muscle damage, and inflammation may correlate with functional deficits and affect functional status and the risk of overload in the population of young athletes. Furthermore, it could support the development of targeted coaching interventions related to players’ functional performance and injury prevention.

Material and methods

Subjects

The study involved 48 young Polish men who were professional team-sport players in football, futsal, handball, volleyball, and rugby. Athletes were stratified into two dominance groups: Group 1—lower-limb dominance and Group 2—upper-limb dominance according to the predominant use and functional capacity of the respective body segments.

In this study, ‘limb dominance’ was operationally defined as a functional advantage resulting from the specific motor demands of a given discipline, rather than as classical lateral dominance (e.g., handedness or footedness). This classification reflects differences in dominant movement patterns associated with the nature of training loads and sporting competition (e.g., the predominance of locomotor activities in football and futsal versus a greater proportion of upper limb activity in handball, volleyball, and rugby). It should be treated as a simplified indicator of the specific nature of sports loads, rather than a measure of individual motor lateralisation.

Demographic and training data were collected from the subjects; body composition was analyzed; blood was collected for biochemical analysis; and functional tests were conducted to assess musculoskeletal function (Table 1).

The criteria for inclusion in the study were: regular participation in the training process, a specified training period, and no acute injury at the time of measurement. All subjects gave their written consent to participate in the study.

Athletes with current severe pain or acute musculoskeletal injury and individuals unable to complete the full study procedure in accordance with the applicable methodology were excluded from the study. Refusal to participate in at least one part of the study and refusal to provide written consent to participate in the study were also exclusion criteria.

Body composition assessment

The analysis of the body composition of the subjects was performed in accordance with the methodology by an experienced researcher. The bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) method was used, employing a Tanita BC-418 segmental analyser (Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan), a professional clinical device that provides multi-segment body composition data via an eight-electrode system that measures body weight and impedance and estimates regional and total body composition parameters.

The study collected parameters determining Body Mass Index (BMI), Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), percentage and absolute fat content (FM%, FM), FFM, Total Body Water (TBW), and Visceral Fat Rating (VFR). VFR is a proprietary index used by Tanita devices to estimate the amount of fat stored within the abdominal cavity around the internal organs. It is derived from the body composition data along with subject characteristics such as height, age, and sex. The VFR scale ranges from 1 to 59, with each incremental unit approximately corresponding to an additional 10 cm2 of visceral fat area, as referenced against Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) imaging “gold standard” estimates. According to the manufacturer, a VFR core of 1–12 is considered within a healthy range, whereas 13–59 indicates an excessive level of visceral fat, associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk21

Characteristics of training loads and functional assessment

Training loads were characterized based on training experience, the number of training units completed per week, and the duration of a single training unit. Only training experience showed a significant difference between the athletes studied. The other two parameters were so similar that they were not included in further analyses. The athletes trained on average 3–4 times a week, and each training session lasted approximately 1–1.5 h. In addition, the athletes were classified according to their sport and category.

The quality of movement patterns was assessed using the Functional Movement Screen (FMS), which was designed to assess the overall stability, mobility, and neuromuscular coordination of the subjects. The test was conducted using a professional FMS testing kit from Perform Better (Perform Better, Cranston, USA). The study was carried out in accordance with the methodology, and each participant took the test without prior exercise or warm-up and performed each test three times under the supervision of a trained researcher. The attempts were assessed in the frontal and sagittal planes. Each participant was required to perform seven components of the test: Deep squat, Hurdle step, In-line lunge, Shoulder mobility, Active straight leg raise (ASLR), Trunk stability push-up, and Trunk rotation stability. In each component, 3 points were awarded for a correctly performed task, 2 points for a partially correctly performed task, 1 point for a task not performed, and 0 points if pain occurred during the trial. The maximum number of points possible was 21. According to the test methodology, a score of 14 points was considered the threshold indicating a high risk of sports injuries3,4.

Dynamic balance assessment

Dynamic balance and lower limb symmetry were assessed using the Y-Balance test. This is a tool used to assess dynamic postural control, stability, and functional symmetry of the lower limbs. The test was conducted using a certified FMS assessment kit from Perform Better (Perform Better, Cranston, USA). The tests were performed in accordance with the applicable procedure, and the participants underwent the test without prior warm-up or preparatory exercises. Each task was performed three times under the supervision of a properly trained researcher, with each subject performing a maximum reach in three directions: anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral, moving the measuring indicator without shifting their body weight.

Based on the values obtained, using dedicated formulas, normalized results for each direction and a composite score were calculated5.

These parameters were used to assess physiological stress, muscle damage, and the body’s inflammatory response.

A basic standardisation procedure was applied to all athletes, involving a morning blood sample taken on an empty stomach. However, it was not possible to fully control all factors that could influence the biochemical response, such as sleep quality, specific training loads over the previous 24–48 h, or individual recovery strategies. All athletes were at the same stage of the training season, specifically during the early preparatory phase (pre-season mesocycle).

Statistical analysis

The research results were analysed with the use of Statistica software (v.13, Stat. Soft., USA). In order to investigate the nature of the distribution of the results, the Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted. In case of rejecting the assumptions of normality of the assessment distribution of the significance of diversity, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test. In order to determine the significance of differences between the values of particular variables for Group 1—lower-limb dominance and Group 2—upper-limb dominance, Student’s t-test for independent variables was applied. The size of the difference between the results of the groups was calculated using the ‘Hedges G’ formula (small effect: < 0.5; medium effect: 0.5–0.8; large effect: > 0.8). The chi-square test (χ2) was used to assess the prevalence of categories of somatic variables, Functional Movement Screen (FMS) classification, and biochemical markers in the two study groups. Cramer’s V coefficient was used to determine the effect size of the chi-square test (small effect: 0.1; average effect: 0.3; large effect: 0.5) while the phi (Φ) coefficient was used as the effect size measure for 2 × 2 tables. Relationships between blood biomarkers and somatic variables, sport variables, and functional test parameters were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), based on Ronald A. Fisher’s F test. The models included selected somatic characteristics, sport-related variables (e.g., training experience), and functional test parameters as independent variables, while inflammatory biomarkers and CK level were treated as dependent variables. For each model, F statistics, corresponding p values, and effect sizes expressed as partial η2 were reported. The overall significance of each model was evaluated using the F statistic, and model fit was assessed based on adjusted coefficients of determination (adjusted R2). Low adjusted R2 values were interpreted as indicating limited explanatory power of the analysed predictors. The effect size was calculated as eta square (η2) (small effect: < 0.06; average effect: 0.06–0.14; large effect: > 0.14)22. In all analyses, levels of significance were: (p < 0 0.05).

Results

Table 1 presents a comparison of selected anthropometric variables, body composition, functional movement quality, balance and blood biomarkers between athletes participating in team sports with lower limb dominance (Group 1) and team sports with upper limb dominance (Group 2). Significant differences between the groups mainly concerned body composition parameters and metabolic indicators. Athletes from Group 2 had significantly higher body mass index (BMI) values, percentage body fat (FM%), fat-free mass (FFM) and total body water (TBW) compared to Group 1 (p ≤ 0.05), with moderate to large effect sizes (Hedges’ g = 0.64–0.89). The training duration was significantly longer in Group 1. For age, the result was at the threshold of statistical significance (p = 0.051), whereas there was no significant difference in height between the groups. Basal metabolic rate (BMR) was higher in Group 2, but this difference was a trend and did not reach statistical significance. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups in the total Functional Movement Screen (FMS) score or in any of its components, indicating comparable quality of basic movement patterns. Similarly, the results of the Y Balance Test, both in terms of the total score and the range of motion in the anterior direction for the left and right limbs, did not differ significantly between the groups, suggesting a similar level of dynamic postural control regardless of limb dominance. Analysis of blood biomarkers showed no significant differences between groups in terms of cortisol, CK, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α or TGF-β concentrations. The effect sizes for biochemical variables were small, indicating no clinically significant differences in markers of stress, inflammation and muscle damage between the analyzed sports categories.

Table 2 presents the prevalence of categories of somatic variables, Functional Movement Screen (FMS) classification, and biochemical markers in two investigated groups. No significant differences were observed between Group 1 and Group 2 in the frequency of the categories of variables analyzed in this study, except for BMI. Significant differences were observed in the frequency of BMI classification. In group 1, a significantly higher percentage had a normal BMI, while in group 2, a higher percentage was overweight or obese. In terms of FM percentage, values within the normal range predominated, with only a few participants achieving low or high results. Analysis of the FMS classification showed that low and medium risk of injury prevailed in Group 1, while in Group 2, most participants achieved a score indicating low risk. The biochemical markers examined, including cortisol, CK, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β, did not show significant differences between the groups, and the values were mostly within the normal range or did not indicate active inflammation. In summary, despite some differences in body composition and functional outcomes, no statistically significant differences in the analysed parameters were found between the prevalence of categories of somatic variables, FMS classification, and biochemical markers in the study groups.

Table 3 presents the results of the ANCOVA models examining the relationships between selected variables and biochemical markers. Body weight showed a significant effect in the ANCOVA models for both cortisol (F = 6.222, p = 0.02, partial η2 = 0.14) and CRP concentrations (F = 58.450, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.06). Training experience significantly differentiated CK activity (F = 4.632, p = 0.04, partial η2 = 0.11). For CRP, the overall model was also statistically significant (F = 13.688, p < 0.001) and demonstrated a high goodness of fit (adjusted R2 = 0.76), indicating a strong combined influence of the analysed variables on this inflammatory marker. In contrast, no significant effects were observed for IL-6, TNF-α, or TGF-β, and the coefficients of determination for these models were low, suggesting limited explanatory power.

Discussion

The study sought to examine the associations between functional performance, body composition, and biochemical markers of stress, muscle damage and inflammation in young male team-sport athletes. The results indicate that significant differences were mainly observed in body composition parameters, while no significant differences were observed in movement quality, dynamic balance or investigated biomarkers.

Body composition and training adaptations.

Athletes with upper limb dominance had higher BMI, FM%, FFM, and TBW values. These differences may result from the specific training loads and biomechanical requirements of individual disciplines. It is known that body composition is an important factor determining exercise capacity and injury risk5,11,23.

Greater muscle mass and total body water content may reflect hypertrophic adaptations typical of disciplines requiring greater force production by the shoulder girdle and upper limbs24

The literature emphasises that both excess body fat and a suboptimal FFM to body mass ratio can affect movement efficiency and exercise economy12,25. Arslan and Dinç25, studying elite youth footballers, showed that higher levels of body fat correlate with lower scores on the Functional Movement Screen (FMS) test, which may suggest a negative impact of excessive adiposity on the quality of basic movement patterns. However, no significant association was found between low body fat levels and higher FMS scores, indicating that this relationship may only become apparent once a certain threshold of adiposity is exceeded.

These findings are consistent with the review by Toomey et al. (2022),11, which indicates that higher levels of adiposity may increase the risk of injury in adolescents, although this relationship is modified by activity level and sport specificity. In turn, the meta-analysis by Moran et al. (2017)26 emphasises the limited predictive value of the FMS total score as a standalone tool for assessing injury risk, especially in highly fit populations. In elite populations, moderate differences in body composition may therefore be less important for the quality of movement patterns than neuromuscular adaptations and level of training.

In turn, other available studies11,12,23,27 suggest that body composition may act as a modulating factor in the relationship between training load and injury risk, affecting both the mechanical tolerance of tissues and the ability to recover after exercise. This is rather important for game-based training, where game constraints and rules are continuously adjusted in relation to the intensity and density of players’ actions28.

Quality of movement patterns (FMS).

The lack of significant differences in the total Functional Movement Screen (FMS) score between the groups suggests that limb dominance in team sports does not significantly translate into the overall quality of basic movement patterns. This result should be interpreted in the context of the current methodological literature on FMS, which emphasises both its diagnostic value and limitations26,29,30. The FMS was designed as a screening tool to identify limitations in mobility, stability, and asymmetry in kinematic chains4. However, its predictive ability in relation to sports injuries remains a subject of debate. A meta-analysis by Bonazzi et al.31 showed high sensitivity of the test in predicting injuries, while more recent systematic reviews indicate that a cut-off value of ≤ 14 points is not universally applicable in sports populations, although it may be an important indicator in individual training planning focused on movement deficits12,25,26,32,33.

In the context of this study, the lack of differences between the groups may suggest that the FMS measures general motor competence rather than adaptations specific to limb dominance. It is increasingly emphasised that the FMS assesses the quality of central control (core stability, segmental control, integration of global patterns) rather than the strength or power characteristic of a given discipline4,33–37. This means that even with differences in body composition or the nature of training loads, global movement patterns may remain comparable in athletes with similar levels of training.

The issue of asymmetry is equally important. The FMS test allows for the identification of asymmetrical patterns; however, research indicates that the mere presence of asymmetry is not always associated with an increased risk of injury, provided it falls within the adaptive limits specific to a given sport36,38,39. In team sports, functional asymmetry may be adaptive in nature (e.g. unilateral throwing or kicking patterns), which does not necessarily indicate a functional deficit36,38. This finding may be quite instructive for coaches, as sport-specific asymmetries may also result from the emphasis placed on unilateral movements during intense competitive seasons.

The design limitations of the FMS also affect the interpretation of the results. The test is based on an ordinal scale (0–3 points), which reduces its sensitivity in detecting subtle motor differences between highly trained athletes. In populations of highly trained athletes, a ceiling effect may occur, limiting the ability to differentiate between groups25,25,30. In this context, the lack of significant differences in this study does not necessarily mean identical movement quality, but may reflect the limited resolution of the measurement tool.

From a practical perspective, the FMS should be used as part of a broader diagnostic battery, including power tests, reactive strength tests, biomechanical analyses and training load monitoring27,40. The integration of these data can provide a more complete picture of an athlete’s motor readiness than the FMS score alone. The lack of differences in FMS between groups can be interpreted as confirmation that basic movement patterns are a relatively universal foundation for motor preparation in team sports. At the same time, these results emphasise the need for caution in treating the FMS as a tool for differentiating between sports populations with similar levels of training.

The greater training experience of athletes in Group 1 may indicate longer exposure to training focused primarily on the lower limbs, which may result in different neuromuscular adaptations, such as improved postural control and movement stabilisation, typical of running and jumping disciplines33–35. No significant differences were found in movement quality as measured by the total FMS score or its components. The literature indicates that the FMS, as a screening tool, has limited clinical reliability in predicting injuries in athletes practising various disciplines and often does not correlate clearly with other functional tests, such as the Y Balance Test (YBT)37,41,42. A meta-analysis conducted by Eckart et al.10 suggests that individual FMS and YBT indicators may not be sufficient to assess injury risk and require analysis in conjunction with other indicators, such as training load or injury history.

Postural control (Y Balance Test).

Similarly, the lack of differences in the results of the Y-Balance Test indicates a comparable level of dynamic postural control. Plisky et al.5 demonstrated that asymmetries in the YBT may be associated with an increased risk of injury; however, their occurrence depends more often on an individual’s history of overuse than on the specific nature of the sport in question. Sikora et al.41 demonstrated a weak to moderate correlation between the FMS and the YBT, confirming that these tests measure different aspects of motor control. This observation is also supported by the research of Chimera et al.42

Recent studies in the field of functional monitoring of athletes emphasise that postural control is strongly dependent on the current training load and level of fatigue40. In this study, the lack of differences may indicate that the athletes in both groups were in a comparable state of adaptation.

When considering the research findings, it is impossible to overlook the ceiling effect observed in well-trained athletes, which may limit the sensitivity of tools such as the FMS and YBT in distinguishing between high-level athletes. This means that individuals with a high level of physical fitness often achieve similar, high scores, which makes it difficult to detect subtle differences between groups. Consequently, the lack of differences in functional test results may stem not only from the actual similarity of the subjects but also from the limited measurement resolution of these tools in the population of elite athletes.

At the same time, it is worth emphasising that the FMS and YBT are universal tests that allow for comparisons across disciplines, unlike sport-specific tools, which would limit the comparability of results, hence their high value in screening studies10.

Stress, muscle damage, and inflammatory biomarkers.

No significant differences in CK, CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β or cortisol concentrations were found between the analysed groups. These results suggest that there is no persistent low-grade inflammation differentiating the sports categories studied. This observation is consistent with the current state of knowledge regarding the inflammatory response to physical exercise. According to the concept of skeletal muscle as an endocrine organ, muscle contraction induces a transient increase in myokines, including IL-6, which have both pro- and anti-inflammatory functions, initiating an adaptive cascade43,44. However, regular training leads to a reduction in resting concentrations of inflammatory markers and an improvement in immunometabolic sensitivity, as confirmed by meta-analyses of intervention studies45,46. Furthermore, it has been shown that acute and short-term training stress, particularly when combined with rapid weight loss, can significantly alter both inflammatory markers and markers of muscle damage, highlighting their sensitivity to transient physiological stressors rather than solely to chronic training status47

The literature emphasises that in trained individuals, basic inflammatory markers are usually within the reference range, and their short-term fluctuations are more strongly related to the current training load, exercise intensity and recovery phase than to the specifics of the sport45,48. A review by Nieman and Wentz48 indicated that the immune response of athletes is dynamic and depends on the balance between exercise stress and recovery. Similarly, Simpson et al.46 showed that regular physical activity has a chronic anti-inflammatory effect, despite a temporary increase in cytokines after a training session.

The relationship between body weight and CRP observed in the analysis is consistent with reports indicating a strong correlation between adiposity and low-grade inflammation. A significant relationship between body weight and cortisol was also observed. Adipose tissue, especially visceral adipose tissue, is a significant source of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, which translates into higher CRP concentrations at rest49,50. Ross et al.49 and Visser et al.50 showed that body composition indices correlate positively with inflammatory markers regardless of the level of physical activity.

CK activity was significantly associated with training experience, which can be interpreted as the result of muscle adaptation to prolonged loads. CK is a commonly used marker of muscle damage; however, its levels depend not only on exercise intensity but also on the degree of training and adaptation to eccentric loads51,52. Brancaccio et al.51 emphasise the high individual variability of this marker and its limited diagnostic value without taking the training context into account. In turn, Baird et al.52 point out that, with adaptation to eccentric exercise, a reduced CK response is observed under comparable loads, reflecting the so-called repeated-set effect. In this context, the lack of differences between groups may indicate a similar level of adaptation to physical exertion among the athletes studied. This is also confirmed by experimental studies, which have shown that rapid weight loss combined with high-intensity sport-specific training leads to a significantly greater increase in markers of muscle damage compared to training alone, confirming the sensitivity of these markers to acute metabolic and mechanical stress53.

Consequently, the absence of significant differences in the FMS and YBT results does not rule out the existence of subtle physiological or adaptive differences between the groups of athletes, as these tools assess more stable patterns of motor control and functional stability, whereas biochemical markers primarily reflect the body’s short-term response to current training loads, recovery and physiological stress. At the same time, the absence of differences in the analysed inflammatory and hormonal markers, which are particularly sensitive to transient changes in training load (including IL-6 and TNF-α), suggests that the athletes studied were at a similar level of short-term exercise adaptation, which may explain the simultaneous absence of significant differences in both functional tests and biochemical profiles.

Integration of results and practical implications

The results obtained make a significant contribution to the discussion on the importance of limb dominance in team sports. Contrary to the often-repeated assumption that functional lateralisation can lead to asymmetries that increase the risk of injury, our data indicate that limb dominance does not significantly differentiate the quality of basic movement patterns assessed by the FMS test or the level of investigated biomarkers. This suggests that under conditions of systematic sports training, potential functional asymmetries can be adaptively compensated for by the neuromuscular system.

At the same time, the observed differences in body composition parameters indicate that lateralisation may be of greater structural than functional importance. In the context of motor preparation, this means that strength training programming and body weight and composition control should be more individualised, even if functional tests do not reveal clear deficits in movement quality. These results emphasise the need for a holistic approach to athlete assessment, in which structure and function are analysed in parallel rather than as separate components. In game-based approaches, performance assessment is always adjusted to game constraints and the way these place load on athletes’ physiological capabilities and anthropometric profiles54.

It seems particularly important to combine functional assessment (FMS, Y Balance Test) with systematic monitoring of training loads, both internal and external27,40. The integration of data from motor tests with information on mechanical and physiological load allows for a more precise determination of training readiness and the identification of early signs of overload. This approach is in line with modern models of training process control, in which training decisions are based on multidimensional data analysis rather than a single indicator. Importantly, the lack of significant differences in inflammatory markers between the groups suggests that limb dominance—at least in the study population—is not associated with increased low-grade inflammation or greater regenerative load. In practice, this means that decisions regarding modifications to training volume or intensity should not be based solely on the criterion of lateralisation, but rather on a comprehensive assessment of the athlete’s condition.

More broadly, these results reinforce the argument for an integrative model of sports diagnostics, in which the analysis of movement quality, body composition, training loads, and biochemical parameters constitutes a coherent system supporting the optimisation of the training process and injury prevention.

Limitations and directions for further research

The limitations of the study point to the validity of longitudinal projects monitoring athletes throughout a full training cycle or competitive season. Such an analysis would allow for a better assessment of the variability of inflammatory markers and functional parameters in response to training loads. It is also worth considering modern monitoring tools and extending the analysis to include dynamic asymmetry indicators and unilateral strength tests, which would allow for a more accurate understanding of the relationship between limb dominance, training adaptation and injury risk. This approach allows for a more precise determination of the functional significance of the observed differences and emphasises the value of multidimensional diagnostics in modern motor training.

Another limitation of the study is that the applied classification of “limb dominance” represents a simplified, sport-specific proxy of movement loading that does not reflect athletes’ individual laterality and may partially limit the consideration of their true functional variability.

Additionally, despite the use of basic standardisation of the procedure (morning blood sampling on an empty stomach), the biomarkers assessed were particularly sensitive to external factors such as recent training loads, recovery, diet, sleep, and circadian rhythm, which were not fully controlled. Furthermore, only a single blood sample was taken at rest; therefore, the results obtained should be interpreted as a momentary ‘snapshot’ of physiological status rather than a stable profile. An additional limitation was the predominant participation of football and futsal players, which may have influenced the nature of the results obtained and limited the possibility of generalising them to other team sports.
