Long-cycling organic flow batteries enabled by electronic-spatial synergistic modulation
Authors: Tao Wang, Yuheng Xia, Chenlong Gao, Tianyu Huang, Zhitao Zhao, Miao Chen, Menghao Yang, Mingjin Cui, Yu Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aqueous organic redox flow batteries (AORFBs) show promise for grid-scale energy storage but are limited by inadequate stability and solubility of active materials. Here we report an electronic-spatial synergistic modulation strategy to simultaneously enhance aqueous dissolution and electrochemical robustness of organic species, demonstrated on 4-aminophenol (PAP)–based molecules. By introducing a piperazine ring and an acetyl group at the amino site, we design 1-(4-(4-hydroxyphenyl)piperazin-1-yl)ethan-1-one (AHPP). This synergistic modulation stabilizes the oxidized state, suppresses side reactions, and boosts solubility to 1.9 molar in aqueous solution (3.8-molar electron concentration). An all-organic flow battery based on AHPP achieves 95.7% capacity retention after 5000 cycles, with stable operation across a wide temperature range. Integrating in situ spectroscopic and electrochemical analysis with computational modeling elucidates the redox chemistry of PAP-based molecules and establishes a link between intermediate stability and functional group effects. Life cycle assessment further reveals the environmental footprint of AHPP-based batteries, demonstrating considerable potential for practical grid-scale storage applications.

INTRODUCTION

Electricity generation from renewable sources is increasingly recognized as a pivotal solution to address the growing global energy requirements and critical environmental challenges (1, 2). However, the inherent intermittency and fluctuations of renewables can easily lead to imbalances between grid supply and demand, potentially causing safety issues such as frequency fluctuations and voltage limit violations. Therefore, large-scale, high-safety, and long-duration energy storage technologies serve as pivotal enablers for the efficient grid integration of renewable energy and the stable operation of power systems. Aqueous organic redox flow batteries (AORFBs) show considerable potential in integrating renewable energy sources and balancing grid-scale energy storage capacity, thanks to the inherent safety, natural availability of their raw materials, and tunable synthesis of organic redox-active molecules (ORAMs) (3–5). However, insufficient energy density, inadequate electrochemical stability, and limited resistance to harsh operating conditions remain critical factors hindering their commercial viability (6).

In recent times, marked progress has been accomplished in the study of ORAMs, such as quinones, viologens (7), nitroxyl radicals (8), aza-aromatic compounds (9), and others (10–19). The limited concentration of most explored ORAMs, resulting from their bulky conjugated frameworks and strong intermolecular π-π stacking, cannot compete with inorganic counterparts, thus notably constraining the delivered capacity of AORFBs (20–27). On the other hand, side reactions, including nucleophilic substitution, disproportionation, dimerization, and irreversible tautomerization, continue to pose substantial challenges to their practical application. The irreversible loss of active material, especially at higher concentrations in severe environments, drives pronounced capacity fade during cycling and severely limits the long-term service life of AORFBs (28–31). Different molecular engineering strategies have been proposed to boost the chemical and physical features of organic redox species, but systematic optimization for simultaneous enhancement of solubility and stability remains elusive. Herein, we developed a methodology that couples electronic and spatial effects of functional groups to tailor the chemical and physical properties of organic compounds across a vast range. This approach presents an alternative paradigm for simultaneously enhancing the energy density and cycle life of AORFBs for practical applications (Fig. 1A). We demonstrate this approach using the 4-aminophenol (PAP) molecule as a model system. PAP is a low-cost organic intermediate widely used in pharmaceutical synthesis and dye manufacturing. Its redox reaction involves valence state changes of N and O atoms, and the aromatic conjugated structure within the PAP molecule provides a pathway for electron transport, which helps reduce the reaction activation energy barrier while facilitating side reactions. Besides, its solubility has yet to be further improved to achieve high energy density for practical applications. By virtue of the methodology developed in this work, both the stability and solubility of PAP-derived organic redox species can be optimized through an electronic-spatial synergistic modulation of introduced functional groups. This strategy is designed to synergistically modulate both the electronic structure and spatial interactions of the molecule. In terms of electronic regulation, it enhances intrinsic stability by fine-tuning the electron density of active sites, thereby lifting activation energy for undesired side reactions and reducing degradation. From a spatial perspective, it optimizes molecular distribution through the introduction of polar groups that promote effective solvation, preventing aggregation and ensuring uniform dispersion, which notably enhances solubility. This strategy overcomes the performance trade-offs often associated with single-factor modification approaches, offering a valuable direction for designing AORFBs that simultaneously achieve high stability and energy density (Fig. 1B).

According to the design principle proposed in this work, the well-designed molecule of 1-(4-(4-hydroxyphenyl)piperazin-1-yl)ethan-1-one (AHPP) exhibits a high solubility of 1.9 mol liter−1, translating to an electron concentration of 3.8 mol liter−1, along with stable electrochemical activity that effectively suppresses side reactions. When assembled into an all-organic flow battery, the device demonstrated 5000 stable cycles at room temperature, while also maintaining 2000 cycles under extreme temperature conditions. In addition, the delivered capacity of the as-fabricated flow battery can reach 40 A·hour liter−1 when coupled with high concentration negolyte. By combining in situ spectroscopic and electrochemical analysis with theoretical calculations, the redox chemistry of the PAP family is fundamentally elucidated, laying the groundwork for validating our methodology. Furthermore, a systematic life cycle assessment (LCA) was used to quantify the environmental footprints of AHPP-based AORFBs. The results were rigorously benchmarked against those of conventional all-vanadium flow batteries across multiple impact categories, revealing considerable environmental advantages of AHPP-based AORFBs from a cradle-to-grave perspective.

The amino group of PAP was modified by introducing a sterically hindered acetyl group, yielding APAP through steric regulation. The electron-withdrawing property of the acetyl group brings about a positive shift in the molecule’s redox potential, which is directly confirmed by cyclic voltammetry (CV). Subsequently, PAP was further modified via electronic effects to obtain HPP. Consistent with the structural modulation, the cyclic voltammogram of HPP exhibits distinct potential shifts compared to those of PAP and APAP, reflecting the altered electronic structure. AHPP, obtained through electronic-spatial synergistic modulation, exhibited optimal electrochemical performance in CV tests. Compared to HPP modified solely via electronic effects, AHPP showed the expected positive shift in redox potential. In contrast to APAP modified only through steric effects, it demonstrated an anodic-to-cathodic peak current ratio approaching unity along with notably reduced peak potential separation. These key electrochemical characteristics indicate that the electronic-spatial synergistic effect contributes to the suppression of irreversible side reactions, which is associated with an observed improvement in the electrochemical reversibility of AHPP.

In redox flow batteries, the dissolved concentration of active substances in the electrolyte is positively correlated with their charge storage capacity. Therefore, designing high-solubility organic compounds is of the utmost importance for AORFBs. A systematic measurement of the solubility of PAP derivatives was conducted through ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) (Fig. 2C and figs. S1 to S4), which is 0.78 mol liter−1 for PAP, 0.12 mol liter−1 for APAP, 0.15 mol liter−1 for HPP, and 1.9 mol liter−1 for AHPP. Within the target compound system, AHPP engineered via electronic-spatial synergistic modulation exhibits optimal solubility, with an equivalent electron concentration of 3.8 mol liter−1. Figure 2D and table S1 systematically compare the chemical and physical properties of typical ORAMs reported. It is shown that the equivalent electron concentration of AHPP reached is higher than most reported ORAMs, giving rise to a high theoretical capacity of the as-fabricated AORFBs. Meanwhile, its redox potential of 0.748 V versus standard hydrogen electrode falls within the typical range of active materials commonly used in flow batteries, proving its applicability potential in high–energy density flow batteries. This result validates that the electronic and spatial synergistic modulation not only enhances electrochemical reversibility but also enables precise tuning of molecular polarity and intermolecular interactions, thereby improving solubility.

To achieve a deeper understanding of the intrinsic nature of p-aminophenol derivatives, we calculated their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels (Fig. 2E). The results demonstrate a strong correlation between the oxidation potentials of p-aminophenol derivatives and their HOMO energy levels, which is attributed to the differential regulation of the benzene ring by substituents (fig. S5). The acetyl group in APAP is directly conjugated to the phenyl ring, imparting a strong electron-withdrawing effect that notably reduces the HOMO energy level. In contrast, the piperazine ring in HPP exhibits electron-donating character, leading to a slight elevation of the HOMO energy. An electron-withdrawing acetyl group introduced into the HPP scaffold makes AHPP’s highest molecular orbital energy level lower than HPP’s yet higher than APAP’s. AHPP exhibits the narrowest HOMO-LUMO gap of 4.82 eV, a result attributed to the synergistic interplay between the steric nature of the piperazine ring and the acetyl group’s electronic properties. The electrochemical characteristics of the PAP-derived molecules were further analyzed using rotating disk electrode (RDE) measurements (figs. S6 to S9). The limiting currents observed at various rotation rates correspond to a diffusion-controlled process. The diffusion coefficients derived from these measurements are 3.614 × 10−5, 4.738 × 10−5, 6.504 × 10−5, and 9.779 × 10−5 cm2 s−1 for PAP, APAP, HPP, and AHPP, respectively. The kinetic current was derived from Koutecky-Levich plots, and the corresponding reaction rate constants were determined using the Butler-Volmer equation. Among the compounds, AHPP exhibits the fastest reaction kinetics, with a rate constant of 9.603 × 10−3 cm s−1. This indicates that electronic-spatial synergistic modulation can effectively enhance the intrinsic reaction kinetics of the active material by narrowing the HOMO-LUMO gap, thereby reducing the reorganization energy. The rapid reaction kinetics of AHPP also show a high correlation with the HOMO-LUMO gap determined by DFT calculations (Fig. 2E). Table S2 summarizes the physicochemical properties of the aminophenol compounds. It is concluded that AHPP has a moderate redox potential, the highest solubility, diffusion coefficient, and reaction rate constant, making it a promising electroactive molecule for AORFBs applications.

To elucidate molecular-level structure-solubility relationships, we used molecular dynamics simulations to analyze the solvation structures of aminophenol derivatives in electrolytes. Spatial distribution analyses revealed distinct solvation patterns (Fig. 3, A to D), with AHPP exhibiting the most homogeneous dispersion with the highest concentration, while all other PAP-derived molecules precipitate with either localized aggregates or clustering formed. The radial distribution function (RDF) (fig. S10) further elucidates the dissolution mechanism. PAP exhibits a sharp peak due to the presence of two hydrophilic groups, thereby achieving moderate solubility. The sterically modified APAP exhibits reduced proton affinity due to the introduced sterically hindered acetyl group and its inherent electron-withdrawing nature, resulting in peak broadening and diminished intensity. The electronically modified HPP, although containing a protonable piperazine group, exhibits a weak peak signal and displays a premature plateau phenomenon attributed to elevated local concentration. This behavior is associated with the rigid structure of its piperazine ring and electron-inductive effects that promote molecular aggregation, thereby suppressing solubility. In contrast, AHHP, prepared via an electronic-steric synergistic modulation strategy, achieves coordinated optimization of multiple key properties. The introduced carbonyl group (C=O) notably enhances the molecular dipole moment, substantially improving its affinity in polar solvents. Moreover, the incorporation of the acetyl group disrupts the ordered stacking of the original piperazine ring, effectively inhibiting close intermolecular aggregation and further promoting solubility. To investigate the relationship between molecular structure and aggregation behavior, the RDF between molecules was analyzed (fig. S11). The RDF profile of APAP shows the highest and sharpest peak, indicating the strongest molecular aggregation tendency and thus the lowest solubility. In contrast, AHPP, through electronic-spatial synergistic modulation, achieves optimized intermolecular packing, leading to notably enhanced solubility. This result aligns with the aggregation states of the aminophenol derivatives observed in molecular dynamics simulation snapshots within their respective simulation boxes, where AHPP exhibits a more homogeneous distribution (figs. S12 to S15). To elucidate the mechanism by which electronic structure and spatial configuration synergistically modulate solubility, we first analyzed the electrostatic potential (ESP) and proton affinity of the molecules in their protonated states (Fig. 3E). The computational results show that PAP exhibits strong proton affinity due to the presence of two hydrophilic groups, with an ESP value near its hydroxyl group of +44.571 kcal mol−1. Upon spatial modification via acetylation, the corresponding ESP value in APAP decreases to +30.967 kcal mol−1, indicating that this structural alteration notably weakens the molecule’s ability to bind protons. For HPP, the introduced group increases the ESP around its hydroxyl moiety to +77.120 kcal mol−1, reflecting enhanced electron affinity; however, the inherent conformational rigidity of the molecule likely restricts its conformational flexibility and solvation efficiency in aqueous solution, thereby limiting further improvement in solubility. By leveraging electronic-spatial synergy, AHPP notably enhances molecular polarity while maintaining high electron affinity, thus successfully overcoming the solubility bottleneck. Notably, ESP analysis in the neutral state (fig. S16) exhibits a fully consistent trend, further validating the above conclusions. The electron localization function (ELF) analysis reveals a consistent trend: APAP, modified with the acetyl group, shows decreased electron density distribution, thereby resulting in notably lower solubility compared to PAP. Compared with HPP, the ELF distribution of AHPP reveals an uneven electron density distribution within the system (Fig. 3F). This result indicates an altered polarity of the molecular system, thereby influencing its dissolution behavior. Such a mechanism notably enhances the protonation capability of AHPP in acidic environments and optimizes its spatial arrangement, providing a theoretical foundation for its superior solubility.

Leveraging the high solubility and molecular stability of AHPP as the positive material, we selected 2,7-AQDS as the negative molecule to assemble AORFBs for systematic evaluation. Figure 4B shows the charge-discharge curves over a current density range of 10 to 60 mA cm−2, with Coulombic efficiency (CE) maintaining close to 99% across all tested conditions. With a current density of 10 mA cm−2, the battery delivers 89% of its theoretical capacity. Relative to this baseline, as current densities rise to 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 mA cm−2, the battery maintains capacity retentions of 96, 91, 88, 85, and 81%. The battery’s decent rate capability is partly contributed by the intrinsic nature of the AHPP with a small HOMO-LUMO energy gap, as well as the high diffusion coefficients and reaction kinetic constants. Extended cycling tests unequivocally demonstrate the high stability of the system. The all-organic flow battery using 0.2 M AHPP demonstrates high cyclability and maintains stable operation for more than 5000 cycles with capacity retention of 95.7% (Fig. 4A). Its cycling performance surpasses that of all those PAP derivatives that are susceptible to degradation via nucleophilic attack by QAI intermediates (figs. S17 to S19). This sustained cycling performance confirms the effectiveness of the symmetric electrolyte strategy in mitigating crossover and maintaining long-term capacity retention. Notably, the internal resistance of the battery remained stable during cycling, indicating generally good stability of the electrode interface and electrolyte structure, which confirms the high electrochemical reversibility and cycling durability (fig. S20). Even increasing the active material concentration to 0.5 mol liter−1 yields a volumetric capacity of 20 A·hour liter−1, with minimal capacity loss observed over 2000 cycles (Fig. 4C). The sustained electrochemical reversibility under these high-concentration conditions is further corroborated by the stable voltage profiles (Fig. 4D), establishing a foundation for notably advancements in practical energy density.

Temperature variation presents a notable challenge to the performance of energy storage systems. Particularly under subzero conditions, battery performance can deteriorate substantially, creating a pressing need to develop systems capable of operating reliably in such harsh environments. The solvation structure of electrolytes acts as a key factor in determining electrochemical behavior under extreme conditions. In this study, urea, an inexpensive and environmentally benign additive, was introduced to inhibit the formation of ordered ice crystals and lower the freezing point of the electrolyte. At −10°C and a current density of 10 mA cm−2, the AHPP-based flow battery showed stable performance across 2000 cycles, with 95% of its initial capacity retained (Fig. 4E). The corresponding charge-discharge voltage profiles show highly overlapping curves with only minor deviations between cycles, further confirming the high electrochemical stability of the battery system under low-temperature conditions (fig. S21). In addition to its good low-temperature performance, the battery system also demonstrates remarkable tolerance in harsh environments. It is known that elevated temperature leads to accelerated side reactions, degradation of active materials, and aging of the separator, resulting in reduced CE and faster capacity fade. As shown in fig. S22, the AHPP-based all-organic flow battery retained 92.5% of its initial capacity after 1000 continuous cycles under the harsh high-temperature condition of 40°C.

Notably, the influence of temperature on the electrolyte’s ionic conductivity aligns with the observed trends in battery performance. As the temperature rose from −10° to 40°C, the ionic conductivity rose from 108.9 to 208.6 mS cm−1 (table S3). This enhancement can be partially attributed to the thermally induced decrease in electrolyte viscosity, which facilitates ion mobility at elevated temperatures. Correspondingly, although the introduction of additives into the electrolyte can effectively reduce the freezing point of the system by adjusting the solvation structure, thereby expanding the low-temperature operating window of the battery, it simultaneously increases the overall viscosity due to additional intermolecular interactions or steric hindrance effects. This results in greater ion migration resistance and consequently a decrease in ionic conductivity (table S4). These results not only demonstrate the effective suppression of side reactions at elevated temperatures but also complement the previously reported low-temperature performance, collectively confirming the robust and reliable operation of this all-organic flow battery within a wide temperature range.

To further evaluate the performance of AHPP as a positive electrode material, silicotungstic acid (SWO) was selected as the matching negative material because of its high solubility and excellent rate capability. Figure S23 illustrates the rate capability of the assembled SWO||AHPP battery. The results reveal that the battery attains 85.8% high capacity utilization at 10 mA cm−2 current density. In practical flow battery use, active material concentration directly affects the system’s volumetric energy density and cost efficiency. High-concentration systems are critical for achieving large-scale energy storage applications. Therefore, a cell with 1 mol liter−1 SWO||AHPP was evaluated. The battery demonstrated stable operation over 1000 cycles (fig. S24). To further elucidate the stability of its electrochemical behavior during long-term operation, charge-discharge curves at key cycling intervals are also provided (fig. S25). During the initial cycling phase, a sharp decline in capacity retention was observed, which is hypothesized to be associated with molecular aggregation at high concentrations. We systematically summarized and compared the cycling stability of currently reported organic flow battery systems (Fig. 4F and table S5). These findings present a flow battery with high cycling stability and capacity retention, outperforming most systems reported to date. As summarized in table S6, which systematically compares key performance metrics and capital cost, the developed battery achieves energy density and cycle life comparable to state-of-the-art flow batteries yet at a reduced cost. These findings highlight its potential for practical application and offer useful insights for designing cost-effective, high-performance flow battery systems.

The electrolysis of water is a common side reaction in flow batteries that can compromise both performance and stability. The oxygen evolution reaction (OER) not only consumes the solvent water but also generates reactive oxygen species, which are strong oxidizing agents that irreversibly accelerate the degradation of cathode active materials and the oxidative degradation of ion-exchange membranes. Concurrently, the hydrogen evolution reaction continuously consumes protons (H+), leading to electrolyte compositional imbalance and pH drift, thereby causing irreversible capacity loss. Acting in concert, these parasitic reactions severely constrain the cycling lifespan and CE of the battery. Furthermore, the generated oxygen or hydrogen may form bubbles in the electrolyte, increasing system resistance, interfering with pumping, and accumulating on the electrode surface, thereby raising interfacial impedance or blocking active sites. In severe cases, gas accumulation may even cause electrolyte leakage or elevated system pressure, posing safety risks. Therefore, monitoring the gas evolution due to water electrolysis is essential for evaluating battery stability. In this experiment, the gaseous atmosphere during battery operation was monitored using a gas analyzer (fig. S26). Results showed that no hydrogen or oxygen generation was detected over a 600-min operational period, confirming the robust stability of the battery under the tested conditions and the absence of notable oxygen or hydrogen evolution side reactions (fig. S27).

To elucidate the structural evolution mechanism of AHPP during the electrochemical cycling process, we conducted a systematic investigation of its dynamic behavior using a combination of in situ and ex situ spectroscopic analyses. As shown in Fig. 5A, the UV-Vis absorption spectrum of AHPP undergoes notable changes during the charging process. The intensity of the characteristic absorption peak at 225 nm gradually decreases, while a new absorption band emerges and broadens around 245 nm. This phenomenon indicates a notable electronic structural reorganization of AHPP during oxidation. The π-π* transition is replaced by the formation of AHPP2+, in which the chromophore is linked to a strongly electron-withdrawing quaternary ammonium nitrogen center, forming a distinct donor-acceptor electronic system. The resulting extended conjugation and intramolecular charge-transfer effects lower the energy required for electronic transitions, accounting for the noticeable red-shift and enhanced intensity of the new absorption feature. To investigate the molecular structural evolution during the redox interconversion between AHPP and AHPP2+, we used synchronized in situ Raman spectroscopy and CV to monitor the entire electrochemical cycling process in real time (Fig. 5B). During the oxidation process, the characteristic peak at 1000 cm−1 intensified, which is ascribed to the C–N+ stretching vibration of the newly formed quaternary ammonium group. Simultaneously, the attenuation of the phenolic C–O stretching mode at 1200 cm−1 confirmed the consumption of the hydroxyl group. Furthermore, the intensity of the stretching vibration of C=O at 1600 cm−1 increased, reflecting the formation of the quinoid carbonyl group. Notably, all the aforementioned spectral changes demonstrated reversibility during the subsequent reduction process, thereby confirming the structural interconversion mechanism between AHPP and AHPP2+.

Furthermore, in situ infrared spectroscopy was applied to simultaneously track the evolution of functional groups and the stability of the electrolyte over two complete electrochemical cycling periods (Fig. 5C). The experimental results revealed a progressive intensification of the absorption peak at 1550 cm−1, which originates from the coupling of quinoid ring skeletal vibrations and C–N+ stretching vibrations, reflecting the electronic reorganization process occurring at the nitrogen center. Meanwhile, the absorption peak at 1650 cm−1 also exhibited a notable increase in intensity, a phenomenon attributed to the stretching vibration of the C=O bond in the quinoid structure, further confirming the generation of the carbonyl group. Notably, the positions and intensities of the characteristic peaks exhibited consistent reproducibility, indicating the reversibility of the structural changes.

To elucidate the intrinsic mechanism underlying the side reactions of these molecules at the molecular level, we used nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to characterize the structural evolution of key molecules during the reaction. Four representative electrolyte systems were selected, and their NMR spectra were recorded before and after cycling (Fig. 5D and figs. S28 and S29). Analysis of these spectra revealed that, in the postcycling samples of three molecular systems, a characteristic signal attributed to benzoquinone was consistently observed at approximately 6.25 parts per million. This observation confirms that these molecules undergo degradation during battery cycling, yielding benzoquinone as a byproduct. In notable contrast, no new characteristic signals appeared in the NMR spectra of AHPP before and after cycling, indicating that it remained free from such degradative side reactions under identical conditions (Fig. 5E).

On the basis of these experimental observations, we propose a plausible degradation pathway for such side reactions (Fig. 5F), taking the degradation of PAP as a model. PAP first undergoes a two-electron oxidation to generate a para-quinone imine intermediate. Subsequently, a water molecule acts as a nucleophile and attacks the imino moiety of this intermediate. This attack triggers an electron transfer, leading to the protonation of the amine group adjacent to the imine, which converts it into a good leaving group. Last, the departure of this leaving group results in the formation of benzoquinone. Further analysis of this degradation pathway identifies the nucleophilic attack of water on the imine group as the key energy-barrier step. To quantitatively assess the reactivity trends of different molecules in this step and explain their stability differences, we computed the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for this step. The results demonstrate that the sterically modified APAP exhibits an increase in its reaction energy barrier from an initial 2.04 to 2.71 eV, confirming that the steric hindrance effect effectively impedes attack by water molecules. The electronically modified HPP, through the electron-donating nature of the piperazine ring, suppresses nucleophilic attack at the key site, thereby raising its energy barrier to 2.16 eV. In contrast, AHPP, designed via an electronic-spatial synergistic regulation strategy, demonstrates a distinct advantage, with an energy barrier as high as 3.39 eV, notably exceeding those of the other comparative molecules. This elevated barrier indicates that nucleophilic attack by water molecules on the imino group of AHPP is kinetically highly unfavorable, thereby effectively suppressing degradation side reactions. These theoretical computational results are fully consistent with the previously mentioned NMR observations, collectively confirming that the superior stability of AHPP in the system originates from the high energy barrier encountered in the key step of its degradation pathway, reflecting a synergistic suppression mechanism combining electronic and steric effects.

Leveraging its ability to store and discharge energy on demand, the flow battery system serves as a flexible platform for renewable energy management. Figure 6A depicts the envisioned application scenario for the developed flow battery system. As the core energy storage unit, it facilitates bidirectional energy exchange between renewable sources, critical loads, and the main grid. By storing surplus renewable energy during periods of high generation and discharging during supply deficits, the system effectively mitigates renewable intermittency. In addition, it provides grid ancillary services, including frequency regulation and peak/valley load shifting. To evaluate the environmental impact of AHPP within the context of large-scale energy storage applications, this study used an LCA framework to systematically simulate and compare two synthesis routes for AHPP, aiming to identify the most environmentally preferable preparation pathway. Both synthesis routes use PAP as the starting material but differ notably in their reaction pathways (fig. S30). In route 1, PAP is first converted to HPP, which subsequently reacts with acetic anhydride to form the target product AHPP. In contrast, route 2 uses a one-step reaction between PAP and bis(2-bromoethyl)acetamide to directly synthesize AHPP. The LCA results indicate that, in terms of global warming potential (GWP), the environmental burden of route 1 is only 75.9% of that of route 2 (Fig. 6B). However, route 2 demonstrates notable advantages across multiple environmental impact categories, further highlighting the guiding value of LCA in analyzing the environmental footprint of battery systems. This distinction underscores the multidimensional trade-offs between the two routes, demonstrating that no single pathway is universally optimal. Selection should therefore be guided by context-specific environmental priorities. Furthermore, this study provides a visual analysis quantifying the contribution proportions of various pollutants to the GWP for the two AHPP synthesis routes (Fig. 6, C and D). A further analysis of bis(2-bromoethyl)acetamide, identified as the dominant contributor in route 2, reveals that the primary environmental impact originates from the use of HBr (fig. S31). This insight extends the scope for improvement beyond the immediate reaction pathway, suggesting that the environmental footprint of route 2 could be further reduced by optimizing the upstream synthesis of HBr itself. The results not only clearly identify the key contributing pollutants dominating the GWP in each route but also offer a concrete basis for formulating targeted pollution control strategies in subsequent research.

Furthermore, this study conducted a simulation analysis of an AHPP redox flow battery (RFB) system with rated parameters including a power output of 5 kW, an energy storage capacity of 15 kWh, and an operating current of 140 A. AHPP was synthesized via route 2, which demonstrated advantages across multiple environmental impact categories in the prior LCA, thereby ensuring both environmental sustainability and technoeconomic viability of the preparation process. Because of the early-stage nature of the AHPP electrolyte, end-of-life phase data are not yet available. Therefore, in accordance with ISO 14044:2006, a “cradle-to-gate” system boundary was adopted (Fig. 6E), encompassing as follows: extraction and processing of raw materials, manufacturing of the stack and auxiliary components, and construction of electrolyte storage infrastructure. Current research indicates that the recycling of materials other than electrolytes has demonstrated positive effects on enhancing the overall environmental performance of the system. Therefore, the development of advanced electrolyte recycling technologies compatible with these advancements is crucial for maximizing the life-cycle environmental benefits of the technical system as a whole. Furthermore, the environmental impact of the AHPP RFB system has been compared and analyzed against that of a conventional all-vanadium RFB (VRFB) system.

Radar chart analysis reveals that the AHPP RFB demonstrates notable advantages in most environmental impact categories (Fig. 6F). A further breakdown of the environmental burden sources in the conventional VRFB indicates that the dominant contributor to its GWP is the high energy consumption associated with vanadium ore mining. Implementing electrolyte recycling could substantially mitigate this impact by reducing the demand for primary vanadium. Although the GWP of the AHPP RFB system does not show a substantial reduction, it remains slightly lower than that of the conventional VRFB, demonstrating its potential value in mitigating global warming effects. This advantage is inherent to its material composition and does not rely on a recycling phase, whereas the environmental profile of the VRFB could be notably improved if electrolyte recycling is adopted. Through a comprehensive LCA, we compared and screened two synthesis routes for AHPP and further quantified the differences in environmental footprint between the AHPP RFB and the conventional VRFB. The superior performance of the AHPP RFB across multiple environmental indicators, along with a slight reduction in GWP, clearly demonstrates the application potential of AHPP in enhancing the environmental sustainability of large-scale energy storage systems, thereby laying an analytical foundation for future technological optimization and environmental performance improvements.

DISCUSSION

This study establishes an electronic-steric synergistic regulation methodology that, through dual functionalization at the molecular scale, has facilitated the development of an organic flow battery system exhibiting both high energy density and long cycle life, thereby paving a previously unavailable scientific foundation for advancing practical applications. Through our molecular engineering strategy, rational design of the PAP scaffold with synergistic integration of the electronic effects from the piperazine ring and steric effects from the acetyl group has led to the successful development of AHPP, an ORAM. The as-designed redox-active molecule shows comprehensive performance, including high electron concentration (3.8 mol liter−1) and high electrochemical reversibility. When assembled into an all-organic flow battery, it demonstrates exceptional cycling stability, maintaining capacity retention of 95.7% for after 5000 cycles at room temperature. Moreover, benefiting from the robust molecular architecture enabled by electronic-steric synergistic modulation, the battery maintains stable operation for 2000 and 1000 cycles under extreme temperature conditions of −10° and 40°C, respectively, demonstrating high environmental adaptability. Through combining in situ characterizations and computational modeling, it has been confirmed that the enhanced reversibility originates from the stabilized quinone imine intermediate enabled by synergistic electronic and steric effects. Furthermore, LCA investigates the environmental aspects of AHPP synthesis and compares its carbon footprint potential in relation to conventional vanadium-based systems. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the universality of the electronic-steric synergistic regulation strategy, which establishes a versatile molecular design platform for developing next-generation sustainable, efficient, and scalable organic energy storage materials, showing promising application prospects in grid-scale energy storage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

For this study, p-aminophenol, APAP, HPP, and N-acetyl-4-hydroxyphenyl piperazine were purchased from Meryer (Shanghai) Chemical Technology Co. Ltd. Urea and D2O (99.9% atomic % D) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd.

Instrument

This work measured the 1H NMR spectra on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz. UV-Vis spectra were collected on a VU-8000 from METASH. Electrochemical measurements encompassing CV and linear sweep voltammetry were acquired using CHI 760E equipment. Single batteries were assessed with CT-4008Tn-5V6A from NEWARE. Operando Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) was measured on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS50. In situ Raman spectroscopy was performed on an XploRA PLUS.

CV test in N2 atmosphere

In this experiment, a three-electrode beaker was first assembled with 0.01 mol liter−1 active substance dissolved in 1 mol liter−1 H2SO4. The working electrode was a glassy carbon electrode, the counter electrode was a platinum mesh electrode, and the reference electrode was a silver/silver chloride electrode; the distance between each electrode was approximately 1 cm. The electrolyte in the beaker was continuously purged with high-purity nitrogen for 10 min to eliminate the oxygen dissolved in the electrolyte, and then the beaker was sealed to make it completely airtight.

Operando FTIR measurements

In situ FTIR spectroscopy coupled with CV was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer. The electroactive material was mounted within the cell proximal to the infrared (IR)–transparent window. During testing, IR radiation reflected off the surface of the electroactive material and penetrated the sample to an appropriate depth to acquire the infrared spectra. The sample solution contained the electroactive material undergoing CV cycling. Changes in transmittance relative to the air background were recorded as a function of time during the electrochemical scans.

In situ Raman measurements

In situ Raman spectroscopy synchronized with CV was performed using a Horiba XploRA PLUS spectrometer integrated with a custom electrochemical cell. The electroactive material was mounted onto the working electrode surface, aligned perpendicular to the incident laser through the optically transparent window. During CV operation (scan rate: 5 mV s−1; potential range: 0 to 1 V versus Ag/AgCl), Raman spectra were acquired in real time via backscattering geometry. Data acquisition was triggered at fixed potential intervals to correlate spectral features with redox states.

Nuclear magnetic resonance

Ex situ 1H NMR spectra (400 MHz, D2O) of the electroactive material were recorded before and after battery testing at the oxidized state.

Characterizations

PAP, APAP, HPP, and AHPP were dissolved in a 1 mol liter−1 H2SO4 solution at a concentration of 0.001 mol liter−1 for the CV test and RDE test. A three-electrode system was configured with a graphite plate electrode as both the working and counter electrodes, while an Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode. The CV tests were performed at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1. In the RDE tests, a graphite electrode acted as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode, with a glassy carbon electrode serving as the working electrode. The RDE tests were conducted with potential ranging from 0.3 to 1.1 V (versus Ag/AgCl) at rotation rates of 625, 900, 1225, 1600, 2025, and 2500 rpm. Battery performance

Full-cell testing was performed using a CT-4008Tn-5V6A-S1 battery test system (Neware Technology Co. Ltd., China). The flow battery (model YTH-1, obtained from Wuhan Zhisheng New Energy) was assembled with a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) frame, graphite plates serving as current collectors, graphite felt (5-mm thickness) as the electrode material, and a Nafion 212 membrane. The graphite felt, sourced from Wuhan Zhisheng New Energy Co. Ltd., was pretreated at 450°C for 4 hours in a muffle furnace before assembly. The battery was tested using the static method. For long-term cycling evaluation of the AHPP system, both the positive and negative electrolytes contained 0.1 to 0.5 mol liter−1 AHPP and 1 mol liter−1 H2SO4, supplemented with 0.12 to 0.6 mol liter−1 2,7-AQDS and 5 mol liter−1 urea. In the SWO||AHPP battery configuration, the positive electrolyte comprised 1 M AHPP in 1 M H2SO4. The identical electrolyte formulations were maintained for AHPP long-term cycling tests conducted at both −10° and 40°C.

DFT theoretical calculation

Geometric structures were optimized, and frequency analyses were performed at the B3LYP/6-311+ G** theoretical level, a reliable choice for organic molecular systems, incorporating Grimme’s D3(BJ) dispersion correction (EmpiricalDispersion = GD3BJ) to account for weak interactions, with implicit solvation effects accounted for through the self-consistent reaction field (SCRF) method using a water solvent model. Subsequent single-point energy evaluations were performed on the optimized geometric structures at the B3LYP/6-311+G(d,p) level, maintaining D3(BJ) dispersion correction and using Merz-Kollman (MK) population analysis (Pop = MK) with IOp(6/33 = 2) and IOp(6/42 = 6) parameters for precise charge distribution characterization. All molecular ESP analyses were conducted under the identical implicit solvent condition [SCRF(solvent = water)] used for the electronic structure calculations. The ESP data were processed using Multiwfn_3.8_dev to derive atomic charges, with subsequent visualization performed in Gaussian. On the basis of the same optimized structures and single-point energy results, frontier molecular orbital (HOMO-LUMO) analyses and visualizations were also carried out within Gaussian. For molecular dynamics simulations, the optimized molecular structures were solvated using PACKMOL to construct the solvent environment, with system topologies generated via sobtop_1.0 (dev5). The simulations followed a standard protocol: energy minimization, NVT (constant number, volume, temperature) equilibration (100 ps, 298 K), and NPT (constant number, pressure, temperature) equilibration (100 ps, 298 K, 1 bar) before the production run. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted with the GROMACS package and the leap-frog integrator at a 2-fs time step for 50,000,000 steps (100 ns total) under NPT ensemble conditions. Hydrogen bonds were constrained, and the Verlet cutoff scheme was applied with 1.2-nm cutoffs in the case of both van der Waals and short-range electrostatic interactions. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the particle mesh Ewald method. The system temperature was regulated using a Nosé-Hoover thermostat (298 K, τt = 1.0 ps), while pressure was controlled via a Berendsen barostat (1 bar, τp = 2.0 ps, isothermal compressibility 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1) with isotropic scaling. Periodic boundary conditions were implemented in all directions, and dispersion corrections were applied to the terms of energy and pressure.

Gas collection and analysis

The electrolytes on both sides of the battery were stored in separate sealed reservoirs. To monitor gas evolution during operation, the headspace gas outlet of each reservoir was connected via PTFE tubing to a gas analyzer. Before each experiment, the entire system (including the cell, tubing, and reservoirs) was purged with high-purity nitrogen (99.999%) at a flow rate of 30 ml min−1 for at least 30 min to establish a stable inert atmosphere. During operation, with the electrolytes circulating, gases generated by the reactions accumulated in the headspace of the reservoirs and were continuously carried by the nitrogen carrier gas (maintained at 5 ml min−1) into the gas analyzer for automatic, periodic sampling and analysis.

LCA simulation

This study uses Simapro 9.0 software to conduct a comparative environmental analysis, aiming to assess the environmental footprints of different flow battery stacks. The software enables modeling and analysis of products from a life cycle perspective, covering all stages from raw material extraction to production. It fully captures the resource consumption and environmental impact chains within the technical processes. In the specific analysis, we first converted the involved processes into quantifiable resource inputs, such as raw material consumption, water usage, and energy consumption, to generate a detailed life cycle inventory. Simultaneously, a comprehensive output inventory covering multiple media was constructed. The ReCiPe 2016 midpoint method was adopted to evaluate the GWP and further assess a range of comprehensive impact categories. To ensure fairness in comparisons between different processes and comparability of data, the functional unit was defined as providing 1 MWh of electrical energy over 10,000 cycles. A Monte Carlo analysis with 5000 iterations was performed to evaluate data uncertainty. The data used for this model are included in tables S7 to S16.
