Development of a machine learning-based model for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS): a retrospective observational study protocol
Authors: Robabe Seyedi, Mojgan Mirghafourvand, Jafar Tanha, Hamid Alavi Majd, Fatemeh Nahidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine condition in reproductive-aged women, which is associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes in pregnancy. In the present study, pregnancy complications—like gestational diabetes, preterm birth, hypertension, low birth weight and neonatal intensive care unit admission—will be predicted with high accuracy by machine learning-based prediction models.Methods and analysisThere are three substudies: (1) Developing a researcher-made questionnaire by literature review, data collection from existing medical records in maternal and neonatal systems of women with PCOS who have given birth, collected from hospitals and private clinics of East Azerbaijan Province and Tehran. Approximately 800–1000 women with PCOS will be included in the study and then data preprocessing will be performed. (2) Developing machine learning-based models for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS using decision trees, random forests, Extreme Gradient Boosting, support vector machines, k-nearest neighbours and neural network algorithms and (3) Developing a user-friendly application or interface that will operate on various devices. Based on model evaluation metrics, the model with the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS will be used as the final model.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran (Ethics Code: IR.SBMU.PHARMACY.REC.1404.017). Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conferences and social media and will also be shared with study participants.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-aged women, with a putative prevalence of 6−15% among them.1 PCOS is a complex syndrome characterised by hyperandrogenism, ovarian dysfunction and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound. PCOS is not just a reproductive disorder; it is a metabolic syndrome with health implications for women’s health throughout the reproductive lifespan and beyond.2 The diagnosis of PCOS is based on the demonstration of two or more of the following three criteria: menstrual irregularity associated with oligo-anovulation, hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound.3 PCOS is associated with numerous complications, including infertility due to anovulation, susceptibility to miscarriage, type 2 diabetes, obesity, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, irregular menstrual cycles, excessive vaginal bleeding, hirsutism, acne, baldness, depression, anxiety and reduced self-esteem. PCOS also increases the risk of endometrial cancer. The metabolic and hormonal changes brought about by PCOS not only affect reproductive health but also could lead to severe physical and psychological disease, thus requiring proper management and treatment through medication and lifestyle modification.46

Pregnant women with PCOS have an increased risk of developing unfavourable maternal and neonatal outcomes.7 Maternal complications are distinguished by the higher risk of miscarriage,8 increased risk of gestational diabetes,9 pregnancy-induced hypertension,10 increased susceptibility to preterm delivery,10 induction of labour,7 caesarean section and excessive gestational weight gain.11 Neonatal complications include small for gestational age infants,12 macrosomia, meconium aspiration syndrome, low 5-minute Apgar score, admission to neonatal intensive care unit (NICU)12 and neonatal mortality.13

These are not only dangerous to short-term maternal and child health but also have the potential for causing long-term health complications, higher healthcare costs and, in severe cases, maternal and neonatal mortality. Prevention through early detection and intervention is essential to reduce these risks and improve pregnancy outcomes.14 Because women with PCOS are at a higher risk of adverse pregnancy and neonatal outcomes, and because the conditions are common and severe, predicting pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS is a pressing requirement. Such information is of significant clinical usefulness in managing pregnancies among women with PCOS. Women with PCOS ought to be made aware of these elevated risks of pregnancy, and they ought to be closely monitored and treated. Screening for these complications is necessary during the preconception, pregnancy and labour periods.7

There are several classification models in statistical science, which can be categorised into classical (traditional) and modern (artificial intelligence (AI) based) models. Some of the classical models include logistic regression and discriminant analysis. Some AI methods for data classification include decision trees, artificial neural networks and other machine learning (ML) models.15 The process of modelling and analysis is determined by the type and number of variables and the particular provisions of the problem.

In the medical field, recent advancements have led to an explosion in the volume and complexity of biological data collected from individuals, laboratory tests, hospitals and environmental settings. The expansion of such data has created both new opportunities and challenges in clinical practice. Due to the enormous volume of information, analysing such data using traditional methods has become difficult for physicians, as it requires more advanced analytical techniques. Classical statistical methods for predicting pregnancy outcomes offer simplicity, interpretability, lower data requirements and the ability to identify principal risk factors. They are, nevertheless, poor at identifying complex patterns, sensitive to outliers, rely on exact statistical assumptions and tend to be less accurate than ML methods.16

Recent developments in AI and ML have been referred to as effective and innovative solutions across a variety of fields, including healthcare.17 AI is a branch of computer science that concentrates on creating machines and systems able to perform tasks typically requiring human intelligence, such as learning, problem-solving, natural language understanding and decision-making.18 ML, a subset of AI, enables machines to learn from data and improve their performance without explicit programming. ML, through algorithms and mathematical models, allows machines to identify complex patterns and correlations in data and make predictions.19 20

In obstetrics and gynaecology, AI will revolutionise the delivery of healthcare services. AI can make more accurate diagnoses and customised treatment plans by analysing large volumes of data, anticipating patterns and forecasting. AI can analyse medical images more quickly and detect abnormalities at an earlier stage. In addition, by processing patient information on a case-by-case basis, AI can customise treatment based on a patient’s medical history and lifestyle. However, for the successful use of AI in medicine, ethical concerns, privacy and transparency of algorithms must be addressed and collaboration between healthcare providers and developers is crucial.21 22 ML has been widely used worldwide to predict the mode of delivery and assess the risk of potential maternal complications during pregnancy. However, after a literature review, there was just one Romanian study directly discussing the prediction of an unfavourable pregnancy outcome in women with PCOS.23 That article was underlined with several limitations, that is, small sample size, single-site recruitment within one local hospital and no useful tools usable in clinical practice. The present work aims to overcome these limitations by examining a large sample of pregnant women with PCOS, collecting additional variables and developing more generalisable and accurate data to inform the development of an ML model. Further, by creating an easy-to-adopt interface, we will aim to apply the predictive model in the clinics to forecast adverse pregnancy outcomes like gestational hypertension (GH), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), preterm labour (PTB), low birth weight (LBW) and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission in women with PCOS.

Study population

Inclusion criteria: Pregnant women with a PCOS diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria: Congenital anomalies detected after birth, records lacking complete information.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcomes of this study will include GDM, GH, PTB, LBW and NICU admission. All outcomes will be identified using standardised guideline-based definitions and extracted from electronic medical records and hospital databases.

To enhance reproducibility and minimise outcome misclassification, each outcome will be determined from documented physician diagnoses in the medical records and verified against internationally accepted diagnostic criteria and clinical practice guidelines.

Gestational diabetes mellitus

GDM will be defined as glucose intolerance or hyperglycaemia first identified during pregnancy in women without previously diagnosed diabetes. Diagnosis will be based on a 75 g OGTT, with one or more of the following plasma glucose values meeting or exceeding the diagnostic thresholds: fasting plasma glucose ≥92 mg/dL (5.1 mmol/L), 1-hour plasma glucose ≥180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥153 mg/dL (8.5 mmol/L). Women with a documented diagnosis of GDM in their medical records will be classified as positive cases.24 25

Gestational hypertension

GH will be defined as new-onset hypertension occurring after 20 weeks of gestation in a previously normotensive woman. Hypertension will be defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg on at least two measurements obtained at least 4 hours apart. Women with a documented diagnosis of gestational hypertension in their medical records will be classified as positive cases.26

Preterm birth

PTB will be defined as any delivery occurring before 37 completed weeks of gestation (<37+0 weeks), regardless of the mode of delivery or the underlying cause.27

Low birth weight

LBW will be defined according to the WHO as a birth weight of less than 2500 g measured within the first hours after birth, regardless of gestational age.28

NICU admission

NICU admission will be defined as admission of a liveborn neonate to an NICU at any time during the initial birth hospitalisation for specialised monitoring, diagnostic evaluation or therapeutic intervention. This outcome will be recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) based on hospital documentation.

All outcomes will be retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records using a standardised data abstraction form. Outcome ascertainment will be based on both documented clinical diagnoses and objective clinical measurements recorded in the medical records whenever available.

Sample size

The sample size will be determined with consideration of contemporary recommendations for prediction model development rather than relying solely on the traditional ‘10 events per predictor variable’ rule.29

Approximately 40 candidate predictor variables are expected to be included in model development. Five adverse pregnancy outcomes will be investigated, including GDM, GH, PTB, LBW and NICU admission.

Based on previous studies among women with PCOS, the expected frequencies of these outcomes are estimated to range from approximately 5–20%, depending on the specific adverse pregnancy outcome under investigation.30 Assuming an initial sample size of 800–1000 women, the anticipated number of outcome events would range from approximately 50 events for LBW to 200 events for GDM.

Because ML algorithms such as RF, XGBoost, SVM and artificial neural network may require larger effective sample sizes than conventional regression models, outcome-specific event frequencies will be continuously evaluated during data collection. The adequacy of the sample size for each outcome will be assessed in accordance with contemporary recommendations for prediction model development, considering the number of outcome events, model complexity and the risk of overfitting.31

Furthermore, adverse pregnancy outcomes are not mutually exclusive. A single participant may experience more than one outcome (eg, PTB and LBW), allowing the same clinical record to contribute information to multiple prediction models. This overlap increases the efficiency of data utilisation across outcomes.

Outcome frequencies will be monitored continuously during data collection. For each adverse pregnancy outcome, data collection will continue until a sufficient number of outcome events is obtained to support reliable model development and internal validation while minimising the risk of overfitting.

If the observed number of events for any outcome is insufficient to support stable model development and internal validation, additional medical records will be reviewed until an adequate number of outcome events is achieved. Therefore, the proposed sample size of 800–1000 participants should be considered an initial target rather than a fixed upper limit.

This adaptive sampling strategy is intended to ensure sufficient outcome events for reliable model training, hyperparameter tuning and internal validation while minimising the risk of overfitting and optimistic model performance estimates.

Data collection

Historical data from maternal and neonatal records, the hospital information system and archived files of women with PCOS from hospitals and private clinics in the provinces of East Azerbaijan and Tehran will be collected. These data will be recorded using a researcher-developed questionnaire designed based on a systematic literature review. To minimise the risk of error or bias during data transfer, preventive measures will be implemented, including standardising data-extraction protocols, training and calibrating data extractors, defining objective variables and conducting random audits of questionnaire entries.34 35

Written informed consent will not be obtained due to the retrospective nature of the study; all data will be anonymised prior to analysis.

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing will be conducted prior to model development using a predefined analytical pipeline to ensure data quality, consistency and reproducibility across all ML models. Data cleaning procedures will include the identification and correction of missing, noisy, inconsistent, duplicate and biologically implausible values.36

Missing data: The proportion and pattern of missingness will first be assessed. Variables with substantial missingness (>20%) will be considered for exclusion following evaluation of their clinical importance. For variables with ≤20% missing data, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations will be applied as the primary imputation method under the assumption of missing at random. Predictive mean matching will be used for continuous variables, while logistic or multinomial regression models will be used for categorical variables. This approach was selected to preserve variable distributions and multivariable relationships while minimising bias. Simple deletion and single imputation methods (eg, mean or mode imputation) will not be used in the primary analysis.37 38

Noisy and inconsistent data: All variables will be screened according to predefined clinical and logical ranges relevant to women with PCOS and pregnancy outcomes. Implausible values (eg, unrealistic maternal age, weight or gestational age) will be verified against source records when available; otherwise, they will be treated as missing values and handled using the predefined imputation procedure. Outliers will be evaluated using the IQR method (1.5×IQR rule). Extreme values will be managed using appropriate statistical techniques, including winsorisation where applicable, to reduce their influence while preserving sample size and data integrity.39

Data reduction: To reduce redundancy and multicollinearity without compromising clinically relevant information, correlations among continuous variables will be assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. When two variables demonstrate a high correlation (r>0.85), one variable will be considered for removal based on clinical relevance, data completeness and predictive contribution. Because maintaining clinical interpretability is a priority, dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis will not be applied in the primary analysis.40

Data transformation: Continuous variables will be normalised using the Min–Max normalisation method to rescale features to a common range prior to model training. Categorical variables will be encoded using one-hot encoding when required by the selected algorithm. Feature engineering will be limited to clinically meaningful variables derived from existing maternal and pregnancy-related characteristics.41

The same preprocessing pipeline will be applied consistently across all ML models, including DT, RF, XGBoost, SVM, KNN and NN, to ensure comparability, reproducibility and transparency of model performance.

Substudy 2

Developing an ML model to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes for women with PCOS.

Developing ML models involves developing and training a prediction model on the given data and ML algorithms. After data collection, data preprocessing and data preparation, the subsequent steps will include selecting the correct algorithm, training the model, testing and tuning. The primary goal will be to develop a model that will uncover the latent patterns in the data and provide accurate and reliable predictions. One of the simplest actions in model development will be to partition the dataset into two major subsets: training data and test data. Proper separation will be essential to ensure the model generalises well to both training and unseen datasets.42 In this study, we will develop separate binary models for each adverse pregnancy outcome rather than a single multiclass model. This strategy will be chosen because the outcomes differ in their underlying risk factors and prevalence, and independent models allow for more precise calibration, more straightforward clinical interpretability and separate validation of predictive performance for each outcome (AUROC, sensitivity, specificity). This approach will be selected over multilabel or multitask modelling because each outcome has distinct clinical mechanisms and risk profiles in women with PCOS, making joint modelling less appropriate in the current phase. Although multilabel approaches may be considered in future work, single-outcome models will be more suitable in the current phase given the clinical and methodological advantages.43

The 70–30 split between training and test sets will be adopted as a commonly used approach in ML studies to balance the need for sufficient training data while retaining an adequate number of samples for model evaluation.44

Model evaluation and validation methods

Evaluation metrics will include accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and the AUROC. Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly classified observations among all observations and provides an overall measure of classification performance.45 Precision indicates the proportion of true positive predictions among all positive predictions,46 while recall measures the ability of the model to correctly identify positive cases.47 The F1-score, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, will be used to provide a balanced assessment of model performance, particularly in the presence of outcome imbalance.48 AUROC will be used as the primary metric for comparing the discriminative performance of the ML models, with higher values indicating better ability to distinguish between women with and without adverse pregnancy outcomes.49

For model development, the dataset will be divided into training (70%) and test (30%) sets. The training dataset will be used to develop the ML models, while the test dataset will be reserved exclusively for independent evaluation of model performance.50 This approach allows assessment of model generalisability and provides an unbiased estimate of predictive performance on unseen data.

To optimise model performance and reduce the risk of overfitting, K-fold cross-validation (k=5) will be performed within the training dataset. The average performance across the validation folds will be used to select the optimal hyperparameters for each ML algorithm. Hyperparameter tuning will be conducted using a grid-search strategy.5154

The final performance of each model will then be evaluated on the independent test dataset using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and AUROC. The model with the highest AUROC, together with clinically acceptable sensitivity and specificity, will be selected as the final prediction model.

To further minimise overfitting, algorithm-specific regularisation techniques will be applied where appropriate. For example, tree-based models will be optimised through pruning and maximum-depth restriction, XGBoost models through regularisation parameters and neural network models through early stopping and dropout methods.

Bootstrap resampling will be used as a supplementary internal validation approach to assess the stability and robustness of model performance estimates.37 Ensemble learning methods, including bagging and boosting approaches such as Random Forest and XGBoost, will be evaluated as part of the candidate ML algorithms rather than as separate validation techniques.5557

Substudy 3

Design of a user-friendly application or interface compatible with various devices.

Initially, based on model evaluation criteria, the algorithm with the highest AUROC for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS (including gestational diabetes, preterm birth, hypertension, LBW and NICU admission) will be selected as the final model. A user-friendly application or interface will then be designed to ensure compatibility across devices (desktop, tablet, mobile). This interface will allow healthcare providers to enter patient data and receive predictive insights from the finalised ML model.58

Steps to be implemented while designing UI

Step 1: System objectives and requirements analysis.

First, the target users, their requirements and the key system features will be determined. The system will enable patient management, medical data input, visualisation of machine-learning model predictions and delivery of personalised recommendations. A careful requirements analysis will form the basis of creating a functional and usable interface.59

Step 2: Medical dashboard and user experience (UX) design development.

The system’s dashboard will display clinical information, laboratory results and AI model predictions both graphically and textually. The design of this section will be informed by Heuristic Evaluation and Cognitive Walkthrough principles to ensure that doctors and midwives can easily view, interpret and analyse patient data.60

Step 3: User interaction and data entry form design.

Data entry forms will be created to ensure accurate, reliable input and reduce user errors. Usability testing, such as the Think-Aloud Protocol and User Testing, will be used to ensure that the form design is user-centred and facilitates easy data entry.61

Step 4: Presentation of predictions and results of the AI model.

Following data entry, the ML model will generate predictive outputs. The findings will be interpretable and understandable to non-technical users. Displaying information both graphically and textually, with clear explanations, will make it easier to use and more transparent.

Step 5: System security and cross-device compatibility.

The user interface will be responsive for proper rendering on mobile devices, tablets and desktops. Information security will be provided by Secure Sockets Layer/Transport Layer Security encryption protocols, secure authentication and secure data storage in trusted cloud servers.62

In addition to the initial UI/UX feasibility study, an exclusive research study will be conducted to validate the user interface model following the design process. In this phase, the interface will be tested with real data and intended users to assess usability, the validity of predictive outputs and user interaction with the system. This sequential validation will ensure the UI/UX design is optimal, based not only on theoretical concepts but also on real user experience and the model’s actual performance.

Strengths and limitations

The study will use an extensive data set comprising demographic information, detailed pregnancy histories and prenatal records. Using multiple sources of diverse data will provide a complete picture of each pregnancy, enhancing the model’s predictive capacity. The study will compare a wide range of advanced ML models, including DT, RF, XGBoost, SVM, KNN and NN. By analysing multiple algorithms and employing ensemble methods, the research will identify the most effective means of predicting pregnancy outcomes. Various outcomes will be analysed to facilitate early decision-making for healthcare professionals, thereby improving the quality of care and reducing complications. The development of an AI-based application that integrates the predictive model into a UI will also be a key focus. However, the study has certain limitations, including incomplete medical record information, limited generalisability to other populations and limited acceptance of AI systems in medical settings.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this retrospective observational study. The study is based solely on analysis of existing medical records, and therefore no direct patient input was applicable.
