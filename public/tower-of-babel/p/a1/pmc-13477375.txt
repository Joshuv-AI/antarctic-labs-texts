Impact of Intimal Tear Location on Clinical Outcomes in Extensive Arch Repair of Acute Stanford Type A Aortic Dissection
Authors: Yumeng Ji, Chenyu Zhou, Kai Zhang, Wei Gao, Bin Hou, Ji Wang, Shiqi Gao, Kai Xu, Xiaogang Sun, Cuntao Yu, Juntao Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of intimal tear location on outcomes in acute Stanford type A aortic dissection remains unclear. This study investigated the impact of different tear locations on early and long‐term death in patients undergoing total arch replacement with frozen elephant trunk.MethodsThis single‐center retrospective study included 1524 patients with acute Stanford type A aortic dissection who underwent total arch replacement with frozen elephant trunk from January 2010 to December 2022. Patients were stratified by primary entry tear location: ascending aorta (n=857 [56.2%]), aortic arch (n=459 [30.1%]), and descending aorta (n=208 [13.6%]). Primary end points were in‐hospital death and long‐term survival. Multivariable regression analyses with further sensitivity analyses for early and long‐term outcomes were performed.ResultsIn‐hospital death differed significantly among groups (P=0.035): descending tear (21 [10.1%]) versus ascending (46 [5.4%]) versus arch (24 [5.2%]). Multivariable analysis identified descending tear as an independent predictor of in‐hospital death (odds ratio, 3.728 [95% CI, 1.934–7.188]; P<0.001). During a median follow‐up period of 48.2 months, descending tear location remained independently associated with increased long‐term mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR], 2.140 [95% CI, 1.418–3.230]; P<0.001), while arch tears showed no significant difference from ascending tears (HR, 1.232; P=0.227). Reintervention rates did not differ among groups after multivariable adjustment.ConclusionsIntimal tear location significantly impacts acute Stanford type A aortic dissection prognosis. Descending aortic tears confer substantially higher early and long‐term mortality rates, requiring aggressive perioperative management and closer surveillance. Tear location should be incorporated into risk stratification systems for individualized treatment planning.

Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) represents one of the most life‐threatening cardiovascular emergencies, with an extremely high mortality rate of ≈1% to 2% per hour. 1 Despite significant advances in diagnosis and treatment, this condition remains characterized by complex pathophysiological features, with a high acute mortality rate and considerable long‐term mortality rate among survivors. 2 Surgery remains the preferred treatment for the vast majority of patients with ATAAD, yet despite continuous advances in surgical techniques, perioperative mortality rates remain as high as 6% to 20%. 3 , 4 , 5

In recent years, with advances in imaging diagnostic technology and deepening understanding of ATAAD pathophysiology, researchers have gradually recognized that the location and extent of the intimal tear are critical factors determining patient prognosis. 6 , 7 The traditional Stanford classification is primarily based on whether the ascending aorta is involved, but this classification system cannot adequately reflect the complexity of the disease and prognostic differences. 8 Mounting evidence suggests that tear location not only affects hemodynamic changes but also directly relates to surgical strategy selection, surgical complexity, and patient short‐ and long‐term prognosis. 9 , 10

Despite growing recognition of tear location's prognostic significance, large‐scale studies comparing outcomes across different anatomic tear sites under standardized treatment protocols remain limited. This paucity of data is particularly pronounced for descending aortic tears, a relatively uncommon but potentially high‐risk subtype that warrants dedicated investigation. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive single‐center analysis to systematically evaluate how intimal tear location (ascending aorta, aortic arch, or descending aorta) impacts early and long‐term mortality outcomes in patients with ATAAD treated with a uniform surgical approach under standardized protocols.

Methods

All detailed data included in the study are available upon request by contact with the corresponding author.

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (No. 2023‐2084; August 20, 2023), and written informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Study Population and Data Collection

This was a single‐center retrospective cohort study conducted at Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College between January 2010 and December 2022. Given the long study period, patients were further stratified according to the year of surgery into 2 eras: 2010 to 2016 and 2017 to 2022, to account for potential temporal changes in surgical techniques and perioperative management. All patients diagnosed with extensive ATAAD involving the ascending aorta, aortic arch, and descending aorta (DeBakey type I) who underwent a total arch replacement (TAR) with frozen elephant trunk (FET) procedure. Exclusion criteria were (1) patients with poor‐quality or unclear imaging studies that precluded accurate assessment; (2) patients with indeterminate or unidentifiable primary entry tear location; (3) patients with previous open cardiac surgery; and (4) patients with prior thoracic endovascular aortic repair.

Primary Entry Tear Classification

All patients underwent emergency computed tomographic angiography upon admission to localize the primary entry tear. Tear location was independently assessed and confirmed by both radiologists and cardiovascular surgeons. Patients were stratified into 3 groups: Ascending aorta group: primary entry tear located in the aortic sinus or ascending aorta proximal to the origin of the brachiocephalic artery.Aortic arch group: primary entry tear located in the aortic arch between the origins of the brachiocephalic artery and left subclavian artery.Descending aorta group: primary entry tear located distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery.

For patients with multiple tears, classification was based on the largest tear by cross‐sectional area. When primary tears extended across anatomic landmarks, classification was determined by the location containing the predominant tear area.

Surgical Procedure

All patients underwent TAR with FET via median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass, using standardized techniques previously described. 11 The right axillary artery was the preferred site for arterial cannulation. Cardiopulmonary bypass was terminated once the target core temperature was achieved (18 °C for the first 5 years, then 25 °C for the following 7 years), and unilateral or bilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion was established and maintained at a flow rate of 8 to 12 mL/kg per min. The aortic arch was transected between the left common carotid artery and left subclavian artery. The FET was inserted into the descending aorta. Prosthetic graft and FET (MicroPort Medical Company Limited) sizing was based on preoperative distal aortic diameter measurements, typically 26 to 28 mm, with matching dimensions for both graft and stent components. FET length selection (100/120/150 mm) was individualized on the basis of patient height and the distance between the left common carotid artery and T8 vertebral level to minimize spinal cord ischemic risk, with distal anchoring positioned at T6 to T7. Lower body perfusion was subsequently restored through the dedicated perfusion branch of the tetrafurcate graft. During the rewarming phase, sequential anastomoses of the innominate artery, left common carotid artery, and left subclavian artery were performed. Finally, proximal reconstruction was completed with end‐to‐end anastomosis of the tetrafurcate graft to the ascending aorta or repaired aortic root, as clinically indicated.

Definition and Outcomes

Preoperative malperfusion was classified according to the type, entry location, and malperfusion (TEM) system: Coronary malperfusion (M1) was defined as dissection involving at least 1 main coronary artery. M1 (+) was diagnosed when evidence of myocardial ischemia was present, including electrocardiographic changes or abnormal cardiac biomarkers. Cerebral or upper extremity malperfusion (M2) was defined as dissection involving at least 1 supra‐aortic vessel or collapse of the true lumen in the aortic arch. M2 (+) was identified when clinical manifestations of cerebral or upper extremity ischemia were documented, such as stroke, pulse deficit, limb pain, pallor, or paresthesia. Visceral, renal, or lower extremity malperfusion (M3) was defined as dissection or false‐lumen origin of at least 1 visceral, renal, or iliac artery or true‐lumen collapse resulting in functional obstruction of these branch vessels. M3 (+) was diagnosed when clinical evidence of organ ischemia was present, including abdominal pain, ileus, or bloody diarrhea for bowel ischemia, anuria, or signs of renal failure for renal malperfusion, and pulse deficit, pain, pallor, or paresthesia for lower extremity ischemia. M1, M2, and M3 are not mutually exclusive. The primary end point was all‐cause death. The secondary end point was cardiovascular‐related reintervention, including both open surgical and endovascular procedures.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and expressed as mean±SD for normally distributed variables or median with interquartile range for nonnormally distributed variables. For 3‐group comparisons, 1‐way ANOVA was used for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test was applied to nonnormally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. Multiple comparisons were conducted for variables showing significant differences, with P values adjusted using Bonferroni's modification. Long‐term survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. For reoperation analysis, competing risk models using the Fine–Gray method were used, with death as the competing event. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify risk factors for early death. For long‐term outcomes, the proportional hazards assumption was first assessed using weighted Schoenfeld residuals, with no violations observed (P>0.05). Cox proportional hazards regression and competing risk Cox regression were performed to explore risk factors for long‐term death and reoperation, respectively.

To further validate the impact of dissection entry on early death, we conducted a comprehensive sensitivity analysis using matching weights analysis of propensity scores followed by conditional logistic regression. The matching weights analysis was performed using the R “VGAM” package for multinomial logistic regression (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to generate propensity scores, and the “survey” package for weighted analysis. To compare the baseline, intraoperative, and postoperative characteristics among the 3 groups after matching weights analysis, weighted statistical tests were used applying the “survey” package. Continuous variables were compared using weighted ANOVA or weighted Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Rao–Scott χ2 test. Conditional logistic regression was performed to further control for potential intraoperative confounding factors using the “survey” package with the svyglm function.

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R version 4.2.1 and Excel version 2402 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). A 2‐tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Clinical Characteristics

A total of 1524 patients with ATAAD were included and stratified by primary tear location: ascending aortic tears (n=857 [56.2%]), arch tears (n=459 [30.1%]), and descending aortic tears (n=208 [13.6%]) (Figure 1).

Baseline characteristics differed significantly among the 3 groups (Table 1). The arch tear group was the oldest (49.96±10.03 versus 47.20±11.23 versus 46.06±9.14 years, P<0.001). Multivariable logistic regression identified descending tear location as an independent predictor of in‐hospital death (odds ratio [OR], 3.618 [95% CI, 1.873–6.99]; P<0.001), while arch and ascending tear locations showed comparable mortality risks (OR, 1.087; P=0.789; Figure 2). Male sex conferred protection (OR, 0.394). Univariable and multivariable logistic regression results are detailed in Table S3.

Long‐Term Outcomes

Clinical follow‐up was obtained through outpatient visits and structured telephone interviews. Follow‐up was complete for all surviving patients, yielding a 100% follow‐up completion rate. Over a median follow‐up of 48.2 months, 238 deaths and 133 reinterventions occurred. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed the lowest survival among patients with a descending tear and highest among patients with an ascending tear, with arch tears demonstrating intermediate survival (P=0.074; Figure 3A).

Long‐term survival and reintervention outcomes by tear location.

A, Long‐term survival curves for the 3 patient groups with different tear locations. B, Competing risk model for death and reintervention.

Cox proportional hazards modeling confirmed tear location as an independent long‐term survival predictor across sequential models (Table 4). Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and period; in this model, descending tears were associated with an increased mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR], 1.685 [95% CI, 1.14–2.491]; P=0.009). Model 2 further adjusted for preoperative variables, and the association strengthened (HR, 1.884 [95% CI, 1.256–2.826]; P=0.002). Model 3 was the fully adjusted model, including preoperative and intraoperative variables, and the association remained robust (HR, 2.075 [95% CI, 1.370–3.142]; P=0.001). Conversely, arch tears showed no significant survival difference compared with ascending tears across all models (all P>0.05). In the fully adjusted model, additional independent predictors included aorta–femoral artery bypass (HR, 2.310; P=0.008), pericardial effusion (HR, 2.550; P=0.001), TEM‐M3 without symptoms (HR, 1.735; P=0.003), and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time (HR, 1.005 per minute; P<0.001). Complete multivariable Cox results are provided in Table S4.

Independent reintervention predictors included Marfan syndrome (HR, 3.704; P<0.001), the Wheat procedure (HR, 4.136; P=0.006), TEM‐M1 with syndrome (HR, 2.223; P=0.016), and prolonged deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (HR, 1.034 per minute; P=0.041), while advancing age (HR, 0.966; P=0.003) and Bentall operation (HR, 0.449; P=0.015) conferred protection.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were further conducted to validate the findings. Following matching weights analysis of propensity score, preoperative baseline characteristics achieved optimal balance across groups (Figure S1). In‐hospital mortality rates remained consistently highest in the descending tear group (9.6%), compared with 4.7% for ascending tears and 3.1% for arch tears, with statistically significant intergroup differences persisting (P=0.001). Major perioperative complications remained nonsignificant across matched groups (Tables S6–S8). Conditional logistic regression confirmed descending tear location as an independent mortality predictor (OR, 2.402 [95% CI, 1.111–5.193]; P=0.026), validating our primary findings (Table S9). Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding patients with multiple entry tears. The results of univariable and multivariable logistic regression, as well as Cox proportional hazards and competing risk regression analyses for reintervention, remained consistent with the primary analyses, further supporting the robustness of our findings (Tables S10–12).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that intimal tear location significantly influences outcomes in patients with ATAAD undergoing TAR with FET, with descending aortic tears conferring substantially elevated early and long‐term mortality risks as an independent prognostic factor.

Contemporary ATAAD management has achieved considerable clinical success through decades of surgical, anesthetic, perfusion, and critical care innovations. 1 , 3 In our cohort, early death was 6%, with permanent neurological dysfunction in 3.2%, spinal cord injury in 3.7%, reoperation for bleeding in 3.3%, and acute kidney injury in 26.2%. Notably, no significant differences in spinal cord injury rates were observed among the different tear location groups, which may reflect the effectiveness of our individualized FET length selection strategy combined with standardized organ protection protocols in mitigating spinal cord ischemia risk. Five‐year survival and freedom from reintervention were 88.2% and 94.7%, with 10‐year rates of 82.9% and 86.9%, demonstrating satisfactory clinical outcomes. However, literature examining tear location's prognostic impact remains limited and controversial. Takahashi et al 12 reached opposing conclusions in 196 patients with ATAAD: While tear location was not independently associated with death, ascending aortic tears independently predicted preoperative neurological dysfunction and demonstrated significantly worse long‐term survival compared with other locations (P<0.01). The discrepancy may be explained by differences in surgical strategy and baseline characteristics. Takahashi et al used a tear‐oriented approach, and distal entry tears were often not resected due to limited surgical access, whereas all patients in our cohort underwent TAR with FET, allowing more consistent treatment of distal pathology. Additionally, the small number of descending tears and worse preoperative status in the ascending group in their study may have influenced their findings. Our findings align more closely with Merkle's observations, 7 revealing significant intergroup mortality differences (P=0.035), with descending tears demonstrating the highest in‐hospital death (10.1%), nearly double that of ascending (5.4%) and arch tears (5.2%).

Prior research indicates that tear location and morphology significantly influence aortic dissection hemodynamics. 9 Larger entry tears typically increase false lumen flow, predisposing the proximal descending aorta and reentry sites to rupture. Proximal ascending tears may increase false lumen pressure and compress the true lumen, elevating retrograde dissection risk. However, our data demonstrate that descending aortic tears confer the highest mortality rate, with this association persisting after comprehensive preoperative matching. Several factors may explain this phenomenon. First, as one of China's largest tertiary aortic centers treating patients nationwide, patients with a hemodynamically unstable ascending tear may not survive transport to our institution. Second, the complex pathophysiology of retrograde ATAAD with descending tears severely compromises the true lumen, risking inadvertent FET deployment into the false lumen with consequent severe malperfusion and death. Among our descending tear fatalities, 9 patients (42.9%) died from multiorgan failure, potentially attributable to false lumen stent deployment.

Patients with a descending tear demonstrated higher male prevalence, elevated body mass index, and increased hypertension compared with other groups. However, neither hypertension nor body mass index emerged as independent risk factors, while male sex proved protective against surgical death. Sex's impact on type A dissection outcomes remains controversial across institutions. Gasser et al found no sex difference in time from onset to surgery but worse 30‐day and long‐term female survival. 13 Conversely, a study of 633 ATAAD cases across 3 cardiac centers reported no significant sex impact on operative death or long‐term survival. 14 Our center's prior large‐scale study demonstrated that male sex serves as a protective factor against surgical death, consistent with the findings of this study. 5 Interestingly, long‐term reintervention rates differed significantly among groups, with patients with a descending tear demonstrating the highest rates. This likely reflects the fact that TAR with FET completely excises ascending and arch tears, while descending tears are merely covered by FET, leaving the tear permanently present. Elevated false lumen pressure may precipitate endoleaks or progressive distal aortic dilatation, ultimately requiring surgical intervention. However, after adjustment using a competing risk model with death as the competing event, tear location was no longer an independent predictor of reintervention. One possible explanation is the higher early and late mortality rates observed in patients with descending entry tears, as some patients may die before developing distal aortic progression that would otherwise necessitate reintervention. In addition, the use of TAR combined with FET may partially mitigate the adverse impact of a descending entry tear by promoting false lumen thrombosis and favorable distal aortic remodeling. Consequently, despite the theoretical anatomic disadvantage, the long‐term reintervention rates in our cohort remained substantially lower than those reported in previous studies, further supporting the durability of this surgical strategy. 15 , 16 , 17

The current mainstream aortic surgical risk model, GERAADA (German Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection Type A) 18 does not incorporate tear location. Our findings suggest significant risk stratification value for this parameter. Descending aortic tears not only increase surgical death but also elevate long‐term death risk. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed the lowest long‐term survival among descending tear patients, while Cox modeling confirmed the tear location's independent impact on long‐term survival, with descending tears presenting the most pronounced risk (HR, 2.075 [95% CI, 1.370–3.142]; P<0.001). Contemporary research emphasizes perioperative management while postoperative dissection follow‐up remains internationally inadequate. Epidemiological surveys reveal that 10% of patients never return for follow‐up, with >50% lost after 28 months. 19 A large single‐center series similarly reported long‐term follow‐up completion rates of only 75% for ATAAD, 20 reflecting widespread challenges in maintaining postdischarge surveillance continuity. Despite obtaining complete follow‐up data through substantial effort, our protocols remain relatively basic. Based on current findings, patients with a descending tear clearly warrant enhanced surveillance attention.

Limitations

This single‐center retrospective study has inherent limitations. Patients with indeterminate preoperative computed tomographic angiography tear localization were necessarily excluded. Subjective imaging interpretation represents another constraint; however, independent dual‐physician review maximized accuracy. TAR with FET constitutes our standardized approach and may not reflect protocols at other centers. Nevertheless, this uniform surgical strategy enabled meaningful comparative analysis. One additional limitation of this study relates to the nature of our institution as a national tertiary referral center. Many patients are transferred from other hospitals, and hemodynamically unstable patients, particularly those with ascending aortic entry tears, may not survive transport to our center. Consequently, the ascending tear group in this cohort may represent a selected population of survivors, which could potentially underestimate the true mortality rate associated with ascending tears and introduce survivorship bias into the comparison of outcomes among tear locations.

Conclusions

Intimal tear location represents a crucial prognostic determinant in patients with ATAAD undergoing TAR with FET. Descending aortic tears warrant intensified perioperative management and enhanced long‐term surveillance. Tear location merits incorporation into future risk assessment algorithms to optimize individualized treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.
