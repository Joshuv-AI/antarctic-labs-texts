Leukemia cutis of the scalp as a first manifestation of B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia: an unusual presentation
Authors: Bahareh Abtahi-Naeini, Zahra Pourmoghaddas, Maryam Derakhshan, Alireza Moafi, Fateme Ahmadinia, Elaheh Foroughi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeukemia cutis (LC) is an extramedullary manifestation of leukemia that occurs when neoplastic cells infiltrate the epidermis, dermis, or subcutaneous tissue of the skin. The clinical presentation of LC can vary significantly and is typically a late sign in advanced disease. In this case report, we describe a diagnostically challenging case of LC in a 10-year-old girl who initially presented with a solitary morphea-like plaque on her scalp, which ultimately led to the diagnosis of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-cell ALL).Case presentationWe present a challenging case of a 10-year-old female who initially presented with a scarring alopecic, morphea-like plaque on the right parietal scalp. Over two months, the lesion proved unresponsive to high-dose topical corticosteroid therapy and demonstrated progressive enlargement. The clinical course was further complicated by the emergence of systemic symptoms, including recurrent fever and significant cervical lymphadenopathy, which prompted referral for further evaluation. A definitive diagnosis of B-cell ALL was established based on congruent histopathological and immunohistochemical profiles from biopsies of the scalp lesion and cervical lymph nodes, which were confirmed by bone marrow examination. The patient achieved complete remission of the cutaneous lesion and systemic disease following one month of chemotherapy according to the Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster (BFM) protocol.ConclusionGiven the highly variable clinical presentations of leukemia cutis, this case highlights the critical necessity of including it in the differential diagnosis of atypical sclerotic scalp lesions in children. A strong clinical suspicion is required for the timely diagnosis of uncommon cutaneous presentations of underlying hematologic malignancy. Appropriate biopsy and the prompt initiation of systemic chemotherapy are paramount for achieving favorable outcomes and remission.Keywords: Leukemia cutis, B-Cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, B-cell ALL

Introduction

Leukemia cutis (LC) is an extramedullary manifestation of leukemia, characterized by the infiltration of neoplastic cells into the skin’s epidermis, dermis, or subcutaneous tissue [1]. Its clinical presentation is highly heterogeneous, ranging from hemorrhagic or erythematous nodules, plaques, and papules to more rare morphologies such as vesicles and bullae [2, 3]. Common sites for LC include the limbs, face, back, and trunk [4].

The frequency of leukemia cutis varies between 2% and 30%, depending on the type of leukemia, and it can appear simultaneously with the presentation of leukemia [1]. LC is most often a late-onset sign in advanced disease; in only 2–3% of LC cases, skin lesions precede systemic symptoms, making the diagnosis more challenging [1].

As scalp involvement is rarely reported, early diagnosis can be challenging and may be mistaken for other dermatologic disorders. Herein, we report the case of a 10-year-old girl who presented with a solitary morphea-like plaque on the scalp as the primary manifestation of B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-cell ALL), posing a significant diagnostic challenge.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old Iranian girl, the second child of non-consanguineous parents, was referred to our tertiary care center with a one-week history of fever and a 10-day history of progressively enlarging cervical swelling. The patient had no prior history of hospitalization. Her neurodevelopmental milestones, including speech, motor skills, and academic performance, were age-appropriate according to WHO standards. She had a four-month history of a solitary 3 × 3 cm cutaneous lesion on the right parietal scalp, accompanied by alopecia. An initial incisional biopsy of the lesion was reported as morphea profunda, based on histological findings of deep dermal and subcutaneous fibrosis, loss of adnexal structures, and a sparse lymphocytic infiltrate. Consequently, high-potency topical steroids (clobetasol (BD) and megacort (BD)) were prescribed. Despite two months of steroidal treatment, the lesion failed to improve and continued to enlarge progressively. Three months post-diagnosis, she developed systemic symptoms, including recurrent fever, neck pain, and a significant increase in neck swelling, prompting her referral to our center.

On physical examination, the scalp lesion presented as a 6 × 7 cm firm, indurated plaque with scarring alopecia extending to the vertex. There was no evidence of atrophy or a depressed region (Fig. 1). A thorough full-body skin examination revealed no other papules, nodules, or patches elsewhere. Multiple, firm, fixed, and non-tender lymphadenopathies were palpated bilaterally in the cervical (levels II-V), supraclavicular, and submandibular regions, with the largest conglomerate mass on the right side. Neurological, rheumatological, and cardiological examinations were unremarkable.

Clinical photograph showing a firm, indurated, morphea-like plaque measuring 6 × 7 cm on the right parietal scalp with scarring alopecia extending to the vertex

Given the progression despite therapy, the development of fever, and the appearance of significant lymphadenopathy, the initial diagnosis of morphea was reconsidered and led to further evaluations for lymphoproliferative disorders, infectious disease, and autoinflammatory disorders. Initial labs revealed elevated inflammatory markers (ESR: 85 mm/h, CRP: 45 mg/L). The CBC showed a normal white blood cell count with no blasts identified on the peripheral blood smear. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated at 550 U/L. Serological tests for HBV, HCV, HIV, EBV, CMV, Brucella, and QuantiFERON-TB Gold were negative. Neck CT and ultrasound confirmed bilateral multiple conglomerate necrotic lymph nodes in right zones II-V, supraclavicular area, and smaller reactive nodes on the left. CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed no mediastinal involvement, organomegaly, or retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy. Subsequently, an excisional biopsy of the right scalp lesion and the largest right cervical lymph node (under general anesthesia) was performed. The repeated histopathological examination of the skin sample suggested a diagnosis of B-cell acute leukemia/lymphoma. Bone marrow aspiration revealed more than 90% lymphoblasts. Multifocal flow cytometry confirmed B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, characterized by the expression of CD10 (31% in blast gate), TdT, CD19, CD20, CD34, CD38, HLA-DR, CD79a, and PAX5, and revealed a high Ki-67 proliferative index. Given the bone marrow involvement detected in the bone marrow aspiration and the flow cytometry results, a diagnosis of B-cell ALL was established (Figs. 2 and 3). Retrospective immunohistochemical re-evaluation of the initial skin biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of leukemia cutis.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) studies were performed for the following clinically relevant translocations in pediatric B-ALL, and all were negative: t (4, 11), t (1, 19), t (12,21), t (9,22) p190, and p210. With the diagnosis of LC, she underwent chemotherapy with the Berlin-Frankfurt-Münster-based treatment regimen (BFM protocol) [5]. The patient is now in the third phase of treatment. The skin lesions have completely healed, and scalp hair has regrown after one month of therapy. The bone marrow disease is in remission. She continues maintenance therapy according to the BFM-based protocol and remains under close follow-up. Given the complete cutaneous and hematological response, the prognosis is considered favorable at this point.

Discussion

We present the case of a 10-year-old girl whose initial manifestation of B-cell ALL was a solitary, morphoea-like plaque on the scalp and scarring alopecia, which completely resolved following chemotherapy. In pediatric leukemia, cutaneous manifestations are generally divided into two categories: specific lesions, known as LC, and non-specific or reactive lesions. LC refers to the direct infiltration of malignant leukemic cells into the epidermis, dermis, or subcutaneous tissue. Clinically, it often appears as nodules, papules, or plaques, and less frequently as purpuric or diffuse eruptions [1]. Among children, LC occurs most often in association with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), though cases have also been reported in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Non-specific or reactive lesions are more common and typically result from systemic issues, such as thrombocytopenia, infections, drug reactions, or paraneoplastic events. It is important to differentiate between these two categories because LC indicates direct tumor involvement, which has specific diagnostic and treatment implications [6, 7].

The anatomic location of LC in pediatric cases varies. Reported locations include the trunk, back, upper and lower extremities, and the face. While lesions on the head and legs are relatively common, scalp involvement is uncommon and may present as indurated plaques, nodules, or tumorous masses. This can result in localized alopecia due to follicular infiltration [7–9].

It is essential to differentiate LC in children from other lymphoid or lymphocyte-rich dermal infiltrates and from cutaneous metastases of solid tumors, since each condition carries distinct prognostic and therapeutic consequences. In children, erythematous infiltrated plaques that lack epidermal changes require a careful differential diagnosis, including leukemia cutis, primary or secondary cutaneous lymphoma, Jessner’s lymphocytic infiltrate, cutaneous pseudolymphoma, and polymorphous light eruption (PMLE). Because of the frequent clinical overlap between benign and malignant lymphoid infiltrates, the clinical condition, lesion progression, and initial laboratory findings play key roles in early management. A final diagnosis based on histopathologic evaluation from excisional biopsy and immunohistochemistry to differentiate malignant hematologic infiltrates from benign lymphoid proliferations. Accurate classification is essential for appropriate management and staging in pediatric cases [10–15].

Comparison with previously reported pediatric cases of scalp leukemia cutis in B-cell ALL (Table 1) highlights both shared and divergent features. The five previously documented pediatric cases show heterogeneity in age (ranging from 10 weeks to 8 years), lesion morphology (from small nodules to larger plaques), and degree of alopecia. While most patients achieved remission with chemotherapy, some had incomplete follow-up data.

Our patient shares several features with these cases, including scalp localization and a favorable response to systemic chemotherapy using the BFM protocol. However, the presentation in our case was distinctive due to the relatively large size (6 × 7 cm), firm morphea-like induration, and prominent scarring alopecia extending to the vertex — features that are rarely emphasized in the prior literature. This morphea-like appearance closely mimicked a primary dermatologic condition and could have delayed diagnosis. The subsequent development of systemic symptoms (recurrent fever and significant cervical lymphadenopathy) ultimately prompted a comprehensive hematologic evaluation, leading to the correct diagnosis.

Conclusion

The diagnostic challenge posed by the variable presentations of leukemia cutis necessitates its inclusion in the differential diagnosis of atypical pediatric scalp lesions. Furthermore, whenever a lymphocytic infiltrate is observed in skin pathology samples, its benign or malignant nature should be determined through careful follow-up and additional investigations. Maintaining a high index of suspicion is crucial for the timely identification of these uncommon cutaneous signs of systemic malignancy. Ultimately, accurate diagnosis via biopsy and the immediate commencement of systemic chemotherapy are vital for a favorable prognosis.
