A prognostic risk model based on programmed cell death genes for breast cancer and its potential clinical application
Authors: Ziran Zhang, Xingxia Yang, Jie Tang, Lifen Cai, Jingying Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to identify programmed cell death (PCD)-associated genes linked to breast cancer prognosis for the construction of a prognostic model. Transcriptomic and clinical information was imported from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and GEO databases. Modulatory genes related to 18 types of PCD were evaluated. Furthermore, the TCGA and GEO datasets were employed as the training and validation datasets, respectively. A risk score prognostic model based on PCD-related genes was generated via univariate, LASSO, and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Further, differences in drug sensitivity, tumor mutation burden (TMB), immune-related pathways and cell infiltration, and immune checkpoints were compared between high-risk and low-risk cohorts to elucidate the clinical applicability of the model. Lastly, the model gene’s expression was verified by RT-PCR. The data revealed 1480 PCD-related genes from the TCGA breast cancer gene expression matrix. Differential expression analysis identified 186 differentially expressed PCD genes. Furthermore, a prognostic model was generated according to the risk scores using multivariate Cox regression. The model comprised 8 PCD-related genes (BRSK2, CD24, IFNG, LAMB3, PDX1, PMAIP1, SLC7A11, and TRIML2). Moreover, breast cancer cases were divided into high-risk and low-risk cohorts per the median risk score. The results indicated that low-risk patients had better prognoses, and the model showed good predictive performance in the GEO validation cohort. The area under the curve values were 0.819, 0.731, and 0.674 for the nomogram’s 3, 5, and 8 years overall survival, respectively. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the prognostic model was markedly linked with the modulation of the immune microenvironment and tumor progression in breast cancer. Immune infiltration assessment revealed that low-risk patients had increased activity in immune-related pathways and infiltration of immune cells. In addition, the low-risk cohort had lower TMB and elevated immune checkpoint-related levels. Correlation analysis between immune checkpoints and risk scores indicated that low-risk patients were more responsive to immunotherapy. Drug sensitivity analysis showed variations in the IC50 values between the risk cohorts, suggesting potential variations in drug efficacy across different risk cohorts. Gene expression was verified by RT-PCR.A prognostic risk model for breast cancer based on 8 PCD-related genes was constructed and its predictive value was validated. The established model may provide novel biomarkers and effective therapeutic targets for breast cancer diagnosis and treatment.

1. Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most prevalent female cancer worldwide and also in China, where its incidence is increasing.[1,2] There have been significant advancements in early BC screening and treatment, including local surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, immunotherapy, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2)-targeted therapy; however, the outcomes and prognosis of advanced BC patients remain suboptimal. Some patients experience rapid recurrence and distant metastasis even after receiving standardized systemic treatment.[3] Therefore, it is crucial to identify HR BC patients with poor prognosis in early tumor stages. Clinical studies have demonstrated the marked heterogeneity of BC, associated with different etiologies, pathological and molecular characteristics, treatment approaches, and prognosis.[4] With the development of precision medicine, high-throughput sequencing, and genomic testing technologies, many researchers have comprehensively investigated the molecular mechanisms and therapeutic strategies for BC. It is crucial to evaluate the underlying molecular biology of the early onset and progression of BC, identify relevant diagnostic and therapeutic molecular markers, discover new prognostic biomarkers for predicting the biological behavior of BC, develop clinical prognostic prediction tools, optimize personalized treatment plans, and identify new therapeutic targets.

Programmed cell death (PCD) is a mechanism in which cells undergo self-regulated and orderly death via specific genes. The primary function of PCD is the maintenance of intracellular homeostasis to sustain tissue stability and eliminate damaged or unnecessary cells.[5] Various mechanisms mediate PCD, including apoptosis, anoikis, alkaliptosis, entosis, immunogenic and lysosome-dependent cell death, necroptosis, methuosis, ferroptosis, entotic cell death, autophagy, NETosis, pyroptosis, oxeiptosis, cuproptosis, netotic cell death, parthanatos, and paraptosis.[5] Different PCD pathways and their interactions significantly influence tumor progression and response to anticancer therapies. PCD has emerged as a critical area of cancer research in recent years. Studies have observed that PCD modulates immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and determines patient outcomes.[6] Moreover, aberrant expression or modulation of PCD-related genes can affect the growth and progression of BC. However, the function of PCD in BC and the relationships between PCD-associated genes and the responses to treatment and outcomes in BC remain elusive.

Thus, this study investigated survival-related PCD genes to establish a novel prognostic model for assessing outcomes in BC. Furthermore, the TME characteristics across different risk subcohorts, including the infiltration of immune cells, tumor mutation burden (TMB), and levels of immune checkpoints, were evaluated, and the variations in predicting immunotherapy and drug sensitivity were assessed. The findings demonstrate the value of this prognostic model for evaluating the survival outcomes in BC, which would help clinicians develop personalized and effective treatment strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data acquisition and processing

2.1.1. Breast cancer sample collection

RNA-seq data from patients with BC were imported from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and consisted of 1086 tumor and 99 normal samples. After excluding male samples and those with incomplete clinical information, 587 cases with complete clinical information and a >30-day follow-up period were included in the analysis. Furthermore, differential gene expression was analyzed via the raw RNA-seq counts. The data were normalized to log2[Transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) + 1] for subsequent analyses. The GSE20685 microarray dataset was utilized as the validation set and was imported from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database, which provides data on the association between BC subtypes and patient outcomes based on microarray data.[7] Figure 1 illustrates the study workflow.

The study flowchart. DEG = differentially expressed gene, GDSC = genomics of drug sensitivity in cancer, GO = Gene Ontology, GSEA = gene set enrichment analysis, GSVA = gene set variation analysis, HLA = human lymphocyte antigen, ICG = immune checkpoint gene, IPS = immunophenoscore, KEGG = Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, RT-PCR = real-time polymerase chain reaction.

2.1.2. Programmed cell death-related genes

The data on 18 PCD models and their modulatory genes were acquired from published studies and comprised 1964 PCD-related genes (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1).[8] In total, 416 duplicates were removed, leaving 1548 unique PCD-related genes. Of these, 68 genes were not identified in the TCGA database; therefore, 1480 PCD-related genes were finally selected for this investigation. The “VennDiagram” package was employed to visualize these genes.

2.2. Research methods

2.2.1. Enrichment analysis and identification of differentially expressed PCD-related genes

To elucidate the levels of 1480 PCD genes, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor and healthy tissues were identified using DESeq2 with criteria set as |log2FC| > 1.5 and P < .05. Heatmaps and volcano plots were utilized for visualizing the results. The DEG-associated pathways were explored via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO). For data visualization, the “clusterProfiler” package was employed with an adjusted P-value < .05 as the selection threshold.

2.2.2. Establishment and validation of the model

Univariate Cox regression was utilized to assess DEGs linked to prognosis, using a selection criterion of P < .05. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis was employed to improve model accuracy and specify the gene set via the “glmnet” package. Moreover, the optimal candidate genes were selected using the 10-fold cross-validation. Further multivariate Cox regression was performed and the selected genes and their associated coefficients, a prognostic risk model was established. Risk scores for cases in the training set were assessed using the formula:

*Exp i where “Coefficient” = the regression coefficients of the candidate genes from the multivariate Cox regression, and “Exp” = expression of individual candidate genes.

The acquired gene’s chromosomal locations were assessed by the “RCircos” package. Furthermore, BC cases were assigned to low-risk (LR) and high-risk (HR) groups according to the median risk score. Differences in survival between the groups were assessed by Kaplan–Meier curves, created with the “survminer” R package. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the “survival” and “timeROC” packages in R. To assess the distribution of gene levels between the 2 risk cohorts, principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out using “scatterplot3d.” Lastly, to validate the model’s predictive performance in an external GEO dataset, the same formula and statistical methods were employed.

2.2.3. Subcohort analysis of clinicopathological characteristics

To comprehensively understand the model’s prognostic value and clinical relevance, BC patients were categorized into different subcohorts based on various clinicopathological features, including age, tumor staging, and hormone receptor status. The survival differences between different risk cohorts within each subcohort were visualized by the Kaplan–Meier survival curves.

2.2.4. Construction and investigation of the nomogram

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression was used to assess the value of the risk score as an independent predictor of prognosis. A predictive nomogram was constructed by integrating the risk score with relevant clinical parameters using “survival” and “rms” in R. The nomogram’s accuracy and predictive performance were elucidated via calibration and time-dependent ROC curves. Furthermore, the nomogram’s clinical net benefit was determined via decision curve analysis (DCA).

2.2.5. Functional enrichment analysis of different risk cohorts

This research study carried out differential expression analysis on the DEGs to identify the characteristic functions of LR and HR cohorts. The acquired DEGs were annotated using the KEGG and GO pathways with the help of the “ClusterProfiler” package. In addition, alterations in the pathway activity between the LR and HR cohorts were assessed via Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) was screened to acquire annotated gene sets,[9] which were analyzed using the “ClusterProfiler” package. Moreover, the “GSVA” package was employed for Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) to examine the variations in functional phenotypes between the 2 risk cohorts.

2.2.6. Analysis of tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) characteristics

The ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized for the evaluation of the ESTIMATE, stromal, and immune scores, together with the tumor purity of each TCGA–BC patient.[10] Because of the essential role of TIME infiltrating immune cells, the infiltration scores of 22 types of immune cells in each sample were assessed using CIBERSORT. Furthermore, the association of the infiltration scores with the gene expression levels in the model was also investigated.

2.2.7. Tumor mutation analysis of different risk cohorts

The TMB values in the samples were assessed using “maftools,” as well as the differences in TMB scores between LR and HR cohorts. Furthermore, mutation data, including somatic mutations and copy number variations (CNVs), were acquired from the TCGA. The types and frequencies of somatic mutations in different risk BC patients were visualized using waterfall plots.

2.2.8. Prediction of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors

This investigation also compared the differences in the expression of human lymphocyte antigens (HLAs) and immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) between the 2 risk cohorts. Patient responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) were predicted using the Immunophenoscore (IPS). To verify the prognostic model’s capability to predict ICIs response, the IMvigor210 cohort was employed, which includes data from a study on atezolizumab treatment for advanced urothelial carcinoma (http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies/).[11] Patients who achieved partial response (PR), complete response (CR), or stable disease (SD) were categorized as responders. The responders were compared to patients with progressive disease (PD), called nonresponders.

2.2.9. Drug sensitivity analysis of different risk cohorts

Drug sensitivity in the HR and LR groups was examined using “oncoPredict,” with the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; www.cancerRxgene.org) dataset.[12]

2.2.10. Clinical samples

This investigation also assessed patients with BC who did not undergo chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery. From May to September 2024, 5 patients scheduled for BC surgery at Jiaxing Women and Children’s Hospital were included in this research. This investigation followed the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2013) and was approved by the hospital’s ethical board (2024-Y-60). All the participants provided their informed consent. Clinical information of the 5 pairs of patient tissues is provided in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2.

2.2.11. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA from tissues (tumor and adjacent normal samples) was isolated by TRIzol (Coolaber, China). The acquired RNA’s quality and integrity were evaluated via a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), and then subjected to reverse transcription to generate cDNA using the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). RT-PCR was carried out using a QuantStudio 3 instrument (ABI) in triplicate. The 2× qPCR SmArt pre-mix (SYBR Green; Low Rox) qPCR kit (DEEYEE, Shanghai, China) was used for amplification. For normalizing gene expression, β-actin was employed. The 2−ΔΔCt method was utilized for relative expression. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical assessments and visualizations were carried out via R (Version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0; GraphPad Software, Inc.) software. Spearman correlations were determined. For comparing the 2 risk cohorts, the Wilcoxon test was performed, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis analysis was conducted for comparing more than 2 cohorts. Furthermore, the paired t test or the paired nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was carried out for paired tissue samples. Moreover, the survival curves for each subcohort were generated by the Kaplan–Meier analysis, and the statistical differences were identified by the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set as P < .05. Nonsignificant differences were indicated by “ns” (P > .05), with significance levels denoted by asterisks: ****P < .0001, ***P < .001, **P < .01, *P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Analysis of PCD-related gene expression profile in breast cancer

3.1.1. Identification of PCD-associated DEGs

This research study identified 1480 PCD-related genes from the TCGA–BC gene expression profiles (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, 76 down-regulated and 110 up-regulated genes were found according to the criteria of P < .05 and |log2FC| > 1.5 (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the heatmap analysis revealed significant expression differences of these 186 DEGs between tumor and healthy tissues (Fig. 2C). GO and KEGG analyses were performed for the determination of DEG functions (Fig. 2D, E). The GO test indicated marked enrichment in functions such as the regulation of apoptotic signaling pathways and autophagy, whereas KEGG analysis highlighted key pathways including apoptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy. These data suggest that PCD is crucially involved in BC incidence and progression.

3.2. Prognostic risk model establishment and validation

3.2.1. Risk model based on PCD-associated genes

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified 34 DEGs, which were substantially linked with prognosis (P < .05; Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 4). Furthermore, LASSO analysis (optimal λ value = 0.026 and 1000 iterations of 10-fold cross-validation) identified 20 candidate genes (Fig. 3A, B), which were imported into the multivariate analysis, yielding 8 optimal genes (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 5). The chromosomal locations of the genes are illustrated in a Circos plot (Fig. 3C). Risk scores were evaluated in terms of the coefficients of these 8 genes: Risk Score = (0.192 * BRSK2 level) + (0.289 * CD24 level) + (−0.35 * IFNG level) + (−0.219 * LAMB3 level) + (0.255 * PDX1 level) + (−0.39 * PMAIP1 level) + (0.301 * SLC7A11 level) + (0.251 * TRIML2 level). Furthermore, the cases were allocated to HR (n = 451) and LR (n = 452) cohorts based on the median risk score (Fig. 3D). The risk distribution scatter plot indicated a notably increased proportion of deaths in the HR cohort compared to the LR cohort (Fig. 3E). Moreover, according to the Kaplan–Meier survival assessment, the overall survival (OS) of LR patients was markedly better than HR patients (P < .001, Fig. 3F). The time-dependent ROC analysis confirmed the performance of the model, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.754, 0.703, and 0.766, at 3, 5, and 8 years, respectively (Fig. 3G). Further, PCA results indicated notable differences between the HR and LR cohorts (Fig. 3H).

3.2.2. Evaluation and validation of the model

The forest plot from the multivariate regression (Fig. 4A) indicated that of the 8 candidate genes, IFNG, LAMB3, and PMAIP1 were protective factors, while BRSK2, CD24, PDX1, SLC7A11, and TRIML2 were risk factors. This is shown in the heatmap (Fig. 4D). The GEO dataset was used as the validation set and was employed to evaluate the model’s stability. Patient risk scores were calculated as in the training set and cases were divided into HR (n = 163) and LR (n = 164) cohorts. A scatter plot of risk distribution revealed enhanced risk scores, reduced survival time, and elevated mortality (Fig. 4B, C). Consistent with the TCGA dataset results, the LR patients had markedly longer OS (P = .012, Fig. 4E). For 3, 5, and 8 years, the ROC curves indicated relatively high AUCs of 0.664, 0.640, and 0.624, respectively (Fig. 4F). PCA indicated good discrimination between the 2 groups in the GEO validation set (Fig. 4G).

The prognostic value of the risk score was evaluated via survival curves for the different subgroups (Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 6). The data indicated that in most subcohorts, LR patients had a better prognosis than HR patients (Fig. 5). Furthermore, regardless of subcohorts by age, tumor stage, or other clinicopathological characteristics, the OS of the LR group was markedly longer than that of HR patients. This further confirms the broad applicability and effectiveness of our prognostic model.

3.2.4. Evaluation of the model and nomogram in the independent prediction of outcome

The ability of the risk score to independently predict outcomes was assessed by univariate and multivariate Cox regression. The acquired data consistently indicated the value of the risk score as independently predictive of prognosis in BC (Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 7). This led to the construction of a nomogram including the risk score, age, and tumor stage for predicting 3, 5, and 8-year survival probabilities for BC patients (Fig. 6A). The corresponding AUC values were 0.819, 0.731, and 0.674, respectively (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the predictive performance of the nomogram was shown by calibration plots to be consistent with the ideal model (Fig. 6C), indicating good stability. Moreover, the AUC and DCA results revealed that the nomogram was associated with better net benefit relative to the use of the individual clinical parameters (Fig. 6D, E). This indicates that the established nomogram was effective in the prediction of BC prognosis.

3.3. Mutation analysis and copy number variation analysis

Here, waterfall plots were generated to show the frequencies and types of somatic mutations in the HR and LR cohorts (Fig. 7A, B). The data indicated that somatic mutation rates were 89.08% and 84.99% in the HR and LR cohorts, respectively. Furthermore, TP53 showed the highest number of mutations in the HR cohort, while PIK3CA was most frequently mutated in the LR cohort. Moreover, the CNV frequencies of the 8 model genes in BC patients were also evaluated, which revealed significant CNV amplifications in LAMB3, PDX1, and IFNG, and a marked CNV deletion in BRSK2 (Fig. 7C).

3.4. Functional enrichment analysis of different risk cohorts

To assess molecular pathway differences in the 2 risk cohorts, DEG assessment was carried out via the “edgeR” package, with the following selection criterion P < .05 and |log2FC| > 1.0. The data revealed 802 DEGs (635 up-regulated and 177 down-regulated genes) between the 2 cohorts (Fig. 8A). GO analysis (Fig. 8B) indicated marked enrichment of these DEGs in several molecular functions and biological processes related to signal transduction and stress response. Moreover, “cellular response to xenobiotic stimulus” and “metal ion transmembrane transporter activity” were notably enriched, suggesting these functions may trigger PCD by modulating changes in the intracellular and extracellular environment. Moreover, during metal ion imbalance and the exposure of exogenous compounds, cells may stimulate stress responses and apoptotic pathways to maintain cellular and tissue homeostasis. KEGG analysis (Fig. 8C) uncovered substantial enrichment in pathways related to metabolism, signal transduction, and immune response, such as “tyrosine metabolism” and “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” indicating these pathways may influence apoptosis and other forms of PCD by regulating intracellular and extracellular signals. The marked enrichment of “drug and xenobiotics metabolism by cytochrome P450” suggests that when exposed to harmful substances, cells may induce stress responses and PCD via detoxification mechanisms. To assess the possible biological role and pathways linked to PCD, GSEA and GSVA analyses were carried out to identify key signaling pathways between the LR and HR cohorts. GSEA analysis (Fig. 8D, E) showed significant enrichment of pathways such as “oxidative phosphorylation” and “cell cycle” in the HR cohort, which are notably linked with cell proliferation and energy metabolism and may influence PCD by regulating cell growth and survival. In the LR cohort, immune-related pathways including “allograft rejection” and “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” were substantially enriched, potentially maintaining cellular homeostasis and inducing PCD by regulating immune responses and inflammation. GSVA analysis (Fig. 8F) demonstrated significant enrichment of multiple PCD-related pathways. For example, “HALLMARK_APOPTOSIS” was significantly enriched, indicating the activation of the apoptosis process. Moreover, “TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB” and “HALLMARK_P53_PATHWAY” were also notably enriched, involving TNF-α-mediated inflammation and p53-mediated stress response, respectively, both known pathways of PCD. Similarly, “INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE” and “HALLMARK_IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING,” which involve cytokine signaling, were enriched, suggesting these pathways may indirectly induce PCD through the regulation of inflammation and immune responses. Taken together, these data suggest that our prognostic model is substantially linked with PCD and immune regulation, underscoring its potential value in predicting BC survival outcomes.

3.5. Tumor immune microenvironment analysis

3.5.1. Tumor immune microenvironment analysis of different risk cohorts

The functional enrichment analysis showed differences in immune function enrichment between LR and HR cohorts. Since the immune response is a crucial component of the prognostic model, the association of PCD-related genes with TIME was assessed. The ESTIMATE algorithm data indicated that, compared to the HR cohort, LR patients had reduced tumor purity and increased immune, ESTIMATE, and stromal scores (Fig. 9A, B). Moreover, the risk score was negatively linked with the immune score (R = −0.30, P < .05), stromal score (R = −0.26, P < .05), and ESTIMATE score (R = −0.32, P < .05), and positively linked with tumor purity (R = 0.32, P < .05; Fig. 9C–F). The CIBERSORT algorithm compared the infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types between the LR and HR cohorts. It was observed that infiltration of CD8 T cells, monocytes, gamma delta T cells, CD4 memory resting T cells, macrophages M1, resting dendritic, and resting mast cells was markedly higher in the LR cohort, whereas infiltration of plasma cells, macrophages M2, activated dendritic cells, macrophages M0, and eosinophils was substantially higher in the HR cohort (Fig. 9G). Furthermore, the relationship between 8 genes in the prognostic model and the abundance of the 22 immune cell types was also observed. The data revealed that most immune cells were notably correlated with these genes (Fig. 9H). The ssGSEA analysis of immune cell subpopulations and related functions showed substantial differences between the LR and HR TCGA cohorts. The HR scores were linked with reduced infiltrating immune cell levels and immune-related pathway activity. Overall, these data suggest that in BC, HR patients have significantly impaired immune functions (Fig. 9I, J).

3.6. Correlations between the prognostic risk model and TMB

Given the close relationship between tumor mutation-generated antigens and immunotherapy, the variations in TMB between the LR and HR cohorts were assessed, which indicated that TMB was markedly greater in the HR group relative to the LR patients (Fig. 7D). Moreover, survival analysis demonstrated that high TMB was linked with poorer clinical prognosis (Fig. 7E).

3.7. Correlation analysis of the immune response and the model

Given the critical function of ICIs in immunotherapy, the expression of 20 ICGs between the LR and HR cohorts was compared. It was found that except for TNFSF15, CD276, TNFSF4, and ICOSLG, the other 16 ICGs were markedly expressed in the LR cohort (Fig. 10A). Thus, LR patients may derive greater benefit from ICI therapies. Furthermore, the analysis of HLA-related gene expression across different risk cohorts revealed that they were substantially up-regulated in the LR cohort (Fig. 10B). This indicates that LR patients may have enhanced antigen-presenting capability, potentially leading to better outcomes with immunotherapy. Currently, ICIs targeting PD-1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) are employed clinically to treat various cancers. To distinguish patients who can benefit more from these ICIs, the IPS assessment indicated that, under any PD-1 and CTLA-4 stratification, the LR patients had higher IPS than HR patients. This indicates that the LR cohort has a higher probability of response to and greater sensitivity to ICI therapy (Fig. 10C). To verify the established model’s capability to predict immunotherapy benefits, the real-world immunotherapy cohort IMvigor210 was analyzed. Although the LR cohort responded better to anti-PD-L1 therapy, the difference was not significant (chi-square test, P = .198; Fig. 10D). However, a marked difference was found in survival rates between the LR and HR cohorts (Fig. 10E). Overall, these results suggest that LR and HR patients respond differently to immunotherapy, with the LR cohort indicating higher sensitivity to treatment and better clinical outcomes.

3.8. Prognostic risk model’s drug sensitivity analysis

This research study evaluated the association of risk scores with drug sensitivity by comparing the sensitivity of different risk subcohorts to commonly used BC chemotherapy drugs using the GDSC database. It was found that the risk cohorts had substantial differences in sensitivity to 4 commonly used chemotherapy drugs: olaparib, 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, and cyclophosphamide. Furthermore, the IC50 values for olaparib and 5-fluorouracil were markedly reduced in the LR cohort, indicating higher sensitivity to these drugs, whereas the HR patients were more sensitive to cyclophosphamide and cisplatin (Fig. 10F).

3.9. RT-PCR verification of 8 prognostic-related genes

The levels of 8 PCD-related genes were assessed in 5 pairs of BC and adjacent healthy tissues via RT-PCR (Fig. 11), which showed that IFNG, LAMB3, and PMAIP1 were down-regulated in BC, with PMAIP1 showing a significant decrease, whereas CD24 and SLC7A11 were up-regulated in BC. However, because of the small sample size, no marked differences were found for BRSK2, PDX1, and TRIML2 between BC and healthy tissues. Further larger sample size studies are required to confirm these findings.

4. Discussion

BC is a significantly heterogeneous malignancy that is categorized into various subtypes in terms of the levels of progesterone receptor, estrogen receptor, and HER-2. Each subtype exhibits distinct biological characteristics. However, treatment sensitivity and survival outcomes vary even in patients with the same BC subtype, despite identical clinical regimens.[13] This variability presents significant challenges for the precision treatment of BC, increasing the complexity of tumor therapy and directly impacting treatment decisions and outcomes. Recently, it has been indicated that cell death represents an important target for cancer treatment. For malignant transformation and tumorigenesis, tumor cells evade death. Therefore, it is significant to investigate the cell death mechanisms and functions, particularly the forms and regulatory steps of PCD for understanding tumor development and anticancer therapies. Currently, 18 different PCD types have been identified, with necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, apoptosis, and autophagy being the most extensively studied.[6] These PCD types are crucially associated with the modulation of the immunosuppressive TME and assessment of cancer-related clinical outcomes. PCD has dual roles, as it can affect tumor growth positively or negatively, according to the release of intracellular factors during the process. Furthermore, PCD modulates the TME’s antitumor immune response by regulating effector or immune cell expression.[14] Although PCD has been notably linked with BC onset and progression, research on prognostic models based on PCD-associated genes in BC is limited. Therefore, this investigation aimed to establish a PCD-related gene risk prognostic model for accurately predicting BC patient’s prognosis while evaluating possible novel treatment targets, which may improve BC prognosis. The established model can assist in clinical decision-making, providing a scientific basis for personalized treatment.

This investigation comprehensively elucidated the expression patterns of 18 types of PCD in BC and established a prognostic signature based on PCD-related DEGs. Furthermore, univariate, multivariate, and LASSO Cox regression tests were carried out to generate a prognostic risk model comprising 8 PCD-related genes. Among these, IFNG, LAMB3, and PMAIP1 were markedly up-regulated in the LR cohort and were linked with favorable outcomes in BC. IFNG encodes interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), an immune cell-produced cytokine with antitumor, antiviral, and immunoregulatory activities. IFN-γ modulates gene transcription through the JAK/STAT axis, playing a role in immune response and apoptosis.[15] The literature has indicated that in BC, IFN-γ enhances immune recognition by up-regulating major histocompatibility complex-I molecules; however, it may also induce immune evasion by increasing PD-L1 expression.[16] LAMB3 encodes laminin, which is associated with extracellular matrix formation, cell adhesion, cell migration, and tissue structure maintenance. Furthermore, it has been observed that LAMB3 is linked with tumor invasion and metastasis in different cancers.[17,18] However, its role in BC is still elusive and requires further investigation to better understand its prognostic significance. PMAIP1 encodes NOXA, a pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family member that promotes mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis by interacting with antiapoptotic Bcl-2 proteins.[19] Moreover, PMAIP1 maintains normal tissue function and stability by removing damaged cells and increasing BC cell’s sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents.[20,21] Of the 8 PCD-related genes, BRSK2, CD24, PDX1, SLC7A11, and TRIML2 were observed to be substantially up-regulated in the HR cohort, potentially causing poor prognosis in BC patients. BRSK2 encodes a serine/threonine protein kinase, which modulates the cell cycle, neural development, and glucose metabolism.[22-24] It has been found that CD24 promotes tumor cell’s ability to attach, migrate, and invade and modulates immune evasion in TME.[25,26] PDX1 is primarily expressed in the pancreas and duodenum and is markedly linked with poor pancreatic cancer prognosis; however, its specific role in BC warrants further research.[27] SLC7A11 is overexpressed in various cancers and has been observed to inhibit ferroptosis by increasing intracellular glutathione levels to prevent lipid peroxidation.[28,29] TRIML2 modulates apoptosis and DNA damage repair by enhancing the activation of p53 target genes.[30] Furthermore, its expression is substantially higher in triple-negative breast cancer tissues than in adjacent healthy tissues, serving as a marker of poor prognosis, consistent with our findings.[31] In this research study, the risk scores of individual samples were determined, with samples then classified into HR and LR cohorts. Kaplan–Meier survival and stratified analyses revealed marked differences in survival rates between HR and LR cohorts in the overall population and across different clinical subcohorts, indicating the model’s effectiveness in identifying HR BC patients. Furthermore, multivariate and univariate Cox regression analyses validated that the risk model comprising PCD-associated genes is an independent prognostic factor for BC patients. Moreover, a nomogram was established by incorporating clinical and pathological factors (age and tumor stage) with the risk score to facilitate clinical decision-making. This model can assist in the prediction of prognosis, facilitating the implementation of individualized management of BC. To investigate the variably expressed PCD-associated genes in HR and LR cohorts, a functional enrichment test was carried out, which revealed that active cell proliferation and energy metabolism pathways in the HR cohort may promote tumor cell growth and survival, whereas immune-related pathway enrichment in the LR cohort may inhibit tumor progression by increasing immune response and inducing cell death. Further, the GSEA and GSVA analyses indicated the crucial involvement of immune regulation in PCD and tumor progression.

Cells within the TME, including infiltrating immune cells, are linked to the progression and metastasis of tumors.[32,33] The importance of these immune cells in prognosis and tumor progression has been demonstrated.[34,35] Therefore, this investigation assessed the variations in immune cell abundance and function in the TME of HR and LR cohorts. The data indicated that antitumor immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages, and CD4+ memory resting T cells, were substantially enriched in the LR cohort, whereas the HR cohort was enriched with protumor immune cells, such as M0 and M2 macrophages. The literature has indicated that M1 macrophage enrichment is associated with a better prognosis, whereas M2 macrophage enrichment is linked with poorer outcomes.[36-39] Moreover, high CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration is related to higher survival rates in BC patients,[40,41] further supporting the findings of the present study. Here, LR patients had greater immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores as well as reduced tumor purity than HR patients, indicating that immune and stromal cells are important factors influencing tumor prognosis. Potential immune-related pathways were also assessed in this investigation, which showed substantial differences between the HR and LR cohorts across multiple immune pathways. These data suggest that the stronger antitumor immune response in the LR cohort may have been responsible for their improved prognosis. Moreover, immune cell infiltration and stromal scores in the TME are crucial factors affecting BC prognosis. Similarly, antitumor immune cell enrichment and higher stromal scores in the LR cohort may improve treatment outcomes. Overall, these data highlighted novel perspectives on the precision treatment of BC and the potential of modulating the TME in anticancer therapy.

To further elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the survival differences between HR and LR cohorts, somatic mutation analysis was conducted. In line with previous studies, TP53 showed the greatest number of mutations in the HR cohort.[42] It has been demonstrated that nearly one-third of BC patients harbor TP53 mutations, which reduce p53’s transcriptional activity, thereby promoting BC progression and lowering OS rates.[43,44] However, in the LR cohort, PIK3CA had the highest frequency of gene mutation, in line with previous studies.[45] PIK3CA is also considered an effective prognostic marker for BC. Lv et al suggested that a higher PIK3CA mutation rate might be related to higher numbers of hormone receptor-positive cases in the LR cohort, consistent with the present findings.[46] Here, it was observed that TMB was higher in HR patients than in LR patients, suggesting that the accumulation of mutations in the HR cohort may contribute to a poorer prognosis. Moreover, the HR cohort had a greater proportion of TNBC and HER-2-positive BC patients, consistent with the higher invasiveness, greater proliferative capacity, and poorer outcomes in TNBC and HER-2-positive subtypes compared to Luminal BC. Overall, these findings highlight the molecular pathways underlying different BC subtypes and their impact on prognosis and will help develop more precise treatment strategies for BC patients.

Both chemotherapy and immunotherapy, specifically ICIs, are used for treating BC. ICIs increase T cell’s immune response against tumors by regulating key molecules such as CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1, thereby alleviating various cancers.[47] For precision medicine, it is essential to develop predictive biomarkers to distinguish patients who are sensitive to ICI therapies. Here, the response of HR and LR cohorts to ICI therapy was evaluated using existing biomarkers and related databases. Immune cell surfaces carry IC molecules, which inhibit their function, thereby preventing an effective antitumor immune response and leading to tumor immune evasion. This research study indicated that most IC-related genes were markedly expressed in the LR cohort, indicating that LR BC patients are more sensitive to ICI therapy. Therefore, targeting ICIs may be a feasible and effective approach for treating LR BC patients. In addition, IPS was employed to further assess patient susceptibility to ICIs, which revealed that the LR cohort had higher IPS across all PD-1 and CTLA-4 stratifications than the HR cohort, suggesting the LR cohort has a relatively higher probability of response to ICI therapy. The established model’s ability to predict immunotherapy effectiveness was validated by the IMvigor210 immunotherapy cohort. The data indicated the potential of the model in ICI treatment in BC patients. This discovery offers a new perspective on personalized immunotherapy and may help clinicians to more accurately select BC patients suitable for ICI treatment.

Immunotherapy and chemotherapy are the primary treatment modalities for BC. Therefore, in this research study, the LR and HR patient’s sensitivity to different chemotherapy drugs was assessed. The IC50 of each chemotherapeutic drug in BC samples was evaluated to identify drugs with substantial differences in sensitivity between the risk cohorts. The data indicated that the IC50 values of olaparib and 5-fluorouracil were notably reduced in the LR cohort than the HR cohort, validating that these drugs may have better efficacy and sensitivity in LR patients. Whereas the IC50 of cisplatin and cyclophosphamide were reduced in the HR cohort, suggesting greater sensitivity in HR patients. Overall, these data indicated that there are notable differences in the sensitivities of chemotherapeutic drugs between BC patients in different risk cohorts, implying that LR and HR cohorts may require different chemotherapy strategies. These stratified treatment approaches could help optimize BC therapy to achieve the best possible clinical outcomes.

Several studies have indicated prognostic models related to PCD in BC. For example, Zou et al developed a model comprising 12 PCD-related genes, while Pu et al constructed a model containing 7 necroptosis-associated genes to forecast the prognosis of TNBC patients.[39,48] Furthermore, Zhong et al and Su et al respectively developed prognostic models related to pyroptosis and lysosomes to predict BC patient’s prognosis.[49,50] Although Zou and Pu’s studies provided more detailed prognostic information for the specific subtype of TNBC, their findings may have limited generalizability. Whereas the studies by Zhong and Su had a broader range of BC types, they focused only on individual PCD-related genes, potentially overlooking the comprehensive impact of PCD diversity on BC prognosis. Here, a model based on 18 types of PCD, covering a broader gene spectrum, was established, which allows its application to the overall BC population, indicating higher clinical applicability and broader representativeness. By comprehensively considering multiple PCD mechanisms, our model provides a more thorough prognostic assessment and offers valuable insights for personalized treatment across different BC subtypes.

5. Limitation

There are several shortcomings in this study. The constructed model was based on retrospective analysis. Although preliminary clinical validations were carried out, the model’s generalizability and accuracy require further validation via large-scale, multicenter prospective data due to the limited sample size. Some patients were excluded from the study because they had missing clinical data or short follow-up periods, which may have led to bias. In the model, the biological functions of certain PCD genes, such as LAMB3 and PDX1, have not been fully evaluated in BC patients, necessitating further experimental studies. Future research should include more clinical data and multi-omics data to verify the reliability and accuracy of the model. Furthermore, the experimental validation of the roles of these PCD genes in BC will further refine the model and provide a more robust basis for precision treatment.

6. Conclusions

To summarize, this research study utilized and integrated PCD-related genes linked with BC prognosis from the TCGA database to construct a prognostic risk model, which was validated by an external GEO dataset. Furthermore, the model effectively identified HR individuals and predicted immune infiltration, immunotherapy response, and chemotherapy sensitivity, providing valuable guidance for personalized treatment selection.
