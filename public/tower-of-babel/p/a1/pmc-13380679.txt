Beyond Acculturation: Ethnic Identity, Tobacco and Nicotine Product Use, and Health Among Arab Americans
Authors: Sarah Alnahari, Arturo Durazo, Paul Brown, Anna Epperson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArab Americans are one of the fastest-growing immigrant populations in the United States, yet they remain largely invisible in tobacco surveillance and prevention efforts due to their classification as “White” in national data. Despite evidence of elevated tobacco use and chronic disease burden, limited research has examined how cultural, psychological, and social factors shape tobacco-related health risks in this community.ObjectivesThis study examined associations between ethnic identity, tobacco use, and health outcomes (self-rated health, chronic disease diagnosis) among Arab American adults, and tested whether ethnic identity moderated the association between tobacco use and health outcomes.MethodsIn a cross-sectional study, a convenience sample of 112 Arab American adults residing in Central California completed a survey assessing ethnic identity, tobacco use, and health. Ethnic identity was measured using an abbreviated, Arabic-translated adaptation of Phinney’s Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure. Tobacco use was categorized as non-use, single-product use, or multi-product use. Multivariable regression models assessed associations and potential moderating effects of ethnic identity on health outcomes.ResultsOver 48% of study participants reported current use, defined as at least some use or more, of at least one tobacco or nicotine product, including cigarettes, e-cigarettes (which may contain synthetic rather than tobacco-derived nicotine), and hookah. Higher ethnic identity scores were associated with significantly lower odds of tobacco use (adjusted OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.15–0.83). Single-product users had 75% lower odds of reporting better self-rated health compared to non-users (OR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.07–0.96). No significant associations were found for multi-product use or chronic disease in adjusted models, and ethnic identity did not significantly moderate associations with health outcomes.ConclusionEthnic identity is associated with lower tobacco use among Arab Americans, though its protective effects on health outcomes remain less clear. These findings underscore the need to move beyond acculturation-based frameworks and incorporate identity-informed, culturally responsive approaches to tobacco control in marginalized immigrant communities.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains the leading preventable cause of disease and death in the United States (U.S.), yet the progress achieved through national tobacco control efforts has not been equitably shared. 1 Although overall smoking rates have declined, significant disparities persist across racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic groups.2,3 Tobacco use remains disproportionately high among American Indian and Alaska Native populations, low-income individuals, and those with lower educational attainment. 4 Immigrant communities are also understudied in tobacco research, despite evidence that they face unique structural, cultural, and tobacco marketing-related risks.5-7 Arab Americans, an estimated 3.7 million people with ancestry in 22 Arabic-speaking countries, are especially overlooked because they are classified as “White” in national racial and ethnic data, rendering them invisible in national health surveillance.8,9 Many countries of origin for Arab Americans have high and rising tobacco use rates, shaped in part by limited regulatory infrastructure and complex intersections between commercial interests and policy environments. 10 Regional studies across the U.S. document elevated cigarette and waterpipe (hookah) smoking among Arab Americans compared to national averages, particularly among young adults and recent immigrants. 11 Despite documented higher educational attainment and income levels relative to many other minority and immigrant communities, Arab Americans experience higher age-adjusted mortality rates than non-Hispanic Whites,10,12 largely attributable to chronic diseases including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer.13-15

Acculturation theory has long been applied in immigrant health research to explain behavioral and health outcomes, referring to the process by which immigrants adapt to the norms, values, and practices of the host society. 16 Dominant frameworks, including Berry’s four-strategy model, conceptualize acculturation along axes of heritage cultural maintenance and engagement with the host society. 17 In practice, acculturation is typically operationalized using proxy measures such as language preference, nativity, and length of U.S. residence, reducing a dynamic and bidirectional process to a single dimension. Critics further argue that acculturation frameworks frequently neglect structural forces including racism, legal status, and socioeconomic inequality,18,19 all of which shape immigrant health outcomes and tend to center dominant cultural norms as the benchmark for health.

Among Arab Americans, acculturation has frequently been used to explain smoking patterns. Several studies link lower acculturation to higher tobacco use: Al-Omari and Scheibmeir found that individuals with stronger ties to traditional Arab culture were more likely to smoke, 20 and a Colorado-based study found stronger Arab cultural identification associated with greater cigarette and hookah use. 21 In contrast, Arab immigrants in Virginia who had spent more of their lives in the U.S. reported more frequent waterpipe use, suggesting a divergent pattern. 22 Greater assimilation and integration have also been associated with stronger desire to quit. 23 These mixed findings reflect a broader limitation: acculturation frameworks operationalized as a single dimension of cultural adaptation may obscure variation across subgroups defined by national origin, gender, religious affiliation, and generational status. Relying solely on acculturation also casts cultural connection in a deficit light and can reinforce stereotypes, particularly within the racialized sociopolitical landscape that marginalizes Arab identities in the U.S.8,24

Acculturation and ethnic identity are conceptually and empirically distinct constructs. Acculturation refers to an externally oriented process of adaptation to the dominant culture. Ethnic identity, by contrast, is an internally held, self-defined sense of belonging to one’s ethnic group. It encompasses cognitive and emotional components, including identification, pride, and cultural engagement, reflecting how individuals make meaning of their cultural membership in diverse and often racialized contexts. 25 Where acculturation asks how much an individual has adapted to the dominant society, ethnic identity asks how strongly an individual identifies with their own group. These constructs operate through different mechanisms and their independent effects on health behaviors warrant separate examination. The most widely used measure of ethnic identity is Phinney’s Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure (MEIM), developed to assess these dimensions across diverse populations. 25 The MEIM has been validated in multiple populations and has demonstrated strong internal consistency and construct validity.26-29

Strong ethnic identity has been consistently associated with a range of protective health outcomes, including buffering the negative effects of discrimination, enhancing psychological well-being, and reducing risk behaviors such as substance use.30,31 These benefits are particularly relevant for racialized immigrant communities, where affirming one’s cultural identity may serve as a critical source of resilience and resistance. 32 Among racial and ethnic minority populations, strong ethnic identity has also been linked to positive coping strategies, higher self-esteem, and engagement in health-promoting behaviors,33-35 and may mitigate the harmful effects of acculturative stress and social exclusion. 36 With respect to tobacco use specifically, strong ethnic identity has been linked to lower smoking rates, greater resistance to peer pressure, and more health-promoting behaviors among African American/Black, Hispanic/Latino, Asian American, and American Indian and Alaska Native populations.30,37-39 Longitudinal evidence indicates that weak ethnic identity in adolescence predicted greater antisocial behavior and later tobacco use. 37 Among foreign-born Hispanic/Latina women, high ethnic identity was associated with significantly lower rates of prenatal smoking. 40 Other research found that ethnic identity contributed to motivation to quit, particularly when identity was tied to family or spiritual values. 41 Even in cultural contexts where certain forms of tobacco use are normalized-such as betel nut, paan, or waterpipe use among South Asians-ethnic identity remained a key influence on behavior, shaping whether such use was perceived as aligned with or in conflict with evolving identity in a new cultural context. 41 Notably, the protective effects of ethnic identity were strongest when cultural pride was reinforced by supportive community structures and when individuals experienced low levels of discrimination. The relationship is not uniformly protective, however: among Hispanic/Latino men, high ethnic identity combined with high perceived discrimination was associated with increased tobacco use, possibly as a coping response to acculturative stress, 42 underscoring that ethnic identity’s protective role depends on broader intersectional factors including gender, generational status, and marginalization.

Despite these insights, Arab Americans remain largely absent from this literature. This is a significant exclusion given the group’s documented experiences with racialization, surveillance, and sociopolitical marginalization. Ethnic identity in this context may function as a form of cultural resilience, reinforcing norms that discourage smoking and fostering community-based values that promote well-being. This study is guided by the Biopsychosocial (BPS) Model, which conceptualizes health as a product of interacting biological, psychological, and social factors. 43 Originally proposed by Engel in 1977, the BPS model challenges traditional biomedical perspectives by emphasizing that health and illness are shaped not only by physiological processes but also by cognition, emotion, behavior, and broader social context. This study tests whether ethnic identity may play a more central role in shaping tobacco-related health behaviors than previously assumed, examining it as a distinct predictor separate from acculturation, language use, and socioeconomic factors. The BPS framework is applied here by integrating both objective (e.g., chronic disease diagnosis) and subjective (e.g., self-rated health) indicators to capture a multidimensional view of health.

Methods

Study Design and Recruitment

This study used a cross-sectional design guided by the biopsychosocial model. 43 Outcome selection reflected this framework directly: chronic disease diagnosis captures the biological dimension, self-rated health captures the subjective psychological dimension, and all models adjusted for social determinants including income, education, employment, and generational status. Arab American adults residing in Central California were recruited using convenience sampling from May 2024 to March 2025 via community flyers distributed through local Arab American networks, social media, and community organizations. Flyers included a link to an eligibility screening survey hosted on Qualtrics.

Participants were first directed to a brief eligibility screening survey. To qualify for the survey, individuals were required to: (1) be aged 18 years or older and (2) reside in one of four counties in Central California: Fresno, Madera, Merced, or Stanislaus. Those who did not meet these criteria were automatically exited from the survey. Of 148 screening attempts, 120 participants met eligibility criteria and were redirected to the full survey. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were informed of the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of their involvement, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. This study was reviewed and approved by the University of California, Merced Institutional Review Board (protocol #UCM2020-100; approval date: December 2, 2020).

The survey was administered in both English and Arabic via Qualtrics. Built-in quality control measures included bot detection, prevention of duplicate responses, and geolocation filtering to restrict participation to individuals located in California. All participants received a $45 incentive for survey completion. Of 120 completed surveys, 8 were excluded due to poor data quality: 6 were completed in under five minutes (average completion time: 17.8 minutes), and 2 were largely incomplete despite ample time (>3 hours). All excluded cases lacked outcome data and key predictors, limiting their analytic value. The final analytic sample included 112 participants.

Outcome Measures

This study included three primary outcome variables: tobacco use status, self-rated health, and chronic disease diagnoses. These outcomes reflect both subjective and objective indicators of health and were operationalized using established health definitions and validated survey items.

Tobacco Use Status

Tobacco use status was operationalized as a three-category variable representing the number of tobacco products currently used. Current use was defined in accordance with U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines. 44 Participants were classified as current cigarette smokers if they reported smoking at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and smoking a cigarette in the past 30 days. This definition, consistent with CDC guidelines, includes occasional smokers and does not assess nicotine dependence or use frequency. Current e-cigarette use was defined as any use in the past 30 days, regardless of lifetime use. E-cigarettes are included in the broader tobacco use category consistent with CDC surveillance practice, although some e-cigarette products contain synthetic nicotine rather than tobacco-derived nicotine; this distinction was not assessed in this study. Current hookah use was defined as any use in the past 30 days, regardless of lifetime use. For the analysis, a composite variable was created: participants reporting no current use were classified as non-users (51.8%); those using only one product: cigarettes (11.6%), e-cigarettes (6.3%), or hookah (4.5%), were categorized as single-product users (22.3% total); and those using two or more products were classified as multi-product users (25.9%), including combinations of e-cigarettes with cigarettes or hookah (11.6%), cigarettes and hookah (8.0%), or all three products (6.3%).

Self-Rated Health

Self-rated health (SRH) is a widely used public health indicator with well-established predictive validity for chronic disease, healthcare utilization, and mortality.45-47 SRH was assessed using a standard single-item measure: “Would you say your physical health is…” with five response options (excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor). For the analysis, SRH was recoded into a three-level ordinal variable: (1) Excellent/Very Good, (2) Good, and (3) Fair/Poor. This collapsed three-category approach is consistent with prior research on immigrant and Arab American populations45,46,48 and preserves the ordinal nature of the variable while ensuring sufficient cell sizes for analysis. The variable was reverse coded so that higher scores correspond to better self-rated health.

Chronic Disease

Participants were asked whether a healthcare professional had ever told them they had any of six chronic conditions: asthma, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, high blood pressure, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). A dichotomous indicator variable reflected the presence of at least one diagnosed condition (1 = Yes, 0 = No). This approach is consistent with standard chronic disease surveillance methodology used in national surveys such as the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS). 49

Independent Measures

Ethnic Identity Scale

Ethnic identity was measured using an abbreviated, Arabic-translated adaptation of Phinney’s Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure (MEIM). 25 The original MEIM comprises 12–14 items assessing ethnic affirmation and belonging, ethnic identity exploration, and cultural behaviors. For this study, 10 items were selected to capture the core dimensions most relevant to the Arab American context, and the scale was translated into Arabic to ensure accessibility for participants with limited English proficiency. Sample items included: “I participate in cultural practices of my own group,” “I have a strong sense of belonging to my own ethnic group,” and “I have a lot of pride in my ethnic group.” Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree; 4 = Strongly Agree); all items were reverse-coded so that higher scores reflected stronger ethnic identity. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.94). A composite ethnic identity score was computed as the mean of the 10 items, with higher values indicating stronger ethnic identity.

Demographic and Socioeconomic Measures

Demographic and contextual variables included gender (female, male), age group (18–30, 31–45, ≥46 years old), educational attainment (high school or less, some college, bachelor’s degree or higher), and marital status (married/partnered vs. not married or partnered). Household income was categorized as low (<$50,000), middle ($51,001–$100,000), or high (>$100,000) based on the 2023 State Income Limits Report from the California Department of Housing and Community Development. 50 Employment status was coded as a binary variable, with employed individuals (full-time or part-time) coded as 1, and all others, including those not seeking work, actively seeking employment, students, retirees, and those on disability were coded as 0. Generational status distinguished between foreign-born (first-generation) and U.S.-born participants. Language spoken at home was categorized as: (1) English or mostly English, (2) mostly another language (e.g., Arabic), or (3) bilingual. Region of origin within the Arab world was grouped into three subregions: the Levant (e.g., Palestine, Lebanon, Jordan), North Africa (e.g., Egypt, Algeria, Sudan), and the Arabian Peninsula (e.g., Yemen, Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates). 51

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic and health-related characteristics, stratified by tobacco use status (no use, single-product, multi-product). Differences across groups were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables. Tobacco use status served dual roles: as a primary outcome in models assessing predictors (including ethnic identity) and as a key explanatory variable in models evaluating associations with self-rated health and chronic disease.

No prior studies have examined ethnic identity in relation to tobacco use among Arab Americans, precluding derivation of effect size estimates for a priori power calculation. Recruitment barriers facing this population, including concerns about political surveillance, discrimination, and social stigma around tobacco disclosure, further constrained enrollment. Post hoc power analyses using observed effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (alpha = 0.05, two-tailed) indicated moderate power for the primary ethnic identity association (67%) and the single-product SRH association (55%), and very low power for interaction terms (5–17%). These estimates should be treated as upper bounds given the tendency for small-sample effect sizes to be inflated. Covariate selection prioritized theoretical and empirical relevance consistent with events-per-variable guidance, and interaction models are interpreted with particular caution. To address the first research question, ordered logistic regression examined whether higher ethnic identity scores were associated with lower tobacco use (operationalized as 0, 1, or ≥2 products). This ordering assumes escalating product burden across categories; it does not capture nicotine dependence or use frequency, and a participant using multiple products occasionally may have lower dependence than a heavy single-product user. This is an acknowledged limitation of the classification. For the second research question, ordered logistic regression assessed the association between tobacco use status and SRH (categorized as Excellent/Very Good, Good, or Fair/Poor). The proportional odds assumption was evaluated using generalized ordered logistic models; no violation was detected. To address the final research question, logistic regression tested whether tobacco use was associated with greater odds of chronic disease diagnosis. All models were adjusted for age, gender, education, income, employment, marital status, generational status, language spoken at home, and region of origin. Interaction terms between tobacco use and ethnic identity were tested to assess potential effect modification of the association between tobacco use and SRH and tobacco use and chronic disease diagnosis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of findings under alternative model specifications. All analyses were performed using Stata 18 BE.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The analytic sample included 112 Arab American adults residing in Central California. Over half of participants (51.8%) reported no current tobacco use; 22.3% were single-product users and 25.9% were multi-product users. Participants were predominantly male (56.1%) and younger than 30 years old (55.8%). Age distribution differed significantly by tobacco use group (p < 0.001), with a higher proportion of multi-product users were 46 years old or older (57.1%). Educational attainment also varied by tobacco use status (p = 0.046), with greater proportions of multi-product users holding a bachelor’s degree. Most participants reported low household income (67.7%) and were employed (58.5%). Overall, 33.6% of participants reported having been diagnosed with at least one chronic disease, with prevalence highest among multi-product users (p = 0.008). Tobacco use did not significantly differ by marital status, generational status, or region of origin, or self-rated health. Ethnic Identity Scores

The mean ethnic identity score for the full sample was 2.97 (SD = 0.66, range 1.1–4.0). Scores differed significantly across tobacco use groups (F(2,107) = 9.33, p < 0.001), with non-users reporting the highest mean scores (M = 3.22, SD = 0.55) compared to single-product (M = 2.67, SD = 0.75) and multi-product users (M = 2.75, SD = 0.60). No significant differences in ethnic identity were observed by age group (F(2,101) = 0.14, p = 0.872), generational status (t(106) = −0.18, p = 0.861), or gender (t(105) = −1.61, p = 0.111). Tobacco Use Status

Table 3 presents unadjusted and adjusted ordered logistic regression estimates for the association between ethnic identity and tobacco use. In adjusted analyses, higher ethnic identity scores were significantly associated with lower odds of tobacco use (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.15–0.83; p = 0.018). Language spoken at home also demonstrated a strong association: participants who reported speaking a non-English language at home had markedly lower odds of tobacco use compared to English or predominantly English speakers (OR = 0.02, 95% CI: 0.00–0.26; p = 0.003). Females, those of higher income, and those with some college education had significantly lower odds of tobacco use (gender: OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.06–0.60; p = 0.005; income: OR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.02–0.72; p = 0.019; education: OR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.03–0.68; p = 0.015). Discussion

This study contributes evidence on the role of ethnic identity in shaping tobacco use behaviors and health outcomes among Arab American adults, a population largely absent from the ethnic identity and tobacco literature. In this sample of Arab American adults residing in Central California, stronger ethnic identity was associated with significantly lower odds of current tobacco use, independent of socioeconomic, demographic, and immigration-related factors. Unlike prior Arab American tobacco research, which has relied predominantly on acculturation measures, this study treated ethnic identity as an independent predictor, capturing the internalized, affective dimensions of cultural belonging that acculturation proxies are not designed to assess. These findings suggest that cultural affiliation, when measured as an internally held sense of pride and belonging rather than as a degree of assimilation or separation from the dominant culture, may function as a protective factor against tobacco use.

The biopsychosocial model, as originally conceptualized by Engel, 43 posits that health outcomes are shaped by the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors. In the context of this study, ethnic identity may be associated with a potential psychological buffering role, intersecting with social determinants to shape tobacco use behavior and health outcomes. Participants with stronger ethnic identity had significantly lower odds of tobacco use independent of socioeconomic status, generational status, and language use, suggesting that the psychological dimension of cultural belonging was associated with tobacco behavior above and beyond the structural and social conditions that typically anchor biopsychosocial explanations of tobacco disparities. Ethnic identity did not, however, significantly moderate the association between tobacco use and health outcomes, which is consistent with a pattern in which its protective association is more proximal to behavior than to downstream health status. These associations suggest a potential adjustment to how the biopsychosocial model is applied in immigrant tobacco research: ethnic identity may be associated with a buffering role that operates upstream of measurable health outcomes. Future research should incorporate validated measures of anxiety, depression, and other psychological factors, as these are well-established correlates of both tobacco use and ethnic identity, and may represent important mediating pathways. Incorporating ethnic identity alongside stress, coping, social support, and mental health variables would produce a more complete biopsychosocial account of tobacco-related health disparities in Arab American and other immigrant populations.

These findings extend prior research on ethnic identity and tobacco use to a population that has been largely absent from this literature. Consistent with studies in African American, Hispanic/Latino, Asian American, and American Indian populations, stronger ethnic identity was associated with lower tobacco use, reinforcing health-promoting values and reducing risk behaviors.30,37-40 In contrast, acculturation has shown more mixed effects: while bicultural adaptation can foster resilience, a strong orientation toward dominant norms without cultural grounding has been associated with identity conflict, heightened stress, and adverse health outcomes. 19 Our findings challenge the assumption that lower acculturation is uniformly associated with greater tobacco use among Arab Americans. Participants who primarily spoke a heritage (non-English) language at home had markedly lower odds of tobacco use compared to English speakers, while other acculturation proxies such as generational status and region of origin, were not significantly associated with tobacco use or health outcomes. These results underscore the importance of treating ethnic identity and acculturation as related but non-interchangeable constructs; public health strategies should move beyond deficit-based frameworks and recognize cultural continuity as a potential foundation for health promotion.

The protective association between ethnic identity and tobacco use may operate through culturally grounded social norms and obligations that discourage smoking. It is important to acknowledge, however, that tobacco use, particularly waterpipe smoking, is often socially accepted in some Arab American communities. Different tobacco products carry distinct cultural meanings, perceived harm profiles, and social contexts in Arab American communities. Waterpipe use is frequently embedded in social and family settings, associated with hospitality, leisure, and communal bonding, and widely perceived as less harmful than cigarettes. Cigarette smoking, by contrast, carries greater stigma in many Arab American households, particularly for women. E-cigarettes occupy a more ambiguous position, often perceived by users as a cessation tool or a harm-reduced alternative, though this perception is not supported by current evidence. These product-specific distinctions have direct implications for intervention: messaging that addresses hookah as a social behavior may require different framing than messaging about cigarette cessation or e-cigarette initiation. Future research should disaggregate tobacco use by product type, frequency, and social context to better capture these heterogeneous patterns. 52 Prior studies have documented environments in which smoking occurs in familial and social settings, contributing to the normalization of tobacco use. 53 The inverse relationship between ethnic identity and tobacco use observed here suggests that cultural affiliation is not inherently linked to tobacco normalization. Rather, strong ethnic identity may reflect internalized religious, familial, or ethical norms that promote health-conscious behavior. The widespread acceptance of smoking in some settings may reflect gaps in culturally appropriate health education rather than deeply held communal values endorsing tobacco use. Public health interventions should avoid framing tobacco use as culturally inherent and instead engage with community narratives that promote collective well-being and discourage harmful behaviors.

Our finding that ethnic identity was not independently associated with self-rated health contrasts with the Detroit Arab American Study, which found that U.S.-born and English-speaking Arab Americans were more likely to report good health than Arabic-speaking immigrants. 54 The divergence may reflect differences in sample composition, recruitment context, and the sociopolitical landscape facing Arab Americans in the post-9/11 era versus the present study period. In our sample, language use was not significantly associated with self-rated health in adjusted models. U.S.-born participants, however, had markedly lower odds of reporting good health compared to first-generation immigrants, a pattern consistent with the immigrant health paradox documented across multiple immigrant groups. 46 This aligns with evidence that newly arrived immigrants, including those from the Middle East, tend to arrive with positive health selection and relatively favorable health profiles that erode with longer U.S. residence. 46 Importantly, among newly legalized immigrants with relatively high education levels, English-language ability has been shown to function as a marker of socioeconomic advantage rather than acculturation to unhealthy U.S. norms, a nuance relevant to our sample, where language at home was strongly associated with tobacco use but not with self-rated health. These findings collectively suggest that acculturation-related factors shape specific health behaviors such as tobacco use differently than they shape overall health self-perceptions, and that future research should examine these pathways separately.

Tobacco use was significantly associated with self-rated health in this study for single-product but not multi-product users. This pattern is unexpected and should be interpreted cautiously given the study’s limited statistical power and the heterogeneity of the multi-product category. The treatment of tobacco use as an ordered variable assumes escalating risk from non-use to single-product to multi-product use, but this ordering does not capture dependence or exposure intensity. An occasional multi-product user with low overall consumption may have substantially lower health impact than a heavy daily single-product smoker. Prior research indicates that poly-users often combine combustible cigarettes with products such as e-cigarettes or hookah, which are commonly perceived as less harmful.55,56 These perceptions may attenuate the negative impact of tobacco use on subjective health evaluations. 57 The wide confidence intervals in the multi-product group also reflect variability in use patterns and limited statistical power. Future studies should incorporate product-specific frequency and quantity measures to disaggregate use within each category and to test whether dependence or cumulative exposure better predict health outcomes than product count alone. These findings collectively point to the need for culturally tailored tobacco cessation interventions that address product-specific norms, leverage ethnic identity as a potential motivational resource, and are designed with the recruitment and engagement constraints of Arab American communities in mind.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between cultural factors, tobacco use, and health outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how identity and behavior evolve over time. The sample size reflects substantial barriers to recruiting Arab American adults in this region. Documented community experiences of political surveillance, discrimination, and social stigma around tobacco disclosure made recruitment challenging, and no formal a priori power analysis was conducted. Post hoc power estimates confirmed these constraints: 67% for the primary ethnic identity association, 55% for the single-product SRH finding, and 5–17% for interaction terms, suggesting null moderation findings reflect insufficient power rather than a true absence of effect. The sample was drawn from one state and may not represent Arab Americans in other geographic or sociopolitical contexts. The tobacco use variable was operationalized as an ordered count of products, which assumes escalating exposure across categories. This assumption does not account for frequency or quantity of use, and occasional multi-product users may have lower nicotine dependence than heavy single-product users. The current cigarette smoker definition includes occasional smokers and does not allow evaluation of nicotine dependence. Mental health variables, including depression and anxiety, were not assessed. Secure ethnic identity has been associated with better mental health outcomes in prior research, and mental health status may represent an important pathway between ethnic identity and tobacco use. Future studies should include validated mental health measures to examine this potential mechanism. All measures relied on self-report, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Finally, while the ethnic identity scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, the abbreviated adaptation used here has not been independently validated in Arab American samples, and cultural expression of identity may vary considerably across subgroups within the community.

Future Directions

Future studies should examine how ethnic identity and acculturation independently and jointly influence tobacco use and related health behaviors. Larger, multi-site samples are needed to disaggregate results by gender, religious affiliation, and national origin. Longitudinal designs would allow investigation of how identity, behavior, and health change across generations. Mixed-method approaches, including qualitative research, can provide deeper insight into the social and cultural contexts in which tobacco use is initiated, maintained, or resisted. Research should also disaggregate multi-product use by product type, frequency, and user motivation, and test culturally tailored, identity-affirming strategies for tobacco prevention and cessation in Arab American communities.

Policy Implications

These findings point to the need for culturally grounded tobacco control strategies that move beyond traditional acculturation frameworks. Tobacco prevention efforts should recognize ethnic identity among Arab Americans as a potential protective resource, rather than framing cultural retention as a potential barrier to health. Community-based interventions can leverage cultural pride, religious values, and collective health narratives to discourage tobacco use, particularly in settings where smoking is socially tolerated. Messaging should address misperceptions about the relative harms of products such as hookah, using culturally resonant language and delivery channels. At the policy level, federal and state surveillance systems should include Arab Americans as a distinct ethnic category to support accurate monitoring and policy development.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that ethnic identity is inversely associated with tobacco use among Arab Americans, independent of acculturation-related and sociodemographic factors. These findings challenge deficit-based frameworks that equate cultural assimilation with health improvement, and highlight the potential of identity-affirming, culturally responsive approaches to tobacco control. Future research should examine these relationships in larger, more geographically diverse samples and test community-based interventions that leverage cultural identity as a foundation for health promotion.
