Protocol for synchronizing waveforms and videos from electropenetrography studies of arthropods
Authors: Anastasia M.W. Cooper, Kaitlin Pfeiffer, Kathryn E. Reif, Kristopher Silver, Dana N. Mitzel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlternating current–direct current (AC-DC) electropenetrography (EPG) is a non-invasive approach for quantifying parasitic arthropods’ probing and ingestion behaviors inside host tissues. Here, we present a procedure for using the Observer XT behavioral coding software to synchronize EPG waveforms with video recordings. We describe the steps for performing, importing, and analyzing simultaneous recordings. This protocol has potential applications for the initial correlation of waveforms with arthropod biological activities.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Cooper et al.1

Before you begin

Electropenetrography (EPG) is a non-invasive technique for quantifying intra-tissue host-arthropod interactions and behaviors. EPG is commonly used to study plant-feeding arthropods with piercing-sucking mouthparts and has been instrumental in understanding host plant resistance, pathogen transmission, and pesticide modes of action in many systems.2,3 In addition, the use of EPG to study oviposition and blood feeding is increasing due to recent advances in EPG instrumentation.3 The third-generation AC-DC EPG has variable settings and improved noise processing capabilities, making it better suited to various arthropods and hosts.3 However, to use EPG on new species, waveforms must be correlated with behaviors and biological activities.2,4 Synchronizing observed waveforms with video recordings of the arthropods during EPG can aid in defining the biological meanings of the waveforms.1

The protocol presented here describes the steps for correlating waveforms and videos of Culex tarsalis mosquitoes feeding on a human hand when recorded with an AC-DC electropenetrograph and a Dino-Lite digital microscope, as performed in Cooper et al.1 This protocol has also been used on other blood-feeding arthropods (Culex quinquefasciatus, Aedes aegypti, and Culicoides sonorensis) and hosts (mice) (Cooper et al., unpublished), and it can be used on any host/arthropod system for which EPG protocols exist or are being developed. It can also be used with DC electropenetrograph recordings and other digital microscope types using slight modifications. However, waveform files recorded using STYLET+ or PROBE5 instead of WinDaq software6 must be converted to appropriately formatted CSV files for import into Observer XT7 software. In addition, the appropriate video recording device must be selected in the MediaRecorder8 software.

The most important thing is to ensure that the variables of interest are in view. The head amp only needs to be in view when turned on, as described in Step 5. After that, the camera can be positioned as required to observe behaviors of interest. No standard point of view or magnification works for all experiments or arthropods, as each arthropod holds itself differently and probes different locations on the host. For example, if the goal is to observe when the abdomen starts to swell, then having the camera to the side where the abdomen can be viewed is usually best. If the goal is to observe maxillary palp movements and correlate them with waveforms, then focusing closely on the head is best. However, sometimes the ideal position is not feasible due to the location where the arthropod is probing, such as between the fingers on a human hand. Similarly, the ideal magnification depends on what the investigator is trying to visualize and how close the arthropod will tolerate the camera being.

Expected outcomes

This protocol enables the correlation of visible behaviors and biological activities with waveforms. AC-DC EPG recordings result in WDQ files containing voltage time series data generated by the arthropod-host interaction. When plotted, the voltage changes appear as recognizable patterns (i.e., waveforms; Figure 5) corresponding to biological activities during the host-arthropod interaction. The digital microscope recordings result in MP4 files containing videos of the general arthropod-host interaction (Video 1). The visualization analysis of the synchronized video and waveforms results in pictorial representations depicting which behaviors occur during each waveform (Figure 4) for each observation/ recording/arthropod. The visible behaviors that occur during each waveform (such as head, palp, and body movements, abdominal changes, and release of excretory droplets) can be used to generate or support hypotheses about the biological meaning of each waveform. For instance, when this procedure was used on Cx. tarsalis mosquitoes probing and ingesting on human hands, the results supported the hypotheses that Waveform family J corresponds to pre-probing, family K to penetration, L to search for a blood vessel, M with blood ingestion, N to an unknown behavior, and W with withdrawal1 (Figure 6). In addition, the synchronized videos and waveforms playing in Observer XT can be recorded and annotated with additional software to showcase specific behaviors that occur during the waveforms (Video 2). Quantification and statistical analysis

The count and duration of the waveforms can be used to calculate sequential and non-sequential EPG variables for statistical analysis as previously described.1,9,13 The exported numerical data from Observer XT can be directly analyzed using standard parametric and non-parametric statistical procedures as appropriate (i.e., Student t-test, ANOVA with a post hoc Kruskal Wallis test, etc.) to detect significant differences in the duration, count, and rate of the observed behaviors during specific waveforms in each treatment.

Limitations

This protocol has several logistical, technical, and experimental limitations. As Cooper et al.1 discussed, it can be challenging to keep the arthropod in focus at an ideal angle for observation while recording, especially if the host is not entirely still. In addition, the bright light on the digital microscope may affect arthropod behavior, and turning off the light using the manufacturer’s software may interfere with visibility. Another limitation is that synchronization may not be precise enough to detect precisely when very brief events happen, such as penetration of biting midge mouthparts.14 However, the main limitation of this procedure is that it is only effective for visible behaviors, such as head and body movements and abdominal changes, which can be used to infer mouthpart activities and positions. Unless the arthropods can be induced to probe on a transparent or semi-transparent substrate (e.g., a mouse ear with a bright light behind it), many mouthpart movements and activities, such as salivation, cannot be correlated with waveforms using this method. Thus, additional experimental approaches (e.g., histology, styletectomy, markers of salivation or ingestion) are needed to correlate the position of the mouthparts and mouthpart activities inside host tissue.4,9 Nonetheless, the protocol presented here can be used to generate hypotheses regarding the waveforms' biological meaning and provide additional lines of support for correlation studies.

It is also important to note that scoring waveforms and arthropod behaviors is subjective and requires a trained eye.5,9 Thus, it is recommended that one person score the entire dataset to ensure that all data is scored consistently.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The waveforms are distorted or not visible in Observer XT after importing CSV files (step 27).

Potential solution

If the text in the cells in rows 1 and 2 of the CSV file does not precisely match the file used to create the import profile, the waveforms may not be displayed. Ensure that every CSV file is formatted the same and matches the import profile. See Figures 1 and 2 for examples of proper formatting.

Verify that the CSV file contains the expected number of rows of numerical data. If the sampling rate of the EPG was set to 100 samples per channel per second during recording, as is the standard in the field, then there should be 100 rows of data for every second of the recording duration. If there are fewer rows of numerical data in the CSV file than expected, then the compression was incorrectly set during the creation of the CSV files (or data was accidentally deleted from the CSV file). As noted in step 15, the compression must be set to 1 (i.e., 0.200 sec/div) before saving as a CSV file, or insufficient data points will be exported, and the waveforms will be very low resolution, if visible. To check the compression, click “Edit” or “Compression” in Waveform Browser.

If a non-standard sampling rate was used to record the EPG files (i.e., anything other than 100 samples per channel per second), adjust the sample rate in the import profile (step 25dii). Likewise, if any other differences exist between the files and recording conditions described here (and in the referenced protocols9,10), adjust the import profile as appropriate. Consult Noldus Customer Support for guidance regarding proper adjustments, if needed.

Problem 2

The waveforms and video do not line up despite multiple synchronization attempts (step 49).

Potential solution

Verify that the correct files were imported (i.e., that the video and CSV file for the same observation were imported).

Review the EPG and video recordings and make sure that neither the media recorder nor the WinDaq Hardware manager stopped recording prematurely (i.e., before the insect finished probing).

The solution for missing voltage data in the CSV file is discussed above (see problem: waveforms are distorted or not visible).

Problem 3

Fine details of the waveforms are not visible in Observer XT, making scoring challenging (step 51).

Potential solution

Zooming in on the waveforms and adjusting the panel size where the waveforms are displayed can help make less-defined waveform features visible. The keystrokes required to adjust the zoom (i.e., the horizontal compression) of the waveforms displayed in Observer XT are described in steps 34–35. The “External Data and Audio” panel, where the waveforms are displayed, can be enlarged (up to a point) by hovering the mouse over the border until the mouse becomes an arrow and then clicking and dragging the border outward. This action is somewhat comparable to adjusting the software gain in WinDaq to expand or shrink the waveform vertically.

If the control box gain is set too low during the initial EPG recording, resulting in a weak EPG signal, waveform details may not be visible in Observer XT even after adjusting the panel size and zoom. In this situation, it can be helpful to open the original WDQ file in the WinDaq Waveform Browser software on an adjacent monitor, position the cursor at the start of the waveform, and then adjust the compression and software gain in WinDaq until the appearance of the waveform in WinDaq matches the appearance of the waveform in Observer XT. The location of the waveform in Observer XT can then be inferred based on identifiable features in the recording (e.g., N starts halfway between the massive peak in the middle of the recording and the two smaller peaks near the end). Ideally, the appropriate control box gain settings are optimized before recording observations for synchronization to ensure a robust EPG signal with well-defined peaks that are clearly visible.Note: Waveform resolution is lower in Observer XT than in WinDaq Waveform Browser. If the waveform features (i.e., peaks and repeating patterns) are not clearly visible in WinDaq Waveform Browser, they will not be visible in Observer XT and must be excluded from the analysis. Likewise, if waveforms cannot be accurately scored for any reason10 (e.g., peaking out, fold over, gain changes, noise, unstable baseline), then the recording is unsuitable for use in this protocol.
