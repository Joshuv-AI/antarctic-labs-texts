RBMX promotes colorectal liver metastasis by stabilizing MTHFD2 mRNA to regulate redox homeostasis
Authors: Chengrong Jiang, Guanyi Liao, Qingling Li, Kun Yang, Tao Guo, Suhe Lai, Hong Peng, Jie Hu, Minli Yang, Jinjun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver metastasis is the primary cause of mortality in colorectal cancer (CRC), yet the molecular mechanisms driving colorectal liver metastasis (CRLM) remain poorly understood. This study investigates the role of RNA-binding motif protein X (RBMX) in CRLM progression and its underlying mechanisms.MethodsRBMX expression and clinical significance were analyzed in public databases (GEO, TCGA) and in-house cohorts. Metastatic function was assessed using in vitro and in vivo models. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), and RNA pull-down assays were employed to identify downstream targets. Metabolic profiling, reactive oxygen species (ROS) quantification, and signaling pathway analyses were used to evaluate redox homeostasis.ResultsRBMX was upregulated in CRLM tissues and correlated with poor prognosis. It significantly promoted CRC cells migration, invasion, and liver metastasis. Mechanistically, RBMX directly bound to and stabilized MTHFD2 mRNA, increasing its protein abundance. This upregulation rewired one-carbon metabolism to boost NADPH production, effectively buffering intracellular ROS. Consequently, RBMX suppressed ROS-dependent p38/JNK stress signaling, sustaining metastatic cell survival.ConclusionsOur study reveals that RBMX drives CRLM by stabilizing MTHFD2 mRNA to maintain redox homeostasis. This “RBMX–MTHFD2–Redox” axis links RNA stability to metabolic plasticity, representing a novel mechanistic vulnerability in CRLM and promising candidate for preclinical therapeutic development and biomarker investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06336-5.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Among the factors dictating patient prognosis, liver metastasis (CRLM) serves as the principal determinant [2]. Despite significant advancements in multimodal therapeutic strategies, approximately 50% of patients eventually progress to metastatic disease [3]. This progression precipitates a drastic decline in clinical outcomes, with the five-year survival rate for metastatic CRC (mCRC) remaining dismally below 15% [4]. Therefore, unraveling the molecular underpinnings of CRLM remains a critical priority for improving patient outcomes.

The metastatic cascade is a multi-step evolutionary process that necessitates rapid phenotypic plasticity [5]. Growing evidence suggests that this plasticity is governed not only by transcriptional networks but critically by post-transcriptional mechanisms, including RNA stability, splicing, and translation [6]. In this context, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have emerged as pivotal effectors that orchestrate tumorigenesis, metastasis, and metabolic reprogramming [7]. Despite the annotation of over 1500 RBPs in the human genome [8], the extent to which these proteins reshape the oncogenic transcriptome remains largely elusive. RNA-binding motif protein X (RBMX), also known as hnRNP G, is a nuclear RBP canonically characterized for its roles in pre-mRNA splicing and maintaining genomic integrity via DNA damage repair [9, 10]. While aberrant RBMX expression has been implicated in malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma and bladder cancer, where it modulates the cell cycle and apoptosis [11, 12], its specific biological function and mechanistic contribution to CRC, particularly in the context of CRLM, remain poorly understood.

Successful liver metastasis is severely constrained by the oxidative stress inherent to the foreign microenvironment [13]. Consequently, metastatic competence hinges on the ability of tumor cells to rewire their metabolism for enhanced antioxidant capacity [14]. Methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (MTHFD2), a pivotal enzyme in the mitochondrial folate cycle, supports this redox balance by generating NADPH [15]. Although current studies have established that MTHFD2 upregulation promotes tumor proliferation, immune evasion and drug resistance [16–18], in various malignancies, the upstream mechanisms regulating its abundance in metastatic contexts are poorly defined. A key open question is whether RBPs contribute to the post-transcriptional stabilization of MTHFD2 mRNA to drive CRLM progression.

In the present study, we found that RBMX was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues from patients with liver metastasis compared with those from patients without metastasis. Based on mechanistic studies, we identified that RBMX-mediated stabilization of MTHFD2 mRNA and the subsequent maintenance of redox homeostasis were responsible for CRLM. Collectively, our findings identify the “RBMX–MTHFD2–redox” axis as a critical mechanistic node in CRLM progression, offering a conceptual advance in understanding how RBPs orchestrate metastatic metabolism.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptomic profiles of CRC and matched adjacent normal tissues (ANTs) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE84984, GSE31905, GSE21815, and GSE73360) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) COADREAD project. RBPs lists were obtained from the RBPWorld database. CLIP-seq data were retrieved from the ENCORI database. For data preprocessing, raw data were normalized and log2-transformed using R software (v4.2.1). Subsequently, differentially expressed RBPs (DERBPs) were identified using limma/DESeq2 with rigorous filtering. Overall survival was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier method.

Clinical specimens and ethics

A total of 40 paired CRC tissues from patients without metastasis and 40 paired CRC tissues from patients with concurrent CRLM were obtained from patients undergoing radical or palliative resection at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Bishan Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. All diagnoses were histologically confirmed by two independent pathologists. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Bishan Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. cqbykyll-20240918-2) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell culture

The human CRC cell lines HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291, MSI-H), RKO (RRID: CVCL_0504, MSI-H), DLD1 (RRID: CVCL_0248, MSI-H), HT29 (RRID: CVCL_0320, MSS) and Caco-2 (RRID: CVCL_0025, MSS) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The normal human colonic epithelial cell line NCM460 (RRID: CVCL_0460, MSS) was obtained from INCELL Corporation (San Antonio, TX, USA). All cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were routinely screened to confirm negative status for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. All cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Lentivirus, plasmids and small interfering RNA (siRNA)

Lentiviral constructs for RBMX/MTHFD2 knockdown and overexpression were obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai). For lentiviral infection, Cells were seeded 24 h prior to infection to reach 20–30% confluence. Virus was added at the volume calculated by Viral volume = (MOI × cell number)/viral titer. HCT116 and DLD1 cells were infected at MOI = 10, RKO cells at MOI = 5, with 2 µg/mL polybrene supplemented. After 16 h, the medium was replaced. At 72 h post-infection, cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 14 days to generate stable polyclonal pools. The pcDNA3.1-RBMX-3×Flag plasmids and truncated mutants were constructed by Ruijing Biotech (Hangzhou) and siRNAs targeting RBMX and MTHFD2 were purchased from Tsingke Biotech (Beijing). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) per manufacturer’s instructions. Knockdown/overexpression efficiencies were validated by qRT-PCR and western blotting. Detailed sequences for all shRNAs, siRNAs, and primers are listed in Table S1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent (Tiangen), and 1 µg RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA (MedChemExpress). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Premix (Accurate Biology) on a real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to the internal control β-actin and calculated using the comparative cycle threshold 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The specific sequences of all primers used are provided in Table S2.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), blocked with 5% skimmed milk, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using ECL detection.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (8-µm pore size; Corning). For invasion assays, membranes were pre-coated with 40 µL Matrigel (1:8 dilution; Corning) and polymerized at 37 °C for 3 h. Cells were harvested, resuspended in serum-free medium, and 5 × 104–1 × 105 cells in 200 µL were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 600 µL medium with 10% FBS as chemoattractant. After 24–48 h, non-migrated/invaded cells were removed with a cotton swab; traversed cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in six random fields per insert under an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany).

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (5–10 × 105 cells/well) and cultured until they reached approximately 90% confluence. A uniform scratch was generated across the monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip. After washing with PBS to remove debris, serum-free medium was added to minimize proliferation effects. Wound closure was monitored by imaging the same field at 0, 24 and 48 h using an inverted microscope. The scratch width was measured with ImageJ software, and the migration rate was calculated as follows: Migration rate (%) = [(Width at 0 h − Width at 48 h)/Width at 0 h] × 100.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and H&E staining

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to EDTA-based antigen retrieval. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton, sections were treated with peroxidase blocking solution, followed by blocking buffer. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB substrate were applied, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Staining was scored semi-quantitatively by two blinded pathologists (IHC score = intensity × percentage of positive cells). H&E staining was performed using standard protocols.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from RKO cells with stable RBMX knockdown and control cells, and transcriptome sequencing was performed using the Illumina platform. Each sample had three biological replicates. Raw reads were quality filtered by fastp and mapped to the human GRCh38 reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). RSEM was used for gene expression quantification, and DESeq2 was employed to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes with absolute fold change > 1.2 and FDR < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction were considered DEGs. KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on DEGs. Additionally, KEGG pre-ranked GSEA was conducted on the full transcriptome to assess overall pathway alterations. The raw RNA-seq data generated in this study have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number (PRJNA1413365).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed using the Magna RIP™ Kit (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells (~ 1 × 107) were collected and lysed; 10% of lysate was reserved as Input. The remaining lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Flag antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) or control IgG (Cell Signaling Technology), then with magnetic beads for 3 h at 4 °C. After washing, 20% of beads were used for protein extraction and western blotting to assess IP efficiency; the remaining 80% were used for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR to analyze MTHFD2 mRNA enrichment (presented as fold enrichment).

In vitro transcription

The procedure followed the High Yield T7 Biotin16 RNA Labeling Kit (APExBIO). Components were mixed at room temperature and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by purification of synthesized RNA. The specific sequences of all primers used are provided in Table S3.

RNA pulldown

RNA pulldown was performed using the BersinBio RNA Pulldown Kit (BersinBio). Biotin-labeled MTHFD2 mRNA probes were synthesized by in vitro transcription and incubated with CRC cell lysates for 1 h at room temperature. Streptavidin beads were then added for 30 min, washed, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS-PAGE loading buffer. RBMX enrichment was detected by western blotting.

mRNA stability assay

Cells were treated with actinomycin D (10 µg/mL) to block nascent RNA transcription. Cells were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 h post-treatment, and total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA in equal volumes. The remaining MTHFD2 mRNA levels were detected by qRT-PCR, with the 0-hour time point set as 100% to calculate relative residual levels and plot degradation curves.

Animal experiments

Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from Chengdu Engo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The phenotypic experiment contained three groups (n = 6 mice per group) and the rescue experiment contained four groups (n = 6 mice per group). For liver metastasis modeling, mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, the spleen was exposed via a left subcostal incision, and ~ 2 × 106 stably transfected CRC cells in 20 µL basal medium were injected into the splenic pulp. Mice were euthanized 3–4 weeks post-injection; livers were harvested for macroscopic nodule evaluation, then fixed, embedded, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-CQMU-2024-0407) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

NADPH/NADP⁺, GSH/GSSG and ROS detection

NADP+/NADPH Assay Kit (WST-8 method), Total Glutathione Assay Kit and ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime) were used according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Standard curves were prepared as instructed. Total NADPH/NADP+, NADPH amount, and GSH and GSSG levels were measured using a microplate reader. ROS expression was directly observed under a fluorescence microscope and quantified using a fluorescence microplate reader.

Antibodies and reagents

The primary antibodies used in this study were as follows: anti-RBMX (Abcam, ab190352, RRID: AB_2179144), anti-MTHFD2 (Proteintech, 12270-1-AP, RRID: AB_2147525), anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804, RRID: AB_262044), anti-β-actin (Servicebio, GB11001-100), anti-ERK (Proteintech, 11257-1-AP, RRID: AB_2139822), anti-p-ERK (absin, abs128832), anti-p38 (abmart, T55600S, RRID: AB_2936971), anti-p-p38 (ABclonal, AP1311), anti-JNK (ABclonal, A4867, RRID: AB_2863367), anti-p-JNK (ABclonal, AP1337, RRID: AB_3697809), and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO, ZB-2305, RRID: AB_2747415).

Reagents for cell culture and transfection included: High-glucose DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT), RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, C11875500BT), Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099-141), Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122), TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, 12605028), Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco, 31985-070), and Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were assessed by two-tailed Student’s t-test; comparisons among multiple groups used one-way ANOVA. Survival was analyzed by Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank test. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. Clinicopathological associations were analyzed by chi-square, Fisher’s exact tests or Mantel-Haenszel linear-by-linear association test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R (v4.2.1). P < 0.05 was considered significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

RBMX is upregulated in colorectal liver metastasis and predicts poor clinical prognosis

To identify RBPs involved in CRC progression, we analyzed transcriptomic data from GSE84984 and GSE31905. Intersecting DEGs with an RBP database yielded 40 candidates (Fig. 1 A, Fig. S1A). Survival analysis of the TCGA-COAD/READ cohort identified RBMX as the most prognostically significant, with high expression correlating with shorter overall survival (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1B).

RBMX mRNA was consistently upregulated in CRC across multiple datasets (TCGA, GSE84984, GSE31905, GSE21815, GSE73360) (Fig. 1C-D, Fig. S1C). In our in-house cohort (n = 80), both RBMX mRNA and protein were elevated in tumors, with higher levels in patients with liver metastasis (Fig. 1E–F, Fig. S1D). IHC staining confirmed a progressive increase from primary tumors of non-metastatic patients to those with liver metastasis (Fig. 1G). These data identify RBMX as a clinically relevant RBP upregulated in CRLM.

RBMX drives the metastatic potential of colorectal cancer cells in vitro and in vivo

Given the clinical correlation between elevated RBMX expression and metastasis, we investigated its functional role in CRC cells. We first examined endogenous RBMX expression in CRC cell lines. Higher RBMX expression was observed in highly metastatic HCT116 and RKO cells, while low basal expression was detected in low-metastatic DLD1 cells (Fig. S1E). Accordingly, we established stable RBMX knockdown in HCT116 and RKO cells, and overexpression in HCT116 and DLD1 cells, which was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2A–B). Using HCT116 for both loss- and gain-of-function assays enables bidirectional verification of RBMX function within the same genetic background.

Functionally, RBMX knockdown significantly reduced cell migration and invasion, while overexpression enhanced these capacities in Transwell and wound healing assays (Fig. 2C–F), indicating that RBMX promotes malignant phenotypes in vitro. Using a splenic-injection liver metastasis model, RBMX-knockdown HCT116 cells produced markedly fewer and smaller hepatic metastatic nodules than controls, as confirmed by macroscopic examination and H&E staining (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrate that RBMX is essential for the survival and growth of CRC cells in the liver.

RBMX directly binds and stabilizes MTHFD2 mRNA

To explore the downstream network of RBMX, we performed RNA-seq on RBMX-silenced RKO cells, identifying 1136 DEGs (790 downregulated, 346 upregulated; Fig. 3 A). Intersecting the downregulated genes with predicted RBMX targets from ENCORI yielded 65 candidates (Fig. 3B). Among these, MTHFD2 emerged as the top candidate after qRT-PCR validation (Fig. 3 C). Clinical data showed that MTHFD2 is overexpressed in CRC and inversely correlates with survival (Fig. 3D–E). RBMX positively regulated MTHFD2 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3 F, Fig. S2A).

RIP assays using Flag-tagged RBMX confirmed enrichment of MTHFD2 mRNA (Fig. 3G, Fig. S2B–C). To map the interacting domains, we generated deletion mutants targeting five canonical domains of RBMX (RRM, RGG1, SRR, TRR, C-RBD; Fig. 3H). RIP assays showed that deletion of the RRM domain (∆RRM), but not others, abolished MTHFD2 binding (Fig. 3I, Fig. S2D–E), identifying RRM as the essential interface. To locate the binding site on MTHFD2 mRNA, RNA pull-down assays using probes spanning the 5’ UTR, CDS, and 3’ UTR revealed specific RBMX binding to the 3’ UTR (Fig. 3 J). Guided by CLIP-seq data from ENCORI (Fig. 3 K), we designed three truncated 3’ UTR fragments (F1–F3). Pull-down assays narrowed binding to F1 (nt 1342–1941). Further truncation of F1 into F4 (nt 1342–1560), F5 (nt 1561–1620), and F6 (nt 1621–1941) pinpointed the minimal motif to nt 1561–1620 (Fig. 3L).

Functionally, actinomycin D assays showed that RBMX knockdown shortened MTHFD2 mRNA half-life, while wild-type RBMX overexpression extended it. The ∆RRM mutant failed to stabilize MTHFD2 mRNA (Fig. 3M–N), confirming that RBMX enhances MTHFD2 stability via RRM-mediated binding to the nt 1561–1620 region.

MTHFD2 is indispensable for RBMX-mediated metastatic progression

To validate MTHFD2 as a functional downstream effector of RBMX, we performed rescue experiments (Fig. 4A–B). Ectopic MTHFD2 overexpression significantly reversed the impairment of migration and invasion caused by RBMX knockdown in Transwell assays (Fig. 4 C), with consistent results observed in wound healing assays (Fig. S3A). Of note, MTHFD2 restoration achieved partial rescue, suggesting the involvement of additional downstream effectors. Conversely, silencing MTHFD2 in RBMX-overexpressing cells abrogated the enhanced migratory and invasive capacities (Fig. 4D, Fig. S3B), confirming that MTHFD2 is required for RBMX-driven metastasis.

In vivo, RBMX knockdown markedly reduced hepatic metastatic burden in a splenic injection model. Re-introduction of MTHFD2 significantly rescued this phenotype, increasing both the number and size of liver metastases (Fig. 4E). H&E staining confirmed that MTHFD2 restoration counteracted the suppression of micrometastasis formation induced by RBMX loss. Together, these data demonstrate that MTHFD2 is an indispensable downstream effector of RBMX-mediated CRLM.

The RBMX–MTHFD2 axis bolsters NADPH production to maintain redox homeostasis

GSEA of RNA-seq data from RBMX-knockdowned cells revealed significant downregulation of antioxidant activity genes and upregulation of oxidative stress response pathways, indicating compromised redox balance (Fig. 5 A). Given that MTHFD2 generates NADPH, a key cofactor for redox maintenance, we hypothesized that RBMX regulates redox homeostasis via MTHFD2.

Consistent with this, RBMX or MTHFD2 knockdown reduced NADPH/NADP⁺ and GSH/GSSG ratios and increased ROS levels, while RBMX or MTHFD2 overexpression produced the opposite effects (Fig. 5B–C, Fig. S4A–B). Collectively, these findings establish the RBMX–MTHFD2 axis as a critical regulator of redox homeostasis in CRC cells.

Redox-mediated suppression of p38/JNK signaling promotes colorectal liver metastasis

KEGG pathway analysis of RBMX-knockdowned cells revealed significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 5 F). Given that ROS activate p38 and JNK stress kinases, we hypothesized that the RBMX–MTHFD2 axis promotes survival by suppressing this pathway. Immunoblotting confirmed that silencing RBMX or MTHFD2 induced hyperphosphorylation of p38 and JNK, but not ERK, indicating specific activation of stress signaling (Fig. 5G). Treatment with the ROS scavenger NAC abolished p38/JNK phosphorylation induced by RBMX knockdown (Fig. 5H), confirming ROS dependency. Together, these data demonstrate that RBMX stabilizes MTHFD2 mRNA to buffer oxidative stress, thereby suppressing pro-apoptotic p38/JNK signaling and facilitating CRLM.

The RBMX–MTHFD2 axis correlates with aggressive features and poor prognosis in colorectal cancer

To assess clinical relevance, we analyzed RBMX and MTHFD2 expression in the TCGA cohort. Consistent with their coordinated role, RBMX expression positively correlated with MTHFD2 expression. In contrast, MTHFD2 expression inversely correlated with JUN, a downstream target of p38/JNK signaling (Fig. 6 A). Patients with high co-expression of RBMX and MTHFD2 had significantly poorer overall survival (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Liver metastasis remains the predominant driver of mortality in CRC [1], underscoring the urgent need to decipher the molecular machinery governing metastatic colonization. Most current studies on tumor metastasis focus on aberrant activation of transcription factors and kinase signaling pathways [19]. Apart from transcriptional dysregulation, post-transcriptional regulation is increasingly recognized as a critical layer controlling tumor phenotypes. Herein, we explored this issue from the perspective of RBPs, and elucidated a novel pro-metastatic mechanism whereby RBMX stabilizes MTHFD2 mRNA to remodel redox homeostasis and suppress the p38/JNK stress signaling cascade.

The dysregulation of RBPs is increasingly implicated in various malignancies. As essential post-transcriptional regulators, they control alternative splicing, mRNA stability, polyadenylation, and translation to shape cellular phenotypes [20]. In CRC, well-characterized onco-RBPs in primary tumor progression include HuR, which stabilizes VEGF and COX-2 mRNAs to promote a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment [21, 22]; the IGF2BP family, which enhances MYC and KRAS expression via m6A-dependent mechanisms to drive proliferation [23, 24]. Beyond proliferation, RBPs are also central to metabolic reprogramming. For example, PTBP1 promotes the “Warburg effect” by regulating the alternative splicing of PKM2, granting cancer cells a metabolic advantage under nutrient stress [25]. Furthermore, stemness-associated RBPs such as Musashi-1 (MSI1) and LIN28 have been shown to sustain the self-renewal capacity of CRC cells, facilitating therapy resistance and metastatic dissemination [26]. Despite these insights, the RBP landscape specific to liver metastasis remains poorly defined. Here, by leveraging a combinatorial analysis of multiple GEO datasets and the TCGA cohort, we prioritized RBPs with significant prognostic value that potentially drive metastatic progression. Among these, RBMX emerged as a standout candidate, serving as both a robust prognostic biomarker and a putative therapeutic target for CRLM.

RBMX is an evolutionarily conserved nuclear protein traditionally characterized as a guardian of genomic stability and a splicing regulator [27, 28]. Its role in tumorigenesis is highly context-dependent: it acts as an oncogene in hepatocellular carcinoma by stabilizing BLACAT1, whereas it functions as a tumor suppressor in bladder cancer through metabolic splicing [9]. The tissue-specific functional diversity of RBMX is largely attributed to the intrinsic transcriptomic profiles of tumor cells and extrinsic tumor microenvironment. As an RBP, RBMX exerts its biological functions in a substrate-dependent manner. Distinct tissue origins and genetic backgrounds across various malignancies lead to divergent transcriptomes, whereby RBMX binds and regulates different downstream RNA targets in different tumor types. Our integrated analyses identified RBMX as a clinically relevant oncogenic factor in CRC and CRLM. RBMX expression was associated with advanced disease and poor survival, and its depletion impaired CRC cell migration, invasion, and liver metastasis. Mechanistically, RBMX stabilized MTHFD2 mRNA by binding the 1561–1620 nt region of its 3’ UTR via the N-terminal RRM domain. Notably, this region lacks the canonical C/A- or G-rich linear motifs that RBMX preferentially binds [29]. Consistent with other hnRNP family members, including HNRNP F/H, RBMX preferentially recognizes RNA secondary and tertiary structures rather than linear nucleotide sequences [8]. Multiple hnRNP proteins with RRM domains also assemble dynamic ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes with partner proteins and non-coding RNAs to execute post-transcriptional regulatory functions [30]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that RBMX recognizes the higher-order structures of MTHFD2 mRNA, or forms functional RNP complexes for targeted binding. To explain how RBMX sustains MTHFD2 mRNA stability, we put forward two non-mutually exclusive models. For the steric shielding model, bound RBMX physically covers AU-rich elements (AREs) on MTHFD2 3’ UTR. AREs are classic mRNA destabilizing sequences, and multiple hnRNP family members including hnRNP A1, hnRNP F and hnRNP H have been confirmed to block ARE access via steric hindrance, thereby preventing the recruitment of degradation machinery such as TTP, KSRP and the exosome complex [31]. Alternatively, the recruitment model proposes that RBMX recruits stabilizing proteins such as HuR to assemble protective complexes. HuR is a well-documented RBP that binds 3’ UTR regions to enhance mRNA stability in multiple tumors [21]. In addition, RBMX may competitively bind with miR-34a and miR-449 families, which have been reported to target metabolic genes and induce mRNA degradation, thereby relieving miRNA-mediated repression [32]. In addition to regulating mRNA stability, RBMX may potentially influence translational efficiency. RBPs frequently coordinate both transcript turnover and translation by modulating RNP assembly or interacting with translation-initiation machinery. Therefore, it remains possible that RBMX contributes to MTHFD2 protein expression through mechanisms beyond mRNA stabilization, although this possibility was not examined in the current study. Future studies using polysome profiling, ribosome footprinting, RNA EMSA, site-directed mutagenesis and RIP-coupled mass spectrometry will be necessary to dissect these mechanisms and identify RBMX-associated cofactors. These investigations may further clarify how RBMX rewires post-transcriptional metabolic regulation in mCRC.

Redox homeostasis, defined as the dynamic equilibrium between the generation of ROS and their elimination by antioxidant defense systems, serves as a fundamental determinant of cellular fate [33]. Physiological ROS levels act as signaling messengers, while excessive ROS trigger oxidative damage and apoptosis [34]. In CRC, metastatic cells must overcome an “oxidative bottleneck”: they survive anoikis-induced ROS in circulation and the hostile oxidative liver microenvironment [35, 36]. To navigate this, metastatic clones adopt metabolic adaptations that enhance antioxidant capacity [37]. For instance, the hyperactivation of the NRF2 pathway has been widely reported to upregulate cytoprotective genes such as HMOX1 and NQO1, thereby conferring resistance to oxidative death [38]. Concurrently, metabolic fluxes are often re-routed to bolster NADPH production, the primary currency for antioxidant defense [37]. This includes the upregulation of the Pentose Phosphate Pathway (PPP) via G6PD overexpression and the enhancement of glutamine metabolism to sustain the glutathione (GSH) pool [39]. Furthermore, recent profiling has highlighted the role of mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in maintaining redox balance under nutrient-deprived metastatic conditions [40, 41]. MTHFD2 functions as a metabolic hub in the mitochondrial one-carbon pathway, generating the NADPH required to neutralize ROS and prevent oxidative cytotoxicity [42]. Our study places the RBMX-MTHFD2 axis at the nexus of this adaptation. We demonstrate that RBMX stabilizes MTHFD2 mRNA, thereby fortifying antioxidant defenses and suppressing ROS-dependent activation of the p38/JNK stress signaling pathway [43]. While these findings clarify the functional hierarchy, the precise molecular “sensor” bridging the redox imbalance to kinase cascade activation remains to be fully elucidated. Future work will aim to map the granular details of this redox-signaling interface to reveal how the RBMX-MTHFD2 axis fine-tunes the metastatic signaling network.

The RBMX–MTHFD2 axis possesses potential for clinical translation in terms of prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Data from the TCGA database and our single-center clinical cohort revealed a positive correlation between RBMX and MTHFD2 expression. Patients with concurrent high expression of RBMX and MTHFD2 exhibited significantly shorter OS, and their expression levels were tightly associated with aggressive clinicopathological features. Compared with conventional serum biomarkers CEA and CA199, tissue-based RBMX and MTHFD2 show better performance for predicting early and occult liver metastasis, and can guide preoperative risk stratification and postoperative follow-up. In terms of targeted therapy, RBMX is considered an undruggable target due to the absence of catalytic domains [44] and its tissue-dependent functions; liver-targeted delivery systems such as lipid nanoparticles(LNPs) or ligand-conjugated delivery systems can be utilized to encapsulate siRNA or CRISPR-Cas9 systems are proposed to achieve local gene silencing [45]. By contrast, tumor-specific MTHFD2 is a preferable therapeutic candidate. Inhibition of MTHFD2 reduces NADPH synthesis, elevates ROS and reactivates p38/JNK-mediated apoptosis. Combining MTHFD2 inhibitors with chemotherapy or targeted agents may reverse drug resistance and improve therapeutic efficacy for advanced CRLM.

Although the present study has yielded meaningful findings, several aspects require further exploration. First, we utilized CRC cell lines with distinct biological characteristics, particularly with respect to their invasive and metastatic potential, for functional validation, yet additional cell models, patient-derived primary cells and tumor organoids are still needed to further verify the generalizability of our conclusions across diverse CRC/CRLM subtypes. Second, all clinical specimens were recruited from a single medical center with a limited sample size; multicenter cohorts are required to consolidate the clinical value of RBMX and MTHFD2 as prognostic biomarkers. Furthermore, the translational potential of the RBMX–MTHFD2 axis for clinical diagnosis and targeted therapy remains to be fully investigated in future preclinical and clinical studies.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identifies RBMX as a critical regulator that stabilizes MTHFD2 mRNA via direct 3’ UTR interaction. This post-transcriptional mechanism enhances cellular antioxidant capacity, thereby suppressing p38/JNK-mediated apoptosis to facilitate metastatic outgrowth in the liver (Fig. 6E). These findings establish a link between RNA metabolism and redox adaptation, highlighting the RBMX–MTHFD2 axis as a key mechanistic node in CRLM.
