Comparison of the Hemodynamic Effects of Epinephrine on Blood Pressure Augmentation at 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐Minute Dosing Intervals
Authors: Gyo Jin Ahn, Kyoung‐Chul Cha, Junghun Lee, Yae Jun Son, Young‐Il Roh, Woo Jin Jung, Yujin Lee, Hyeon Young Im, Sung Oh Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough current cardiopulmonary resuscitation guidelines recommend administering epinephrine at 3‐ to 5‐minute intervals during advanced life support (ALS), scientific evidence for the optimal dosing interval for enhancing hemodynamic parameters remains limited. Therefore, we compared the hemodynamic effects of 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐minute epinephrine dosing intervals on blood pressure augmentation in a porcine ventricular fibrillation cardiac arrest model.MethodsForty‐two pigs were randomly assigned to 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐minute epinephrine dosing‐interval groups. After ventricular fibrillation induction and a 2‐minute downtime, basic life support was initiated with a 30:2 compression‐to‐ventilation ratio for 8 minutes, followed by 30 minutes of ALS, including asynchronous ventilation at a rate of a single ventilation every 6 seconds, with oxygen delivered at 15 L/min. Epinephrine (0.02 mg/kg) was administered at predetermined intervals of 1, 3, or 5 minutes. We compared the pressure–time integrals for mean blood pressure, coronary perfusion pressure, and diastolic blood pressure among the groups over the 30‐minute ALS period.ResultsThe mean blood pressure (P<0.001), coronary perfusion pressure (P=0.001), and diastolic blood pressure pressure–time integrals (P=0.005) were significantly higher in the 1‐minute group than in the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups. Crucially, mean blood pressure and coronary perfusion pressure pressure–time integrals remained positive in the 1‐minute group but became negative in the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups during ALS. The diastolic blood pressure pressure–time integral also remained positive for a longer duration in the 1‐minute group.ConclusionsA 1‐minute epinephrine dosing interval may be significantly more effective in augmenting blood pressure and critical hemodynamic parameters during ALS than are the currently recommended 3‐ or 5‐minute intervals.Keywords: advanced life support, blood pressure, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, epinephrine, hemodynamic effectSubject Categories: Cardiopulmonary Arrest, Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care, Basic Science Research

Epinephrine plays a key role in increasing coronary perfusion pressure (CPP) and improving resuscitation outcomes in patients with cardiac arrest. 1 It primarily acts on α‐adrenergic receptors, increasing systemic vascular resistance and thereby augmenting CPP, which contributes to improved resuscitation outcomes. 2 The current American Heart Association guidelines for advanced life support (ALS) in adults recommend administering 1 mg of epinephrine every 3 to 5 minutes. 3

Although shorter dosing intervals may promote better resuscitation outcomes in patients with cardiac arrest with nonshockable rhythms, the currently recommended epinephrine dosing intervals are based on expert opinion rather than on robust scientific evidence. 4 , 5 , 6 In addition, for shockable rhythms, high‐quality evidence for guiding the appropriate timing of epinephrine administration is markedly lacking. A previous study had suggested that the recommended 3‐ to 5‐minute interval for epinephrine administration might not be optimal. 7 Therefore, investigations aiming to optimize epinephrine dosing strategies to maximize hemodynamic benefit while minimizing potential risks, including increased myocardial oxygen demand, arrhythmogenicity, and postresuscitation myocardial dysfunction, are ongoing. 8 , 9

In this study, we investigated the effect of epinephrine on blood pressure augmentation and critical perfusion parameters across different dosing intervals by analyzing the pressure–time integral (PTI) after epinephrine administration in a porcine cardiac arrest model.

Methods

Data Availability Statement

All relevant data are provided within the article and its supporting files.

Study Design

This was a randomized controlled animal study with parallel groups that compared hemodynamic parameters among 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐minute epinephrine dosing intervals in a porcine model of cardiac arrest. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yonsei University Wonju College of Medicine, Wonju, Republic of Korea (YWC‐ 230316‐1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guidelines. 10

Animal Preparation

Forty‐two Yorkshire pigs (weight, 32–43 kg), including both females and noncastrated males, were used in this study. The pigs had full access to water and food until the day before the experiment and were then fasted from midnight. They were initially sedated with intramuscular ketamine (15 mg/kg) and xylazine (2 mg/kg), followed by inhaled 1% to 3% isoflurane. After sedation, they were endotracheally intubated with a cuffed endotracheal tube and were ventilated with oxygen and nitrous oxide via a volume‐controlled ventilator (Drager Fabius GS; Drager Medical, Telford, PA). A tidal volume of 10 mL/kg and a ventilation rate of 18 breaths per minute were initially set and were then adjusted to maintain arterial oxygen saturation between 94% and 98% and end‐tidal CO2 between 35 and 45 mm Hg. ECGs with lead II and end‐tidal CO2 were continuously monitored.

Under aseptic conditions, the right femoral artery was cannulated using a 5.5F introducer sheath via the Seldinger technique, and aortic blood pressure was continuously recorded using a 5F micromanometer‐tipped catheter introduced into the femoral artery. An introducer sheath was placed in the right external jugular vein, and right atrial pressure was recorded via a 5F micromanometer‐tipped catheter. The right internal carotid artery was exposed, and a vascular flowmeter (Transonic Systems, Ithaca, NY) was used to monitor carotid blood flow. An introducer sheath, placed via the left external jugular vein, was used as the insertion route for a 5F pacing catheter for inducing ventricular fibrillation (VF) and for administering saline and epinephrine. Left femoral artery cannulation was also performed for arterial blood sampling. Once the catheters were in place, a 100‐unit/kg intravenous heparin bolus was administered to prevent thrombosis.

Selection of a New Experimental Dosing Interval of Epinephrine

A previous study found that the time to reach maximum hemodynamic parameters after epinephrine administration was between 78 and 97 seconds, regardless of the number of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) cycles. 7 These findings suggested that maintaining a 3‐ or 5‐minute epinephrine dosing interval may reduce the optimal efficacy of epinephrine. Therefore, in this study, a 1‐minute dosing interval was selected as the new experimental regimen to optimize the hemodynamic effects of epinephrine and to facilitate memorability for health care providers.

Study Protocol

According to indication contained in a sealed opaque envelope opened by an investigator before cardiac arrest induction, the pigs were randomized to 3 groups. Randomization envelopes, each specifying a different epinephrine dosing interval (1, 3, or 5 minutes), were shuffled in a box, and an envelope was drawn from the top to determine group allocation.

After baseline data were collected, a pacing catheter was positioned in the right ventricle. VF was induced by delivering an alternating electrical current at 60 Hz to the endocardium and was confirmed by the ECG waveform and an aortic pressure decrease. Once VF was induced, the endotracheal tube was disconnected from the ventilator, and the pigs were observed for 2 minutes without any procedure or treatment. After 2 minutes of untreated VF, basic life support was provided for 8 minutes to mimic a situation in which a bystander recognizes cardiac arrest and calls for help. Mechanical chest compressions (LUCAS2; Stryker Medical, Kalamazoo, MI) were delivered at a 5‐cm depth throughout the experimental period. Following 30 chest compressions at a rate of 100 compressions per minute, 2 consecutive ventilations were performed. Positive‐pressure ventilation at approximately 300 mL of tidal volume was delivered using a resuscitator bag (Silicone Resuscitator 87 005 133; Laerdal Medical, Stavanger, Norway).

Following basic life support, 30 minutes of ALS was provided. Chest compressions were switched to continuous mode, and single ventilation with 15 L/min of oxygen was delivered every 6 seconds, mimicking ALS. Epinephrine (0.02 mg/kg), diluted in 20 mL of saline, was administered rapidly as a single bolus at predetermined intervals until 30 minutes after ALS began, at which point the experiment was terminated. 11 This predilution and 20 mL volume was used to ensure the complete and rapid delivery of the full dose into the central circulation, serving the function of a postbolus flush.

Measurements

Data were digitized using a digital recording system (PowerLab; AD Instruments, Colorado Springs, CO). Aortic and right atrial pressures and end‐tidal CO2 were continuously recorded and analyzed at baseline, at 2 minutes, and then every 1 minute until 42 minutes had elapsed. CPP during CPR was calculated as the difference between the aortic and right atrial pressures using an electrical subtraction unit. Arterial blood gas analyses, including pH, partial pressure of CO2, partial pressure of O2, bicarbonate, O2 saturation, and lactate, were performed using a blood gas analyzer (i‐STAT1; Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL) at baseline and at 10, 20, 30, and 40 minutes.

Preprocessing

An adaptive threshold‐based peak‐detection algorithm was applied to extract systolic and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from the aortic pressure waveform. 1 Originally developed based on the characteristics of photoplethysmographic waveforms, this algorithm was adapted to aortic pressure signals to detect the maximum (systolic blood pressure) and minimum (DBP) values reliably. Each aortic pressure cycle, defined according to the predetermined epinephrine dosing interval, was preprocessed by appending 20‐second segments before and after the cycle to mitigate the influence of adjacent cycles. Peaks were then detected in the modified cycles by using the algorithm. Outliers and abrupt fluctuations were removed using a median filter, and linear interpolation was used to correct irregular sampling intervals. A 30‐second moving average was applied to smooth the signal, and the appended segments were excluded from the final analysis (Figure 1A). This preprocessing reduced short‐term noise and enabled identification of long‐term variations in systolic blood pressure and DBP (Figure 1B). Mean blood pressure (MBP) was calculated as the time‐averaged invasive aortic pressure over each cardiac cycle, defined as MBP = 1T∫0TPtdt, where 𝑃(𝑡) denotes invasive aortic pressure, and 𝑇 denotes the cardiac cycle duration.

Pressure–Time Integral

During CPR, hemodynamic assessments are typically based on discrete blood pressure values measured at specific time points. Given the complex and time‐dependent hemodynamic response to epinephrine, 12 , 13 relying on single‐point measurements may be insufficient to reflect overall perfusion status or pharmacodynamic response accurately. 14 Considering the importance of both blood pressure augmentation and its duration in enhancing perfusion during ALS, we defined the PTI as a metric for comparing the hemodynamic effect of epinephrine across different dosing intervals. The PTI represents the area under the pressure curve over time, thus incorporating both the amplitude and duration of blood pressure elevation. 15

To capture complementary aspects of perfusion, we prespecified the PTI for 3 key pressure variables (ie, MBP, CPP, and DBP) as our main hemodynamic outcome set. The MBP represents the driving pressure for systemic blood flow at a given vascular resistance and serves as a physiologically relevant surrogate for global organ perfusion. 16 The CPP is directly related to myocardial perfusion and the likelihood of achieving return of spontaneous circulation. 17 The DBP primarily reflects α‐adrenergic vasoconstriction and is a sensitive marker of the vascular response to epinephrine during ALS. 2 Therefore, evaluating PTIs for DBP, MBP, and CPP in parallel provides an integrated view of how different dosing intervals influence vascular tone, systemic perfusion pressure, and coronary driving pressure over time. For each of these variables, the value at the time of the first epinephrine administration was set as the baseline, and all subsequent values were expressed relative to this reference level (Figure 2).

Because 15 and 30 minutes are common multiples of the 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐minute dosing intervals, PTIs derived from the MBP, CPP, and DBP were compared among the 3 groups during 3 periods: from the start of epinephrine administration to 15 minutes, from 15 to 30 minutes, and over the total cumulative period.

Sample Size

The sample size was determined with reference to a preliminary study that used 5 pigs per group, because the PTI after epinephrine administration under current CPR guidelines had not previously been evaluated. In the preliminary study, PTI values were (15.70 ± 15.75)×106 mm Hg/s in the 1‐minute group, (−0.04 ± 9.31)×106 mm Hg/s in the 3‐minute group, and (−9.98 ± 12.37)×106 mm Hg/s in the 5‐minute group. A sample size of 14 pigs per group was calculated as needed for a 2‐sided α value of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means with 95% CIs. Nominal variables are reported as counts and percentages and were compared using the Fisher exact test. Between group comparisons of PTIs and other summary hemodynamic measures were performed using permutation‐based 1‐way tests, with permutation‐based pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni correction. Longitudinal hemodynamic data and arterial blood gas profiles were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models with a subject‐specific random intercept. The robustness of inference was evaluated using permutation‐based tests as sensitivity analyses and by visual inspection of spaghetti plots showing individual animal trajectories. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; coin and lme4 packages), and Python 3.11. Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐tailed P<0.05.

Hemodynamic Parameters During ALS

Linear mixed‐effects models showed no significant group‐by‐time interaction for systolic blood pressure, DBP, CPP, and end‐tidal CO2 across the 3 groups (Figure S1–S6).

Comparisons of PTI Among Groups by Cumulative Time

The MBP‐derived PTI was highest in the 1‐minute group across all time intervals, and over the total 30‐minute ALS period, this value in the 1‐minute group was significantly different from that in the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups (P<0.001), whereas this value did not differ significantly between the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 3A). CPP‐derived PTI followed the same pattern; the 1‐minute group demonstrated a greater PTI than did the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups, in all time windows, with the difference reaching significance over the total 30‐minute ALS period (P=0.001), whereas the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups did not differ significantly (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 3B). The DBP‐derived PTI followed a similar trend; the 1‐minute group demonstrated a greater PTI than did the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups in all time windows, with the overall difference reaching significance for both the 0‐ to 15‐minute interval (P=0.002; Table 2; Figure 3C) and the total 30‐minute ALS period (P=0.005; Table 2; Figure 3C). Notably, the groups also exhibited overall significant differences during the 15‐ to 30‐minute interval (P=0.033; Table 2), although no pairwise comparison remained significant after Bonferroni correction (Table 3).

Time‐Resolved Analysis of PTI Trajectories

For MBP and CPP, the cumulative PTI in the 1‐minute group remained above baseline throughout the 30‐minute ALS period, whereas both the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups crossed below baseline and accumulated net negative PTIs over time (Figure S2; Tables S1–S6–S4). In contrast, for DBP, the cumulative diastolic PTI in the 1‐minute group shifted from a positive to a negative value at about 26 minutes, which was later during the ALS period than the transition to net negative values in the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups (Figure S2; Tables S5 and S6).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that administration of epinephrine at 1‐minute intervals during ALS was associated with significantly higher PTI values for MBP, CPP, and DBP than those obtained with the currently recommended 3‐ and 5‐minute administration intervals. Over the total 30‐minute ALS period, the 1‐minute group consistently showed the greatest cumulative PTI for all 3 pressure variables. Notably, MBP‐ and CPP‐derived PTIs remained positive only in the 1‐minute group. DBP‐derived PTI also stayed positive for a longer duration in the 1‐minute group, crossing below baseline at about 26 minutes of ALS, whereas this transition occurred earlier during ALS in the 3‐ and 5‐minute groups. Collectively, these results suggest that the 1‐minute dosing interval is superior to the 3‐ and 5‐minute intervals in terms of both the magnitude and the maintenance of blood pressure augmentation during ALS.

Although current guidelines recommend terminating resuscitation when return of spontaneous circulation is not achieved after >30 minutes of CPR, this study extended the ALS duration to 30 minutes to evaluate the duration of the hemodynamic effects associated with a 1‐minute epinephrine dosing interval. We showed that both MBP and CPP PTI values remained positive throughout the extended period, demonstrating that the 1‐minute dosing interval was more effective than the 3‐ and 5‐minute dosing intervals in terms of augmenting blood pressure.

As a cumulative metric, the time at which the PTI becomes negative does not reflect the first instance when the pressure value drops below baseline. Instead, it denotes the point at which the total accumulated negative integral (time spent below baseline) exceeds the total accumulated positive integral (the initial pressure augmentation). This indicates that the actual pharmacodynamic pressure effects likely declined below the reference baseline well before this cumulative 0‐crossing point.

Plasma catecholamine levels are known to rise markedly during cardiac arrest as compared with normal circulatory conditions, 18 , 19 but the endogenous surge alone is insufficient to maintain adequate blood pressure. 20 In addition, given the consistent evidence that exogenous epinephrine administration improves the CPP, epinephrine use is essential for effective resuscitation. 21 , 22 Maintaining adequate perfusion during ALS is associated with improved resuscitation outcomes, and epinephrine plays a key role in augmenting the CPP by increasing systemic vascular resistance. 17 , 23 However, the optimal dosing interval of epinephrine, the sole vasopressor currently recommended in guidelines, has not been scientifically established. 4 , 24

A recent study demonstrated that the hemodynamic response to epinephrine attenuates with repeated administration, likely due to receptor desensitization and progressive myocardial dysfunction. 7 Previous animal studies have demonstrated that more frequent dosing improves hemodynamic stability and CPP, likely due to better alignment with epinephrine's short pharmacodynamic window under low‐flow conditions. 25 , 26 Recent clinical evidence has further reinforced this concept by demonstrating improved outcomes with shorter epinephrine dosing intervals. 5 , 6 These findings highlight the potential benefit of using a more frequent dosing strategy for mitigating the decline in drug responsiveness and supporting more consistent perfusion throughout the resuscitation period. Therefore, we propose that the 1‐minute dosing interval, which demonstrated superior effects on blood pressure augmentation in this study, may be an alternative to improve resuscitation outcomes in patients with cardiac arrest.

In this study, the prolonged maintenance of positive PTI values observed with the 1‐minute dosing interval, as compared with the 3‐ or 5‐minute intervals, may indicate that the duration of blood pressure augmentation following epinephrine administration is shorter than the currently recommended 3‐ to 5‐minute dosing interval. Because the current recommendation for the epinephrine dosing interval is based on pharmacokinetic data from non‐cardiac arrest animal models and pragmatic clinical studies, the applicability of this interval to patients in cardiac arrest may be limited. 4 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 These historical data suggested a several‐minute circulatory half‐life for epinephrine. However, such recommendations have not been substantiated by direct comparative studies of varying dosing intervals. More recent evidence, including pharmacokinetic studies, indicates that the vasopressor effect of epinephrine may peak within 60 to 90 seconds and diminish rapidly thereafter, raising concerns about the adequacy of longer dosing intervals for maintaining consistent perfusion pressures during CPR. 26 , 27 , 30 , 31

From a practical standpoint, epinephrine administration at a 1‐minute interval may be feasible in modern resuscitation environments, particularly in systems with structured team‐based CPR. 32 , 33 Importantly, this approach does not require substantial deviations from current protocols and could be readily integrated into existing ALS algorithms.

Our study had some limitations. First, this study focused on a surrogate hemodynamic parameter (PTI) rather than on critical outcomes, such as return of spontaneous circulation, survival, or neurological recovery. Although the 1‐minute dosing interval provided superior blood pressure augmentation, this finding must be interpreted with caution. This frequent dosing strategy results in a higher cumulative epinephrine dose, which could lead to significant adverse outcomes that were not measured in our model. The trade‐off between the desired α‐adrenergic hemodynamic benefits and these potential adverse effects remains a critical, unsettled question in resuscitation science. Second, because this investigation was conducted using a preclinical swine model, the findings may not be directly generalizable to human patients, particularly those experiencing in‐hospital cardiac arrest in clinical settings, such as the intensive care unit, emergency department, or catheterization laboratory, where advanced interventions are often initiated at an earlier time point. Third, this study used a porcine cardiac arrest model in previously healthy animals. This model may not fully represent the clinical scenario where lethal arrhythmias occur more commonly in patients with preexisting cardiac disease. The underlying pathophysiology and the myocardial response to epinephrine in a diseased heart may differ significantly from those in a healthy heart. Findings from healthy animal models may overestimate the efficacy and safety of frequent epinephrine dosing intervals. Fourth, our study was conducted using a porcine model of VF. Although this model, involving using a shockable rhythm, provides stringent experimental control and is clinically relevant, we acknowledge that the pathophysiology is distinct from that of nonshockable rhythms. Accordingly, the findings in regard to the optimal epinephrine administration interval in this study may have limited generalizability to patients with nonshockable rhythms. Fifth, this study did not include a placebo‐controlled comparison, which limits the ability to attribute the observed hemodynamic effects definitively and solely to epinephrine administration. Nevertheless, potential confounding was minimized by standardizing compression quality and withholding other pharmacologic agents during resuscitation. Sixth, although high‐quality chest compressions were ensured using a mechanical CPR device, the pharmacodynamic response to epinephrine may differ under real‐world conditions where CPR quality is inherently variable. Seventh, the experiments were conducted under general anesthesia, which is necessary for animal welfare, but does not reflect the typical unanesthetized state of a patient with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest. These anesthetic agents have known cardiovascular effects that may have influenced the baseline hemodynamic parameters and the subsequent response to epinephrine, thereby limiting the direct extrapolation of our findings to clinical scenarios. Last, the route of epinephrine administration we used (external jugular vein) differed significantly from the route most commonly used in clinical practice, particularly in patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (peripheral intravenous access). This discrepancy introduces major translational limitations. The central route in our model bypasses the pharmacokinetic delays that are associated with peripheral drug delivery during the low‐flow CPR state, and may thus alter the observed hemodynamic effects. Furthermore, our model could not assess a critical safety concern related to the peripheral route, the potential for significant local tissue adverse effects (eg, severe vasoconstriction, ischemia, and necrosis) that might result from the frequent 1‐minute peripheral dosing. This potential for local tissue damage is a crucial safety consideration that must be carefully evaluated in future translational or clinical studies.

Conclusions

This study challenges the conventional fixed 3‐ to 5‐minute interval paradigm for epinephrine administration during cardiac arrest. Our data support the concept that a physiology‐guided approach (ie, using more frequent vasopressor dosing such as at a 1‐minute interval), may enhance perfusion and improve the hemodynamic milieu during CPR. Future investigations, including randomized controlled trials and translational large animal studies, are warranted to determine whether the hemodynamic advantages observed with a 1‐minute interval can translate into improved clinical outcomes, such as survival to discharge and favorable neurological recovery.
