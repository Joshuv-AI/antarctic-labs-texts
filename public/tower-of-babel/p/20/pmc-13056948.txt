Effect of curcumin on the gut microbiota of patients with ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease and healthy participants
Authors: M. A. G. M. Kroon, K. Wortelboer, M. Davids, E. L. Swart, O. van Tellingen, M. Nieuwdorp, G. R. A. M. D’Haens, H. W. M. van Laarhoven, N. K. H. de Boer, E. M. Kemper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Curcumin exhibits anti-inflammatory properties, but clinical evidence is limited, in part because of its low systemic bioavailability. Nevertheless, its limited absorption may favor local activity in the gut, where it could influence inflammatory bowel disease via microbiota modulation. This study assesses the impact of curcumin on gut microbiota diversity, as well as clinical and biochemical parameters in patients with ulcerative colitis, and Crohn’s disease in remission and healthy individuals. In a single-center, open-label, single-arm study, 29 male participants aged 18–65 were included. Participants received 3 g of curcumin twice daily for 8 weeks. Blood, urine, and fecal samples were collected at baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks. Clinical and biochemical parameters, along with curcumin plasma, urine, and fecal concentrations, were assessed. Microbiome diversity was analysed using 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing. The study was registered in the Dutch Clinical Trial Register with ID NL8770. Twenty-nine participants completed the study. Curcumin was well tolerated with stable clinical scores (SSCAI ≤ 2, HBI ≤ 5). Plasma levels were near the lower limit of quantification, while fecal levels were markedly higher. No significant changes in alpha-diversity were found. A temporary shift in beta-diversity appeared at 4 weeks but reversed by week 8. Curcumin caused only transient microbiota changes and slight alterations in taxa abundance, suggesting limited potential for sustained microbiota modulation in IBD management.Clinical trial registration: The study was registered in the Dutch Clinical Trial Register with ID NL8770.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42095-w.

Introduction

Curcumin is extracted from the root of the turmeric plant, which has been recognized in ancient Indian medicine for its anti-inflammatory role in various pathological conditions, including gastrointestinal1–3. Despite the lack of convincing evidence for efficacy from clinical trials, curcumin supplements remain popular and new formulations are constantly being developed4. This lack of efficacy is mainly explained by a low systemic bioavailability due to the inherent properties of curcumin, a low absorption in the intestine and a rapid phase-II metabolism5. Previous studies have shown that after ingestion of various dosages of curcumin, a large amount remains in the gut in a unconjugated (active) form (unpublished data). This relatively high concentration of active curcumin could potentially provide a local beneficial effect within the gastrointestinal tract. So far, effects of curcumin have been investigated in clinical studies with patients diagnosed with Crohn’s disease (CD)6–9 or ulcerative colitis (UC)10–12, however none of the studies used curcumin as sole treatment and was only given as add on to existing FDA and EMEA approved medication, like mesalamine and sulfasalazine.

The interest in the gut microbiota continues to grow and clinical studies have linked alterations in the gut microbiota to UC13 and CD14. Compared to pediatric patients who did not have gut inflammation on endoscopy, a decreased abundance of members of the Clostridiales order was observed at the time of diagnosis in pediatric patients with UC13. Clostridiales have been linked to maintaining gut barrier function and inducing immune tolerance, thus might have a negative impact on gut inflammation when decreased15. Additionally, 14 genera were identified to be associated with pediatric CD14. Of these 14 genera, the largest increase in abundance was for Aggregatibacter, which is associated with a decrease in mucosal pattern-recognition receptors, while the largest decrease in abundance was found for Roseburia, which is associated with the improvement of the epithelial barrier through butyrate production14. While curcumin has been extensively investigated for a systemic effect, its impact on the gut microbiota is not well studied. Some studies indicate that curcumin may influence gut microbiota composition, as seen in an 8-week trial where turmeric and curcumin supplementation increased bacterial diversity by 7% and 69%, respectively, compared to a 15% decrease with placebo16. However, the small sample size limited the findings. In another study, diabetic nephropathy patients treated with curcumin showed increased abundance of beneficial bacteria like Bacteroidetes, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus, hinting at a potential anti-inflammatory effect, though the changes were not statistically significant17.

While there is continuing more knowledge on the impact that gut microbiota has on various conditions. The impact of curcumin on gut microbiota requires more research. In this study, we aimed to assess the impact of prolonged curcumin supplementation on gut microbiota diversity and composition using 16 S rRNA sequencing in patients diagnosed with UC or CD in remission and healthy individuals18. Additionally, we investigated whether curcumin-induced changes in gut microbiota could influence clinical and biochemical parameters associated with UC and CD18.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 29 male participants were enrolled in the study after providing consent (see Fig. 1); 10 participants in the Crohn’s disease (CD) group, 10 participants in the ulcerative colitis (UC) group, and 9 healthy volunteers. One participant in the UC group and one participant in the CD group were excluded after the first visit (week 0) due to an increase in fecal calprotectin, judged as unrelated to curcumin since fecal calprotectin turned out to be increased before curcumin was taken. Furthermore, one participant in the CD group was excluded after the week 4 visit yet before the week 8 visit due to an shingles diagnosis which needed subsequent medical treatment. All available data until the moment of withdrawal was used in the analysis. Although a causal relationship between curcumin and shingles cannot be established, a possible indirect effect by the gut microbiota modulation cannot be excluded19,20. Compliance control based on capsule counts showed that participants in the curcumin group had > 90% adherence to the supplementation regimen. Detailed information regarding the mean age, weight, height, and BMI of all included participants is provided in Table 1. Participants were enrolled between November, 2021 and November, 2022. Of these, 28 participants were of Dutch nationality, and one participant was of British nationality. All participants resided in the Amsterdam and Utrecht regions of the Netherlands. Based on the food diaries, dietary intake overall was similar among most participants and between study groups. Most of the participants consumed a varied diet containing carbohydrates, fibers, and fats with a regular intake of fruits and vegetables. In general, the CD and UC participants tended to have a more varied diet, where some participants in the healthy participant group consumed more occasional fast food products. Fluid intake was similar among the different study groups consisting mostly of water, tea, coffee and/or fruit juices. Physical activity was also consistent among the various groups which consisted mostly of walking or biking for a duration of 30 to 60 min per day.

Microbiome analysis

A total of 99 samples (48 samples collected before treatment as baseline, 26 samples after 4 weeks of curcumin intake, and 25 samples after 8 weeks of curcumin intake) resulted in the identification of 2,715 different ASVs. One CD baseline sample and one week 8 UC samples were excluded due to insufficient data. Alpha- and beta-diversity measures were calculated and visualized to observe the impact of curcumin on the gut microbiota in the various participant groups. All microbiome data from the study has been supplied as supplementary table (see supplementary Table IV).

Alpha-diversity

The alpha-diversity of the samples was calculated on two individual stool samples as baseline, one stool sample at four weeks, and one at eight weeks. This is represented through observed species, Shannon index, and Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (FPD) plots (Fig. 2). Because V3–V4 16 S rRNA gene sequencing does not resolve all taxa at species level, observed richness is reported as the number of observed ASVs (amplicon sequence variants), which approximate genus-level diversity. A linear mixed model fit by REML using Satterthwaite’s method for t-test showed no significant differences in the total number of observed species between groups, time points, or their interaction (Fig. 2 – Observed). Furthermore, participants with CU and CD in remission had a trend towards a lower Shannon index compared to healthy participants (Fig. 2 – Shannon); which was not statistically significant (p = 0.309 and p = 0.459, respectively).

Nonetheless, an overall downward trend in diversity is noticeable across all groups (p = 0.0682). Beta-diversity

Based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. 3) and PERMANOVA analysis, significant differences in microbial community composition were observed in time (R2 = 0.006, p = 0.0003) and between groups (R2 = 0.0667, p = 0.045). A specific group interaction in time was also observed (R2 = 0.01, p = 0.017). The global group independent shift was most pronounced at 4 weeks (R2 = 0.0076, p = 0.0001) but microbiome returned to baseline after 8 weeks when the shift was no longer significant (R2 = 0.0064, p = 0.057). It is important to note that the study design only explained 9% of the variation in microbial composition considering the participants’ conditions, curcumin intake, and their interaction. As Fig. 3 illustrates most of the microbiome variance is interindividual (R2 = 0.67, p = 0.0001).

The multi-level PCA plot, which corrects for interindividual variance, confirmed that curcumin affected the microbiota composition in all groups, as indicated by a clear shift to the left along PC1 at week 4 (Fig. 4). However, this temporary effect diminished after 8 weeks of curcumin intake, with a tendency to reorient back towards the origin of the plot and baseline values. The most substantial shift in microbiome composition was observed in the healthy participant group, while the shift was less prominent among participants with UC and CD. When examining group-specific effects over time using pairwise Adonis, no significant differences over time between the groups were observed, and the patient groups did not become more similar to healthy controls.

More specifically, only a few taxa showed significant associations with patient groups or exhibited differences after treatment. Three ASVs were significantly increased at week 4 compared to baseline: “ASVX_48 (Roseburia faecis) (p < 0.05)”, “(Faecalibacterium longum CM04-06) (p < 0.05)” and “ASVX_27 (Faecalibacterium CM04-06) (p < 0.05)” (Fig. 5). Aligning the sequences against the NCBI rRNA/ITS database showed that ASVX_48 had an identical match with Roseburia faecis M72/1 while ASVX_1842 had 99.75% sequence identity with Faecalibacterium longum CM04-06. Importantly, ASVX_1842 and ASVX_27 only differ by one base position and are highly correlated, suggesting they originate from the same genome or species. However, the abundance of these three ASVs seems to revert back to baseline values after 8 weeks of curcumin treatment. Furthermore, we observed that ASVX_197 is specifically enriched in patients with CU and persists over time (Fig. 6). The taxonomy of this ASV is not specific, and the sequence could represent an undescribed species. The closest reference is Neglectibacter timonensis with less than 97% identity, indicating that ASVX_197 belongs to a novel genus.

Plasma, urine and feces curcuminoids concentration

Concentrations of unconjugated (active) curcumin were below the lowest limit of quantification in plasma, whereas unconjugated (active) curcumin urine levels were near the lowest limit of quantification. Active, unconjugated levels of curcumin in the feces were a factor 6,000 times higher compared to plasma levels at week 4 and this ratio was maintained until week 8. Median plus lower and upper limit active (unconjugated) and total (unconjugated + conjugated) plasma, urine and feces concentrations of curcumin, demethoxycurcumin, bisdemethoxycurcumin, and tetrahydrocurcumin are presented in Supplemental III – Tables 1, 2, 3.

After processing the samples with β-glucuronidase to assess total (unconjugated + conjugated) curcumin, no significant differences in plasma curcumin or curcumin metabolite levels were observed between groups at any time point. We were able to assess levels of curcumin with a median; lower – upper limit plasma concentration at 4 weeks of 79 nM; range 20–118 nM and at 8 weeks of 95 nM; range 21–151 nM in the HV group (with similar results in the CU and CD groups) (Fig. 7). No total curcumin was assessed at HV week 0 as participants did not take curcumin yet or values were below the LOQ. Furthermore, a mixed-effects analysis did not show a significant difference in mean curcumin, demethoxycurcumin, bisdemethoxycurcumin or tetrahydrocurcumin plasma concentrations between groups over the different time points. Additionally, there were no significant differences between active, unconjugated curcumin and total (unconjugated + conjugated) curcumin levels in feces samples.

A mixed-effect analysis also did not show any significant differences in the concentrations of all compounds in urine and feces between the study groups over the different time points. The levels of curcumin in plasma and urine remained stable and, despite their low concentrations, appear have reached steady-state levels after 4 weeks. Furthermore, curcumin metabolism into tetrahydrocurcumin is extensive as seen from the total tetrahydrocurcumin plasma and urine concentrations (Supplemental III - Fig. 1A,B).

Discussion

An eight week intake of 3 g curcumin bidaily has modest and transient effects on the gut microbiome’s composition and diversity in participants with Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis in remission, and healthy individuals. In more detail, a significant impact on microbial community compositions (β-diversity) after 4 weeks of curcumin intake occurred, yet this effect was not sustained as overall β-diversity shifted back to baseline values after 8 weeks. This temporary shift and return to baseline in β-diversity was similar for all groups, indicating that the gut microbiota response between patients in remission and healthy individuals is similar. This could be partly explained by the possibility that patients with CD and UC in remission have less variation in β-diversity and more closely resemble healthy individuals21,22. This is further confirmed by the fact that we did not observe significant difference in evolutionary relationships between microbial species among the participant groups. However, despite no significant differences were observed after 8 weeks, three ASVs (ASVX_48, ASVX_1842 and ASVX_27) were significantly increased in all groups after four weeks of treatment compared to baseline. These taxa were related to the Faecalibacterium (ASVX_1842 and ASVX_27) and Roseburia faecis (ASVX_48) genera. Faecalibacterium, a prominent butyrate-producing genus, could have potential health benefits23,24. However, clinical trials showed mixed results and have not confirmed significant clinical effects of SCFAs on ulcerative colitis, where additional research is needed for Crohn’s disease25–30. These results should be interpreted carefully since out of 2,715 detected different ASVs, only 3 ASVs (or 0.1%) were influenced due to the possible effects of curcumin. It seems unlikely that curcumin could sufficiently influence the gut microbiome to promote the growth of more SCFA-producing species, since ASVX_1842 and ASVX_27 trended back to baseline values after 8 weeks.

The use of various medications among our participant groups could potentially explain the lower observed species diversity in participants with CD or UC. Participants with Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis were prescribed medications such as mesalazine, mercaptopurine, vedolizumab, or infliximab. While all participants maintained on stable medication regimens (i.e., no new initiation of medication within 3 months of the study start), the immunosuppressors infliximab and vedolizumab are administered at intervals of 4–8 weeks. It is plausible that the time of administration of these immunosuppressors leading to peak levels of antibodies which coincided with the curcumin treatment period, potentially influencing the gut microbiome. It is shown that several drugs used in treating CD and UC are subjected to microbial metabolism and this interaction can influence the gut microbiota31. Furthermore, studies present conflicting findings regarding the impact of different immunosuppressors on the gut microbiome. The TNF-α inhibitor infliximab, has been associated with increases in certain SCFA-producing bacteria, while reducing the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcaceae, and Streptococcaceae32. In contrast, the α4β7 integrin antagonist vedolizumab reduced the abundance of Bifidobacterium, Eggerthella, Ruminococcus, and Veillonella significantly33. Our study-population was too small to conclude about the effect of the immunomodulators combined with curcumin on the gut microbiome.

Despite administering a high dose of 6 g of curcumin per day, we still observed relatively low total (unconjugated plus conjugated) plasma and urine curcumin concentrations (factor 1,000–1,000,000 times lower compared to the µM concentrations used in vitro). As previously reported, unconjugated, active curcumin levels in plasma remained below the lowest limit of quantification and are not expected to have a systemic effect34. In contrast, our measurements in feces samples, used as a proxy for intestinal concentrations, demonstrated very high intra-luminal exposure to unconjugated curcuminoids, which may explain some of the aforementioned effects. Furthermore, this could clarify why it was found that the majority of the significant (p < 0.001) changes on the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) were observed in the first 4 weeks of curcumin treatment19. This suggests that if curcumin is associated with beneficial health effects, these effects are likely due to its high intra-intestinal exposure. Curcumin concentrations in plasma, urine, and feces showed no significant differences across all participant groups, indicating that IBD in remission does not affect curcumin pharmacokinetics. Additionally, in line with other studies, our study confirmed that a daily dosage of 6 g proved to be safe, with no adverse effects reported by any of the participants16,35.

While this study provides valuable insights into the effect of curcumin on the gut microbiota, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. The relatively small sample size and the exclusive focus on males of European descent limit its generalizability. Given that the gut microbiome is strongly influenced by factors such as age, diet, weight, medication use, disease activity, and geography, future studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are needed to validate and expand upon these findings36. Our study groups showed significant differences in age and BMI, which may have influenced our results. Additionally, the use of different medications within groups is a potential confounding factor that cannot be ignored. Although only patients with CD or UC in remission were included in the current study, a study involving patients with active CD or UC could help determine if curcumin can influence clinical biochemical parameters and clinical activity indices (such as HBI or SSCAI) and potentially contribute to remission. Additionally, it is important to note that the urine and fecal samples collected should be considered qualitative (rather than quantitative) measures of curcumin concentrations, as 24-hour samples were not obtained; instead, only single samples from each stool were analyzed. Finally, while the observed changes in a few ASVs were statistically significant, their biological significance remains modest. We emphasize that only 3 of 2,715 ASVs were affected, highlighting the transient and limited nature of curcumin’s effect on the microbiota. Moreover, interindividual variability in gut microbiota composition is substantial, and this may overshadow small intervention effects. We acknowledge that differences in age, BMI and medication use among participants could further confound observed effects, and that these factors limit interpretive strength. Although the study lacked a placebo or control group, the transient nature of microbial shifts and return to baseline by 8 weeks suggests that curcumin alone is unlikely to induce durable changes in gut microbiota composition.

In conclusion, despite high intra-luminal concentrations of unconjugated, active curcumin, our study shows that daily supplementation with 6 g of curcumin for 8 weeks did not significantly impact the alpha or beta diversity of the gut microbiome in individuals with Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, or healthy volunteers. Although an initial shift in microbial composition (β-diversity) was observed after 4 weeks, with several ASVs affected across all study groups, these changes were not sustained. The gut microbiota adapts and returns to baseline values by 8 weeks. Therefore, the potential of curcumin as a beneficial supplement for microbiota modulation remains uncertain.

Methods & materials

Study design and participants

In this single-center, single-arm, open label study, 29 participants (10 with UC in remission, 10 with CD in remission, and 9 healthy individuals) were included. Participants signed informed consent prior to screening. Participants were screened three weeks prior to the start of the intervention, involving physical exams, body measurements (weight, height, BMI and waist circumference), and blood sampling for clinical chemistry (supplemental II) at the Amsterdam UMC location AMC, Amsterdam, The Netherlands. CD and UC patients also provided fecal samples for calprotectin levels and microbiome profiling. All participants kept a food diary and recorded physical activity for three days before each visit day. Participants graded their stool using the Bristol Stool Scale (BSC) at each visit. The curcumin treatment period started at day 0 and lasted a total of 8 weeks, with 3 scheduled visits at week 0 (day 0), week 4 (day 28), and week 8 (day 56).

At each visit, morning fecal (5–10 g), urine (50 mL), and blood samples were collected before participants took an oral dose of 3 g of curcumin (five capsules 801 Curcuma Longa, 600 mg extract per capsule, 95% curcuminoids, AOV, MCO Health B.V., The Netherlands), followed by another 3 g at the end of the day and continuing with 6 g in two doses daily for 8 weeks. Supplements were supplied for free by a supplement manufacturer and contracts were signed to maintain scientific independence. During the final visit, participants submitted their food diaries and physical activity reports. All curcumin capsules bottles that were initially handed out, were returned to assess compliance. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Amsterdam UMC, location Academic Medical Centre, The Netherlands (approval 2020_155#B2020640 dated 09-Nov-2020). The study was registered in the Dutch Clinical Trial Register with ID NL8770 (09–11-2020). The study started 01-Nov-2021, with the first participant recruited on 24-Nov-2021 and the last participant recruited on 02-Dec-2022. The research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants eligible for inclusion were biologically male (to exclude sex-specific gut microbiota effects), aged 18–65, and, if taking any prescribed medication, had a stable dose in the last 3 months before the first visit day and during the study. All participants were required to provide written informed consent. General exclusion criteria included any tobacco use (as it may influence the gut microbiota37, alcohol consumption exceeding 1 unit per day, weight loss exceeding 10% in the last 3 months, plasma aspartate aminotransferase (ASAT) and alanine aminotransferase (ALAT) levels 2.5 times or more above the upper limit of the normal range, other liver abnormalities, known intolerance to curcumin or its derivatives, daily use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS), proton pump inhibitor usage (known to influence intestinal microbiota composition38, antibiotic use during or three months before the start of the study, incomplete information or unwillingness to comply with the intervention, or participation in other interventional studies within 3 months before the start of the current study. Additionally, each study group had its own set of specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, which can be found in Supplemental I. Patients with Crohn’s disease were eligible if in clinical remission (HBI score < 5 for > 3 months) confirmed by endoscopic or histologic criteria. Ulcerative colitis patients were included if in remission (SSCAI < 4) per Lennard-Jones criteria.

Stool sampling

Stool samples were collected by each participant using a universal sterile specimen container equipped with a spoon and fitted screw cap. The baseline samples included two individual fecal samples collected in the 3 days before week 0 to establish robust baseline values. Following, in the three days preceding each study visit, participants were instructed to collect stool samples at home and store them immediately in the freezer. Only stool samples collected in the morning of the study visit remained unfrozen. Participants were provided with cooling bags containing frozen elements to transport already frozen or fresh samples to the hospital on the day of their study visit. Upon arrival, stool samples were immediately transferred to a −80 °C freezer. As stool samples were almost immediately frozen after collection, no preservation buffer was added.

Gut microbiome analysis

DNA Isolation and 16 S rRNA sequencing

All stool samples were processed and analysed by the Microbiota Center Amsterdam (MiCA). Microbial DNA was extracted from 200 mg of each sample using a modified mechanical lysis protocol involving repeated bead-beating, following principles adapted from previously validated method39–41. Samples were combined with STAR buffer (Roche) in bead-beating tubes and subjected to three rounds of homogenization using a Precellys 24 tissue disruptor (Bertin Technologies) at 5.5 m/s for 60 s each, with 20-second pauses between cycles. Following mechanical disruption, lysates were heated at 95 °C for 15 min to enhance lysis and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was re-extracted by repeating the bead-beating process with fresh buffer. The resulting supernatants were combined to maximize yield, and DNA purification was carried out using the Maxwell RSC Blood DNA Kit (Promega) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated DNA was assessed with the Qubit® dsDNA Broad Range Assay, and purified DNA was stored at − 20 °C pending amplification.

For microbial profiling, the V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using barcoded primers in a one-step PCR approach42,43. Reference 341 F (sequence CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 785R (sequence GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC) were used as primers. Twenty nanograms of template DNA was added to the PCR mix: 6 µL 5× HF buffer (Thermo Scientific), 0.75 µL PCR Grade Nucleotide Mix (10 µM) (Thermo Scientific), 0.3 µL Phusion DNA Polymerase (2 U/µL), 18.95 µL nuclease free water, 1.5 µl forward Index Primer (10 µM) and 1.5 µl reversed Index Primer (10 µM). Thermal cycling was performed on a Biometra thermocycler using the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s; 25 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 90 s; followed by a final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 min43.

Amplification success was verified by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels containing ethidium bromide. Amplicons were purified using Ampure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter) on a Biomek FX robotic platform. Purified PCR products were quantified again using the Qubit® dsDNA BR assay. Samples were pooled in equimolar amounts, and the resulting library was evaluated for quality and concentration using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 with the High Sensitivity DNA chip and the Qubit® dsDNA BR Assay Kit system. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq platform using v3 chemistry with paired-end 2 × 251 bp reads43.

Microbiome data analysis

Microbiome data analysis was performed by the Microbiota Center Amsterdam (MiCA) using a well know method based on a protocol previously described41,44. Sequencing data were analyzed by MiCA using a standardized pipeline based on VSEARCH (v2.15.2)45. Forward and reverse reads were merged, allowing staggered alignment and a maximum mismatch tolerance of 100 bases. High-quality reads (expected error < 1.5) were denoised using the cluster_unoise algorithm with a minimum abundance threshold of 4 reads. Chimeric sequences were removed through de novo detection using the UCHIME3 method. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were quantified by aligning reads to ASV centroids with the usearch_global algorithm using a 97% similarity threshold46. Taxonomic classification was performed in R (v4.2.0) using DADA2’s taxonomy assignment tools and the SILVA reference database (release 132)47,48. Multiple sequence alignment and tree construction were conducted using MAFFT (v7.310) and FastTree (v2.1.11), respectively49,50. Taxonomy, ASV counts, and phylogenetic data were imported into a phyloseq object for downstream analysis45. Datasets were rarefied to 12,500 reads per sample to standardize sequencing depth, and low-depth samples were excluded.

All statistical and exploratory analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). Alpha diversity was assessed using observed richness, the Shannon diversity index (H′), and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (FPD). Shannon index accounts for both richness and evenness, while FPD represents the total branch length of the phylogenetic tree encompassed by taxa in a sample. Alpha diversity indices were calculated using phyloseq (v1.46.0) and picante (v1.8.2), and group comparisons were assessed with linear mixed-effects models using the lme4 package (v1.1–34)46,51,52. Observed richness was calculated as the total number of unique ASVs detected per sample after rarefaction. All alpha diversity metrics are inherently influenced by sampling depth and sequencing error, and therefore should be interpreted comparatively within a study rather than as absolute values across studies. While abundance filtering can reduce the contribution of spurious taxa, it may also introduce bias when groups differ in sequencing depth, community structure, or abundance distributions. Therefore, keeping spurious taxa may inflate the actual number but does not introduce a bias. For these reasons, we did not apply additional abundance or prevalence filtering prior to statistical testing for both alpha and beta-diversity. Beta diversity (community structure) was tested using PERMANOVA with 9999 permutations in the vegan package (v2.6.4), employing Bray-Curtis dissimilarity53. To account for the repeated measures and missing data, a customized permutation strategy was applied in which the grouping label was permuted over the subjects and the timepoint within a subject. For multivariate visualization of individual microbiome trajectories, multilevel principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out using mixOmics (v6.24.0) on center log ratio (clr) transformed data from participants with complete longitudinal profiles54. Group and time-specific effects were further assessed via pairwise PERMANOVA (Adonis). Taxa differing in abundance between time points were identified using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for subjects, treating time as a categorical fixed effect (pre-, 4-week, and 8-week)52. Results for individual taxa analysis were corrected for multiple testing using the BH procedure.

HPLC-MS/MS analysis of curcumin, demethoxycurcumin, bisdemethoxycurcumin, and tetrahydrocurcumin in plasma, urine, and fecal samples

To determine concentrations of curcumin, demethoxycurcumin, bisdemethoxycurcumin, and tetrahydrocurcumin in plasma, urine, and stool, a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method was applied55,56. Fecal material was initially mixed 1:1 with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to ensure homogeneity. Once processed, all biological samples were frozen at − 20 °C pending analysis. Prior to analysis, samples were thawed and split for enzymatic treatment. One portion was incubated with β-glucuronidase/sulfatase (Type H-1, Helix pomatia, Sigma-Aldrich), containing ≥ 300,000 U/g β-glucoronidase and ≥ 10,000 U/g sulfatase at a final activity of 20 U/µL in sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0), maintained at 37 °C and agitated at 850 rpm for one hour. This procedure facilitated hydrolysis of conjugated metabolites. No significant increase in conversion was observed beyond this incubation period, and average deconjugation efficiency exceeded 90%. Following enzymatic processing, analytes were extracted from all matrices (plasma, urine, feces) by liquid-liquid extraction using tert-butyl methyl ether (TBME). Internal and external standards (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MOI, USA) for all curcuminoids and piperine were included in the extraction batch.

The LC-MS/MS instrumentation comprised a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (API 4000, Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) with an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface coupled to an UltiMate 3000 chromatographic system (Thermo Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA). Chromatographic separation was carried out at ambient temperature on a ZORBAX Extend-C18 analytical column (100 × 3 mm, 3.5 μm; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), fitted with a C18 guard cartridge (Phenomenex, Utrecht, The Netherlands). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and methanol (B). The gradient program transitioned from 50% to 95% B over 3 min, held at 95% B for 6 min, then returned to baseline (20% B) over another 6 min. The flow rate was 0.2 mL/min, and total run time per sample was 18 min. Analyst® software (v 1.5.1, Sciex) was used to acquire and process data. All steps in sample handling and analysis were performed under low-light conditions to protect photosensitive compounds, using aluminum foil shielding and closed instrument compartments where possible. Calibration curves were prepared fresh for each analytical batch, covering a validated dynamic range from 2 nM to 400 nM. Samples exceeding this range were appropriately diluted with blank matrix prior to re-analysis to ensure accurate quantification within the linear range.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

A reliable sample size calculation before start of the study was not feasible due to the limited number of studies quantifying the effect of curcumin on clinical parameters mediated through the gut microbiome. Due to the exploratory nature, expected low variability and homogenous population within groups, 10 participants per group were considered sufficient to identify trends or provide directional insights. Non-microbiome data were analyzed using Graphpad Prism, v10. Nonparametric tests were employed, and data presented as median and range or mean and standard deviation. Multivariate analysis and ANOVA for repeated measures or mixed-effects were utilized, along with Spearman’s rank test or Geisser-Greenhouse’s epsilon to calculate correlations. Mann-Whitney U tests were employed to compare results between groups. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and < 0.1 as indicating a trend. No Bonferroni adjustment was performed due to the explorative nature of this pilot study, which serves as a starting point for larger investigations into the role of curcumin on clinical parameters and the microbiome in different IBD patient groups. Physical and biochemical parameters were analyzed by comparing baseline measurements with weeks 4 and 8, both at individual and group levels.
