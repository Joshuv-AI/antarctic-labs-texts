MicroRNAs as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for viral myocarditis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Markus A. Martinez Holst, Francisco A. Silva Navarro, José Israel León Pedroza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent diagnostic tools for viral myocarditis (VM) present significant limitations. Recent studies have shown that microRNAs (miRNAs) are promising circulating biomarkers. The aim of this meta-analysis is to evaluate the diagnostic and prognostic role of miRNAs in VM.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted across five databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library). Eligible studies reported miRNA diagnostic and/or prognostic accuracy in human subjects with VM diagnosis. Methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool. Pooled estimates of area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were calculated.ResultsA total of 26 studies were included in our analysis; 42 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified; 30 exhibited upregulated expression, 9 demonstrated downregulation, and 3 had variable expression patterns. The pooled diagnostic AUC for VM was 0.897 (0.863–0.931; I2 = 93.4%); bivariate analysis yielded a pooled sensitivity of 0.758 (0.582–0.876; I2 = 87.1%) and specificity of 0.881 (0.811–0.928; I2 = 78.5%). Regarding fulminant myocarditis (FM) detection, the pooled AUC was 0.883 (95% CI: 0.837–0.930; I2 = 87.3%).ConclusionsCirculating miRNAs represent promising candidate biomarkers for the diagnosis of VM, although the available evidence remains preliminary. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to reproduce and validate these results.

Introduction

Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease caused by a wide range of etiologies [1]. Frequent triggers include infections, autoimmune responses, and exposure-related conditions, with viral pathogens being the predominant contributors [2]. The clinical presentation is highly variable. Patients may present asymptomatically or develop chest pain, fever, new onset heart failure, and life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias [3], [4]. This broad range of clinical presentations, along with significant overlap with other conditions, makes the diagnosis of acute viral myocarditis (VM) particularly challenging.

Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) is the gold standard for diagnosing VM [5]. However, in practice, EMB is associated with procedural complications, including cardiac perforation, thromboembolism, valvular trauma, and sustained ventricular arrhythmias [6]. Consequently, non-invasive tools such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [7] and biomarkers, such as troponin I [8], are often employed, though both have shown notable limitations in their diagnostic performance.

Recent studies have linked the pathogenesis of VM to microRNA (miRNA) [9]. MiRNAs are small non-coding RNA molecules, approximately 21–22 nucleotides long. Their mature form plays a crucial role in gene expression, by binding the 3′ untranslated regions (3′ UTRs) of target mRNAs [10]. One of the remarkable features of miRNAs is their stability in biological fluids such as plasma, serum, and saliva [11], [12]. Because of this stability and their tissue specific expression patterns, miRNAs have emerged as promising non-invasive biomarkers for diagnostic applications.

A previous systematic review demonstrated that miRNAs could be potential biomarkers for myocardial inflammation, highlighting individual miRNAs such as miR-155, miR-206, and hsa-Chr8:96 [13]. Despite encouraging data from individual miRNAs, the clinical applicability remains uncertain due to inconsistent findings across studies. This, in addition to the growing need for novel, non-invasive, and specific biomarkers for VM, underscores the value of a comprehensive assessment of miRNAs' role in VM. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to update available data and quantitatively assess the diagnostic and prognostic potential of miRNAs in VM as a biomarker class, aiming to map the current evidence landscape and identify the most promising candidates.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [14]. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the research board of the Centro de Investigación en Ciencias de la Salud (CICSA) of Anáhuac Mexico University.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted on 25 July 2024 (updated on 27 October 2025) in PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Embase, and Cochrane Library. A combination of keywords, their variations and Boolean operators was used: (“myocarditis” OR “viral myocarditis” OR “inflammatory cardiomyopathy” OR “acute myocarditis” OR “fulminant myocarditis”) AND (“microRNA” OR “miRNA” OR “microRNAs” OR “miRNAs”) AND (“diagnosis” OR “prognosis” OR “biomarkers” OR “diagnostic marker” OR “prognostic marker” OR “ROC curve” OR “AUC”). No language restrictions were imposed. Additionally, reference lists of the included studies were screened manually to identify potentially relevant articles.

Eligibility criteria

We included original, peer-reviewed studies that evaluated the expression of miRNAs in human subjects with clinical diagnosis of viral myocarditis, with a focus on diagnostic and/or prognostic value. Only studies involving human participants and reporting quantifiable miRNA expression data were included. Animal research, non-viral myocarditis studies, editorials, reviews, and case reports were excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers performed the study selection and data extraction (MMH and FSN). Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (JLP). Duplicated article removal was aided by Rayyan, an artificial intelligence that matches identical reports, and was further manually reviewed by the authors. Initially, reports were screened by title and abstract, followed by a full-text review.

A pre-established form was employed for structured data extraction. The following data were recorded from eligible studies: study characteristics (First author and year of publication), participants' characteristics (number of participants, mean age, diagnosis), miRNA sample sources, miRNAs evaluated, miRNA expression profile (upregulated or downregulated), diagnostic and prognostic accuracy, and additional findings.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included articles was evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool by two reviewers (MMH and FSN), with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (JLP). Risk of bias and applicability concerns were assessed for each manuscript, in the following domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain was rated as low, high, or unclear risk of bias, and the results were presented in a summary table.

Data analysis

The meta-analysis was performed in R version 4.5.2. A random-effects model with 95% confidence intervals was applied to all analyses. An inverse-variance method using the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment was used to calculate the pooled overall AUC. The Hanley and McNeil method was employed to approximate standard error for missing confidence intervals. To further characterize diagnostic accuracy, a bivariate model meta-analysis using Reitsma's method was conducted with studies providing sensitivity and specificity data. When multiple miRNA estimates were contributed by the same study, these were treated as statistically independent units of analysis. Heterogeneity was determined with the Cochran's Q test and I2 statistic; values of I2 ≥ 75% or Q-test p < 0.10 indicated high heterogeneity. To evaluate robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed. Publication bias was assessed subjectively by visual inspection of the funnel plot and objectively using Egger's test.

Results

Study characteristics

In total, 1673 articles were identified in the initial database search. After removing duplicates, 1320 records were assessed for title and abstract screening. Irrelevant and non-retrievable articles were excluded, and 40 articles were analyzed for full-text review. Diagnostic use of miRNAs in acute myocarditis

In total, 42 distinct miRNAs showed differential expression in VM patients. Of these, 30 were upregulated, 9 were downregulated, and 3 showed variable expression (Table 1). The meta-analysis demonstrated a pooled overall AUC for VM diagnosis of 0.897 (95% CI: 0.863–0.931; I2 = 93.4%) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis of exosomal miRNA and circulating miRNA showed an aggregated AUC of 0.903 (95% CI: 0.852–0.954; I2 = 88%) and 0.891 (95% CI: 0.846–0.937; I2 = 93.4%), respectively (Fig. 2). Bivariate analysis yielded a sensitivity of 0.758 (95% CI: 0.582–0.876; I2 = 87.1%), specificity of 0.881 (95% CI: 0.811–0.928; I2 = 78.5%) (Fig. 3A, B), and an AUC of 0.899 (95% CI: 0.789–0.939); the SROC curve is depicted in Fig. 3C. For the FM subgroup, the pooled AUC was 0.883 (95% CI: 0.837–0.930; I2 = 87.3%) (Fig. 4). Prognostic use of miRNAs in acute myocarditis

Beyond diagnosis, five miRNAs demonstrated independent prognostic value. miR-208b was significantly associated with left ventricular functional recovery (ρ = − 0.847). Similarly, endomyocardial miR-133a levels at diagnosis correlated with both baseline left ventricle function and functional recovery at 12 months in inflammatory cardiomyopathy patients [26]. In a 1-year follow-up study, Yan et al. further supported the prognostic role of miRNA, reporting that patients with upregulated miR-146b had an odds ratio of 1.321 (1.253–1.397) for poor prognosis [35]. Furthermore, Gao et al. reported that downregulation of miR-425-3p at 1 year of follow-up was associated with adverse outcomes, including recurrent myocarditis, arrhythmia, heart failure, cardiac transplantation, and death [38]. While in pediatric FM, serum miR-21 was identified as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in a multivariate regression analysis (OR = 68.990; 95% CI: 1.498–3177.094; p = 0.030) [39].

Correlations with clinical parameters

The correlation between cardiac troponins (cTn) and miRNA expression was further evaluated, revealing a consistent positive association across all miRNAs. The pooled correlation coefficient was r = 0.68 (95% CI:0.53–0.79; I2 = 79.0%) (Supplementary Material). Subgroup analysis by troponin subtype showed an r of 0.71 (95% CI: 0.45–0.85; I2 = 62.4%) for Troponin I and 0.67 (95% CI: 0.43–0.83; I2 = 79.3%) for Troponin T. Similar associations have been reported with other cardiac biomarkers. Zhang et al. assessed the correlation between multiple miRNA expression levels and CK-MB, with miR-146a-5p showing the strongest correlation (r = 0.55) [17].

A formal meta-analysis of echocardiographic correlations was not performed due to substantial data heterogeneity. Examined parameters included global longitudinal strain (GLS), left ventricular shortening, left ventricular functional recovery (LVFR), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Findings were inconsistent across studies, and most miRNAs did not demonstrate a statistically significant correlation with echocardiographic parameters; however, notable exceptions included miR-21-5p (GLS; r = 0.71) [16], miR-208b (LVEF; ρ value = − 0.847) [18], and miR-133a (LVFR; HR = 2.281) [26].

Funnel plot and sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed no meaningful variation in the pooled AUC, demonstrating robustness in our findings. Funnel plot inspection revealed some asymmetry, which may partly reflect the heterogeneous nature of the miRNA markers included rather than publication bias alone. Egger's test yielded a statistically significant result (p < 0.05), suggesting potential publication bias that should be interpreted with caution. Detailed findings are provided in the Supplementary material.

Discussion

There is a growing body of knowledge regarding the role of miRNAs in both the pathogenesis and diagnostic evaluation of myocarditis. Our study aimed to provide a quantitative assessment of miRNA in VM. Previously, Lewandowski et al. conducted a systematic review of miRNAs in related cardiac conditions, suggesting that a differential miRNA expression could be present in myocarditis, though predominantly in non-ischemic idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy populations [13]. Building upon this foundation, the findings of our study provide an exploratory effect size analysis, useful for their potential diagnostic role. The pooled AUC to differentiate VM from other myocardial conditions was 0.897 (0.863–0.931), while the bivariate analysis demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.758 (0.582–0.876) and specificity of 0.881 (0.811–0.928), reflecting a favorable effect across the included studies. For FM detection, miRNAs showed a pooled AUC of 0.883 (0.837–0.930), further suggesting their relevance across distinct clinical presentations of VM. Collectively, these results reflect the signal of miRNAs as a biomarker class in early clinical development, laying the foundation for future standardized studies.

Current guidelines recommend the use of cardiac biomarkers as part of the diagnostic workup for VM [47], [48]. cTn have reported sensitivities of 34–71% and specificities of 86–94% for VM detection [49]. However, cTn levels may remain within normal ranges despite active VM [50], [51]. Moreover, cTn elevation reflects nonspecific myocardial injury, which inherently limits its diagnostic accuracy [52]. In contrast, miRNAs reflect disease-specific inflammatory pathways; their expression depends on intracellular signaling elicited by diverse stimuli, reflecting particular immunological responses [53]. Thus, miRNA profiling could provide not only diagnostic value but also the potential for etiologic identification. Data reported by Blanco-Domínguez et al. [34] and Zhang et al. [21] further support this reasoning, showing that selected miRNAs (hsa-Chr8:96, hsa-miR-30a, hsa-miR-192, hsa-miR-146a, hsa-miR-320a) demonstrated greater discriminatory capacity compared with traditional cardiac biomarkers, including CRP and cTn, for VM identification.

Th17-related miRNAs were consistently implicated in VM pathogenesis, notably hsa-Chr8:96 [34], miR-146b, and miR-155 [37]. These miRNAs regulated target Th17 genes during VM, including Pparg, Nos2, Stat3 [34], and ROR-γt (miR-146b and miR-155 [37]), as well as Tgfβ and Cd69 [34], suggesting a direct modulating effect in Th17 cell activity. Th17 cells have a key role in the acute phase of VM [54]; their upregulation facilitates viral replication during the acute phase [55], promotes the production of IgG anti-heart autoantibodies [56], and induces cardiac fibrosis and dilated cardiomyopathy [54]. Furthermore, in mouse models, IL-17 inhibition reduces viral replication and improves pathological outcomes [55].

In murine models, mmu-miR-721 has been identified as a highly specific marker of Th17-mediated myocarditis, while its proposed human homologue (hsa-Chr8:96) emerged as the most promising individual miRNA in our analysis, demonstrating a discriminatory capacity from acute myocardial infarction (AUC: 0.93). Additionally, hsa-Chr8:96 was validated in cohorts including myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries and Th17-related diseases [34]. Nevertheless, its status as a canonical human miRNA remains debated [57], and it has not yet been incorporated into official databases such as miRBase.

The biological source of miRNA extraction represents a relevant consideration for eventual clinical implementation. Subgroup analysis comparing exosomal miRNAs [AUC 0.903 (0.852–0.954)] and circulating cell-free miRNAs [AUC 0.891 (0.846–0.937)] revealed comparable diagnostic performance. Despite this, exosomal miRNAs are more stable in biological fluids, as their lipid membrane encapsulation protects them from ribonuclease degradation [58]. However, their technical ease, when compared with the extraction and measurement of circulating miRNA, decreases considerably. Exosomal miRNA vesicles require additional processing steps and specialized equipment, potentially limiting their use in routine clinical practice [45].

The findings in this meta-analysis carry several research implications that merit further discussion. Developing a miRNA panel that accounts for the interplay among miRNA expression patterns could enhance diagnostic power [21]. Beyond diagnosis, miRNAs' involvement in VM pathophysiology positions them as candidate therapeutic targets. To date, therapeutic targeting of miRNAs in VM has been studied exclusively in rodent models, where inhibition of miR-155 [19], [59], miR-30a-5p [60], miRNA-21 and miR-146b [61] reduced cardiac injury and disease progression. Extrapolation to human VM remains unexplored; however, in other disease contexts, miRNA targeting therapies have shown promising advances in human subjects. For instance, anti-miR-122 (miravirsen) reached phase II clinical trials for hepatitis C, demonstrating dose-dependent viral suppression and a favorable safety profile in humans [62]. This suggests that similar approaches could eventually be translated to VM.

This study has several limitations. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the main analyses, likely reflecting differences in miRNA expression patterns, biological sample sources, extraction methods, comparator groups, and patient populations. Pooling data across these heterogeneous conditions may limit the direct clinical interpretability of the summary estimates; therefore, the pooled results should be interpreted as an indicator of the overall potential of miRNAs as a biomarker class rather than a definitive measure of the combined miRNAs' diagnostic performance. Standardization of methods remains a critical prerequisite for future validation efforts. Related to this, EMB was inconsistently used as the reference standard and viral etiology was not uniformly confirmed by myocardial viral PCR or histopathology, which may introduce diagnostic verification bias. Therefore, ‘viral myocarditis’ in this review reflects a clinically diagnosed entity based on the diagnostic criteria applied in the primary studies, rather than a virologically confirmed diagnosis in all cases. This represents an inherent limitation, as EMB carries procedural risks and current guidelines restrict its indication to specific clinical scenarios. The predominantly retrospective, case-control design of the included studies may overestimate diagnostic validity and not accurately reflect real-world clinical settings, where disease prevalence and spectrum differ substantially. Of note, Egger's test yielded a statistically significant result, suggesting that publication bias represents a potential limitation. This may be explained by the fact that investigators are unlikely to report statistically insignificant individual miRNA findings. Evidence for FM was limited to individual study-level findings, and the available data remain highly preliminary. Lastly, the inclusion of studies utilizing biobank material may not accurately reflect in vivo miRNA expression profiles. Overall, despite positive pooled results, miRNAs in VM should still be regarded as biomarkers under investigation, as limited high-quality studies do not yet provide conclusive evidence. Nevertheless, this analysis of effect size could provide valuable insights for planning further clinical validation.

Conclusions

MiRNAs represent promising biomarkers for VM, with a pooled AUC of 0.897 (0.863–0.931), sensitivity of 0.758 (0.582–0.876) and specificity of 0.881 (0.811–0.928) in our exploratory analysis. Their tissue-specific expression, stability in circulation, and close association with disease pathophysiology collectively support their clinical potential. Although current literature acknowledges that miRNAs may help identify specific etiologies of VM and guide risk stratification, their standardized clinical implementation remains to be defined. Further prospective, multicenter clinical trials are warranted to validate the diagnostic and prognostic accuracy of miRNAs in VM.
