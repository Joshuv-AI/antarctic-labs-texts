Clarity in ex-vivo cardiac transport system naming conventions1
Authors: Robert L Gottlieb, Joseph G Rogers, Amarat Kongsompong, Francesco Fioretti, Melina Giugni, Yaron D Barac, Dirk Van Raemdonck
Published: 2025

Solid organ transplantation has advanced to include a panoply of organ transport techniques designed to protect organ allografts from ischemia and ischemia-reperfusion injury. Heart transplantation includes a variety of stakeholders with proprietary interests in distinguishing a particular device, transportation strategy, or cardioprotective or cardioplegic agent from its competing alternatives. This may be undertaken in either an “open source” or “copyleft” approach, for the ongoing and broad benefit of the transplant community as a whole, or may reflect a restrictive approach including trademarks and patent protection claims for intellectual property. Neither strategy is clearly “right” or “wrong” as research investments merit at least some degree of financial compensation… after all, when generic medications become accessible, the public generally benefits from lower pricing, but with the Janus-like risk of scarcity if business margins become leaner.

With respect to organ transport, there are only a finite number of variations that can be undertaken, and this offers the ability to propose a conceptional nomenclature and classification, to accompany proposed or accepted names. Such a framework would offer the ability to compare and contrast strategies, devices, or preservative solutions, while facilitating clarity in communication with respect to organ transport options. We propose that there is a need for an ISHLT working group for organ transport nomenclature to develop a shared communication framework, with input from the various salient stakeholder constituencies.

In this issue of JHLT Open,1 the authors of “Mid-term follow-up of Non-Ischemic Heart Preservation in heart transplantation” expand on the results of their initial pilot experience of cold and relatively deep hypothermic (8°C), cardioplegic, non-beating transport for cardiac donor allografts in a configuration that involves an ex-vivo oxygenator and roller-ball perfusion system delivering 20 mm Hg of perfusion pressure in the aortic root,1 (Figure 2). They have termed their strategy “non-ischemic heart preservation” or NIHP. Notably, the name “non-ischemic heart preservation” and its acronym, “NIHP” generated considerable debate amongst the reviewers, editorial team, and the authors of the investigation. The debate stemmed from a name that can be seen to imply efficacy, as well as the risk of pre-empting others from using similar names. While the authors have not addressed trademark status, it is likely a salient consideration. Interestingly, descriptive trademarks, derived from a product feature, may provide weaker protection even after acquisition of secondary meaning.2 Thus, another organ protection strategy that also claims to be “non-ischemic” may also be entitled to use such a descriptive term, creating further ambiguity and thereby further muddying the waters.

This current investigation of NIHP concatenates a small number of randomized patient (N = 16) with 7 assigned to classic cardioplegic transport using static, cold storage and 9 assigned to their hypothermic, oxygenated and ventilated cardioplegic perfusion system. When combined with the 6 non-randomized NIHP cases reported previously,3 the new cases more than double the reported cases of heart transplant using the “NIHP” and provide a greater duration of follow-up. The study is numerically underpowered for their primary outcome of event-free survival at 1 year, defined by survival free of severe primary graft dysfunction (PGD), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation use, acute cellular rejection (ACR ≥ 2R), and death. Nevertheless, although the number (n = 15) of total heart transplants assigned to this transport method from the prior and current report is modest, the additional data are exciting and encouraging because it provides pilot feasibility and safety to justify an expanded clinical study of this NIHP technique.

The authors’ implementation of NIHP adds to the field of alternative beating and non-beating preservation and transport systems. The gamut of other investigational and approved transport techniques and devices for cardiac allografts include another investigational hypothermic oxygenated transplant system (“HOPE,” XVIVO Heart Assist Transport, XVIVO),4 a commercial-stage, cold static cardioplegic transport system (SherpaPack, Paragonix),5 and the commercially-available beating heart transport system (Organ Care System from TransMedics).6

Our excitement is tempered by the ambiguous name, and we believe that an alternative, more objective name, would be much more suitable than “NIHP.” The authors have not established that their system is truly non-ischemic, even if there is a likelihood that it is a “less ischemic” system as compared to cold, static storage. Moreover, we are concerned that the name “NIHP” may encourage misinterpretation or over-imputation of the efficacy and safety of the system in ways that are not yet established. Although a device strategy, and although separate rules may apply to medications and devices, many regulatory agencies, including the United States Food and Drug Administration, have rules to control claims with respect to safety or efficacy, particularly if they are deemed inaccurate or misleading.7 Similarly, guidance from the European Medicines Agency notes “The… name of a medicinal product should not convey a promotional message. A[n]… name is considered promotional if it is overly fanciful, so as to misleadingly imply unsubstantiated unique effectiveness, composition or superiority claims, if it overstates the product efficacy, minimises the risk or broadens the product indications,”8 (Section 4.1.8). The allusion to a safety or efficacy implication in a name thus places a medical product at risk. We do not know if such claims are right or wrong, and take no position. Rather, we are concerned that such implied claims have not been established, and believe that the name should not be used to restrict access to the space by future transplant pioneers. The name “NIHP” is too generic, and could encumber future cardiac allograft transport strategies.

As a pragmatic solution, we recommend ISHLT create a working group tasked with developing a nomenclature framework that would allow organ transport devices or strategies to be individuated alternately by a conceptual or proprietary method to ensure clarity and precision of communication. At a minimum, this framework would include the name of the device or strategy, the transport temperature [deep hypothermic (0-20C), modest hypothermia (21-35C), or normothermia], whether the organ preservation is static versus perfused, whether the heart is beating or cardioplegic, non-beating. Consistent with ISHLT principles, the working group should be assembled to ensure a breadth of perspectives, with representation of the varied constituencies across generations, geography, gender, specialties, and professional roles, inclusive of both academic and non-academic voices. There may be other key nomenclature details, and for that, we look forward to the recommendations of the proposed working group.
