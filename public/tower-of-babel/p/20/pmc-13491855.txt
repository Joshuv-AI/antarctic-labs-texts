Association of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index and stroke outcomes: results from the China National Stroke Registry III
Authors: Liye Dai, Yiting Sun, Jinfeng Yin, Yong Jiang, Hao Li, Yongjun Wang, Xia Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high-sensitivity C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (hsCTI) is an integrated biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. This study aimed to evaluate the association between hsCTI levels and the risk of stroke recurrence in a large, real-world cohort.MethodsWe analyzed data from the China National Stroke Registry-III, including patients with acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). The hsCTI was calculated as 0.412 × ln(hsCRP [mg/L]) + ln(TG [mg/dL] × FPG [mg/dL]/2). Outcomes included stroke recurrence, ischemic stroke recurrence, and composite vascular events. Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models were employed to evaluate the association between hsCTI and clinical outcomes, stratified by sex, age, and glycemic status. The area under the curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to evaluate the incremental risk prediction capabilities of hsCTI and TyG index beyond traditional risk factors.ResultsAmong 4,969 included patients (67.8% male), those in the highest hsCTI quartile (Q4) experienced increased risks of recurrent stroke and composite vascular events during a maximum follow-up of 5 years. Compared with the lowest quartile, Q4 was associated with significantly elevated 3-month stroke recurrence (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.83; 95% CI, 1.23–2.73) and composite vascular events (aHR, 2.04; 95% CI, 1.38-3.00), with associations remaining significant at 1 year. The prognostic value was most pronounced in individuals aged ≥ 60 years. Subgroup analyses identified significant interactions between hsCTI and age (P for interaction = 0.009), as well as National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at admission (P for interaction = 0.017). Adding hsCTI to the reference model significantly improved clinical risk prediction (all P < 0.001for NRI and IDI).ConclusionsElevated baseline hsCTI level was associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence and composite vascular events. HsCTI may serve as a potential biomarker for identifying residual vascular risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05141-8.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, with China experiencing the heaviest burden [1–4]. Effective prediction of recurrent stroke requires more reliable biomarkers to predict high-risk populations in secondary prevention strategies. However, currently available indicators remain insufficient for accurately identifying patients at high risk of recurrence.

Insulin resistance (IR), characterized by a reduced response to insulin, is central to metabolic disorders and a major contributor to atherosclerosis [5–7]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been proposed as a reliable surrogate marker of IR and is calculated as ln (fasting triglyceride [mg/dL]×fasting glucose [mg/dL]/2) [8]. In IR, metabolic abnormalities and chronic systemic inflammation coexist, collectively driving cardiovascular disease (CVD) progression [9–11]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) is a well-recognized systemic inflammatory indicator, sensitive to low-grade inflammation. Elevated hsCRP levels are associated with ischemic stroke incidence, recurrence, and poor outcomes [12–16]. Therefore, the comprehensive indicators of metabolic-inflammation imbalance may have prognostic value for recurrence.

The C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) integrates the role of the interrelationship between inflammation and metabolic response [17, 18]. CTI has demonstrated significant prognostic value in relation to cancer-related mortality and assessing the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) [19–21]. As a comprehensive index, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index (hsCTI) may effectively integrate these metabolic and inflammatory factors. It is hoped that it can more comprehensively and precisely assess the overall biological regulatory disorders of the body, capturing information that individual indicators cannot capture. Some studies suggested that the hsCTI has been reported to be associated with cardiovascular risk, advanced liver fibrosis, sleep apnea, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [22–24]. While various indicators are utilized in clinical practice, predicting and identifying patients at high residual risk of recurrence remains a clinical challenge. Comprehensive indicators reflecting both metabolic and inflammatory burdens may offer additional prognostic value.

This study was a secondary analysis of the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR-III), a large-scale and high-quality registry designed to identify prognostic biomarkers in patients with acute ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). We aimed to evaluate the differential impact of baseline hsCTI levels on long-term stroke recurrence, with a specific stratification according to glycemic regulation states.

Methods

Data availability statement

All anonymized data from this study are available to qualified researchers upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Study design and participants

The Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR-III) was a nationwide, prospective registry enrolling patients with acute ischemic stroke or TIA admitted to 201 hospitals in China between August 2015 and March 2018. The design of the CNSR-III has been reported previously [25]. This study strictly adheres to the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration (2013 edition) and has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (IRB approval number: KY2015-001-01).

Eligible patients were consecutively enrolled if they were ≥ 18 years of age, had a diagnosis of ischemic stroke or TIA within 7 days of onset, and provided written informed consent from the patient or a legally authorized representative.

Sample collection

Fasting blood samples were collected within 24 h of hospital admission. EDTA-treated plasma and serum samples were processed, stored at − 80 °C, and transported under cold-chain conditions to the central laboratory at Beijing Tiantan Hospital. All the testing was carried out by laboratory personnel blinded to clinical information. The concentrations of hs-CRP was measured from serum on Roche Cobas C701 analyzer. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was measured from plasma by enzymatic assay and triglycerides (TG) were determined from serum using enzymatic colorimetric methods.

Assessments of covariates

The data for the participants was meticulously collected by trained interviewers using structured questionnaires [25]. Lifestyle characteristics included smoking and alcohol consumption. Information on disease and medication history confirmed by physicians covered hypertension, dyslipidemia, previous ischemic stroke/TIA, atrial fibrillation, peripheral vascular disease and cardiovascular disease (CVD). We recorded the scores of the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [26], the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [27], as well as the medications used in the pre-hospital setting (antihypertensive drugs, hypoglycemic drugs, antiplatelet drugs, anticoagulant drugs, lipid-lowering drugs). Glycemic status was classified as follows: ① Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined by meeting at least one of the following criteria: [1] FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL, or [2] HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [3], and / or current use of antidiabetic medications [4], and / or self-reported diabetes diagnosed by a doctor. ② Pre-diabetes mellitus (Pre-DM) was characterized by an FPG of 100 to 125 mg/dL or an HbA1c of 5.7–6.4%. ③ Normal glucose regulation (NGR) was defined as FPG < 100 mg/dL and HbA1c < 5.7%.

Follow-up and outcomes

Participants were followed face-to-face at 3 months and by telephone at 6 months, 1 year, and 5 years after enrollment. The primary outcomes included recurrent stroke and ischemic stroke recurrence. Secondary outcomes included composite vascular events (ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, myocardial infarction, or vascular death) and all-cause mortality. Hospitalized cerebrovascular events were confirmed by the treating hospital, while non-hospitalized suspected events were adjudicated by an independent endpoint committee. Mortality was verified using death certificates obtained from either the treating hospital or the local citizen registry.

Calculation of hsCTI

The high-sensitivity C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index (hsCTI) was calculated using the formula [28]:

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into quartiles based on hsCTI, with the lowest quartile considered as reference group for analysis. For continuous variables, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), whereas categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test were used to compare outcome incidence across hsCTI quartiles. Subgroup analyses were conducted in an exploratory manner to examine associations between hsCTI and 1-year stroke outcomes stratified by glucose regulation, sex, and age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years). Additionally, we conducted a comparison of baseline variables between included and excluded patients. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association of hsCTI with clinical outcomes. Outcomes included recurrent stroke, composite vascular events, and mortality. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, body mass index, relevant medical histories, and prehospital medications.

Relationships between continuous hsCTI and outcome risk were explored using restricted cubic splines (RCS) based on Cox models, with non-linearity assessed via the Wald test overall and within age and sex strata. To evaluate incremental predictive value, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. Areas under the curve (AUCs) for the reference model and models adding hsCTI or the TyG index were compared using the DeLong test. Furthermore, clinical risk reclassification improvement was quantified using continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).

As a sensitivity analysis, an inverse probability weighting (IPW) approach was employed to further minimize confounding bias. Propensity scores were calculated using the same covariates adjusted in the multivariable model. Subsequently, Cox proportional hazards models were refitted in the IPW-weighted cohort to re-evaluate the association between baseline hsCTI levels and the risk of stroke recurrence.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Of the 15,166 patients enrolled in CNSR-III, 10,197 were excluded due to missing baseline data, 4,408 without hsCRP, 266 without TG, 2,151 without FPG and 3,372 without HbA1C, leaving 4,969 patients for the final analysis in Fig. 1. Compared with excluded individuals, included participants had similar baseline age, sex, and BMI, but exhibited higher fasting plasma glucose levels and a greater prevalence of diabetes, along with more frequent use of antidiabetic and lipid-lowering medications. Included patients also showed elevated LDL-C and HDL-C levels but lower hsCRP levels (Table S1).

Association between the hsCTI and the risk of stroke

As shown in Table 2, higher baseline hsCTI was consistently associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence.Compared to Q1, the risk of 3-month stroke recurrence increased with higher hsCTI levels, with aHR 1.83 (95% CI, 1.23–2.73) for Q4. Similarly, the 1-year stroke risk was significantly higher in Q4 (aHR, 1.64; 95% CI, 1.22–2.22). These associations remained significant but were attenuated over the 5-year follow-up period.

Given the imbalances in baseline characteristics between the included and excluded participants, an inverse probability weighting (IPW) analysis was further performed to balance the confounding factors and evaluate the robustness of our findings (Table S2). After IPW adjustment, higher baseline hsCTI levels remained consistently and significantly associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence. Specifically, after fully adjusting for all potential covariates in Model 3, the hazard ratios were 1.42 (95% CI, 1.05–1.92) for Q2, 1.73 (95% CI, 1.29–2.32) for Q3, and 1.62 (95% CI, 1.20–2.18) for Q4.

As shown in Table S3, a similar pattern was observed for ischemic stroke recurrence, with increasing risk across hsCTI quartiles at 3 months (Q2: aHR 1.65, 95% CI, 1.09–2.50; Q3: aHR 2.10, 95% CI, 1.40–3.13; Q4: aHR 1.82, 95% CI, 1.20–2.76; Q1 vs. Q2-Q4: aHR 1.85, 95% CI, 1.29–2.66). Furthermore, elevated hsCTI was associated with an increased risk of composite vascular events in Table S4. Patients in the highest quartile exhibited more than a two-fold risk at 3 months (aHR 2.04; 95% CI, 1.38-3.00; P < 0.001), the relationship that remained significant at 1 year (aHR 1.76; 95% CI 1.32–2.36; P < 0.001) and throughout the 5-year follow-up.

Sex- and age-specific associations

Table S5 examined the association between hsCTI and 1-year stroke risk stratified by gender and glycemic status. Fully adjusted analyses (Model 3) revealed that higher hsCTI was significantly associated with increased stroke risk only among males with NGR, where the combined Q2-Q4 group had a 76% higher risk than Q1 (aHR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.05–2.95; P = 0.032). No significant associations were observed in males with pre-DM or DM, or in females with NGR or pre-DM (all P > 0.05). Notably, HRs for the female DM subgroup were not estimable due to zero events in the reference group.

As shown in Table S6, the association between hsCTI and stroke was stronger and more consistent in participants aged ≥ 60 years across all glycemic categories. These relationships persisted after multivariable adjustment in the all state subgroups (NGR: aHR 2.14, 95% CI 1.20–3.80, P = 0.010; Pre-DM: aHR 1.94, 95% CI 1.09–3.45, P = 0.025; DM: aHR 1.88, 95% CI 1.01–3.50, P = 0.046).

RCS analysis (Fig. 4) demonstrated a consistent positive relationship between baseline hsCTI levels and stroke recurrence risk, which was particularly prominent in participants aged ≥ 60 years and males. No significant non-linear trend was observed in any of the subgroups (non-linear P value > 0.05).

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective cohort study, we demonstrated that elevated hsCTI is independently associated with both short- and long-term risks of stroke recurrence and composite vascular events, particularly in older adults (aged ≥ 60 years). The inclusion of hsCTI in risk assessment models significantly enhances clinical reclassification performance for stroke recurrence, supporting its potential utility as a potent and integrated marker for risk stratification and targeted intervention.

Accumulating evidence links an elevated TyG index to atherosclerosis, stroke, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [29, 30]. A meta-analysis of 18 studies involving 592,635 participants confirmed its strong association with ischemic stroke risk, recurrence, and mortality [31]. TyG is also linked to vulnerable carotid plaque, which is relevant in older adults and patients with diabetes [32]. IR may contribute to recurrent stroke partly by aggravating structural fragility in senescent vascular beds. Furthermore, the TyG index has been shown to mediate over 50% of BMI’s effect on stroke risk, highlighting weight control’s role in IR-driven vascular damage [33]. Beyond systemic metabolism, emerging evidence suggests the gut microbiota-metabolite axis significantly modulates IR and systemic inflammation [34].

CTI is a composite metabolic marker associated with cardiovascular diseases, cancer, depression, and diabetes [17, 22, 35], and it serves as a predictor for new-onset CVD and mortality [36]. Elevated CTI levels were independently related to increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks in patients with CHD and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [37]. In addition, Ma et al. demonstrated that cumulative CTI (cuCTI) and longitudinal changes in CTI are independently associated with CVD risk [38]. Moreover, Tang et al. found that elevated CTI levels predicted a higher incidence of stroke in hypertensive individuals [39]. Our findings extend this body of evidence by showing that hsCTI, which further incorporates inflammatory information, is associated not only with incident risk but also with recurrent stroke and subsequent vascular events.

Compared to standard CRP, hsCRP serves as a more sensitive clinical indicator of inflammation, capable of simultaneously reflecting both chronic low-grade inflammation and acute-phase responses [40]. HsCTI may capture the convergence of vascular vulnerability and inflammaging [41]. Previous studies have shown that worsening glycemic status is associated with a higher prevalence and extent of coronary atherosclerosis, high-risk plaque, and stenosis even in asymptomatic individuals [42]. Individuals in the higher hsCTI quartile may exhibit a greater inflammatory burden and more severe IR, highlighting the need for early screening in this high-risk group.

Sex-specific associations may stem from the differential effects of sex hormones on inflammation and glycemic status. In men, the lack of estrogen protection allows low-grade inflammation and IR to impair endothelial function and accelerate atherosclerosis. In women, estrogen may mitigate these effects, but protection diminishes as glycemic dysregulation progresses in pre-DM. Additionally, sex differences in fat distribution, inflammation, and vascular responsiveness may explain the delayed risk manifestation in women, highlighting biological variability in the metabolic-inflammatory axis.

Our multiple subgroup and interaction analyses revealed significant interactions in older patients (≥ 60 years old) and those with a lower initial stroke severity (lower NIHSS score). However, as these subgroup analyses are exploratory in nature and aim to propose hypotheses regarding potential risk stratification. Our results must be interpreted with caution.

Older patients have typically experienced longer cumulative exposure to adverse metabolic and inflammatory stimuli and are more likely to have advanced atherosclerosis and impaired vascular resilience [43]. In older adults, a multidimensional risk approach may be more effective than relying on single biomarkers. HsCTI is inexpensive, easily obtained, and suitable for clinical use. It offers a more integrated measure of residual vascular risk after stroke.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, 10,197 of 15,166 patients (67.2%) were excluded primarily due to missing baseline hsCRP, triglyceride, fasting plasma glucose or HbA1c data. This reflects the pragmatic constraints of achieving uniform 24-hour fasting blood collection across a nationwide multicenter emergency stroke registry Second, despite extensive multivariable adjustments, residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, hsCTI was assessed only at baseline, precluding the evaluation of dynamic risk trajectories during secondary prevention. Finally, imaging data on plaque characteristics were unavailable; future studies incorporating vascular imaging could further clarify the biological basis of hsCTI in secondary stroke prevention.

Conclusion

Elevated baseline hsCTI level was associated with an increased risk of stroke recurrence and composite vascular events. HsCTI may serve as a potential biomarker for identifying residual vascular risks.
