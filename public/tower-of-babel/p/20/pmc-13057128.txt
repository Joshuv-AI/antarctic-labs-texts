Poorly cohesive carcinoma diffusely involving the whole gastrointestinal tract: a case report
Authors: Wei Gao, Yusheng Yang, Xinyi Hu, Yujuan Shi, Kai Liu, Minmin Gu, Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer death worldwide.Case summaryWe reported a 64-year-old male patient with paroxysmal dull pain around the periumbilical region after meals. After admission, the patient underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scans of the upper and lower abdomen, which revealed marked thickening of the gastric antrum and multiple enlarged lymph nodes around the gastric antrum and in the retroperitoneum. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed possible tumor lesions at the cardia, gastric body, duodenal bulb, and descending part of the duodenum. Colonoscopy suggested possible inflammatory changes in the terminal ileum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and sigmoid colon. Based on subsequent hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemical analyses, the patient was diagnosed with intramucosal poorly differentiated invasive adenocarcinoma. The final diagnosis was Poorly Cohesive Carcinoma (PCC) with mucosal surface implantation in the whole gastrointestinal tract.ConclusionsPCC typically corresponds to a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma characterised by a high degree of malignancy. The occurrence of tumours of the same histological type diffusely involving the entire gastrointestinal tract is exceedingly rare.

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a common gastrointestinal cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. According to the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma, the histological type of gastric cancer is classified into common and special types. The common types are adenocarcinoma, papillary adenocarcinoma, tubular adenocarcinoma of well or moderate differentiation, poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas of the solid or non-solid type, signet-ring cell carcinoma, and mucinous adenocarcinoma [2, 3].

The WHO classification categorises gastric cancer into four types: tubular adenocarcinoma, papillary adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and PCC, which includes the signet-ring cell phenotype and other cell types [4]. According to the Lauren classification, most PCCs belong to the diffuse type. These types have a high incidence of peritoneal metastasis at diagnosis and a poor prognosis [5].

Moreover, the biological behaviour of PCC differs notably from that of conventional adenocarcinomas: PCC generally demonstrates strong invasiveness and aggressiveness, tends to infiltrate beyond the serosa, and is prone to peritoneal dissemination, resulting in a generally poor prognosis for affected patients [6].

PCC most commonly occurs in the stomach, where it constitutes an important histological subtype of gastric cancer, and is particularly more common in younger patients [7]. Among malignant tumours of the intestine, adenocarcinoma is the most frequent type, whereas reports of PCC arising in the whole tract are exceedingly rare. Due to the limited size of biopsy specimens and tumour heterogeneity, there is a high risk of underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis. PCC typically demonstrates rapid progression and is associated with a poor prognosis. Imaging features of PCC are often nonspecific, and definitive diagnosis usually relies on histopathological examination.

PCC is a distinct and aggressive histological subtype of gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma, most commonly arising in the stomach. PCC is characterised by diffuse infiltration of discohesive tumour cells and is associated with rapid progression and poor prognosis. Although PCC has been reported sporadically in other segments of the gastrointestinal tract, its occurrence beyond the stomach remains exceedingly rare. To date, involvement of multiple gastrointestinal segments by PCC is limited to isolated case reports, and cases affecting the entire gastrointestinal tract have rarely been described. The present case is therefore clinically significant, as it highlights an unusual pattern of multifocal gastrointestinal involvement by PCC, underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with its diffuse and nonspecific presentation, and emphasises the importance of integrating endoscopic, radiologic, and pathological findings for accurate diagnosis.

Case report

We reported a 64-year-old man who presented to our hospital with intermittent postprandial abdominal pain, mainly described as paroxysmal dull pain around the periumbilical region. The patient denied any other history of chronic diseases and the history of infectious diseases such as hepatitis, tuberculosis, and schistosomiasis.

After the patient’s admission, tumour marker evaluation revealed carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) of 8.36 ng/mL, Carbohydrate antigen19-9 (CA19-9) of 19,638 U/mL, and Carbohydrate antigen 72 − 4(CA72-4) is 8.92 U/mL, and these markers were all elevated.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the upper abdomen revealed marked thickening of the gastric antrum, along with multiple enlarged lymph nodes around the antrum and in the retroperitoneum. Contrast-enhanced CT of the lower abdomen revealed nodular thickening and enhancement of the colonic wall in the ascending, transverse, and descending colon, accompanied by mesenteric infiltration, suggestive of multifocal tumour involvement (Fig. 1).

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed diffuse positivity for cytokeratin (CKpan), confirming the epithelial origin of the tumour. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 70%, and p53 was diffusely expressed in the lower to middle layers of the neoplasm, with reduced expression in the superficial layer, indicating a high proliferative activity. Preserved E-cadherin and membranous β-catenin are all active in supporting expression, indicating intact cell adhesion (Fig. 5).

Although CK7/CK20 immunostaining may provide supportive information regarding tumour origin, its diagnostic specificity in PCC is limited, and the primary site in this case was determined based on the overall clinicopathological context. The absence of CK7/CK20 staining, therefore, represents a limitation of this case.

Considering the advanced stage of PCC with diffuse involvement of the entire gastrointestinal tract and systemic metastasis, radical surgical resection was not feasible, and the patient was referred for palliative management. The patient and his family decided to undergo a palliative treatment. However, the patient requested to be discharged and transferred to another hospital for further treatment. The patient was followed up in another hospital for anti-tumour treatment. The specific treatment plan was not disclosed.

About treatment, PCC is generally associated with an aggressive clinical course and limited therapeutic options in advanced stages. Recommend systemic chemotherapy as the mainstay of treatment. Platinum-based combination regimens, such as fluoropyrimidine plus cisplatin or oxaliplatin, are commonly used as first-line therapy for advanced gastric PCC. In selected patients with good performance status, taxane-containing regimens may be considered. Targeted therapies are often less effective in PCC due to the low frequency of actionable molecular alterations, such as HER2 overexpression. Nevertheless, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising option for select patients, particularly those with microsatellite instability-high status, high tumour mutational burden, or elevated PD-L1 expression. Although treatment details after transfer were unavailable in this case, these therapeutic considerations reflect current clinical practice and may help guide management in similar scenarios.

Discussion

PCC is a relatively uncommon histological subtype in the gastrointestinal tract, most frequently observed in the stomach and particularly among younger patients. It is characterised by a diffuse infiltrative growth pattern composed of isolated single tumour cells or small clusters, lacking well-formed glandular structures. The absence of organised gland architecture and the presence of scattered or nested malignant cells are hallmarks of this entity. PCC exhibits high aggressiveness and a strong propensity for serosal invasion and lymph node metastasis, which together contribute to its generally poor clinical prognosis [8].

Reports of PCC arising in other parts of the gastrointestinal tract, such as the small intestine and colon, are exceedingly rare and are mostly limited to isolated case reports or small retrospective series, suggesting an extremely low incidence.

PCC typically demonstrates altered expression of adhesion-related markers, particularly E-cadherin and β-catenin, which play essential roles in maintaining intercellular cohesion and are important for histopathological diagnosis [9]. In the present case, immunohistochemical analysis revealed a largely uniform profile across all sampled sites, including diffuse pan-cytokeratin positivity, a high Ki-67 proliferation index, strong p53 expression, and preserved E-cadherin and β-catenin expression. These findings suggest partial retention of adhesion complexes despite the poorly cohesive morphology.

Although multiple lesions were identified throughout the stomach, duodenum, ileum, and colon, several lines of evidence support the interpretation of a single continuous disease process rather than synchronous primary tumours. Histologically, tumour samples from different gastrointestinal sites consistently demonstrated typical PCC features, including diffuse infiltration of discohesive tumour cells lacking glandular formation. Radiologically, contrast-enhanced CT revealed diffuse gastrointestinal wall thickening with extensive lymph node involvement, favouring widespread dissemination rather than anatomically distinct primary tumours. Given the extreme rarity of synchronous PCC arising independently at multiple gastrointestinal sites, these findings collectively support the diagnosis of a single disease entity with multifocal gastrointestinal involvement.

Therefore, a comprehensive diagnostic approach integrating imaging, histopathology, and immunohistochemical findings is essential to improve diagnostic accuracy in such rare and complex presentations.

In the present case, elevated serum tumour markers supported the gastrointestinal origin of the malignancy. Endoscopy played a crucial role in establishing the diagnosis through direct visualisation and biopsy, while contrast-enhanced CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were essential for evaluating the extent of disease, including gastrointestinal wall involvement, lymph node metastasis, and potential distant spread. These imaging findings were critical for disease staging and clinical decision-making [10, 11].

Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed suspected lesions at the cardia, gastric body, duodenal bulb, and descending duodenum, whereas colonoscopy demonstrated possible inflammatory or neoplastic changes in the terminal ileum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and sigmoid colon.

Taken together, these findings indicate that no single diagnostic modality alone could fully delineate the disease distribution. Instead, an integrated evaluation combining endoscopic, radiological, serological, and pathological findings was essential for accurately characterising the extent of involvement and establishing the final diagnosis in this rare presentation of poorly cohesive carcinoma affecting the entire gastrointestinal tract.

Histopathological evaluation remains the diagnostic cornerstone.Immunohistochemistry revealed partial retention of preserved E-cadherin expression, suggesting incomplete loss of adhesion complexes typically associated with PCC. The immunohistochemical findings were consistent across all sites and supported the diagnosis of PCC, particularly the preserved E-cadherin expression despite poorly cohesive morphology. These findings collectively supported the final diagnosis of PCC.

The potential mechanism underlying diffuse implantation of PCC throughout the gastrointestinal tract is noteworthy. Classically, loss of cell adhesion molecules, particularly preserved E-cadherin encoded by the CDH1 gene, promotes tumour cell detachment and intraluminal migration, allowing implantation on downstream mucosal surfaces [12]. Interestingly, in this case, preserved E-cadherin expression was partially preserved. This finding underscores that while loss of preserved E-cadherin is a hallmark, its preservation does not preclude the diagnosis of PCC. The histomorphological features remain the gold standard for diagnosis. Furthermore, local inflammation, ulceration, or mucosal injury caused by primary gastric or intestinal tumours may facilitate implantation by exposing basement membranes or submucosal tissue, thus promoting secondary adherence of detached tumour cells.

At the molecular level, PCC is often associated with mutations in CDH1, TP53, or SMAD4, which collectively enhance invasiveness and metastatic potential [13]. Such alterations may underlie both the aggressive nature of the disease and its capacity for diffuse gastrointestinal involvement, as observed in this case.

In this case, the extensive gastrointestinal involvement suggests dissemination from a dominant primary lesion. The extensive involvement of the duodenum, ileum, and colon may be explained by two potential mechanisms: intraluminal implantation and metastatic spread via the serosal surface and peritoneal cavity. Peritoneal dissemination is a recognised pathway in PCC, particularly in advanced disease, and the presence of widespread lymph node involvement supports this possibility. However, several findings favour intraluminal implantation as a major contributing mechanism. Endoscopically, the secondary lesions were predominantly superficial, presenting as erosions or mucosal irregularities rather than deep infiltrative masses. Histologically, tumour cells were mainly distributed within the mucosal layer, which is more consistent with implantation on the mucosal surface than with serosal metastasis. Furthermore, the highly uniform histological and immunohistochemical features across all gastrointestinal sites support dissemination from a common primary lesion. Taken together, these findings suggest that intraluminal implantation likely played a central role, although serosal and peritoneal spread cannot be completely excluded.

In summary, PCC is characterised by aggressive biological behaviour, diffuse infiltration, and a high risk of early dissemination and metastasis, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case illustrates that PCC may involve the entire gastrointestinal tract and that partial retention of preserved E-cadherin expression does not exclude the diagnosis. A comprehensive evaluation combining imaging, endoscopy, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry is critical for accurate diagnosis. Once confirmed, thorough staging and an individualised treatment strategy, typically radical resection when feasible, along with adjuvant chemotherapy or targeted therapy, are essential for improving patient outcomes.
