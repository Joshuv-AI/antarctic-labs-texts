Multi-site evaluation of an interprofessional education and systems intervention to improve geriatric screening in primary care
Authors: Debra K. Litzelman, Julie Vannerson, Emilie L. Garrison, Yan Tong, Qing Tang, Melissa R. Thomas, Dawn Butler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo address gaps in geriatric care, the Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement Program (GWEP) implemented a multi‑year educational and systems-level intervention aligned with the Age-Friendly Health System 4Ms framework across eight diverse primary care sites. This study examined the association between the intervention and changes in geriatrics screening practices and care processes for older adult patients.MethodsIn collaboration with social workers and health system educators, the GWEP team developed and implemented seven structured educational sessions for primary care teams, in addition to integrating geriatric consultants into primary care clinics and electronic medical record enhancements. Topics of the educational sessions, based on the 4Ms framework, included polypharmacy, advance care planning, and Medicare Annual Wellness Visits, with accompanying, distinct changes to workflow and care processes. Unadjusted pre–post comparisons were conducted using chi-square tests and Generalized Estimating Equation logistic regression models adjusted for covariates were used to assess screening outcomes of interest. Changes in fall risk screening rates across study phases were analyzed using a piecewise linear mixed-effects regression model.ResultsFifty-six primary care providers, along with 180 medical residents under their supervision, cared for 8,818 older adults throughout the study period. Adjusted analyses demonstrated improvements in dementia (odds ratio 1.27, 95%CI: 0.98–1.63) and fall risk screening (odds ratio 1.40, 95%CI: 1.00–1.95) following implementation of the intervention, with variability across sites and the largest gains observed in two clinics with stronger systems integration. Fall risk screening demonstrated the strongest improvement following intervention implementation (+ 0.64% per month, p < 0.001). Screening for opioid use (odds ratio 0.57, 95%CI: 0.36–0.90) and documentation of advance care planning (odds ratio 0.85, 95%CI: 0.80–0.91) significantly declined over time, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. No overall significant changes were observed in hypertension control or high-risk medication measures, despite site-level variation.ConclusionsClinics receiving additional workflow support or enhanced collaboration with geriatric consultants showed the most robust improvements. These findings highlight the importance of combining interprofessional education with team‑based infrastructure and community partnerships to strengthen geriatric care in primary care settings, while highlighting the vulnerability of complex geriatric processes during periods of healthcare disruption.

Background

The rapid growth of the older adult population in the United States has intensified pressure on primary care systems already strained by workforce shortages, rising complexity of care, and widening inequities in access to geriatric expertise [1, 2]. Older adults frequently present with geriatric syndromes—such as cognitive impairment, fall risk, polypharmacy, and functional decline—often under-recognized and inadequately addressed in routine primary care settings [3, 4]. These gaps contribute to preventable morbidity, fragmented care, and poor patient and caregiver outcomes [5].

In response, national efforts have emphasized transforming primary care delivery through workforce development, interprofessional education (IPE), and system redesign. The Age-Friendly Health Systems 4Ms framework (What Matters, Medication, Mentation, and Mobility) provides a structured, evidence-based approach to improve care for older adults across settings [6]; however, sustained changes to clinical practice remain challenging, particularly in primary care environments. IPE has been identified as a key strategy for strengthening geriatric competence and improving care delivery [7]. Prior studies demonstrate how team-based educational models can improve clinician knowledge, attitudes, and confidence in managing geriatric syndromes [8–10]. When paired with workflow integration and electronic medical record supports, such interventions have shown promise in increasing screening for dementia and fall risk in primary care [10–13]. Nevertheless, outcomes vary widely across sites, underscoring the influence of local context, leadership, and implementation readiness [14].

Dementia and fall risk screening are particularly salient targets which may be best clinically integrated within primary care practices. Cognitive impairment is common among older adults yet frequently under-detected, despite structured screening during Medicare Annual Wellness Visits being feasible and acceptable [15, 16]. Concerns about overdiagnosis, limited treatment options, and downstream resource constraints continue to shape clinician uptake [13, 17, 18]. Falls represent another high-impact geriatric syndrome, with strong guideline support and validated screening tools embedded in national quality initiatives [19–21]. Both clinical guidelines and empirical studies support the importance of early detection and intervention for dementia and fall risks in older adults, though the dissemination of these necessary risk management techniques is sparse in interprofessional primary care settings.

IPE interventions for both dementia and other geriatric screenings, such as fall prevention, have been found to be severely lacking [22]. The Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement Program (GWEP) was established to address these gaps, with prior evaluations highlighting the importance of iterative educational strategies incorporating interdisciplinary collaboration and community partnerships [6, 10, 23, 24]. The present study evaluates the association between a GWEP-based IPE intervention among eight primary care sites in central Indiana and changes in geriatric screening and care processes, including dementia, fall risk, opioid use, advance care planning, hypertension control, and risky medication use. We hypothesized that screening practices most closely aligned with structured workflows and team-based care—particularly mentation and mobility—would demonstrate the greatest improvement, while more complex or time-intensive domains (discussing advance directives, complex risky medication, and pain management) might be more vulnerable to time pressures experienced in busy clinics settings.

Methods

Program development

Following a six-month pilot education intervention from January to June 2019, Indiana Geriatrics Education and Training Center (GETC) trainers developed and implemented a multi-year IPE and systems-level intervention from July 2019 through June 2024 in Central Indiana. The Indiana GETC trainers included a board-certified geriatrician, a geriatrics-trained nurse, a social worker, and two highly experienced primary care physicians who care for large panels of older adults. This intervention aligned with the 4Ms framework of the Age-Friendly Health System from the Institute for Health Improvement [25] and was designed to improve the care provided to older adult patients and primary care screening and processes around common geriatric syndromes. This project was reviewed and approved by Indiana University’s Institutional Review Board (Protocol #: 10490).

Settings and participants

As a follow-up to a prior study of system improvement within the interprofessional workforce, the Indiana GWEP program implemented a five-year IPE and system-level intervention aligned with the Age-Friendly 4Ms framework across eight diverse primary care community health centers (CHC), classified as Federally Qualified Health Centers, in Central Indiana. The intervention targeted primary care physicians, internal medicine residents, nurses, front office staff, and social workers in both clinical and academic settings. There were 56 primary care physicians and 180 internal medical residents trained across the eight sites, who collectively cared for 8,818 older adults during the five year-study period. The number of physicians and residents at each CHC were proportional to the number of patients served at each center. All providers and residents who cared for older patients were included. Incoming providers and residents were onboarded and trained as they joined the CHCs, with new residents picking up patient panels from graduating residents each year.

Education intervention

Indiana GETC delivered distinct “Geriatrics Learning Enhancement Exercises” (GLEE) sessions to primary care clinic staff and providers, incorporating Age-Friendly principles related to the 4Ms framework in all sessions. The GLEE topics included: Hypertension; Dementia; Caregiving; Polypharmacy; Advance Care Planning; What Matters Most; Mobility, Mentation, Medication; and Dementia Friends Training (GLEE curriculum available from authors upon request). Each GLEE session focused on improving the care provided to older adult patients and screening processes around common geriatric syndromes. To track the impact of the education on the 4Ms, Indiana GETC linked session content to specific Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) quality measures including: screening for dementia; screening for opioid use; screening for use of risky medications; discussion of advance care plans; screening for future fall risk; and hypertension control [26].

GLEE sessions were initially designed as 45–60 minute, in-person trainings. Each topic session was delivered every six months over the five-year grant period during existing clinic in-service sessions at each of the eight CHCs. Due to COVID-19, most GLEE sessions shifted to 30-minute virtual educational sessions using the Zoom platform. The first three GLEE sessions were delivered fully via videoconferencing, while the following GLEE sessions were provided in a hybrid format based on each clinic’s preference. Post-pandemic, clinics selecting virtual participation often joined remotely via a smart screen in each meeting room where the Indiana GETC trainers were located.

Beginning in fall 2022, Indiana GETC trainers also participated in health system physician and advanced practice provider onboarding and refresher trainings occurring once per quarter (in-person or virtual). These onboarding sessions included a brief review of the 4Ms framework using a patient case, followed by an overview of geriatrics services available at medical and community-based services available to older adults in Marion County.

Systems-level intervention

Embedded geriatric consult in primary care sites

Beginning in 2020, a geriatrics-trained team was embedded in three primary care clinics. These geriatric consultants were led by a geriatrician and included a home-based geriatric social worker. To further integrate Age-Friendly care delivery in routine practice, the geriatric consultant clinics implemented an electronic medical record (EMR) note template incorporating the 4Ms. Indiana GETC trainers, geriatricians, and social workers from the consultant clinics met monthly to review implementation progress, address barriers, and align clinic workflows with 4Ms of care.

Advance care planning and medicare annual wellness visit

In preparation for GLEE Topic (What Matters/advance care planning content), Indiana GETC collaborated with one health system’s legal team to support development of the new advance directives form in response to updated State of Indiana requirements. After finalization, the updated form and associated documentation was integrated into this GLEE session content to review the new advance care planning requirements with primary care staff and providers.

Following this GLEE session, members of the Indiana GETC team partnered with social workers to pilot workflows to introduce and complete advance care planning documentation for older adult patients at all eight CHC sites. Two Indiana GETC trainers met monthly with social workers to review progress and refine processes. Because clinical team processes for completion of forms varied widely by clinical site, Indiana GETC trainers applied the plan, do, study, act (PDSA) improvement cycle to support process improvement work during the pilot.

Building on the previous pilot project, Indiana GETC joined an interdisciplinary health system taskforce to develop a standardized approach to implementing Medicare Annual Wellness visits in all eight CHCs. Indiana GETC team members participated in recurring monthly meetings focused on reviewing and revising the Medicare Annual Wellness form in the EMR for approval and adoption across services and departments of the health system. Additionally, the Indiana GETC teams supported PDSA work at each clinical site to improve implementation of this new process.

In early 2023, the Indiana GETC team collaborated with the health system’s Provider Efficiency Coach to improve provider efficiency in the EMR. The Coach identified an opportunity to improve the “risky medications” screener by embedding a hover-over list of high-risk medications which could be viewed when clinic staff selected the item “Is the patient on risky medication?” Following this update, Indiana GETC incorporated brief instructions on using the enhanced screener in the EMR.

Statistical methods

The population demographic characteristics (age, gender, race, and ethnicity) of patients cared for during the 18-month pre- and 24-month post-training period were summarized and compared by each individual site and for all sites combined. Two sample t-tests or Chi-square tests were used for the comparisons as appropriate.

Unadjusted screening rates of each outcome measure during the 18-month pre- and 24-month post- training period were summarized using frequencies and percentages and compared using Chi-square tests. Analyses were conducted and reported for each individual site, as well as for all sites combined. To account for the clustering of patient encounters within specific clinics and repeated measures across time, Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) logistic regression models were utilized to assess changes in screening rates between the pre- and post- training period across all sites combined, adjusting for the patients’ demographic characteristics (age, gender, race, and ethnicity).

To evaluate longitudinal trajectories across distinct operational phases of the intervention, changes in the screening rate for fall risk were analyzed across the 6-month baseline period prior to the study, the 18-month pre-training phase, and the 24-month post-training phase. A piecewise linear mixed-effects regression model was applied specifically to the fall risk data to account for the unique, time-dependent variations and complex baseline trends observed in this metric across all sites (Fig. 1), which could not be fully captured by an aggregate pre-post analysis. The estimates of the regression slope (interpreted as rate of change over time during a specific phase) and the 95% confidence intervals were reported for each phase in Table 4. A 5% significance level was used for all the tests. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

The study took place at eight primary care CHCs from July 2019 to June 2024. Providers at the eight CHCs included 56 faculty-level primary care physicians and 180 internal medicine residents who provided primary care to their own panel of patients one half-day per week. Demographics of these providers was: average age of 45 years (25–70 years); 58% being female; and the following racial/ethnic breakdown: White 59%, Black 12%, Hispanic 11%, Asian 7%, Biracial 1%, Not provided 10%. Collectively, these providers cared for 8,818 unique patients aged 65 years or older during the study period.

Statistical analyses showed patient age, sex, and racial composition did not differ significantly between the pre-training and post-training periods at any site or across all sites combined (all p > 0.05). The percentage of patients identifying as Hispanic or Latino also did not differ significantly between the two periods at any individual site (all p > 0.05). However, when data from all sites were combined, the proportion of Hispanic or Latino patients was higher during the post-training period compared with the pre-training period (16.4% vs. 14.5%, p = 0.0021) (Table 1).

The GEE models adjusted for patients’ demographic characteristics (age, gender, race, and ethnicity) revealed significant improvement in fall risk screening across all sites combined from the pre- to post- training period (odds ratio 1.40, 95%CI: 1.00 – 1.95, p=0.050). The overall increase in dementia screening did not reach statistical significance in the adjusted model (odds ratio 1.27, 95%CI: 0.98 – 1.63, p = 0.066), though unadjusted improvements were highly significant (28.1% vs. 35.3%, p=0.002) (Table 2). Several of the eight CHCs contributed most to the significant overall improved outcomes and had more robust outcomes than others. The unadjusted pre–post comparison showed significant increases in dementia screening rates at Site 5, from 14.4% to 29.1% (p = 0.0010), and at Site 7, from 29.8% to 47.4% (p = 0.0054). Significant increases in fall risk screening rates were observed at Site 1 (83.2% vs. 87.6%, p = 0.0083), Site 4 (86.0% vs. 89.2%, p = 0.0422), Site 5 (81.7% vs. 92.2%, p < 0.0001), and Site 7 (75.2% vs. 88.0%, p < 0.0001) (Table 3). The piecewise linear mixed-effects regression model demonstrated the strongest improvement in fall risk screening in the 24-month period following intervention implementation (+0.64% per month, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

The GEE-adjusted model results also showed significantly decreased screening for opioids use (odds ratio 0.57, 95%CI: 0.36–0.90) and documentation of advance care planning (odds ratio 0.85, 95%CI: 0.80 − 0.91) across the study period (Table 3). No statistically significant changes were seen in hypertension control or in reducing risky medications.

Discussion

This study demonstrates how an iteratively developed IPE and system-level intervention aligned with the Age-Friendly Health System 4Ms framework was associated with meaningful improvements in selected geriatric screening practices within primary care, even amid substantial disruption from the COVID-19 pandemic. Dementia and fall risk screening improved overall, whereas opioid screening and advance care planning documentation declined, and no significant changes were observed for hypertension control or risky medication measures. These mixed findings reflect both the promise and the limitations of workforce-focused geriatric interventions implemented in complex, real-world primary care settings [6].

Improvements in fall risk and dementia screening are consistent with prior IPE training combined with system-level supports leading to enhanced clinician screening behaviors for geriatric syndromes [9, 10, 23]. Fall risk screening demonstrated the only significant improvement overall and the most consistent improvements across sites, aligning with strong guideline support and standardized screening embedded in national quality initiatives [19, 20]. Similar gains have been reported in prior implementation studies pairing fall screening with education, EMR tools, and interdisciplinary workflows [10, 11, 27]. However, attrition in positive gains can occur once implementation studies are completed unless sustainability plans are structurally built into workflow along with educational refreshers. In this study, regression analyses demonstrate the decline in fall screening following completion of an earlier intervention and a reversal from declining to increasing fall screening rates following implementation. This reinforces the value of longitudinal systems interventions paired with iterative educational strategies. Evidence supports the success of Age-Friendly integration when complemented by site-specific leadership, feedback, and EMR prompting [28].

Previous studies also support the feasibility of cognitive screening in primary care when supported by workflow integration and team-based approaches [12, 15], with the most consistent gains in dementia screening in our study occurring with site variability. Although dementia screening were only borderline significant overall, significant improvements occurred in select clinics, reflecting known variability in implementation success driven by local context, leadership engagement, and readiness for change [14]. These findings are notable given ongoing debate regarding dementia screening in primary care, including concerns about false positives, downstream resource availability, and patient burden [13, 17, 18]. National organizations continue to emphasize structured cognitive assessment, particularly within standardized rooming or the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit [16, 21].

Substantial heterogeneity across sites was observed in our study, consistent with prior work highlighting variation in staffing, infrastructure, and implementation capacity in primary care [14]. Sites with the greatest improvements often benefited from additional system-level supports or more in-person collaboration and process improvement discussions with the embedded geriatric education team members, reinforcing evidence demonstrating IPE is most effective when paired with local access to consultants [8, 10]. While this level of variation may reflect limitations in the intervention’s generalizability, prior Age-Friendly implementation studies highlight site variation as an anticipated outcome of flexible 4Ms adoption across diverse contexts [25, 29, 30], which can be explicitly measured with EMR metrics. Given our access and use of EMR data to evaluate this study, the site variation we found can serve as a roadmap for refining workflows, processes, and team roles in scaling Age-Friendly care and then re-evaluating each site. Where system support is most robust and integrated into workflow, improvements in care may be expected to be largest, whereas education alone is less likely to translate into sustained changes in practice.

In contrast to gains in mentation and mobility screening, opioid screening and advance care planning documentation declined significantly. These declines likely reflect broader shifts in care delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic rather than deficiencies in the educational intervention itself. Since face-to-face provider-patient visits decreased and were replaced by tele-visits during and post- the COVID pandemic period, providers may have been reluctant to decrease pain medication without being able to examine the patient or discuss what can be considered a sensitive discussion, such as end-of-life preferences, during virtual visits. Rapid expansion of telehealth and competing clinical priorities may have limited opportunities for medication reassessment and complex, values-based discussions, particularly among older adults with multiple comorbidities. Similar disruptions have been reported in pandemic-era studies of vulnerable older adult populations [31, 32]. Indiana GETC implemented several strategies to support advance care planning, including documentation updates and pilot workflows with social work teams. Prior qualitative studies highlight the importance of social workers in supporting medication safety and advance care planning within interdisciplinary geriatric teams [33]. Similarly, advance care planning during the COVID-19 pandemic increased significantly when paired with clinician communication training, supporting the importance of adding targeted trainings to other IPE interventions [34]. Our observed declines on MIPS measures that rely on personal provider-patient communication and supportive guidance and touch bases with social workers may reflect the less personal telehealth visits during the COVID period.

No significant improvements were observed in hypertension control or risky medication measures. These outcomes are influenced by factors beyond clinician awareness, including patient adherence, access to care, and social determinants of health. Prior research suggests educational interventions alone are often insufficient to produce short-term changes in complex chronic disease management or deprescribing outcomes [3, 4], particularly during periods of limited in-person care. In our intervention, hypertension and risky medication use were primarily targeted through educational components and EMR adaptation, rather than formal process improvement work. In the future studies, clinical decision support systems interventions to reduce the initiation of potentially inappropriate medications or substitution of safer medications may have a greater impact the reducing the use of risky medications [35].

This study is limited by its observational pre–post design, reliance on EMR documentation, and potential confounding from disruptions in in-person provider visits related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Variation in implementation of fidelity across sites could not be fully assessed, and patient-centered outcomes beyond screening were not evaluated.

Conclusions

An interprofessional, Age-Friendly educational and system-level intervention was associated with significant improvements in fall risk screening and borderline significant improvement in dementia screening in primary care during a period of unprecedented disruption. Declines in opioid screening and advance care planning documentation highlight the vulnerability of certain interventions with older adults when experiencing a reduction in face-to-face delivery of care. Future GWEP efforts should continue to integrate education with workflow redesign, interdisciplinary team support, and protected time for complex geriatric care discussions.
