A prospective comparison of ultrawide-field and two-field fundus imaging for diabetic retinopathy assessment
Authors: Yanzhen Li, Chenxi Fu, Liheng Xiu, Linyi Zhang, Xi Zhang, Chunhong Chen, Zhongwen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the efficacy of nonmydriatic ultrawide-field (UWF) imaging in diagnosing diabetic retinopathy (DR) compared to nonmydriatic 2-field 45° fundus photography (FP).MethodsA total of 769 eyes with diabetes were included in this prospective, observational, comparative, real-world study. We captured both nonmydriatic 2-field 45° FP and UWF images for each patient. Both imaging modalities were analyzed according to the International Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy Disease Severity Scale to assess agreement in DR grading and image gradability. We also investigated DR lesions visible in UWF images beyond and within the scope of the 2-field 45° fundus images.ResultsUWF imaging and 2-field 45° FP showed substantial agreement in DR grading (weighted κ = 0.798), which decreased to moderate agreement (weighted κ = 0.495) after excluding the eyes graded as ‘no DR’ in both modalities. UWF imaging yielded higher severity grades in 14.33% of cases (87 eyes). A statistically significant difference was observed between UWF imaging and 2-field 45° FP in detecting DR (56.96% vs. 44.47%, p < 0.0001) and referable DR (46.81% vs. 36.02%, p < 0.0001). Among the 369 DR-affected eyes, 24.39% exhibited lesions mainly within the central field, 45.25% showed primarily peripheral lesions, and 30.35% presented lesions uniformly distributed. UWF imaging demonstrated a significantly lower ungradable rate for DR assessment compared to 2-field 45° FP (3.77% vs. 19.90%, p < 0.0001).ConclusionsUWF imaging offers significant advantages over 2-field 45° FP in DR detection, severity grading, and image gradability, demonstrating strong potential to enhance clinical DR management.Trial registrationThis study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05836194) on May 1, 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02260-z.

Introduction

The global population of diabetic patients is projected to surpass 600 million by 2040 [1, 2]. In China, by 2030, diabetes is projected to affect approximately 9.7% of the population (108.4 million individuals), with the estimated total costs for diabetes in adults reaching $460.4 billion.[3] With an aging population and increasing life expectancy, the prevalence of diabetic retinopathy (DR) and associated vision impairment is anticipated to rise significantly [4, 5]. As a result, the increasing prevalence of diabetes and related complications would give rise to a crisis of public health and economic burden[6, 7]. In patients with DR, visual symptoms are often minimal during early disease stages or when pathological changes are confined to the peripheral retina. Symptoms of visual impairment become apparent only when diabetic macular edema or vitreous hemorrhage (VH) occurs, which may lead to irreversible visual damage. However, early detection of DR and appropriate medical intervention can help prevent permanent visual loss and reduce the societal burden imposed by DR.

The Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) standard uses 7-field 30° color fundus photography (FP) and serves as the gold standard for DR assessment [6–8]. However, this method is complicated and time-consuming as it requires pharmacological pupil dilation, skilled photographers, and image montage. To simplify the process of DR progression monitoring in clinical practice, studies and clinical guidelines have advocated for the use of 2-field 45° FP with one macular-centered field and one disc-centered field, which has become a pragmatic alternative in screening contexts.[9] Moreover, advancements in artificial intelligence (AI)-based technologies, particularly deep learning systems designed for detecting early-to-late stages of DR, predominantly utilize 2-field 45° FP [10, 11]. While 2-field 45° FP is a pragmatic and widely adopted method in large-scale screening programs due to its relative simplicity compared to the 7-field protocol, it may overlook DR pathology in the peripheral retina, leading to missed diagnoses and inaccurate grading.

Ultrawide-field (UWF) imaging is an innovative fundus imaging technique that presents several advantages, including nonmydriatic capability, the ability to capture widefield images (180°–200°), rapid image acquisition (0.25 s per image), and high resolution [12]. Compared with 2-field 45° FP, UWF imaging might improve the detection of DR screening and grading with adequate posterior pole and peripheral information. Therefore, UWF imaging might hold significant potential and immense value in optimizing DR management and reducing the occurrence of vision-threatening DR.

To evaluate the value of using UWF in DR screening and grading, our study aimed to compare nonmydriatic UWF imaging with nonmydriatic 2-field 45° FP in the detection and grading of DR. Additionally, we investigated DR lesions beyond and within the scope of the 2-field 45° FP to analyze the differences in DR severity grading. Moreover, we conducted a comparative evaluation of image gradability between UWF imaging and 2-field 45° FP.

Methods

Subjects

This prospective, observational, comparative real-world study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ningbo Eye Hospital (2020-qtky-017). The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05836194), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants included adults (≥ 18 years) with diabetes. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Concurrent non-DR retinal diseases (e.g., retinal vein occlusion, age-related macular degeneration, or retinal periphlebitis); (2) History of retinal laser photocoagulation; (3) Prior posterior segment eye surgery.

Imaging process of 2-field 45˚ FP and UWF

All participants were imaged using predefined retinal imaging protocols for both 2-field 45˚ FP and UWF imaging modalities. All images were obtained through non-mydriatic pupils by certified ophthalmic imaging technicians. The 5-minute interval was implemented to minimize potential accommodation effects and pupil size variations inherent in non-mydriatic imaging protocols, thereby standardizing conditions for both imaging techniques.

Following the EURODIAB protocol, [9] images were acquired using 2-field 45° FP (KOWA nonmyd ɑ-D III; KOWA, Japan) at two anatomical fields: (1) the Macular field, with the optic disc center aligned to the nasal end of the horizontal meridian; and (2) the Disc/nasal field, with the optic disc positioned one disc diameter temporal to the field edge along the horizontal meridian. After obtaining 2-field 45° FP images, UWF images were collected using the Optos P200T system (Daytona P200T; Optos, PLC, United Kingdom).

DR grading

After excluding 107 eyes with confounding conditions (concurrent non-DR retinal diseases, prior retinal laser photocoagulation, or posterior segment eye surgery), the final analysis included 769 eyes. All images were graded independently by two senior ophthalmologists at the ophthalmic reading center. To ensure the consistency and reliability of our DR grading, the two senior ophthalmologists (Y.L. and C.F.) underwent standardized training using a set of reference images prior to the study commencement. The inter-grader agreement, assessed by the weighted kappa (κ) statistic, was 0.80–0.90 for UWF images and 0.81–0.90 for 2-field 45° FP images. UWF images were graded with the Optomap viewing software (Optos V2 Vantage P200T; Optos, United Kingdom). The DR grade of each image was determined based on the agreement achieved by both senior ophthalmologists (Y.L. and C.F.). In cases of disagreement, a retinal expert (L.Z.) made the final decision at the ophthalmic reading center. The detailed process of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

An eye was considered ungradable if the image quality was inadequate to determine whether DR lesions were present. According to the ETDRS definitions, an eye was graded as ‘no evidence of DR’ (rather than ‘ungradable’) when either imaging modality (2-field 45° FP or UWF) met both criteria: (1) clear visualization of ≥ 1 disc area within each ETDRS-defined photographic field, and (2) complete absence of characteristic DR lesions in all visible retinal areas. [13].

DR severity was graded into five levels according to the International Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy Disease Severity Scale (ICDR).[14] For all gradable UWF images, the presence and severity of the following lesions were assessed in the superior nasal, inferior nasal, inferior temporal, and superior temporal (fields 2–5, Fig. 2A) and the central field (defined by 2-field 45° FP; field 1, Fig. 2A): hemorrhages and/or microaneurysms (H/MA), hard exudates, venous beading (VB), intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMA), new vessels on the disc (NVD), new vessels elsewhere (NVE), VH/preretinal hemorrhage (PH) and retinal fibrovascular membranes (RFM) [15, 16].

Adapted from the method of Aiello et al. [17] for comparing UWF with 2-field FP, each lesion type was characterized as follows in Fig. 2A: (1) not observed, (2) predominantly observed within the central field (field 1), (3) predominantly observed outside the central field (fields 2–5), (4) observed uniformly distributed within and outside the central field, or (5) ungradable. For each field, a lesion was classified as predominantly central if 50% or more of the lesion occupied the central field. Conversely, a lesion was considered predominantly outside the central if more than 50% of the lesion located outside the central field. A lesion was deemed uniformly distributed if it exhibited equivalent distribution both within and outside the central field areas.

Statistical analysis

According to the pretest conducted on a total of 278 images graded according to ICDR, the PASS software was used to calculate the sample size with kappa test for agreement using the two-sided test. With an alpha value of 0.05 and the image defect rate set at 10%, the required sample size was approximately 752 eyes.

Participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). The frequencies of categorical variables between groups were compared using chi-square tests. Agreement levels were assessed utilizing κ statistics. For outcomes with multiple levels, quadratic weights were employed to estimate κ statistics and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The evaluation of agreement beyond random chance alone was based on the Landis and Koch interpretation of κ statistics. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0, IBM, USA. All p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant with two-sided tests.

The differences between UWF imaging and 2-field 45° FP in terms of DR present, DR severity, referable DR present, and ungradable rate were statistically significant. UWF imaging screened more cases of DR and referable DR, as well as reduced the ungradable rate.

Assessment of agreement in DR severity scale between 2-field 45˚ FP and UWF imaging

In this study, 153 eyes (19.90%) were classified as ungradable, while 9 eyes (1.17%) demonstrated ‘no evidence of DR’. The remaining 607 eyes (78.93%) were successfully graded by both 2-field 45° FP and UWF imaging. The distribution of DR severity, graded according to ICDR, is presented in Fig. 3A (detailed numbers described in Table S1). According to the 2-field 45° FP, 265 eyes (34.46%) had no DR, 65 eyes (8.45%) had mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), 115 eyes (14.95%) had moderate NPDR and 67 eyes (8.71%) had severe NPDR, and 95 eyes (12.35%) had proliferative DR (PDR). In contrast, the UWF imaging results showed that 294 eyes (38.23%) had no DR, 78 eyes (10.14%) had mild NPDR, 131 eyes (17.04%) had moderate NPDR, 88 eyes (11.44%) had severe NPDR, and 141 eyes (18.34%) had PDR (Table 1).

DR severity grading demonstrated exact concordance between UWF imaging and 2-field 45° FP in 519 eyes (85.50% of gradable cases), with substantial agreement (weighted κ, 0.798; 95% CI, 0.740–0.856). After excluding 238 eyes that were graded as ‘no DR’ in both 2-field 45° FP and UWF images, only 281 eyes showed consistent grading between UWF images and 2-field 45° FP, with moderate agreement (weighted κ, 0.495; 95% CI, 0.370–0.620). Additionally, 87 eyes (14.33% of 607 eyes) were graded higher with UWF imaging than 2-field 45° FP, with 65 eyes (10.71%) having a 1-grade worse rating, and 22 eyes (3.62%) had ≥ 2 grades worse rating (Fig. 3B). Among these images with higher ratings, 32 eyes (5.27%) were diagnosed as moderate NPDR or below using 2-field 45° FP, but as severe NPDR (25 eyes, 4.12%) or PDR accompanied by NVD or NVE (7 eyes, 1.15%) using UWF images. Additionally, 21 eyes (3.46%) diagnosed with no DR using 2-field 45° FP were identified as mild NPDR using UWF (Fig. 3A).

Distribution of lesions

Of the 369 eyes affected by DR, 90 eyes (24.39%) exhibited lesions primarily located within the central field, 167 eyes (45.25%) demonstrated predominantly outside the central field, and 112 eyes (30.35%) were uniformly distributed. Of these fields, 1,461 had H/MA, with 894 fields (61.19%) predominantly outside the central field; 337 IRMA, with 238 fields (70.62%) predominantly outside the central field; and 232 NVE, with 158 fields (68.10%) predominantly outside the central field (Fig. 2B, Table S2). Lesions outside the central field were predominant in the 167 eyes, with 586 fields (70.18%) containing H/MA, 145 fields (17.37%) showing IRMA, and 114 fields (13.65%) presenting new vessel formations (Table S3). The distribution of predominantly peripheral DR lesions is presented in Figure S2.

Ungradable rate and identification of DR

The ungradable rate for DR was significantly lower with UWF imaging compared with 2-field 45° FP (3.77% vs. 19.90%, p < 0.0001). The identification of DR and referable DR (moderate NPDR or more severe levels of DR) was significantly higher with UWF imaging, being 56.96% vs. 44.47% (p < 0.0001) and 46.81% vs. 36.02% (p < 0.0001), respectively (Table 1; Fig. 4). Among the 153 eyes ungradable with 2-field 45° FP, the corresponding UWF images revealed that only 29 eyes were ungradable. Of the remaining 124 eyes, no evidence of DR was found in 8 eyes, no DR in 46 eyes, mild NPDR in 10 eyes, moderate NPDR in 30 eyes, severe NPDR in 5 eyes, and PDR in 25 eyes (7 eyes with NVD/NVE, 18 eyes with PH/VH). A total of 70 eyes were identified with DR and 58 eyes with referable DR using UWF imaging, but these cases were ungradable using 2-field 45° FP.

Discussion

Our objective was to compare the performance of UWF imaging versus conventional nonmydriatic 2-field 45° FP in detecting and grading DR. Our main finding demonstrated substantial agreement in DR severity grading between UWF imaging and 2-field 45°FP (weighted κ = 0.796). When excluding eyes graded as ‘no DR’ in both modalities, agreement decreased to a moderate level (weighted κ = 0.495). Compared to 2-field 45˚ FP, UWF imaging assigned higher DR severity grades in 14.33% of eyes, increased the overall DR detection rate (56.96% vs. 44.47%), improved the identification of referable DR (46.81% vs. 36.02%), and significantly reduced the ungradable rate (3.77% vs. 19.90%). These results suggested that UWF imaging may enhance DR management by enabling more accurate screening and grading, potentially improving long-term visual outcomes.

In this study, a total of 32 eyes were diagnosed with severe NPDR or PDR (with NVD or NVE) using UWF imaging, but were misclassified as moderate NPDR or less when assessed using 2-field 45˚ FP. Timely implementation of pan-retinal photocoagulation is crucial for patients graded as severe NPDR or PDR to prevent disease progression and reduce the need for surgical interventions such as vitrectomy [18]. Another significant subgroup (n = 21, 3.46%) were diagnosed with no DR using 2-field 45° FP but as mild NPDR using UWF. This subgroup is often overlooked in clinical practice, as mild NPDR indicates the presence of DR lesions without necessitating immediate treatment. However, existing studies have demonstrated that DR is potentially reversible with proper blood glucose control following the onset of mild NPDR [11, 19]. Therefore, it is recommended for patients at the mild NPDR stage to prioritize optimal blood glucose control and shorten follow-up intervals. Our study showed that UWF imaging provides more comprehensive information than 2-field FP, which may have important implications for treatment decisions and the assessment of disease progression and prognosis in patients with DR. However, it is also important to recognize the possibility of over-grading in the periphery, as UWF is not yet a certified grading standard.

Aiello et al. compared ETDRS 7-field and UWF images, revealing that 15.0% of H/MA, 12.9% of IRMA, 11.8% of VB, and 53.8% of new vessels were predominantly observed outside the ETDRS fields [17]. In our study, more lesions (61.19% of H/MA, 70.62% of IRMA, 68.10% of NVE, and 61.22% of PH) were predominantly observed outside of the 2-field 45° FP because of the smaller fields compared to 7-field FP. This result suggested that a considerable number of lesions were located outside the central field, significantly impacting the DR detection and grading. Therefore, utilizing UWF imaging for DR screening and grading, as opposed to the 2-field 45° FP approach, could provide more comprehensive diagnostic information, enabling timely intervention and improving prognosis of DR. Recently, research on AI for DR screening and grading primarily focused on the 2-field 45° FP approach. Our results suggest that the integration of UWF imaging and AI may enhance the accuracy of DR screening and grading, which merits further investigation in the future.

The ungradable rate of UWF imaging in our study was remarkably low at 3.77%, consistent with the rates in previous studies ranging from 1.6% to 6.1% [13, 17]. UWF imaging demonstrated a 16.26% reduction in the ungradable rate compared to 2-field 45° FP, establishing it as a valuable diagnostic tool for grading DR in patients with initially ungradable images. Many studies suggested that in traditional FP screening for DR, patients whose images cannot be graded should be referred, which may include many false positive cases [20–22]. Using UWF for DR screening may help reduce unnecessary referrals, optimize the allocation of medical resources, and lessen the burden on both individuals and the healthcare system.

Silva et al. reported that nonmydriatic UWF imaging nearly doubled the identification rate of DR compared to nonmydriatic multifield fundus photography. Their findings were based on a retrospective analysis of separate patient cohorts for each imaging modality.[13] Compared to their study, our study had several characteristics. First, our study was a prospective analysis comparing the agreement between UWF imaging and 2-field 45° FP in DR screening and grading. In addition, we evaluated both imaging modalities within the same patient cohort, minimizing selection bias. Third, we divided the UWF image into five regions to assess lesions both inside and outside the 2-field 45° FP area, allowing for a more fine-grained evaluation of the impact of peripheral lesions on DR identification and grading.

Our study had several limitations. First, no follow-up was conducted to monitor the progression and prognosis of DR in patients with higher UWF imaging grades following intervention. Long-term follow-up studies are needed to further validate the true benefits of UWF imaging in DR screening and grading, such as improvements in screening performance. Furthermore, future research should incorporate multivariate regression analyses to explore the factors associated with higher DR grades on UWF. Second, image acquisition times were not systematically recorded in this study, precluding accurate comparison of examination durations between the 2-field 45° FP and UWF imaging. Third, the high cost of UWF imaging was another limitation. Financial support from the government or a reduction in its price could facilitate its widespread adoption, enhancing DR detection in real-world settings. Fourth, the comparison between UWF and FP was based solely on 2-field imaging and did not include ETDRS 7-field imaging, which restricts conclusions about diagnostic equivalence. Fifth, the image acquisition sequence may have potential order effects, while standardized, were not evaluated in this study.

In summary, our study demonstrated that UWF imaging offered significant advantages over 2-field 45° FP in detecting and grading DR. UWF imaging enhanced screening performance for DR by visualizing peripheral retinal pathology while significantly reducing ungradable rates. These findings suggest that UWF imaging has substantial potential to improve DR management and contribute to better long-term visual outcomes for patients.
