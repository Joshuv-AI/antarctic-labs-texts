Bailout Renal Stenting Associated With Endovascular Aortic Repair: A Single Centre Experience
Authors: Alba Alonso Pastor, Francisco Abril Ramiro, Paloma Alonso Diego, Ahmad Amer Zanabili Al-Sibbai, Francisco Álvarez Marcos, Manuel Alonso Pérez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study describes the midterm results of renal stenting as a bailout technique during endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) of abdominal aortic aneurysms.MethodA single centre retrospective study of renal artery rescue procedures during consecutive elective EVARs was performed in patients without pre-existing renal stenosis over a 22 year period. Demographic characteristics, aneurysm neck morphometry surgical details, technical success, peri-operative complications, re-interventions, renal function, and death during follow up were analysed.ResultsSeven renal stents were implanted (all men; mean age 72.3 ± 7 years) in 748 EVARs (0.9%) in the study period. Two of seven (29%) patients had chronic kidney disease before the intervention. Two of seven (29%) aortic necks fulfilled hostility criteria (one case of conical neck and one case with a diameter >28 mm at renal level). The Herculink Elite (Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA) stent was used in all patients (seven of seven) (median length 18 mm, median diameter 6.14 ± 0.7 mm). Technical success was 100%, with no complications associated with the renal stenting and no peri-operative death. The two patients with chronic kidney disease presented with worsened renal function (creatinine 2.72 ± 3 mg/dL, glomerular filtration rate 62.3 ± 32) a median 17 months after the intervention, with no renal replacement therapy requirements during follow up. The renal stent patency rate was 100% after a median follow up of 21 months (range 6–92 months); no cases of renal infarction were identified on follow up imaging.ConclusionThe need for bailout renal stenting during EVAR was low in this cohort. It is essential to adopt all measures that guarantee precise endograft deployment, even in favourable anatomies.

INTRODUCTION

During previous years, endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) of abdominal aortic aneurysms has become the main treatment method owing to good morbidity and mortality outcomes, especially in the short term.1 However, the growing number of procedures, associated with their use in increasingly complex anatomies, entails a higher risk of endograft deployment complications, such as the unintentional coverage of renal arteries. The incidence of renal artery coverage after EVAR is rare, with <1% of cases reported in the literature. However, it is a major complication that requires rapid action to avoid further complications.2,3 The use of bailout strategies when this occurs is important, as it allows the problem to be solved at the same time or in the first post-operative hours without renal damage becoming established, avoiding these complications.2, 3, 4

Several bailout techniques have been described in the literature (chimney stents, in situ fenestration, etc.) to address this unwanted situation. The choice of a specific technique depends primarily on the experience of the surgical team and the individual characteristics of the patient. Nevertheless, the available data are limited and mostly derived from single centre studies with small sample sizes. Currently, there is no consensus on the optimal technique for these cases.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

The objective of this study was to describe the results of renal stenting as a bailout technique during EVAR of abdominal aortic aneurysms in a single, tertiary care institution.

Study design

A retrospective analysis of all renal artery rescue procedures performed consecutively during elective EVAR was performed. The study period ranged from 2003 to 2024 and was constrained to a single tertiary care institution with a prospectively maintained database.

METHOD

In all cases, the need for renal stent implantation during EVAR was justified by partial coverage of the lowest renal ostium, based on intra-operative findings during angiography. All elective EVARs with no previous renal artery stenosis were included, and accordingly non-elective EVARs were excluded from the analysis, to avoid confounding factors (e.g., higher risk of misdeployment) associated with procedure emergency. Additionally, patients with a pre-existing renal stenosis were also excluded, as in these cases the need for a renal stent is not associated with a bailout approach secondary to EVAR. Given the retrospective nature of data and the anonymised processing of both data and images, the local ethics board dismissed the need for specific informed consent to be obtained from patients.

Bailout stenting was always performed during the EVAR procedure, after the completion angiogram. Using one femoral access, the affected renal artery was catheterised using a Cobra C1 catheter or modified SIM catheter and a 0.014 inch guidewire. After exchange for a stiff guidewire (Hi-Torque Spartacore, Abbott, Chicago, IL, USA), the stent was deployed aiming one third of its length inside the aortic lumen and the remaining two thirds inside the renal artery. The Herculink Elite (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL, USA) stent was used in all patients.

The variables analysed included demographic characteristics, aneurysm neck morphometry, and procedure data. The aneurysmal neck characterisation included length, diameters every 5 mm, conicity (percentage), presence of calcium (percentage over circumference), and presence of thrombus (percentage over circumference and maximum thickness). The aortic neck was classified as hostile if it presented one or more of the following anatomic characteristics: a proximal neck length <10 mm, diameter >28 mm, angulation >60°, conical morphology, or the presence of significant mural thrombus or calcification (≥50% of the circumference).1,10

The follow up protocol included a thirty day, six month and 12 month contrast enhanced computed tomography angiogram (CTA). Although the periodicity of CTAs changed during the study period according to evolving evidence, all patients had annual ultrasound surveillance and at least one CTA performed every two years. Serum creatinine and urea were measured annually; patients with chronic renal impairment were followed up by the nephrology department. The primary outcome was technical success, defined as the successful deployment of the renal stent with renal artery patency on the completion angiogram. Secondary outcomes included stent patency over time, major adverse events, re-interventions, renal function, and mortality over the time.

Statistics

Categorical variables were described using frequencies. Mean and standard deviation were used for normally distributed quantitative variables, with median and range for non-normally distributed data. Normality was tested using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. All calculations were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). No inferential statistics were performed because of the limited sample size.

RESULTS

During the study period, 748 elective EVARs were performed. In seven of these, a renal stent was needed during the procedure, which represents an incidence of 1%.

All patients were men (seven of seven), with a mean age of 72.3 ± 7 years. The main comorbidities were arterial hypertension and dyslipidaemia. Only two of seven (29%) patients had chronic kidney disease before the intervention, with a mean serum creatinine of 1.68 ± 0.3 and a mean glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 41.0 ± 4.2, both corresponding to G3b category in the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (K-DOQI) classification. No anatomic abnormalities or ostial stenoses were observed in these patients in computed tomography scans before the intervention. The mean maximum aortic diameter was 56 ± 0.3 mm. The mean neck length was 38.9 ± 17 mm, and the mean proximal and distal neck diameters were 22.4 ± 4 mm and 23.3 ± 3 mm, respectively. Technical success was 100%, with no complications associated with the renal stent and no peri-operative death. The renal stent patency rate was 100% after a median follow up of 21 months (range 6–92 months), based on the latest imaging study available. Only the two patients with chronic renal impairment presented with worsened renal function at end of follow up, with a mean serum creatinine of 2.54 ± 0.4 mg/dL and eGFR of 22.5 ± 2 at a median 17 months after the intervention. Both downgraded to G4 category in the K-DOQI classification. This worsening of renal function was not attributed to vascular or stent related causes, and appears to be related to the progression of the underlying renal disease. None required renal replacement therapy during follow up.

During follow up, a type II endoleak was detected in two patients. One on these patients required re-intervention owing to aneurysm sac enlargement. No additional complications were reported in the remaining patients during the study follow up period. No cases of renal infarction were identified in follow up imaging. Three of seven patients died a median of 54 months after EVAR from unrelated causes.

DISCUSSION

This study shows a bailout renal stenting rate of less than 1%, in agreement with the published literature.2 Of the seven patients who required renal bailout stenting, only two had criteria for a hostile neck. Although a hostile neck might be a predisposing factor for partial renal ostium coverage, friendlier anatomies are not exempt of this risk. No other clear risk factors or predictors could be identified in this series; possible contributing factors to partial renal artery coverage include minor imprecision in device deployment, stiff guidewire anatomy deformation, or lack of fusion image guidance. In all cases, stent implantation was performed in the same surgical procedure as the EVAR, after discovering the partial coverage of the lowest renal ostium due to an imprecise deployment of the stent graft. Renal stent patency rate was 100% in the follow up period, and none of the patients required renal replacement therapy.

The incidence of renal artery coverage after EVAR is rare, with <1% of cases reported in the literature. However, it is a major complication that requires rapid action to avoid further problems.2,4 Several techniques for renal artery salvage after EVAR have been described including open and endovascular techniques.2 Hamish et al.6 described open techniques to solve unexpected coverage, as did Adu et al.5 Both mentioned the use of open hepatorenal and splenorenal bypass. In terms of endovascular bailout techniques, the use of chimneys stents and encroachment grafting has been described.2, 3, 4,7 The use of in situ fenestrations with a trans-septal needle has also been reported.11,12 More recently, this fenestration could be performed using laser or electrified guidewire. The use of endovascular techniques for renal artery bailout after EVAR offers a less aggressive alternative to open surgery, with the advantages that this implies. In addition, it offers a relatively simple and fast solution, during the same intervention, reducing the renal ischaemia time and thus decreasing the risk of worsening renal function.3 The selection of bailout technique should be guided by the surgeon's expertise, the resources available, and the individual patient's characteristics. In the authors' experience, only renal bailout stenting has been performed, which is safe and effective, allowing immediate restoration of vessel patency. Because of limited experience with other bailout techniques, comparisons between the different approaches were not made.

Regarding the type of stent implanted, the use of bare balloon expandable stents, with high radial strength and flexibility, is usually preferred. However, the type of stent will depend on local experience.3,4,7 These renal stents are in constant contact with the upper part of the stent graft, even more so if it has a proximal free stent. Although patency is not expected to be compromised by atherosclerosis, fractures might occur over time. However, no such cases have yet been described in the literature.

Renal dysfunction is one of the main complications after EVAR, which is associated with decreased long term survival. This renal dysfunction has a multifactorial origin. These causes include problems in the deployment of the stent. Rapid renal artery rescue is associated with complete recovery of renal function or mild dysfunction.2,3,8,13 Several studies suggest that renal stenting is an independent risk factor for renal dysfunction and thirty day death. However, those are studies in which the stent requirement is marked by atherosclerotic pathology concomitant with the presence of abdominal aortic aneurysm. Some differences were seen in the bailout cases, when these were healthy renal arteries. However, it should be noted that these are single centre studies with a small sample size.9,14 Some authors report a slightly higher risk of renal complications for endograft with suprarenal fixation, but further studies on larger series are needed.15

This study has several limitations that should be considered. It was a single centre study, with a small sample size and limited follow up. The limited sample size prevents reliable inferential statistics to be performed and accordingly does not allow identification of potential predictors of deployment issues and the need for a bailout stent. However, the very limited evidence available in the literature regarding this issue gives this experience an added value.

Conclusion

The need for bailout renal stenting during EVAR is low. Even anatomies not meeting formal hostility criteria may carry a risk of imprecise deployment and subsequent need for bailout stenting. It is mandatory to adopt all available measures to ensure precise endograft deployment. When needed, bailout renal stenting shows excellent patency rates with minimal complications over time and no repercussions for long term renal function.
