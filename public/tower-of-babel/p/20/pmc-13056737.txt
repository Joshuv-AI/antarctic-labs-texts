Safety and feasibility of combined transanal total mesorectal excision with delayed coloanal anastomosis in high-risk patients with low rectal cancer
Authors: Y Yue, Y Sun, Y Huang, L Xue, T Wang, G Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the safety and effectiveness of immediate coloanal anastomosis (ICAA) with diverting ileostomy versus delayed coloanal anastomosis (DCAA) following transanal total mesorectal excision (taTME) in reducing anastomotic leakage (AL) and stoma-related complications in high-risk patients with low rectal cancer.MethodsFrom April 2021 to November 2024, a prospective cohort study enrolled 74 high-risk patients with low rectal cancer. On the basis of surgical strategy preference, patients underwent either taTME with delayed anastomosis (DCAA, n = 35) or taTME with immediate anastomosis and diverting ileostomy (ICAA, n = 39). The primary outcome was AL incidence. Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications, inflammatory markers, and anorectal functional outcomes.ResultsA total of 65 patients were analyzed (DCAA, n = 32; ICAA, n = 33). AL occurred only in the ICAA group (12.1%; five cases: two grade A, two grade B, and one grade C), whereas no AL was observed in the DCAA group. The rate of major postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) was comparable between groups (P > 0.05). C-reactive protein levels were significantly lower in the DCAA group on postoperative days 1 (P < 0.001) and 3 (P = 0.034), with no difference by day 7 (P = 0.185). Wexner and LARS scores at 3 and 12 months did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionTaTME with DCAA was associated with a lower incidence of AL and avoidance of stoma-related morbidity compared with ICAA and diverting ileostomy, while maintaining comparable functional outcomes in high-risk patients with low rectal cancer.Keywords: Delayed coloanal anastomosis, Transanal total mesorectal excision, High-risk patients, Low rectal cancer, Functional outcomes

Introduction

Laparoscopic complete mesorectal excision (TME) is still technically difficult in patients with low rectal cancer who have high-risk surgical characteristics, such as obesity, a limited pelvis, or changed tissue planes after neoadjuvant therapy. These elements frequently impair vision and mobility, which results in less-than-ideal resection quality, a higher chance of positive circumferential resection margins (CRM), and insufficient distal resection margins (DRM) [1–3]. Transanal TME (taTME), a hybrid approach combining transanal and abdominal dissection, has been developed to overcome these restrictions. By enabling direct view of the distal rectum, taTME has been reported to facilitate precise tumor localization, and is associated with improved CRM clearance and lower CRM positivity rates in some studies compared to traditional laparoscopic methods, especially in instances that are anatomically complex [4–7].

However, widespread use of taTME has revealed a paradoxical increase in anastomotic leakage (AL) rates. The European Society of Coloproctology (ESCP) multinational investigation found that AL incidence following taTME was significantly greater than that following laparoscopic TME (12.9% vs. 8.9%), consistent with the 15.7% AL rate noted in the international taTME registry [8]. To reduce AL-related morbidity, diverting ileostomy is frequently carried followed during taTME with immediate coloanal anastomosis (ICAA). Ileostomy can effectively lessen the severity of AL, but it comes with significant risks, such as stoma-related problems (43%) [9] and morbidity after closure (> 20%) [10–14].

One such remedy is delayed coloanal anastomosis (DCAA), which was first proposed by Turnbull and Cutait in 1961 for rectal disorders, offers a potential solution [15, 16]. This two-stage process involves colonic exteriorization after TME, followed by delayed anastomosis after 10–14 days. By postponing anastomosis, DCAA may lower the risk of AL through promoted tissue repair and avoidance of diverting stomas. Despite its previous use, evidence supporting DCAA in conjunction with taTME (DCAA-taTME) for high-risk low rectal cancer remains limited.

While the DCAA technique itself is not new, its application in the context of modern minimally invasive surgery and in specifically selected high-risk cohorts warrants further investigation. This prospective cohort study aims to establish the safety and feasibility of combining DCAA with taTME for high-risk low rectal cancer. Distinguishing our study from prior reports on DCAA, we specifically focused on a prospectively enrolled cohort of patients with defined high-risk features, systematically combined DCAA with the contemporary taTME technique, and incorporated rigorous follow-up with serial inflammatory marker assessment and patient-reported functional outcomes. By directly comparing this approach to the standard of taTME with immediate anastomosis and diverting ileostomy (ICAA-taTME), we seek to provide contemporary evidence on whether DCAA-taTME can effectively reduce anastomotic leakage and stoma-related morbidity without compromising functional results in this challenging patient population.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

Seventy-four consecutive patients with high-risk low rectal cancer who were having curative surgery at the General Hospital of Western Theater Command between April 2021 and November 2024 were included in this prospective cohort research. Patients were assigned to either taTME with DCAA or taTME with ICAA and diverting ileostomy, depending on their informed surgical preferences following thorough discussion of the risks and benefits of the procedure. High-risk factors included male sex, ASA class ≥ III, body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2, diabetes, chronic renal insufficiency, alcoholism, smoking, alcohol use, and previous neoadjuvant therapy. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who fulfilled the following requirements were eligible: (1) rectal adenocarcinoma with histological confirmation; (2) tumors ≤ 5 cm from the anal verge; (3) clinical stage cT1–2N0M0, or cT3-4/N + disease necessitating neoadjuvant therapy followed by restaging confirmation using endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

The exclusion criteria included (1) preoperative fecal incontinence or impaired anal function; (2) history of colorectal malignancy; (3) evidence of distant metastases; (4) acute presentations requiring emergency surgery (e.g., obstruction, perforation, hemorrhage); (5) expected positive CRM despite neoadjuvant therapy, or sphincter involvement necessitating abdominoperineal resection [17].

Surgical procedures

A standardized surgical team carried out every procedure at the Western Theater General Hospital.

DCAA-taTME group

Abdominal phase: The inferior mesenteric artery and vein were dissected laparoscopically, with lymphadenectomy performed at the mesenteric root while preserving the left colic artery. Mobilization of the sigmoid colon and proximal rectum was carried out in accordance with TME principles. Splenic flexure mobilization was selectively performed on the basis of colonic length and anticipated anastomotic tension. Anterior rectal dissection extended anteriorly to the seminal vesicles or posterior vaginal fornix, posteriorly to the S3–S5 or coccygeal level, and laterally to the pelvic nerve plexus. When further distal mobilization posed a risk to mesorectal integrity or neurovascular structures, dissection was deferred to the transanal phase.

Transanal phase: As shown in Fig. 2A, a disk retractor was positioned, and two purse-string sutures were placed at least 1 cm distal to the tumor to minimize tumor cell dissemination. For ultra-low rectal tumors, a 2-cm circumferential incision distal to the tumor was performed following upward dissection and purse-string closure. Pneumorectum was established using a transanal platform. A circumferential marking line was drawn at least 1 cm distal to the purse string (Fig. 2B), followed by full-thickness rectal wall incision along the TME plane (Fig. 2C). Longitudinal muscle fibers were divided at the 1–5 o’clock and 7–11 o’clock positions to access the avascular plane. After rendezvous with the abdominal dissection, the rectourethral muscle at the 12 o’clock position and the rectococcygeal muscle at the 6 o’clock position were separated (Fig. 2D). The specimen was then extracted transanally (Fig. 2E). After fixation of the proximal colon to the anal canal with eight interrupted sutures, delayed coloanal anastomosis was performed 3–4 weeks later using 3–0 absorbable sutures (Fig. 2F).

A Placement of a disk retractor to stabilize the anal canal during transanal dissection; B Creation of a circular marking line ≥ 1 cm distal to the tumor using electrocautery, following dual purse-string sutures to minimize tumor dissemination; C Full-thickness rectal wall incision along the TME plane, guided by the premarked circular line; D Division of longitudinal muscle fibers at the 1–5 and 7–11 o’clock positions, followed by dissection of the rectourethral (12 o’clock) and rectococcygeal (6 o’clock) muscles to complete circumferential mobilization; E Transanal exteriorization of the rectal specimen after complete TME dissection; F Delayed hand-sewn coloanal anastomosis performed 3–4 weeks postoperatively using 3–0 absorbable sutures

ICAA-taTME group

Immediately after taTME and specimen extraction, immediate hand-sewn or stapled coloanal anastomosis and a diverting loop ileostomy were carried out.

Outcome measures

The International Study Group of Rectal Cancer criteria for classifying the incidence of AL into three categories—grades A (AL requiring no intervention), B (leakage necessitating antibiotics, drainage, or endoscopic therapy without reoperation), and C (leakage requiring surgical intervention)—were the main outcome. Secondary outcomes including postoperative complications graded by the Clavien–Dindo classification system, inflammatory markers such as white blood cell count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured on postoperative days 1, 3, and 7, and functional outcomes evaluated using the validated Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS) score and Wexner incontinence score [18, 19].

Specifically, the Clavien–Dindo system divides complications into the following five grades: grade I refers to minor deviations from the postoperative course, such as self-limiting infections; grade II involves pharmacological intervention; grade III involves surgical, endoscopic, or radiological interventions; IIIa involves no general anesthesia; IIIb involves general anesthesia; grade IV involves life-threatening complications that require intensive care; and grade V involves death (Figs. 3 and 4).

Functional outcomes were assessed using the LARS score (range 0–42; categorized as no LARS [0–20], minor LARS [21–29], or major LARS [30–42]) and the Wexner score (range 0–20; higher scores indicating more severe fecal incontinence). Furthermore, longitudinal analysis of inflammatory markers were performed to compare systemic responses between groups.

Statistical analysis

Independent t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess continuous variables, which are reported as mean ± SD (normal distribution) or median (IQR). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 74 eligible patients, 35 underwent taTME with DCAA, and 39 underwent taTME with ICAA. Two patients were excluded because they were unable to complete postoperative questionnaires. One patient in the DCAA group was excluded because of ischemic necrosis of the exteriorized colon that resolved without the need for reintervention, and two patients in the ICAA group were excluded because ileostomy reversal was not performed. Consequently, 65 patients were included in the final analysis. At the 12-month follow-up, four patients were lost to follow-up, yielding 32 patients in the DCAA group and 33 in the ICAA group.

Baseline demographic characteristics and high-risk surgical factors, including BMI, ASA class, and receipt of neoadjuvant therapy, were comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05; Table 1).

Discussion

For the surgical management of low rectal cancer, achieving oncological radicality while preserving anal function remains a major clinical challenge. taTME has effectively addressed technical difficulties associated with distal rectal dissection, particularly in patients with male sex, prostatic hypertrophy, a narrow pelvis, visceral obesity, or bulky tumors, and has been shown to yield satisfactory specimen quality. However, this approach is accompanied by a substantial incidence of AL, reported to range from 2.4% to 15.9%, with severe AL carrying a non-negligible risk of mortality [20–22]. Beyond delaying postoperative recovery, AL frequently necessitates reintervention and has been associated with impaired long-term oncological outcomes [23, 24]. Although diverting ileostomy does not eliminate AL, it can mitigate its clinical severity and reduce the need for urgent reoperation [25, 26]. An alternative strategy is to avoid immediate anastomosis altogether. In this context, the DCAA approach, which omits primary sutured anastomosis, was associated in our study with a markedly lower incidence of AL while preserving the technical advantages of taTME.

Evidence from prior comparative studies supports this observation. In a cohort of 223 patients with low rectal cancer, Melka et al. reported significantly higher AL rates following ICAA compared with DCAA [27]. Consistently, in our cohort, no AL occurred in the DCAA group, whereas five events (two grade A, two grade B, and one grade C) were observed in the ICAA group. Anastomotic height of less than 5 cm from the anal verge has been identified as an independent risk factor for AL, increasing leakage risk by more than eightfold in multivariate analyses [28, 29]. Conventional taTME combined with ICAA frequently results in ultra-low anastomoses, which may partly explain its higher leakage rates. The absence of AL in the DCAA group may be attributable to avoidance of immediate suture-line tension, as the delayed anastomosis relies initially on adhesion formation between the colon and anal canal rather than direct sutural stress. In contrast, ICAA exposes the anastomosis to immediate traction forces generated by pelvic floor contraction, thereby increasing mechanical tension at the suture line [30].

La Raja et al. conducted a systematic review comparing DCAA and ICAA with respect to postoperative complications and pelvic infections [31]. No significant difference was observed in Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III complications (13% vs. 21%; OR 1.17; 95% CI 0.38–3.62; P = 0.78; I2 = 20%). Similarly, in our study, the incidence of major complications did not differ significantly between groups (P > 0.05). Nevertheless, although statistically nonsignificant, the relatively high proportion of ICAA patients experiencing Clavien–Dindo grade I complications following stoma reversal (45.45%) warrants clinical attention. From a technical standpoint, excision of the exteriorized colon during the second-stage procedure in DCAA is generally less complex than stoma reversal following ICAA, which involves bowel mobilization, resection, and reconstruction. Moreover, while DCAA effectively eliminates AL and stoma-related morbidity, complications related to the exteriorized colon, such as bleeding or local infection, require careful surveillance and management. The single case of anal stenosis observed in the DCAA group likely reflects circumferential scar formation during delayed anastomotic healing rather than the duration of exteriorization itself, underscoring the importance of meticulous technique during the second-stage anastomosis (Table 4).

Notably, one patient in the DCAA group developed ischemic necrosis of the exteriorized colonic segment, representing a complication inherently related to the pull-through technique. In this case, spontaneous sloughing of the necrotic colon occurred shortly after discharge, which precluded completion of the planned second-stage anastomosis and resulted in missing data for second-stage surgical outcomes. To maintain consistency in the analysis of staged surgical indicators, this patient was therefore not included in the final comparative analysis of second-stage outcomes. From a clinical standpoint, this complication was managed conservatively. Endoscopic evaluation demonstrated spontaneous adhesion between the proximal colon and the anal canal mucosa, without evidence of active bleeding or local infection. Accordingly, no additional surgical intervention was required, and the patient was treated with temporary bowel rest, fluid support, and close observation. Importantly, subsequent experience outside the study period identified additional cases of partial ischemic necrosis with spontaneous sloughing, most of which could be managed without major reoperation when the residual colon was minimal and local healing was satisfactory.

Although ischemic necrosis of the exteriorized colon is clearly associated with the DCAA technique, it should not be regarded as an inevitable consequence. The relatively low incidence observed suggests that this complication likely reflects a confluence of patient-specific and technical factors rather than a direct causal effect of the procedure itself. Potential contributors include excessive anal sphincter tone leading to compression of the exteriorized segment, increased colonic wall thickness impairing microcirculation, and compromised vascular supply in cases requiring extensive mobilization or left colic artery ligation. Based on these observations, refinements in patient selection, intraoperative vascular assessment, and postoperative management have been implemented to mitigate this risk.

Surgical trauma-induced inflammatory stress has been linked to increased risks of local recurrence and distant metastasis, highlighting the importance of minimally invasive and tissue-sparing approaches [32]. A randomized trial by Chen et al. demonstrated comparable postoperative inflammatory responses between taTME and laparoscopic TME [33]. In our study, leukocyte counts and neutrophil ratios did not differ significantly between groups on postoperative days 1, 3, or 7. However, CRP levels were significantly higher in the DCAA group on postoperative days 1 (P < 0.001) and 3 (P = 0.034). This elevation likely reflects localized inflammatory activation related to colon exteriorization. Importantly, multiple observations argue against pathological significance. CRP levels returned to baseline by postoperative day 7 (P = 0.185), without concomitant elevations in leukocyte or neutrophil counts, and this transient increase was not associated with a higher incidence of anastomotic leakage or major postoperative complications. Taken together, these findings suggest that exteriorization in DCAA induces a transient, self-limited acute-phase response without adverse clinical consequences.

Functional outcomes are central to evaluating sphincter-preserving strategies. Koedam et al. reported that approximately 80% of patients undergoing taTME experienced severe LARS within 1 month, decreasing to 33% by 6 months [34]. In our cohort, the incidence of severe LARS declined from 62.5% (DCAA) and 51.5% (ICAA) at 3 months to 46.9% and 36.4%, respectively, at 12 months. Although DCAA may theoretically impair early anorectal function by precluding J-pouch formation and prolonging anal canal dilation, Marti et al. demonstrated comparable long-term functional outcomes between DCAA and J-pouch reconstruction [35]. Similarly, Biondo et al. reported no significant difference in Wexner scores at 1 year, and Jarry et al. observed that 73% of DCAA patients achieved Wexner scores below 10 by 2 years [30, 36]. Collectively, these findings suggest that careful nerve preservation and surgical technique may play a more decisive role in functional recovery than the anastomotic configuration itself [37].

The comparable functional outcomes observed in our study, despite colon exteriorization in DCAA, may be explained by effective preservation of pelvic autonomic nerves. The taTME approach, combined with a first-stage procedure free from immediate anastomotic tension, likely facilitated optimal nerve sparing. Furthermore, DCAA avoids the potential “second hit” to anorectal function associated with stoma reversal surgery. Together, these factors may account for the preserved functional outcomes observed with DCAA.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the non-randomized design introduces potential selection bias, as surgical strategy was determined by surgeon preference rather than random allocation. Although patients were prospectively enrolled, surgeons may have preferentially selected DCAA for anatomically challenging or higher-risk cases. If present, such bias would have disadvantaged the DCAA group, rendering the absence of AL in this cohort particularly notable. Second, while the sample size was sufficient to detect differences in the primary outcomes (AL), it may have limited power for certain secondary outcomes. Third, a baseline imbalance in BMI was observed, with higher BMI in the DCAA group, suggesting preferential use of this strategy in patients with greater visceral obesity. This imbalance would be expected to increase, rather than decrease, complication risk in the DCAA group, further strengthening the observed findings. Finally, although validated patient-reported instruments were used, the absence of preoperative Wexner and LARS scores limits adjustment for baseline functional status.

In summary, taTME combined with delayed coloanal anastomosis was associated with a lower incidence of anastomotic leakage and avoidance of stoma-related morbidity compared with immediate anastomosis and diverting ileostomy, while achieving comparable functional outcomes. This strategy appears to represent a safe and feasible alternative for high-risk patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery for low rectal cancer.
