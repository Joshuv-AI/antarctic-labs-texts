Toward Authentic Resources Among Mothers: Impact of Cyberchondria on Maternal Self‐Efficacy and Breastfeeding Competency
Authors: Shaimaa Mohamed Amin, Nagwa Ibrahim Mohamed Hamad, Mahmoud Ahmed Elsheikh, Doaa Kadry Mahmoud Abd El‐Razek, Esraa Mohammed Abd El Aziz Al Hanbaly, Hibah Abdulrahim Bahri, Sally Mohammed Farghaly Abdelaliem, Heba Emad El‐Gazar, Mohamed Ali Zoromba, Marwa Mohamed Ahmed Ouda, Rodaina Ahmed Mokbel, Mohamed Hussein Ramadan Atta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe postpartum period is a critical stage where maternal confidence and self‐efficacy significantly influence breastfeeding success and overall adjustment. Cyberchondria refers to the repetitive searching for health‐related information online, which can lead mothers to doubt their caregiving abilities, lower their confidence, and negatively impact their perceived competence in breastfeeding and infant care. This study explored the relationship between cyberchondria, maternal self‐efficacy, and breastfeeding perceived competency among postpartum mothers. A cross‐sectional survey was conducted with 300 mothers within 2 months of childbirth, recruited through a two‐stage random sampling method from primary health care facilities in Zagazig district, Egypt. Participants completed validated Arabic versions of the Cyberchondria Scale (CS), the Perceived Maternal Parenting Self‐Efficacy Scale, and the breastfeeding competency scale (BCS). Findings revealed that higher cyberchondria levels were linked with lower maternal self‐efficacy and diminished breastfeeding competency. Maternal self‐efficacy demonstrated a significant positive association with breastfeeding perceived competency and partially mediated the relationship between cyberchondria and breastfeeding competency. These results suggest that online health‐seeking behaviors may undermine maternal confidence and skills, highlighting the importance of incorporating digital health literacy interventions into postpartum care. Supporting mothers in navigating online health information effectively may enhance self‐efficacy and improve breastfeeding outcomes.Keywords: breastfeeding, cyberchondria, internet, mothers, self‐concept, self‐efficacy

Introduction

Cyberchondria refers to the repetitive online search for health information. The term was created by the UK media in the mid‐1990s, derived from a combination of “cyber” and “hypochondriasis” (Loos 2013). It is a distinctive construct linked to health anxiety, uncertainty intolerance, and obsessive‐compulsive symptoms in addition to anxiety sensitivity (Schenkel et al. 2021). Among breastfeeding mothers, cyberchondria can show up in numerous ways, including worries about milk supply, concerns about their infant's health, and uncertainties about health ailments like mastitis and thrush (Güleç Şatır and Bakır). The constant search for information may raise maternal stress regarding health, since the diversity of online sources might lead to misinterpretation of information, causing needless worry about what is alarming or normal (Gulec Satir and Bakir 2024; Tiryaki et al. 2024).

A main adverse effect of cyberchondria is heightened anxiety due to dwelling on perceived issues and adverse effects mothers read about, rather than recognizing their achievements (Doherty‐Torstrick et al. 2016; Schenkel et al. 2021). This negative mindset can initiate a cycle of anxiety, as the mother's confidence is further eroded by increasing preoccupation with potential issues (Uslu Şahan and Purtul 2023). Cyberchondria can also impair health literacy by inducing uncertainty, thereby obstructing a mother's capacity to accurately perceive and utilize health information pertinent to breastfeeding (Sansakorn et al. 2024).

The literature elucidates that breastfeeding outcomes are significantly mediated by psychological determinants, particularly maternal self‐efficacy (Matare et al. 2019; Rocha et al. 2018). Nevertheless, cyberchondria can have a substantial impact on self‐efficacy among breastfeeding mothers, sometimes leading to increased worries and feelings of inadequacy. Given that, a barrage of conflicting information may be encountered by mothers due to online searches, which creates self‐doubt regarding breastfeeding practices and capabilities (Tiryaki et al. 2024).

Breastfeeding Competency is the sum of mothers' knowledge, abilities, confidence, and self‐ in effectively breastfeeding their infants (Wu et al. 2023). The extensive pursuit of health information online might result in both beneficial and detrimental consequences. Access to reliable materials may provide mothers with essential knowledge regarding breastfeeding practices, infant nutrition, and solutions to common breastfeeding problems (Amin et al. 2024; Newby et al. 2015). Conversely, mothers frequently encounter a diverse array of information, encompassing deceptive and contradictory instructions that may result in self‐doubt over their breastfeeding approach (Al‐Thubaity et al. 2023). Mothers may have uncertainty over optimal practices, resulting in inconsistent breastfeeding patterns, potentially compromising the mother‐infant bond due to feeding challenges (Modak et al. 2023). As a consequence, women's breastfeeding competence may be substantially affected by cyberchondria, impairing their ability to breastfeed efficiently and address typical difficulties (Tiryaki et al. 2024).

Dependence on internet information may diminish the pursuit of in‐person assistance from healthcare professionals and peer networks (Abu Khait et al. 2022). When mothers seek answers online about breastfeeding practices, they may forgo conversations with specialists who might provide personalized guidance and tailored support (Moon and Woo 2021; Regan and Brown 2019). This isolation can intensify feelings of incompetence and impede the formation of a supporting network, which is essential for nurturing self‐efficacy (Leeming et al. 2022). To mitigate these impacts, breastfeeding mothers must adopt an equitable approach regarding knowledge acquisition. Restricting internet inquiries and concentrating on reliable sources, besides interacting with healthcare experts and support organizations, may boost mothers' self‐assurance in their capabilities and alleviate anxiety (Regan and Brown 2019).

Despite the expanding research on maternal mental health and breastfeeding (Dhaurali et al. 2023; Nagel et al. 2022), there is a noticeable dearth of studies that particularly address cyberchondria and its impact on breastfeeding mothers. Hence, most research has concentrated on general health anxiety. However, the particular impacts of online health information seeking‐ especially with breastfeeding‐ are still underexplored (Ali et al. 2024; Billings et al. 2024). While maternal self‐efficacy is acknowledged as an essential component in effective practices, the correlation between these variables and cyberchondria has not been adequately investigated. Therefore, these gaps underscore the necessity for a study examining the impact of cyberchondria on maternal self‐efficacy and breastfeeding proficiency, together with the underlying mechanisms of these associations (Amin et al. 2024). Although this study is conducted among Egyptian mothers, there is no evidence that the Egyptian population is uniquely or disproportionately affected by cyberchondria compared with other contexts. Rather, Egypt offers a relevant setting because of the rapid digital transformation and increasing reliance on the internet as a primary source of health information among postpartum women (Abdelsalam et al. 2021; Elhady et al. 2021). Internet penetration and smartphone use have expanded widely across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, including Egypt, which creates both opportunities for health promotion and risks related to misinformation and online health anxiety (Pew Research Center 2009). Consequently, findings from Egyptian mothers may reflect challenges that are globally relevant to maternal and child health, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries where structured postpartum digital health literacy programs remain limited.

Comprehending these dynamics can enlighten healthcare providers about the difficulties mothers experience in the digital era. Also, recognizing the correlation between cyberchondria and breastfeeding might facilitate the creation of effective health communication strategies to empower mothers while eliminating misinformation and worry (Tiryaki et al. 2024). Eventually, the current study may serve as a basis for further research on the effects of internet‐based medical searching in diverse maternal and child health areas.

Subjects and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study utilized a cross‐sectional descriptive design, adhering to the STROBE checklist guidelines for cross‐sectional research. It was conducted in primary health care (PHC) facilities located in the Zagazig district of Egypt.

Sample Size and Study Population

Participants eligible for this study included mothers aged 18 years and older who had given birth within the past 2 months and were currently breastfeeding. To ensure relevant experience with online health‐seeking behavior, participants were required to have conducted at least one internet search related to health since childbirth. Additionally, all participants needed to provide informed consent and demonstrate a willingness to engage in the study. Exclusion criteria comprised mothers diagnosed with significant psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairments, or any condition that could hinder their ability to participate, as these factors could introduce biases related to mental health and cyberchondria. Mothers who experienced severe postpartum complications requiring hospitalization beyond routine postnatal care were also excluded, as their circumstances may differ significantly from typical breastfeeding experiences and maternal self‐efficacy.

The required sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software (Faul et al. 2007) to ensure sufficient statistical power for the primary mediation analysis. The calculation was based on a power of 0.95, a significance level (alpha) of 0.001, and a moderate effect size of 0.15. Based on these parameters, a minimum of 290 participants was required. To account for potential non‐responses or incomplete data, the target sample size was increased to 315 participants.

Recruitment and Sampling

This study utilized a two‐stage random sampling method to ensure a representative sample of mothers. In the first stage, two urban and two rural PHC facilities were randomly selected from a total of 43 facilities in the Zagazig district. This step was intended to capture a diverse range of settings, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

In the second stage, a systematic random sampling approach was employed within the selected facilities. Specifically, every fifth mother visiting the clinic during the recruitment period was invited to participate in the study. Instruments

Demographic Form

The demographic form utilized in this study collected essential information regarding participants' characteristics to facilitate a comprehensive analysis of the sample. It included questions on age, level of education, marital status, place of residence, type of family, employment status, family income, and current child's age. Additionally, to assess health‐related internet use, participants were asked about their current frequency of internet use, specifically during pregnancy for seeking health information (e.g., “How often do you use the internet to search for information related to your pregnancy or your baby's health?”). This item was designed to capture real‐time engagement with online health resources rather than retrospective recall, ensuring greater accuracy and relevance to the study objectives.

Perceived Maternal Parenting Self‐Efficacy (PMP SE)

The Scale of Perceived Self‐Efficacy of Maternal Parenting, developed by Tristão et al. (2015), evaluates mothers' confidence in their ability to effectively manage parenting tasks. This scale comprises 20 items divided into four dimensions: Taking Care (4 items), Eliciting Behavior (7 items), Reading Behavior (6 items), and Situational Beliefs (3 items). Participants rate each item on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5). A higher total score reflects greater confidence in a mother's capacity to perform parenting tasks effectively. According to Tristão et al. (2015), the scale demonstrates strong reliability, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.87, indicating excellent internal consistency. Validity was confirmed through exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which showed that the scale accounted for 62.3% of the total variance. Furthermore, CFA produced satisfactory fit indices: CFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.90, and RMSEA = 0.06, thereby validating the scale's construct. In our study, we translated the scale into Arabic to enhance its cultural relevance and clarity for participants. A thorough validation process was implemented for the translated instrument. Reliability assessment using Cronbach's alpha yielded a value of 0.91, indicating high internal consistency. To further validate the Arabic version, we conducted both EFA and CFA. The EFA, utilizing varimax rotation, revealed initial factor loadings between 0.530 and 0.925 before rotation, improving to loadings between 0.670 and 0.980 afterward, with all exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.35. The EFA results indicated that the translated scale accounted for 74.2% of the total variance, confirming a robust factor structure. Subsequently, the CFA evaluated the fit of the translated model, yielding favorable fit indices: CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.95, and RMSEA = 0.03.

Breastfeeding Competency Scale (BCS)

The BCS, developed by Wu et al. (2021), is a comprehensive tool designed to assess breastfeeding competency among women. It comprises 38 items distributed across four dimensions: Breastfeeding Knowledge (15 items), Self‐concept and Psychology (11 items), Management of Breast Milk (4 items), and Breastfeeding Skills (8 items). Participants respond to each item using a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all corresponding) to 5 (completely corresponding). The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all items, with the mean score derived from dividing the total score by the number of items answered. Higher scores indicate greater breastfeeding competency. Wu et al. (2021) reported strong reliability for the BCS, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient exceeding 0.7, and acceptable split‐half reliability, with Spearman‐Brown and Guttman coefficients also above 0.7. Validity was supported by a four‐factor model confirmed through both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, showing χ² = 898.152 (p > 0.05), χ²/df = 1.363, and RMSEA = 0.039, although some fit indices, such as GFI (0.838) and AGFI (0.817), were slightly below recommended values. In our study, the scale was translated into Arabic to enhance cultural relevance and clarity for participants. A thorough validation process for the translated instrument was conducted. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha, yielding a strong value of 0.92, which indicates high internal consistency. To further validate the Arabic version, we conducted both EFA and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The EFA, using varimax rotation, revealed initial factor loadings ranging from 0.600 to 0.925 before rotation, improving to loadings between 0.700 and 0.980 after rotation, with all loadings surpassing the acceptable threshold of 0.35. The EFA results confirmed that the translated scale accounted for 75.5% of the total variance, indicating a robust factor structure. The subsequent CFA assessed the fit of the translated model, yielding favorable fit indices: CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.95, and RMSEA = 0.03.

Cyberchondria Scale (CS)

The CS, developed by Durak Batıgün et al. (2018), is a measurement tool designed to evaluate the phenomenon of cyberchondria, characterized by obsessive online searches for health‐related information that heighten anxiety about health issues. This scale was selected over other instruments, such as the Cyberchondria Severity Scale (CSS), because its theoretical structure was better aligned with the objectives of this study. Specifically, the CS's subscales capture the dual nature of online health‐seeking, including both “Anxiety‐Increasing Factors” and “Anxiety‐Reducing Factors.” This nuance was crucial for exploring how online searching might both increase anxiety and, as our findings suggest, potentially reinforce confidence, rather than focusing solely on the pathological severity of the behavior. This scale comprises 27 items distributed across five domains: Anxiety‐Increasing Factors (6 items), Compulsion/Hypochondria (6 items), Anxiety‐Reducing Factors (5 items), Physician‐Patient Interaction (4 items), and Non‐Functional Internet Usage (6 items).

In our study, we translated the scale into Arabic to enhance cultural relevance and clarity for participants. A comprehensive validation process for the translated instrument was conducted. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha, resulting in a strong value of 0.90, indicating high internal consistency. To validate the Arabic version further, we conducted both EFA and CFA. The EFA, utilizing varimax rotation, revealed initial factor loadings ranging from 0.650 to 0.890 before rotation, which improved to loadings between 0.720 and 0.950 after rotation, with all loadings exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.35. The EFA results confirmed that the translated scale explained 74.2% of the total variance, demonstrating a robust factor structure. The subsequent CFA assessed the fit of the translated model, yielding favorable fit indices: CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.93, and RMSEA = 0.04.

Study Procedures

Tool Preparation and Pilot Study

The research instruments, including the PMP SE, BCS, and CS, were meticulously translated into Arabic by bilingual experts who are proficient in both English and Arabic. Throughout the translation process, we prioritized both accuracy and cultural relevance. To validate these translations, we conducted a rigorous back‐translation into English to ensure linguistic equivalence and to identify any discrepancies. Following this, we assessed face validity for each instrument by having expert panels review the translated tools to confirm that they accurately represented the intended constructs within the Arabic context.

We assessed face validity for each instrument by having expert panels review the translated tools to confirm that they accurately represented the intended constructs within the Arabic context. Reliability was assessed using statistical methods, including Cronbach's alpha, to ensure internal consistency across the scales. A pilot study with 30 participants was conducted to further evaluate the clarity, relevance, and reliability of the translated instruments. The results of the pilot study indicated that no further modifications were needed for the instruments, confirming their suitability for the target population.

Data Collection

The data collection process began with a comprehensive orientation session conducted by trained researchers. Participants received a detailed explanation of the study's purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of their responses to promote trust and transparency. Any questions or concerns raised were addressed promptly to ensure clarity and comfort. Although a convenience sampling method was used, two healthcare sites were randomly selected, and within each site, participants were approached using a systematic and structured method during their scheduled childhood vaccination visits. This approach allowed recruitment to align with participants' existing appointments, enhancing convenience and response rates. All data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews conducted by trained researchers, rather than self‐administered questionnaires. This ensured a consistent understanding of the questions, minimized missing data, and accommodated variations in literacy among participants.

Data collection occurred between November and December 2024. Interviews were held in the clinic's waiting area, a familiar and supportive environment. Each interview lasted approximately 30–35 min and was conducted 3 days a week between 9:00 a.m. and 2:00 p.m., allowing for an organized and participant‐centered data collection schedule. Written and oral informed consent was obtained from each participant before their involvement.

Ethical Considerations

The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Nursing, Zagazig University, Egypt (Ref. No: Zu. REC#:0202), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with informed consent obtained from all participants following clear communication of their rights, including voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the freedom to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize the study variables. Means and standard deviations (M ± SD) were reported for continuous variables, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. To assess internal consistency, Cronbach's alpha coefficient (α) was used to evaluate the reliability of the scales. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine relationships between BFC, cyberchondria, and PMP S‐E, including their domains and subdomains.

To further investigate these relationships, mediation analysis was conducted to assess direct, indirect, and total effects. Mediation was analyzed using path analysis, estimating beta coefficients (β), standard errors (SE), t values, and p values for each path. Indirect effects were assessed primarily using bootstrap methods with 5000 resamples to generate 95% confidence intervals. The Sobel test was also performed for comparison.

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 27, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed).

The mediation analysis in Table 3 and Figure 2 confirms that cyberchondria may play a role in mediating the relationship between BFC and PMP S‐E. Discussion

This study aimed to examine the mediating role of cyberchondria in the relationship between breastfeeding competency and postpartum maternal self‐efficacy. Unexpectedly, our findings indicate no direct relationship between breastfeeding competency and postpartum maternal self‐efficacy. One possible explanation for this is that maternal self‐efficacy is a multifaceted construct influenced not only by breastfeeding skills but also by social support, emotional well‐being, and personal resilience (Shang et al. 2022). Even if a mother perceives herself as competent in breastfeeding, factors such as stress, sleep deprivation, or lack of partner and family support may hinder her overall confidence in broader maternal responsibilities (Deater‐Deckard et al. 2021).

This is a vital finding that brings attention to the importance of studying additional factors that may influence postpartum maternal self‐efficacy. In contrast to our results, previous research has shown that breastfeeding knowledge can enhance maternal confidence in managing breastfeeding‐related tasks (Webber et al. 2021). Additionally, breastfeeding training has been shown to improve mothers' self‐efficacy (Blixt et al. 2023). Future research should explore how educational interventions, psychological support, and healthcare guidance can enhance maternal self‐efficacy beyond breastfeeding competency alone.

As expected, our findings indicate that breastfeeding competency is significantly and positively associated with cyberchondria among mothers, suggesting that higher breastfeeding confidence may lead to increased engagement in online health‐seeking behaviors. Possibly, a mother who perceives themselves as competent in breastfeeding is likely to be more proactive in acquiring additional knowledge, searching for new techniques, troubleshooting challenges, and seeking validation of their practices. This aligns with research suggesting that self‐efficacious individuals tend to engage in continuous learning, which, in the digital age, often translates to frequent internet searches for health‐related information (El‐Gazar et al. 2024). Additionally, mothers with greater breastfeeding competency may develop heightened vigilance about infant health and nutrition (Marshall et al. 2022), leading them to consult online sources regularly for reassurance and evidence‐based recommendations. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have highlighted how mothers actively seek to fulfill the responsibilities associated with the motherhood role, have higher cyberchondria levels, and often turn to online health resources (Tiryaki et al. 2025).

Our results also showed that cyberchondria positively influences perceived maternal parenting self‐efficacy, suggesting that increased engagement in online health‐seeking behaviors may enhance maternal confidence in caregiving. This finding can be explained by the accessibility and immediacy of online health information, which provides mothers with a sense of preparedness and knowledge in managing various aspects of infant care (Wolfers 2021). When mothers actively search for health‐related information, they gain exposure to expert recommendations, peer experiences, and practical strategies, which can reinforce their belief in their ability to handle parenting challenges effectively (Whitworth et al. 2024). Additionally, the reassurance gained from finding solutions to common concerns online—such as breastfeeding issues, infant sleep patterns, or developmental milestones—may contribute to a greater sense of competence and autonomy in their caregiving role (Rathbone and Prescott 2019).

Finally, our results showed that cyberchondria plays a mediating role in the relationship between breastfeeding competency and perceived maternal parenting self‐efficacy, indicating that the association between breastfeeding competency and maternal self‐efficacy may be influenced by mothers' engagement in online health‐seeking behaviors. This suggests that mothers with higher breastfeeding competency may increasingly rely on online health information, which in turn reinforces their confidence in broader maternal responsibilities. This is an important finding that adds a novel contribution to the literature, extending previous research that has demonstrated an association between maternal self‐efficacy and maternal role competence (Dlamini et al. 2023; Yadollahi et al. 2024). We build on this by highlighting the role of cyberchondria as a key mechanism linking breastfeeding competency to maternal self‐efficacy, suggesting that online health‐seeking behaviors may play a role in shaping maternal confidence.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the complex interplay between cyberchondria, breastfeeding competency, and maternal self‐efficacy among primigravida mothers. The findings highlight that while breastfeeding competency alone does not directly influence maternal self‐efficacy, cyberchondria serves as a significant mediating factor. Specifically, higher breastfeeding competency is associated with increased cyberchondria, which in turn significantly enhances maternal self‐efficacy. However, certain aspects of cyberchondria, such as heightened health‐related anxiety, may negatively affect maternal behaviors.

These results underscore the importance of fostering digital health literacy among new mothers to ensure that online health‐seeking behaviors contribute positively to their confidence and decision‐making rather than exacerbating anxiety. Healthcare professionals should focus on guiding mothers toward reliable sources of health information and developing interventions that support informed, balanced internet use. By addressing both the benefits and risks of cyberchondria, maternal self‐efficacy, and breastfeeding competency can be optimized, ultimately improving overall maternal and infant health outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

One of the primary strengths of a robust statistical approach, including an adequate sample size determined through G*Power analysis, strengthened the study's validity and the reliability of its conclusions. Furthermore, adherence to STROBE guidelines enhanced the transparency and replicability of the research process, reinforcing the study's credibility.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied exclusively on self‐report measures. Consequently, the term “competency” reflects the mothers' perception of their skills rather than an objective, observed measure. While maternal perception is a clinically important construct, it may not perfectly correlate with actual proficiency. Furthermore, as with all self‐report data, there is a potential for social desirability bias, where participants may have responded in a way they felt was more socially acceptable, potentially inflating their self‐reported competency and self‐efficacy. The study was also conducted in a single district, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions with different socio‐cultural and healthcare dynamics. Additionally, due to the cross‐sectional design, the causal direction of the relationships in our mediation model cannot be definitively established. Future research employing longitudinal designs and diverse geographic samples could provide deeper insights into these relationships.

Implication

The findings of this study have important implications for clinical practice, professional education, and future research in supporting maternal well‐being in the digital age. To address the challenges of cyberchondria, clinical practitioners—including nurses, physicians, midwives, lactation consultants, doulas, and community health workers—should provide anticipatory guidance to new mothers. This involves assessing online health information–seeking behaviors and offering personalized counseling to direct mothers toward evidence‐based resources, thereby reducing anxiety associated with frequent or anxiety‐driven internet searches. Integrating digital consultations and telehealth services into routine maternal care can further provide timely professional support, enhancing mothers' confidence in self‐management.

Professional education for maternal health clinicians should include digital health literacy training. Curricula can incorporate modules on counseling families to critically appraise online content and manage anxiety stemming from misinformation. Healthcare organizations can support this by providing new parents with curated, reliable online resources during prenatal and postnatal care. Future research should examine the long‐term impact of cyberchondria on maternal and child health and focus on designing and evaluating interventions that enhance digital health literacy, fostering a more informed and resilient maternal population.
