Undergraduate dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction following a step-by-step course in microscopic dentistry
Authors: Zimeng Li, Guang Yang, Hailin Zhang, Cui Huang, Shanshan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite growing use of dental operating microscopes, structured undergraduate training is limited and often lacks hands-on integration. This study evaluates undergraduate dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction following a step-by-step course in microscopic dentistry.MethodsThis single-group quasi-experimental study included 103 second-year dental undergraduates from Wuhan University, completing a 4-session step-by-step microscopic dentistry course. Two researcher-developed questionnaires, which were pilot-tested and contained Likert-scale and binary-response items, were administered to participants at the following time: (1) a pre-course questionnaire assessing core knowledge areas and attitudes toward microscopic dentistry, (2) a post-course questionnaire evaluating changes in knowledge and attitudes as well as satisfaction with this course. Statistical analysis included paired t-tests for knowledge and attitude scales, McNemar’s tests for binary responses, and one-sample t-tests for satisfaction data (p < 0.05).ResultsAmong 103 enrolled students, response rates were 99.0% (102/103) for the pre-course assessment and 95.1% (98/103) for the post-course assessment, with 88 matched pairs retained for final analysis. The knowledge assessment scores demonstrated a statistically significant improvement following the course intervention (2.90 ± 0.83 to 4.20 ± 0.69, p < 0.001). Regarding participants’ attitudes, perceived dental operating microscope necessity increased (4.53 ± 0.61 to 4.82 ± 0.42, p < 0.001), with clinical benefit recognition rising from 89.7 to 97.7% (p = 0.021) and treatment time misconceptions declining from 44.8 to 21.8% (p = 0.001). These improvements suggest the course effectively addressed both conceptual and perceptual gaps. Course satisfaction was high (97.7% overall), with 100% agreement on content relevance and teaching method effectiveness.ConclusionsThe course appears to be an effective tool for enhancing dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction. Further controlled studies are needed to validate its long-term impact.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07947-5.

Introduction

The dental operating microscope (DOM) provides magnified visualization and enhanced illumination, making it essential for microscopic dentistry applications in endodontics, periodontics, and prosthodontics [1–4]. Like magnification technologies in other medical fields, DOM enhances precision, improves visual clarity, increases efficiency, and reduces patient discomfort [5–7]. Recognizing its value, the American Association of Endodontists (AAE) has reinforced microscopic dentistry’s importance [8], with recent expert consensus has further standardized dental applications [9, 10]. Despite these benefits, many dentists still rarely use DOM due to insufficient training and misconceptions about their clinical utility [11, 12].

Although training enhances proficiency and attitudes, existing microscopic dentistry programs frequently overlook the discipline’s dual requirements of theoretical mastery and precise motor skills [13, 14]. Traditional approaches often rely on fragmented task training such as tooth preparations or lecture-based instruction which often lack systematic progression, resulting in unclear learning objectives and low student engagement [15–17]. Unlike conventional methods that mismatch microscopic dentistry’s learning curve, structured progressive training is crucial for skill acquisition.

Step-by-step (SBS) teaching, a progressive instructional method, has demonstrated significant effectiveness in medical and dental education, particularly in skill-based training such as surgical simulations and restorative procedures [18, 19]. Rooted in educational theories like cognitive load theory and mastery learning, this approach systematically breaks down complex tasks into sequential, manageable steps, enabling learners to develop competency incrementally [20]. By structuring training into distinct phases ranging from basic instrument handling to advanced microscopic operative skills, SBS aligns perfectly with the multi-dimensional demands of microscopic dentistry training, ensuring students solidify foundational skills before advancing to complex techniques. Studies indicate that this method reduces cognitive overload, minimizes errors, and enhances retention by allowing learners to focus on one skill component at a time [21]. Additionally, the gradual progression fosters learner confidence and self-efficacy, as each mastered step serves as a building block for subsequent challenges [22]. While SBS has been validated in both complex clinical and dental procedures, its application in microscopic dentistry training remains unexplored.

Knowledge, attitude, and satisfaction constitute essential dimensions for evaluating clinical training effectiveness, enabling targeted improvements in teaching delivery [23, 24]. Knowledge enhances technical comprehension, attitude influences clinical adoption, and satisfaction reinforces skill retention, all of which collectively optimize the effectiveness of skill-based training [25, 26]. This evaluation framework may be applicable to microscopic dentistry training, where mastering complex techniques requires not only a thorough understanding of the technology but also a positive behavioral disposition and iterative skill refinement [27, 28].

This study aims to evaluate undergraduate dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction following a SBS course in microscopic dentistry, with the goal of developing more effective instructional strategies for this essential technology. We hypothesized that the SBS course would effectively enhance students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction in microscopic dentistry.

Methods

Study design & study participants

This single-group pretest-posttest design was conducted at the School of Stomatology, Wuhan University between March and April 2022. The study included all 103 second-year undergraduate dental students enrolled in the course of Microscopic Dental Practice, who had met the inclusion criteria by completing their first-year medical courses. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study began, with no exclusions. None of the students had prior formal microscopic dentistry training. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (No. 2021B30; Date: July 12th, 2021).

Implementation of teaching methods

The course of Microscopic Dental Practice is designed for second-year undergraduate students in the Wuhan University (WHU) dentistry program. The course comprises 4 sessions completed within a 2-month period. Each two-hour session is divided into three parts: theoretical lecture (20 min), teacher’s demonstration (20 min), and students’ practice (80 min). This course aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of microscopic dentistry, cultivate students’ recognition of its clinical necessity, develop hand-eye coordination and technical proficiency in microscopic operative skills. The course instructors are faculty members at Wuhan University School of Stomatology who also serve as clinical practitioners, all possessing over 10 years of teaching experience.

Microscopic operative skills require proficient hand-eye coordination and fine motor control, which can be trained by starting with basic planar exercises and progressively advancing to more complex models with restricted operative fields [14]. This learning curve aligns with the logic of SBS training in skill acquisition, which breaks down complex techniques into smaller, manageable units for skill development [22]. Therefore, this course implemented the SBS teaching approach for the course especially in students’ practice section (Fig. 1). The practical training consisted of four skill modules arranged from simple to complex: (1) horizontal plane operations (line drawing), (2) vertical plane operations (diamond dot placement), (3) combined horizontal-vertical operations (endodontic access opening), and (4) integrated triplanar operations with rotational movements (suturing). Following each session, students’ practical performance was evaluated through practice outcomes, enabling targeted identification and remediation of learning gaps.

Data collection

Two researcher-developed questionnaires containing both 5-point Likert scales (0 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) and binary (yes = 1, no = 0) items were administered to all 103 second-year dental students (Table 1). The knowledge and attitude items were adapted from a Knowledge-Attitude-Practice survey [29]. To establish content validity and ensure item clarity, the questionnaires were pilot-tested with five randomly selected third-year undergraduate dental students. The pre-course questionnaire was distributed one week before the course initiation, assessing baseline knowledge (1 Likert-type single item) and attitudes (1 Likert-type single item and 2 binary items) toward microscopic dentistry (see Additional File 1). Following course completion, students completed a post-course questionnaire comprising two sections: (1) evaluation of knowledge and attitude changes (identical to the pre-course questionnaire), and (2) assessment of satisfaction with course content, teaching methodology, and overall experience using six Likert-scale items (α = 0.808). The complete post-course questionnaire is provided in Additional File 2. Both questionnaires were distributed and managed through the SurveyStar (Changsha, Hunan, China), during which demographic information (gender, age, and student ID) was collected, with the student IDs used for paired analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were exported to Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA) and analyzed using SPSS Statistics (version 26, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Paired samples t-tests analyzed Likert-type items (Q1&2) responses for knowledge and attitude assessments, with results reported as mean ± SD. Binary attitude items (Q3&4) were analyzed using McNemar’s tests and reported as frequencies. Satisfaction items (Q5-Q10) were analyzed using one-sample t-tests against the scale midpoint. The result was considered to be significant if the p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Among 103 enrolled second-year dental students, 102 completed the pre-course questionnaire (99.0% response rate). For the post-course assessments, 98 participants submitted both the knowledge/attitude follow-up questionnaire and the satisfaction questionnaire (95.1% response rate). After matching pre- and post-course responses using anonymized IDs, 88 complete datasets were retained for all analyses (85.4% matched dataset completion rate). This study population included 54 females (61.4%) and 34 males (38.6%), with a mean age of 19.47 ± 0.74 years.

Initially, students demonstrated a relatively low level of understanding regarding microscopic dentistry, with an average score of 2.90 ± 0.83. Post-course, there was a significant improvement in knowledge scores, which rose to 4.20 ± 0.69 (group difference = 1.31, 95% CI [1.12, 1.50], p < 0.001).

The mean score for the necessity of using DOM increased from 4.53 ± 0.61 pre-course to 4.82 ± 0.42 post-course (group difference = 0.28, 95% CI [0.15, 0.42], p < 0.001). Additionally, students’ perception of DOM as time-consuming dropped from 44.8 to 21.8% (p = 0.001), with a majority shifting to favorable evaluations after training (Table 2).

Discussion

The hypothesis is accepted as the results showed improvements in theoretical understanding, greater willingness to adopt DOM, and high satisfaction ratings after the SBS course. The aim of this study was to evaluate undergraduate dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction following SBS microscopic dentistry training to develop effective instructional strategies. These findings provides preliminary evidence for the successful implementation of SBS teaching in microscopic dentistry education.

The SBS approach has been demonstrated to enhance comprehensive competency in ultrasound diagnostic techniques through integrated theoretical learning and skill practice [18]. In the present study, we adapted this approach to microscopic dentistry training, with observable improvements in students’ knowledge improvement. As noted in earlier studies, initial exposure to such advanced technologies may cause cognitive overload without gradual preparation [30]. This underscores the value of SBS in prioritizing fundamental knowledge and basic operational skills during early instructional phases.

The progressive instructional design used in this course enhances students’ willingness to use DOM by first introducing fundamental interest-generating tasks before addressing complex clinical procedures [21]. Some studies indicate that although students recognize the advantages of DOM, they do not consider them essential, which leads to low clinical adoption [5]. In our cohort, SBS was associated with high levels of perceived necessity and value of DOM.

Importantly, the proportion of students who believed DOM use unnecessarily prolongs treatment time decreased, suggesting that SBS training helps students develop operative proficiency and understand the potential of DOM to improve clinical efficiency. These results align with existing evidence on the benefits of DOM for precision and workflow optimization, while further demonstrating the effectiveness of SBS in addressing common misconceptions [31, 32]. The early structured microscopic dentistry training effectively promoted positive attitudes and increased clinical adoption potential, aligning with practitioner-reported evidence on the benefits of early-stage microscopic dentistry education [33, 34]. These results strongly support integrating structured SBS courses into standard dental curricula.

The high satisfaction ratings observed in this study reflect the successful integration of the SBS teaching method in microscopic dentistry training. Participants agreed that the course effectively expanded their knowledge and enhanced practical skills. This achievement was made possible by the method’s unique progressive structure where students first master basic horizontal movements, then advance to vertical movements, next integrate both directions, and finally incorporate rotational maneuvers. This graduated progression follows microscopic operations’ natural difficulty gradient, effectively bridging the theory-practice gap [35]. The high satisfaction ratings regarding course content and its applicability may result from the SBS method’s effectiveness in developing the essential microscopic operative skills of hand-eye coordination and fine motor control. Previous studies have confirmed the SBS approach works well for developing these skills in dental training [22, 36]. The high ratings for teaching methodology effectiveness and overall course satisfaction highlight the success of the SBS approach in microscopic dentistry training. Consistent with prior research on structured dental training, this method progressively builds skills from basic to advanced levels, enabling learners to clearly track their improvement while reinforcing motivation and confidence [21, 22]. These findings highlight the importance of early implementation of this course.

Although the SBS course proved effective in enhancing dental students’ knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction, there are still some limitations in this study. These findings are based on short-term observations, and the single-group pretest-posttest design’s lack of a control group limits our ability to account for external influences (e.g., maturation effects, concurrent learning, or placebo effects), potentially affecting the internal validity of the results. Additionally, incorporating objective performance metrics such as OSCE scores and practical examination results would provide a more comprehensive evaluation.

Conclusion

This step-by-step microscopic dentistry course demonstrated significant positive effects on second-year dental students’ knowledge acquisition, attitude formation, and course satisfaction. The step-by-step approach decomposes complex procedures into progressively structured learning units while following the natural difficulty gradient of microscopic operations, and shows considerable potential for application in dental microscopic skill training. Based on these findings, we recommend integrating step-by-step microscopic dentistry training into standard dental curricula.
