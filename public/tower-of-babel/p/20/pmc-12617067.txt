Discovery of Genetically Distinct, Sympatric Coral Lineages With Temporal but Not Gametic Reproductive Isolation
Authors: Valérie F. Chamberland, Matías Gómez‐Corrales, Kristen L. Marhaver, Pim Bongaerts, Kelly R. W. Latijnhouwers, Diana Carolina Vergara‐Flórez, Skylar Snowden, Juan A. Sánchez, Mark J. A. Vermeij
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCoral species abundance and biodiversity estimates are typically based on colony macromorphology. However, such measurements often underestimate the true diversity within coral communities because morphology does not necessarily reflect behavioral or genetic divergence. We previously reported on the unusual spawning behavior of the brain coral Diploria labyrinthiformis (Linnaeus, 1758) in Curaçao, Southern Caribbean, where this species spawns in both spring and autumn. Here, using data collected from 2013 to 2021, we show that in Curaçao, D. labyrinthiformis comprises two behaviorally and genetically distinct lineages, with 93% of colonies spawning exclusively in one season or the other. The two lineages could not be distinguished based on obvious macromorphological differences or depth but represented clearly distinct genetic clusters (F ST = 0.098) based on genome‐wide sequencing. We tested for prezygotic and postzygotic gametic barriers between them by fertilising eggs released in spring 2019 with sperm collected and cryopreserved in autumn 2018. Fertilisation in this unidirectional cross was successful and the resulting larvae developed normally, thus eliminating complete gametic incompatibility or early life postzygotic barriers as explanations for their divergence. Using observations from 19 other localities across the Wider Caribbean Region, we confirmed the co‐occurrence of discrete spring‐ and autumn‐spawning populations across a range of latitudes. Thus, we show that seasonal, temporal reproductive isolation (allochrony), but not gametic reproductive isolation, is a strong barrier to gene flow in sympatric lineages of this critically endangered reef‐building coral. More broadly, our findings underscore the role of allochrony in the creation and maintenance of cryptic coral lineages and the urgency of identifying, quantifying, and conserving this diversity before it is lost.

Introduction

Reproductive isolation in sexually reproducing organisms can occur when the homogenising effects of mating and recombination break down within a population (Bolnick and Fitzpatrick 2007; Nosil 2008; Potkamp and Fransen 2019). Because the geographic barriers to gene flow that are common on land (such as mountains and rivers) are rare in the ocean (González et al. 2018; Potkamp and Fransen 2019), many consider the large number of coral and other sessile animal species found on tropical reefs to be remarkable (Bowen et al. 2013; Reaka‐Kudla 1997; Yasuda et al. 2014). While physical barriers often lead to reproductive isolation and subsequent allopatric speciation, reproductive isolation that develops in the absence of such barriers depends more heavily on factors such as assortative mating or disruptive selection (Coyne and Orr 2004; Rice 1984). These isolating factors are particularly difficult to identify and study in the mixed, fluid medium of the ocean and for species such as corals with extremely long lifespans (Bythell et al. 2018; Devlin‐Durante et al. 2016), overlapping generations (Babcock 1991), and long‐distance larval dispersal (Thompson et al. 2018; Wood et al. 2014).

Coral species diversity has traditionally been inventoried based on diagnostic skeletal characteristics, but modern genetic estimates of species richness often exceed estimates based on variation in overall colony morphology (e.g., Bongaerts et al. 2021; Johnston et al. 2021; Warner et al. 2015). Although phenotypic plasticity, hybridisation, and incomplete lineage sorting can lead to overestimations of species diversity when a single taxon is erroneously split into multiple, total species diversity will be underestimated when cryptic, morphologically indistinguishable species are common. While this diversity remains largely overlooked in both ecological research and conservation, the recent discovery of several cryptic coral species, that is, species that are genetically and reproductively distinct but morphologically homogeneous, (e.g., Bongaerts et al. 2021; Gómez‐Corrales and Prada 2020; Richards et al. 2016; Rosser 2015; Villanueva 2016; Yasuda et al. 2014) points to the likelihood that a much greater coral diversity exists than is currently described. However, the evolutionary processes that result in the formation of cryptic coral species are not yet well studied or understood (Bowen et al. 2013; Fukami et al. 2004; Furukawa et al. 2020; Warner et al. 2015).

Reproductive isolation can arise prezygotically, for example, through the rapid evolution and divergence of gamete recognition proteins (Vacquier and Swanson 2011; Zigler et al. 2005), or postzygotically due to, for example, hybrid inviability (Bierbaum and Bush 1990; Lu and Bernatchez 1998) or reduced fitness (Fishman and Willis 2001; Ouyang et al. 2010). Allochrony, or differences in breeding time among conspecifics, can also lead to reproductive isolation and thus contribute to speciation, particularly in species with short reproductive periods (Taylor and Friesen 2017). In many marine invertebrates, including many free‐spawning corals, the reproductive period can be extremely short, with fertilisation occurring in the minutes following brief mass‐spawning events (Lotterhos and Levitan 2010; Mumby et al. 2024). Mass spawning behaviours are often coordinated in response to environmental cycles of sunlight, moonlight, tides (Kaniewska et al. 2015; Lin et al. 2021; Wolstenholme et al. 2018), and temperature (Crowder et al. 2017; Van Woesik et al. 2006). Environmentally synchronised mass‐fertilisation events also occur in flowering plants (Devaux and Lande 2008; Fox 2003) and migratory fish such as salmon (Hendry and Day 2005). Within populations, allochrony can arise due to random mutations that affect reproductive timing, due to localised differences or changes in environmental cues, or due to genotype‐specific responses to these changes, creating prezygotic barriers to gene flow among previously interbreeding conspecifics (Hendry and Day 2005; Taylor and Friesen 2017; Weis and Kossler 2004). Subsequent assortative mating based on reproductive timing can lead to and/or reinforce genetic divergence and eventual speciation in a population, even in sympatry (i.e., when sharing the same range) or syntopy (i.e., when co‐occurring locally) (de Cara et al. 2008; Taylor and Friesen 2017).

Divergence driven or reinforced by allochrony is well documented in many terrestrial organisms, including insects (e.g., Devries et al. 2008; Marshall and Cooley 2000; Santos et al. 2007), birds (e.g., Taylor et al. 2019) and plants (e.g., de Carvalho et al. 2013; Franklin 2004). While allochrony is not as well studied in marine invertebrates (Taylor and Friesen 2017), several compelling cases of temporal reproductive isolation exist in corals, including in common and ecologically important Acropora (Fukami et al. 2003; Furukawa et al. 2020; Rosser 2015) and Orbicella species (Knowlton et al. 1997; Levitan et al. 2004). Because the gametes of mass‐spawning corals rapidly dilute in moving water and only remain viable for a few hours after release (Marshall and Bolton 2007), the degree of spawning synchrony among coral colonies greatly influences fertilisation success and genetic mixing within a population (Levitan et al. 2004). Small differences in the timing of gamete release, on the scale of min–h, can therefore constitute a prezygotic reproductive barrier among closely related species, even if gametes are compatible when synchronous spawning is induced and colonies are crossed in a laboratory setting (Fukami et al. 2003; Knowlton et al. 1997).

Here, we focus on the critically endangered Caribbean brain coral Diploria labyrinthiformis (Linnaeus, 1758). In a year‐long study in 2013 in Curaçao (Southern Caribbean), we monitored 39 syntopic colonies, revealing that each colony either spawned in spring or autumn (Chamberland et al. 2017). While the observed differences in spawning times of Acropora and Orbicella species correspond to gross morphological differences (e.g., thick versus thin branches in Acropora (Furukawa et al. 2020) and mounding versus columnar growth morphs in Orbicella (Knowlton et al. 1997)), colonies of D. labyrinthiformis that spawned in different seasons showed no such obvious morphological differences nor any apparent ecological clustering. In this study, we continued monitoring the spawning activity of the same colonies from 2014 to 2019 and in 2021. Because consistent differences in spawning behaviour should theoretically lower gene flow between these subpopulations, we used reduced representation genome sequencing to explore potential genetic differences between them. We then sequenced the two nuclear introns PaxC and Calmodulin (CaM) to assess the phylogenetic relationships of the two subpopulations with closely related coral taxa (Schwartz et al. 2012). To determine whether genetic differences between the two subpopulations resulted in genetic incompatibility (e.g., gamete incompatibility, hybrid inviability), we then used cryopreserved sperm to cross gametes of spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies, testing whether colonies belonging to these subpopulations could still fertilise each other and produce viable offspring. We also collected spawning observations for D. labyrinthiformis from 19 other localities in the Wider Caribbean Region (i.e., tropical western Atlantic, Florida and the Gulf of Mexico) to assess whether differences in reproductive timing were present and consistent across the region. Overall, we found that the coral species commonly known as D. labyrinthiformis in fact consists of two sympatric, cryptic genetic lineages of incipient or recent origin which—in at least one location—are able to successfully interbreed when their gametes are crossed experimentally, but which remain reproductively isolated through seasonal allochrony, with one lineage spawning in spring and the other in autumn. Based on our findings, we discuss evolutionary mechanisms that may have led to the divergence of these D. labyrinthiformis lineages and their reproductive timing as well as the implications of cryptic diversity for coral conservation.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Coral Population

For this study, long‐term monitoring, gamete collection, and tissue sampling took place at Double Reef (12.106944°, −68.94833°) on the leeward coast of Curaçao in the Southern Caribbean. In 2013, we individually marked 40 D. labyrinthiformis colonies by fixing a plastic cattle ear tag to the adjacent substrate (Figure 1A). All colonies occurred within a ∼100 × 20 m area parallel to shore and between 4 and 9 m depth. Only colonies with a total surface area greater than 100 cm2 were included to ensure they were sexually mature (Weil and Vargas 2010). We recorded the depth and took several photographs of each colony to assess whether the spring‐ and autumn‐spawning groups could be distinguished based on depth or any evident morphological differences (e.g., colony shape, colour, structural arrangement of valleys and grooves), though we did not quantify potential micro‐morphological differences using for example, electron microscopy. In R Studio (R Core Team 2025), we visualised the depth ranges of spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies using a box plot generated with the ggplot2 package. Differences between the two groups were assessed with a non‐parametric Kruskal‐Wallis test, conducted using the dplyr package.

Spawning Records

In 2013 we monitored the spawning activity (Figure 1B) of all 40 D. labyrinthiformis colonies with multiple observers on SCUBA during this species' known spawning window (Chamberland et al. 2017). Observations were made between April and October during the hour preceding sunset, daily from 9 to 14 days after the full moon (AFM). During each dive, four pairs of divers each monitored a subset of 10 colonies located in a small area of reef (∼500 m2), roving from one colony to another to ensure that no spawning activity was missed. Spawning was recorded per colony as a ‘yes’ or ‘no’ for each day of monitoring. In 2013, none of the colonies that spawned in spring (May to early July) spawned in autumn (end of July to September), and vice versa (Chamberland et al. 2017). To verify if these differences in reproductive timing of individual colonies were consistent for longer time periods, we conducted 37 additional surveys between June 2014 and August 2021 following the same approach used in 2013. One colony was excluded from the final dataset because it died during the study.

To determine whether spring‐ and autumn‐spawning subpopulations also occurred in locations other than Curaçao, we compiled all available fecundity and spawning records for D. labyrinthiformis , consisting of histological analyses and field‐ and aquarium‐based observations made between 1997 and 2024, by members of 42 different organisations from 20 locations across the Wider Caribbean Region (i.e., tropical western Atlantic, Florida and the Gulf of Mexico).

Genomic Analyses

Sample Collections

To quantify the genetic divergence between spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies, coral sperm and tissue samples were collected between 2013 and 2019 and subjected to single gene and genome‐wide sequencing analyses (Table S1). We collected gametes (egg‐sperm bundles) released by individual D. labyrinthiformis colonies on multiple days in spring 2013 and autumn 2015 following methods previously described in, for example, Chamberland et al. (2017) and Vermeij (2006). Details are available in Extended Materials and Methods (Supporting Information), but briefly, spawning colonies were tented with weighted, cone‐shaped plastic tarp nets with an inverted plastic funnel attached to a 50‐mL polypropylene conical tube (BD Falcon) in which buoyant egg‐sperm bundles accumulated. Sample tubes were transported to the CARMABI Research Station within 60 min after collection, where they were gently agitated so that egg‐sperm bundles broke apart. To obtain coral DNA free of contaminating DNA from endosymbiotic Symbiodiniaceae, 0.5 mL aliquots of sperm‐seawater solution (n = 3 per colony) were preserved by adding 1 mL of ethanol (96%) and then stored at −20°C. Sperm samples were collected from 11 of the 39 marked colonies and from 12 additional unmarked colonies that spawned during collection dives (Table S1). We incorporated these additional colonies into our genetic analyses to increase sample size in spawning groups from both seasons, but these colonies were not included in the spawning behaviour data set since they were not marked or monitored consistently between 2013 and 2021. Because a limited number of colonies spawned during autumn gamete collection dives (spring; n = 17, autumn; n = 6, Table S1), we further expanded our sampling effort to include holobiont tissue samples (∼1 cm2) of the 39 surviving, marked colonies Samples were collected using a hammer and chisel, preserved in ethanol (96%) or in DMSO‐EDTA buffer, and stored at −20°C for later genetic analyses. An initial sequencing effort in 2017, using both sperm and tissue samples, yielded low coverage and only hundreds of SNPs. A second tissue collection and sequencing effort was therefore conducted in 2019, and both datasets were included in downstream analyses to maximise SNP recovery. In total, sampling efforts across years yielded sperm and/or tissue samples from 51 colonies (Table S1).

DNA Extractions

We performed DNA extractions from sperm and tissue using the modified cetrimonium bromide (CTAB) protocol (Coffroth et al. 1992) with a PureLink RNase A incubation (10 mg mL−1, Invitrogen) to remove RNA residues. Extractions were successful for 46 of the 51 total sampled colonies (Table S1). DNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo‐Fisher), and DNA quality and purity were assessed through agarose gel electrophoresis (0.7% w/v). All DNA extractions were conducted at the BIOMMAR lab, after which samples were outsourced to SNPsaurus for library preparation and sequencing.

Genome‐Wide Sequencing

To assess population structure between spring‐ and autumn‐spawning subpopulations, NextRAD genotyping‐by‐sequencing libraries were constructed by SNPsaurus (Eugene, OR) from genomic DNA following the manufacturer's protocol (Russello et al. 2015). First, genomic DNA was fragmented with Nextera reagent (Illumina), which also ligates short adapter sequences to the ends of the fragments. The Nextera reaction was scaled for fragmenting 7 ng of genomic DNA, although 10.5 ng of genomic DNA was used for input to compensate for the amount of degraded DNA in the samples and to increase fragment sizes. Fragmented DNA was then amplified for 26 cycles at 73°C, with one of the primers matching the adapter and extending 9 nucleotides into the genomic DNA with the selective sequence GTGTAGAGG. Thus, only fragments starting with a sequence that can be hybridised by the selective sequence of the primer were efficiently amplified. The nextRAD libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer with two partial lanes of 150 bp reads, with an average coverage depth of 20× (University of Oregon). The genotyping analysis used custom scripts (SNPsaurus) that trimmed the reads using bbduk (BBMap tools (Bushnell 2014)) with the following parameters: bbmap/bbduk.sh in = $file out = $outfile ktrim = r k = 17 hdist = 1 mink = 8 ref. = bbmap/resources/nextera.fa.gz minlen = 100 ow = t qtrim = r trimq = 10.

A contaminant screening was performed by randomly selecting 1000 reads from each sample, conducting BLAST searches against the NCBI nt database, and recording the species identity of the top hit for each read. We cleaned reads of contaminating species using bbduk and the reference genomes of bacteria Nocardia farcinica (Ishikawa et al. 2004) and N. nova (Luo et al. 2014), as well as the reference genome of Breviolum minutum (Shoguchi et al. 2013) (formerly ITS2 type B1) to remove reads from endosymbiotic dinoflagellates Symbiodiniaceae in samples derived from tissue collections. We then created a de novo reference genome by collecting 10 million reads in total, evenly from the samples and excluding reads that had counts fewer than 6 or more than 700. The remaining loci were then aligned to each other to identify allelic loci and collapse allelic haplotypes to a single representative. All reads were mapped to the reference genome with an alignment identity threshold of 87% using bbmap (BBMap tools), and genotype calling was conducted using SAMtools and BCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011; Li et al. 2009) (samtools mpileup ‐gu ‐Q 12 ‐t DP, DPR ‐f ref.fasta ‐b samples.txt | bcftools call ‐cv ‐ > genotypes.vcf). We filtered the resulting variant call format (VCF) file to remove alleles with a population frequency of less than 3% and removed heterozygous loci in all samples or loci that had more than 2 alleles in a sample (suggesting collapsed paralogs). We checked for the presence of artefacts by counting single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) at each read nucleotide position and by determining that SNP number did not increase with reduced base quality at the end of the reads. All of the above steps were conducted by SNPsaurus. After obtaining the VCF, standard filters were applied to retain loci and remove potential clones based on genetic similarity (Extended Materials and Methods).

The curated genome‐wide sequencing data yielded a total of 1481 neutral and independent Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) after excluding four common outliers between BayeScan and PCADAPT, and loci in linkage disequilibrium. Of the 39 monitored colonies, plus the additional 12 unmarked colonies for which sperm samples were collected during spawning, 37 met our loci filtering criteria. One set of 3 samples was identified as clones (based on a Multi‐Locus Genotype similarity of < 0.05%), of which 2 colonies were removed (Figures S2 and S3), leaving 35 unique colonies for downstream analyses: 18 colonies spawning only in spring, 9 colonies spawning only in autumn, 1 colony observed spawning in both seasons, and 7 colonies with an unknown reproductive season because they were never observed spawning. We examined genome‐wide variation via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) using the R package ADEGENET (Jombart 2008). For the latter, the number of clusters (from 1 to 8) was estimated using find.clusters and the optimal number of groups was selected using a Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) approach. The optim.a.score function was then used to determine the number of Principal Components (PCs) to be retained for the DAPC analysis. Once the optimal number of PCs was determined, a second DAPC analysis was conducted using this value (Miller et al. 2020). We further assessed population structure in Admixture (Alexander et al. 2009) (v 1.3.0) to estimate individual ancestry. The number of clusters (K) varied from 1 to 8, with 10 replicates for each value of K, and we identified the value of K with the lowest cross‐validation (CV) error method (Alexander and Lange 2011). Lastly, to infer phylogenetic relationships from SNPs among colonies, we built a Maximum Likelihood (ML) species tree in RaxML‐NG (Kozlov et al. 2019), using the GTR + FO model of sequence evolution. Node support was evaluated with a maximum of 1000 bootstrapping replicates (autoMRE, cutoff = 0.03). Additionally, we inferred a coalescent‐based phylogeny using SVDquartets (Chifman and Kubatko 2014) as implemented in PAUP* (v 4.0a168) (Swofford 2003). We evaluated all possible quartets and assessed node support using nonparametric bootstrapping with 100 replicates. iTOL (v 5) (Letunic and Bork 2021) was used for tree visualisation and editing.

Next, we compared observed (H O ) with expected (H E ) heterozygosity for each lineage and estimated their inbreeding coefficients (F IS ) with mean confidence intervals using the basic.stats function in the R package HIERFSTAT (Goudet 2005) and the mean_cl_boot function in the R package HMISC (Harrell Jr 2019), respectively. Pairwise F ST values between lineages were then calculated following Weir and Cockerham (1984), using the genet.dist function in HIERFSTAT. F ST confidence intervals (95%) were determined using boot.vc with 100 bootstrap runs.

Single‐Gene Sequencing

We assessed the phylogenetic relationship of the spring‐ and autumn‐spawning subpopulations by sequencing the nuclear introns of PaxC and CaM (Extended Materials and Methods), two commonly used molecular markers in evolutionary studies of scleractinian corals, including Faviidae species (Fukami 2008; Rosser et al. 2017; Schwartz et al. 2012). Evolutionary nucleotide substitution models were obtained using JModelTest 2.1.2 (Darriba et al. 2012), and Bayesian Inference was used to assess phylogenetic relationships for each marker in Beast v1.8.2 (Drummond et al. 2012) with 10 million generations, four chains, and 10% burn‐in. MCMC chain convergence was assessed through Tracer v1.6 (Rambaut et al. 2018) with ESS values above 200. ML trees were estimated with RaxML (Stamatakis 2006) using 1000 bootstrap replicates. PaxC and CaM sequences from four coral species closely related to D. labyrinthiformis (Pseudodiploria clivosa, P. strigosa , Colpophyllia natans and Manicina areolata ; Table S2) were downloaded from GenBank to serve as outgroups. We used iTOL (v 5) (Letunic and Bork 2021) for tree visualisation and editing.

Inter‐Seasonal Crosses Between Spring‐ and Autumn‐Spawning Subpopulations

In May 2019, we used cryopreserved sperm to experimentally cross gametes from spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies (Extended Materials and Methods) and determine if the populations were reproductively incompatible (due to gamete incompatibility or hybrid inviability) and thus represented distinct species according to the Biological Species Concept. Experimental crosses included five treatments following the experimental design described in Hagedorn et al. (2017): (1) inter‐seasonal crosses (spring eggs × frozen–thawed (FT) autumn sperm), (2) spring‐only crosses (spring eggs × FT spring sperm), (3) cryoprotectant toxicity controls (spring eggs × fresh spring sperm mixed with cryoprotectant), (4) fresh sperm controls (spring eggs × fresh spring sperm), and (5) no sperm controls (spring eggs × filtered seawater (FSW; Whatman GF/F, GE Life Sciences)). Inter‐seasonal crosses were prepared using pooled sperm samples that were collected from 4 autumn‐spawning colonies and cryopreserved in September 2018 and freshly released eggs that were collected from 3 spring‐spawning colonies in May 2019 (Table S3). Spring‐only crosses served as experimental comparisons for the inter‐seasonal crosses, using fresh eggs from the same 3 colonies and a mixed pool of sperm samples from 4 spring‐spawning colonies that were collected, cryopreserved and thawed that same day. These last three control treatments allowed us to test for potential sources of error and confounding factors such as self‐fertilisation and sample contamination with non‐self sperm before the start of the experiment (false positives), and for cell sensitivity to handling, freezing, and cryoprotectant toxicity (false negatives). Each of the 5 treatments included 3 biological replicates (eggs from 3 donors exposed to each treatment) and 3 technical replicates (each cross repeated in 3 separate fertilisation containers), resulting in a total of 45 individual containers. We aimed to perform reciprocal inter‐seasonal crosses (autumn eggs × frozen–thawed [FT] spring sperm) in September and October 2019; however, limited spawning events prevented the collection of sufficient gamete volumes to conduct these assays.

The experimental crosses were conducted in 355‐mL polycarbonate kitchen fat‐separating pitchers (Scandicrafts Cuisine International) filled with 50 mL of FSW. Upon returning to the laboratory following in situ gamete collections, we first completed all steps for sperm and egg separation and washing, as well as sperm freezing and thawing (Extended Materials and Methods). Then, we transferred batches of ∼500 eggs to each fertilisation container using a micropipette fitted with a wide‐bore tip (∼1 mm, Ø) to limit egg shearing. Fresh or FT sperm was then added to the fertilisation containers at a final concentration of 106 cells mL−1, resulting in a sperm‐to‐egg ratio of ∼1:100,000 as suggested by Hagedorn et al. (2017) for successful fertilisation using FT sperm. Sperm and egg mixtures were kept at their initial density for 20 min with occasional stirring, after which the volume in the fertilisation containers was increased to 350 mL to dilute the cryoprotectant (DMSO; dimethyl sulfoxide) and reduce further risks of toxicity to sperm and egg cells. 110 min later, fertilisation was stopped by rinsing eggs/zygotes in the fertilisation containers to remove residual sperm and cryoprotectant (Extended Materials and Methods). Clean eggs/zygotes were then transferred into 1‐L clear polystyrene containers (Dart Container Corporation) filled with 750 mL of FSW where embryogenesis and larval development took place.

We quantified fertilisation success for each combination of treatment × egg donor by subsampling up to 150 eggs/zygotes from each replicate 3–4 h after they were crossed. The proportion of eggs undergoing embryogenesis was quantified under a stereomicroscope (Amscope). At that time, most fertilised eggs had reached the gastrulation stage and were easily distinguishable from round, unfertilized eggs (Chamberland et al. 2017).

The following morning, fully developed and motile larvae were transferred using glass pipettes into new, 1‐L clear polystyrene containers (Dart Container Corporation) filled with 750 mL of FSW. Developing larvae from the three technical replicates from each treatment × egg donor were pooled and allocated to new containers to assess whether larvae resulting from inter‐seasonal crosses developed similarly to those from spring‐only crosses. We compared survival and settlement success among treatments by placing subsets of 20–30 larvae (n = 6 per treatment) into 355‐ mL clear polystyrene cups (Darnel) containing 300 mL of FSW and a substrate for settlement. These substrates consisted of flat, 5 × 5 × 1 cm (L × W × H) square ceramic tiles previously conditioned for ∼1 year in an aquarium system to allow the development of biofilms that promote larval settlement (Heyward and Negri 1999). Larval survival and settlement were recorded after 7 days using a fluorescence‐inducing blue light (Nightsea) and glasses with a yellow filter which facilitate larval detection (Roth et al. 2013). All substrates with settlers were then returned to the aquarium system for post‐settlement development. Surviving individuals were surveyed after 3 months under blue light, though survivorship could not be compared statistically between crosses due to limited and uneven replication between treatments as well as low overall settler survival.

Differences in fertilisation success (Fertlilised/Total), larval survival (Alive/Total), and larval settlement (Settled/Alive) between spring‐only and inter‐seasonal crosses were assessed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) approach. The response variables were modeled with a binomial distribution and a logit link function. Two models were fitted: first, a full model including ‘Treatment’ (experimental cross) as a fixed effect and random intercepts for ‘Egg donor’ and ‘Replicate’ to account for variability across egg donors and technical replicates, and second, a reduced model including only ‘Treatment’ as a fixed effect, with a random intercept only for ‘Replicate’. For each response variable, models were compared using likelihood ratio tests (ANOVA), where the full model was compared to the reduced model to determine whether the addition of the random intercept for ‘Egg donor’ significantly improved the model fit. All GLMM analyses were run in R Studio (R Core Team 2025) using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015).

Results

Discrete Reproductive Seasonality of Diploria labyrinthiformis Subpopulations Across Years

Combined with our 2013 observations, surveys made in Curaçao between 2014 and 2021 resulted in 79 total monitoring dives during which 120 colony‐level spawning events were recorded. Of the 39 marked colonies, 10 were never observed spawning, 7 were observed spawning once, and 22 were observed spawning in 2–6 separate years. When spawning times were plotted by calendar date, a clear split into two breeding groups was evident (Figure 2): 18 colonies only spawned in spring (May to early July) and 9 only spawned in autumn (late July to September). Two colonies spawned in both seasons (Figure 2). Reproductive season was therefore predictable and discrete for 27 out of the 29 colonies (93%) observed spawning, and this behaviour remained consistent across 8 years of observation. No gross morphological differences (in terms of colony shape, colour, or the structural arrangement of valleys and grooves) between spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies could be detected from in situ photographs of these colonies (Figure S1). The two spawning groups also did not segregate by depth at our study site (Kruskal–Wallis H‐test: H = 2.04, p = 0.15), with spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies spanning similar depth ranges (median depth and [IQR, n] of 5.9 m [2.7, 17] and 5.5 m [1.4, 9], respectively) (Table S1, Figure S2).

Genome‐Wide Differentiation Between Spring‐ and Autumn‐Spawning Subpopulations

Analyses of genome‐wide single nucleotide variants based on 1481 neutral loci clearly showed that the spring‐ and autumn‐spawning subpopulations in Curaçao were genetically distinct. Principal components analysis (PCA) showed two segregated genetic clusters along the first axis (accounting for 12% of the genome‐wide variance), which coincided with the observed spawning seasons for 96% (26 out of 27) of the colonies in the genetic dataset with known reproductive timing (Figure 4A). Thus, the genetic data supported the division of the D. labyrinthiformis population into two discrete lineages based on both genome‐wide SNPs and spawning season. All 9 colonies observed spawning only in autumn clustered with the autumn genetic lineage, and 17 of the 18 colonies observed spawning only in spring clustered with the spring genetic lineage, with colony 35 clustering with the autumn lineage (Figure 5A) despite the fact that it was observed spawning twice in spring and never in autumn (Figure 2). Colony 32, which was observed spawning once in spring and six times in autumn (Figure 2), also clustered with the autumn genetic lineage (Figure 5A).

Clustering assignment analyses based on the same 1481 neutral loci also supported the existence of two separate lineages as the optimal number of K clusters derived from the DAPC was 2 (BIC = 163, Figure S6). No overlap between these two clusters existed (Figure 4B) after retaining 1 PC for analysis (Figure S6). An admixture ancestry plot was created for K = 2, which provided support for the same two genetic lineages identified by PCA and DAPC. This analysis revealed a single colony (21) of admixed ancestry (Figure 4A–C), with an average pairwise differentiation (F ST ) of 0.098 across all loci (Table 1). The ML SNP‐based phylogenetic reconstruction also distinguished between autumn‐spawning and spring‐spawning subpopulations (97% bootstrap support) (Figure 5A). In contrast, the coalescent‐based phylogeny (Figure 5B), while recovering clades that included the spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies identified in the ML tree, also grouped 7 colonies into distinct clades not recovered in the ML inference, with low bootstrap support across all clades (< 80).

Single‐Gene Phylogenetic Reconstruction Supporting Monophyly of Seasonal Lineages

Two D. labyrinthiformis lineages were identified via genome‐wide sequencing. To assess their phylogenetic relationship to one another and to closely related Faviidae species, thus testing for possible paraphyly of the subpopulations, we further sequenced the nuclear introns of PaxC (380 bp) and CaM (382 pb). Successful amplification of these two markers was possible for 26 and 21 colonies, respectively. The evolutionary nucleotide substitution models identified for these markers were F81 (Felsenstein 1981) and HKY (Hasegawa et al. 1985), respectively. Because both Bayesian and ML methods produced the same topology, only the Bayesian tree is presented here (Figure 5C), which shows that spring‐ and autumn‐spawning lineages were part of a monophyletic clade (most closely related to M. areolata ) with no clear genetic distinction between colonies with different spawning seasons.

Successful Inter‐Seasonal Crosses Between Spring‐ and Autumn‐Spawned Gametes

After identifying genetically distinct D. labyrinthiformis lineages with temporal reproductive isolation, we used sperm cryopreservation and in vitro fertilisation techniques to test whether these lineages were further isolated by pre‐ and/or postzygotic gametic barriers. Eggs from all three spring‐spawning colonies were successfully fertilised by the pooled, frozen–thawed sperm from autumn‐spawning colonies, with per‐colony fertilisation rates of 57% [±1, 3], 32% [±11, 3], and 78% [±8, 3] (mean [±SE, n]) (Figure 6A,B). Despite variability in gamete compatibility among egg donors, inter‐seasonal crosses were equally or more successful than spring‐only crosses in terms of fertilisation success (GLMM; Table 2, Table S4). No sperm controls yielded fertilisation rates below 2% (Figure 6B), indicating that the high fertilisation rates in inter‐seasonal crosses were unlikely to result from self‐fertilisation or contamination with non‐self sperm. Additionally, cryoprotectant toxicity controls resulted in fertilisation success rates comparable to or higher than those of fresh sperm controls, indicating that egg exposure to the cryoprotectant dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) had minimal impact on fertilisation potential in this experimental setup (GLMM; Table 2, Table S4). Zygotes resulting from the inter‐seasonal crosses underwent normal embryogenesis and larval development and became motile ∼12 h after fertilisation (Figure 6A). Larval performance varied among egg donors but was overall similar between the inter‐seasonal and spring‐only crosses both in terms of survival and settlement success (GLMM; Table 2, Table S4, Figure 6C,D). A total of 8 settled and metamorphosed individuals resulting from the inter‐seasonal crosses survived in our aquarium system until the age of 3 months, after which they were no longer monitored. This unidirectional cross confirmed gamete compatibility and subsequent zygote viability between the two lineages, indicating that despite the observed genetic differentiation, the two lineages are not fully isolated by prezygotic or early post‐zygotic barriers.

Discussion

In Curaçao, we found evidence that the critically endangered reef‐building brain coral D. labyrinthiformis consists of two genetically distinct lineages with clear temporal reproductive isolation, but not gametic reproductive isolation. The two lineages occur in sympatry and syntopy but exhibit genome‐wide differentiation and distinct spring and autumn spawning seasons, yet they remain reproductively compatible when experimentally crossed between the two seasons using cryopreserved sperm. These findings illustrate how temporal reproductive isolation, in this case through seasonal spawning asynchrony, can act as a mechanism contributing to or reinforcing genetic diversification within coral populations despite gametic compatibility and syntopic occurrence on the reef.

Recent and Incipient Divergence of Spring‐ and Autumn‐Spawning Coral Lineages in Curaçao

Clustering methods consistently identified two genetically distinct lineages of D. labyrinthiformis that overwhelmingly coincided with two distinct spawning seasons in spring and autumn. The observed differences in spawning seasons were clear and consistent over a period of 8 years. Nonetheless, divergence between these two lineages appears to be incipient or of recent origin. This is supported by discrepancies between tree topologies, a low F ST value at the whole‐genome level (1481 neutral loci), the lack of resolution at single‐locus markers (PaxC and CaM genes), and the absence of conspicuous morphological differences between spring‐ and autumn‐spawning colonies. Further, the observation of two colonies spawning in both seasons, one colony of admixed ancestry, along with one colony assigned to opposite genetic and reproductive clusters, supports the presence of incomplete lineage sorting and/or hybridisation, as shown in the coalescent‐based phylogeny. Experiments using cryopreserved sperm to bridge the period between spring and autumn spawning seasons also showed that the ability to produce viable offspring persists between the two genetic lineages.

Although we found clear evidence of gametic compatibility between spring‐spawning egg donors and autumn‐spawning sperm donors, we cannot exclude the possibility that gametic compatibility between the two lineages may be unidirectional. Crosses between autumn‐spawning egg donors and spring‐spawning sperm donors were not conducted during this study and might yet turn out to be unsuccessful. Furthermore, uni‐ or bidirectional gametic incompatibility might occur at other localities. Notably, a pattern of unidirectional gametic compatibility has been observed in Caribbean Acropora and Orbicella; A. cervicornis eggs show no signs of incompatibility when crossed with A. palmata sperm, whereas fertilisation largely fails when A. palmata eggs are exposed to A. cervicornis sperm (Fogarty et al. 2012). Similarly, O. franksi sperm readily fertilise O. annularis eggs whereas the reciprocal cross often fails (Levitan et al. 2004). Such asymmetrical incompatibilities in D. labyrinthiformis would reduce chances of interbreeding during (rare) overlapping spawning events, further contributing to or reinforcing genetic divergence. In the case of D. labyrinthiformis , we also cannot rule out the possibility that postzygotic barriers such as poor hybrid fitness (e.g., reduced viability, sterility) could arise later in life (Fishman and Willis 2001; Ouyang et al. 2010). We only observed offspring generated via inter‐seasonal crosses until the age of 3 months, that is, several years before they could reach sexual maturity. Overall, temporal prezygotic reproductive isolation in the form of asynchronous spawning seasons appears to be a strong mechanism limiting gene flow between these two D. labyrinthiformis lineages, which are thus very unlikely to mate under natural conditions.

Allochrony as a Mechanism of Reproductive Isolation in Corals

For many coral species, mass spawning is finely synchronised among conspecific colonies (e.g., Baird et al. 2009; Fogarty and Marhaver 2019; Shlesinger and Loya 2019). Because of these narrow reproductive windows, the short viability period of coral gametes, and their rapid dilution in the sea, small temporal differences in spawning among conspecifics will greatly lower fertilisation success and thus gene flow among colonies spawning at different times (Levitan et al. 2004; Mumby et al. 2024). For example, the Caribbean coral species O. franksi spawns only 1–2 h before O. annularis , which appears to be sufficient to perpetuate reproductive isolation between these sympatric, but morphologically‐ and genetically distinct species (Levitan et al. 2004, 2011). Despite these differences, the two species are reproductively compatible when synchronous spawning is induced in the lab and heterospecific gametes of the same age are crossed in vitro, indicating that a permeable species boundary occurs in these coral species (Knowlton et al. 1997), similar to that observed in D. labyrinthiformis in this study. However, compared to Orbicella species, natural gamete encounters between the two lineages of D. labyrinthiformis are far less likely due to a reproductive isolation period of weeks–months instead of min–h.

Consistent, multi‐year efforts to monitor the spawning activity of benthic invertebrates such as corals are rare and inherently challenging; thus, the prevalence of spawning asynchrony among cryptic subpopulations is likely to be underestimated in such organisms. However, the number of studies showing temporal reproductive isolation in coral species is growing, suggesting that allochrony may be a common and effective mechanism by which coral subspecies can undergo, reinforce, or maintain genetic divergence while in sympatry. A recent literature review identified at least 24 cases of sympatric cryptic coral lineages across ocean basins, spanning 18 stony coral and 6 octocoral genera (Grupstra, Gómez‐Corrales, et al. 2024). Of these, distinct reproductive phenology between lineages was observed in 6 stony coral genera (Acropora: Furukawa et al. 2020; Gilmour et al. 2016; Ohki et al. 2015; Rosser 2015; Rosser et al. 2017, 2020, Coelastrea: Mitsuki et al. 2021, Fungia: Grinblat et al. 2021, Galaxea: Abe et al. 2008, Pachyseris: Bongaerts et al. 2021, Pocillopora: Schmidt‐Roach et al. 2013), and in 2 octocoral genera (Heliopora: Iguchi et al. 2019, Plexaura: Pelosi et al. 2021 ). Here, D. labyrinthiformis represents a new case of distinct reproductive phenology between lineages, and in this case, temporal reproductive isolation appears to be a far greater barrier to gene flow than gametic incompatibilities or early postzygotic barriers.

Possible Scenarios of Coral Lineage Divergence, Dispersal and Persistence

The evolution of cryptic coral species in sympatry can be driven by ecological specialisation across environmental gradients, by the emergence of differences in reproductive timing, or by both factors concurrently or in series (González et al. 2018; Grupstra, Gómez‐Corrales, et al. 2024). While spring‐ and autumn‐spawning lineages of D. labyrinthiformis currently co‐occur in sympatry and syntopy, the spatial and geographic history and context of their divergence cannot be inferred solely on the basis of this study. However, given this species' current range and observed behaviour, the possibility exists that the two lineages could have undergone sympatric divergence based on allochrony, for example, if one or more mutations occurred in the genes or transcription factors responsible for the timing of spawning, yielding a rapid and strong barrier to gene flow. Spawning records from across the Wider Caribbean Region indicate that spring‐ and autumn‐spawning D. labyrinthiformis colonies co‐occur in some locations (i.e., Havana [Cuba], Bayahibe [Dominican Republic], Bonaire and Curaçao), but not in others (i.e., Miami [Florida], Samaná [Dominican Republic], La Parguera [Puerto Rico], Tobago [Trinidad and Tobago]), where spawning has only been observed in spring despite monitoring efforts in both seasons. Determining whether corals with these respective spawning behaviours correspond to the same genetic lineages found in Curaçao will require additional genomic analyses across the region. If the two genetically distinct lineages are indeed widespread throughout the wider Caribbean, it may still remain difficult to ascertain whether their expansion occurred following sympatric divergence in one or more localities, or if secondary contact is taking place following allopatric divergence. In the latter case, divergence of spawning times may have occurred due to drift or as a secondary effect of adaptation to local environmental factors. In either case, seasonal allochrony clearly acts as a mechanism maintaining and reinforcing current reproductive isolation in locations where both spring‐ and autumn‐spawning lineages occur.

Potential factors underlying the regional distributions of the two D. labyrinthiformis groups include local environmental variables that independently impose selective pressures while also influencing the timing of coral spawning, such as solar irradiance (Van Woesik et al. 2006), sea surface temperature (Lin and Nozawa 2023), rainfall (Mendes and Woodley 2002), wind (van Woesik 2010), and even anthropogenic factors (Fogarty and Marhaver 2019; Shlesinger and Loya 2019). It is further possible that random dispersal with ocean currents, founder effects, and/or bottleneck events drove migration, colonisation, and extirpation patterns of the two lineages following their divergence, and this may therefore explain their current geographic range (Torquato et al. 2022; Vogt‐Vincent et al. 2023; Wood et al. 2014). More exhaustive spawning records from across the region will provide a better understanding of how environmental and stochastic factors may control the geographic distribution of spring‐ and autumn‐spawning D. labyrinthiformis behaviours and lineages.

In the present study, we did not observe any indications that the genetic differentiation between spring‐ and autumn‐spawning lineages was associated with environmental gradients or habitat heterogeneity (e.g., light availability: Knowlton et al. 1997; depth: van Oppen et al. 2018; distance to shore: Rippe et al. 2021; sedimentation: Vermeij et al. 2007). At our study site, colonies from the two lineages co‐occurred in syntopy, within the same depth range, and often less than a meter apart from one another. We further did not detect any gross morphological differences between them, though detailed, fine‐scale skeletal analyses required for taxonomic classification were not conducted in the scope of this study. Accurately quantifying differences in (micro)morphological traits is technically challenging, particularly among closely related coral species and even more so between lineages or populations (Budd et al. 2012). Thus, we did not find major support for the types of habitat‐driven divergence that have been identified in other coral species (Bongaerts et al. 2021; Prada and Hellberg 2021; Rippe et al. 2021).

Importantly, we were also not able to test for barriers to gene flow that occur later in life such as sterility or reduced fitness of hybrids and backcrosses. Thus, it remains possible that later‐life and later‐generation isolating factors could have substantial additional effects on gene flow, contributing to the creation and/or reinforcement of the divergence of the lineages identified in this study. It remains possible for these isolating mechanisms to play a substantial role as our inter‐seasonal crosses using cryopreserved sperm revealed an absence of complete gametic or early post‐zygotic incompatibilities.

In sum, while a variety of interacting factors likely explain the initial divergence of the coral lineages identified in this study, temporal reproductive isolation via seasonal allochrony appears to be a strong, consistent, and regionally common factor currently maintaining and reinforcing the differentiation between spring‐ and autumn‐spawning lineages of the reef‐building coral D. labyrinthiformis . The existence of this previously unidentified diversity suggests that additional cryptic coral diversity and the mechanisms underlying its emergence remain yet to be uncovered.

Conclusion: The Urgency of Identifying and Conserving Cryptic Coral Diversity

A growing number of genetic studies show that the taxonomic diversity in reef‐building corals greatly exceeds estimates derived from morphological traits alone. Unfortunately, the evolutionary mechanisms responsible for the emergence of cryptic coral species remain largely unknown. Even in the few examples where genetic, morphological and ecological information is available (e.g., Aichelman et al. 2025; Bongaerts et al. 2021; Carlon and Budd 2002; Gilmour et al. 2016; Grupstra, Meyer, et al. 2024), the mechanisms and processes driving the formation and maintenance of cryptic diversity remain elusive for most coral taxa. Here, we provide a clear case study that illustrates how closely related and gametically compatible cryptic lineages maintain reproductive isolation through seasonally allochronous spawning behaviour. From a conservation perspective, the underestimation of cryptic coral diversity will affect estimates of species ranges and distributions, population sizes and extinction risks. Our study on D. labyrinthiformis clearly illustrates the conservation threat posed by missed or unrecognised coral lineages. This species as a whole was recently listed as “critically endangered” under IUCN's Red List (Grupstra, Meyer, et al. 2024) given severe population declines in some parts of its range and its high susceptibility to Stony Coral Tissue Loss Disease (SCTLD) (Williams et al. 2021). Our discovery of two lineages that rarely interbreed means that such threats pose an even higher risk to the survival of each lineage and their unique genetic diversity. Such hidden, cryptic coral diversity poses new challenges for managers, who must now ensure the long‐term persistence of a much larger number of coral species, subspecies, or lineages, each with smaller populations that cannot be identified through traditional, morphology‐based inventories of species diversity. Amidst the coral conservation crisis, there is a continued urgent need to investigate and understand the evolutionary mechanisms responsible for the emergence and maintenance of cryptic coral species diversity, so that not only populations and lineages but also the ecological and behavioural forces underpinning their existence can be preserved.
