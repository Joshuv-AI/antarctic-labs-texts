ABSTRACT
Valgus extension overload syndrome encompasses a spectrum of soft tissue and osseous changes occurring in the elbow of athletes participating in throwing or overhead sports, with manifestations varying by skeletal maturity. The three main components of valgus extension overload at the elbow are medial tensile stress, lateral compressive forces, and posteromedial shear, collectively known as thrower’s elbow. Significant valgus stresses are placed on the medial elbow during the late cocking and acceleration phases of throwing. Understanding the biomechanics of the throwing motion is essential to recognize characteristic adaptive changes occurring in the throwing athlete and identify typical injury patterns occurring in the medial, lateral and posteromedial elbow as a result of repetitive valgus overload. It is imperative to recognize the full spectrum of injuries that can occur from valgus extension overload in the skeletally mature and immature throwing athletes for accurate diagnosis, effective clinical management and informed return-to-play decisions.

INTRODUCTION

Elbow pain is a common presenting symptom in athletes, particularly those involved in throwing or overhead sports including baseball, softball, tennis, cricket, javelin throwing, golf, water polo and swimming. Injuries of the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) are common in throwing athletes, due to chronic repetitive microtrauma to the elbow. The repetitive valgus forces acting on the elbow can result in anatomic adaptations in the throwing athlete, which include thickening of the UCL, as well as osseous remodeling (12345). If these adaptive mechanisms fail, a spectrum of injuries can occur as a consequence of compromised medial ligamentous integrity, and valgus instability leading to increased loading of the radiocapitellar joint and excessive shear forces on the posteromedial elbow, termed valgus extension overload (VEO) syndrome (678910). Elbow injuries occurring in the setting of VEO show predictable patterns in terms of clinical presentation and imaging findings (11112). However, anatomic differences between skeletally immature and mature throwing athletes can result in different patterns of injury (Tables 1, 2, 3). Skeletally immature athletes have open growth plates, which are softer and weaker than the ligaments and tendons inserting into them, resulting in apophyseal, osteochondral, and stress injuries (131415) whereas skeletally mature athletes with closed growth plates are at greater risk of soft tissue injury and a propensity to degenerative change (1116).

BIOMECHANICS OF THROWING

The overhead throwing motion in baseball generates tremendous demands on the elbow and is typically divided into 6 distinct phases: wind-up, early cocking, late cocking, acceleration, deceleration and follow-through (Fig. 1) (17). In the windup phase, the elbow is flexed, with no substantial kinetic stresses acting on it. Shoulder abduction and external rotation are initiated in the early cocking phase. During the late cocking phase of throwing, the shoulder is placed in maximal external rotation with a flexed elbow. As the throw transitions from the late cocking to early acceleration phases, there is a large transfer of potential energy to kinetic energy as the humerus begins to internally rotate about the shoulder and the elbow rapidly extends, generating a large valgus torque across the elbow, most of which is transmitted to the anterior bundle of the UCL (Fig. 2A) (181920). The combination of valgus forces and rapid extension results in tensile forces on the medial elbow, compressive forces on the lateral elbow and shear forces on the posterior elbow. Elbow biomechanics play an important role in the throwing motions in other overhead sports, such as football, javelin throwing and tennis, although the forces and torques acting on the elbow differ slightly, depending on the sport (17).

THE ROLE OF IMAGING IN THE VALGUS EXTENSION OVERLOAD OF THE ELBOW

Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosing and characterizing VEO in the elbow. Radiographs are still commonly obtained in the initial evaluation of throwing-related elbow pain, due to the easy accessibility and low cost. Radiographs can depict some of the more advanced findings in VEO such as soft tissue mineralization, osteophytes, joint space narrowing and joint bodies, but are insensitive to the majority of soft tissue changes. In younger athletes, X-ray may show bony changes within the capitellum from osteochondral injury.

Ultrasound is excellent in the evaluation soft tissue pathology, including the UCL, common flexor tendon, ulnar nerve abnormalities and the presence of joint effusion. Dynamic stress ultrasound has been used to demonstrate UCL insufficiency in throwing athletes (212223). Ultrasound is excellent in the assessment of ulnar nerve pathology, particularly in the dynamic assessment of ulnar nerve subluxation. Ultrasound has also been used as a screening tool for the early detection of osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum in adolescent baseball players, as well as assessment of unstable osteochondral lesions (24). However, ultrasound is relatively user-dependent, and the operator needs to be familiar with the appropriate techniques. Ultrasound is also limited in the evaluation of structural changes within the elbow joint and cannot reliably assess chondral abnormalities.

CT can be very helpful to detect osseous abnormalities such as joint space narrowing, bony reactive changes, osteophytes, joint bodies, bony avulsive injuries and stress fractures (25). 3D CT reconstructions can also be helpful for surgical planning. However, CT is more limited in the evaluation of soft tissue pathology.

Although radiographs, ultrasound, and CT all have a role to play in the investigation of elbow pain, MRI has emerged as the imaging modality of choice in the evaluation of elbow pain in athletes, particularly in a setting of VEO. The lack of ionizing radiation, high spatial resolution, superior soft tissue contrast and multiplanar imaging capabilities of MRI make it ideal for evaluating the complex anatomy around the elbow. MR arthrography with intra-articular injection of dilute gadolinium or saline may be helpful in patients without a joint effusion to look for subtle ligament tears, osteochondral lesions or joint bodies (232627). More recently, the flexed elbow valgus external rotation (FEVER) view has been proposed to evaluate the UCL of professional baseball pitchers (2829). In the FEVER view the patient’s shoulder is placed in external rotation, the shoulder and elbow are flexed to 90° with the palm up (Fig. 2B). The elbow is elevated approximately 4 inches above the table with foam pads and a sandbag is subsequently placed along the distal forearm to create valgus stress. Coronal oblique images are performed along the expected course of the anterior bundle of the UCL. This position places traction on humeroulnar joint, which can show ligamentous laxity and increases sensitivity for subtle UCL tears (29).

UCL

The UCL is divided into 3 bundles: anterior, posterior and transverse (Fig. 2A). The anterior bundle of the UCL provides the main static restraint against valgus stress at the elbow when the elbow is flexed, making it particularly susceptible to injury in throwing athletes. The anterior bundle arises from the medial humeral epicondyle and attaches into the sublime tubercle of the proximal ulna, providing the main constraint to valgus stress between 30–120 degrees of flexion. The anterior bundle can be further subdivided into functionally separate anterior and posterior bands that tighten in a reciprocal fashion in flexion and extension, ensuring that one or both bands are taut throughout the full arc of flexion (30). The posterior bundle of the UCL is taut only when the elbow is flexed beyond 90 degrees (31).

Cumulative microtrauma to the medial elbow from valgus forces in overhead athletes can result in adaptive thickening of the UCL (Fig. 3), even in asymptomatic adolescent throwers (1242232). Traction spurs and bone marrow edema may be seen along the sublime tubercle of the proximal ulna (32), and the spurs may become very large in elite athletes (Fig. 4A). The thickened anterior bundle of the UCL becomes more susceptible to acute injury, manifesting as a sudden onset of medial pain whilst throwing.

Acute complete UCL tears manifest as discontinuity of ligament fibers, irregularity and laxity of the ligament with periligamentous edema commonly associated with injury of the overlying flexor-pronator mass (Fig. 4A-C). Complete tears most commonly involve the proximal or mid-substance of the UCL. However, tears of the UCL can often be seen in more than one location in throwing athletes (33). Partial tears more frequently involve the distal fibers of the anterior band of the UCL (Fig. 4D) and may be difficult to detect in the absence of joint fluid. MR arthrography is often helpful in evaluating distal tears, with fluid tracking between the distal fibers of the UCL and the sublime tubercle forming the “T sign” (Fig. 4E). Avulsion fractures of the sublime tubercle can also occur but are rare (Fig. 4D) (3435), and may require operative intervention. In lower grade sprains the UCL may appear thickened with increased signal and partial tears. The FEVER view is helpful to assess the severity of the tear (Fig. 4F, G). The degree of gapping correlates with the level of play, with increased widening associated with poorer performance (29). Chronic injury to the UCL can present with a more insidious onset of medial elbow pain with decreased throwing velocity and may result in heterotopic ossification within the thickened ligament (Fig. 4H, I).

Conservative management is favored for partial thickness UCL tears and may be combined with platelet rich plasma. Surgical options for complete UCL tears and partial tears that have failed conservative management include UCL repair or UCL reconstruction (36).

COMMON FLEXOR TENDON

Repetitive valgus stress on the elbow also leads to contraction of the overlying common flexor-pronator mass, which in turn can lead to muscle fatigue and injuries ranging from tendinosis, tendon tears, muscle strains (Fig. 4B) or acute muscle tears (10).

ULNAR NERVE

Ulnar neuropathy is common in throwing athletes, particularly in those that have concomitant injury to the UCL, which can place increased traction on the ulnar nerve during the acceleration and late cocking stages of throwing. Structural changes occurring around the medial elbow in throwing athletes, such as osteophytes or muscle hypertrophy can contribute to compression of the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel (Fig. 5) (181037). The cubital tunnel retinaculum may be absent leading to subluxation of the ulnar nerve during the pitching motion. Typical symptoms include tenderness along the cubital tunnel, paresthesia in the ulnar 1 ½ digits, intrinsic hand weakness and decreased velocity of throwing and throwing endurance (3738). Imaging findings of ulnar neuropathy include thickening and fascicular prominence of the ulnar nerve and increased T2 signal.

MEDIAL APOPHYSEAL INJURIES

Skeletally immature overhead throwing athletes are more vulnerable to apophyseal injuries (Fig. 6). Repetitive valgus stress resulting in medial epicondylar apophysitis, which can be exacerbated by improper technique, weakness in the muscles around the elbow and an excessive number of pitches over a short period of time. Medial apophyseal injuries most commonly occur in children aged between 9 and 12 years and are prevalent in baseball players and have been termed “Little League elbow.” Radiographs may show widening and irregularity of the physis, and comparison with radiographs of the unaffected elbow in these cases may be helpful (Fig. 6A, B). Hypertrophy of the medial epicondyle may also occur. MRI may show bone marrow edema within the apophysis or periphyseal edema (Fig. 6C, D) (39). Conservative treatment including rest, bracing, physical therapy and techniques to improve throwing mechanics can result in a rapid improvement in symptoms, with return to play within 6 weeks (40).

The medial epicondylar apophysis may also be fractured or avulsed in young throwing athletes (Fig. 6E, F) as a result of medial traction forces (13941).

Non-operative management can be successful if the apophyseal fragment is separated by 3 mm or less and is not rotated. Displacement of over 3 mm or rotation of the avulsed fragment necessitates open reduction and internal fixation (40).

LATERAL COMPRESSIVE FORCES

Compressive loading of the radiocapitellar joint occurs in throwing athletes and can lead to thicker trabecular bone in the capitellum (4243) as well as increased sclerosis in the radial head (1). Compressive forces can result in both chondral and osteochondral injuries along the anterior articular surface of the capitellum and joint bodies, eventually leading to osteoarthritis (1). Chondral injury is more common in older athletes, and includes focal chondral defects and chondral delamination, which can lead to unstable chondral fragments and joint bodies.

Lateral compressive forces in younger athletes can result in Panner’s disease or osteochondritis dissecans (Fig. 7). Panner’s disease, a benign self-limiting osteochondrosis of the developing ossification center of the capitellum, more commonly seen in males between the ages of 7 and 12 (414344). Patients typically present with mild lateral elbow pain and stiffness, swelling and tenderness, exacerbated by activity. The capitellum is solely supplied by cartilaginous epiphyseal vessels, and repetitive injury can affect this tenuous blood supply, leading to disordered endochondral ossification. Bony resorption of the capitellar epiphysis occurs, manifesting as lucency, irregularity, fragmentation and sclerosis of the capitellar epiphysis on radiographs. MRI can delineate the extent of subchondral involvement and bone marrow edema. Panner’s disease is benign and self-limiting, with rest and cessation of the precipitating activity leading to eventual regeneration and remodelling of the capitellum within 1–2 years (Fig. 7A-C), with no residual deformity or collapse in the majority of patients.

Adolescent athletes over the age of 12 years are more susceptible to osteochondritis dissecans, a leading cause of elbow pain and disability in this age group, more commonly affecting males (45). The tenuous blood supply of the capitellum makes the chondro-osseous junction particularly vulnerable to vascular insufficiency from repetitive overuse in sports such as baseball, racquet sports, gymnastics and weightlifting. Repetitive compressive and shear forces can result in separation of the epiphyseal cartilage from the underlying subchondral bone, with subsequent fragmentation of articular cartilage and bone. There may also be a genetic predisposition (24). Osteochondritis dissecans may manifest as an insidious onset of activity-related pain, stiffness, and loss of extension. A patient may present with locking if there are associated joint bodies. Osteochondral lesions occur more anteriorly in overhead athletes where lateral compressive forces are greatest between 30 to 90 degrees of flexion, compared to gymnasts where the greatest compressive forces are applied in full extension, and the osteochondral lesions occur more distally (46).

Radiographs of patients with osteochondritis dissecans may show subchondral lucency along the anterior capitellum, often with associated collapse and fragmentation of the articular surface (Fig. 7D). However, standard orthogonal radiographs may miss about 50% of osteochondral lesions (47) and an anteroposterior (AP) radiograph of the elbow in 45 degrees of flexion is recommended to improve the accuracy of diagnosis in adolescent throwing athletes (2448).

Ultrasound has also been used as a screening tool for early asymptomatic osteochondritis dissecans in adolescent baseball player who may have minimal or no symptoms, as it allows visualization of the cartilage and underlying subchondral bone (24). Findings include an irregular contour of the capitellar cartilage, irregularity or discontinuity of the subchondral bone plate, and osteochondral defects indicating a displaced osteochondral fragment.

MRI evaluation is important for treatment planning to assess the location and size of the lesion, and the integrity of the overlying articular cartilage (Fig. 7E). Potential instability of lesions is indicated by fluid undermining the fragment on MRI or MR arthrography, a rim of high T2 signal intensity, an osteochondral defect or joint body (49) (Fig. 7F, G). Non-operative management may be considered in patients with small, stable capitellar lesions, an open capitellar physis and absence of mechanical symptoms (50). However, surgery may be indicated for unstable lesions, presenting with mechanical symptoms, symptomatic joint bodies, and failure of conservative management (4143). Surgical treatment can include debridement of the articular fragment with drilling or microfracture of the underlying subchondral bone. Lesions that involve greater than 50% of the articular surface or involving the lateral margin of the capitellum may have a worse prognosis after debridement and may be treated with surgical fixation of the unstable fragment with bioabsorbable screws or an osteochondral allograft (51).

One important caveat to be aware of in the elbow is the pseudodefect of the capitellum, which can simulate an osteochondral lesion on MRI (52). The anterior portion of the capitellum is covered by articular cartilage, but the posterior bare area is non-articular, and the bone is often pitted and irregular with fibrocystic change. Frontal radiographs may show subchondral lucency and irregularity along the capitellum. Coronal MR images may show cortical irregularity and subcortical cystic change, mimicking an osteochondral defect. However, on sagittal imaging, the cortical irregularity and cystic change is located along the posterior non-weight-bearing surface of the capitellum.

POSTEROMEDIAL SHEAR

Posteromedial shear forces acting on the elbow, particularly in the setting of UCL insufficiency, cause the posteromedial tip of the olecranon process to impinge on the medial wall of the olecranon fossa, which can lead to adaptive subchondral sclerosis in the olecranon and trochlea, even in young throwing athletes (13237). Repetitive posteromedial shear may initially result in soft tissue changes such as synovitis and fluid in the posteromedial recess on MRI, as well as capsular thickening.

Repetitive posteromedial impingement in skeletally mature athletes may produce posterior humeroulnar osteophytes, chondral injury, fragmentation of the olecranon tip and joint bodies (Fig. 8A-D) (111). Patients typically present with posterior pain and mechanical symptoms such as locking, catching, and crepitus in the posterior elbow, as well as an extension deficit. Although conservative treatment may be beneficial if there is soft tissue impingement, arthroscopic or open debridement is often necessary (37).

Olecranon stress fractures are also more common in older throwing athletes with closed physes, because of repetitive microtrauma against the olecranon fossa from valgus stress or excessive tensile forces from the triceps tendon during the deceleration phase of throwing (3753). The most common stress fracture is an obliquely oriented fracture starting from the ulnar aspect of the olecranon articular surface (13). Athletes with olecranon stress injuries typically complain of posterior elbow pain exacerbated by throwing activities. Conventional radiography may be normal, and CT or MRI may be necessary to confirm the diagnosis (Fig. 8E-G). Conservative management is usually adopted for athletes younger than 13, while surgical management with internal fixation is often adopted in older athletes, particularly in athletes wanting to return to competitive sport (3854).

Repetitive posteromedial shear forces in the skeletally immature athletes can result in olecranon apophysitis, essentially a Salter type 1 injury with delayed closure and nonunion of the olecranon apophysis (Fig. 9A, B), as well as olecranon stress fractures (134355), and are commonly seen in association with UCL insufficiency or injuries of the medial epicondyle (13). These olecranon stress injuries are primarily seen in throwing athletes, and manifest clinically with an insidious onset of vague, activity-related posterior elbow pain that progresses over several weeks, with subsequent loss of extension strength and range of motion. The olecranon apophysis usually closes between 15–17 years of age. Physeal stress injuries are most commonly seen in younger adolescent throwers, typically manifesting as widening and irregularity of the growth plate and delayed union compared to the contralateral non-throwing elbow (13).

Posteromedial shear in adolescent throwing athletes, as well as gymnasts and weightlifters can result in osteochondral injuries of the trochlea. The posteroinferior aspect of the lateral trochlea is an area of relative hypovascularity. Abutment of the olecranon in repetitive elbow extension can affect the blood supply leading to the development of an osteochondral lesion (Fig. 9C-F). The typical location of an osteochondral lesion is located along the inferolateral aspect of the trochlea, thought to develop as a result of ischemia to the subchondral bone (56). Atypical osteochondral lesions occur along the posterior medial aspect of the trochlea and are more commonly associated with abnormalities of the UCL in association with VEO and may occur in conjunction with osteochondritis of the capitellum (57).

In conclusion, VEO is a common source of elbow pain and dysfunction in overhead athletes, particularly in baseball. The biomechanical stresses occurring in the elbow of throwing athletes secondary to VEO result in a spectrum of anatomic adaptations and injury patterns that differ depending on the degree of skeletal maturity. It is important to recognize the common imaging findings related to medial tension overload, lateral compression and posteromedial shear in both adolescents and skeletally mature individuals for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning and return-to-play decisions. The open growth plates of pediatric athletes are softer and weaker than the ligaments and tendons inserting into them, resulting in apophyseal, osteochondral, and stress injuries, whereas the closed growth plates of adult athletes are at greater risk of soft tissue injury and degenerative changes.
