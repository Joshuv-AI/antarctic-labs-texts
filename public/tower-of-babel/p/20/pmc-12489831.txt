ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Background

Despite significant advancements in cardiovascular therapeutics and interventional cardiology over recent decades, including the widespread adoption of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and the development of sophisticated mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices, the mortality associated with cardiogenic shock secondary to acute myocardial infarction (AMI-CS) has seen only modest improvements.1, 2, 3, 4 Extensive literature and large-scale registries consistently report in-hospital mortality rates for AMI-CS that can range from a concerning 30 % to as high as 60–70 % in some series.5, 6, 7, 8 Even for those patients who manage to survive the initial hospital stay, the long-term prognosis often remains suboptimal.1, 9, 10

A particularly vulnerable and prognostically challenging subgroup from AMI-CS patients is individuals who experience an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). OHCA adds complexity, management challenges and urgency to an already critical clinical scenario, potentially worsening the overall prognosis even if a return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) is successfully achieved.11, 12 Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the distinct characteristics and management challenges of those patients is crucial for optimizing tailored treatment strategies and ultimately improving survival rates in this exceptionally high-risk cohort.

The ability to perform accurate risk stratification is an integrated step in managing OHCA patients. Risk stratification is crucial for guiding therapeutic decisions, allocating resources, and providing realistic prognostic information to patients and their families.13 Numerous scoring systems and predictive models have been developed and validated to aid clinicians in this challenging process and the Cardiac Arrest Hospital Prognosis (CAHP) score was designed to predict prognosis in the heterogeneous population of patients successfully resuscitated from cardiac arrest.13, 14 While the utility of the CAHP score has been explored across various settings, its specific prognostic value in AMI-CS patients in the Gulf region remains an area that requires more focused and rigorous investigation.

This study aims to address these critical knowledge gaps by analyzing patients with AMI-CS from the Gulf-CS registry. This study seeks to characterize and compare clinical outcomes of AMI-CS patients who experienced an OHCA versus those who did not. Additionally, this study evaluates the discriminative ability of the CAHP score for in-hospital mortality among AMI-CS patients who presented with OHCA.

Methods

Design and setting

This is a retrospective cohort multicenter study that analyzed data from the Gulf-CS registry. This registry systematically enrolled consecutive eligible patients from 13 tertiary care cardiovascular centers geographically distributed across six Gulf Cooperation Council countries. The study enrolled patients from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2022.

Study population

The study population included adult patients (≥18 years) who were admitted with a primary diagnosis of AMI-CS, and who subsequently underwent coronary angiography. Patients were excluded from this analysis if they did not undergo coronary angiography during their index hospitalization. Further exclusion criteria included normal or insignificantly diseased coronary arteries, the presence of concomitant severe valvular heart disease (e.g., severe aortic stenosis or acute severe mitral regurgitation unrelated to the AMI), or if the cardiogenic shock was due to other non-AMI causes, such as fulminant myocarditis, end-stage cardiomyopathy from other etiologies, pulmonary embolism, septic shock with cardiac dysfunction, or severe complications related to COVID-19 infection without clear evidence of an acute coronary syndrome. For patients with OHCA, we included patients with witnessed cardiac arrest who had ROSC.

Patients were grouped based on the occurrence of OHCA. The non-OHCA group comprised all other AMI-CS patients who did not experience a cardiac arrest outside the hospital setting during the index event.

Cardiogenic shock was defined as systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg for ≥30 min or the need for vasopressors, inotropes, or mechanical circulatory support (MCS) to maintain a systolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg with evidence of tissue hypoperfusion.15

Data collection

Data were collected from patient medical records, including demographics, angiographic, echocardiographic and laboratory data. Detailed data collection of this registry was previously published.1

For patients in the OHCA group, specific data pertaining to the arrest event were collected, including: location of arrest (public vs. home), witnessed status, bystander CPR provision, initial documented cardiac rhythm (shockable vs. non-shockable), time from collapse to initiation of CPR, time from CPR to ROSC, and details of pre-hospital interventions, including the number of epinephrine doses administered. The CAHP score was calculated at the time of arrival to the emergency room, when data about pH and lactate are first available. Clinical, hemodynamic, and laboratory data were collected at the time of first medical contact, unless otherwise specified.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure for the comparative analysis between the OHCA and non-OHCA groups was the in-hospital all-cause mortality rates. Secondary clinical outcomes included the rates of reinfarction, target lesion revascularization, target vessel revascularization, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), and major bleeding events.1 Long-term survival data were also ascertained for patients where available.

For the OHCA subgroup, the primary outcome of interest for the prognostic model evaluation was in-hospital all-cause mortality. The Cardiac Arrest Hospital Prognosis (CAHP) score was calculated for each patient in the OHCA group using the formula and parameters as originally described by Maupain et al.16 This score incorporates six variables: age (years), initial cardiac rhythm (shockable vs. non-shockable), time from collapse to CPR (minutes), time from CPR to ROSC (minutes), arterial pH on admission, and serum lactate level on admission. Each variable is assigned points, and the total score is calculated. Patients were also categorized into risk groups (low, moderate, high) based on established CAHP score thresholds.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata, version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were employed. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) if non-normally distributed. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using visual inspection of histograms and Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages.

For comparisons between the OHCA and non-OHCA groups, Student’s t-test (for normally distributed continuous variables), the Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed continuous variables), and the chi-squared (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables, as appropriate based on expected cell counts) were utilized. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for these comparisons.

To evaluate the value of the CAHP score for predicting in-hospital mortality specifically within the OHCA subgroup, univariable logistic regression analysis was first performed for the CAHP score and for other potentially relevant clinical and arrest-related variables (variables presented in Table 1, Table 2). Variables demonstrating a P-value < 0.1 in the univariable analysis were then considered for inclusion in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. Age, sex, diabetes, left ventricular ejection fraction, right ventricular dysfunction, severe mitral regurgitation, serum K, creatinine, lactate, pH, HCO3, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, SCAI stage, CAHP score risk categories, duration from arrest to CPR, and duration from CPR to ROSC were included in a stepwise logistic regression with forward selection and a P-value <0.05 was set for inclusion of the variable in the final model. Odds ratios (OR) with their corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to quantify the strength of association. The discriminative ability of the CAHP score and the final multivariable model was assessed by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Univariable logistic regression was performed for the association between hospital mortality and the continuous CAHP score. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were reported. Furthermore, the optimal cut-off point of the CAHP score for predicting mortality was calculated using the Youden index and was reported with its sensitivity and specificity. Complete case analysis was used to handle missing data. Results

OHCA details

A total of 1513 patients were included in the study, of which 138 (9.1 %) experienced an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Among the OHCA cases, 67.39 % presented with a shockable rhythm, and the majority of arrests occurred at home (64.49 %). The median time from collapse to CPR initiation was 4.5 min (IQR: 3–7), and the median duration from CPR to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was 15 min (IQR: 9–23). The mean CAHP score for OHCA patients was 162.03 ± 39.60, with 24.64 % classified as high-risk for poor prognosis (Table 1).

Comparison between patients with and without OHCA

Significant differences were observed between the OHCA and non-OHCA groups. OHCA patients were younger (P < 0.001) and had a lower prevalence of diabetes mellitus (P = 0.014), previous PCI (P = 0.020), and congestive heart failure (P = 0.006). OHCA patients also exhibited higher rates of STEMI (P < 0.001) and left main (LM) involvement (P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences in pH, creatinine, or baseline lactate between patients with and without OHCA (Table 2).

Hospital management and outcomes

Survival rates

Survival rates at 12 and 24 months were comparable between groups, with 21 % and 9 % for non-OHCA patients versus 24 % and 9 % for OHCA patients, respectively (Log-rank P = 0.816) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Kaplan-Meier survival curve comparing survivors of AMI-CS with and without out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

Mortality predictors

The CAHP score was strongly associated with hospital mortality in OHCA patients (OR: 1.067, 95 % CI: 1.04–1.08, P < 0.001). Mortality risk increased markedly for scores above 160, with the highest risk observed at a score of 200 (Fig. 2). The predicted mortality agrees with the CAHP score categories. Mortality occurred in 64.58 % of patients with CAHP score >200 (n = 31), 33.33 % in patients with scores between 150 and 200 (n = 16) and one patient (2.08 %) in patients with score <150. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated excellent discriminative performance of the CAHP score for predicting in-hospital mortality. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.90 (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 0.85–0.96). The CAHP score had a sensitivity of 73 %, 91 % specificity, 81 % positive predictive value, and 86 % negative predictive value. Discussion

This study offers insights into the complex clinical landscape of patients presenting with AMI-CS within the Gulf region. The focus of this research was the detailed characterization and outcome comparison of the particularly vulnerable subgroup of AMI-CS patients who experience an OHCA and survive to hospital presentation. Furthermore, a key objective was the validation of the utility of the CAHP score in accurately predicting in-hospital mortality specifically for these high-risk OHCA patients.

Our findings reveal that 9.1 % of the total AMI-CS cohort experienced an OHCA. A large Danish registry reported 32 % prevalence of OHCA among AMI-CS patients.17 In a national registry analysis of STEMI patients, 1138 out of 23,703 (4.8 %) presented with both CS and OHCA.18 Ostenfield et al., reported that OHCA was not an independent risk factor for hospital mortality in patients with CS who survive to presentation.19 A pivotal observation from our comparative analysis was that OHCA patients who survived to presentation were younger and exhibited a lower prevalence of several significant pre-existing cardiovascular comorbidities. This finding suggests that survival in OHCA may predominantly occur in individuals who possess a relatively healthier baseline cardiovascular profile. This could imply that the OHCA event is often precipitated by a more acute ischemic event rather than representing the terminal progressively worsening chronic cardiac disease. The significantly higher prevalence of STEMI in this group further lends substantial support to this notion of a severe, acute, and extensive ischemic insult as the primary driver of the cardiac arrest. Furthermore, hospital arrival may present a natural selection for healthier patients with cardiogenic shock. The finding of younger age and lower comorbidities was observed by Lauridsen et al., in patients with cardiogenic shock and OHCA.17

A crucial finding of this study was the observation of a statistically significant lower in-hospital mortality rate in the OHCA group with survival to presentation (34.78 %) compared to the non-OHCA AMI-CS group (46.47 %). Several interacting factors might contribute to this unexpected observation in our cohort. The younger age and lower comorbidity burden of the OHCA survival group, as previously highlighted, could inherently predispose them to better short-term survival if they successfully reach the hospital and receive timely intervention. Additionally, it is plausible that patients who survive an OHCA to achieve hospital admission and subsequently undergo coronary angiography represent a selected, more resilient cohort. Rusnak and colleagues reported worse outcomes in patients who had in-hospital cardiac arrest compared to OHCA.20 The median time to CPR initiation of 4.5 min and median time to ROSC of 15 min observed in our OHCA cohort are indicative of reasonably efficient pre-hospital care, which could contribute to this selection bias. Patients with OHCA presented in lower SCAI stage, highlighting the prognostic value of SCAI stage irrespective of the pre-hospital status.21, 22, 23 Pareek and associates reported higher risk of hospital mortality after OHCA in patients with high SCAI stage.22 Tabi and colleagues reported association between mortality and severity of shock stage in patients with OHCA treated by targeted temperature management.24 However, it is crucial to note that the apparent hospital survival benefit did not extend to all critical outcomes. The OHCA group with survival experienced a significantly higher incidence of CVA. Neurological complications are common in patients who achieved ROSC after OHCA and can reach up to 70 %.25

Despite the differences in in-hospital mortality, the long-term survival rates were found to be comparable between the OHCA who survived to presentation and non-OHCA groups. This important finding suggests that while aggressive initial resuscitation and acute in-hospital management may lead to better immediate survival outcomes in a selected subgroup of OHCA patients, the profound underlying severity of the AMI-CS syndrome ultimately dictates the long-term prognosis. This underscores the critical and ongoing need for continued vigilance, comprehensive secondary prevention strategies, and diligent long-term management extending well beyond the acute hospital phase for all survivors of AMI-CS, irrespective of whether they experienced an OHCA. This finding is consistent with the Danish registry, in which OHCA did not influence the long-term survival.17

Several risk scores were used to predict cardiac arrest outcomes.26 The validation of the CAHP score as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in OHCA patients with concomitant AMI-CS is a clinically relevant finding of this study. The CAHP score demonstrated a clear and statistically significant graded association with mortality risk. As the CAHP score increased, the probability of in-hospital death rose substantially. Specifically, our data indicated that a CAHP score above 160, and particularly a score exceeding 200, was associated with a markedly and progressively increased risk of in-hospital death. Paul and colleagues reported strong association between CAHP score and death after resuscitated cardiac arrest.27 A study by Sauneuf and colleagues reported good discriminant ability of CAHP score in predicting neurological complications after OHCA in elderly patients.28 This finding suggests that the CAHP score can serve as a valuable tool for early and effective risk stratification for this critically ill patient population within the Gulf region. The concurrent identification of other independent predictors of in-hospital mortality in our OHCA patients further refines our understanding of the complex array of risk factors in this cohort. These findings also highlight critical areas for potential intervention and quality improvement initiatives, particularly re-emphasizing the absolutely critical importance of rapid bystander response and the delivery of effective, high-quality CPR in the pre-hospital setting.

Strength and limitations

This study possesses several strengths that enhance the validity and relevance of its findings. These include its multicenter design that included a relatively large and contemporary cohort of AMI-CS patients. However, it is equally important to acknowledge certain limitations inherent in the study design and data. The primary limitation is the retrospective observational nature of this analysis. The potential influence of unmeasured or unmeasurable confounders on the observed outcomes cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, despite the use of standardized data collection forms and protocols, some degree of variability in clinical practice patterns and data recording practices across the 13 participating tertiary care centers could have existed and potentially influenced the results. Specific details of pre-hospital care beyond the documented time to CPR and time to ROSC, such as the precise quality of CPR administered, the use of advanced airway management techniques, or specific pharmacological interventions provided by EMS personnel, were not uniformly captured in the registry and could represent important unmeasured factors impacting outcomes. Furthermore, the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have affected the presentation pattern of patients with AMI-CS. Finally, while the CAHP score showed good discriminative performance (AUC 0.90), the positive predictive value of 81 % indicates that the score should not be used in isolation for clinical decisions regarding treatment limitation, as this would inappropriately withhold care from approximately 19 % of patients who could survive to hospital discharge. The score may be more appropriately used as one component of a comprehensive clinical assessment rather than as a standalone decision-making tool.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that AMI-CS patients who survive to hospital presentation after OHCA in the Gulf region possess a distinct profile of baseline characteristics. These patients exhibited lower in-hospital mortality compared to their AMI-CS counterparts without OHCA, although this apparent survival advantage was offset by a higher risk of CVA. The CAHP score demonstrated good discriminative ability for in-hospital mortality in OHCA patients with AMI-CS, but the positive predictive value was 81 %. Further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and to explore the impact of targeted interventions based on CAHP risk stratification.
