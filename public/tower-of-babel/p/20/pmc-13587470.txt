MRI-based perfusion-diffusion habitat analysis for characterizing intratumoral heterogeneity in rectal adenocarcinoma
Authors: Cui Tang, Jingwen Zhang, Jie Kuang, Wenying Mou, Hua Hao, Dongmei Wu, Yongming Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate whether MRI-based perfusion-diffusion habitat imaging derived from dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) can characterize intratumoral heterogeneity and evaluate associations with pathologic stage, synchronous distant metastasis, and exploratory survival outcomes in rectal adenocarcinoma.MethodsThis retrospective study included 73 patients with pathologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma who underwent preoperative DCE-MRI and DWI scans. The volume transfer constant (Ktrans) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps were generated from DCE and DWI data, respectively. After manual tumor segmentation and image co-registration, voxel-wise Ktrans and ADC values were clustered using a k-means algorithm to identify distinct spatial habitats. Quantitative metrics extracted from each habitat were compared across pathologic subgroups and entered into multivariable logistic regression models for T staging and association with synchronous distant metastasis.ResultsThree reproducible habitats were identified: Habitat 1, hyper-vasopermeability; Habitat 2, hypo-vasopermeability with hypo-cellularity; and Habitat 3, hypo-vasopermeability with hyper-cellularity. Compared with T1-2 tumors, T3-4 lesions showed significantly higher mean Ktrans and the extracellular-extravascular volume fraction (Ve) in Habitats 1 and 2 and lower mean ADC in Habitats 2 and 3. Multiple perfusion metrics from Habitat 1 significantly correlated with distant metastasis. The optimized logistic regression models achieved AUCs of 0.81 for T staging and 0.78 for distant metastasis prediction.ConclusionCombined DCE-MRI and DWI habitat imaging enables voxel-level assessment of intratumoral heterogeneity in rectal cancer and may provide a noninvasive imaging biomarker for preoperative T staging and assessment of tumor heterogeneity and systemic disease-associated imaging features.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40644-026-01081-1.

Introduction

Rectal cancer represents a major global health burden, and accurate preoperative assessment remains essential for individualized treatment planning [1, 2]. The Tumor-Node-Metastasis staging system provides the anatomic framework for prognostication and treatment stratification [3]. However, contemporary rectal cancer management is increasingly risk-adapted rather than determined solely by a binary distinction between T1–2 and T3–4 disease [4]. Treatment selection incorporates tumor height, depth of extramural spread and T3 substage, threatened mesorectal fascia or circumferential resection margin, extramural vascular invasion, nodal risk including lateral pelvic nodes, sphincter involvement, and systemic disease status [4–6]. Pathologic nodal status remains one of the strongest prognostic factors in rectal cancer, whereas accurate assessment of distant metastasis is essential because systemic disease substantially changes treatment strategy and may prompt systemic therapy, metastasis-directed therapy, or curative-intent multimodality treatment in selected patients with limited metastatic burden [4, 5]. Although high-resolution MRI plays a central role in rectal cancer staging, conventional anatomic assessment provides limited information about the spatial biological heterogeneity that may underlie synchronous distant metastasis and prognosis [6, 7].

Intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) – the spatial variation in cellularity, angiogenesis, and stromal composition within a tumor – underlies differences in aggressiveness and treatment response [8]. Conventional MRI analyses that rely on single averaged parameters, such as the mean apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) derived from diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), may oversimplify this complexity and obscure clinically relevant subregions [7, 9]. Thus, imaging biomarkers capable of spatially resolving tumor heterogeneity are needed to improve biological characterization and staging accuracy.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI and DWI provide complementary information about tumor microvasculature and cellularity through quantitative parameters such as the volume transfer constant (Ktrans) and ADC [10–12]. While these parameters individually correlate with tumor aggressiveness, their integrated, spatially resolved analysis may be more informative than isolated whole-tumor averages. Habitat imaging – an unsupervised, multiparametric approach that clusters voxels into subregions with distinct pathophysiologic properties (e.g., hyper-permeable or hyper-cellular niches) – has emerged as a noninvasive strategy to quantify ITH [13–19]. Although DCE-MRI is not currently a routine or guideline-supported component of standard rectal cancer staging, it provides quantitative information about tumor perfusion and permeability and may therefore serve as a research tool for characterizing biologically relevant tumor heterogeneity.

In this study, we hypothesize that a habitat-based analysis combining DCE-MRI and DWI could capture spatially distinct perfusion-cellularity phenotypes within rectal adenocarcinoma. Therefore, this study aims to characterize intratumoral heterogeneity using MRI-derived perfusion-diffusion habitats and to explore the associations of habitat-derived metrics with pathologic tumor extension, pathologic nodal status, synchronous distant metastasis, and survival outcomes.

Methods

Participants

The research was conducted ethically in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective study was approved by our institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Between August 2015 and February 2025, patients were retrospectively screened from the institutional rectal MRI database. This imaging-biomarker cohort was restricted to patients with biopsy-proven primary rectal adenocarcinoma who underwent preoperative MRI including both DCE-MRI and DWI at our institution, received no neoadjuvant therapy before MRI, subsequently underwent surgical resection, and had complete pathologic TNM staging available. Because DCE-MRI was not routinely acquired in all patients undergoing rectal MRI, only 85 patients met the initial imaging and clinical eligibility criteria. After exclusion of patients with neoadjuvant therapy before MRI (n = 5), incomplete clinical or pathologic records (n = 3), or poor image quality due to artifacts (n = 4), 73 patients were included in the final analysis. Histopathologic examination and survival evaluation

Following surgical resection, all surgical specimens were fixed in formalin and processed routinely for histopathological examination. Pathologic TNM stage was determined according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [3]. Distant metastasis (M1) was confirmed by imaging (CT or PET-CT) and/or biopsy at the time of initial diagnosis.

The patients were followed up until death or the study cutoff (February 2025). In fact, our study included 39 patients who underwent surgery between August 2015 and December 2019, and 34 patients who underwent surgery between January 2024 and February 2025. 35 patients of the 39 ones had adequate follow-up for OS assessment, and the other 4 were lost to follow-up. The 34 patients were not included into survival analysis because of very short follow-up period. For survival evaluation of the 35 patients, overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up, with the median follow-up time of 42 months, number of death (23) and number of censored patients (12). Survival analysis was performed as an exploratory analysis in patients with adequate long-term follow-up data. Patients without available survival status or follow-up information sufficient for overall survival assessment were excluded from the survival analysis but retained in the cross-sectional staging and metastasis analyses.

MR imaging

All MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0 T scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a 18-channel phased-array body coil after bowel preparation. The standard rectal MRI protocol included T1- (axial, TR = 612 ms, TE = 20 ms, FOV = 320 × 320 mm2, matrix size = 320 × 320, slice thickness = 4 mm, acquisition time=1 min 47 s) and T2-weighted (axial, TR = 1600 ms, TE = 96 ms, FOV = 380 × 297 mm2, matrix size = 320 × 250, slice thickness = 4 mm, acquisition time = 45 s; sagittal, TR = 600 ms, TE = 96 ms, FOV = 350 × 273 mm2, matrix size = 320 × 250, slice thickness = 4 mm, acquisition time = 15 s) images in axial and sagittal planes. DWI was acquired in the axial plane using a single-shot echo-planar imaging sequence with b-values of 50, and 800 s/mm² (TR = 5500 ms, TE = 67 ms, FOV = 230 × 230 mm2, matrix size = 140 × 140, slice thickness = 4 mm, acquisition time = 4 min 15 s). The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps were automatically generated.

For DCE-MRI, a T1-weighted volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) sequence (TR = 3.97 ms, TE = 1.29 ms, FA = 9°, FOV = 380 × 310 mm2, matrix size = 320 × 260, slice thickness = 3 mm) was performed before and after a bolus injection of gadoterate meglumine (0.1 mmol/kg body weight) at a rate of 2.5 ml/s, followed by a 20-ml saline flush. A total of 36 phases and 1728 DCE images were captured with a scanning time of 9 min, corresponding to a temporal resolution of 15 s per phase. The DCE-MRI data were transferred to a dedicated workstation (Syngo, Tissue-4D, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Pharmacokinetic modeling was performed using the Tofts model to derive quantitative maps of the volume transfer constant (Ktrans), extracellular extravascular space fraction (Ve), reflux rate constant (Kep), and initial area under the curve (iAUC). We chose a default population-based AIF and model fitting was performed using the vendor-implemented nonlinear least-squares algorithm with physiologically constrained parameter ranges. Because dedicated pre-contrast T1 mapping was not acquired, baseline T1 handling followed the vendor-default approach, which derives T1 values from the signal intensity of the first pre-contrast dynamic using a fixed T1 value of 1000 ms as the initial estimate, refined through the fitting procedure.

Image co-registration and VOI definition

All image registrations were performed using ANTs v2.4.0. For DCE-MRI motion correction, each dynamic volume was rigidly registered (6 degrees of freedom: 3 translations, 3 rotations) to the first time point using mutual information metric to correct for respiratory motion and patient movement. For cross-modality registration between DWI and DCE-MRI, a two-stage approach was implemented: first, an affine transformation (12 degrees of freedom: rotation, translation, scaling, shearing) with mutual information similarity metric, followed by a SyN diffeomorphic transformation with cross-correlation metric (radius = 4 voxels) to correct for residual local distortions. Because field-map or reversed-phase-encoding data were not available in this retrospective cohort, explicit EPI distortion correction was not performed. We therefore conducted visual quality control for all cases, excluded images with severe distortion or motion artifacts. Regarding ADC map resampling, linear (trilinear) interpolation was used to preserve quantitative accuracy. Registration quality was assessed by visual inspection of tumor boundaries on paired DCE/DWI images and, where feasible, by landmark-based errors in five representative cases.

A radiologist (C.T.) with over 8 years of experience in gastrointestinal imaging manually delineated the entire three-dimensional volume of interest (VOI) of the viable solid component of the primary rectal tumor on each slice of DCE-MRI images. Macroscopic necrosis or cystic regions, obvious vessels, and the intestinal lumen were excluded from quantitative habitat analysis to minimize contamination from nonviable tissue and non-tumoral structures. We performed the reproducibility analysis for VOI delineationin a randomly selected subset of 30 patients. A second radiologist (J.Z.) with five years of experience independently delineated the tumor VOIs, and the original reader repeated the segmentation after an interval of four weeks while blinded to the initial segmentation and clinical outcomes. We calculated Dice similarity coefficients for VOI overlap and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for Ktrans and ADC. The reproducibility analysis showed good to excellent agreement, with Dice coefficients of 0.83 ± 0.05 for interobserver agreement and 0.91 ± 0.04 for intraobserver agreement, ICCs of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.81–0.89) and 0.89 (95% CI: 0.82–0.96) for interobserver and agreement of Ktrans, and ICCs of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82–0.94) and 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88–0.98) for interobserver and agreement of ADC.

Multiparametric spatial habitats and habitat-based metrics

The voxel-wise Ktrans and ADC values within each patient’s tumor VOI were extracted and normalized (z-score) to reduce inter-patient variability. The combined features were subjected to an unsupervised k-means clustering algorithm to identify distinct spatial habitats. Specifically, we tried k values from 2 to 5, calculated the corresponding silhouette score and Calinski-Harabasz index, and evaluated the clustering stability using adjusted rand index and normalized mutual information across 100 repeated random initializations. All these steps were performed using the CVIK toolbox in Matlab (R2022b; MathWorks, Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA). The clustering results of different k values were showed in supplementary Table S1. A cluster number of 3 was selected because of its high silhouette score and Calinski-Harabasz index, and also because it provided biologically interpretable compartments while maintaining stable cluster assignment and avoiding fragmentation into small, less interpretable subregions [20].

Three habitats were thereby defined: Habitat 1, hyper-vasopermeability (highest Ktrans); Habitat 2, hypo-vasopermeability and hypo-cellularity (higher ADC between the remaining two clusters); Habitat 3, hypo-vasopermeability and hyper-cellularity habitat (the remaining cluster). For each habitat, the mean values of Ktrans, Kep, Ve, iAUC, and ADC and the volume fraction (%) were extracted for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis and survival analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between different pathologic stage groups (e.g., T1-2 vs. T3-4, N0 vs. N1-2 and M0 vs. M1) using t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as counts and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Habitat-based metrics that showed significant differences in group comparison were entered into multivariable logistic regression analysis (with a backward selection method) and 5-fold cross-validation for predicting advanced T-stage (T3-4), and lymphatic (N1-2) and distant metastasis (M1), respectively. The performance of these models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparison.

To further investigate the prognostic value of significant habitat-based metrics, we separately divided all patients into two subgroups using the median of each metric. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to identify habitat-based metrics associated with OS, and log-rank tests were used to assess the significance. Cox proportional hazards regression was conducted to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for paired subgroups. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox model was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, with a global p > 0.05 indicating no violation.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 73 patients with pathologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma were finally included in the study (52 men, 21 women; mean age, 64.8 ± 12.0 years). According to the 8th AJCC TNM staging system [3], 23 patients (31.5%) were classified into the T1-2 group, while 50 patients (68.5%) were in the T3-4 group. Regarding nodal status, 38 patients (52.1%) were N0, and 35 patients (47.9%) were N1-2. Distant metastasis (M1) was identified in 18 patients (24.7%) at the time of diagnosis. Long-term follow-up data sufficient for exploratory survival analysis were available in 35 of 73 patients. In this subset, the mean overall survival was 52.03 ± 41.94 months. Habitat identification and group comparisons

Unsupervised clustering of normalized Ktrans and ADC values yielded three distinct intratumoral habitats across all patients: Habitat 1, hyper-vasopermeability; Habitat 2, hypo-vasopermeability and hypo-cellularity; and Habitat 3, hypo-vasopermeability and hyper-cellularity. Figures 3 and 4 illustrates four representative cases of different TNM stages (T1N0M0, T2N1M0, T3N2M0 and T4N2M1), respectively. With advanced stage, Ktrans distributions seem to shift rightward in the whole tumor and Habitats 1–2, whereas ADC distributions shift leftward in Habitats 2–3, consistent with increased permeability and restricted diffusion in less-vascular compartments (Fig. 4).

Diagnostic performance of habitat-based metrics

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of habitat-based and whole-tumor metrics for pathologic T category and synchronous M1 disease (Tables 2 and 3, supplementary Table S2 and S3). Using DeLong’s test, most of significant habitat-based metrics showed higher predictive value than whole-tumor ones. Multivariable logistic regression combining significant parameters yielded AUCs of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.72–0.91) for differentiating T3-4 from T1–2 tumors (Fig. 5a) and 0.78 (95% CI: 0.66–0.89) for identifying synchronous M1 disease (Fig. 5b). The detail of models was showed in supplementary Table S4. Across the 5-fold cross-validation, the mean AUC was 0.78 ± 0.05 (range: 0.72–0.83), with a mean sensitivity of 64.3 ± 4.5% and mean specificity of 86.2 ± 6.1% for pathologic T category, and the mean AUC was 0.74 ± 0.04 (range: 0.70–0.81), with a mean sensitivity of 80.1 ± 5.5% and mean specificity of 61.4 ± 3.8% for distant metastasis.

Prognostic value of tumor habitats

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis among patient with > = 5 years of follow-up identified two habitat-based metrics significantly associated with OS. Patients with higher mean Ktrans value in Habitat 2 (> 0.22 min− 1) had significantly short OS than those below this threthold (log-rank P = 0.005; Fig. 6a). Likewise, patients with lower mean ADC value in Habitat 2 (< 1.40 × 10− 3 mm²/s) showed significantly inferior OS (log-rank P = 0.013; Fig. 6b). Cox proportional hazards regression analysis confirmed these findings. These two metrics were also independent predictors of poor prognosis, with HRs of 3.14 (95% CI: 1.35–7.31, P = 0.015) and 0.37 (95% CI: 0.16–0.87, P = 0.042) for OS, after adjusting for sex, age and TNM stages. Using Schoenfeld residuals, no violation was detected for the two metrics (global P = 0.32 for mean Ktrans value in Habitat 2 and P = 0.24 for mean ADC value in Habitat 2).

Discussion

This exploratory study successfully implemented a multiparametric habitat imaging framework based on DCE-MRI and DWI to non-invasively characterize ITH in rectal cancer. Three biologically plausible habitats – distinguished by vascular permeability and cellularity – were identified. Habitat-derived metrics, rather than whole-tumor averages, were associated with pathologic T stage and distant metastasis and predicted overall survival. The habitat-based multivariable models achieved promising performance in preoperatively differentiating T3-4 from T1-2 tumors (AUC = 0.81) and predicting distant metastasis (AUC = 0.78). These findings suggest that habitat imaging may provide quantitative information about tumor heterogeneity and systemic disease-associated biology, but its clinical value still requires validation against established rectal cancer biomarkers.

Habitat analysis improves biological characterization by moving beyond whole-tumor averaging that masks aggressive subregions. In this study, none of the whole-tumor parameters (mean Ktrans, Kep, Ve, iAUC, or ADC) differed significant across pathologic stages, underscoring the limitations of conventional averaging in reflecting tumor biology. Tumor behavior is spatially heterogeneous, with aggressive phenotypes often confined to specific subregions [21]. By resolving the tumor into its distinct habitats – specifically, a hyper- vasopermeability region (Habitat 1), and two hypo-vasopermeability regions with varying cellularity (Habitats 2 and 3) – our method captures this critical spatial complexity. Similarly, Lee et al. identified an increased hypovascular cellular habitat being associated with the risk of recurrence (i.e., treatment resistance) and indicative of recurrence site after stereotactic radiosurgery for brain metastasis [15]. Besides, Chen et al. developed ITH signatures based on habitat-based MRI radiomics to differentiate HER2-positive from HER2-negative breast tumors, and further distinguish HER2-low from HER2-zero phenotypes [22]. Thus, these studies and the present results highlight that habitat analysis provides a more nuanced and biologically grounded framework for visualizing and quantifying tumor microenvironmental diversity, which is otherwise averaged out in conventional analysis [7, 8].

Advanced T-stage (T3-4) was associated with a distinct habitat signature characterized by higher extracellular volume (Ve) in the hyper-vasopermeability habitat (Habitat 1) and increased permeability (Ktrans) in the hypo-vasopermeability, hypo-cellularity habitat (Habitat 2), and restricted diffusion (low ADC) in both hypo-vasopermeability habitats (Habitat 2 and 3). Elevated Ve in Habitat 1 may reflect expansion of the extravascular-extracellular space and increased matrix permeability, a hallmarks of invasive tumor environment [23–25]. The rise in Ktrans within Habitat 2 and diffusional restriction across both hypo-vasopermeability habitats suggest that locally advanced tumors exhibit more aberrant perfusion and compact cellular architecture extending beyond the most vascular niche. According to the Tofts model, Ktrans can be affected by blood flow, vascular permeability, and microvascular surface area, while Kep (Ktrans/Ve) reflects contrast efflux from extracellular space to plasma and depends on both permeability and extracellular volume [26], which might explain the lack of significant difference in Kep between T-stage groups. Furthermore, the moderate diagnostic performance of ADC-based ROC models from Habitats 2 and 3 indicates that diffusion characteristics in less vascularized compartments are particularly informative for assessing local invasion.

Perfusion metrics within Habitat 1, including Ktrans, Kep, and iAUC, were significantly higher in patients with distant metastasis. This finding supports the concept that hyper-vascular niches, which are metabolically active and angiogenic, may harbor aggressive subclones with greater synchronous distant metastasis. Elevated perfusion in this niche may facilitate intravasation, a critical step in the metastatic cascade [12]. However, mean whole tumor DCE-MRI metrics showed no significant differences between patients with and without distant metastasis, contrasting with the findings by Yu et al. [27]. Differences in cohort composition (e.g., prevalence of M1 disease), imaging protocols, kinetic modeling, and confirmation criteria for metastasis (e.g., minimum follow-up duration) may account for this discrepancy. Therefore, our results suggest that the functional status of specific habitats, rather than global tumor perfusion, may serve as an early imaging marker of metastatic propensity. This aligns with the biological understanding that metastasis is driven by specific subpopulations within the primary tumor. The ability to identify these “metastasis-prone” habitats preoperatively could have profound implications for risk stratification and the intensification of systemic therapy in selected patients.

The prognostic analysis further validates the clinical relevance of habitat imaging. In recent years, habitat analysis has been widely used in the field of tumor imaging, especially on tumor prognosis assessment [28–31]. Cho et al. identified a habitat risk score to be an independent risk factor for predicting worse disease-free survival in breast cancer [29]. In a hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) study, habitat imaging of the tumor and its peritumoral microenvironment could predict the recurrence-free survival of HCC (< = 5 cm) [31]. Moreover, Verma et al. demonstrated that habitat-derived radiomics features stratified glioblastoma patients by progression-free survival [30]. In our study, the finding that higher Ktrans and lower ADC in Habitat 2 were independent predictors of worse overall survival, even after adjusting for TNM stage, is particularly noteworthy. This implies that the biological aggressiveness encoded in these specific habitats—manifested as aberrant permeability in a quiescent zone and high cellularity—confers a worse prognosis that is not fully captured by the anatomic TNM stage alone. This habitat-derived information could potentially refine postoperative follow-up strategies and aid in identifying patients who might benefit from more aggressive adjuvant therapy.

The clinical translation of DCE-MRI-based habitat imaging requires cautious consideration. Contemporary rectal MRI practice primarily relies on high-resolution T2-weighted imaging and DWI for local staging, whereas contrast-enhanced DCE-MRI is not routinely recommended for standard staging. Therefore, the present DCE-MRI-based habitat framework should be interpreted as an exploratory quantitative imaging biomarker rather than a currently established clinical staging tool. Practical barriers include additional scan time, contrast exposure, cost, protocol variability, post-processing complexity, dependence on arterial input function selection and pharmacokinetic modeling, and limited standardization across scanners and institutions.

Several limitations of this study warrant mention. First, this retrospective, single-center study included a selected imaging-biomarker cohort restricted to patients with available preoperative DCE-MRI/DWI, primary surgery, and complete pathologic staging; therefore, it may not represent all patients with rectal adenocarcinoma, particularly those who receive neoadjuvant therapy. Although this design preserved untreated pathologic staging as the reference standard, it limits the applicability of our findings to clinically high-risk patients managed with neoadjuvant treatment. In addition, DCE-MRI-based habitat analysis is not part of routine guideline-supported rectal cancer staging, and its clinical translation is limited by additional scan time, contrast exposure, protocol variability, post-processing complexity, and limited standardization of pharmacokinetic modeling. Prognostic analysis was exploratory and limited to 35 patients with adequate long-term follow-up. Larger prospective multicenter cohorts are needed for validation. Second, clustering was performed using only Ktrans and ADC with several cluster numbers3. Future studies should investigate alternative clustering strategies (e.g., consensus, hierarchical, or fuzzy c-means clustering) to refine habitat characterization. Third, because the quantitative habitat analysis focused on the viable solid tumor component, macroscopic necrotic or cystic regions were excluded. Although this approach improves the interpretability of DCE-MRI and ADC-derived habitat metrics, it may underestimate whole-tumor heterogeneity because necrosis itself represents a biologically relevant component of tumor aggressiveness. Fourth, the limited number of distant metastasis (n = 18) resulted in a low predictors relative to events (EPV) of 6, raising the possibility of model overfitting. Our models requires external validation in larger independent cohorts before clinical implementation. Future studies should incorporate bootstrap optimism correction, calibration assessment, and decision curve analysis to further evaluate clinical utility. Finally, the biological interpretation of MRI-defined habitats remains inferential because spatially co-registered histopathology was not available. Future studies should validate these habitats using MRI–whole-mount histopathology co-registration and immunohistochemical markers of microvessel density, tumor cellularity, hypoxia, fibrosis, and extracellular matrix composition.

In conclusion, habitat imaging based on combined DCE-MRI and DWI enables voxel-level quantification of intratumoral heterogeneity in rectal adenocarcinoma. By identifying physiologically distinct sub-regions, particularly hyper-vasopermeable, hypo-cellular, and hyper-cellular compartments, this approach revealed tumor subregional features associated with pathologic T category, synchronous distant metastasis, and exploratory survival outcomes, whereas whole-tumor average metrics showed limited discriminatory value in this cohort. These findings suggest that habitat imaging may provide complementary biological information for preoperative tumor assessment. Larger prospective studies are needed to validate its reproducibility and determine its incremental value beyond established clinical, radiologic, and pathological risk factors.
