BCOR‐Mutated Conventional and Dedifferentiated Chondrosarcoma: A Clinicopathologic Study
Authors: Diego M. Montoya‐Cerrillo, Mark G. Evans, Andrew Elliott, Renzo Calderon Anyosa, Jaylou Velez Torres, Elizabeth A. Montgomery, Francis J. Hornicek, H. Thomas Temple, Brooke Crawford, Jonathan Trent, Emily E. Jonczak, Gina D'Amato, Andrew E. Rosenberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConventional and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma encompass a group of malignant neoplasms that produce cartilaginous matrix and arise within or on the surface of bone. Conventional chondrosarcomas are graded on a three‐tiered scale, whereas dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma is typically not graded but is considered a high‐grade sarcoma and represents the most aggressive subtype with a poor prognosis. IDH1 (isocitrate dehydrogenase‐1) and I DH2 (isocitrate dehydrogenase‐2) are the most commonly mutated genes in conventional and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma, followed in frequency by COL2A1 and TP53. IDH1/2 driver mutations are also commonly found in enchondroma, considered a benign precursor lesion of chondrosarcoma, and other malignancies such as gliomas, cholangiocarcinoma, and acute myeloid leukemia. In acute myeloid leukemia, the presence of concurrent BCOR (BCL‐6 corepressor) loss‐of‐function mutations has been linked to disease relapse and resistance to treatment with IDH inhibitors. After identifying an index case of conventional chondrosarcoma with unusually aggressive clinical evolution, we investigated the clinicopathological features of 12 cases of BCOR‐mutated conventional and dedifferentiated chondrosarcomas against a control group of 15 BCOR‐wildtype (WT) cases to determine whether BCOR‐mutated tumors had patterns of biological progression different from tumors with intact BCOR. All identified BCOR alterations led to loss‐of‐function by either missense or nonsense mutations. The prevalence of BCOR mutations occurred in 5% of conventional and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma, and these were associated with larger tumor size (p = 0.024), metastasis at the time of diagnosis (p ≤ 0.001) and higher T category (3–4 vs. 1–2) (p = 0.009). Although larger studies are necessary to clarify the full impact of BCOR mutations on patients with conventional and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma, our data indicate that BCOR genetic aberrations are associated with adverse clinical features.

Introduction

Chondrosarcoma (CSA) is a group of primary malignant bone tumors that produce neoplastic cartilage matrix and arise in the medullary cavity or surface of bones. The vast majority are the conventional (CCSA) and dedifferentiated (DCSA) types and originate in the long bones and flat bones of the pelvis and trunk. IDH1 and IDH2, the genes coding for the enzymes isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 and 2, are the most commonly mutated genes in CCSA and DCSA. Recurrent hotspot mutations involving the arginine residue of IDH1 (p.R132) and IDH2 (p.R140/p.R.172) have been identified in 50%–80% of CCSA and DCSA [1, 2, 3].

CCSA is graded on a three‐tiered scale; DCSA is often not graded but is considered a high‐grade sarcoma and is the most aggressive type of CSA. CCSA and DDCSA are often treated with surgical resection initially and are considered intrinsically resistant to conventional chemotherapy. The metastatic rates of Grades I and II conventional CSA are 1.2%–8% and 10%–30%, respectively, and the 10‐year survival rates are 79%–100% and 53%–90%, respectively [4, 5]. DCSA has a metastatic rate of 8%–40% [6] and a 5‐year survival rate of < 30% [5]. The presence of metastases at the time of diagnosis significantly lowers the median and overall survival to 5 months and 2 years, respectively [7]. Age, tumor grade, size, and location are traditional well‐established prognostic factors [8]. Recently, drugs targeting mutant IDH1 and 2 have been developed and are promising forms of molecular therapy [9, 10].

IDH1 and IDH2 alterations are mutually exclusive, and their prognostic significance is controversial. The same mutations of IDH1/2 have been reported in the majority of enchondromas (> 80%) and in other non‐chondrogenic malignancies, such as AML, gliomas, and cholangiocarcinoma with a prevalence of 10%–20%, > 70%, and 20%, respectively [11, 12, 13, 14].

Under normal conditions, IDH enzymes are part of the tricarboxylic acid cycle catabolizing isocitrate to α‐ketoglutarate (α‐KG). When mutated, the enzymes lose their normal function and acquire the capability to produce δ‐2‐hydroxyglutarate (2HG), an oncometabolite that accumulates and, due to its structural similarity, competitively inhibits and functionally inhibits α‐KG‐dependent enzymes [15]. The disrupted activity of this large group of enzymes impacts epigenetic regulation, growth signaling, migration, and DNA repair that leads to an abnormal epigenetic status and oxidative stress due to a reduced capacity to regenerate NADPH [16, 17, 18]. Accordingly, tumor progression and higher histological grades have been statistically associated with a hypermethylated genome in CSA patients [19].

Acquired BCOR mutations have been reported in IDH‐driven AML cases, with recent data suggesting that it is one of the driver genes for leukemia stemness. BCOR mutations are frequently identified in tumors at the time of relapse and are possibly associated with disease progression and resistance to IDH inhibitors [20, 21].

The significance of BCOR mutations in the biological progression of CCSA and DCSA has not yet been defined. Materials and Methods

We retrospectively queried the database of all CSA cases submitted to a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)‐certified laboratory (Caris Life Sciences, Phoenix, AZ) with available next‐generation sequencing (NGS) results to identify those with BCOR mutations. Subtypes other than CCSA and DCSA were excluded. Fifteen in‐house cases from the University of Miami with available NGS results, also performed by Caris Life Sciences, were used as the group control. The digitalized histological slides from all BCOR‐mutated CSA and control cases were reviewed by the main author (D.M.M‐C.) and senior author (A.E.R.), and the tumors were classified and graded. For statistical analysis, we considered cases arising in the flat bones (pelvis, scapula, and skull bones) as part of the axial skeleton. Immunohistochemistry for BCOR (C‐10; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) was performed on four in‐house BCOR‐mutated CCSA. The T category of the Pathologic Stage Classification (pTNM) was assigned following the 8th edition TNM staging system for bone tumors of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). Given the differences of T categories depending on the site of origin (i.e., Pelvic tumors staging include subgroups that are not considered for appendicular tumors) when performing the statistical analysis, the T value was simplified as T1, T2, T3, and T4. Linear regression was used to assess mean differences, while logistic regression was used to evaluate differences in proportions. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (Version 4.4.0).

Results

Two hundred thirty‐one cases of CCSA and DCSA with available NGS results (RNA sequencing, DNA sequencing or both) were identified. Twelve cases harbored pathogenic or likely pathogenic BCOR mutations. Five of these 13 cases originated from the University of Miami. One case was excluded based on the histological features and the presence of a FN1::ACVR2A fusion—this tumor was reclassified as a secondary CSA arising from synovial chondromatosis. The overall prevalence of BCOR mutations in CCSA and DCSA was 5% (12/231). Seven tumors were CCSA (58%) and five tumors were DCSA (42%) (Table 1). Of these 12 cases, only two (17%) had standalone BCOR mutations and nine cases (75%) harbored one or more classical driver mutations (i.e., IDH1, IDH2, COL2A1, and/or TP53) (Table 2). The BCOR mutations included six frameshift mutations and six nonsense mutations. The age range of the patients was 38–74 years (mean 55), and the male‐to‐female ratio was 3. Six CCSA were Grade II, one tumor was Grade III, and five cases were DCSA. Clinical information regarding anatomical location and the presence of metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis was available in 11 and nine cases, respectively. Eight tumors (73%) involved the axial skeleton and three tumors arose from the appendicular skeleton. Seven of nine tumors (78%) had metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis. The mean tumor size was 11.7 cm. The pT stage was available for 9 of 12 cases; one case was pT1, three cases were pT2, four cases were pT3, and one case was pT4. All of the 15 University of Miami BCOR‐WT control group tumors harbored classical driver mutations (IDH1/2, COL2A1, TP53). The age range was 38–79 years (mean 55) and the male‐to‐female ratio was 0.4. Two tumors were Grade I, seven were Grade II, one was Grade III, and five were dedifferentiated. Nine cases involved the axial skeleton (60%) and six the appendicular skeleton (40%). Four out of 15 cases (27%), all of them DCSA, had metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis. The tumor size was available in 12 cases of the control group, and the mean tumor size was 7.8 cm. The pT stage was available for 12 of 15 cases; five cases were pT1, five cases were pT2, and two cases were pT4.

Discussion

Approximately 75% of CSA are of the conventional type, while only 10% are DCSA. In these tumors, activating mutations of IDH1 and IDH2 are the most common genetic driver events followed by inactivating mutations of COL2A1 [1, 22, 23]. Mutations in IDH1/2 result in a hypermethylated genome; BCOR is one of the most commonly affected genes [24]. Currently, genetic alterations associated with aggressive biological behavior in these tumors have not been well described.

In our study, we found that BCOR mutations were associated with larger tumor size and higher incidence of metastases at the time of diagnosis. Using cBioPortal, we included all pan‐cancer and nonuterine sarcoma studies, 10 and 16, respectively, and queried for BCOR alterations, obtaining seven cases of CCSA and DCSA. Five of these mutations are truncating by either disruption of the reading frame (missense) or gaining a stop codon (nonsense) mutations. None of the cases had standalone BCOR mutations, and two had concurrent mutations of IDH2; the two cases had gene fusions between BCOR and ZC3H7B or DDX3X, and both fusions lead to loss of function of the protein [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Additional cases of BCOR‐mutant CSA have been published, but no details about their histology or course of disease were reported [31, 32]. Therefore, our study is the first to establish the prognostic significance of the presence of BCOR mutations in CCSA and DCSA. After analyzing a large group of cases, we identified a low prevalence of 5% for pathogenic/likely pathogenic BCOR mutations in CCSA and DCSA. The prevalence of BCORL1 (BCL6 corepressor‐like 1) mutations was also 5%, but none of these alterations could be classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic, and for this reason, these cases did not undergo statistical analysis. Coincidentally, a similar prevalence of BCOR and BCORL1 mutations was found in a large cohort of AML cases, 4.6% and 3.5%, respectively; however, in this group of tumors, the BCORL1 mutations were pathogenic, and 23% had co‐occurring BCOR mutations [33].

BCOR gene is a tumor suppressor gene located on the short arm of chromosome Xp11.4. Its homologue gene, BCORL1, is located on chromosome Xq26.1. BCOR and BCORL1 protein are highly expressed in embryonic stem cells and are core proteins of the polycomb repressive complex (PRC1). BCOR forms part of the polycomb group RING finger 1 (PGCF1) protein that, along with RING1A/B protein, creates the core of the PRC1. The key function of PRC1 is to ubiquitinate histone H2A to modulate gene expression, particularly repression. As an integral part of PRC1, BCOR is an important player in cellular differentiation and maintenance of cell identity [34].

All BCOR mutations observed in our cohort were either missense or nonsense mutations that lead to loss of function of the protein. The high frequency of co‐occurring mutations that are known driver mutations in CSA, such as IDH1/2, COL2A1, and TP53 in nine out of 12 cases, suggests that, in most cases, BCOR mutations are a consequence rather than an initial event in the oncogenesis of CCSA and DCSA. However, like what is encountered in fusion‐negative rhabdomyosarcomas, loss‐of‐function alterations of BCOR infrequently can act as genetic drivers [35].

Immunohistochemistry for BCOR performed on four BCOR‐mutant in‐house cases was uniformly negative. This finding contrasts with the immunohistochemical staining typically observed in other sarcomas characterized by activating BCOR alterations, such as BCOR‐fusion or internal tandem duplication (ITD)‐driven sarcomas, where BCOR expression is usually amplified and consistently detectable by immunohistochemistry. The negative staining observed in our cases likely reflects the biological consequences of the mutations identified, which were exclusively loss‐of‐function alterations—frameshift or nonsense mutations—predicted to disrupt or truncate the BCOR protein. Although direct studies evaluating BCOR immunohistochemistry with loss‐of‐function mutations are limited, analogous findings from hematologic malignancies indicate that loss‐of‐function mutations similarly result in reduced or absent BCOR protein expression [33, 36].

In other IDH‐driven neoplasms, such as AML, the presence of loss‐of‐function BCOR mutations is an independent marker of adverse outcome and is believed to contribute to chemotherapy resistance [33, 37]. Similarly, we found a statistically significant association between the loss‐of‐function BCOR mutations and variables associated with worse clinical outcome, including larger tumor size and the presence of metastasis at diagnosis. Recent data from AML studies suggest that BCOR mutations can also play a role in acquired resistance to IDH inhibition (IDHi), which is a systemic therapeutic option for CCSA and DCSA [20, 21]. In our study, only patient #2, whose BCOR‐mutant DCSA is currently under adjuvant treatment with ivosidenib, is free of recurrent or metastatic disease 24 months after surgery with negative margins and chemotherapy.

Interestingly, our cohort did not include any grade I CCSA, which could represent either a selection bias as few grade I CSA undergo NGS, or evidence of the relationship between the presence of the loss‐of‐function of BCOR and a more aggressive phenotype. Our cases may also be subject to referral bias as more aggressive and unresponsive tumors may be selected for molecular testing and referral to our center. This possibility is supported by the difference in the frequency of BCOR genetic alterations in our study versus the one identified during our search of cBioPortal, which included 361 cases of CCSA and DCSA with a frequency of 5% and 1.9%, respectively [25, 26, 27].

The conflicting data regarding the prognostic significance of IDH mutations and the identification of genetic alterations with new prognostic value in CCSA and DCSA, such as TERT [38], indicate that the type of IDH mutation alone does not provide adequate information to predict biological behavior [39]. In our study, all four cases with alterations of TERT had concurrent IDH1 mutations and were dedifferentiated tumors.

Copy number alterations of p16/CDKN2a have been linked to the progression of IDH1 mutant‐CCSA and are present in 30% of Grade II CSA, 60% of Grade III CSA, and 90% of DDCSA [40]. In our cohort, we identified only one case with a variant of unknown significance (CDKN2A p.I49T). One study suggested that this variant causes loss of function of the CDKN2A protein [41]. However, this variant is also considered a high‐frequency variant found primarily in the Hispanic population; this patient was Hispanic [42].

In summary, this is the first study to establish the biological significance of BCOR mutations in CCSA and DCSA. We identified that these are infrequent genetic alterations in these subtypes of CSA, usually co‐occurring with other known driver mutations, and are either frameshift or nonsense mutations without ITDs or rearrangements, as occur in other sarcoma types. When present, BCOR mutations are statistically associated with a larger tumor size, presence of metastasis at diagnosis, and higher pT category; while these findings suggest a potential link between BCOR mutations and more aggressive clinical behavior, we acknowledge that our study is limited by the lack of survival data, which precludes a direct assessment of their impact on overall or relapse‐free survival. Future studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal follow‐up will be necessary to confirm these observations and to determine the prognostic and therapeutic implications of BCOR mutations, including their potential role in resistance to IDH inhibitor therapy.
