Diagnostic accuracy of mid-upper arm circumference-for-age Z-score (MUACZ) for detecting malnutrition in children with cerebral palsy: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gülümse Oruçoğlu Başka, Hülya Kayılıoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition is common in children with cerebral palsy (CP) due to feeding difficulties, altered body composition, and functional limitations, making an accurate nutritional assessment essential. The mid-upper arm circumference-for-age Z-score (MUACZ) is a practical screening tool, yet its diagnostic performance across childhood and adolescence remains insufficiently defined. The objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of undernutrition in children suffering from CP as well as the diagnostic performance of MUACZ in comparison with other anthropometric indicators across different age groups.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 81 children with CP aged 2–18 years. Nutritional status was assessed using BMI-for-age (BAZ), height-for-age (HAZ), and MUACZ. Functional severity was classified using validated systems (GMFCS and EDACS). The diagnostic accuracy of MUACZ was evaluated with ROC analysis, and predictors of undernutrition were identified using multivariate logistic regression.ResultsUndernutrition (BAZ or HAZ ≤ − 2) affected 63% of the cohort, and its prevalence increased markedly with functional severity, reaching 88.6% in GMFCS level V compared with 10% in level I (p < 0.001). MUACZ showed fair accuracy for detecting wasting (AUC = 0.757), with its strongest performance observed in children aged 5–10 years (AUC = 0.866). Older age and higher GMFCS levels were independent predictors of undernutrition.ConclusionMalnutrition in CP is closely linked to functional severity. MUACZ is simple, feasible, and may serve as a supportive screening tool to help identify undernutrition in children with CP.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07351695 (Retrospectively registered).

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is the most frequent chronic neurological disorder in childhood, and it is defined as permanent, non-progressive disturbances of movement and posture, resulting from damage to the developing brain [1]. Its prevalence has ranged between 1.5 and 4.4 per 1,000 live births worldwide without apparent changes throughout recent decades, despite improvements in perinatal care [2–4].

Malnutrition is one of the most common and clinically significant comorbidities in children with CP [3, 5]. The combination of neurological dysfunction, feeding challenges, altered body composition, and reduced energy expenditure are the causes of the malnutrition [6, 7]. Feeding and swallowing difficulties, oropharyngeal dysphagia, and gastrointestinal dysfunction (including gastroesophageal reflux) are commonly observed in this population [8]. These not only affect growth but also worsen motor disability, make them more prone to infections, and adversely impact their quality of life [7].

Traditional anthropometric measures like body mass index (BMI) may not reliably reflect nutritional status in children with CP due to the reduced mass and altered distribution of fat that can distort the standard indices [9, 10]. Thus, there is growing interest in alternative and complementary methods for assessment. Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is non-invasive, inexpensive, and easy to conduct and has thus been a reliable anthropometric parameter in large pediatric populations for nutritional screening [11]. Recent national data also supported the clinical utility of MUAC-for-age Z-score (MUACZ) bands for diagnosis of childhood malnutrition across different age groups [12, 13].

In neurologically damaged children, particularly those with CP, MUAC may be more indicative of the reserves of muscle and fat stores than are BMI or weight-related indices [11]. Previous studies have already demonstrated strong correlations between MUAC and other nutritional indicators, suggesting its possible role in early identification and follow-up of malnutrition [11–14].

However, despite a recent multicenter Turkish study reported that MUACZ identified a high proportion of malnutrition and showed strong agreement with other anthropometric indicators [12], comprehensive nationally standardized data on nutritional assessment in Turkish pediatric CP population remain limited. Moreover, functional classification systems, such as the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) and the Eating and Drinking Ability Classification System (EDACS), which can potentially reflect feeding safety and efficiency, are rarely applied in combination with anthropometric assessment in this setting.

We hypothesized that MUACZ would demonstrate good diagnostic accuracy for detection of malnutrition in children with CP. The main purpose of this study was therefore to determine the diagnostic performance of MUACZ in detecting malnutrition using previously established cutoffs [15]. Secondary endpoints were (1) to determine the nutritional status of CP-diagnosed children; (2) to evaluate the relationship of nutritional indices with feeding characteristics, postural position, and functional motor levels; and (3) to determine factors related to malnutrition in CP populations.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was carried out at the Pediatric Neurology clinic of a tertiary hospital in Muğla, Türkiye. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No: 14/V, dated 26.07.2023), and it was performed in alignment with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants prior to their inclusion.

Participants

Sample size was calculated in a diagnostic-accuracy power analysis based on the assumption of a malnutrition prevalence of 40%, an expected sensitivity and specificity of around 75% for MUACZ, using a two-sided α of 0.05, 80% power with an allowable absolute precision of ± 15%. This suggested that 80 children would be the minimum required. For the present study, all patients presenting to the outpatient clinic from October 2023 until January 2025 were evaluated. In this regard, the study population involved children between 2 and 18 years old with confirmed CP, according to the definition by the Surveillance of Cerebral Palsy in Europe criteria. All consecutive patients attending the clinic during the period of study who fulfilled the selection criteria for study inclusion were invited to participate. Those with coexisting genetic syndromes, progressive metabolic or neurodegenerative disorders, and those with an acute infectious illness at the time of assessment were excluded from consideration. Overall, 84 children were recruited in an initial cohort. Three subjects were excluded in the final analyses due to incomplete essential data; thus, 81 subjects formed the final sample.

Data collection and variables

The researchers collected data on a standardized case report form. The sociodemographic information included the age and gender of the patients. Clinical information, such as CP subclassification (spastic quadriplegia, spastic diplegia, spastic hemiplegia), co-morbid conditions like epilepsy, and feeding characteristics (the person responsible for feeding, feeding position, feeding method), was gathered from the medical records and from interviews with the parents. Feeding modes were classified into exclusively oral, partially oral, where oral feeding is supplemented with enteral nutrition, or exclusively enteral, including feeding by both nasogastric and gastrostomy tubes.

Functional classification

Functional status was systematically classified with two internationally recognized and validated CP classification systems: GMFCS, which classifies a child’s self-initiated movement and use of assistive mobility devices. It ranges from level I, representing the highest level of motor function, where a person walks without limitations, to level V, indicating a severe limitation, where the individual is transported in a manual wheelchair [16]. EDACS describes the safety and efficiency of eating and drinking in individuals with CP across five levels. Levels range from I, reflecting safe and efficient eating and drinking, to level V, indicating inability to safely eat or drink, thereby requiring tube feeding [17].

Anthropometric measurements

All the anthropometric measurements were carried out by the authors according to the technique described by the World Health Organization (WHO). Height (cm) and weight (kg) were measured using an automatic height-weight-BMI device (GL-150, Densi, Bursa, Türkiye), with a precision of 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively. In children who could not, due to contractures or deformities of the spine, segmental lengths such as tibial length were used to estimate height, as previously described by Stevenson et al. [18]. The intra-observer reliability was measured by randomly selecting a subsample of 10 participants and repeating all anthropometric measurements by the same researcher within 24 h. The measurements showed excellent consistency, with intraclass correlation coefficients exceeding 0.98 for weight, height and MUACZ measurements.

MUAC was measured on the left arm at the midpoint between the acromion process of the scapula and the olecranon process of the ulna, using a flexible, non-stretchable insertion tape. The measurement was taken twice, and the average was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. MUACZ was measured using the Abbott MUACZ tape (Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, USA), which is specifically designed and validated for use in children up to 18 years of age. This selection was based on the updated MUAC reference charts developed by Abdel-Rahman and colleagues, who used age- and sex-specific lambda, mu, sigma (LMS) values allowing precise MUACZ calculation across the full pediatric age range [15]. Recent studies further validated the applicability of MUACZ as a reliable determinant of nutritional status and examined appropriate Z-score thresholds for classifying malnutrition in children, using mentioned LMS values [18].

Definition of malnutrition

Nutritional status was assessed using Z-scores for weight-for-age (WAZ), height-for-age (HAZ), and body mass index-for-age (BAZ), which were calculated using WHO AnthroPlus version 1.0.4 based on WHO growth references [19]. HAZ and BAZ were preferred since they can be used across all age groups from 0 to 18 years. Wasting, indicating acute malnutrition, was considered to be present when BAZ ≤ − 2, while stunting, representing chronic malnutrition, was determined when HAZ ≤ − 2. Undernutrition was determined by the presence of either wasting (BAZ ≤ − 2) or stunting (HAZ ≤ − 2), while BAZ ≥ 2 was considered overnutrition. Malnutrition was considered moderate when − 3 < Z-score ≤ − 2, and severe if the Z-score ≤ − 3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.11). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and the area under the curve (AUC) analyses were performed in R with the pROC package. Descriptive statistics have been used to summarize participant characteristics. The prevalence of undernutrition has been calculated and expressed as percentages. The association of categorical variables, such as the prevalence of undernutrition across GMFCS and EDACS levels was tested using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s Exact test, as appropriate. AUC, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. ROC analysis was used to assess the discriminatory power of MUACZ for distinguishing the presence of malnutrition using the fixed cutoff values (≤ − 2, and ≥ 2). These cutoffs were based on previously established age- and sex-adjusted reference standards for MUACZ [15, 20], which also form the basis of the MUACZ-measuring tape employed in the study. This was because the aim here was to assess the performance of the established cutoffs, not to look for a better one. Agreement between MUACZ and other anthropometric indices was tested using Cohen’s Kappa. Continuous Z-scores (MUACZ vs. BAZ and MUACZ vs. HAZ) have been correlated using the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. In all the above-mentioned analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 has been considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 84 children initially enrolled, 81 (46 female, 56.8%) were included in the analysis. Three children were excluded due to incomplete data (Fig. 1). The median age of the participants was 8.8 years (Interquartile Range: 5.7–12.7 years). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Feeding was provided solely by the mother in 9 patients (11.1%), while in 65 patients (80.2%) both parents were involved in the feeding process; in 7 patients (8.6%), feeding was carried out by caregivers other than the parents. Regarding feeding position, 47 patients (58.0%) were fed in an upright sitting position, 20 patients (24.7%) in a semi-upright position, and 14 patients (17.3%) in a supine position.

Prevalence of malnutrition

Undernutrition was highly prevalent in the study cohort, affecting 63% (n = 51) of the children. Stunting (HAZ ≤ − 2) was observed in 42.0% (n = 34) of participants, with the majority of these cases being severe (35.8% of the total sample had HAZ ≤ − 3). Wasting (BAZ ≤ − 2) was present in 38.3% (n = 31) of children, with severe wasting (BAZ ≤ − 3) accounting for 27.2% of the total sample. The prevalence of patients with MUACZ ≤ − 2 was 56.8% (46 patients), while 35.8% (29 patients) had MUACZ values ≤ − 3. Overnutrition (BAZ ≥ 2) was identified in 4.9% (4 patients) of the children; when undernutrition and overnutrition were considered together, the overall prevalence of malnutrition was 61.7%. Considering age groups, MUACZ were lower in adolescents than in the other age groups (p = 0.004). Association with functional severity

A strong, statistically significant association was found between the prevalence of undernutrition and the severity of both gross motor and eating/drinking impairments. The rate of undernutrition increased progressively with worsening GMFCS level, from 10.0% in GMFCS level I to 88.6% in GMFCS level V (Fisher’s Exact test, p < 0.001). A similar trend was observed for EDACS, where undernutrition prevalence rose from 18.8% in EDACS level I to 87.5% in EDACS level V (Fisher’s Exact test, p < 0.001). Table 4 summarizes the distribution of undernutrition and MUACZ ≤ − 2 according to GMFCS and EDACS levels. The association between MUACZ ≤ − 2 and functional motor level was evaluated using ordinal analysis. The frequency of MUACZ ≤ − 2 increased with higher GMFCS levels, demonstrating a statistically significant, weak-to-moderate positive correlation (Spearman’s ρ = 0.286, p = 0.010). In contrast, no statistically significant association was observed between MUACZ ≤ − 2 and EDACS level (Spearman’s ρ = 0.136, p = 0.225).

Diagnostic performance of MUAC Z-Score

MUACZ demonstrated a strong, positive correlation with BAZ (Spearman’s ρ = 0.585, p < 0.001) and a moderate positive correlation with HAZ (Spearman’s ρ = 0.467, p < 0.001, Fig. 2).

In ROC analysis, using the predefined cutoff of ≤ − 2, MUACZ showed good diagnostic accuracy for detecting wasting (BAZ ≤ − 2), with an AUC of 0.720. Its performance was better for detecting severe wasting (BAZ ≤ − 3), where the AUC rose to 0.766. The sensitivity for detecting any wasting was 83.9%, but specificity was lower at 60.0%. For detecting stunting (HAZ ≤ − 2), MUACZ had lower accuracy, with an AUC of 0.670, a sensitivity of 76.5%, and a specificity of 57.4% (Fig. 3). For both severe wasting and severe stunting, MUACZ at the fixed − 2 cutoff showed high sensitivity but low specificity (severe wasting: 95.5%/57.6%, severe stunting: 79.3%/55.8%). In addition, it provided a high NPV for severe wasting, correctly ruling out the condition in 97.1% of cases. Using a MUACZ ≤ − 2 cutoff, Cohen’s κ values were 0.40 for moderate wasting, 0.40 for severe wasting, 0.32 for moderate stunting, and 0.31 for severe stunting, indicating moderate agreement for wasting outcomes and low-to-moderate agreement for stunting outcomes. The corresponding receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves illustrating the diagnostic performance of MUACZ in detecting different degrees of wasting and stunting are presented in Fig. 3. The overall diagnostic performance metrics are summarized in Table 5. MUACZ ≥ 2 identified only one out of 4 overnutrition cases, and no false-positive overnutrition results were observed.

When specifically evaluating its ability to identify severe malnutrition, a MUACZ cutoff of ≤ − 3 had a very high negative predictive value (97.1%) for ruling out severe wasting (BAZ ≤ − 3), although its positive predictive value remained modest (45.7%).

Risk factors for undernutrition

To identify factors associated with undernutrition, we first conducted univariate analyses. Among the significant variables, the person responsible for feeding, the feeding position, GMFCS level, EDACS level, and CP type were all strongly associated with undernutrition (p for all < 0.001). Age, sex, epilepsy, and feeding route showed no meaningful association with undernutrition. All variables with p < 0.1 were included in the multivariate analysis, including age (p = 0.055), GMFCS level, EDACS level, CP type, the person responsible for feeding, and the feeding position. In the final model, increasing age and higher GMFCS levels remained independently associated with undernutrition, while the feeding position demonstrated a borderline association. EDACS level, CP type, and the person responsible for feeding did not retain significance after adjustment. Variance inflation factor values for all predictors were below 2.5, indicating no meaningful multicollinearity within the model. The overall model demonstrated good discriminatory performance (AUC ≈ 0.84) and strong explanatory power (pseudo-R² = 0.45).

Discussion

This study evaluates the diagnostic performance of MUACZ in children with CP and provides an overview of their nutritional status. Our major findings point out that MUACZ demonstrated utility as a practical anthropometric screening indicator of undernutrition, particularly for wasting. In addition, the overall rate of malnutrition was 67.9%, while undernutrition was 63%. Undernutrition rate appears to be increasing with greater levels of functional impairment.

The undernutrition rate (63%) in our cohort is in agreement with, or in some instances, higher than, other studies. It has been found that malnutrition exists in CP pediatric populations ranging from 40% to 90% depending on socioeconomic conditions, and preferred measurement approaches [21, 22]. A recent global meta-analysis estimated an average prevalence of approximately 40%, with lower rates reported in high-income European countries and higher rates in low-resource settings [23–25]. The relatively high prevalence in our study likely reflects the tertiary referral setting and the high proportion of children with severe motor impairment. The coexistence of stunting and wasting indicates that children in this cohort are affected by both chronic and acute forms of malnutrition, with potential clinical consequences including impaired immunity, reduced muscle strength, and lower quality of life [26]. Overnutrition was infrequent in our cohort (4.9%).

Our findings demonstrate a clear gradient in undernutrition risk with increasing GMFCS and EDACS levels, with prevalence rising from 10.0% in GMFCS level I to 88.6% in level V. This pattern may reflect factors, including but not restricted to, energy expenditure in view of increased muscle spasms, decreased capacity for proper positioning during meals, progressing to loss of capacity for self-feeding [27]. Similar patterns have been reported in multicenter studies, supporting the strong association between functional impairment and nutritional risk, particularly in children with severe motor limitations [9, 28].

Higher EDACS levels reflect feeding-related difficulties that may compromise the safety and efficiency of oral intake, thereby contributing to inadequate nutritional intake [17, 29]. Although our results suggest GMFCS to be the better predictor, EDACS continues to provide clinically relevant information. It serves as a direct functional marker of the feeding challenges that contribute to the risk defined by GMFCS. Hence, EDACS provides complementary clinical information on feeding-related challenges and may support nutritional assessment alongside GMFCS. GMFCS, thereby, gives information on overall risk, whereas EDACS identifies particular mechanisms, thereby enabling appropriate action to be directed toward their correction [30]. This link between feeding difficulties and malnutrition is further confirmed by Mouilly et al., who reported that children with CP suffering from swallowing disorders or oral malformations had significantly lower WAZ, HAZ, and BMI-for-age Z-scores, emphasizing the nutritional impact of oro-motor dysfunction [31].

One of the key strengths and contributions of our study is the multivariate analysis that moves beyond simple associations to identify the most critical independent predictors of undernutrition. Our final model demonstrated that only increasing age and higher levels of GMFCS remained independently associated with undernutrition. The fact that EDACS level, despite its strong univariate correlation, did not retain significance after adjusting for GMFCS is particularly instructive: it implies that while oropharyngeal difficulties are clinically important, the global functional impairment captured by the GMFCS appears to represent a more robust risk factor. It is conceivable that it is the severe motor limitation encapsulated by GMFCS that is the principal driver of energy imbalance leading to malnutrition and that it is this very same factor that leads to the feeding difficulties described by EDACS [16, 17]. Moreover, the emergence of increasing age as an independent risk factor is consistent with the cumulative nature of nutritional deficits in this population. This aligns with previous longitudinal studies suggesting that as children with CP grow older, their absolute nutritional needs increase, and feeding challenges may become more pronounced, leading to a progressive decline in nutritional status if not matched by proactive and escalating interventions [32]. Although undernutrition clearly followed functional severity, MUACZ was more closely related to nutritional depletion than to functional severity per se.

Beyond functional classifications, we further evaluated the diagnostic performance of MUACZ as an anthropometric screening tool. Considering the difficulties in taking accurate height and weight in children with severe CP, our study on the use of MUAC is of particular clinical relevance [18]. Overall, MUACZ demonstrated a prevalence estimate for malnutrition that was broadly comparable to WHO-based standards, identifying any kind of malnutrition in 61.7% of patients compared with the 67.9% prevalence observed using the WHO criteria. In the small subgroup of overweight children, MUACZ ≥ 2 did not produce false-positive results for overnutrition and identified one of four affected patients; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of cases. On the other hand, our findings suggest that MUACZ is particularly useful for identifying wasting. There was a moderate association between MUACZ and BAZ (both ≤ −2, ρ = 0.59), indicating that MUAC is an indicator for assessing body mass and tissue bulk. The sensitivity for identifying wasting (BAZ ≤ −2) with MUACZ ≤ −2 was high (83.9%) with good diagnostic accuracy, indicating good screening performance for identifying wasting. Its high negative predictive value (85.7%) also gives some assurance for excluding wasting in a child with normal MUACZ. Nevertheless, an important observation made in our research is that the performance accuracy for MUACZ is not consistent across different ages, indicating marked variability with increasing age. In the 5–10 years age group, MUACZ showed a favorable performance for identifying wasting, with an AUC of 0.87, sensitivity of 90.0%, and specificity of 81.8%, suggesting that it may serve as a useful screening measure in children with cerebral palsy. The observed level of agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.66) indicates that MUACZ assessment could be considered a supportive tool for nutritional evaluation in this age group.

In contrast, MUACZ performance was limited in younger children and adolescents. Among children under five years of age, its discriminatory ability for undernutrition was poor, consistent with previous reports [32]. In adolescents, although sensitivity remained high, specificity was low, resulting in a substantial rate of false-positive classifications, likely reflecting age-related changes in body composition. Studies on general adolescent populations have noted that MUAC is useful in this group, however, its cutoff points and performance can be influenced by pubertal stage, sex, and changes in fat-free mass, leading to a higher rate of false positives when screening for undernutrition [11, 12].

Our results are in keeping with the recent study conducted by Topçu et al. [13], which indicated a similar AUC (0.805–0.821) for MUACZ in identifying wasting in children with CP, although with slightly lower sensitivity at 67%. Differences in diagnostic performance across studies may partly reflect variations in age distribution and functional severity of the study populations. Importantly, our study shows MUACZ performing well in identifying severe wasting (BAZ ≤ −3), having an NPV of 97.1%, which is an extremely useful property for such a screening test, aiding in directing clinical personnel to those subjects testing positive for wasting.

On the other hand, its performance in identifying stunting (HAZ ≤ −2) was relatively lower (Cohen’s kappa: 0.32); in fact, its performance was not significantly different in any particular group. It wasn’t expected to perform better in identifying stunting, given its nature to measure primarily muscle and fat mass (acute nutritional status), whereas stunting is an indicator of growth failure in height, indicating chronic malnutrition [11]. This finding reinforces the concept that MUAC should not be used as a standalone tool to assess overall nutritional status but rather as a specific indicator of wasting [12, 33]. A comprehensive assessment must still include an evaluation of linear growth, even if it requires estimations from segmental measures.

Various studies have emphasized that absolute WHO-recommended cutoffs for MUAC, drafted for normally developing children, could not be appropriate for children with CP due to their body composition differences [12]. Another major study conducted by Zhao et al. [24] observed WHO-recommended cutoffs to have high specificity, yet unreasonably low sensitivity, in preschool-age children with CP, culminating in its suggestion for new, higher cutoff points. Our study avoids these problems by employing Z-scores, which are age- and sex-adjusted, thereby being more precise in their measurement over a wide range from 2 to 18 years, in contrast to absolute cutoff points.

Data from our cohort’s age-stratified analyses point towards such an improved clinical utilization pattern for MUACZ in their overall nutritional screening in pediatric CP. In addition, the very high NPV (97.1%) for severe wasting is an indicator that MUACZ has potential to serve effectively as a screening tool. It has to be kept in mind, though, our sample size is small. Therefore, these results should be viewed with some caution, and considered as tentative findings pending verification on larger samples. These results point out how their overall performance could significantly vary with increasing ages. For example, in pediatric populations aged 5–10 years, its overall accuracy was observed to be highest, while in those < 5 years, such applications appeared to be limited. Likewise, in adolescents, high sensitivity rates, and low specificity rates point out the need for validation after being positively screened by MUACZ.

From a clinical perspective, MUACZ may be incorporated as a rapid screening measure during routine follow-up, as a practical complement to standard anthropometric evaluation. Positive screening results should prompt comprehensive nutritional assessment, ideally within a multidisciplinary care framework [34].

This cross-sectional study provides age-stratified data on the performance of MUACZ for identifying undernutrition in children with cerebral palsy across the full pediatric age range. The use of standardized functional classifications and measurements performed by trained study authors strengthens the internal consistency of the findings.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, due to its cross-sectional nature, there is no way to establish causality between functional status and malnutrition. Secondly, despite the age and sex adjustment provided by Z-scores, they cannot fully overcome the limitations of applying WHO growth references to children with CP. In addition, being restricted to one tertiary referral center could lead to problems with biased sampling, resulting in an underrepresentation or overrepresentation of CP severity, including malnutrition, in comparison to other CP populations. Finally, we did not examine either nutritional or socioeconomic status in our subjects, which could have acted as an important covariate for malnutrition status.

In the context of cerebral palsy care, the current results suggest that MUACZ is associated with nutritional status (particularly wasting) while undernutrition risk clearly follows functional severity. On the one hand, this is in accordance with the well-established observation that motor and feeding problems are key elements of nutritional risk. On the other hand, the fact that the measurement of MUACZ is very easy to perform and feasible to obtain in children with varying degrees of functional impairment means that it may serve as a supplement to the existing methods of nutritional status analysis, ideally when comprehensive anthropometric evaluation is not feasible.

Conclusions

Undernutrition was common among children with CP and increased with greater functional severity as classified by GMFCS and EDACS. MUACZ was related to nutritional status, particularly wasting, and had acceptable performance as a practical screening measure, especially for ruling out severe wasting. It demonstrated more consistent diagnostic performance in school-aged children and adolescents (≥ 5 years). Overall, MUACZ performance appeared to be more closely related to nutritional depletion than functional severity itself, and may serve as an additional screening tool when comprehensive anthropometric evaluation is not feasible.
