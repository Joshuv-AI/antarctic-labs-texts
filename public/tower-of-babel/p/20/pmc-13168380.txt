miR-155-5p promotes osteosarcoma progression and cisplatin resistance by targeting the SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos signaling axis
Authors: Jian Lu, Hou Ren, Wenxiao Song, Shishun Xie, Xiangjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimOsteosarcoma remains challenging to treat due to metastasis and chemoresistance. This study investigated the role and mechanisms of miR-155-5p in osteosarcoma progression and chemoresistance.MethodsWe analyzed miR-155-5p expression in clinical datasets and evaluated its biological functions in osteosarcoma cell lines through proliferation, migration, and apoptosis assays. Target identification and validation were performed using bioinformatics analysis, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and molecular studies. Cisplatin-resistant cells were used to examine chemoresistance mechanisms.ResultsElevated miR-155-5p expression correlated with poor survival in patients and was significantly upregulated in osteosarcoma. miR-155-5p promoted proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis across multiple cell lines. Mechanistically, miR-155-5p directly targeted SOCS1, leading to decreased STAT1 and increased c-Fos expression. This axis modulated downstream effectors including P-glycoprotein, Bcl-2, and Bax. In cisplatin-resistant cells, dysregulation of the miR-155-5p/SOCS1 axis contributed to drug resistance.ConclusionsmiR-155-5p functions as an oncogenic microRNA by targeting the SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis, promoting malignant behaviors and chemoresistance in osteosarcoma. This pathway represents a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04913-5.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most common primary malignant bone tumor characterized by high invasiveness and metastatic potential. This neoplasm predominantly originates from mesenchymal stem cells at the metaphyseal regions of long bones, primarily occurring in the distal femur or proximal tibia, producing tumor-like bone and osteoid tissue through direct or indirect pathways (via cartilaginous differentiation stages) [1]. The pathogenesis of osteosarcoma involves complex networks of genetic mutations and epigenetic dysregulation that remain incompletely elucidated [2]. Despite advances in multimodal treatment strategies combining surgery with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, distant metastasis and chemoresistance in osteosarcoma patients continue to be major challenges affecting prognosis [3]. Therefore, investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying osteosarcoma development and the regulatory networks driving metastasis and drug resistance may provide insights for developing potential therapeutic targets and improving patient outcomes [4].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous non-coding RNA molecules approximately 22 nucleotides in length that mediate post-transcriptional regulation by binding to the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) of target gene mRNAs, thereby regulating gene expression. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that miRNAs play important regulatory roles in key biological processes including proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis in various tumors [5]. MiR-155-5p, a functionally implicated small non-coding RNA, has been reported to participate in multiple pathological processes. Previous studies have shown that miR-155-5p regulates osteosarcoma-associated pain by directly acting on TCF4 in spinal neurons [6] and plays roles in the progression of several malignancies including colorectal cancer [7]. However, the molecular functions and potential regulatory mechanisms of miR-155-5p in osteosarcoma progression and chemoresistance remain to be fully characterized.

The JAK-STAT signaling pathway mediates cellular responses to cytokines and growth factors, regulating key processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [8]. The Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling (SOCS) family, comprising SOCS1-SOCS7 and CIS, functions as negative feedback regulators of this pathway [9]. SOCS1, in particular, inhibits JAK-STAT signaling by suppressing JAK kinase activity and promoting STAT protein degradation [10], and has been reported to play a role in regulating autoimmune diseases and various malignancies through induction of cellular apoptosis [11, 12]. Among STAT family members, STAT1 exhibits distinct tumor-suppressive properties in several malignancies, contrasting with the oncogenic roles of STAT3 and STAT5 [13]. STAT1 has been reported to regulate anti-tumor immune responses, and its reduced expression in multiple tumor types compared to normal tissues suggests a role in tumor growth and chemoresistance development [14, 15]. c-Fos, an essential component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, participates in transducing extracellular signals to the nucleus and influences cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and cell fate determination [15]. While dysregulation of the SOCS1/STAT1 axis has been implicated in tumor progression and chemoresistance in various cancers, the potential regulatory relationships among SOCS1, STAT1, and c-Fos in osteosarcoma remain poorly understood.

Although miR-155-5p has been implicated in various malignancies, its specific regulatory mechanisms in osteosarcoma, particularly regarding the SOCS1/STAT1 signaling axis and chemoresistance, remain largely unexplored. In this study, we hypothesized that miR-155-5p regulates osteosarcoma progression through the SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis. Through bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation using real-time quantitative PCR and Western blotting, we demonstrate that miR-155-5p promotes proliferation and migration of osteosarcoma cells while inhibiting apoptosis and conferring cisplatin resistance. Mechanistically, we identify SOCS1 as a direct target of miR-155-5p and demonstrate that this interaction modulates the STAT1/c-Fos signaling cascade, providing the first evidence linking miR-155-5p to the JAK-STAT pathway in osteosarcoma. These findings characterize a regulatory mechanism involving the miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos signaling axis in osteosarcoma progression and chemoresistance development, which may contribute to understanding the molecular basis of osteosarcoma and inform future therapeutic strategies.

Bioinformatics methods

Clinical survival analysis Overall survival data for pediatric osteosarcoma patients were retrieved from the TARGET-OS cohort through the OncomiR platform (http://www.oncomir.org). Normalized miRNA-seq counts were log₂-transformed and patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to the median miR-155-5p expression level. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the survival and survminer packages in R (v4.3.2), and statistical significance was assessed using the two-sided log-rank test.

Independent cohort validation To validate miR-155-5p expression in an independent cohort, the GSE65071 dataset was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. This dataset included 20 osteosarcoma samples (comprising ten cases with pulmonary metastasis) and 15 age-matched healthy controls for comparative analysis of serum miR-155-5p levels. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and appropriate statistical tests were applied accordingly.

Target prediction and pathway analysis Potential targets of miR-155-5p were predicted using the TargetScan database (https://www.targetscan.org/). Target genes of miR-155-5p were subsequently analyzed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. Results were visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1 software. For GO and KEGG analyses, a p-value threshold of 0.05 was applied to identify significantly enriched terms.

Statistical analysis All bioinformatics analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Kaplan-Meier curves were generated with p-values and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained via log-rank test and univariate Cox regression analysis. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout all analyses.

Cell culture

MG-63 cells, U2OS, and 143B osteosarcoma cell lines were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cisplatin-resistant MG-63/DDP cell line was established from parental MG-63 cells through intermittent exposure to increasing concentrations of the cisplatin (1–10 µM) over a six-month period, with maintenance of resistant phenotype confirmed by IC50 determination. For cell recovery, frozen cells were quickly thawed at 37℃. The thawed cells were transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 2 mL complete culture medium (Modified Eagle Medium (MEM) for MG-63 cells, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) for U2OS and 143B cells) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, mixed thoroughly, and then centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 5 min to remove DMSO. The cell pellet was resuspended in fresh complete medium. The resuspended cells were then transferred to a 10 cm culture dish and incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2. MG-63/DDP cells were maintained in the same medium supplemented with 1 µM cisplatin to maintain the resistance phenotype. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase (approximately 70–80% confluence) were used for all subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection

Cell transfection was performed when cells reached 60–70% confluence. For miR-155-5p mimics, miR-155-5p inhibitor, and their corresponding negative controls (NC), transfection was conducted using GP-Transfect-Mate Reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MG-63 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well 24 h prior to transfection. For transfection, two series of 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes were prepared (Series A: tubes A1-A5; Series B: tubes B1-B5). Each tube in Series A contained 200 µL basal MEM medium. Tube A1 received 5 µL basal medium (blank control), while tubes A2-A5 each received 5 µL transfection reagent. In Series B, each tube contained 200 µL basal MEM medium. Tube B1 received 7.5 µL basal medium, while tubes B2-B5 received 7.5 µL of miR-155-5p mimics, miR-155-5p mimics NC, miR-155-5p inhibitor, or miR-155-5p inhibitor NC, respectively, at a final concentration of 50 nM. After 5 minutes of incubation at room temperature, the contents of each tube in Series A were transferred to the corresponding tube in Series B and mixed gently. The mixtures were incubated for an additional 15 min at room temperature to form transfection complexes. Prior to transfection, culture medium was removed from the 6-well plates, cells were washed once with PBS, and 1600 µL of basal MEM medium was added to each well. Subsequently, 400 µL of the appropriate transfection mixture was added to each well, resulting in a final volume of 2 mL per well. Cells were incubated with transfection complexes for 6 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, after which the medium was replaced with complete MEM medium to terminate transfection.

For SOCS1 knockdown, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting SOCS1 (si-SOCS1) or non-targeting control siRNA (si-NC) at a final concentration of 50 nM using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and transfected when they reached 60–70% confluence. The transfection medium was replaced with complete medium after 6 h, and cells were incubated for an additional 24–48 h before subsequent experiments. For STAT1 overexpression, cells were transfected with STAT1 overexpression plasmid (STAT1-OE) or empty vector control using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For c-Fos knockdown, cells were transfected with si-c-Fos or si-NC at 50 nM using Lipofectamine 2000. For co-transfection experiments combining miR-155-5p mimics with STAT1-OE, or miR-155-5p inhibitor with si-SOCS1, appropriate amounts of each reagent were mixed during the transfection complex formation step. The sequences of miRNA mimics, inhibitors, plasmids, and siRNAs used for transfection are provided in Table S1.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Following 24–48 h of cell transfection, total RNA was extracted using the SevenFast Total RNA Extraction Kit for Cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 considered acceptable for further analysis. For miRNA analysis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the StarScript III miRNA RT Kit (by Poly A) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For mRNA analysis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Real-time quantitative PCR was conducted on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using 2×RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix (GenStar, China) with an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Each reaction was performed in triplicate, and melting curve analysis was conducted to confirm amplification specificity. The relative expression levels of miRNA and mRNA were determined using the 2−∆∆Ct method, with U6 snRNA serving as an internal reference control for miRNA and GAPDH for mRNA quantification. Primers were custom-designed and synthesized by Gemma Genetics (Shanghai, China), with the primer sequences provided in Table S2.

Cell proliferation assay and determination of half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50)

Cell proliferation assay Twenty-four hours after cell transfection, cells were harvested and 200µL of cell suspension was added into each well of a 96-well plate, ensuring a cell density of 5000 cells/well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Following the incubation period, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h. Subsequently, the medium was carefully removed and 150µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plates were placed on a low-speed shaker for 10 min to facilitate complete dissolution of the formazan crystals. The absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA reader with a reference wavelength of 630 nm (Molecular Devices, China).

IC50 determination For cisplatin sensitivity assays, cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 200 µL of complete medium. Plates were incubated overnight until cells adhered to the well surface. Upon cell adhesion, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing cisplatin at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 µM diluted from a stock solution of 10 mM cisplatin in PBS. Cells were incubated with cisplatin for 24 h, and the MTT assay was then performed as described above. The IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, USA) by fitting the dose-response curves.

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

The EdU assay was performed using the Cell-Light EdU Apollo 567 In Vitro Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for an additional 24 h. Once cells adhered, 50 µM EdU was added to the culture medium and cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, permeabilization solution (PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100) was applied and incubate for an additional 15 min. Next, Click reaction solution (containing Alexa Fluor 567 azide) was added and incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Finally, Hoechst 33,342 solution (5 µg/mL) was added and incubated for 10 min to counterstain nuclei. The samples were then observed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti fluorescence microscope and images were captured from at least five random fields per well. Quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (version 1.53e, NIH, USA). The EdU-positive cell rate was calculated as the ratio of the number of EdU-positive cells (red fluorescence) to the total number of cells (blue fluorescence), multiplied by 100%.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry using the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Lianke Biologicals, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four to forty-eight hours after cell transfection, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in 500µL of 1×Binding Buffer, followed by addition of 5µL of Annexin V-FITC and 10µL of PI to each sample. Samples were incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were then analyzed within 1 h using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). For each sample, at least 10,000 events were collected. The percentages of early apoptotic (Annexin V-FITC+/PI-) and late apoptotic (Annexin V-FITC+/PI+) cells were calculated using FlowJo software (version 10.0, Tree Star, USA), and total apoptosis rate was calculated as the sum of early and late apoptotic cells.

Wound-healing and transwell migration assays

Wound-healing assay The wound-healing assay was performed to evaluate cell migration ability in a two-dimensional space. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells/well, and cultured until they reached approximately 90% confluence. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell monolayer perpendicular to a reference line marked on the bottom of the plate. The wells were gently washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells, and serum-free medium was then added to minimize the effect of cell proliferation on migration. The initial wound was photographedimmediately after scratching (designated as 0 h) using an Olympus IX71 inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) equipped with a digital camera at 100× magnification. The cells were then cultured for 24 h, and subsequent photographs were taken at the same location under the microscope. Wound closure rate was analyzed using ImageJ software and calculated as: (initial wound width - final wound width)/initial wound width × 100%.

Transwell migration assay The Transwell assay was performed to evaluate cell migration ability through a three-dimensional matrix. Twenty-four hours after cell transfection, cells were harvested and resuspended in serum-free culture medium. Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning, USA) were placed in 24-well plates. A total of 700µL of culture medium containing 15% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant, while 200µL of cell suspension containing 5 × 104 cells in basal culture medium was added to the upper chamber. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, the non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed using a cotton swab, and the migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, rinsed several times with PBS, and observed under an Olympus IX71 inverted microscope at 200× magnification. Five random fields were photographed for each sample. The cells from the upper surface of the bottom membrane of the upper chamber were carefully removed using a wet cotton swab. Migrated cells were counted using ImageJ software and normalized to the control group.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on a 12.5% gel at 100 V for approximately 1.5 h. Following electrophoresis, the proteins were electrotransferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane at 300 mA for 90 min at 4 °C. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST buffer (TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 5% BSA targeting SOCS1 (1:1000), STAT1 (1:1000), c-Fos (1:1000), P-glycoprotein (P-gp, 1:1000), Bcl-2 (1:1000), Bax (1:1000), cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:5000), all purchased from ZENBIO, Chengdu, China. After washing three times with TBST, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:5000, ZENBIO) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with TBST, protein bands were visualized using the High-sig ECL detection system (Tanon, China) and captured using the Tanon-5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China). The intensity of the bands was quantified using ImageJ software to analyze the grayscale values. GAPDH was used as a loading control to normalize the expression levels of target proteins.

Luciferase reporter assay

To validate the direct binding of miR-155-5p to the SOCS1 3’UTR, luciferase reporter plasmids (300 ng) (pGL3-control vector) encoding wild-type or mutant 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of SOCS1 were co-transfected with miR-155-5p mimic or miR-NC, and anti-miR-155-5p or anti-NC, along with 30 ng of pRL-TK Renilla luciferase plasmid as an internal control. Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) was used for transfection. At 48 h after transfection into MG-63 cells, luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to control for transfection efficiency.

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., USA) and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were applied when data did not meet the assumptions for parametric testing. Statistical significance was set as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 for all analyses.

Results

Clinical relevance of miR-155-5p in sarcoma patients

To investigate the clinical relevance of miR-155-5p in osteosarcoma, we initially analyzed publicly available datasets to assess its association with patient prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of the TARGET sarcoma cohort revealed that patients with high miR-155-5p expression had significantly shorter overall survival compared to those with low expression (log-rank p = 0.03373, Fig. 1A), establishing miR-155-5p as a marker associated with poor prognosis. To assess whether elevated miR-155-5p could be detected in patient samples, we analyzed the GSE65071 dataset comparing serum miR-155-5p levels between osteosarcoma patients and healthy controls. Circulating miR-155-5p was significantly elevated in osteosarcoma patients (Fig. 1B), consistent with its potential role in disease pathogenesis and suggesting utility as a diagnostic biomarker.

miR-155-5p promotes proliferation of osteosarcoma cells

Having established the clinical relevance of miR-155-5p, we next investigated its functional role in osteosarcoma cells by modulating its expression using miR-155-5p mimics or inhibitor. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed successful transfection, demonstrating significant upregulation of miR-155-5p in the mimics group and marked downregulation in the inhibitor group compared to their respective controls (Fig. 1C).

We first assessed the impact of miR-155-5p on cell proliferation using EdU incorporation assays. miR-155-5p overexpression substantially increased the proportion of EdU-positive MG-63 cells, indicating enhanced proliferation (Fig. 1D), whereas inhibition significantly reduced EdU incorporation (Fig. 1E). To determine whether these effects were specific to MG-63 cells or represented a broader phenomenon, we extended our analysis to U2OS and 143B cells. Both cell lines showed similar responses: miR-155-5p overexpression significantly enhanced proliferation, and inhibition suppressed proliferation (Fig. 1F-G), confirming that miR-155-5p consistently promotes osteosarcoma cell growth across multiple cell lines.

miR-155-5p promotes migration of osteosarcoma cells

Beyond its effects on proliferation, we examined whether miR-155-5p also influences the migratory capacity of osteosarcoma cells. Wound healing assays in MG-63 cells showed that miR-155-5p overexpression significantly accelerated wound closure compared to the NC-mimic control, whereas miR-155-5p inhibition markedly delayed wound closure compared to the NC-inhibitor control (Fig. 2A-B).Transwell migration assays confirmed these findings, with miR-155-5p overexpression significantly increasing the number of migrated MG-63 cells and inhibition substantially reducing migration (Fig. 2C–D).

miR-155-5p inhibits apoptosis of osteosarcoma cells

We next investigated whether miR-155-5p affects cell survival by examining its effect on apoptosis. Flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI staining revealed that miR-155-5p overexpression significantly reduced the apoptotic cell population in MG-63 cells compared to the NC-mimic control, whereas miR-155-5p inhibition markedly increased the percentage of apoptotic cells compared to the NC-inhibitor control (Fig. 3A–C). indicating that miR-155-5p suppresses apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells. These anti-apoptotic effects were reproduced in U2OS and 143B cells (Fig. 3F–G), demonstrating that miR-155-5p consistently suppresses apoptosis across multiple osteosarcoma cell lines.

Bioinformatics analysis and target validation of miR-155-5p

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying these oncogenic effects, we performed bioinformatics analysis to identify potential miR-155-5p target genes. TargetScan prediction identified SOCS1 (suppressor of cytokine signaling 1) as a high-confidence candidate due to its established role in cancer signaling pathways (Fig. 4A).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of predicted miR-155-5p targets revealed significant enrichment in multiple cancer-related pathways, most notably “Pathways in cancer,” confirming the involvement of miR-155-5p in oncogenic processes (Fig. 4B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further showed enrichment in biological processes including protein phosphorylation and RNA polymerase II-related transcription, cellular components such as membrane and organelles, and molecular functions related to protein binding and catalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 2). KEGG pathway network analysis illustrated the complex regulatory network of miR-155-5p targets, revealing their involvement in diverse signaling cascades including JAK-STAT, PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and cell cycle pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Sequence analysis using TargetScan revealed a highly conserved binding site for miR-155-5p in the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of SOCS1 mRNA (position 24–31), with strong predicted binding affinity (context + + score: -0.33), supporting SOCS1 as a direct regulatory target of miR-155-5p (Fig. 4C).

We next assessed the clinical relevance of SOCS1 in osteosarcoma. Analysis of the TARGET osteosarcoma cohort showed that patients with low SOCS1 expression had significantly shorter overall survival compared to those with high expression (log-rank P = 0.025; HR = 0.472, 95% CI: 0.245–0.909) (Fig. 4D). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further demonstrated that SOCS1, along with STAT1 and STAT2, serves as an independent prognostic factor in osteosarcoma patients (Fig. 4E), underscoring the clinical significance of the miR-155-5p/SOCS1 axis.

These bioinformatics analyses identify SOCS1 as a high-priority miR-155-5p target and suggest that miR-155-5p may exert its oncogenic functions by targeting SOCS1 and modulating downstream JAK-STAT signaling pathways.

miR-155-5p modulates the SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos signaling axis

Since SOCS1 is a well-known negative regulator of JAK-STAT signaling, we investigated whether miR-155-5p regulates downstream signaling pathways through SOCS1. We examined the expression of STAT1, a key member of the STAT family, and its downstream target c-Fos in response to miR-155-5p modulation.

RT-qPCR analysis in MG-63 cells revealed that miR-155-5p overexpression significantly decreased SOCS1 and STAT1 mRNA levels while increasing c-Fos expression compared to the NC-mimic control (Fig. 7A). Conversely, miR-155-5p inhibition elevated SOCS1 and STAT1 expression while reducing c-Fos levels (Fig. 7B), demonstrating bidirectional regulation of this signaling axis. Western blot analysis confirmed these changes at the protein level, showing that miR-155-5p mimics reduced SOCS1 and STAT1 protein expression while enhancing c-Fos expression, whereas miR-155-5p inhibitor produced the opposite effects (Fig. 7C-E).To validate the generalizability of these findings, we examined U2OS cells and observed similar regulation of the SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis (Supplementary Fig. 4A–E), confirming that miR-155-5p consistently modulates this signaling pathway across multiple osteosarcoma cell lines.

STAT1 functionally mediates miR-155-5p effects through c-Fos-dependent mechanisms

To further dissect the functional relationships within the SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos signaling cascade, we performed rescue experiments to determine whether STAT1 and c-Fos mediate the oncogenic effects of miR-155-5p.

We first examined the regulatory relationship between STAT1 and c-Fos. Western blot analysis showed that STAT1 knockdown (si-STAT1) increased c-Fos expression, while STAT1 overexpression (STAT1-OE) decreased c-Fos levels (Fig. 8A), establishing an inverse regulatory relationship between these two proteins. Functionally, MTT assays demonstrated that STAT1 knockdown enhanced cell viability, whereas STAT1 overexpression reduced viability (Fig. 8B), consistent with STAT1 functioning as a tumor suppressor in osteosarcoma.

We next assessed whether c-Fos serves as a downstream effector linking STAT1 to cell survival pathways. c-Fos knockdown (si-c-Fos) reduced c-Fos expression and altered the levels of downstream apoptosis-related proteins including P-glycoprotein (P-gp), Bcl-2, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 (cl-Casp3) (Fig. 8C), demonstrating that c-Fos regulates key mediators of cell survival and drug resistance.

Functionally, STAT1 overexpression significantly attenuated the proliferation-promoting effect of miR-155-5p mimics (Fig. 8E) and rescued the anti-apoptotic effect of miR-155-5p (Fig. 8F), confirming that STAT1 functionally mediates miR-155-5p’s effects on cell growth and survival.

These rescue experiments establish that miR-155-5p exerts its oncogenic functions through a hierarchical SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos signaling cascade, with STAT1 serving as a critical functional mediator and c-Fos acting as a key downstream effector regulating cell survival and chemoresistance pathways.

miR-155-5p/SOCS1 axis regulates cisplatin sensitivity in osteosarcoma

Given the critical role of chemoresistance in osteosarcoma treatment failure and the demonstrated involvement of the miR-155-5p/SOCS1 axis in regulating apoptosis-related proteins, we investigated whether this axis contributes to cisplatin resistance.

We first established a cisplatin-resistant cell line model. MG-63 cells were subjected to stepwise exposure to increasing cisplatin concentrations to generate MG-63/DDP cells. MTT assays confirmed that MG-63/DDP cells exhibited significantly higher resistance to cisplatin compared to parental MG-63 cells, with IC50 values of 37.72 µM and 18.27 µM, respectively (Fig. 9A), representing a 2.1-fold increase in drug resistance.

We next examined whether the miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis is dysregulated in cisplatin-resistant cells. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that MG-63/DDP cells expressed significantly higher levels of miR-155-5p and c-Fos, but lower levels of SOCS1 and STAT1 compared to parental cells (Fig. 9B), suggesting that dysregulation of the miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis contributes to cisplatin resistance.

To directly assess the role of SOCS1 in cisplatin sensitivity, we performed functional assays in both MG-63 and MG-63/DDP cells. SOCS1 knockdown significantly enhanced cell proliferation in both cell lines under basal conditions and reduced cisplatin-induced growth inhibition (Fig. 9C). Furthermore, SOCS1 silencing markedly decreased cisplatin-induced apoptosis in both MG-63 and MG-63/DDP cells (Fig. 9D), indicating that SOCS1 depletion confers cisplatin resistance.

To determine whether the miR-155-5p-mediated cisplatin resistance is SOCS1-dependent, we performed rescue experiments by co-transfecting cells with miR-155-5p inhibitor and si-SOCS1. In both MG-63 and MG-63/DDP cells, miR-155-5p inhibition reduced cell proliferation and enhanced cisplatin sensitivity (Fig. 9E-F). However, simultaneous SOCS1 knockdown significantly attenuated the sensitization effect of miR-155-5p inhibitor, partially restoring cell proliferation under both control and cisplatin-treated conditions (Fig. 9E). Similarly, SOCS1 knockdown reversed the pro-apoptotic effect of miR-155-5p inhibition in the presence of cisplatin (Fig. 9G–H).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the miR-155-5p/SOCS1 axis plays a critical role in regulating cisplatin sensitivity, and targeting this pathway may represent a potential strategy to overcome chemoresistance in osteosarcoma.

Discussion

Osteosarcoma remains a significant therapeutic challenge due to its aggressive nature, metastatic potential, and chemoresistance tendencies, with limited improvement in patient outcomes despite treatment advancements [16–19]. Our study provides evidence that miR-155-5p is associated with oncogenic functions in osteosarcoma by enhancing proliferation and migration while suppressing apoptosis in multiple osteosarcoma cell lines. This profile aligns with previous investigations documenting aberrant miR-155 expression across diverse malignancies including breast, pancreatic, thyroid, and colorectal cancers [20–23]. While previous studies have established roles for miR-155-5p in various cancers, our work is the first to demonstrate its direct regulatory relationship with SOCS1 in osteosarcoma and to delineate the downstream STAT1/c-Fos signaling cascade. This represents a significant advance over prior reports of miR-155-5p in osteosarcoma, which were limited to its role in pain modulation [6], and extends our understanding of how miRNA-mediated regulation of the JAK-STAT pathway contributes to osteosarcoma pathogenesis. The consistent effects observed across experimental manipulations—where miR-155-5p overexpression promoted malignant behaviors while its inhibition reversed these effects—indicate that this microRNA may represent an important regulatory factor in osteosarcoma biology.

Through integrative bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation, we have identified a regulatory axis whereby miR-155-5p exerts its oncogenic effects by directly targeting SOCS1, subsequently modulating the STAT1/c-Fos signaling pathway. SOCS1, recognized for its tumor-suppressive properties and regulatory role in immune and inflammatory responses [24], emerged as a mediator of miR-155-5p’s biological effects in our model. SOCS1 suppression phenocopied miR-155-5p overexpression, enhancing proliferation and migration while attenuating apoptosis, consistent with previous studies in gastric, prostate, and colorectal cancers [25–27]. Our molecular analyses further revealed that miR-155-5p-mediated suppression of SOCS1 resulted in decreased STAT1 expression and concurrent increases in c-Fos.

The paradoxical SOCS1-STAT1 regulatory relationship

A critical observation requiring mechanistic clarification is the coordinate downregulation of SOCS1 and STAT1 in response to miR-155-5p overexpression, which appears counterintuitive given SOCS1’s canonical role as a negative regulator of JAK-STAT signaling [28]. This apparent paradox can be reconciled by considering cross-regulatory STAT family members in malignant cells. Chronic SOCS1 loss permits constitutive activation of oncogenic STAT3 and STAT5, which competitively suppress tumor-suppressive STAT1 through transcriptional competition, promotion of STAT1 protein degradation, and sequestration of essential coactivators such as CBP/p300 [29, 30]. This “STAT1:STAT3 seesaw” mechanism has been well-characterized in interferon and gp130 receptor signaling contexts [28–30].

Critically, our clinical data support this interpretation: both SOCS1 and STAT1 low expression independently predict poor patient prognosis (Fig. 4D-E; log-rank P = 0.025 for SOCS1), with multivariate Cox regression confirming their role as independent prognostic factors (Fig. 4E). This coordinate loss in patient samples suggests clinically relevant adaptive reprogramming during osteosarcoma progression rather than an in vitro artifact.

Molecular mechanisms of STAT1-mediated c-Fos suppression

Our rescue experiments demonstrated that STAT1 functions as a critical negative regulator of c-Fos, with STAT1 overexpression reversing miR-155-5p-induced molecular changes and functionally attenuating its pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic effects. The inverse STAT1-c-Fos relationship operates through well-characterized mechanisms, most prominently competition for transcriptional coactivators CBP/p300. STAT1 directly interacts with CBP/p300 through two distinct contact regions [31, 32], and upon activation, sequesters these coactivators from c-Fos-containing AP-1 complexes that also require CBP/p300 for optimal transcriptional activity [33]. This competitive mechanism, elegantly demonstrated by Zhang et al. (1996) and Horvai et al. (1997) [31, 33], determines the balance between JAK/STAT and Ras/AP-1 signaling pathways. Additionally, STAT1 can function as a direct transcriptional repressor under certain contexts [32, 34].

In osteosarcoma, high c-Fos expression correlates with increased proliferation and metastasis [35, 36], while STAT1 activation inhibits tumor growth [37]. Our finding that STAT1 suppresses c-Fos provides a molecular link explaining how the SOCS1/STAT1 axis antagonizes c-Fos-driven oncogenic programs—a regulatory connection not previously characterized in osteosarcoma. The downstream effects on P-glycoprotein, Bcl-2, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 demonstrate that this axis integrates cell survival and chemoresistance signaling.

Cisplatin resistance: apoptotic dysregulation versus drug efflux

An important aspect of our investigation concerns chemoresistance mechanisms. In cisplatin-resistant MG-63/DDP cells, we observed coordinate dysregulation of the miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis with P-glycoprotein (P-gp) upregulation. However, careful mechanistic interpretation is required: cisplatin, as a hydrophilic platinum complex, is not a classical substrate for P-gp, which predominantly transports lipophilic compounds [38]. The P-gp upregulation likely reflects a multidrug-resistant phenotype marker rather than a direct cisplatin efflux mechanism.

Our data emphasize that the miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos axis regulates cisplatin sensitivity primarily through modulation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Alterations in Bcl-2, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 (Figs. 8C–D and 9D and G–H) indicate control of the apoptotic threshold—the cellular readiness to undergo programmed cell death upon cisplatin-induced DNA damage. This apoptotic dysregulation, rather than altered drug transport, constitutes the central resistance mechanism. We acknowledge that platinum-specific transporters (CTR1, ATP7A/ATP7B) warrant investigation in future studies. The rescue experiments demonstrating that SOCS1 knockdown partially abrogated miR-155-5p inhibitor’s cisplatin-sensitizing effects (Fig. 9E–H) establish functional dependency on SOCS1 suppression and support a model wherein chemoresistance operates through apoptotic control rather than drug efflux.

Tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy implications

Recent studies have illuminated the critical role of STAT signaling dynamics in cancer progression and therapeutic resistance. Friedrich et al. demonstrated that carcinoma progression is steered by the opposing interactions between tumor-suppressive STAT1 and oncogenic STAT3 [39], while Liu et al. showed that STAT1-mediated inhibition of FOXM1 enhances chemotherapy sensitivity [40]. Particularly relevant to osteosarcoma, Zhang et al. demonstrated that STAT1 repression promotes lung metastasis through activation of pulmonary cancer-associated fibroblasts [37], directly linking STAT1 loss to tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling. Wang et al. documented c-Fos involvement in osteosarcoma cell proliferation and invasion [35], establishing the functional significance of the STAT1-c-Fos axis.

The importance of TME modulation in cancer therapy has been increasingly recognized. Recent investigations employing multi-omic approaches have identified key regulators of cancer stem cells and their microenvironmental niches [41], while advances in antibody-drug conjugates have demonstrated that therapeutic efficacy depends on coordinating direct tumor cell targeting with immune system engagement [42]. Xu et al. comprehensively characterized the interplay between cellular stress pathways and the TME across multiple cancer types, revealing that TME composition profoundly influences both prognosis and therapeutic responses [43]. Furthermore, integration of immunological and molecular signatures has proven valuable for predicting treatment outcomes [44], collectively establishing that therapeutic strategies must address the entire TME ecosystem rather than tumor cells in isolation.

In osteosarcoma, STAT1 loss due to miR-155-5p-mediated SOCS1 suppression may impair tumor cell responsiveness to IFN-γ, manifesting as reduced MHC-I expression and enhanced immune evasion. Furthermore, c-Fos activation may alter chemokine secretion, potentially recruiting immunosuppressive populations while excluding cytotoxic effectors. These considerations suggest that combining miR-155-5p inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade might yield synergistic effects by simultaneously enhancing tumor immunogenicity and unleashing T cell function. However, these hypotheses require validation in immunocompetent animal models.

Study limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our exclusively in vitro experiments preclude assessment of tumor-stromal interactions, immune surveillance, and metastasis in physiologically relevant contexts. Animal models are needed to validate therapeutic potential. Second, while we demonstrated apoptotic pathway modulation drives cisplatin resistance, we did not examine platinum-specific transporters (CTR1, ATP7A/ATP7B) that may play complementary roles. P-glycoprotein upregulation likely reflects multidrug-resistant phenotype rather than direct cisplatin efflux. Third, the STAT1→c-Fos regulatory mechanisms, though well-supported by established CBP/p300 competition literature [31–34], would benefit from direct ChIP validation and genome-wide transcriptional profiling. Fourth, validation in primary patient specimens and patient-derived models is needed. While our findings are supported by clinical dataset analyses (Figs. 1A-B and 4D-E), prospective cohort studies could determine whether miR-155-5p/SOCS1 expression predicts therapeutic responses. Finally, we did not investigate TME modulation or immune responses—a translational immunotherapy strategies. Future studies should employ immunocompetent models to assess how this axis influences immune cell infiltration, TME composition, and responses to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

Despite these limitations, our study provides comprehensive characterization of the miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos signaling axis in osteosarcoma, establishing its role in promoting malignant behaviors and chemoresistance, thereby laying a foundation for future translational investigations.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that miR-155-5p exhibits oncogenic properties in osteosarcoma by promoting proliferation and migration, suppressing apoptosis, and being associated with resistance to cisplatin chemotherapy. Mechanistically, miR-155-5p exerts these effects by directly targeting and downregulating SOCS1, which subsequently leads to decreased STAT1 expression and increased c-Fos activation. This characterized miR-155-5p/SOCS1/STAT1/c-Fos regulatory axis provides insights into the molecular pathogenesis of osteosarcoma and reveals potential targets for therapeutic intervention. Our findings suggest that elevated miR-155-5p expression may serve as a biomarker for aggressive disease and poor response to cisplatin-based chemotherapy in osteosarcoma patients. Furthermore, strategies targeting miR-155-5p, such as antisense inhibitors, or approaches to restore SOCS1 function could potentially sensitize osteosarcoma cells to conventional chemotherapy, though these possibilities require further validation. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse osteosarcoma models and patient cohorts, exploring the therapeutic potential of miR-155-5p inhibition in combination with standard chemotherapy, and further elucidating the complex regulatory networks involving miR-155-5p in osteosarcoma.
