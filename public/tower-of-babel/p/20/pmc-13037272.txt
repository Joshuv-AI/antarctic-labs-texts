Two decades (2005 to 2025) of avian influenza research trends, knowledge gaps and distribution of emerging H5N1 2.3.4.4b clade in South Asia
Authors: Md Zulfekar Ali, Uwem Okon Edet, Md Salauddin, Clement Meseko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian influenza virus (AIV) remains a persistent zoonotic and economic threat in South Asia, yet a comprehensive understanding of its research trajectory, outbreak patterns, and viral evolution in the region remains limited. This study integrates bibliometric, epidemiological, metadata, and phylogenetic analyses to assess two decades (2005–2025) of AIV research and H5N1 dynamics in South Asia. Bibliographic records were retrieved from Scopus and analysed using VOSviewer and Biblioshiny, while hemagglutinin (HA) gene sequences of H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b were obtained from GISAID and analysed using maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree. A total of 858 documents were identified, exhibiting a strong upward research trend (R²=0.90), with peak outputs between 2021 and 2024; primary research articles dominated (84.9%), and Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan emerged as the most productive countries and institutional hubs. Keyword evolution revealed sustained focus on avian influenza, H5N1, poultry, and Bangladesh, alongside the post-2020 emergence of One Health, zoonoses, and mutation-related themes. Epidemiological synthesis showed 18 confirmed human H5N1 cases reported between 2005 and 2025, predominantly in Bangladesh, indicating low but recurrent spillover events. Phylogenetic analysis of 267 clade 2.3.4.4b HA sequences revealed multiple well-supported country- and time-specific clades, with Bangladesh acting as a major center of viral persistence and evolution, particularly in domestic ducks, while cross-country clustering among Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives suggests regional viral movement likely mediated by migratory birds and poultry trade networks. Collectively, these findings highlight an interconnected H5N1 ecosystem shaped by surveillance intensity, host ecology, and transboundary transmission, underscoring the need for strengthening One Health–oriented surveillance, cross-border collaboration, and sustained genomic monitoring in South Asia.

Introduction

The avian influenza virus (AIV) is a member of the Orthomyxoviridae family and the Influenza A genus [1]. It is an enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus with octasegmented genome encoding at least 11 proteins [2]. Its hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) surface glycoproteins, which mediate viral binding, entry, and release, are also used in the classification of the virus into subtypes [3]. Furthermore, it is classified as either highly pathogenic (HPAIV), when they cause systemic infections with high mortality, or low-pathogenic (LPAIV), usually associated with mild or subclinical disease in birds [4]. The HPAIV hold a high pandemic concern over the risk of potential sustained human-to-human transmission [5, 6]. Additionally, since 2005, several AIV subtypes have caused an estimated 2,634 confirmed human cases and over 100 deaths worldwide, alongside the loss of approximately 422 million birds [6]. Continuous genetic events, including antigenic drift and shift, and selective pressure, drive the emergence of novel AIV strains globally [7], posing persistent threats to poultry production, food security, human health, and global health.

The South Asia region plays a pivotal role in the global spread of avian influenza virus, beginning with the detection of the highly pathogenic A(H5Nx) strain A/Goose/Guangdong/1/1996 (Gs/Gd) in poultry in China [8]. Over the last two decades, the virus has rapidly evolved into multiple lineages, most notably the widely circulating clade 2.3.4.4 among various H5 variants, including H5N1, H5N2, H5N6 and H5N8 [9]. This evolution is increasingly complex and less predictable [10]. Several H5Nx clades have been reported across South Asia, driving cross-species transmissions and sporadic but severe human infections [11–13]. Of particular concern is the HPAIV clade 2.3.4.4b, which emerged in 2013 and has since spread beyond Asia to Europe, Africa, and the Americas, causing widespread outbreaks in poultry and wild birds [8]. The dense human population, high poultry density, live bird markets, food processing practices, and porous transboundary animal movements in South Asia amplify the risks of viral persistence and spread [14].

India, Bangladesh, and Nepal have reported recurring H5N1 and H5N8 outbreaks since the mid-2000s, with devastating consequences for poultry industries [14–16]. Bangladesh, in particular, has been identified as a hotspot, with ongoing circulation in domestic flocks and live bird markets, and at least eight confirmed human cases to date [15]. In the same vein, India has reported the detection of various clades of the virus including H5N1, H5N8, and H9N2 strains in poultry and sporadic human cases [16]. Nepal and Pakistan have also recorded outbreaks of the virus in poultry farms, especially low-pathogenic avian influenza virus strains [17, 18]. In contrast to India, Nepal, and Bangladesh, the remaining countries, including Afghanistan, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives have reported fewer outbreaks, which could be a result of under-reporting or limited cases. However, these nations remain more vulnerable due to weak surveillance systems, close ties (economic and cultural) with others countries with higher case burdens and shared borders [19]. The epidemiological landscape of these South Asian countries is seemingly complicated by variability in diagnostic capacities, and differences in control and preventive measures which has direct implication on overseas transmission of AIV.

From the foregoing, our understanding of AIV in South Asia has several gaps. What is the current state of knowledge on AIV in the region? What is the current state of the outbreaks in the region? What is the phylogenetic relatedness of all the H5N1 2.3.4.4b clades? Bridging these gaps requires holistic approaches to provide a robust and systematic analysis of the existing knowledge landscape to identify research trends, outbreak status and phylogenetic relatedness of the various 2.3.4.4b clades in the region. Conducting a bibliometric analysis will provide a timely and rigorous assessment of the state of avian influenza research in the aforementioned South Asian nations [20]. It will provide quantitative and qualitative insights into publication trajectories, citations, collaboration networks, and thematic and keyword evolution. Furthermore, bibliometric studies enable researchers to identify knowledge trends, research hotspots, collaborative networks among authors and institutions, author and institutional productivity, and underexplored areas. Second, there is a need to understand the spread of AIV in the region by examining outbreak reports from 2005 to 2025, organized by country and year, to establish potential patterns in viral spread. Third, a phylogenetic analysis of the H5N1 2.3.4.4b clade based on the HA gene is required to evaluate its evolutionary dynamics in South Asia over time using a maximum-likelihood tree. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study on AIV covering all eight South Asian member countries and integrating bibliometric, phylogenetic, and outbreak data analyses.

Methods

Study design and source of data

To address the research questions outlined above, we employed a combination of bibliometric analysis, outbreak report analysis, and phylogenetic analysis as our study design. For the bibliometric analysis, we followed the Scientific Procedures and Rationales for Systematic Literature Reviews (SPAR-4-SLR) framework proposed by Paul et al., [21]. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the maximum likelihood method [22], and its dataset, the H5N1 sequences were source from GISAID database (https://gisaid.org/) using H5N1 2.3.4.4b clade as the search word applied to the various South Asian countries. Human cases of H5N1 in the region were obtained from outbreak report from the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) (https://www.woah.org/) and cross-references with the World Health Organisation (WHO) situation reports and peer-reviewed publications documenting confirmed human infections. Discrepancies between cases from the various sources were resolved using WOAH and WHO reports. Our bibliometric dataset was sourced from the Scopus database. The database was chosen for its comprehensiveness and coverage of diverse fields, including biomedical, medical, and social science aspects of the pathogen within the One Health framework. Scopus, being a globally recognized and comprehensively indexed abstract and citation database, offers extensive coverage of international and regional scientific journals, conference papers, and related publications [23]. Scopus has also been widely utilized in similar studies [24].

Search and analysis process

We applied the search terms highlighted below in the Scopus database to perform the search as previously reported [24]. The keywords were: TITLE-ABS-KEY(“avian influenza”) AND LIMIT-TO(AFFILCOUNTRY, “Bangladesh”, “India”, “Pakistan”, “Afghanistan”, “Bhutan”, “Maldives”, “Nepal”, “Sri Lanka”) AND (PUBYEAR > 2004 AND PUBYEAR < 2026) AND ( LIMIT-TO ( DOCTYPE, “ar” ) OR LIMIT-TO ( DOCTYPE, “re” ) ) AND ( LIMIT-TO ( PUBSTAGE, “final” ) ) AND ( LIMIT-TO ( LANGUAGE, “English” ) ). In conducting the analysis, the SPAR-4-SLR framework that encompasses identification, organization, and assessment of pertinent scholarly publications. Assembly of research articles

The search process was conducted using the SPAR-4-SLR framework, as outlined in Fig. 1. The assembling phase encompassed two key areas: identification, including the domain (AIV), research question, search type, and source quality, and acquisition, covering the search mechanism, period, keywords, and total number of documents. These terms were searched within the “article title, abstract, and keywords” fields on the Scopus database. The initial query yielded a total of 19,827 documents.

Arrangement of research articles

The retrieved documents were then refined using specific filters, including publication year, document type, source type, and language. The selection was limited to publications from 2005 to 2025, encompassing only journal articles written in English, and not in other languages, including French, Russian, and Spanish. No restrictions were made to the indexing types (whether SCI or SSCI) as well as study types. Only original peer-reviewed research and review articles were included, while book chapters and conference proceedings were excluded. This screening process reduced the dataset to 860 articles. Subsequently, two articles that were unrelated to the study’s focus were removed, leaving a final total of 858 articles for bibliometric analysis. Two reviewers performed the review separately and areas of discrepancies were settled with both parties coming to a consensus. The search query applied in Scopus is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Assessment of research articles

The bibliometric assessment, derived from a final dataset of 858 articles, utilized quantitative methods to evaluate scientific productivity and research impact. Performance analysis was conducted to explore publication trends, most-cited studies, prominent journals, authors, institutions, and contributing countries, as well as the methodological orientations and research contexts represented in the literature. Additionally, science mapping techniques were applied, such as temporal analysis using word clouds and network analysis through keyword co-occurrence, to uncover major themes in avian influenza research, identify existing knowledge gaps, and track the distribution of the emerging H5N1 2.3.4.4b clade. The overall workflow illustrating the compilation, organization, and evaluation process based on the SPAR-4-SLR framework is depicted in Fig. 1.

Bibliometrics and visualization analysis

The final dataset was analysed using a combination of diverse tools, including VOSviewer (version 1.6.20) Biblioshiny [25], RStudio (Version 2025.05.1 + 513), following established bibliometric approaches [20, 25–27]. The analysis focused on identifying research trends, emerging themes, leading authors and institutions, as well as the evolution of keywords over the past two decades. VOSviewer, a widely utilized bibliometric software, facilitated the analysis and visualization of scientific networks, covering relationships among authors, keywords, and publications, and supported advanced features such as co-occurrence mapping, network visualization, heat map generation, and cluster analysis [28].

Metadata and phylogenetic analyses

A total of 267 hemagglutinin (HA) gene sequences of H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b from South Asia were retrieved from the GISAID EpiFlu™ database (https://www.gisaid.org) following data access and usage agreements, representing isolates from multiple countries, hosts, and collection years to capture regional genetic diversity. The nucleotide sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.525 [29] with default settings, and the alignment was manually inspected in AliView v1.26 to correct misaligned regions and remove sequences with large gaps or ambiguous nucleotides. A maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE v3.0.1 [30], with the best-fit nucleotide substitution model automatically selected (GTR + F+I+G4) via the ModelFinder function, and node support was evaluated with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates, where GTR represents the General Time Reversible model with empirical base frequencies (+ F), a proportion of invariant sites (+ I), and gamma-distributed rate heterogeneity across sites with four discrete categories (+ G4). The resulting tree was visualized and annotated in iTOL v7 [31], incorporating metadata such as country of origin, host species, and year of isolation, with clade assignments following the WHO/OIE/FAO H5N1 nomenclature.

Results

Study trajectory, types and journal destinations

The publication trend of studies on AIV over the last two decades is shown in Fig. 2. The scatter plot is fitted with trend line, its equation and correlation coefficient (R2). The publication trend shows alternating periods of growth and declines in research output within the period under study. Periods of declines occurred in 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019, 2022, and 2025, each recording fewer papers than the preceding years. The most productive years were from 2021 to 2024, with 57, 67, 68, and 73 publications, respectively. In contrast 2005 to 2008, recorded the lowest levels of publications that were under 20 per year. The trend line equation and coefficient of correlation were y = − 5.78 × 10³ + 2.89x, and R² = 0.90. Despite alternating periods of increase and decline, two phases of growth were distinct. The phase of low growth spans 2005 to 2016, with publications less than 50 per year. On the other hand, the high growth phase spans 2017 to 2025 with publications ranging between 50 and slightly above 70.

Discussion

Bibliometric analysis

The publication trajectory indicates that the first report of AIV in the region appeared in 2004. Since then, there has been increasing reports of studies addressing different thematic areas of AIV. The low number of publications in the early years reflects the early stage of AIV research in the region, suggesting limited awareness and scientific engagement at that time. This observation is consistent with previous bibliometric studies on AIV globally and HIV in Nigeria [20, 27]. In contrast, the last four years of the second decade (2021–2024) recorded publication volumes at least 3.5 times higher than the average for the first four years. This sharp increase suggests growing research interest, likely driven by enhanced funding opportunities, stronger collaborations, and the continued emergence of AIV as a public health threat and economic concern. The coefficient of correlation (R² = 0.89) demonstrates a very strong positive correlation between year and publication output.

Our analysis of study types revealed a clear dominance of primary studies over secondary studies (reviews) in a ratio of approximately 6:1. This distribution is consistent with previous bibliometric assessments of various pathogens, where original studies typically account for over 84% of research output, as seen in investigations on highly pathogenic avian influenza virus (HPAIV) A, and H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b [20, 34] and HIV/AIDS in Nigeria [27]. The dominance of primary research underscores the field’s strong focus on experimental and observational investigations aimed at deepening the understanding of the virus biology, likely fuelled by its recurring outbreaks across the region.

Although, we did not discriminate the various types of reviews in our study, an earlier study showed the presence of diverse types that included scooping, narrative and systematic reviews. Reviews play a key role in synthesis of evidence in any field of research [35]. Their presence further supports the abundance of primary studies, which provide the foundation for such evidence synthesis. Similarly, the presence of systematic reviews, with or without metaanalysis, also reflects the availability of sufficient primary studies to enable evidence synthesis. Systematic reviews are regarded as the highest level of synthesized evidence in medicine and related disciplines [36].

Yearly representation of the studies showed a gradual increase in the number of studies from 2005 to 2025, and a corresponding increase in the number of citations. The most cited year was 2020, with 1,220 citations, suggesting that publications from that year had a strong scientific impact, possibly due to renewed global focus on zoonotic and emerging infectious diseases during the COVID-19 era. This aligns with previous studies that showed COVID-19 impacted other fields, including AIV globally in a previous bibliometric study, where they showed a decrease in research activity during COVID-19 [20].

Analysis of journal destinations or outlets can reveal patterns in the choice of journals by authors. Our analysis showed the presence of core journals dealing with Transboundary and or Emerging Diseases/Infections. Furthermore, it revealed the presence of speciality and multidisciplinary journals. This distribution of journal types is consistent with earlier findings that also reported the presence of multidisciplinary and core speciality journals [20, 34]. Authors’ choice of these journals may have been influenced by factors, including but not limited to journal’s reputation, peer-review quality, or journal impact factors, and the nature of the access [37–39]. The presence of high impact factor journals reflects their global prominence and influence in infectious disease research. Also, the presence of regional journals like Pakistan Journal of Zoology and Indian Journal of Medical Research suggest strong national engagement in avian influenza studies. Most publications appeared in mid- to high-impact journals (Q1–Q3), demonstrating that avian influenza research in South Asia has achieved considerable visibility in reputable international and regional scientific outlets. The choice of these outlets by the authors may have been driven by several factors, including but not limited to their IFs and or ranking, scope of the journals among other factors [20, 40].

The significant links within journal clusters indicate the increasing integration of human, animal, and environmental health research under a One Health approach [15, 41]. Co-citation patterns reflect dynamic interdisciplinary collaboration, underscoring the interconnectedness of infectious disease research. In accordance with previous research, Bradford analysis indicates that avian influenza articles are mostly found in a limited selection of core journals, according to a standard Bradford distribution pattern [42].

Keyword analysis showed the presence of core terms, sub-themes and country-related terms. The presence of core terms indicates strong and consistent co-focus on the primary viral agent, the specific H5N1 subtype, the main regional setting, and the primary reservoir species and it aligns with previous bibliometric report on AIV H5N1 [20]. On the other hand, other sub-themes, including “H9N2,” “India,” highly pathogenic avian influenza,” and “surveillance,” showed lower but steady cumulative counts that were between 38 and 40 occurrences by 2025. The moderate growth could be explained by the growing importance of H9N2 clade and surveillance in the region [43]. Further analysis of the keywords showed the evolution of keywords, including public health, and pandemic with similar usage span, highlight the growing public health and pandemic importance and potential of the virus, aligning with previous reports highlighting public health significance and the pandemic potentials of the virus [44]. In addition, keywords related to zoonoses, one health and mutation strongly emerged from 2020 onwards [45].

Authorship analysis on AIV from the region showed the dominance of a few authors. Specifically, the top two authors from India have led molecular and genetic studies on AIV strains such as H1N1, H5N1, and H9N2, as well as anti-replication work on H5N1 [46–48]. In addition, their studies covered serology, pathology, prevalence, risk factors, and molecular characterization of H5N1 and H9N2 [49–51]. The top authors have collaborated extensively across multiple studies. Together, the top ten authors produced 271 documents, representing 31.5% of total publications, underscoring their strong influence in regional virology research. This output is lower than that of the top 15 global AIV authors, who collectively published 1,043 documents (12.4%), with each contributing at least 53 papers [20]. In addition, the clear dominance of authors from the South Asia regions, including India and Bangladesh indicates local owner of the research in the region and aligns with a previous bibliometric analysis on vaccine uptake in Africa that showed dominance of African led authors and institutions [40]. Worthy of mention is the presence of authors from the USA, indicating the involvement of income rich nations in cross-border studies and this aligns with previous bibliometric studies [27, 40]. The most cited paper is by Salzberg et al., [52] in Emerging Infectious Diseases, with 191 total citations and an average of 10.05 citations per year, indicating its long-term relevance since publication. The second and third most cited studies are by Iqbal et al., [53] in Plos One and Fusaro et al., [10] in Journal of Virology, with 166 and 163 citations, respectively, and strong annual citation rates. More recent publications, such as Imran et al., [54] in Pakistan Journal of Medical Sciences and Khetrapal et al., [55] in Indian Journal of Medical Research, demonstrate high citation frequencies per year (24.00 and 18.17, respectively), suggesting that newer research on emerging strains and regional responses has gained rapid scholarly attention.

In the region, only five countries, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Thailand, engaged in multiple-country publications or collaborations, while the remaining countries had none. Other member countries in the region were notably missing from the network, indicating underreporting. These findings indicate that India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh are the main drivers of AIV-related publications in the region. Several reasons could be responsible for the higher volume of publications compared to other countries in the region. These include a combination of disease burden, research infrastructure, and structural factors influencing scientific visibility. Furthermore, the three countries are deemed richer than others and surveillance studies emanating from their institutions come with stronger international collaborations, English-language publication practices, and established funding mechanisms and as a result more likely to appear in indexed journals. On the other hand, countries such as Nepal, Bhutan, Afghanistan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives may conduct surveillance or outbreak investigations that are not published but only reported by government, thereby remaining underrepresented in bibliometric datasets. Overall, the findings highlight the limited extent of international collaborations among countries, aligning with earlier studies that emphasized the need for greater cross-border cooperation in AIV research [20].

Institutional affiliation analysis revealed the dominance of institutes and universities, with speciality in veterinary and animal sciences, particularly in India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan. They further highlight the unique nature of AIV research, demonstrated by the prominence of veterinary and animal science-based institutions, highlighting their central role in advancing AIV studies. This pattern is consistent with findings from earlier global bibliometric analyses on AIV [20].

Reported human cases of AIV in the South Asia region (2005 to 2025)

Our analysis of reported human cases showed the presence of H5N1 human cases across South Asia between 2005 and 2025. From the region, 18 human cases have been reported so far from Bangladesh, India, Nepal and Pakistan. Collectively, these events indicate low but recurrent spillover events. Spillover describes the transmission of a pathogen from its natural animal reservoir to humans [56]. These events typically arise when ecological, behavioural, or environmental conditions bring humans into close contact with infected animals [56, 57]. As a region, South Asia has a dense poultry population, backyard farming, and extensive live bird markets [58]. These economic activities fuel many livelihoods and at the same time, provide naturally heighten opportunities for zoonotic exchange [58]. In a modelling-based study of the risk factors of human cases of H5N1 avian influenza in South-east Asian countries and China, it was reported that the presence of cases in one neighbouring country increases in the risk of cases in another country, as well as an increase in human population [59]. This aligns with our finding, especially for the reported cases as these were always preceded by other cases in the following years.

They may also indicate gaps in biosecurity, as well as informal poultry trade networks that facilitate the circulation of H5 viruses among domestic and wild birds. Together, these factors continue to drive recurrent human exposure pathways and spillover events. Regardless of the underlying causes, the reported human cases underscore the dynamic nature of zoonotic influenza in the region and represent a significant public health concern.

Bangladesh recorded the highest number of human cases in the region, followed by India. Several factors may explain this pattern. First, the higher case numbers may reflect more sustained and effective surveillance compared to other Asian countries. It could also imply weak biosecurity measures, informal trade networks driving the circulation of H5 viruses among domestic and wild birds. Collectively, these factors have continued to drive recurrent human exposure pathways and spillover events being witnessed [59, 60]. On the other hand, the limited number of human cases in India, Nepal, and Pakistan illustrates substantial heterogeneity in spillover risk. The sporadic and low number of human cases suggest that existing poultry control measures, vaccination campaigns, or rapid outbreak responses may help limit human exposure, though gaps in surveillance remain possible. Regardless of cause, these human cases highlight the dynamic nature of zoonotic influenza in the region and it is great public health concern. The recent outbreak of H5N1 in dairy cattle in the USA further highlights the fact H5N1 is a great public health concern [61]. In South Asia, where poultry production is deeply integrated into livelihoods and food systems, there is a need for heightened biosecurity to avert potential outbreak in the future.

Metadata characteristics of clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 viruses in South Asia

Metadata associated with viral genome sequences provide essential information that strengthens molecular and phylogenetic analyses by linking viral evolution to host species, geographic origin, and time of collection [62]. Properly curated and annotated metadata for pathogens like H5N1 allows for the reconstruction of transmission pathways, identification of reservoir hosts, and assessment of spatiotemporal trends in virus circulation. In this study, metadata of clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 viruses circulating in South Asia were analysed to elucidate host distribution patterns, country-level sampling intensity, and temporal dynamics.

Our country against source analyses of the metadata indicates that ducks are the predominant sampling host across the region, with Bangladesh contributing the majority of duck-derived sequences, indicating that they are principal and critical reservoir for the persistence and maintenance of H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b viruses in South Asia [63]. Their ability to act as critical reservoirs could be explained from two points of views. First, experimental studies indicate that ducks experience subclinical or mild infections, allowing sustained viral replication and shedding without obvious disease [64]. Furthermore, ducks act as bridge between wild birds and poultry, and in the process facilitates silent transmission within backyard farming systems, live bird markets, and wetland-associated agro-ecological interfaces [63, 65]. The high number of duck-derived sequences from Bangladesh indicates the epidemiological importance and targeted surveillance strategies on-going in the region and focusing on high-risk poultry species [66, 67].

In addition to ducks, additional hosts including chickens, wild birds, quail, crows, and environmental samples were identified, although at lower frequencies. The presence of chicken among the hosts is in line with previous reports in the region [68]. From an epidemiology and public health point of view, the detection of the virus is very important due to their high susceptibility to highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) A/H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b and their central role in regional poultry production, where outbreaks have been linked to economic losses in the region [69]. The duck and wild birds act as the primary source for viral reassortment and human spillover in South Asia. While improving baseline surveillance in Nepal or Sri Lanka is important for regional biosecurity, the immediate priority must be the intensive monitoring of viral flow at the live-bird market interface in high-burden zones.

Detection of clade 2.3.4.4b viruses in wild birds and synanthropic species such as crows suggests ongoing spillover and potential bidirectional transmission between domestic poultry and wild avifauna. Environmental detections of the virus indicate potential indirect transmission routes through contaminated water bodies or live bird market environments. The presence of the H5N1 in other reservoirs affirms the multiple host ability of the H5N1 virus [70]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the broad host range and ecological flexibility of clade 2.3.4.4b viruses, which have demonstrated exceptional capacity for cross-species transmission and geographic expansion during the ongoing global panzootic [71]. Despite clade 2.3.4.4b’s widespread mammalian tropism [72], we detected no report in mammals, livestock or wild animals in South Asia throughout the research period. The lack of records might be a surveillance gap. Given the region’s high-density interaction between people, domestic animals, and nature, mammalian data may be scarce due to a lack of non-avian host monitoring. Peridomestic animals and cattle may operate as “intermediate vessels” for human adaptation, disguising the region’s viral circulation, making observation of them vital.

The country-versus-year heatmap reveals substantial heterogeneity in sampling intensity across South Asia. Bangladesh consistently exhibits the highest positive samples throughout the study period. This pattern likely reflects sustained virus circulation combined with well-established national surveillance programs and active sequence sharing. In contrast, India, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives contribute relatively few sequences. Such disparities may result from limited surveillance coverage, under-reporting, or reduced sequencing capacity rather than true absence of viral circulation. Inclusively, our metadata analysis highlights Bangladesh as a regional hotspot for clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 surveillance and emphasizes the central role of ducks in viral maintenance and dissemination. The uneven distribution of metadata across countries and hosts highlights critical surveillance gaps that may obscure the true epidemiological burden of H5N1 in South Asia. Strengthening coordinated, multi-host, and region-wide surveillance remains essential for improving early detection, understanding transmission dynamics, and mitigating the risk of future poultry and zoonotic outbreaks.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic and metadata patterns reveal a dynamic, interconnected H5N1 ecosystem in South Asia probably shaped by ecological, human, and geographic factors. Sequence and metadata indicate that Bangladesh is the hub of viral evolution, with large, continuous clusters, especially the 2023–2024 and 2025 clusters, indicating sustained circulation in domestic ducks. Ducks are well known key amplifying hosts that present with high shedding, mobility, and frequent contact with wild birds and poultry [73]. This stable duck reservoir supports ongoing viral maintenance and recurrent spillover into chickens, environments, and occasionally humans. Strong yearly clustering also suggests that distinct lineages emerge and sometimes persist across multiple years [74].

The phylogenetic proximity of viral genotypes across South Asian borders indicates a shared ancestry, which is likely facilitated by the Central Asian Flyway and transborder poultry trade [75]. Nevertheless, these clusters may be a sampling artifact of the available data, as a result of the disproportionate volume of sequences from India and Bangladesh compared to the Maldives or Sri Lanka. In order to verify whether these clades represent direct transmission in the documented regional viral diversity, future studies require denser sampling from under-represented regions.

Mixed Bangladesh–India clades in 2024 suggest recent introductions or parallel evolution, while Maldives isolates nested within broader South Asian clades point to introductions likely via migratory birds, and this aligns with a previous report that showed mixed circulation of various earlier clades in South Asian countries [76]. Metadata patterns support these interpretations. For instance, the dominance of duck and Bangladesh samples reflects both true viral ecology and surveillance focus. Environmental and fecal sampling also indicates active contamination in farms and markets [77]. The evidence underscores the need for stronger One Health surveillance across South Asia, integrating duck and wild-bird monitoring with genetic tracking to detect new lineages, understand cross-border spread, and reduce spillover risks to poultry and humans.

Limitations

The present study has some limitations that we must acknowledged. First, we utilised only the Scopus database and in so doing may have left out unique studies in other databases, including the Grey literature, PubMed and Web of Sciences. Grey literature was left out due to the fact that its data cannot be analysed using the VOSviewer and Biblioshiny tools. Second, we limited the search to English Language and in the process, may have left out studies written in other languages. Though we found only three documents written in French, Russian and Spanish, respectively in the Scopus database, we did not consider and it will not affect the results.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated bibliometric, epidemiological, metadata, and phylogenetic assessment of avian influenza research and H5N1 dynamics in South Asia from 2005 to 2025. Bibliometric trends reveal uneven but accelerating research growth, peaking between 2021 and 2024, with India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh accounting for nearly 96% of publications and citations, reflecting both disease burden and research capacity. Analysis of outbreak data indicates recurrent but low-level human H5N1 spillover events, predominantly in Bangladesh, underscoring sustained zoonotic risk despite limited case numbers. Metadata patterns highlight the dominance of duck-associated and Bangladesh-derived samples, reflecting both true viral ecology and targeted surveillance, while frequent environmental and fecal detections point to ongoing viral contamination in farms and live bird markets. Phylogenetic analysis of clade 2.3.4.4b HA sequences reveals multiple well-supported, temporally and geographically structured lineages, with Bangladesh acting as a major center of viral persistence and evolution, and evidence of cross-border viral movement linking Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives, likely mediated by migratory birds and poultry trade networks. Despite these insights, collaboration within South Asia and with key global partners remains limited, and critical research themes, including genomic surveillance, biosecurity, live bird market dynamics, wildlife reservoirs, and cross-border transmission, remain underrepresented in the literature examine. Collectively, these findings highlight an interconnected regional H5N1 ecosystem shaped by host ecology, surveillance intensity, and transboundary transmission, emphasizing the urgent need for a coordinated One Health–oriented surveillance, strengthened regional collaboration, and sustained genomic monitoring to mitigate future zoonotic and pandemic risks in South Asia.

In particular, we suggest the following actionable steps:

Creating a regional agreement or harmonization of policies for the quick exchange of AIV genomic data in order to monitor viral changes in real time.Making use of South Asian counties current research leadership to provide diagnostic assistance and laboratory training to countries with less capability for surveillance.The transition from disease-specific financing to integrated surveillance models via intersectoral funding that concurrently track human clinical cases and live bird markets.
