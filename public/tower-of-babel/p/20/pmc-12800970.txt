Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With Additional Remnant-Preserving Stump Repair Using a Button and Suture-Tensioning Technique
Authors: Lun Li, GuoDong Wu, Yuanyue Shi, Guofu Ma, Baoyu Chen, Guangping Deng, Yuqiang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior cruciate ligament injuries or ruptures significantly impair knee mobility and overall joint function. Surgical reconstruction is the standard treatment, most commonly performed using autologous semitendinosus and gracilis tendons. Recently, remnant-preserving anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction has gained popularity as a result of favorable clinical outcomes, and it is increasingly adopted by surgeons. The essential steps of the button with suture-tensioning technique are as follows: before graft passage, sutures are preloaded through the button portals; after drilling the bone tunnels, the preloaded sutures are advanced through the tibial tunnel into the joint cavity and retrieved for later use; once the graft is fixed in the standard manner, the femoral stump is sutured arthroscopically, tensioned, and secured at the femoral tunnel aperture. This method is cost-effective, reproducible, and technically straightforward. By pulling back the prepositioned sutures, the button’s flipping mechanism can be tested, minimizing the risk of incomplete seating against the femoral cortex caused by interposed soft tissue. Simultaneously, this maneuver tensions the residual stump, enhances joint stability, seals the femoral tunnel opening, prevents joint fluid backflow, and decreases the likelihood of graft impingement during knee extension.

The most commonly performed procedures for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR)are remnant-preserving ACLR and conventional ACLR. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that remnant-preserving ACLR provides superior outcomes in functional scores (Lysholm, International Knee Documentation Committee), knee stability, and tibial tunnel preservation compared with standard ACLR.1 In pursuit of improved outcomes, several remnant-preserving techniques have been developed. In 2008, a hamstring graft with looped sutures was introduced for remnant-preserving ACLR that yielded favorable clinical results.2 In 2015, Boutsiadis et al.3 described the “lasso-loop” knot configuration, followed in 2020 by Singh et al.,4 who reported the “remnant envelope technique.” Both techniques involve suturing the ACL stump and securing the knots at the extra-articular femoral end through a small incision after reconstruction. In 2022, El-Desouky et al.5 reported the use of direct intra-articular suturing with a suture hook, concluding that remnant-preserving ACLR is safe and provides comparable functional outcomes with standard ACLR while offering superior proprioceptive benefits. Although remnant-preserving ACLR is increasingly accepted, standardized procedural criteria are lacking. To address this gap, we propose a modified approach—the button with suture tensioning technique—which incorporates remnant preservation into ACLR. In this technique, remnant preservation is achieved using a button preloaded with sutures. After graft fixation, the stump is secured with a crossed suture pattern and intra-articular knotting, thereby anchoring the graft to both the reconstructed ligament and the femoral tunnel aperture. This method requires no additional incision, ensures consistent suture quality, and provides excellent reproducibility.

Surgical Technique

Positioning

The patient is placed in the supine position under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Femoral tunnel drilling and passage of the traction sutures are performed with the knee flexed to 90° in the standard “figure-4 position.” The remainder of the procedure is completed with the knee maintained at 90° flexion and suspended at the bedside.

Surgical Procedure

The arthroscope is introduced through the anterolateral portal for a comprehensive inspection of the suprapatellar pouch, patellofemoral joint, medial and lateral recesses, cruciate ligaments, and menisci. Proliferative synovium is debrided using a shaver and radiofrequency probe. Under arthroscopic visualization, a femoral-sided ACL tear is confirmed (Fig 1), and the ACL stump is preserved. Through a small incision at the proximal calf, the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are harvested and prepared into a graft with a diameter >7.5 mm and a length >8 cm. A No. 5 high-strength suture is passed through each portal of the button and folded into equal-length traction lines for later use (Fig 2). If a meniscal injury is encountered, it is repaired or resected according to the lesion type and severity.

Using a femoral aiming guide positioned at the anteromedial bundle footprint (figure-4 position for the left knee), a guide pin is inserted (Fig 3). The femoral tunnel is reamed sequentially with a 4.5-mm cannulated drill and then with a reamer matching the graft diameter (Fig 4). The tibial tunnel is created immediately posterior to the ACL stump footprint (Fig 5), with care taken to preserve the remnant tissue (Fig 6). Traction line 1 is passed through the femoral and tibial tunnels via the guide pin. It is then used to pull the preloaded traction line 2 (button sutures) and traction line 3 through the tibial tunnel into the joint cavity (Fig 7). Two equal lengths of traction line 2 are retrieved from the joint through the anterolateral portal and reserved (Fig 8). Traction line 1 is subsequently pulled out of the tibial tunnel via traction line 3 to ensure that the graft and preloaded sutures are not obstructed by the ACL stump. After removing traction line 3, the graft is pulled into the bone tunnels using traction line 1 (Fig 9). The femoral button is then flipped and secured at the lateral femoral cortex (Fig 10). The graft is tensioned, the femoral side confirmed to be securely fixed, and the tibial side was fixed with a bioabsorbable interference screw. Arthroscopic inspection confirmed good tension and anatomic positioning of the reconstructed ACL (Fig 11).

Discussion

An increasing number of surgeons are embracing and using stump-preserving ACLR because its remarkable results. Compared with standard ACLR, ACLR with preserved stumps (mainly using autologous popliteal grafts) results in better clinical outcome scores, significant improvement in knee stability, and no increase in complication rates.6 This may be because the retained stump fibers from the stump-preserving technique can act as a “bioscaffold” for the graft, providing additional mechanical support and reducing early postoperative laxity.7 In addition, bone tunnel enlargement has become an unavoidable early complication after ACLR, whether it is a single-bundle or double-bundle ACLR. This may be caused by a combination of mechanical factors (e.g., the “elastic cord effect” and the “wiper effect”) and biological factors (e.g., cytokines and inflammatory mediators).

Li et al.8 have shown that preservation of the ACL stump can help to minimize the enlargement of the bony tunnel. Additional benefits include accelerated graft ligamentization, enhanced synovial coverage and vascularization, superior proprioceptive function, and improved knee stability. It also closes the femoral tunnel, prevents leakage of joint fluid, promotes tendon healing, and prevents tunnel enlargement. Lee et al.9 suggest that this is because ACL stump preservation accelerates graft revascularization and increases the number of mechanoreceptors. High levels of ACL stump gene expression in the acute phase contribute to ligament healing and graft remodeling. Stump preservation also has the potential benefit of reducing postoperative laxity and improving quadriceps muscle tone.10 Stump preservation for ACLR has been increasingly accepted and used by surgeons because its advantages; nevertheless, its potential to increase the rate of adverse effects and postoperative complications remains to be resolved.11 Therefore, the search for simple and reproducible stump-preserving reconstructive treatments that reduce the complication rate plays an important role in improving patient prognosis.

We modified the procedure for residual-preserving reconstruction by prepositioning the suture in the portal in the button before delivering the graft into the joint. After preparation of the femoral and tibial marrow tracts, the prepositioned suture was first entered into the knee joint cavity through the tibial tunnel and then was pulled out of the knee joint cavity through the arthroscopic approach for backup, and after the fixation of the grafted tendon for ACLR was completed in a routine way, the residual end was sutured using suture hooks to form a crossed fixation, and the suture was tightened by tying a knot. The stump was fixed in the intra-articular tunnel at the femoral end. This is known as the button with suture-tensioning technique. In the early surgical procedure, we pulled the traction line 2 together with the reconstructed ACL into the femoral and tibial tunnels and then searched for the traction line 2 in the articular cavity after fixation of the reconstructed femoral end of the ACL, but we found that it was difficult to locate the prepositioned traction line 2 behind the reconstructed tendon, and even if we found it without difficulty, it was difficult to pull it out because it was being squeezed by the reconstructed tendon in the tibial tunnel. Therefore, we modified the surgical procedure by placing the prepositioned traction line 2 on the reconstructed ACL at the end of the femoral tunnel and then drawing the traction line 2 out of the joint cavity through the tibial tunnel before pulling the reconstructed ACL into the tibial tunnel. The use of traction line 3 allows traction line 2, which is used to suture the residual tendon, and the reconstructed ACL to be in the same tunnel, so that they will not be compressed by the residual tendon or the soft tissues, and at the same time avoiding the intertwining of the over-lines.

This technique has the advantages of traditional residual preservation procedures, whereas this technique has the following advantages over traditional residual preservation reconstruction (Table 1). In the button with suture tensioning technique for residual ACLR, the force of the suture line is consistent with the direction of the tendon, which can greatly improve the tension of the residual tendon and synergistically enhance the stability of the joint, and at the same time, close the tunnel opening of the end of the femur, which is more effective in preventing the flow of the joint fluid backward. The button with suture-tensioning technique only sutures the residual tendon without touching the reconstructed tendon, and the retraction direction of the suture is at the inner opening of the femoral tunnel, which increases the contact area between the residual tendon and the reconstructed tendon, accelerates the revascularization of the reconstructed ligament, and increases the number of mechanoreceptors. The same consumables are used as in conventional ACLR, which is cost-effective. The procedure is easy to perform and maneuverable. The tendon reconstructed by the button with suture tensioning technique is posterior to the residual tendon because the anchor point for intra-articular suture fixation is at the inner portion of the reconstructed femoral tunnel, and the force of the suture will pull the residual tendon toward the inner portion of the femoral tunnel. The crossed fixation binds the residual tendon as a whole while simultaneously depressing the residual tendon, which reduces the volume of the residual tendon and minimizes the incidence of impingement during extension. Another type of residual preservation scheme3,4 sutures the ligament in the same direction as the ACL, but it needs to suture the residual tendon before pulling the reconstructed tendon into the bone tunnel for fixation, which increases the risk of entanglement of the suture line with the button line, and the location of the knotting is outside the femoral lateral articulation, which adds a surgical incision, and it cannot be seen directly to see whether there is any soft tissue snagging in the knotting. Button with suture-tensioning technique fixes the reconstructed tendon before suturing, which reduces the risk of thread entanglement during the tendon crossing process. The location of the knot is inside the joint, and the tightness of the knot can be determined microscopically without soft tissue snagging, by pulling back with force on the preset retractor thread, it can be tested to see whether the femoral side is flipping the button and at the same time, it can reduce the chances of the button not being able to tightly attach to the femur bone wall because of the soft-tissue blockage.

However, the proposed button with suture-tensioning technique is not without potential drawbacks. The final knot cannot be tied with a slip knot and requires excellent arthroscopic knotting skills to complete the procedure. If the residual ACL is of poor quality, this technique cannot be used. Because of the obstruction of the surgical field by the residual tendon, the establishment of the bone tunnel is compromised. There is a risk of medical injury to the residual tendon during the creation of the femoral and tibial tunnels. These risks can be avoided if the surgeon using the technique is skillful in the technique and has a strict understanding of the indications. The surgical pearls and pitfalls are summarized in Table 2. Equipment required for technique are summarized in Table 3. In conclusion, the modification of the traditional ACL reconstruction technique by the use of a button with line distraction is an effective, safe, and reproducible ACLR technique.
