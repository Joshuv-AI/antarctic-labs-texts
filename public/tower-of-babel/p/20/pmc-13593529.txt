Anteroseptal implantation of a leadless atrial pacemaker: Relevant anatomy for alternate sites into the Bachmann area
Authors: Alexandra Brigido, Jake Martinez, Roderick Tung
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Implantation of a leadless atrial pacemaker in the right atrial appendage can be limited by anatomical variability and suboptimal fixation parameters, requiring alternative strategies.•The anteroseptal aspect of the the right atrial appendage base may provide a stable substrate for leadless device fixation with acceptable electrical performance and potentially superior atrial synchrony.•Anteroseptal implantation adjacent to bifurcating fibers of the Bachmann bundle can promote faster and more synchronous atrial activation, as suggested by narrower paced P waves compared to sinus rhythm.

Introduction

Leadless pacemaker (LP) systems represent an emerging alternative to conventional transvenous systems, offering a less invasive approach for patients requiring pacing.1, 2, 3 More recently, single-chamber leadless atrial pacing has expanded options for selected patients with sinus node dysfunction and preserved atrioventricular (AV) conduction.4,5 Current implantation techniques typically target the posterolateral base of the right atrial appendage (RAA) and adjacent arcuate ridge, where tissue characteristics and catheter orientation usually allow stable fixation and acceptable electrical performance.6,7 However, atrial pacing site influences intra- and interatrial conduction, and alternative sites that engage the Bachmann bundle and adjacent regions have been proposed to shorten P-wave duration and improve atrial synchrony.8, 9, 10, 11 We report a case of intracardiac echocardiography (ICE)–guided alternate site implantation of a leadless atrial pacemaker (Aveir, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) within the right atrium (RA), after unsuccessful attempts at standard targets, resulting in P-wave narrowing, suggestive of recruitment of anterior interatrial conduction pathways, with stable device performance at 6-month follow-up.

Case report

A 78-year-old woman with sick sinus syndrome, presenting with symptomatic bradycardia (heart rates in the 30s), nonperfusing ventricular escape beats, and chronotropic incompetence detected by an implantable loop monitor, was referred for pacemaker implantation. As AV conduction was preserved, she was considered an appropriate candidate for AAI(R) pacing with a leadless atrial device (Aveir AR, Abbott).

An alternative implantation site was sought anterior to the fossa ovalis. This region was examined and delineated by ICE and as a thick portion of the most septal aspect of the RAA base adjacent to the torus aorticus (indentation within the RA across from the noncoronary cusp) (Figure 2). Fixation at this location (overall attempt #12) resulted in excellent initial electrical parameters: capture threshold 1.25 V at 0.4 ms, sensing amplitude 1.5 mV, and impedance 350 Ω. A current of injury with a spike-and-dome pattern was observed (Figure 1B), confirming stable fixation. 1 day postimplantation, chest radiography confirmed stable septal device location, and the electrocardiogram showed a narrowed P-wave during atrial pacing (Figure 1C and 1D). At discharge, device interrogation demonstrated improved electrical parameters: capture threshold 0.75 V at 0.4 ms, sensing amplitude 2.7 mV, and impedance 300 Ω. The patient remained asymptomatic and experienced no complications. At 6-month follow-up, capture thresholds (<0.5 V at 0.4 ms), sensing, and impedance parameters remained stable.

Discussion

The development of LP technology has expanded pacing options by reducing the incidence of pocket- and lead-related complications compared with conventional transvenous systems.1, 2, 3 Although initially developed for ventricular pacing, the Aveir AR system extended these benefits to atrial-only pacing, particularly in patients with sick sinus syndrome and preserved AV conduction, and challenged the broad use of dual-chamber systems for sinus node dysfunction.4,5 In addition, newer technologies such as the Micra AV LP (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MA) and the recently introduced Aveir DR have further broadened the clinical indications for leadless pacing by enabling AV-synchronous pacing and true dual-chamber pacing, respectively.12,13

The Aveir AR device has demonstrated robust mechanical fixation and favorable electrical characteristics in preclinical models, supporting its safe application in multiple atrial regions.4 Standard implantation technique for atrial LPs recommends targeting the posterolateral base of the RAA (approximately 10–11 o’clock in the left anterior oblique view) using fluoroscopy and ICE. This approach aims to optimize tissue contact and stability during device positioning as well as pacing and sensing performance.5,6

In the presented case, repeated testing at standard RAA targets failed to provide acceptable electrical measurements, prompting successful off-label placement of the LP in the interatrial septum, anterior to the fossa ovalis. Higher portions of the RA lateral wall were also briefly assessed but not pursued, given variable wall thickness related to epicardial fat and proximity to the phrenic nerve and sinoatrial artery, with phrenic capture reported to be more consistent near the superior vena cava–RA junction.7 Prior anatomic and imaging analyses have raised concerns regarding septal implantation (septal mobility, potential for inadvertent left atrial deployment in the setting of a patent foramen ovale, and catheter angulation constraints from the femoral approach) while also demonstrating that septal regions can exhibit favorable contact metrics in dynamic modeling.7 These considerations emphasize the role of imaging guidance and real-time electrical assessment when considering nonstandard sites.

Safety near the aorta is also a central concern at our target site. Zoppo et al14 described the close relationship between the proximal (“antral”) RAA and the ascending aorta and highlighted that thin atrial tissue between pectinate muscles in this region may be susceptible to perforation and even aortic injury with active fixation leads. In our case, ICE enabled selection of a visibly thick muscular ridge at the septal RAA base and continuous real-time visualization of adjacent structures during deployment and repositioning, supporting a pragmatic safety approach when operating near the torus aorticus. However, long-term follow-up is necessary to rule out aortic injury from potential erosion into this structure, as this is the RA bulge that is anatomically correlated with the noncoronary sinus of Valsalva.15

From an electrophysiologic standpoint, we observed a P-wave duration of 136 ms during atrial pacing, compared with 148 ms in sinus rhythm, suggesting improved interatrial synchrony (Figure 3A). Anatomically, this atrial pacing site lies anterior to the traditional RA lead position at the septal aspect of the superior vena cava–RA junction. Therefore, it may engage anterior interatrial conduction via Bachmann bundle, a bow tie–shaped tract that bifurcates around both atrial appendages, and, on the left side, extends toward the ligament of Marshall and then to fibers anterior to the left atrial appendage (Figure 3B).15 The size and extent of the anatomic region in which atrial activation fulfills electrocardiographic Bachmann bundle criteria remain unknown and are the subject of active investigation within our group. Although the paced P-wave morphology in our case did not meet all published electrocardiographic criteria proposed for definitive Bachmann bundle capture,16 our findings may reflect recruitment of anterior interatrial conduction pathways.

Clinical outcome benefits of septal and alternative atrial pacing strategies remain conflicting. Mechanistically, comparative assessments of septal and RAA pacing support the impact of the pacing site on electromechanical delay, and Bachmann bundle pacing has been shown to reduce intra- and interatrial conduction delays and enhance biatrial synchrony,9, 10, 11 with an associated lower burden of atrial arrhythmias.16,17 However, these physiologic improvements have not translated consistently into durable clinical benefit. A meta-analysis of interatrial septal pacing did not demonstrate improved persistent/permanent atrial fibrillation–free survival,18 and the Septal Pacing for Atrial Fibrillation Suppression Evaluation Trial similarly found that septal pacing did not prevent the development of persistent atrial fibrillation compared with RAA pacing.19

To our knowledge, 2 leadless cases targeting the Bachmann bundle region have been reported. Bailin et al20 described a wireless atrial pacemaker successfully implanted at the Bachmann bundle region, and Hollis et al21 reported leadless atrial pacing targeting the Bachmann bundle for atrial resynchronization. Our case highlights relevant septal anatomy for alternate septal implantation of an atrial LP that could signal an important shift away from lateral RAA base implantation. Anteroseptal implantation at the base of the RAA may also engage the bifurcating and redundant fibers that comprise the Bachmann bundle and shorten the P-wave duration relative to traditional implantation within the RAA and sinus rhythm.

Conclusion

Anteroseptal implantation of a leadless atrial pacemaker may represent a safe and effective alternative site when standard RAA positioning is unattainable, offering potential benefits in electrical synchrony, which, in turn, may reduce the burden of atrial arrhythmias. In this case, an alternate septal site was associated with P-wave narrowing, suggestive of recruitment of anterior interatrial conduction pathways. Although this approach remains off-label, it expands the versatility of leadless atrial pacing and warrants further evaluation in larger patient cohorts and longer-term follow-up.
