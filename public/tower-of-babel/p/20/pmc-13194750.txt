Cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 stabilizes BNIP3 to facilitate mitophagy and mitigate myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury
Authors: Lingfeng Zhong, Zhenfeng Cheng, Yucong Zhang, Xiaoxi Fan, Yixin Zhou, Fan Yu, Ruihan Zheng, Derong Chen, Keke Ye, Jiahui Lin, Xi Chen, Zhouqing Huang, Chan Chen, Deling Yin, Weijian Huang, Bozhi Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
GPX4 is a crucial regulator of ferroptosis, yet its role in mitochondrial dysfunction during myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI) is unclear. This study aims to clarify the effect and molecular mechanisms of GPX4 in MI/RI. We analyzed the spatiotemporal dynamics of GPX4 during MI/RI and observed high expression levels in border and normal areas but a significant reduction in the ischemic region utilizing spatial transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and single-cell sequencing. Cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 notably reduces myocardial damage and mitochondrial dysfunction in MI/RI while also alleviating long-term ventricular remodeling. Mechanistically, our findings reveal that GPX4, through its critical U46 active site, enhances the interaction between BNIP3 and USP20, decreasing ubiquitination at K131 of BNIP3. This process stabilizes BNIP3, promotes mitophagy, improves mitochondrial function, and ultimately preserves cardiac function. Our research defines the role of the GPX4/BNIP3/USP20 complex in MI/RI and uncovers a mechanism linking GPX4 to ferroptosis-related mitochondrial damage, providing valuable insights for advancing ferroptosis studies.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) continues to be a leading cause of mortality globally1,2. The Current standard treatment for AMI involves swift and effective myocardial reperfusion, achieved through either thrombolytic therapy or primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), which is essential in alleviating ischemic injury and minimizing infarct size3. Despite the associated benefits, reperfusion can paradoxically induce additional myocardial injury, a phenomenon known as myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI)4,5. This reperfusion injury can initiate various forms of cardiomyocyte death, including apoptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis6,7. Given the terminal differentiation and limited regenerative capacity of cardiomyocytes, strategies aimed at suppressing cardiomyocyte death are critical for preserving cardiac function following reperfusion8.

Ischemia followed by reperfusion inflicts significant detrimental effects on cardiomyocytes, particularly by disrupting intracellular glutathione (GSH) metabolism9,10. This disruption culminates in the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which is closely associated with ferroptosis11. Ferroptosis, an emerging form of programmed cell death characterized by the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxides, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of various cardiovascular diseases, including MI/RI12,13. This non-apoptotic form of cell death is primarily driven by the excessive accumulation of intracellular ROS resulting from impaired ROS-scavenging systems, such as disturbances in GSH biosynthesis and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) homeostasis14. GPX4 serves as a vital inhibitor of ROS-induced phospholipid peroxidation and has been recognized as a key regulator of ferroptosis15,16. Researches have demonstrated that MI/RI correlates with the downregulation of GPX4, leading to ferroptosis processes17,18. Conversely, upregulation of GPX4 has shown promise in mitigating myocardial injury and improving cardiac function19,20. Therefore, elucidating mechanisms through which GPX4 enhances outcomes following MI/RI will significantly bolster its therapeutic potential.

An imbalance in mitochondrial morphology disrupts energy production, enhances mitochondrial ROS generation, and destabilizes calcium homeostasis. Collectively, these factors contribute to cardiomyocyte death following MI/RI in the context of AMI21,22. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving mitochondrial morphology and functionality may provide a crucial approach for cardioprotection. Mitophagy, which maintains mitochondrial quality by selectively degrading damaged mitochondria and preserving a healthy mitochondrial population, plays a crucial role in cardiomyocyte survival and cardiac function stabilization23–25. BNIP3 (BCL2/adenovirus e1B 19 kDa protein interacting protein 3) serves as a mitochondrial autophagy receptor essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality control and has significant implications in cardiovascular diseases26–28. Recent studies have demonstrated that BNIP3-related mitophagy can mitigate ferroptosis through the reduction of mitochondrial ROS levels29. However, there remains a limited understanding regarding the direct correlation between GPX4 and BNIP3-mediated mitophagy.

In this study, we present the direct evidence of a link between GPX4 and BNIP3-mediated mitophagy. We initially elucidate the functional significance of a tripartite complex comprising GPX4, BNIP3, and deubiquitinating enzyme USP20 in the context of MI/RI. GPX4 facilitates the interaction between USP20 and BNIP3, thereby stabilizing BNIP3 protein and enhancing its role in mitophagy. This mechanism helps maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, improves mitochondrial function, and ultimately mitigates MI/RI. Consequently, our findings contribute to a deeper molecular understanding of GPX4’s involvement in MI/RI while providing valuable theoretical insights into mitochondrial impairment associated with ferroptosis.

Results

Spatiotemporal distribution of GPX4 in MI/RI

MI/RI triggers multiple forms of programmed cell death, but the timing and interplay of these processes remain unclear, prompting us to investigate the role of ferroptosis in its early stages. To explore this hypothesis, we administered various inhibitors targeting cell death via intraperitoneal injection in mice two hours prior to the MI/RI procedure. The mice subsequently underwent surgery to induce 45 minutes of myocardial ischemia followed by 24 hours of reperfusion (Supplementary Fig. S1a). The results from triphenyltetrazolium chloride/Evans Blue (TTC/Evans Blue) staining suggested that emricasan (Emr), an inhibitor of apoptosis, and ferrostatin-1 (Fer), an inhibitor of ferroptosis, reduced myocardial infarct size after 24 hours. In contrast, neither 3-Methyladenine (3MA), an autophagy inhibitor, nor necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), a necroptosis inhibitor, exhibited similar effects during this time frame (Supplementary Fig. S1b–d). Furthermore, treatments with Emr and Fer-1 inhibited the elevation of MI/RI-induced cardiac injury biomarkers, such as serum cTnT, CK-MB, and LDH. 3MA mildly alleviated the elevation of cTnT and CK-MB following myocardial injury but had no significant effect on LDH levels. Notably, Fer-1 demonstrated superior efficacy among them (Supplementary Fig. S1e–g). Consistent with these findings, Fer-1 significantly improved cardiomyocyte viability while reducing cell injury (Supplementary Fig. S1h, i). In conclusion, our study provides substantial evidence indicating that ferroptosis is critically involved in the acute phase of MI/RI.

Given the significant therapeutic efficacy of Fer-1, we conducted a Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on differentially expressed genes in cardiomyocytes treated with either the vehicle (HR + Veh) or Fer-1 (HR + Fer-1). Notably, lipid-related signaling pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. 1a). Among ferroptosis-related genes, GPX4, an enzyme responsible for scavenging lipid peroxides (LPs), plays a critical role in ferroptosis associated with MI/RI (Fig. 1b). To delineate the spatial distribution of GPX4 in myocardium affected by MI/RI, we analyzed the published data to investigate its spatial expression pattern. In the previous study, myocardial tissues from patients with myocardial infarction were categorized into necrotic regions (ischemic zones and border zones) and unaffected left ventricular myocardium (normal zones)30 (Fig. 1c). We observed that GPX4 was highly expressed in both the border and normal zones, whereas its expression was significantly diminished in the ischemic zone (Fig. 1d). Subsequently, we examined the expression levels of GPX4 in myocardial tissues from patients suffering from ischemic cardiomyopathy. Utilizing non-ischemic hearts as controls, we found that both mRNA and protein levels of GPX4 were significantly reduced in ischemic myocardial tissues (Fig. 1e–g). To further enhance clinical relevance, we subsequently assessed circulating GPX4 levels in patients with acute myocardial infarction who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Interestingly, when applying the widely used Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) scoring system to assess risk stratification and prognostic outcomes31, we observed that lower serum GPX4 levels were strongly associated with higher GRACE scores (Fig. 1h). To further validate our findings in mouse myocardial tissues subjected to MI/RI, we employed spatial proteomics to investigate the spatial expression profile of GPX4 under these conditions. Similarly, we categorized the mouse myocardial tissue into three distinct regions: normal, border, and ischemic areas (Supplementary Fig. S2a, b). Consistent with prior observations12,32, GPX4 protein levels showed a declining trend in the ischemic area. Conversely, levels in the border area were comparable to those found in the normal area (Supplementary Fig. S2c). Immunofluorescence staining for GPX4 in mouse heart tissues subjected to MI/RI demonstrated a similar phenomenon (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Additionally, our results revealed that both protein and mRNA levels of Gpx4 decreased in a time-dependent manner in MI/RI-induced myocardium tissues (Supplementary Fig. 2e–g).

To identify the cellular source of downregulated GPX4 in ischemic myocardium, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) on approximately 19192 single cells isolated from the MI/RI-treated heart tissues. Based on the expression of specific marker genes, we classified the cells into seven distinct types: cardiomyocytes (CMs), endothelial cells (ECs), fibroblasts (FBs), macrophages (MPs), smooth muscle cells (SMC), and T cells. (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Interestingly, Gpx4 was broadly expressed across various cell populations in the heart, with the highest proportion observed in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 3b). Besides, scRNA-seq data showed that Gpx4 expression was highest in cardiomyocytes under baseline conditions (39.31% in sham) and accounted for 27.05% after MI/RI (Fig. 1j, Supplementary Fig. S3c). Consistently, isolated cardiomyocytes from adult mice after MI/RI also exhibited markedly decreased GPX4 expression (Fig. 1k, Supplementary Fig. S3d). Based on canonical markers, cardiomyocytes were further subdivided into ischemic, damaged, and remodeling clusters (Supplementary Fig. S3e). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that Tnnt2 decreased and then recovered, Myh7 progressively increased during remodeling, while Gpx4 declined sharply during the ischemic phase and showed no substantial rebound thereafter (Supplementary Fig. S3f–h). These findings highlight cardiomyocytes as the predominant source of GPX4 and indicate that its loss is closely linked to ischemic injury and impaired recovery. Although the role of GPX4 expressed in other cell types in ferroptosis cannot be overlooked33,34, we focused on cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 for the subsequent studies. Consistent with our in vivo results, H/R induction triggered a decrease in GPX4 protein and mRNA levels of neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) (Fig. 1l, m, Supplementary Fig. S3i). Altogether, our findings delineate the spatiotemporal distribution of GPX4 in MI/RI, demonstrating a time-dependent reduction in ischemic cardiomyocytes. Importantly, this experimental evidence is supported by clinical observations, where decreased circulating GPX4 levels in acute myocardial infarction patients were associated with adverse prognostic indicators.

Cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 was identified as a protective factor in MI/RI

We constructed a cardiomyocyte-targeting adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vector that encodes Gpx4 (AAV9-cTnT-Gpx4oe) or an empty vector (AAV9-cTnT-EV). WT (wild-type) mice received tail vein injections of either AAV9-cTnT-Gpx4oe or AAV9-cTnT-EV, resulting in confirmed overexpression of GPX4 in myocardial tissue. After a four-week period that ensured stable GPX4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d), the mice were subjected to MI/RI surgery, involving 45 minutes of ischemia followed by 24 hours of reperfusion, or underwent a sham operation (Fig. 2a). Infarct size was evaluated through Evans blue/TTC staining. Our findings revealed that cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression of GPX4 significantly reduced the myocardial infarct area induced by MI/RI (Fig. 2b–d). Notably, similar trends were observed in cardiac injury biomarks, including serum cTnT, serum CK-MB, and serum LDH levels (Fig. 2e–g). To further investigate the relationship between GPX4 and MI/RI, WT mice were administered RSL3, a specific inhibitor of GPX4, at a dosage of 30 mg/kg via oral gavage daily for 7 consecutive days prior to the onset of ischemia, Subsequently, a model of MI/RI was established (Fig. 2j). Treatment with RSL3 results in an exacerbation of the myocardial infarct area induced by MI/RI (Fig. 2k–m). The levels of cardiac injury markers, including serum CK-MB, LDH, and cTnT, exhibited similar trends indicative of increased damage (Fig. 2n–p). Additionally, the number of TUNEL-positive cells in myocardial tissues elevated under MI/RI conditions and was significantly aggravated in mice treated with RSL3 (Fig. 2q, r). Our findings indicate that cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 plays a protective role in the progression of MI/RI, mitigating myocardial tissue damage.

Cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 mitigates ventricular remodeling induced by MI/RI and MI

MI/RI can lead to ventricular remodeling and subsequent heart failure if not treated promptly35. Therefore, we assessed whether overexpression of GPX4 in cardiomyocytes could mitigate ventricular remodeling induced by chronic MI/RI, characterized by 45 minutes of ischemia followed by 4 weeks of reperfusion (Fig. 3a). Non-invasive echocardiography demonstrated that cardiomyocyte-specific GPX4 overexpression markedly improved cardiac function, as evidenced by significant increases in ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) (Fig. 3b–d and Supplementary Table 2). Correspondingly, serum ANP levels, a biomarker of heart failure, were significantly reduced in GPX4-overexpressing mice (Fig. 3e). Histological analysis further revealed that GPX4 overexpression attenuated adverse ventricular remodeling, with a pronounced reduction in interstitial fibrosis when compared to that observed in mice treated with AAV9-cTnT-EV (Fig. 3f, g). Consistently, the overexpression of GPX4 significantly mitigated the levels of fibrotic markers, including Col-1a1 and Tgf-β, in the infarcted regions of mouse myocardial tissues subjected to MI/RI compared to the EV group (Fig. 3h). To further confirm the long-term protective role of GPX4, we employed a chronic myocardial infarction (MI) model with 4 weeks of follow-up, which reproduces advanced heart failure and ventricular remodeling36 (Fig. 3i). In this model, cardiomyocyte-specific GPX4 overexpression markedly preserved cardiac function (Fig. 3j–l and Supplementary Table 3), accompanied by reduced serum ANP levels (Fig. 3m), alleviated myocardial fibrosis, and attenuated maladaptive remodeling (Fig. 3n–p). These findings demonstrate that GPX4 confers sustained protection against chronic ventricular remodeling and progression to heart failure. In contrast, mice treated with RSL3 exhibited exacerbated cardiac dysfunction and ventricular remodeling compared to those receiving Veh treatment (Supplementary Fig. S5a–k and Supplementary Table 4).

Confirming the effects of cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 in vivo, we further explored its function in NRCMs utilizing an H/R model. We initially demonstrated that increasing levels of GPX4 protein significantly reduce H/R-induced cardiomyocyte death (Supplementary Fig. S6a, b). Additionally, iron levels and markers of ROS, evidenced by the decreases in SOD activity, elevations in MDA content, and reduction in GSH levels, indicating lipid peroxidation, were diminished in the GPX4oe + H/R group compared to the EV + H/R group (Supplementary Fig. S6c–f). Immunohistochemical staining for 4-HNE, a recognized molecular marker of ferroptosis, revealed that GPX4 overexpression significantly mitigates myocardial ferroptosis following I/R-induced cardiac tissues (Supplementary Fig. S6g). When GPX4 function was inhibited by RSL3, there was a notable increase in cardiomyocytes injury (Supplementary Fig. S6h, i), alongside elevated ROS and ferroptosis levels (Supplementary Fig. S6j–n). In summary, cardiomyocyte-derived GPX4 effectively inhibits ferroptosis and ameliorates MI/RI both in vitro and in vivo.

GPX4 serves as a crucial regulator of mitochondrial function in the progression of MI/RI

Previous studies have established a close association between GPX4 and mitochondrial dysfunction in ferroptosis37. However, the specific regulatory molecular mechanisms involved remain unclear. Mitochondrial dysfunction, recognized as a principal theory underlying MI/RI, plays a pivotal role in mediating the associated pathophysiological processes38,39. We initially analyzed transcriptome sequencing data from NRCMs subjected to H/R with or without GPX4 overexpression. The Go analysis of this transcriptome revealed numerous differentially regulated mitochondrial signaling pathways (Fig. 4a). Consequently, we proceeded to measure the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in NRCMs and found that GPX4 overexpression restored both basal, ATP production and maximal oxygen consumption rate (Fig. 4b, c). As shown in Fig. 4d–g, exogenous supplementation of GPX4 significantly ameliorated mitochondrial dysfunction induced by either H/R or I/R injury, as evidenced by elevated ATP level (Fig. 4d), increased complex I activity (Fig. 4e), and improved mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 4f, g). Further ultrastructural analysis using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that MI/RI induced severe mitochondrial cristae disruption, manifested as fragmented and broken cristae with reduced cristae density. Notably, GPX4 overexpression markedly decreased the proportion of mitochondria exhibiting such cristae damage in MI/RI mice (Fig. 4h, i). Consistently, H/R-induced NRCMs displayed pronounced cristae breakage and density loss, whereas GPX4 overexpression partially preserved cristae structure (Fig. 4j, k). It is widely accepted that mitophagy can be initiated by BNIP3, critical regulators of mitochondrial quality and function26,40. To investigate whether the binding of GPX4 to BNIP3 influences BNIP3-mediated mitophagy, we performed co-staining utilizing a mitophagy dye and mitoTracker to assess the intensity of mitophagy in NRCMs. Our data indicate that, compared with the EV + H/R group, the cardiomyocytes in GPX4oe + H/R group demonstrated significantly elevated mitophagy levels (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Fig. S9a). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed an increased expression of LAMP in H/R-treated NRCMs with GPX4 overexpression, with clear colocalization with the mitochondrial marker TOMM20 (yellow dots, Supplementary Fig. S9b, c). To clarify the role of mitophagy in regulating mitochondrial quantity, we evaluated mitochondrial numbers by measuring mtDNA copy number measurement. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S9d, GPX4 amplified the observed decline trend regarding mitochondrial numbers. In contrast, inhibition of GPX4 with RSL3 led to a reduction in mitophagy levels (Supplementary Fig. S10a, b) and was accompanied by decreased LAMP1 fluorescence signal (Supplementary Fig. S10c, d). Mitochondrial proteins were subsequently extracted. Our results revealed that GPX4 overexpression led to significantly elevated levels of mitochondrial BNIP3 protein and LC3B-II in H/R-treated NRCMs (Fig. 5h, i). These findings suggest that GPX4 may promote mitophagy primarily by upregulating BNIP3 protein expression. To test this hypothesis, we employed siRNA-mediated Bnip3 knockdown to evaluate whether GPX4 could still enhance mitophagy in the absence of BNIP3. Interestingly, compared with the si-NC + EV + H/R group, BNIP3 silencing reduced mitophagy levels, and the loss of BNIP3 also abolished the GPX4 overexpression–induced increase in mitophagy in NRCMs (Fig. 5j, k, Supplementary Fig. S10e). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GPX4 directly interacts with BNIP3 and enhances BNIP3-dependent mitophagy, thereby contributing to the maintenance of mitochondrial quality and function under ischemic stress.

GPX4 prevents proteasomal degradation of BNIP3

To clarify how GPX4 increases BNIP3 protein levels, we treated NRCMs with the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 and applied either autophagy or proteasome inhibitors to determine whether BNIP3 degradation occurs via the autophagy-lysosome pathway or the proteasome pathway. We found that the proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively prevented BNIP3 degradation, whereas autophagy inhibitors, including 3-methyladenine (3-MA), chloroquine (CQ), and bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1), had no effect on GPX4-mediated BNIP3 regulation (Fig. 6a, b). These results indicate that BNIP3 degradation primarily depends on the proteasome pathway. Interestingly, existing researches indicates that the degradation of BNIP3 is regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway41. Through Co-IP experiments, we further confirmed that BNIP3 undergoes ubiquitination (Fig. 6c). Moreover, using the cycloheximide to inhibit protein synthesis, we demonstrated that inhibition of proteasome by MG132 activity effectively prevents BNIP3 degradation in NIH/3T3 (Fig. 6d, e). Subsequently, to further validate this hypothesis, we overexpressed GPX4 in cardiomyocytes and co-treated them with MG132 and cycloheximide, and confirmed that overexpressing GPX4 plasmids in NIH/3T3 cells significantly inhibited BNIP3 degradation (Fig. 6f, g). Finally, we co-overexpressed GPX4, BNIP3, and UB in NIH/3T3 cells and conducted Co-IP experiments. The obtained results demonstrated a significant reduction in ubiquitination levels of BNIP3 upon GPX4 overexpression (Fig. 6h). This effect was consistently observed in both HR-induced NRCMs (Fig. 6i) and MI/RI-treated myocardial tissues (Fig. 6j). USP20 acts as a crucial factor in enhancing the stability of BNIP3 protein through its interaction with GPX4

However, its potential involvement in regulating protein ubiquitination has not been reported. Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) counteract the ubiquitination of substrate proteins, often preventing their targeting for degradation by the proteasome42. Therefore, we hypothesized that DUBs may be involved in the GPX4-BNIP3 interaction system. To investigate this, we utilized Co-IP and LC-MS/MS to screen for potential DUBs functioning within the GPX4-BNIP3 complex. We transfected HL-1 cells with plasmids encoding either GPX4 or BNIP3 for 24 hours, followed by H/R treatment. Subsequently, Co-IP and LC-MS/MS were conducted (Fig. 7a). To verify our hypothesis that GPX4 influences BNIP3 degradation by removal of its ubiquitin chains through USP20 activity, we employed CRISPR-Cas9 technology to generate Usp20-knockout NIH/3T3 cells (gUsp20-NIH/3T3). Following transfection of these knockout cells with plasmids encoding either an empty vector or GPX4, cells were treated with cycloheximide. In gCtrl cells, GPX4 overexpression delayed BNIP3 degradation, whereas in the absence of USP20, the degradation rate of BNIP3 was markedly higher than in gCtrl. Notably, under USP20-deficient conditions, GPX4 overexpression failed to attenuate BNIP3 degradation, suggesting that the regulation of BNIP3 stability by GPX4 is dependent on the involvement of USP20 (Fig. 7f, g). Furthermore, co-transfecting plasmids encoding either GPX4, BNIP3, and UB into gUsp20-NIH/3T3 cells, we observed that GPX4 no longer influenced the ubiquitination level of BNIP3 in the absence of USP20 (Fig. 7h).

We next performed Co-IP experiments in H/R-stimulated cardiomyocytes with or without GPX4 transfection, which demonstrated that GPX4 markedly enhanced the interaction between USP20 and BNIP3 (Supplementary Fig. S12a, b). To further evaluate the molecular basis of this interaction, we employed AlphaFold3-based virtual docking combined with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The BNIP3-USP20 dimer exhibited considerable fluctuations, showing only transient stability between 20 ns and 60 ns before returning to an unstable state (Supplementary Fig. S12c). In contrast, the BNIP3-USP20-GPX4 trimer displayed only minor fluctuations around 40 ns and reached a more stable conformation after 60 ns (Supplementary Fig. S12d). Consistently, the trimer showed substantially reduced RMSD values compared with the dimer, indicating improved structural stability (Supplementary Fig. S12e). Complementary surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis revealed a dissociation constant (KD) of 4.29e-07 M for the BNIP3-USP20 interaction (Fig. 7i), which decreased to 1.61e-8 M upon GPX4 inclusion (Fig. 7j). Given that KD values are inversely proportional to binding affinity, these results indicate that GPX4 significantly strengthens the interaction between BNIP3 and USP20. In addition, co-IP experiments confirmed that USP20 binds to BNIP3 and deubiquitinates it, while the presence of GPX4 further potentiates the USP20-BNIP3 interaction, thereby enhancing the removal of ubiquitin chains from BNIP3 (Fig. 7k and Supplementary Fig. S12f). Taken together, the results from Co-IP, MD simulations, and SPR consistently demonstrate that GPX4 stabilizes the BNIP3-USP20 complex.

The U46 residue of GPX4 enhances the deubiquitination effect of USP20 on the K131 residue of BNIP3

The U46 residue represents the critical active site of GPX4, and mutation at this site leads to complete loss of enzymatic activity43,44. Interestingly, AlphaFold3-based structural modeling revealed that the U46 site is located within the binding interface of the GPX4-BNIP3-USP20 complex, suggesting a potential role of U46 in facilitating the interaction between USP20 and BNIP3 (Fig. 8a). Since RSL3 acts by inhibiting the U46 site of GPX443,45, we first performed IP assays using myocardial tissues subjected to I/R injury with RSL3 treatment and found that BNIP3-USP20 interactions were attenuated (Supplementary Fig. S13a, b). Consistently, in NIH/3T3 cells co-transfected with GPX4, BNIP3, and USP20, GPX4-enhanced BNIP3-USP20 binding was markedly reduced upon RSL3 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S13c, d). To further validate this hypothesis, we generated a GPX4 U46A mutant plasmid (leading to loss of activity) and conducted Co-IP assays in NIH/3T3 cells. The results demonstrated that the U46A mutation abolished the ability of GPX4 to promote BNIP3-USP20 interaction. Moreover, in the U46 mutant background, RSL3 treatment no longer exerted additional inhibitory effects (Fig. 8b and Supplementary Fig. S13e). To accurately identify the deubiquitination site of BNIP3 mediated by USP20 in the presence of GPX4, we conducted an affinity-based ubiquitinated peptide enrichment ubiquitylome analysis in HL-1 cells expressing Flag-GPX4 (Fig. 8c). Through a comprehensive analysis of BNIP3 ubiquitination sites, we identified four potential ubiquitination lysine residues susceptible to ubiquitination within the BNIP3 protein: K46, K83, K128, and K131 (Fig. 8d). Consequently, we selected these sites and constructed corresponding BNIP3 mutants plasmids by substituting lysine residue with arginine to mimic the deubiquitinated state of BNIP3. Subsequently, HL-1 cells were transfected with plasmids encoding wild-type BNIP3 (WT) plasmids as well as the respective mutant plasmids, followed by H/R modeling and assessment of cell viability. The results indicated that mutation at the K131 site significantly enhanced cell viability (Fig. 8e). Additionally, we observed that the polyubiquitination levels of BNIP3-K131R was significantly lower than that of BNIP3-WT and did not exhibit any further reduction by overexpressing GPX4 both in NIH/3T3 and H/R-stimulated HL-1 (Fig. 8f and Supplementary Fig. S13f). This observation suggests that lysine residue K131 in BNIP3 is crucial for GPX4-mediated USP20-BNIP3 deubiquitination system (Fig. 8g).

To assess the functional significance of K131 ubiquitination, cardiomyocytes were subjected to H/R treatment under different conditions of BNIP3 and USP20 expression. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S13g, USP20 deficiency markedly reduced cell viability, while restoration of BNIP3 expression—particularly the K131R mutant—significantly improved cardiomyocyte survival. Consistent results were obtained for LDH release (Supplementary Fig. S13h). These findings indicate that K131 ubiquitination is essential for BNIP3-mediated protection and that the K131R mutant can partially compensate for USP20 deficiency.

In USP20fl/fl mice, GPX4 overexpression significantly reduced infarct size and acute myocardial injury, as evidenced by decreased cardiac injury markers and fewer TUNEL-positive cells following MI/RI. In contrast, USP20 CKO mice displayed larger infarct size and aggravated acute injury, and GPX4 overexpression failed to provide protection (Fig. 9b–h). Besides, we isolated cardiac mitochondria, and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using sequential substrate and inhibitor titrations. Our results showed that GPX4 overexpression significantly promoted the complex I OXPHOS capacity, enhanced the complex II OCR, and increased the respiration of complex IV in USP20fl/fl mice. However, when comparing the EV-IR groups, USP20 CKO mice displayed a marked reduction in multiple OCR parameters relative to USP20fl/fl mice, indicating impaired mitochondrial function. Notably, GPX4 overexpression in USP20 CKO mice did not reverse these defects, suggesting that during the acute phase of I/R, the protective effect of GPX4 on mitochondrial respiration is dependent on the presence of USP20 (Supplementary Fig. S14c, d).

At the chronic stage (4 weeks after MI/RI) (Fig. 9j), GPX4 overexpression improved cardiac function in USP20fl/fl mice, as evidenced by higher EF and FS values, whereas USP20 CKO mice exhibited worse cardiac function compared with their USP20fl/fl counterparts. Interestingly, GPX4 overexpression partially restored cardiac function in USP20 CKO mice but failed to reach the level observed in USP20fl/fl + GPX4oe mice (Fig. 9k–m and Supplementary Table 5). Consistent results were obtained for serum ANP (Fig. 9n) and myocardial fibrosis. Histological staining and qPCR analysis of fibrosis-related genes demonstrated reduced fibrotic remodeling in USP20fl/fl + GPX4oe mice, whereas USP20 CKO mice showed more severe fibrosis, and the protective effect of GPX4 was insufficient to fully reverse the damage caused by USP20 deficiency (Fig. 9o–q).

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the spatial distribution of GPX4 in MI/RI, revealing its high expression in the border and normal zones, with a significant reduction observed in the ischemic region. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the U46 residue of GPX4 facilitates USP20-mediated deubiquitination of BNIP3 at K131, thereby stabilizing BNIP3 and enhancing BNIP3-dependent mitophagy to eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria. This mechanism enhances mitochondrial function, maintains mitochondrial homeostasis, and ultimately mitigates MI/RI. Our findings provide evidence of the protective role of the GPX4/BNIP3/USP20 tripartite complex in MI/RI, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target for intervention in MI/RI.

GPX4, an enzyme responsible for the scavenging of lipid peroxides (LPs), serves as an endogenous regulator of ferroptosis14,15. By upregulating the activity of GPX4, lipid peroxidation can be suppressed, thereby rescuing cardiomyocytes from MI/RI19,20. Notably, we explored the spatial distribution of GPX4 in cardiac tissues. Analysis of previously published data revealed that the mRNA level of Gpx4 was significantly reduced in the ischemic zones of human heart tissues, while exhibiting increased expression in border zones compared to normal zones. Consistent with these observations, our spatial proteomics analysis and immunofluorescence staining conducted on mouse heart tissues subjected to MI/RI treatment revealed similar trends. Furthermore, our findings revealed that Gpx4 is more abundantly expressed in cardiomyocytes compared to other cell populations in the heart and exhibited a temporal correlation with the progression of MI/RI. More importantly, we found that reduced serum GPX4 expression in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy was strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Thus, augmenting the expression of GPX4 in ischemic myocardium presents a potentially effective therapeutic approach for ameliorating MI/RI.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a pivotal pathological event in MI/RI, contributing not only to mitochondrial impairment but also to the exacerbation of tissue damage21,22. Notably, cardiomyocyte ferroptosis is a significant contributor to MI/RI and is frequently accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction12,46. Through our LC-MS/MS analysis and subsequent validations, we identified a direct binding between GPX4 and BNIP3, representing a discovery that adds substantial value to the field. Previous study has demonstrated that BNIP3- and NIX-mediated mitophagy confers protection against ferroptosis by reducing mitochondrial ROS levels29. However, the involvement of GPX4 in this process remains poorly understood. Our research indicated that GPX4 inhibits the proteasomal degradation of BNIP3, thereby stabilizing this protein and promoting mitophagy, which ultimately improves mitochondrial function. This finding provides insights into the relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, deepening our understanding of the underlying mechanisms involved in this pathological process.

The PRKN-independent mitophagy pathway, primarily mediated by BNIP3 (BCL2/adenovirus E1B-interacting protein 3), has been reported to protect cardiomyocytes from MI/RI by maintaining mitochondrial quality control24,28. Previous studies have implicated multiple proteins in regulating BNIP3 turnover through the ubiquitin–proteasome system41,47. In our study, we identified GPX4 as a key stabilizer of BNIP3 by attenuating its ubiquitination, thereby promoting BNIP3-mediated mitophagy. This finding highlights a previously unappreciated role of GPX4 beyond its canonical function as an anti-lipid peroxidation enzyme. Conceptually, our data suggest that GPX4 functions as a central regulator that bridges ferroptosis suppression with ubiquitin-deubiquitinase-mediated mitochondrial quality control. Pharmacological agents or gene therapy approaches aimed at enhancing GPX4 activity or stabilizing BNIP3 may provide potential strategies to boost mitochondrial quality control and attenuate myocardial injury following ischemia/reperfusion.

The U46 residue of GPX4 is the catalytic selenocysteine essential for reducing lipid hydroperoxides and maintaining membrane antioxidant defense43,44. RSL3, via its chloroacetamide electrophile, irreversibly alkylates U46, blocking the GSH-dependent catalytic cycle of GPX4 and thereby triggering ferroptosis43,45. The U46 residue of GPX4, the catalytic selenocysteine irreversibly targeted by RSL3, proved indispensable for stabilizing the GPX4-BNIP3-USP20 complex, as shown by structural modeling and mutational assays. Mutation of U46 abolished GPX4’s ability to promote BNIP3–USP20 binding and BNIP3 stabilization. In parallel, ubiquitinomics identified the BNIP3 K131 residue as a key site for deubiquitination, further confirming that GPX4 promotes BNIP3 stability by facilitating USP20-mediated deubiquitination. Functionally, these results not only extend the canonical understanding of GPX4 as a lipid peroxidation scavenger but also reveal an unanticipated function in modulating protein-protein interactions, thereby linking its enzymatic active site to mitochondrial quality control mechanisms. Importantly, our in vivo studies revealed phase-specific effects (Fig. 9): in the acute stage of MI/RI, GPX4 overexpression failed to protect USP20-deficient hearts, whereas in the chronic stage, it partially ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and remodeling. These findings indicate that the GPX4-BNIP3-USP20 axis mediates cardioprotection through mitophagy regulation, acting independently of ferroptosis, and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target beyond canonical lipid peroxidation defense. Although GPX4 overexpression partially restored cardiac function in USP20-deficient mice during the chronic phase, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. We hypothesize that GPX4 may exert USP20-independent protective effects by suppressing lipid peroxidation, reducing inflammation, or modulating fibrotic remodeling. Future work will focus on directly evaluating oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation to test this hypothesis.

In conclusion, we have elucidated the functional role of the GPX4/BNIP3/USP20 tripartite complex in MI/RI and established a robust foundation for potential therapeutic strategies targeting MI/RI by revealing a GPX4-BNIP3-mitophagy pathway.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 1996) and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (approval document No. WYYY-AEC-YS-2023-0097). Wild-type mice, α-myosin heavy chain promoter-driven heterozygous Cre (T055034, C57BL/6 J background, Myh6-Cre+/-) mice, and USP20 floxed mice (T052123, C57BL/6 J background, USP20fl/fl) mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Cardiac-specific deletion of USP20 mice (USP20flox/flox Myh6-Cre, USP20 CKO) was generated by crossing the floxed USP20 mice with Myh6-Cre+/- mice. Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with free access to food and water under a 12 h light/dark cycle.

The myocardial ischemia and reperfusion injury (MI/RI) model was established as previously described48. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and ventilated using a MiniVent system. A left thoracotomy was performed, and the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) was occluded with a 7-0 silk suture slipknot. Myocardial blanching confirmed complete occlusion. After 45 minutes, the slipknot was released to initiate reperfusion (24 hours for acute I/R injury or two or four weeks for chronic I/R injury). Sham-operated mice underwent identical procedures without tying the suture. Mice received supplemental heat for 30 minutes postoperatively and continued overnight recovery.

Hypoxia-reoxygenation (H/R) injury treatment of cardiomyocytes

Hypoxia was induced by replacing ambient air with 94% N2, 1% O2, and 5% CO2 in a controlled incubator, and using DMEM lacking FBS and glucose for 4 hours. Afterward, cells were reoxygenated by replacing the medium with normal DMEM and incubating under normoxic conditions (21% O2, 74% N2, 5% CO2) for 6 hours. Control groups were kept under normoxia with glucose and FBS-deprived DMEM for the same duration.

Co-IP combined with LC-MS/MS analysis

For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), antibodies and protein A + G beads were used. For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments, protein complexes were isolated using specific antibodies and protein A/G magnetic beads. Proteins were extracted in SDT lysis buffer and digested using the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) method. Peptides were analyzed by LC–MS/MS at PTM Bio Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). Peptide separation was performed on a C18 column (1.8 µm, 0.15 × 100 mm) using an EASY-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 98-min gradient at 600 nL/min. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B). MS/MS analysis was conducted on an LTQ Orbitrap ETD mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Raw data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (v1.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for database searching and protein identification. Candidate substrates were identified based on relative protein intensities. In total, four independent samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS (n = 1 per group).

Spatial proteomics and spatial transcriptomics analysis

For spatial proteomics, heart samples from I/R-treated mice were washed with PBS, immersed in liquid nitrogen-precooled isopentane for 1 minute, and then frozen in O.C.T. Compound. Tissue sections (10 μm) were cut using a CryoStar NX60 cryostat and stained with H&E. The normal, border, and ischemic zones were identified and marked. These regions underwent laser capture microdissection (LCM) and spatial proteomics analysis. Protein extraction, trypsin digestion, and spatial proteomics were performed by PTM Bio Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China).

Spatial transcriptome raw data was form the previously published study30. All data analysis was conducted by LC Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SD). For in vitro data, the assumption of normal distribution was based on the central limit theorem, as each experimental dataset represents an average of a large number of cultured cells. For in vivo data, with a sample size of ≥6, the normality of each dataset was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. A P-value > 0.05 indicated that the data were approximately normally distributed. Statistical analyses were conducted by using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA). Depending on the analysis, either a Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA followed by post hoc multiple comparisons was performed. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
