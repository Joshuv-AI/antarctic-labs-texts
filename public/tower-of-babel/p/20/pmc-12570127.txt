Multi‐Level Investigation in Arabidopsis of the Growth‐Stimulating Effects of a Protein Hydrolysate Biostimulant
Authors: Justine Broutin, Isabelle Jéhanno, Marina Berthet, Julie Fournier, Samuel Laurent, Gilles Clément, Stéphanie Pateyron, Christine Paysant‐Le‐Roux, Anne Marmagne, Olivier Loudet, Elodie Gilbault, Benjamin Ourliac, Anne‐Sophie Leprince, Christian Meyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiostimulant use is a promising agricultural strategy to maintain yield while decreasing mineral fertilisers and pesticides. Whereas the positive effect of protein hydrolysates (PH) on growth and yield has already been described, the underlying processes are not well understood. To better identify and characterise the physiological and molecular targets of PH, we first carried out in vitro experiments using the model species Arabidopsis thaliana . PH stimulated Arabidopsis root growth in a dose‐dependent manner, with high concentrations inhibiting growth. After having determined a stimulating PH concentration, we performed metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses. We observed that PH application increased amino acid levels in the plants, including a high level of glutamine, although it was only present in trace amounts in PH. These results suggest that amino acids are taken up and metabolized in the plant. PH also had a profound impact on the Arabidopsis transcriptome, with genes involved in nitrate and amino acid transport and assimilation showing a clear up‐ and down‐regulation, respectively. For example, the expression of the high‐affinity nitrate transporter NRT2.1 (NITRATE TRANSPORTER 2) was decreased, and consistently high‐affinity nitrate uptake and the development of lateral roots were diminished. The PH contained high levels of branched amino acids, which are known to induce the TOR kinase activity, a major driver of growth. Finally, high‐throughput phenotyping also showed that PH supply increased shoot growth. In conclusion, our findings show that PH has a clear and robust effect on Arabidopsis growth, but also on the transcriptome, metabolite levels, and physiological processes like nitrogen metabolism.Keywords: amino acid, arabidopsis, biostimulant, nitrogen, tor kinase

Introduction

Since the Green Revolution, the use of agricultural inputs has only increased to feed the world's growing population. Mostly, these inputs are synthetic fertilisers, which provide essential elements that are required for crop growth, yield, and quality. In the EU, their use allows farmers to meet crop needs and stay competitive in the global market. However, their application is being debated in response to social and regulatory demands. Indeed, synthetic fertilisers present many risks to the environment, such as the risk of leaching in aquatic and marine ecosystems, which leads to their eutrophication or volatilization, resulting in air pollution (Cowan et al. 2019; Ramanantenasoa et al. 2019). Nitrogen fertilisers (urea, ammonium, and nitrate) are the most used inputs in the European Union (EU), but their biosynthesis depends on the Haber‐Bosch process, which consumes fossil energy and emits greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. As a result, the EU policy is encouraging farmers to turn to sustainable and environmentally friendly practices.

With regard to these constraints, the use of plant biostimulants appears to be a promising strategy. Their definition is given by the EU regulation 2019/1009, which defines the biostimulant as ‘any substance or microorganisms that enhance the nutritional process of the plant, the tolerance to abiotic stress, quality traits and the availability of nutrients from the soil independently of their nutrient content’ (Baglieri et al. 2014; Colla et al. 2015). Biostimulants can be of many different types, from algae extracts to humic substances, protein hydrolysates (PH), inorganic minerals or beneficial microorganisms, and their composition can vary from single or a few components of known nature to complex and unknown mixes (Colla et al. 2015; du Jardin 2015; Xu and Geelen 2018). Some biostimulants, like PH, are produced by the upcycling of farming or crop waste using partial hydrolysis (Colla et al. 2015). Because of their production mode, PH contains a mix of amino acids and peptides in various compositions and amounts. Usually, chemical hydrolysis is stronger and results in higher concentrations and proportions of amino acids relative to oligopeptides, whereas enzymatic hydrolysis produces more and longer peptides (Colla et al. 2015). Depending on their sources and manufacturing process, other molecules like organic acids, polyamines or carbohydrates can also be found in PH (Veselá and Friedrich 2009; Colla et al. 2015; Tejada et al. 2018).

The fact that PH from animal or vegetable sources can promote plant growth and yield is well documented in the literature. For example, foliar application of animal‐derived PH increases soybean yield by 25% without affecting grain quality (Kocira 2019) and carrot yield at the early stage (Grabowska et al. 2012). These experiments were conducted in the field; one issue with these two studies, as often, was the non‐repeatability of the stimulating effect the following year. Apart from the effects of PH on global biomass, the impact on roots was often studied in more detail. Indeed, total root biomass was increased in different plants, such as tomatoes or maize (Polo and Mata 2018; Ertani et al. 2019). In addition, root architecture, which depends on the availability of nutrients, was modified. For example, the application of animal‐based PH containing 10% (w/w) of amino acids increases the root length and surface area of lateral roots of maize seedlings by 7 and 1.5 times, respectively (Santi et al. 2017). Some hypotheses have been made to explain the components of PH that could modify root architecture. First, PH contains amino acids, and some are known to stimulate root growth, such as Glutamine (Gln), Serine (Ser), and Asparagine (Asn) (Walch‐Liu et al. 2006; Ingargiola et al. 2023). This increase in root growth could be due to the activation of the TOR kinase by amino acids, particularly Gln and branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs) (Cao et al. 2019; O'Leary et al. 2020; Ingargiola et al. 2023). Indeed, the TOR kinase is a central regulatory element connecting nutritional or hormonal information to mRNA translation, cell division, and ultimately growth and development (Dobrenel et al. 2016). TOR inhibition also increases autophagy, leading to protein remobilization and degradation to amino acids and ammonium (Liu and Bassham 2010). Second, PH also contains peptides, and adding dipeptides like 100 nM Glycine–Alanine (Gly‐Ala) and Gly‐Asp increases root length, root hair length, and density in tobacco seedlings (Fedoreyeva et al. 2022). Thirdly, some PH have shown an auxin‐like activity that could regulate root architecture. For example, two key genes (SlIAA2 and SlIAA9) involved in the IAA (Indole Acetic Acid) signalling pathway in tomato leaves have been up‐regulated with the application of legume‐derived PH, and the root length increased (Buffagni et al. 2021).

In addition to the modification of root architecture, PH addition also induces changes in nutrient uptake. Nitrate is an important source of inorganic nitrogen in most plants and is taken up from the soil by either high‐affinity transport systems (HATS) operated by the NRT2 gene family or low‐affinity transport systems (LATS) linked to the NRT1/NPF transporter family (Orsel et al. 2002). In Arabidopsis, the NRT2.1 transporter is the main contributor to HATS and is known to be induced by nitrate but repressed by amino acids, especially Gln (Nazoa et al. 2003). More generally, amino acids seem to decrease nitrate influx in roots. Accordingly, in tomato, a legume‐derived PH decreased the expression of genes encoding high‐affinity nitrate transporters (NRT2.1 and NRT2.3) (Sestili et al. 2018). Root hairs are also important for plant mineral nutrition and it is known that amino acids can influence their development and length (Montiel and Dubrovsky 2024). Moreover, Arabidopsis plants affected in leucine (Leu) synthesis displayed modifications in root hairs in relation to the TOR (Target of Rapamycin) kinase (Schaufelberger et al. 2019). PH also modifies other steps of nitrogen metabolism, including the reduction of nitrate, the synthesis of amino acids by the glutamine synthetase/glutamate synthase (GS/GOGAT) cycle and other metabolic pathways (Malécange et al. 2023).

As only 40%–60% of the nitrogen supplied is used by the plants in the field, studying how plant biostimulants can improve nitrogen use efficiency is an important current challenge. To understand the mode of action of PH, it is important to better identify its biological targets in controlled reproducible conditions, and this is more easily achieved in well‐characterised model plants like Arabidopsis.

This study aims to characterise the effect of a PH biostimulant (Aminovital Power, provided by Fertinagro company) on the growth and nitrogen metabolism of Arabidopsis seedlings. Currently, Aminovital Power (AVP) is used as an agronomic additive to N fertilisers in the field and is derived from the hydrolysis of animal protein. Because we were studying AVP effects on in vitro seedlings, we first identified the stimulating concentration on Arabidopsis root growth. Then, we used this concentration to analyse the amino acid composition of the product. Next, we investigated the effect of AVP on the root architecture of plants supplied with either a deficient or an adequate supply of nitrate and compared omics data of AVP‐ or non‐treated seedlings. Finally, we tried to determine if the stimulating concentrations of AVP in vitro could increase the growth of plants in soils.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Plant Growth Conditions

In this study, we used Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) ecotype Columbia 0. For in vitro experiments, seedlings were grown on vertical plates containing Arabidopsis medium with 1% sucrose (w/v), 2.5 mM KH2PO4, 2 mM MgSO4, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.05% 2 − (N‐Morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid sodium salt (MES, w/v), 1X microelements [70 nM H3BO3, 14 nM MnCl2(4H20), 0.5 nM CuSO4 (5H20), 0.2 nM Na2MoO4 (2H20), 10 nM NaCl, 1 nM ZnSO4 (7H20), 0.01 nM CoCl (6H20)], 0.2 mM Iron Ammonium Citrate, 0.8% Agar (w/v), and nitrate was supplied in the form of KNO3 (Moreau et al. 2012). Depending on the experiment, nitrate was used at different concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, and 5 mM) in the medium mix.

In each experiment, we used AVP (Aminovital Power product commercialised by the Fertinagro Company). AVP is derived from animal waste products after acid hydrolysis. It contains 12% (w/w) N, which is mainly organic N (55% free amino acids and 20% peptides), together with 0.6% ammonium and a negligible amount of nitrate (less than 0.2%). Elemental analysis showed that AVP contains potassium (0.87%), sodium (2.4%), calcium (1.7%), phosphorus (0.41%), sulfur (7.4%), magnesium (0.29%), and iron (0.1%). Metals like manganese, copper, molybdenum, and zinc were present as traces (less than 0.05%). The seeds were stratified at 4°C for 2 or 3 days and then transferred to controlled growth chambers (Aralab) at 21°C with a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h night (270μE). For phenotypic analysis, primary root length was measured with the NeuroJ plugin in the ImageJ software or EZRhizo for the root ramification. For in vitro cultures, AVP (in powder form) is used to prepare a stock solution that is then added to the culture media. 100 mg/L of AVP contains 11.9 mg of total nitrogen/L and 10.9 mg of organic nitrogen. This means that around 0.6 mg of organic N is added to each plate (50 mL). KNO3 at 5 mM contains 71 mg of N/L and thus 3.55 mg of N is provided per plate. In this system, AVP is only provided once before sowing. The equivalent in fields of 100 mg/L of AVP is around 3.5 kg/ha. For soil experiments, wild‐type seedlings were grown on a high‐throughput phenotyping robot (https://phenoscope.versailles.inrae.fr/) and treated with control conditions as previously published in Xue et al. (2024). Briefly, stratified seeds are sown on peatmoss plugs saturated with water and initially complemented with 0.3 mmol of nitrate (equivalent to 4.18 mg of N); after germination, plugs are gradually adjusted to 60% of the maximum soil water content (and maintained throughout the experiment at this setting, which corresponds to 3.9 g H20 per g of dry soil, in excess with respect to the water needs of the plant) and providing in total an extra 0.625 mmol of nitrate (equivalent to 8.75 mg of N) across the experiment. This is largely in excess with respect to the N needs of the plants since each Arabidopsis plant contained, on average, 1.25 mg N at the end of the culture period. 500 μL of AVP were applied four times during the Phenoscope culture. The AVP solution concentrations are 0.1, 1, 5, 10, and 50 g/L, bringing in total 0.024–12 mg of N in the plug compared to 8.75 mg of total N provided by the nitrate supply. The plugs were automatically weighted every day and the weights were adjusted to the required soil water and N contents by the robot. The different AVP treatments were applied at the root level. Three waterings, provided directly to the soil, each with a volume of 500 μL of an AVP solution at the indicated concentrations, were carried out on days 14, 21, and 28 after sowing. Projected Rosette Area (PRA), which corresponds to the average cumulative growth trajectory extracted from daily zenithal imaging across time (day after sowing), was measured every day during the 31 days of culture.

Metabolomic and Transcriptomic Analysis

For metabolite and RNAseq analysis, 14‐day‐old seedlings were grown in vitro on media containing 100 mg/L of AVP or without it. Around 15 seedlings were harvested for each sample of each condition, and frozen in liquid nitrogen before grinding. All metabolite levels were determined by GC–MS on whole seedlings. Details of sample preparations, metabolite quantifications, and analyses have been previously published in Forzani et al. (2019). Quality controls (QC) were a mix of the same volumes from all samples, which were injected and analysed independently three times. Total RNAs were extracted from the same experiment using Trizol reagent method, including DNAse treatment. Total RNA was repurified using the RNeasy plant mini kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA‐seq libraries were constructed with 500 ng of total RNA by the POPS platform (IPS2, Saclay, France) using the QuantSeq 3′ mRNA‐Seq Library Prep Kit (FWD) for Illumina 96 preps, (#LEX015.96 LeXogen) according to the supplier's instructions. Libraries' quality was checked on Agilent DNA HS chip, (#5067‐4627 Agilent) and they were quantified with Quant‐iT PicoGreen dsDNA Reagent (Thermo FisherScientific Inc.) to generate an equimolar pool. Libraries were sequenced in single‐end (SE) mode with 75 bases for each read on a NextSeq500 (Illumina). Reads where any Unique Molecular Identifiers base quality score falls below 10 were removed. To remove adapter sequences, poly(A), poly(G) sequences, and low‐quality nucleotides, reads were trimmed with BBduk from the BBmap suite. The RNA‐seq samples have been sequenced in single‐end (SE) with a sizing of 260 bp and a read length of 75 bases. Eighteen samples by lane of NextSeq500 using individual bar‐coded adapters, and giving approximately 16 million of SE reads by sample, were generated. RNA‐Seq data were analyzed as previously described in (Forzani et al. 2019). The dataset has been submitted to the NIH Gene Omnibus (GEO) database (accession GSE297649). For the RT‐qPCR, total RNA (1 μg) was treated with DNAse I (Thermo Scientific) and RT reaction using RevertAid H Minus RT Thermo Scientific (Ingargiola et al. 2023). The RT‐qPCR was performed using blue SyberGreen from Biorad. The relative expression of NRT2.1 was normalised to the level of a synthetic reference gene (SRG) using both ACTIN2 (At3g18780) and APT1 genes (At1g27450) as reference genes. We used the following calculation:

%SRG = √(2^(CtAPT1−CtNRT2.1) × 2^(CtActin2−CtNRT2.1)) as previously described by Kravchenko et al. (2015).

Root N Uptake Capacity Analysis

We used 7‐day‐old seedlings grown in vitro on media containing 1 mM KNO3 with 0 or 100 mg/L of AVP in vertical square Petri dishes. The roots of all seedlings from each dish were submerged for 15 min by adding directly on the surface of the Petri dishes a liquid Arabidopsis media containing a 15N labelled source of nitrogen. The N sources used were 0.2 mM (for HATS) or 5 mM (for LATS) K15NO3 (99% labelling) or 0.1 mM (for HATS) and 2.5 mM (for LATS) (15NH4)2SO4 (99% labelling) with 0 or 100 mg/L of AVP. After that, each plate was drained, and seedlings were harvested and washed with 0.1 mM CaSO4 solution. Each sample was then dried for 48 h in an oven (70°C).

N Isotope Analysis

15N content was determined as described by Ingargiola et al. (2023) by isotope ratio mass spectrometry (Thermo Scientific Delta V Advantage) using around 1 mg of dry matter. The uptake of N was then calculated as: uptake (μmolN/gDryMatter/h) = %N × (10,000/14) × ((At%15 N/14 N sample—At15 N/14 N natural abundance)/99%) × (60(min)/ 15 N incubation time) where At is the 15N enrichment. Total nitrogen and carbon content were determined by elemental mass spectrometry (Thermo Scientific Flash 2000).

Protein Extraction, Western Blot Analysis, and TOR Activity Measurements

7‐day‐old seedlings were grown in vitro on solid Arabidopsis medium (1 mM KNO3 and 0.3% sucrose, w/v) containing 0 or 100 mg/L of AVP. For each sample of each condition, 30 seedlings were then transferred for 24 h of starvation in liquid Arabidopsis media either without sucrose or without sucrose but with 100 mg/L AVP. Following starvation, some seedlings were transferred to the same medium without sucrose (‐Suc), while others were transferred to a medium supplemented with either only sucrose (+S) or with sucrose and AVP (+Suc + AVP). The level of RPS6 phosphorylation (P‐RPS6), as measured with a specific antibody, was used as a readout of TOR activity. Total proteins were extracted from whole seedlings using 1× Laemmli Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 10% Glycerol, 0.0025% bromophenol blue, w/v), and 400 mM DTT. Protein extracts were heated for 5 min at 95°C and then centrifuged to remove cell debris. Protein concentrations were determined using Bradford reagent (Bio‐Rad) at 595 nm. 20 μg of proteins were loaded in a 12% acrylamide‐resolving SDS‐PAGE gel (Invitrogen, NP0343BOX) and separated by migration for 1 h at 100 V and 2 A (using Buffer Invitrogen, NP0001). Proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes following the Trans‐Blot Turbo Blotting System protocol. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk‐PBS solution (BioRad, #1610780) and 0.05% Tween 20 (w/v). Then, membranes were incubated with rabbit primary antibodies directed against either total RPS6 or P‐RPS6 (for both dilution 1:5000) as described in (Dobrenel et al. 2016). We have produced these antibodies using synthetic peptides as antigens (Dobrenel et al. 2016). Goat anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP (Sigma) was used as a secondary antibody (dilution 1/5000). Immunodetection was performed using the Clarity Western ECL detection kit (Bio‐Rad). The equal transfer of proteins on the membrane was evaluated by Ponceau Staining. The signals for both P‐RPS6 and RPS6 were measured with the ImageJ software after normalization with the Ponceau red staining as a proxy for protein loading. The R‐PS6/RPS6 signal ratios were then calculated.

Results

Stimulating Effect of Aminovital Power (AVP) on Root Growth

To begin with, we wanted to identify the concentrations of Aminovital Power (AVP) that have an impact on Arabidopsis thaliana root growth. Because root growth and architecture depend on the availability of nutrients in the medium, these phenotypes are commonly used for measuring plant responses to the nutritional environment. First, the primary root of Col‐0 seedlings was measured after 12 days of growth on Arabidopsis medium with increasing AVP concentrations and 5 mM KNO3 (Figure 1A). We found that 100 mg/L of AVP stimulated root length. Higher AVP concentrations inhibit root length and seem toxic to the plant. This negative effect on root growth could be linked to increasing amino acid concentrations, some of which are known to inhibit plant growth (Ravelo‐Ortega et al. 2021). In all the following in vitro experiments, we used 100 mg/L of AVP, which was considered the concentration with a biostimulant effect.

Root lengths were measured after 12 days of culture. 100 mg/L of AVP stimulates Arabidopsis root growth, whereas higher concentrations inhibit root growth. (B) AVP effect depending on nitrate supply. This effect on primary root growth is only visible when nitrate supply is sufficient to allow growth of the root (n = 35–95 per condition). Aminovital Power's Composition

AVP is a protein hydrolysate product composed of 55% free amino acids and 20% peptides. AVP composition was analysed by GC–MS to determine its composition in amino acids and other small molecules. All proteogenic amino acids were found in AVP. The most abundant proteogenic amino acids in AVP are, in that order, alanine (Ala), leucine (Leu), asparagine (Asn), glutamate (Glu), and glycine (Gly) (Supplemental Fig. S1). These amino acids are present in a 30–50 μM range in the 100 mg/L AVP concentration used in the following experiments. Gln, known to have nutritional and biostimulant effects (Lardos et al. 2024; Liao et al. 2024), is found in concentrations in the nanomolar range (Figure S1). Sugars and amines like cadaverine were also present in AVP. A few dipeptides were detected by the GC–MS analysis, like valine‐valine (Val‐Val) and valine‐leucine (Val‐Leu). Because of the different amino acids present in the product, we expect a diversity of dipeptides to be found.

Phenotypic Analysis of AVP‐Treated Plant

One characteristic of biostimulants is their ability to improve the assimilation of nutrients by the plant. Depending on the class of the biostimulant, this effect could be mediated by a better development of the root system or the stimulation of the nutrient uptake (Santi et al. 2017; Sestili et al. 2018). We thus asked the question of whether the positive effect on Arabidopsis root growth of AVP depended on the N status of the plant. To address this question, we measured the root length of plants treated with AVP and grown in a range of nitrate concentrations (Figure 1B). We used Col‐0 seedlings grown for 12 days on solid Arabidopsis medium without (control) or with 100 mg/L of AVP and 0.1, 0.5, 1, or 5 mM of KNO3. At very low nitrate concentrations (0.1 and 0.5 mM), there were no differences between AVP and control plant root lengths (Figure 1B). The fact that AVP could not sustain root growth at 0.1 mM nitrate shows that AVP is not an efficient N source by itself. On the other hand, at higher nitrate supply, there were several phenotypic changes between treated and control plants. First, the primary root length of AVP‐treated plants is significantly longer than that of the control, and this difference was higher at 5 and especially at 1 mM of KNO3. Secondly, the study of the root architecture of plants grown with 1 mM KNO3 (Figure S2A) showed strong differences with a higher number of lateral roots for AVP‐treated plants, but they are shorter (Figure S2B,C). However, the total surface area of the root system was not different between these two conditions.

Stimulation of Nitrogen Metabolism in Seedlings Treated With Aminovital Power

After having seen a clear effect of AVP on Arabidopsis root growth, we performed a metabolomic analysis of treated plants to investigate the impact of AVP on plant metabolism. Col‐0 seedlings were grown in vitro for 14 days on solid Arabidopsis medium with 5 mM of KNO3 and 0 (control) or 100 mg/L AVP. Metabolomic analysis by GC–MS showed a strong effect of AVP treatment on the Arabidopsis metabolite profiles (Figure 2A). Two patterns of differentially accumulated metabolites between control and AVP‐treated seedlings were observed in the hierarchical cluster analysis. Coordinated accumulation of most amino acids in AVP‐treated seedlings was observed, which could result from either the uptake of amino acids from AVP or the neosynthesis within the plants. For example, Ala is found at 0.02 μg/mg FW in AVP‐treated seedlings (i.e., around 240 μM) (Figure 2B), whereas the amino acids part supplied by AVP is around 50 μM (Figure S1). This suggests that plants may have taken up and concentrated the amino acids from the growth medium. To ascertain that metabolite concentrations are comparable between control and AVP‐treated plants, we have determined the percentage of water in in vitro‐grown plants. There is no obvious effect of AVP on water content with 93.6% (SD 0.1) and 93.5% (SD 0.2) water for, respectively, control and AVP‐treated plants (4 replicates with 9–14 plants each). Given that AVP product results from a harsh acidic hydrolysis and that Gln is unstable in these conditions, its content is in the nanomolar range in the growth medium complemented with AVP (Figure S1). Nonetheless, Gln accumulates twice as much in AVP‐treated seedlings as in the control (Figure 2B). Gln is the first organic molecule produced during N assimilation, allowing the synthesis of other amino acids and nucleotides (Lee et al. 2023; Liao et al. 2024). The fact that Gln accumulates strongly suggests that AVP induces a stimulation of the overall N assimilation in plants (Lea et al. 2006). The same is true for gamma‐amino butyric acid (GABA), which accumulates in AVP‐treated plants while being present in very low amounts in AVP (Figure S3). Amounts of both agmatine and putrescine, two polyamines, are also higher (Figure S3). This could be explained by the increased content of Arg, which is used to synthesise putrescine via agmatine produced by arginine decarboxylase (Hanfrey et al. 2001; Takahashi 2020). These polyamines are involved in the regulation of stress responses and growth (Takahashi and Kakehi 2010; Takahashi 2020). Sugars like sucrose, a major product of photosynthesis in green leaves that serves as a long‐distance transport compound, or hexose phosphates are also found in larger quantities in treated plants (Figure 2B). However, the levels of citrate and fumarate were lower in AVP‐treated seedlings compared to the control (respectively Figures 2B and S3). These two metabolites are intermediates in the citric acid cycle and serve as substrates for various pathways, such as the synthesis of Glu and Asp. Since the content of these two amino acids is already high in AVP‐treated seedlings, their synthesis may be repressed, which could decrease the amounts of the needed carbon skeletons from the Krebs cycle.

AVP Modifies the Expression of a Substantial Number of Genes Linked to Nitrogen Pathways

After seeing an accumulation of metabolites like amino acids in AVP‐treated plants, we wondered if AVP could have influenced gene expression. First, we performed an RNAseq analysis on the same Col‐0 seedlings used for the metabolomic experiment [grown for 14 days on solid Arabidopsis medium with 0 (control) or 100 mg/L of AVP]. The volcano plot (Figure 3A) shows, using a global approach, that AVP strongly affects gene expression, as previously shown for metabolite accumulation. The expression of many genes is modified by applying AVP: 307 are significantly up‐regulated and 378 down‐regulated (Figure 3A). According to KEGG pathways analysis (Figure 3B,C), nitrogen metabolism pathways are significantly enriched. The Ala, Asp, and Glu metabolism pathway is specially enriched with genes that are up‐regulated, such as LHT1 (LYSINE, HISTIDINE TRANSPORTER 1), CAT1 (CATIONIC AMINO ACID TRANSPORTER 1), and ProT1 (PROLINE TRANSPORTER 1), which are amino acid transports, and GDH2 (GLUTAMATE DEHYDROGENASE 2), GAD1, and GAD2 (GLUTAMATE DECARBOXYLASE) (Figure 4A,B). Yet, the nitrogen metabolism pathway is also enriched with genes involved in nitrate assimilation that are down‐regulated, like NRT2.1, a high‐affinity nitrate transporter, NIA1 (NITRATE REDUCTASE 1), NIA2 (NITRATE REDUCTASE 2), or NIR2 (NITRITE REDUCTASE 2) (Figure 4A,C).

Expression and Activity of High‐Affinity Nitrate Transporters Are Decreased by AVP Treatment

We saw previously that plants treated with AVP accumulate Gln and other amino acids (Figure 2B). They could come from either proteolysis, ammonium recycling, or assimilation of inorganic N after uptake from the media. Since Gln is the first organic N form synthesized in plant cells and is the result of the first step of N assimilation (Lee et al. 2023), it was important for us to measure the uptake of nitrate and ammonium in plants treated with AVP. Col‐0 seedlings were grown for 7 days on solid Arabidopsis medium (1 mM KNO3, 1% sucrose) with 0 mg/L (control) or 100 mg/L AVP. They were then treated with liquid Arabidopsis media with or without 15N‐labelled nitrate or ammonium and AVP. Nitrate uptake was measured with two different 15N concentrations: 0.2 mM (HATS) and 5 mM (LATS, Figure 5A). Ammonium uptake was also measured with two different 15N concentrations: 0.1 mM (HATS) and 2.5 mM (LATS, Figure 5B). The uptake of nitrate was more important by the LATS transporters but was not different between control and AVP conditions. On the contrary, HATS activity is decreased by the application of AVP (Figure 5A). This could be explained by the fact that the high nitrogen content resulting from the accumulation of amino acids in plants inhibits high‐affinity nitrate transporters. We indeed observed that plants treated with AVP have a higher total nitrogen content than control plants (Figure 5C), whereas the total carbon contents were similar (Figure 5D). Plants treated with AVP also contained significantly more nitrate (Figure S4). Moreover, we confirmed by RT‐qPCR that the expression of the NRT2.1 gene (HATS) is decreased in seedlings treated with AVP (Figure 5E). Finally, ammonium uptake by LATS or HATS is not affected by AVP application (Figure 5B). Altogether, these results suggest that AVP does not induce a higher transport of nitrate or ammonium from the medium.

Stimulation of TORC1 Activity by AVP Treatment

TOR (TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN) is a protein kinase whose activity depends on the nutrient environment and, in response, regulates many biological processes like development and amino acid metabolism. In plants, amino acids, especially branched amino acids, induce TOR activity. Since AVP is composed of more than half of amino acids, we measured TOR activity in plants treated with AVP. TOR activity was quantified on a western blot measuring Ribosomal Protein S6 (RPS6) phosphorylation, an indirect target of TOR, by using specific antibodies against RPS6 and P‐RPS6. Indeed, TOR phosphorylates the S6 Kinase (S6K), which phosphorylates RPS6. Col‐0 seedlings were sown on Arabidopsis medium with 5 mM of NO3, 1% sucrose without (control) or with 100 mg/L of AVP to determine the effects of AVP application on TOR activity. After 7 days, the seedlings were transferred to the same Arabidopsis liquid medium but without sucrose for 24 h. The addition of 1% sucrose during 5 h stimulated TOR activity as shown by the increased RPS6 phosphorylation (Figure 6A,B). Interestingly, seedlings treated with 100 mg/L AVP showed higher TOR activity compared to control seedlings and slightly but reproducibly higher than when sucrose was applied alone (Figures 6A and S5). In conclusion, AVP seems to further induce the TOR complex activity.

Soil Experiment Shows Only an AVP‐Stimulating Effect on the Aerial Part

Because we only studied Arabidopsis root response to AVP on seedlings grown in vitro, we decided to use a phenotyping platform to follow growth parameters from plants cultivated in soil. Col‐0 seedlings were grown in soil under short‐day conditions on a phenotyping robot (Xue et al. 2024). Each day, N supply was controlled in order to provide non‐limiting nitrogen content to each plant by the robot. Twice a week, a fraction of the plants was additionally watered with AVP solution at 0, 0.1, 1, 5, 10, or 50 g/L. Growth parameters like the projected rosette area (PRA) were acquired throughout the whole experiment from pictures taken by the robot. At the end of the experiment, aerial biomass was measured for each plant. Increasing AVP content stimulated the growth of the rosette in both projected surface area (Figure 7A,B) and weight in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 7C). Since the control plants were grown in optimal conditions of nutrients, temperature, light, and water, these data suggest that AVP can further enhance Arabidopsis growth.

Discussion

The first aim was to characterize the phenotypical response of Arabidopsis to the AVP biostimulant. To estimate the plant's responses to this protein hydrolysate product, we followed the growth of the primary root in vitro in fully controlled conditions as a proxy for plant growth. This parameter depends on many physiological, nutritional, and hormonal conditions but allows for a quantitative and easy estimation of plant growth. By including AVP at 100 mg/L on Arabidopsis media, we observed a positive and significant stimulation of Arabidopsis main root length (Figure 1A). This positive effect could be assigned to one of the several components of AVP: the amino acids, the peptide part, or the combined action of the two since both of them have been described to have an impact on the growth or the phenotype of Arabidopsis (Ingargiola et al. 2023; Minen et al. 2023; Lardos et al. 2024). Indeed, the use of some amino acids like Gln, Asn, Ala, Asp, Gly, and Arg as a source of nitrogen has been shown to increase plant biomass (Forsum et al. 2008). However, the concentration of each amino acid is much lower in our conditions compared to the concentrations used in previous publications (Forsum et al. 2008; Lardos et al. 2024). Moreover, AVP application in the absence of nitrate does not stimulate root growth (Figure 1B), meaning that AVP does not have a nutritional function but a biostimulant action, as expected.

On the other hand, AVP concentrations above the stimulating 100 mg/L concentration inhibit root growth (Figure 1A). The phenomenon is probably linked to the amino acid composition of AVP (Figure S1) since some amino acids, such as Val, Leu, and Ile, are known to be toxic to the plant at high concentrations (Miflin 1969; Bourgin 1978). Indeed, BCAA biosynthesis involves allosteric regulation by the end‐products of this pathway, Val, Leu, and Ile. This means that these three amino acids need to be provided in similar amounts to avoid the toxicity of one of them (Bourgin 1978; Bonner et al. 1992; Pratelli et al. 2010). Therefore, the stimulating or toxic effect of AVP could depend on the equilibrium between each amino acid content in the AVP product. We cannot exclude that the biostimulant action of AVP originates from the peptidic fraction too. It could thus be interesting to identify more precisely the peptides present in AVP and to test them on Arabidopsis root growth. Indeed, it has been shown that peptides play an important role in root growth and development (Hastwell et al. 2024).

On the other hand, experiments in soil were conducted to see if the effect of AVP on plants grown in vitro can be transposed to plants grown in soil. By measuring the rosette area and the aerial biomass, AVP seems to have a positive and dose‐dependent effect on these two parameters (Figure 7). Even with higher AVP content, we did not detect any inhibitory effect on plant growth that could be explained by the degradation of toxic compounds of the biostimulant by the soil micro‐organisms. Apart from the main root length, the rosette architecture of Arabidopsis depends on the nitrogen concentration. Indeed, plants can perceive the availability of nitrate through their root tips (Gaudinier et al. 2018). Low nitrate content induces the expression of the NRT2.1 gene, which belongs to the high‐affinity transporters of nitrates (HATS). This, in turn, promotes root branching (Little et al. 2005; Remans et al. 2006). On the contrary, high nitrate content or the presence of amino acids inhibits the expression of the HATS and the branching (Nazoa et al. 2003). This is consistent with our results showing that AVP, by bringing amino acids into the plant, increases its content of organic nitrogen, thus repressing NRT2.1 expression and activity (Figure 5E) and the growth of the lateral roots (Figure S2B,C). Indeed, the expression of LHT1 (LYSINE HISTIDINE TRANSPORTER 1), which is a transporter of amino acids such as Ala, Glu, Gln, and Asn (Hirner et al. 2006; Forsum et al. 2008; Svennerstam et al. 2011; Ganeteg et al. 2017), is induced, meaning that amino acids from AVP are probably transported from the media to the roots. Other amino acid transporters like CAT1 (CATIONIC AMINO ACID TRANSPORTER 1) and ProT1 (PROLINE TRANSPORTER 1), which are involved in the phloem loading of respectively Lys, Arg, Glu, and Pro, GABA (Frommer et al. 1995; Grallath et al. 2005; Havé et al. 2017; Dinkeloo et al. 2018), are also induced, probably to remobilize these amino acids. In fact, Glu and Asp are in plants precursors of many amino acids, including Gln, Val, Leu, and GABA from Glu; as well as Asn and Ile from Asp (Forde and Lea 2007). This is in line with the metabolomic analysis showing that AVP‐treated plants accumulated more amino acids than control plants. Gln, for example, which was present in the nanomolar range in the composition of AVP, was much more accumulated in AVP plants, probably as a result of stimulation of nitrogen metabolism. Other amino acids were accumulated, such as glutamate, which is probably converted mostly into Gln but also into GABA or α‐ketoglutarate through the action of, respectively, GDH (GLUTAMATE DEHYDROGENASE 2) or GAD (GLUTAMATE DECARBOXYLASE) (Benidickson et al. 2023). GABA is a non‐proteogenic amino acid with multiple functions in plants, including signaling N and C metabolisms and playing a role in stress adaptation (Caspi et al. 2023). Another non‐proteogenic amino acid that accumulates is pipecolic acid (Figure 2A), a Lys catabolite which can be hydroxylated to N‐hydroxypipecolic acid, an important molecule that primes and amplifies plant defenses against bacterial and other pathogens (Hartmann et al. 2018). The increase in GABA concentration is in line with the induction of GAD observed in the transcriptomic analysis. Together with GDH, which transcript is also induced, and the synthesis of Gln, these reactions may serve as a sink for the large quantities of Glu provided by the supply of AVP. Indeed, Glu levels, as often in plants (Forde 2014), have been found to be relatively unchanged by AVP. Glu could also influence the Glu receptors, which function in amino acid signaling (Forde 2014). Among the other accumulated amino acids, Leu is also found in higher quantities in AVP‐treated plants than in control plants (Figure S3). Through this accumulation and because BCAA and especially Leu stimulate TOR pathways (Cao et al. 2019), it was not surprising to see a slight but reproducible TOR activation in AVP plants (Figure 6A). Therefore, some of the effects of AVP on plant growth and development could be ascribed to the induction of the stimulating effect of TOR on cell expansion and proliferation.

Further research is now needed to identify the components of PH that stimulate plant growth and the mechanisms of this action. Moreover, we only studied the effects of AVP PH in controlled in vitro or soil conditions; the potential of this product, as well as the optimal dose for the field or other plants, remains to be determined. Nevertheless, the use of high‐throughput phenotyping in soil plugs with the conditions we established in this work will probably be very helpful in the future.
