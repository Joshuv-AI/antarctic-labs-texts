Exploring microbiome shifts across taxonomic and ecological groups of birds at a key stopover site in Punjab, Pakistan
Authors: Amina Tufail, Tingbei Bo, Na Zhao, Sandi Willows-Munro, Bushra Nisar Khan, Jundong Duan, Jianshi Jin, Yanhua Qu, Fumin Lei, Gang Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First comprehensive report describing avian gut microbiome diversity across multiple taxonomic and ecological groups from a migratory stopover in Pakistan, addressing a major geographic and data gap in avian microbiome research.•Host taxonomy exerted a stronger influence on gut microbiota than ecology, with diverse and group-specific microbial and functional profiles, including potential zoonotic pathogens (Enterococcus, Escherichia–Shigella, Acinetobacter).•The dataset provides a baseline resource and framework for future studies on avian microbial ecology, environmental surveillance, and conservation along migratory flyways in Pakistan.

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract of bird harbours a diverse and dynamic microbial community (Ley et al., 2008), that is crucial to host physiology, development, and adaptation (Waite and Taylor, 2015). These microbial symbionts contribute to nutrient absorption, immune modulation, energy metabolism, and even neurobehavioral processes (Flint et al., 2012, Nicholson et al., 2012, Browne et al., 2017, Sherwin et al., 2019). In recent years, the gut microbiome (encompassing both the microbial community and its functional potential (Aagaard and Segars, 2014)) has been recognized not only as a critical regulator of host health but also as a key player in ecological adaptation, influencing traits such as diet specialization (Li et al., 2025a), reproduction, nd even hybrid viability (Sharon et al., 2010, Brucker and Bordenstein, 2013).

Among vertebrates, birds present a unique model for microbiome research due to their distinct evolutionary history, compact gastrointestinal morphology, and high metabolic demands (Ottinger et al., 2024, Weeks et al., 2022). Features such as short food retention time, high body temperature, and specialized digestive anatomy support the energetic requirements of flight, but also create a selective environment for microbial colonization (Kohl, 2012, Waite and Taylor, 2014). Birds also exhibit extraordinary ecological diversity, occupying a range of trophic levels and habitats from aquatic waders, waterfowl, kingfishers, to terrestrial raptors and Passerines (Tu et al., 2020). These ecological differences, along with variations in their diet and foraging strategies, leading to distinct microbial signatures (Włodarczyk et al., 2024, Fan et al., 2022, Xiao et al., 2021). In particular, host diet, habitat type, and evolutionary lineage interact to shape microbial assemblages, often resulting in strong taxonomic and functional differentiation among avian ecological groups (Yang et al., 2016, Grond et al., 2019).

Emerging evidence even suggests that a single host gene can significantly influence gut microbial architecture, highlighting the complexity of host microbe interactions and the potential for fine scale evolutionary adaptation (Li et al., 2025b, Spor et al., 2011). Notably, birds, as highly mobile organisms, can avoid unfavourable habitats and fly over ecological barriers (Arya et al., 2024). Stopover sites serve as critical ecological filters where birds are exposed to new environments and interact with cohabitating species. These shared spaces and foraging grounds facilitate microbial exchange between individuals of different species and ecological types via contact with water, soil, and faeces (Fablet et al., 2024, Alm et al., 2018, Groussin et al., 2017). Such horizontal transmission, especially among mixed-species assemblages, may blur host-specific microbial signatures and introduce both beneficial symbionts and potential pathogens (Perofsky et al., 2019, Huang et al., 2014).

This ecological overlap not only promotes microbial connectivity across species but also enables the formation of shared microbial “metacommunities,” potentially accelerating microbial evolution and dissemination across geographic landscapes (Jing et al., 2024, Fujita et al., 2023). These dynamics have far-reaching ecological and health implications (Custer et al., 2022). Birds may act as carriers of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and zoonotic pathogens, such as multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli, Enterococcus, and Pseudomonas, raising concerns for both wildlife and public health (Kobuszewska and Wysok, 2024, Shobrak and Abo-Amer, 2014, Bonnedahl and Järhult, 2014). Studies have also linked gut microbiome diversity and composition to reproductive outcomes and chick growth in endangered birds, reinforcing the value of the microbiome as a diagnostic indicator of host health and stress. As such, profiling avian gut microbiome has emerged as a promising tool in wildlife conservation and One Health frameworks.

Yet, despite this importance, a comprehensive and comparative analysis of gut microbiome across multiple avian orders and ecological types from South Asia is still scarce. This gap is particularly notable in ecologically rich stopover regions such as the Punjab province of Pakistan, which lies along the Central and East Asian migratory flyways. The River Ravi Siphon near Lahore is one such understudied site, which hosts diverse migratory bird species. Environmental degradation, urban encroachment, and changes in hydrological regimes (Akhtar and Nawaz, 2012, Ezdi and Ijaz, 2025), further amplifies the need for baseline microbial surveillance in this region. Understanding microbial community shifts at these ecological pinch points offers an early warning system for ecosystem health, zoonotic risk, and migratory connectivity.

To address these gaps, the present study aims to investigate the gut microbiome of wild birds representing five ecological groups: waders, waterfowl, kingfishers, passerines, and raptors, and seven taxonomic orders, inhabiting the River Ravi Siphon. We aim to (i) characterize microbial diversity and community structure across ecological types and taxonomic orders, (ii) identify taxonomic biomarkers and potentially pathogenic taxa, and (iii) infer functional roles of the gut microbiome in shaping host adaptation. By integrating ecological and functional perspectives, this study would establish a microbial baseline for birds in Punjab, Pakistan. Our findings may serve as a springboard for future investigations into microbial resilience, cross-species transmission, and avian health in rapidly changing ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Study area

The River Ravi Siphon is an ecologically significant site near Lahore, Pakistan, between 31.689885° N, 74.409032° E and 31.715251° N, 74.460807° E. The River Ravi, a 750 km-long tributary of the Indus Basin, originates from the Himalayan glaciers in Himachal Pradesh, India, and flows through Punjab, Pakistan, before merging with the Chenab River. As it meanders along the Pakistan-India border, the river intermittently crosses into both territories (Akhtar and Nawaz, 2012). The Siphon site, located in Sheikhupura District, is a well-known fishing and wildlife habitat, where the Ravi River flows over the BRB (Bambanwala-Ravi-Bedian) Canal, accessible via G.T. Road from Lahore, Islamabad Motorway M-1, and Ghazi Kakka Road (Fig. 1). The surrounding region represents a typical agro-ecological landscape dominated by irrigated croplands, primarily cultivated with rice, wheat, and fodder crops supported by the extensive canal system of the Ravi River. Such intensive agricultural practices and frequent irrigation likely influence the physicochemical characteristics of the river and nearby soils. This site provides stopover grounds for migratory birds, making it an ideal location for studying avian foraging, roosting, and movement patterns (Khan et al., 2024).Fig. 1Spatial representation of the study area along the River Ravi Siphon, Lahore, Punjab, Pakistan.(A) Overview map of Pakistan highlighting Punjab Province and its drainage network.(B) Enlarged map of Punjab showing the location of Lahore District and the River Ravi within the Indus Basin along other rivers.(C) High-resolution satellite-based map of the Ravi Siphon sampling area depicting survey points (red dots), surrounding farmlands, and the river channel. Sample collection

We collected in total 41 fresh fecal samples from bird individuals inhabiting the wetland and riparian ecosystems of the Ravi River Siphon during the late wintering period from February to March, 2024 (Fig. 2). The migratory status and approximate seasonal ranges of each species are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The assemblage of birds comprised long-distance migrants, short-distance migrants, and resident birds, representing five distinct ecological groups across seven avian orders, covering 17 species (Supplementary table S1). The waders (n = 10) included species such as the Common Crane (Grus grus), Greater Flamingo (Phoenicopterus roseus), Green Sandpiper (Tringa ochropus), and Yellow-wattled Lapwing (Vanellus malabaricus). The waterfowl group (n = 19) comprised the Garganey (Anas querquedula), Bar-Headed Goose (Anser indicus), Gadwall (Anas strepera), Northern Pintail (Anas acuta), Mallard (Anas platyrhynchos), Red-crested Pochard (Netta rufina), Common Pochard (Aythya ferina), and Spotted-Billed Duck (Anas poecilorhyncha). Among the passerines (n = 6), we sampled the Citrine Wagtail (Motacilla citreola), Stonechat (Saxicola torquatus), and Barn Swallow (Hirundo rustica). The raptors (n = 3) were represented by the Marsh Harrier (Circus aeruginosus), and the kingfishers (n = 3) by the White-throated Kingfisher (Halcyon smyrnensis). The birds were identified and observed prior to sampling to confirm species and individual identification using field guides (Grimmett et al., 2008). Birds were either captured using mist nets or observed in the field, depending on their species and behavior. Samples were collected shortly after the birds naturally defecated in the presence of the research team. Only freshly deposited fecal material was sampled using noninvasive methods under strict field hygiene protocols to prevent contamination, ensuring minimal environmental degradation (Darimont et al., 2008). For waders, waterfowl, passerines, and kingfishers, only the middle layer of the fecal deposit was extracted to avoid contamination from external surfaces. Raptor feces were collected using sterile forceps or syringe suction following established protocols (Becker et al., 2020). DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of each faecal sample using the QIAamp PowerFecal DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. To ensure DNA purity and integrity, extracted DNA was assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and visualized through 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. Additionally, DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to confirm sufficient yield for downstream applications. The extracted DNA was stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 341F (CCTACGGGNBGCASCAG) and 805R (GACTACNVGGGTATCTAATCC). PCR amplification was carried out in a 25 µL reaction mixture containing 5 µL of 5× GC Buffer, 0.5 µL of KAPA dNTP Mix, 0.5 µL of KAPA HiFi HotStart DNA Polymerase, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 pM), and 50–100 ng of template DNA. The PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min.

PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA) to remove residual primers and unwanted by-products. A second PCR was performed for library indexing, where a unique eight-base barcode sequence was added to each sample. This indexing PCR was conducted in a 25 µL reaction volume, containing 5 µL of 5× GC Buffer, 0.75 µL of KAPA dNTP Mix, 0.5 µL of KAPA HiFi HotStart DNA Polymerase, 1.5 µL of each primer (10 pM), and 5 µL of purified amplicon. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 8 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Following amplification, the indexed amplicons were purified using AMPure XP beads, pooled in equimolar concentrations, and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2×250 bp) on the Illumina MiSeq platform following standard Illumina protocols.

Sequencing data processing and statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were conducted using QIIME2 (version 2024.2) (Bolyen et al., 2019). Raw paired-end sequences were quality-checked, filtered, denoised, and chimera-removed using DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016) to generate high-confidence feature sequences (amplicon sequence variants, ASVs). Primers were removed using the Cutadapt plugin in QIIME2, following primer trimming, sequences with a length below 200 bp were filtered out to improve sequence quality and minimize spurious ASVs. The SILVA 16S rRNA reference database (silva-138–99–515–806-nb) (Quast et al., 2012) was used for taxonomic classification with a Naive Bayes classifier, trained for the V3–V4 hypervariable region. ASVs identified as mitochondrial, chloroplast, archaeal, or eukaryotic sequences were filtered out to minimize non-target amplification (Risely et al., 2018, Baker et al., 2003). Additionally, rare ASVs (≤4 occurrences) were removed to enhance dataset robustness, following a common practice in microbiome research (Callahan et al., 2016). To effectively represent the biological composition of the samples, bar plots were generated to visualize the average relative abundance of bacterial communities at the phylum and genus levels. The feature table was then rarefied to a uniform sequencing depth prior to diversity analysis and ASV richness calculations, minimizing bias from uneven sequencing effort.

The sequencing effectiveness was evaluated using alpha rarefaction curves, generated in tidyverse (version 2.0.0) in R (version 4.4.2). Microbial diversity within samples (alpha diversity) was assessed using the Shannon index, calculated in QIIME2. Alpha diversity between the groups and orders was compared statistically using the non-parametric Wilcoxon Rank-sum test. To explore differences in community composition across orders and ecological types, beta diversity was calculated using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Weighted and Unweighted UniFrac distances, visualized in ggplot2 (version 3.5.1, R version 4.4.2). Statistical differences in beta diversity were tested using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) (Oksanen, 2007). The UpSetR (version 1.4.0) package in R was used to visualize the number of unique and shared ASVs across different species and ecological groups. The ecological background of the 17 species, such like dietary habits, were obtained from EltonTraits 1.0 (Wilman et al., 2014).

Differential abundance analysis was performed using LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) using microeco package (version 0.14.0) in R (version 4.4.2) (Liu et al., 2021). Taxa significantly enriched across ecological groups and taxonomic orders were identified with an LDA score threshold of ≥ 3.5 and p < 0.05. Finally, functional predictions of microbial communities were carried out using PICRUSt2 (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) (Douglas et al., 2020). Functional pathway differences between ecological groups and taxonomic orders were statistically analyzed using the Lefse tool of BIC (Bioinformatics cloud) (https://www.bic.ac.cn/BIC/#/), with a significance threshold of ≥ 3.5 and p < 0.05 applied to assess differential metabolic capabilities across ecological types.

Results

Sequencing metrics and ASV richness across different orders /ecological types

After quality control and filtering, high-throughput sequencing generated a total of 677,201 effective reads across 41 samples, with an average of 16,517 ± 4759 reads per sample (mean ± SD) (Supplementary table S2). Quality control with DADA2 resulted in 10,381 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The ASV distribution varied across ecological types, with differences in the total number of observed ASVs among waders, waterfowl, passerines, raptors, and kingfishers exhibiting distinct microbial compositions (Supplementary table S3).

Waterfowl exhibited the highest total number of ASVs (766), followed by waders (625) and passerines (560). Kingfishers had 281 ASVs, while raptors displayed the lowest ASV count at 214. In terms of orders, Anseriformes annotated with the highest ASV count (766), followed by Passeriformes (560) and Charadriiformes (507). While the orders Coraciiformes, Accipitriformes, Phoenicopteriformes, and Gruiformes exhibited moderate ASV richness, with counts of 281, 214, 232, and 203, respectively. The UpSet plot showed that only 117 ASVs were found shared across the ecological types, while 68 ASVs were common across the different orders (Supplementary Figure 1A, B). The rarefaction curve (Supplementary Figure 2A, B) confirmed adequate sequencing depth based on the Shannon index across Ecological types and orders.

A significant difference in alpha diversity was observed across both ecological types and orders. Among the five ecological groups (Fig. 3A), pairwise comparisons revealed that passerines had a significantly higher Shannon index compared to waterfowl, waders, and kingfishers (p < 0.05). In contrast, raptors exhibited a significantly lower Shannon index compared to all other ecological types (p < 0.05). Gut microbial composition of different orders /ecological types

A total of 36 bacterial phyla were identified across all samples (n= 41). The most abundant phyla were Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, and Bacteroidota. Phyla with a relative abundance of less than 1 % or without taxonomic annotation were grouped as others. Significant differences in the relative abundance of core phyla were observed among the five ecological types (Fig. 5A). Specifically, waterfowl and waders exhibited higher proportions of Firmicutes (51.40 % and 26.60 %), Proteobacteria (43.96 % and 19.17 %), and Actinobacteriota (41.99 % and 19.69 %) compared to other groups. In contrast, passerines were enriched in Bacteroidota (49.30 %) and Chloroflexi (45.98 %). Notably, Fusobacteriota was significantly more abundant in waterfowl and raptors (47.49 % and 27.24 %, respectively) than in other ecological types (Supplementary table S7).Fig. 5Community composition of avian gut microbiome across five ecological types (A, C) and taxonomic orders (B, D), based on relative abundance profiles annotated at the, (A, B) Phylum level and, (C, D) Genus level.Each stacked bar represents the mean relative abundance of bacterial taxa (indicated by color) within each ecological group and taxonomic order, highlighting differences in microbial composition.Fig 5

There were also significant differences in the relative abundance of bacterial phyla between different avian orders (Fig. 5B). For example, the average relative abundance of Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota were higher in Anseriformes (51.40 %, 43.96 % and 41.99 %), whereas it was considerably lower in other orders. While Fusobacteria were higher in Anseriformes and Accipitriformes (47.49 %, and 27.24 %). A distinct pattern was observed for Chloroflexi and Bacteroidota (45.98 %, and 49.30 %), which were most abundant in Passeriformes, with relatively low abundance in the remaining orders (Supplementary table S8).

A total of 806 bacterial genera and 99 uncultured genera were identified in the samples (Fig. 5C). Genera with a relative abundance of low entries, less than 1 %, or unclassified entries were grouped as 'others'. The most dominant genera across ecological types were Enterococcus, Gallicola, Escherichia–Shigella, Bacillus, and Lactococcus. Specifically, Lactococcus (85.88 %), Enterococcus (62.17 %), and Gallicola (54.01 %) were notably more abundant in waterfowl than in other ecological types. Lactococcus (78.52 %) and Bacillus (25.60 %) were the prevailing genera in waders compared to other ecological types. The genus Escherichia–Shigella exhibited its highest abundance in waterfowl (39.91 %) and kingfishers (34.52 %), while observed in lower abundance in others (Supplementary table S7).

Among the dominant genera, distribution patterns varied significantly across avian orders (Fig. 5D). For instance, Enterococcus was most abundant in Anseriformes (62.17 %) and Passeriformes (14.78 %) but relatively scarce in other orders. Gallicola showed the highest abundance in Anseriformes (54.01 %), Coraciiformes (14.76 %), and Phoenicopteriformes (12.73 %), with markedly lower levels elsewhere. Escherichia–Shigella was enriched in Anseriformes (39.91 %) and Coraciiformes (34.52 %), while reduced in other orders. Lactococcus was dominant in Anseriformes (85.88 %) but remained low in all other orders. Notably, Bacillus showed extreme enrichment in Gruiformes (74.48 %) while appearing at lower levels in other orders (Supplementary table S8). Importantly, several identified genera in this study, including Enterococcus, Escherichia–Shigella, Pseudomonas, Corynebacterium, Acinetobacter, Proteus, Paracoccus, Serratia, and Mycobacterium are also known for their pathogenic potential.

In addition to the group-level microbiome analysis, we conducted a species-level overview to better understand how individual species contribute to the overall microbial diversity within each ecological type and taxonomic group. This analysis was important because it allowed us to explore the variation in gut microbiomes among closely related species, even within the same ecological group or order. By incorporating taxonomic classifications, dietary composition, and microbial diversity metrics, we gained a more detailed perspective on the species-specific microbial dynamics, which are essential for understanding how host characteristics influence microbial communities. This species-level analysis complements the group-level findings, providing deeper insights into the microbial shifts across taxonomic and ecological groups at the study site (Fig. 2).

At the taxonomic level, seven significantly different genera were identified: Murdochiella, Actinomycetaceae, Marinilactibacillus, Eisenbergiella, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Pygmaiobacter, and Eggerthella (Fig. 6C). Discussion

Birds, as highly mobile organisms, encounter diverse environments, leading to significant variations in their gut microbiome (Li et al., 2024). These microbial communities play a crucial role in digestion, metabolism, and immune function (Grond et al., 2018). Recent studies have highlighted the influence of stopover habitats on the composition of avian gut microbiomes, indicating that environmental factors at these sites can drive microbial diversity and functions (Włodarczyk et al., 2024). However, limited research exists on avian gut microbiome in South Asian regions. We aim to address this gap by providing a pioneering assessment of microbiome shifts across various ecological types and taxonomic orders of birds from a key stopover site in Punjab.

Among the five avian ecological groups co-inhabited the same geographical location, each group exhibits distinct habitat preferences and dietary habits (see Fig. 2), which are key factors influencing their gut microbiome composition. For instance, Waders typically forage near shallow water bodies, shorelines, marshes, and mudflats, while waterfowl are inhabitants of freshwater or saltwater bodies (Zhao et al., 2022a). Kingfishers are often found along riverbanks, primarily hunting fish by diving from perches. Whereas Passerines also known song birds, comprise more than half of all avian species, they have diverse habitats and varied diets (Dorrestein, 2009). In contrast, Raptors, or birds of prey, are carnivorous and inhabit forests, deserts, tundra, and grasslands (McClure et al., 2019).

These ecological and dietary distinctions are reflected in the gut microbiome profiles, as evidenced by significant differences in alpha diversity across groups. Significant variation in alpha diversity, measured by the Shannon index, was observed among groups. Passerines (Passeriformes) exhibited the highest diversity, suggesting a more complex and balanced gut microbial community, whereas raptors (Acciptiformes) showed markedly lower diversity, indicative of a more specialized microbiome in relation to protein food consumption (see Fig. 3A, and B). These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that ecological lifestyle and feeding behavior can strongly influence microbial richness and evenness (Włodarczyk et al., 2024, Abdul Rahman et al., 2015, Yu et al., 2022).

Beta diversity revealed partial clustering by ecological type and order. Although most pairwise comparisons were not statistically significant, notable differentiation was observed between waterfowl and raptors, and between Anseriformes and Accipitriformes, as confirmed by PERMANOVA. Avian orders exhibited greater dispersion along the PCoA1 axis, suggesting higher variability in gut microbiome community structure (see Fig. 4A, B, C and D). These patterns suggest that host associated factors, such as physiology and foraging ecology, may subtly shape gut microbial community structure. Similar trends have been reported in other wild species, where environmental exposure, rather than host taxonomy alone, influenced microbial assembly (Fan et al., 2022, Yu et al., 2022).

Spectrum of taxonomic and ecological control of avian gut microbiome

The composition of avian gut microbiome varies significantly across different ecological groups, influenced by factors such as diet, habitat, and host taxonomy (Wang et al., 2022). Across all avian groups, the predominant bacterial phyla identified were Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Chloroflexi, and Bacteroidota (see Fig. 5A), consistent with the core avian gut microbiome reported in earlier studies (Waite and Taylor, 2014, Xenoulis et al., 2010, Dewar et al., 2013, Wang et al., 2016, Zhao et al., 2017).

In this study, Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota were observed dominating in both waterfowl and waders, but their abundance was notably higher in waterfowl, consistent with previous studies (Zhao et al., 2022a, Wang et al., 2022). The high abundance of Firmicutes has been linked to enhanced fermentation of complex carbohydrates, resulting in the production of short-chain fatty acids that serve as key energy sources for the host (Flint et al., 2012, Ouyang et al., 2023, Tap et al., 2009). Actinobacteria, often acquired from environmental reservoirs such as soil and water, are known for breaking down complex organic matter and producing bioactive compounds, indicating their role in modulating host metabolism and immunity (Barka et al., 2016, Mikaelyan et al., 2015). Their enrichment in waterfowl and waders is consistent with studies that suggest regular exposure to aquatic and sediment-associated microbiome (Bathia et al., 2024).

In addition to these core phyla, Cyanobacteria was detected at high abundance in waterfowl and kingfishers, likely due to their consumption of aquatic prey and vegetation (Gilbert et al., 2016). Cyanobacteria, typically known for their photosynthetic and nitrogen fixation capabilities, have also been reported in the guts of aquatic animals (Wang et al., 2022, Di Rienzi et al., 2013). Importantly, some Cyanobacteria species are often associated with zoonotic transmission and gastrointestinal inflammation in birds and mammals (Gilbert et al., 2016). Although a few studies suggest that certain strains of Cyanobacteria may occur in the gut flora of wild birds without causing clinical symptoms (Song et al., 2021, Gallet et al., 2023), it is more likely that these taxa are transiently acquired from dietary ingestion through contaminated water, algae, or aquatic prey (Zanchett and Oliveira-Filho, 2013, Tornabene et al., 2024). Their presence in water-associated birds from the River Ravi, a river known for its deteriorating water quality (Lai, 2022), therefore, most likely reflects local environmental acquisition . Given their sensitivity to water pollution, Cyanobacteria may serve as bioindicators (Gaur and Bera, 2023). Their detection in the gut microbiome supports the potential of waterbirds for monitoring freshwater ecosystem health (Mansfield et al., 2024, Carrasco et al., 2025, Maznikova et al., 2024, Wei Zhang and Zhang Ma, 2011, Amat and Green, 2009).

Several less explored important phyla were also observed in the waders. While their roles in birds remain underexplored, their known functions in other ecosystems suggest potential relevance to waders' foraging behaviors and wetland habitats. For instance, Bdellovibrionota, recognized for preying on Gram-negative bacteria, may help regulate gut microbial communities (Bratanis et al., 2020). While Fibrobacterota, known for cellulose degradation in herbivores (Neumann and Suen, 2018), might assist in fiber metabolism for birds consuming plant material.

Similarly, waterfowl and waders displayed the gut microbiome dominated by Proteobacteria, a metabolically diverse phylum that includes many species involved in nutrient acquisition and nitrogen cycling, as well as opportunistic pathogens (Waite and Taylor, 2014, Wang et al., 2022, Gilbert et al., 2016). This phylum includes numerous genera associated with pathogenicity, such as Escherichia, Shigella and Campylobacter. The dominance of Proteobacteria in waterfowl and waders is likely influenced by their aquatic diet and habitat, which exposes them to environmental microbes prevalent in aquatic ecosystems. Similar trends have been reported in fish gut microbiome, where water and host exhibit distinct microbial communities but share Proteobacteria as a dominant overlapping group (Sadeghi et al., 2023). This overlap suggests a common association between Proteobacteria abundance and aquatic dietary habits (Liu et al., 2016, Gajardo et al., 2016, Eichmiller et al., 2016), and indicates that certain members may function as environmentally derived or transient taxa linking aquatic and host microbial communities (Talwar et al., 2018, Viver et al., 2023). The prevalence of Proteobacteria in the gut microbiome of waterfowl and waders may therefore reflect both environmental acquisition and ecological adaptation, with implications for their role in harboring and potentially transmitting various pathogens.

The relative abundance of Fusobacteriota was significantly higher in waterfowl and raptors, consistent with earlier observations (Wang et al., 2022). Studies have shown that, in waterfowl, Cetobacterium, a dominant genus, produces acetate through carbohydrate fermentation, which may improve glucose homeostasis in fish (Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, Fusobacteriota species are known to produce butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid that promotes fat accumulation and enhances immunity (Panda et al., 2009). On the other hand, raptors, with their carnivorous diet consisting of protein-rich prey, provide a suitable environment for Fusobacteria. Fusobacterium species are known to thrive in proteinaceous environments, as they can degrade proteins to obtain amino acids and peptides (Hanifeh et al., 2024).

Among passerines, Bacteroidota and Chloroflexi were the dominant phyla. Members of the phylum Bacteroidetes are renowned for their ability to hydrolyze a wide range of polysaccharides, including complex carbohydrates and plant cell wall components, within the gastrointestinal tracts of vertebrates (Tomova et al., 2019). The high relative abundance of Bacteroidetes observed in our study may be attributed to the substantial intake of fiber-rich plants, as diets high in dietary fiber have been shown to promote the growth of Bacteroidetes in the gut microbiome (Tomova et al., 2019). Bacteroidota abundance in passerines aligns with previous findings in herbivorous animals, such as ruminants, where it aids in fermenting plant cell wall compounds, facilitating energy extraction from otherwise indigestible fiber (Thomas et al., 2011). Furthermore, the SCFAs produced by Bacteroidota not only provide energy but also play a role in regulating host metabolism and maintaining gut health (Den Besten et al., 2013).

Similarly, Chloroflexi, formerly known as green non-sulfur bacteria, exhibits remarkable metabolic versatility, including thermophilic adaptation, anoxygenic photosynthesis, and anaerobic degradation of complex organic compounds like (Freches and Fradinho, 2024, Thiel et al., 2018, Pierson and Castenholz, 1974, Sánchez-González et al., 2019). Their high abundance in passerines may reflect environmental acquisition from diverse terrestrial niches and suggests a potential role in degrading resistant compounds or contributing to microbial resilience under fluctuating gut conditions. Among the avian orders examined, Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, and Proteobacteria were predominantly abundant in Anseriformes, while Chloroflexi and Bacteroidota were notably enriched in Passeriformes. Fusobacteriota was highly represented in both Accipitriformes and Anseriformes, and Cyanobacteria showed greater relative abundance in Anseriformes and Coraciiformes. These phylum-level distinctions may reflect dietary preferences, habitat interactions, and order-specific physiological traits.

At the genus level, waterfowl, specifically the order Anseriformes, harbored a high abundance of Enterococcus, Pseudomonas, and Gallicola (see Fig. 5C), which are recognized as opportunistic zoonotic pathogens in humans (Dworkin et al., 2006, Chan et al., 2015, Hermes et al., 2009). This finding is consistent with earlier reports linking these genera to environmental contamination from polluted water and soil (Zhao et al., 2022a, Li et al., 2016, Funk and Smith, 1981), suggesting that extrinsic environmental exposure may shape the gut microbial community in waterbirds. Similarly, Escherichia–Shigella was highly abundant in both waterfowl and kingfishers. This genus, which includes pathogens capable of causing diarrhea and other enteric diseases in animals and humans (Kotloff et al., 2013, Nyaoke et al., 2020), is known for its environmental persistence in secondary habitats such as water, sediments, and algae (Mathai et al., 2019). Its detection in both avian groups may thus be attributed to their frequent interactions with aquatic ecosystems. Additionally, Escherichia–Shigella has been found in herbivorous carnivores like red pandas (Zeng et al., 2018), further supporting its broad ecological distribution.

In contrast, genera such as Catellicoccus, Bacillus, and Lactococcus were significantly more abundant in waders and waterfowl, corresponding to the orders Gruiformes and Anseriformes, respectively. Catellicoccus, a Gram-positive bacterium phylogenetically related to Lactobacillus (Lee et al., 2013) along with the other two genera, are considered commensal microbes. These taxa are often associated with fiber-rich diets and are believed to contribute to gut health by enhancing nutrient absorption, producing antimicrobial compounds like bacteriocins, and modulating immune responses (Zhong et al., 2015, Liu et al., 2014, Milani et al., 2016). In aquatic animals such as fish, these genera have been shown to inhibit pathogens through competitive exclusion, production of lytic enzymes, and competition for binding sites and nutrients (Kawser et al., 2022). Their functional traits suggest a similar probiotic-like role in waterbirds, supporting a balanced and resilient gut microbiome.

Our findings highlight the crucial interplay between habitat preference and evolutionary lineage as key factors shaping the avian gut microbiome, offering deeper insights into host–microbe co-adaptation across ecological landscapes (Loo et al., 2019, Lee et al., 2020, Skeen et al., 2021).

Pathogenic profiling in wild birds across ecological types

Although the avian gut harbors diverse commensal and probiotic bacteria, our analysis revealed the presence of several potential pathogenic genera (Supplementary table S9), underscoring important implications for both avian and public health. Monitoring these taxa is essential, particularly for species exposed to human-influenced environments. For instance, Enterococcus and Escherichia-Shigella, known zoonotic pathogens, and their overabundance may lead to gut dysbiosis (Zhao et al., 2022b, Said et al., 2021), were abundantly present in Spotted-Billed Duck, Bar-Headed Goose, Common Pochard, Flamingo, and White-throated kingfisher, potentially reflecting acquisition through contaminated water sources, aligning with previous reports that waterbirds tend to harbor higher pathogen loads compared to other ecological types (Qiu et al., 2024).

Similarly, Pseudomonas, a widespread environmental pathogen present in water and soil, commonly causes nosocomial infections in humans (Reynolds and Kollef, 2021), showed high abundance in Northern Pintail and Citrine Wagtail. A related study demonstrated that early detection of such potential pathogens in endangered birds like Strigops habroptila can provide critical insights into conservation efforts before health declines occur (Waite et al., 2012). Larus melanocephalus (Camarda et al., 2006), Himantopus himantopus, and other waterbirds (Santos et al., 2012, Grond et al., 2014) have similarly been reported to harbor pathogens of public health concern. Supporting this, our detection of Corynebacterium, notably abundant in the Spotted-Billed Duck, adds further weight, as this genus includes species known to cause diseases in both birds and mammals (Hoelzle et al., 2013, Potti et al., 2002).

Moreover, genera like Proteus, Paracoccus, Serratia, and Mycobacterium, though often part of the normal gut flora (Jamil et al., 2017), are also associated with opportunistic infections ranging from enteric to systemic disorders (Guentzel, 1996, Drzewiecka, 2016), and were frequently observed in Green Sandpiper, Barn swallow, Citrine Wagtail, and White-throated Kingfisher. Notably, Acinetobacter, an emerging multi-drug-resistant pathogen (Wareth et al., 2019), was found in a raptor, Marsh Harrier, consistent with the previous observation of its prevalence in birds (Dahiru and Enabulele, 2015), poses growing concern due to multidrug resistance. These findings collectively highlight the value of gut microbiome surveillance for early pathogen detection and provide a baseline for evaluating zoonotic risks and avian health threats, especially among bird populations.

Functional implications of microbiome composition

In line with prior studies (Waite and Taylor, 2014, Langille et al., 2013a), the most abundant functions across ecological types were associated with carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, membrane transport, and energy metabolism, reflecting core microbial roles in nutrient processing and host energy regulation (see Fig. 7). Notably, ecological types exhibited distinct functional profiles.

Waterfowl showed enrichment in lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and terpenoid and polyketide metabolism. This likely reflects their diverse omnivorous and herbivorous diets, consisting of protein and fat-rich aquatic prey, which include aquatic invertebrates, small fish, aquatic plants, and agricultural vegetation (Fredrickson and Reid, 1988, Steckel, 2003). Studies have shown that in aquatic animals, the gut microbiome is known to facilitate lipid digestion and contribute to lipid diversity by accelerating the breakdown of exogenous lipids and transforming lipid molecules (Wu et al., 2024), which may explain the high abundance of lipid metabolism pathways observed in waterfowl. Similarly, the enrichment of terpenoid and polyketide metabolism pathways may relate to microbial processing of secondary plant metabolites and aquatic-derived compounds present in wetland environments (Yoon et al., 2024).

Waders harbored pathways related to xenobiotic biodegradation, viral and parasitic infections, and neurodegenerative disease, possibly reflecting exposure to contaminated wetlands (Wu et al., 2018). Raptors were characterized by high abundances of bacterial infection pathways, antimicrobial resistance, and immune-related functions, where microbes may play a role in immune defense (Marrow et al., 2009), likely shaped by their carnivorous diet and environmental exposure to pathogens, but also by their position as apex predators at the top of the food web. For instance, research has shown that apex predators like raptors are particularly vulnerable to environmental contaminants (Bariod et al., 2025). In passerines, increased abundance of glycan metabolism, membrane transport, and endocrine-related pathways suggests microbial specialization toward fiber-rich diets and gut homeostasis (Domer et al., 2025). Glycans play major metabolic (e.g., carbohydrates as carbon sources for storage and manipulation of animal behavior: pollination and seed dispersal), structural (extracellular scaffolds), and physical roles in biological systems (Gagneux et al., 2022).

While kingfishers have a high abundance of Membrane transport, Carbohydrate metabolism, Translation, and Replication and repair pathways, reflect microbial adaptations to their protein-rich diet and high metabolic demands, supporting nutrient uptake and cellular maintenance. These ecological differences imply that microbial community structure is tightly coupled with functional capacity, influenced by dietary niche, trophic level, and environmental interactions. Despite the predictive limitations of PICRUSt2, including dependency on reference genomes (Langille et al., 2013b), these findings support the hypothesis that gut microbiome functions co-adapt with host ecology and contribute to avian health and resilience.

Study limitations and future directions

While this study provides insights into the gut microbiome of avian orders /ecological groups, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size per ecological group was limited, which may constrain the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Although rarefaction and normalization techniques were applied to reduce biases associated with uneven sequencing depth and sampling effort, these methods cannot fully compensate for unbalanced group sizes. Future studies are encouraged to include larger and more evenly distributed sample sets across ecological types to improve statistical robustness and validate the observed patterns.

Second, our analysis focused solely on host-associated microbial diversity without incorporating environmental variables (e.g., water quality, soil microbiome, or diet composition). Since external factors likely influence gut microbial communities, the absence of such data limits our ability to disentangle host-specific adaptations from environmental effects. Integrating habitat metadata in future work would provide a more nuanced understanding of microbiome-environment interactions. These limitations underscore the need for expanded sampling and multidisciplinary approaches in avian microbiome research. Addressing these gaps could further elucidate the ecological drivers shaping microbial assemblages across bird guilds.

Beyond these limitations, future research should employ shotgun metagenomic sequencing to expand current insights by identifying functional genes, antibiotic-resistance determinants, dietary components, and non-bacterial taxa (e.g., fungi and protists). Additionally, longitudinal and multi-seasonal sampling across breeding, migratory, and wintering grounds would be valuable to capture temporal variation and assess how migratory behavior influences gut microbiome composition. In parallel, cross-study meta-analyses integrating raw microbiome datasets across multiple bird species, habitats, and regions would enable standardized re-analyses and facilitate the detection of consistent host–microbiome–environment patterns across broader ecological scales. Together, these approaches would provide a more comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of the ecological and functional complexity of avian gut microbiome.

Conclusions

This study presents the first comprehensive investigation of gut microbiome composition across diverse ecological types of birds at a key stopover site from south Asia, such as River Ravi Punjab, Pakistan. We recorded significant differences in microbial diversity and functional pathways among avian groups, largely driven by diet, habitat, and foraging strategies. Passerines exhibited the highest alpha diversity, indicating complex and balanced gut microbial communities, while raptors displayed more specialized gut microbial communities, and waterfowl showed microbiome enriched in taxa with known pathogenic potential, reflecting their role as potential carriers or reservoirs. The detection of zoonotic pathogens such as Enterococcus and Escherichia–Shigella underscores the relevance of the avian gut microbiome in disease surveillance, especially in human-impacted wetland habitats. Functional predictions revealed ecological type-specific enrichment in metabolic and immune-related pathways, supporting the hypothesis that gut microbial functions are shaped by ecological niche and host lifestyle. Despite some limitations, this research emphasizes the role of the gut microbiome as a dynamic biomarker of host environment interactions and implies that migratory routes act as corridors for microbial exchange and potential pathogen dissemination. These findings offer valuable contributions to avian microbiome ecology through microbial monitoring along bird flyways.
