Nano-curcumin potentiates antitumor immunity in prostate cancer via co-culture with adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Farid Zeynali, Melika Hadad Tehran, Fatemeh Faraji, Mahmoud Mahmoudi, Nafiseh Sadat Tabasi, Sevda Ghoreyshi, Fahimeh Lavi Arab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurcumin is a polyphenolic compound from Curcuma longa that exhibits immunoregulatory and anti-cancer properties. It is formulated as nano-curcumin. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) also show anticancer effects and can potentially activate resting immune cells. This study investigates the combined effects of nano-curcumin and adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASCs) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from prostate cancer (PCa) patients.MethodsASCs were isolated and characterized. PBMCs were isolated from patients and co-cultured with and without ASCs in the presence of different concentrations of nano-curcumin to examine cell survival using the MTT assay. Based on the results of the MTT assay, selected concentrations of nano-curcumin were used to investigate apoptosis and cell subpopulations of PBMCs in co-culture with ASCs using apoptosis assay and flow cytometry.ResultsNano-curcumin increased PBMC survival at concentrations of 3, 6, 12, 25, and 50 µM in co-culture with ASCs. In the absence of ASCs, a significant increase in PBMC survival was observed at concentrations of 6, 12, 25, 50, and100 µM. Concentrations of 6 and 12 µM caused a significant reduction in apoptosis. A concentration of 12 µM increased the number of CD3+CD4+ cells, and the number of CD3+CD8+ cells increased at concentrations of 6 and 25 µM. At a concentration of 25 µM, the number of CD45+CD19+ cells was significantly increased.ConclusionNano-curcumin in combination with ASCs has the potential to affect immune cells, indicating its potential as a complementary therapy for use in cancer. Further research is required to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05129-w.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting men worldwide and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, especially in advanced stages where therapeutic resistance to therapy and immune evasion pose substantial clinical challenges. The complex tumor microenvironment in prostate cancer not only supports malignant growth but also contributes to the creation of an immunosuppressive milieu that prevents effective antitumor immunity [1]. In recent years, the investigation of naturally derived bioactive compounds as adjuvants to conventional therapies has attracted considerable attention. Among these, curcumin, a natural polyphenolic compound extracted from the Curcuma longa (turmeric), has demonstrated broad-spectrum inhibitory activities against various cancers, including leukemia, breast, gastrointestinal, brain, and prostate cancers [2–4]. These anticancer activities are attributed to the ability of curcumin to modulate different cellular processes, such as proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and metastasis [5], as well as to regulate key signaling cascades that shape the tumor microenvironment and influence cancer progression [4]. In addition, curcumin exhibits notable immunoregulatory properties. It affects various immune cell populations and regulates cytokine production and the activity of transcription factors [6]. Despite these promising attributes, the clinical application of curcumin is hampered by its poor water solubility and low bioavailability, which limit its therapeutic potential [7]. To overcome these challenges, nano-formulations of curcumin have been developed that improve its solubility, stability, and cellular uptake, thereby offering a more effective approach to cancer treatment [8].

In parallel, adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASCs), known for their immunomodulatory and tumor-homing properties, influence immune responses and tumor progression [9, 10]. They have been reported to enhance the differentiation of CD4+ T cell, which play an important role in maintaining immune homeostasis and mounting effective immune responses [11, 12]. Therefore, the interaction between ASCs and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment may offer new avenues for immunotherapeutic strategies.

Recent evidence suggests that modulating the interaction between stem cells and immune cells through innovative delivery systems can enhance antitumor immunity [13, 14]. The use of nano-curcumin in a co-culture system with ASCs provides a promising research platform to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the enhanced immune response against prostate cancer cells [15].This study aimed to reveal whether nano-curcumin can enhance anti-tumor immunity in prostate cancer through interaction with ASCs and provide insights into novel combinatorial approaches for immunotherapy. Therefore, the effect of nano-curcumin in co-culture with ASCs on the survival, apoptosis, and immune cell subpopulations of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from prostate cancer (PCa) patients was investigated.

Method and material

Isolation and cultivation of ASCs

Under strict sterile conditions, 35 ml of adipose tissue was collected from 5 healthy patients aged between 30 and 35 years who were undergoing cosmetic surgery at a plastic surgery clinic. Sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (Gibco, USA) containing collagenase type 1 enzyme (Worthington, USA) was added to an equal amount of adipose tissue along with collagenase type 1 enzyme. The incubation period was set to 37–45 min, and periodic stirring was performed every 10 min for effective enzyme distribution and cell separation. Collagenase activity was neutralized by adding an equivalent volume of Dulbeccos Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Atocel, Austria) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (PAN Biotech, Australia). After washing the resulting solution twice with Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS), the centrifuged cells that had settled at the bottom of the tube were transferred to ten T175 flasks (SPL Life Science, Korea). The flasks were placed in an incubator after adding 20 ml of DMEM culture medium containing 10% FBS. MSCs adhered to the bottom of the flask in less than a day due to their adhesive properties. The following observations showed the onset of cell proliferation in the form of elongated, spindle-like shape morphology. To remove adipose debris and floating cells and provide purer enriched environment for the growth of MSCs, the culture medium was replaced after 2 days.

Characterization of ASCs

Analysis of cell surface markers and multi-lineage differentiation

Trypsin was used at passage 3 to detach ASCs from the bottom of the flask. In the next step, FBS was used in the entire medium to inhibit trypsin enzyme activity. Following this, the cells were suspended in PBS containing 2% FBS. Two groups of CD-markers were used to confirm the characteristics of MSCs. To confirm the expression of FITC-CD90, PECD105, and FITC-CD73 as well as the absence of PE-CD31, FITC-CD14, APC-CD45, PE-CD34, cells were incubated with specific monoclonal antibodies for 45 min in the dark. FITC-IgG1 was used as an isotype control. After cellular examination using FACS Calibur flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, San), FlowJo software was used for data analysis. In addition, the differentiation of ASCs into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes was investigated using specific differentiation media, and details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Preparation of nano-curcumin

Curcuminoid nanomicelles were formulated using excipients that are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) [16, 17]. The encapsulation efficiency of curcuminoids in these nanomicelles reached nearly 100%. Dynamic light scattering measurements indicated an average diameter of approximately 10 nm for the nanomicelles. Additionally, the content and size distribution of curcuminoid nanomicelles remained stable for at least 24 months.

Isolation of PBMCs from peripheral blood

Whole blood samples were independently collected from five healthy individuals and five PCa patients for isolation of PBMCs. The Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Iran (IR.MUMS.REC.1404.020) issued its approval for this study. Before the experiments began, all participants provided written informed consent. At the time of diagnosis, PCa patients aged 60–80 years were not taking any medications. The control group consisted of healthy individuals, between 60 and 80 years old, without any underlying disease (self-reported or confirmed by questioning). Tumor grades, ranging from 3 to 5, were classified as acinar adenocarcinoma of the prostate. Furthermore, lymphovascular invasion (positive/negative) and perineural invasion (negative/positive) were assessed. Prostate biopsies confirmed the Gleason scores (ranging from 6 to 10) for the patients. A 2 cc blood sample was taken from each individual and diluted with equal volume of PBS under sterile conditions in the laboratory. Then, the diluted blood was transferred to tubes containing Ficoll at a ratio of 2:1. The tubes were centrifuged at 2800 rpm for 15 min. After centrifugation, the cell pellets were washed with PBS and subsequently resuspended in DMEM culture medium to create a solution. In this study, Ctrl-H refers to PBMCs from the healthy control group, consisting of individuals without prostate cancer, and PCP refers to PBMCs from prostate cancer patients co-cultured with ASCs without any treatment with nano-curcumin. CTRL-P refers to PBMCs from prostate cancer patients without co-culture with ASCs and nano-curcumin treatment. To improve understanding of the study design, a schematic representation of all control groups is included in the Supplementary Materials.

MTT assay

Flat-bottom 96-well plates were seeded with ASCs. After reaching 50–60% confluence (5 × 103 cells/ml), they were incubated for 24 h with PBMCs (2 × 104 cells/ml) derived from whole blood of PCa patients and nano-curcumin at concentrations of 3, 6, 12, 25, 50, and 100 µM. Each concentration of nano-curcumin was repeated eight times in a 96-well plate. After this incubation period, 20 µL of MTT solution was added to each well and the plates were incubated for another three hours at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. Formazan crystals formed after this three-hour incubation period, indicating cell viability. After that, 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. Two control groups were included in the MTT assay to evaluate cell viability. The first control group consisted of PBMCs isolated from healthy individuals without any treatment. The second control group included PBMCs obtained from prostate cancer patients without any treatment. An ELISA reader (Stat Fax 3200 Microplate Reader; Awareness Technology Inc. USA) was used to measure absorbance at a wavelength of 570 nm. Based on the results of the MTT assay, concentrations of 6, 12, and 25 µM nano-curcumin in the presence of ASCs were used in subsequent experiments.

Apoptosis assay

In this study, PBMCs (2 × 105) from PCa patients were treated with selected concentrations of nano-curcumin (6, 12, 25µM) in the presence of ASCs (5 × 10¹) in 6-well plates for 24 h to assess apoptosis. Analysis was performed using flow cytometry, and cell viability was evaluated using Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide (PI) staining. 1x binding buffer (500 µL) was used to resuspend the cells. The resuspended cells were then exposed to 2 µL of Annexin V-FITC conjugate for 10–15 min at room temperature while protected from light. Furthermore, 1–2 µL of PI solution was added to the cell suspension and then incubated at room temperature for 1–5 minutes while protected from light. Flow cytometry (BD FACS Calibur Flow Cytometer) was used to quantify apoptotic cells and Flow.jo software was used for data analysis.

Surface antibody staining of PBMCs

After treatment and co-culture, 10 µl of each antibody (CD3: FITC, CD4: FITC, CD8: PE, CD45: PTP, and CD19: PE) was added to the tubes containing the collected PBMCs and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. This was done to stain cell surface biomarkers on PBMCs. 100 µl of lysis buffer solution was added after incubation and the mixture was vortexed. The tube was placed at room temperature in the dark for ten minutes. After this, 2 cc of distilled water was added to complete the lysis reaction and the tube was kept in the dark at room temperature for another 10 min. After a second 10-minute interval, 2 cc of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to stop the lysis reaction, and the contents were centrifuged for 5 min at 1200 rpm to precipitate the white blood cells. After removing the supernatant and adding 2 cc of PBS, centrifugation was performed for 5 min at 3200 rpm. The supernatant was discarded and 2 cc of PBS was added to the cell pellet. Afterwards, surface marker expression on stained cells was assessed using flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

The sample size for isolation of ADSs or PBMCs was determined based on the previous studies [18]. In addition, in the MTT assay, each concentration of Nano-curcumin was tested in eight replicates (n = 8). In apoptosis assay and immune cell subpopulation analysis, each Nano-curcumin concentration was tested in triplicate (n = 3).

The results were expressed as mean ± SD. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9. The normality of data was examined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to analyze the data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of ASCs

MSCs adhered to the bottom of the flask and, after reaching full confluence, exhibited fibroblast-like spindle morphology. Flow cytometry analysis, based on the protocol [19], revealed that MSCs were positive for the surface markers CD105, CD73, and CD90 while they were negative for CD31, CD14, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (Fig. 1). To further confirm their multipotency, MSCs were cultured in specialized differentiation media. After 21 days, Oil Red O staining confirmed adipogenic differentiation by detecting lipid droplets. Osteogenic differentiation was confirmed by mineralized matrix nodules observed after Alizarin Red or alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining with Fast Red, indicating ALP activity. Additionally, chondrogenic differentiation was confirmed by Alcian Blue, Safranin, Toluidine Blue, and Hematoxylin-Eosin staining of chondroitin sections, which revealed the presence of glycosaminoglycan strands and proteoglycan aggrecan, which are hallmarks of chondrogenesis (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Curcumin, a natural polyphenol extracted from turmeric, is well known for its broad biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties [16, 20]. It is formulated as nano-curcumin and modulates immune responses and enhances the body’s defense against tumors by affecting various immune cells such as T cells, B cells, and macrophages [16, 17]. Curcumin has also been reported to act synergistically with MSCs and enhance antitumor immune responses. MSCs secrete a range of cytokines and growth factors that can activate immune cells such as T cells and NK cells, thereby enhancing immune surveillance [19, 21]. This strategy of combining curcumin with MSCs holds promise for cancer immunotherapy, potentially enhancing efficacy while reducing side effects associated with conventional treatments [6, 19, 22]. In our study, we observed that treatment with nano-curcumin significantly increased the viability of PBMCs in co-culture with ASCs at concentrations of 3, 6, 12, 25, and 50 µM. Additionally, we evaluated the viability of PBMCs in the absence of ASCs, and the results demonstrated that concentrations of 6, 12, 25, 50, and 100 µM significantly increased the viability of PBMCs. These results show that in the presence of ASCs, nano-curcumin at lower concentrations can increase PBMC survival, and the increase in survival of PBMCs treated with nano-curcumin in the presence of ASCs was greater than that treated with the same concentration of nano-curcumin in the absence of ASCs. However, in both treatments, in the absence and in the presence of ASCs, the observed increase in viability was not uniform across all concentrations. In addition, based on the results of the viability assay, concentrations of 6, 12, and 25 µM nano-curcumin were used to treat PBMCs in the presence of ASCs to assess apoptosis. The results showed that all concentrations reduce PBMC apoptosis, however, at concentrations 6 and 12 µM this reduction was statistically significant. These findings further confirm that the effect of nano-curcumin is dose-dependent, with lower doses enhancing immune cell survival and higher doses potentially leading to immunosuppression [23].

Consistent with our findings, Luo et al. showed that curcumin at low doses increased T-cell numbers, while at high doses had detrimental effects on immune cells [23]. Furthermore, in tumor-bearing mice, curcumin administration resulted in the prevention of tumor-induced apoptosis in both splenocytes and thymocytes, thereby restoring immune cell numbers and causing tumor regression [24]. Other studies have investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying curcumin’s effects on immune cells. The JAK3-STAT5a pathway has been shown to maintain levels of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 in T cells. Disruption of this pathway in cancer leads to decreased levels of BCL-2. This increases levels of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX, which is responsible for tumor-induced T cell apoptosis. The effects of curcumin on T cells can result in the phosphorylation and activation of the JAK3-STAT5a pathway, thereby restoring BCL-2 levels and consequently reducing T cell apoptosis in tumor-bearing mice [25, 26].The results of our study demonstrated that treatment with 6 and 12 µM concentrations of nano-curcumin in the presence of ASCs increased the number of CD3+CD4+ T cells, but this increase was only statistically significant at the 12 µM concentration. The concentration of 25 µM had no effect on the number of CD3+CD4+ T cells. These findings indicate that the effects of nano-curcumin on T cell subsets are dose-dependent, and low doses of nano-curcumin increase the number of CD4 + T cells. Previous research supports these observations, demonstrating that curcumin increases the proportion of CD4+ T cells while reducing immunosuppressive signals in the tumor microenvironment [27]. Sa et al. demonstrated that curcumin successfully restores CD4 + and CD8 + immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby shifting Th2 immune responses towards a Th1 type [24, 26]. Administration of curcumin to tumor-bearing mice restores CD4 + and CD8 + cells in all immune compartments and prevents the depletion of effector memory and central memory T cells. The increase in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes leads to enhanced anti-tumor responses, thereby destroying tumor cells and eliminating tumor from the body [25].In our study, treatment with nano-curcumin at concentrations of 6, 12, and 25 µM in the presence of ASCs increased the number of CD3+CD8+ T cells. This increase was significant at concentrations of 6 and 25 µM, and the effect of the 6 µM concentration was greater than that of the 25 µM concentration. Curcumin treatment has been found to increase levels of CD8+ T cells and decrease Treg cells, which contributes to a stronger immune response in studies [28, 29]. Furthermore, curcumin has been shown to enhance the cytotoxic capacity of CD8⁺ T cells, particularly in the tumor microenvironment. This is achieved in part through suppression of immunosuppressive signals such as TGF-β and expansion of CD4⁺ helper T cells. Notably, in adoptive T-cell therapy models, curcumin has been shown to potentiate the antitumor function of CD8⁺ T cells by improving their proliferation, survival, and effector function [30, 31]. Finally, our study also showed that treatment with nano-curcumin at concentrations of 12 and 25 µM in the presence of ASCs increased the number of CD45+CD19+ B cells. This increase was only significant at a concentration of 25 µM.

Beyond T cells, curcumin has also been shown to exert regulatory effects on B cell function. It modulates the differentiation of regulatory B cells (Bregs) and increases beneficial Breg subsets such as CD19⁺CD1d⁺, CD19⁺CD25⁺, and CD19⁺Foxp3⁺, while simultaneously reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory B cell subtypes [32]. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that curcumin can regulate B cell activation and apoptosis through suppression of signaling pathways such as ERK, IκB, and p38, particularly in response to TLR ligands [33]. This study also has some limitations, including being limited to in vitro conditions, the lack of positive controls such as LPS (lipopolysaccharide) or PHA (phytohemagglutinin) in the experiments, the lack of studies over different time periods, and the lack of studies of cellular pathways and cell signaling pathways.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that nano-curcumin at low doses in combination with ASCs significantly increases PBMCs viability, reduces apoptosis, and increases the population of CD3+CD4+, CD3+CD8+, and CD45+CD19+ cells. This promising response highlights the potential of nano-curcumin and ASCs co-therapy for further investigation of its clinical application in treatment of prostate cancer, as well as its broader implications in the management of other types of cancer.
