Chemotherapy with bevacizumab and pembrolizumab followed by radiotherapy for unclassified round cell sarcomas of the gallbladder: A case report and review of literature
Authors: Dandan Wang, Ting Li, Xuanyi Wang, Ying Wang, Guixuan Xu, Xue Xiao, Lanlin Hu, Chuan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First report of chemo-immuno-targeted therapy plus radiotherapy in URCS.•CHEK1/ERCC3/TP53 mutations suggest ICI may benefit in MSS/PD-L1-low URCS.•VEGFR amplification suggests that bevacizumab may prolong PFS in URCS patients.

Introduction

The undifferentiated small round cell sarcoma (USRCS) is a rare tumor with an incidence of 0.1 per 100,000 worldwide. Owing to the poorly differentiated features of USRCS, the diagnosis is challenging, with a diagnostic accuracy of 20–30% [1]. Currently, a comprehensive diagnostic framework that combines cellular morphology, immunohistochemical profiling, and molecular genetic testing is the standard for definitive USRCS diagnosis and precise subtype stratification[2]. The USRCS is made up of 50% to 70% Ewing sarcoma (ES) and 30% to 50% Ewing-like sarcoma (ELS). ELS is composed of four subtypes, including CIC rearranged sarcoma (CRS), sarcomas with BCL6 corepressor (BCOR) genetic alterations, round cell sarcomas with EWSR1-non-ETS fusions, and unclassified round cell sarcomas (URCS)[3]. URCS represent rare high-grade mesenchymal neoplasms. Owing to the absence of specific gene rearrangements, it could not be classified as typical ELS. Surgery serves as the standard treatment modality for all adult patients with localized soft-tissue sarcomas, including URCS. Nevertheless, for patients with unresectable URCS, guidelines of treatment from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology remain unclear. Based on the general experience in sarcoma management, the treatment is multidisciplinary, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and best supportive care. The roles of targeted therapy and immunotherapy in URCS remain a topic of ongoing discussion.

The present study reports a case of a 60-year-old patient who was diagnosed with gallbladder URCS. Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) gene amplification was identified in the patient's tumor tissue, along with mutations in checkpoint kinase 1 (CHEK1), excision repair cross-complementing rodent repair deficiency, complementation group 3 (ERCC3), and tumor protein 53 (TP53). Following treatment with chemotherapy combined with targeted therapy (bevacizumab) and immunotherapy (pembrolizumab), the patient achieved a progression-free survival (PFS) of 11 months. The treatment outcomes of this patient indicate that chemotherapy combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy may confer therapeutic advantages to these patients.

Case presentation

In January 2024, a 60-year-old male presented to the general surgery clinic with a 10-day history of abdominal pain, distension, and nausea. The patient smoked for over 50 years and has ceased smoking for a year. There was no relevant family history of cancer or chronic disease.

Physical examination revealed abdominal distension and tenderness in the right upper quadrant. Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a 27 × 17 mm soft-tissue-density nodule at the gallbladder neck, demonstrating avid homogeneous enhancement. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes were observed in the hepatic hilum, para-aortic region, hepatogastric ligament, and retroperitoneum. These nodes exhibited partial confluence and heterogeneous enhancement (Fig. 1A-B). The portal vein, splenic vein, and inferior vena cava were focally compressed and narrowed, and the main portal vein caliber was increased to 21 mm (normal 6–10 mm). These findings suggested the possibility of a malignant tumor with extensive lymph-node metastases. Given the gallbladder location of the tumor, the patient underwent serum gastrointestinal tumor marker profiling. The results showed that all markers were within the normal range, including ferritin, carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 72–4, carbohydrate antigen 50, carbohydrate antigen 19–9, carbohydrate antigen 15–3, cancer antigen 125, and alpha-fetal protein, which are insufficient to support a diagnosis of relevant diseases. Histopathological examination revealed aggregates of atypical and round cells (Fig. 2A). Based on the histopathological finding, lymphoma and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine neoplasm are in the realm of possible diagnoses. To establish the definitive histotype, a comprehensive immunohistochemical panel was performed. The Ki67 proliferation index was approximately 90%, indicating a highly proliferative tumor (Fig. 2B). The absence of CDX2, INSM1, and CK expression excluded gallbladder adenocarcinoma or neuroendocrine neoplasia (Supplementary Fig. 1A-C). Furthermore, lymphoma's markers were negative, including BCL2, BCL6, CD20, CD21, CD3, and MUM1. Given the round-cell morphology and high proliferative index, a second-line immunohistochemical panel was undertaken to distinguish among round-cell sarcomas. Tumors that manifest as round cells include Ewing sarcoma (ES), rhabdomyosarcoma, melanoma, clear cell sarcoma, and peripheral nerve sheath tumors. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed diffuse membranous positivity for CD99 and nuclear expression of both NKX2.2 and FLI1, markers pathognomonic for ES or Ewing-like sarcoma (ELS) (Fig. 2C-E). Additionally, the markers of other round-cell sarcomas are negative, including WT1, DES, S100, and Cam5.2 (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. 1D-F). These findings collectively support the diagnosis of ES or ELS. Since over 99% of ES have a fusion of the EWSR1 gene with other genes[4], fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed. The result showed an intact EWSR1 locus (22q12), thereby excluding EWSR1-rearranged ES. To clarify the subtype of ELS, next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis was performed (Supplementary Table 1). The sequencing results exhibited the absence of BCOR and CIC gene fusions. Therefore, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with URCS on January 24, 2024. Based on CT findings, the patient presented with extensive lymph node metastasis without distant metastasis. Due to the patient’s age and extensive lymph node metastasis, surgery was considered to carry a high risk and was unable to achieve complete tumor resection. After consulting the multidisciplinary team and obtaining the patient's informed consent, a sequential chemoradiotherapy treatment strategy was determined. The patient received five cycles of a three-weekly regimen of combination therapy with gemcitabine (900 mg/m²), paclitaxel (80 mg/m²), bevacizumab (15 mg/kg), and pembrolizumab (200 mg) from January 24 to April 17, 2024. A follow-up abdominal CT showed a partial remission (PR) of the gallbladder nodule and reduced lymphadenopathy (April 22, 2024, Fig. 3A-B). The patient received intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) to the primary tumor and adjacent metastatic lymph nodes with a conventional fractionated dose of 1.8 Gy at one fraction per day, five days per week, for a total dose of 45 Gy from May 6 to June 7, 2024 (45 Gy/25 fractions). The organs-at-risk (OAR) dose constraints included: liver (mean dose < 20 Gy), spinal cord (maximum dose < 40 Gy), the right kidney (mean dose < 8 Gy), the left kidney (mean dose < 10 Gy), stomach (maximum dose < 50 Gy), duodenum (maximum dose < 50 Gy), and bowel (maximum dose < 45 Gy). Approximately two weeks after radiotherapy, the patient developed decreased appetite and mild diarrhea, which were successfully managed through collaborative supportive care between the nutrition department and oncology department. No other acute or late toxicities have been observed to date. To further reduce lymph node lesions and preserve therapeutic benefits, the patient received two cycles of pembrolizumab (200 mg) and bevacizumab (15 mg/kg) as maintenance therapy from July 8 to July 29, 2024. A follow-up PET-CT (August 7, 2024) showed a PET-negative complete remission of the gallbladder lesion. However, lymph nodes in the hepatic hilum, hepatogastric ligament, and para-aortic region exhibited focal hypermetabolism (SUVmax: 2.6), exceeding the upper limit of benign lesions (SUVmax ≤ 2.0, Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on the patient’s prior CT findings and the focal hypermetabolism of the lymph nodes on PET-CT, these hypermetabolic nodes are more likely to represent residual tumor rather than post-treatment changes. Maintenance therapy continued every three weeks for a total of three cycles from August 19 to September 30, 2024. Abdominal CT (October 16, 2024) showed further reduction of retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy (Fig. 3E). The treatment was continued.

On December 31, 2024, a contrast-enhanced abdominal CT revealed a new low-density, weakly enhancing lesion in the head of the pancreas (Fig. 3F), suggesting a metastatic lesion. The PFS was 11 months for the treatment of chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy and targeted therapy, followed by radiotherapy. The patient received oral anlotinib (12 mg daily) as the second-line agent on January 9, 2025. The patient then returned home to continue anlotinib therapy and underwent follow-up imaging in a local hospital (March 21 and June 27, 2025). The scan reports in another hospital demonstrated lymph-node and lesion in the head of the pancreas were similar to the baseline, corresponding to stable disease (SD). However, due to incompatibility in imaging data formats between institutions, we were unable to obtain the patient's most recent two CT images for direct comparison. As of the last documented imaging assessment, the PFS for anlotinib therapy was seven months. Discussion

URCS is a rare subtype of ES, with a low incidence rate and presenting significant diagnostic difficulties. To date, only 16 cases of URCS have been reported in the English literature (Table 1, Table 2). There is no definitive treatment guideline for URCS. Clinically, management of URCS is typically based on the protocol established for ES, primarily involving a multimodal approach combining surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy[5,6]. For early-stage disease, patients (n = 4) who received surgery alone have a mean PFS of 51 months, indicating that surgery confers favorable outcomes in early-stage URCS patients. For locally advanced patients, combined surgery and chemotherapy were employed. The surgery plus adjuvant chemotherapy group (n = 2, VDC/IE or VIDE protocol) demonstrated a mean PFS of 2.5 months, while the neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus surgery group (n = 2, VDC/IE protocol, with or without adjuvant chemotherapy) achieved a substantially prolonged mean PFS of 15.5 months[[7], [8], [9], [10]]. For one advanced patient with extensive adjacent tissue invasion, chemotherapy (VDC/IE protocol) did not improve the prognosis, with both the PFS and OS of three months[11]. These results suggest that for locally advanced patients, neoadjuvant chemotherapy might confer a superior prognosis. In one case, the patient (T1N1M0) who received surgery plus postoperative radiotherapy (60 Gy/two fractions, SBRT) achieved a PFS of 9 months[12]. Other groups also attempted multi-modality regimens. A combination of chemotherapy (VDC/IE protocol) and bevacizumab in the patient with multiple metastatic tumors in both lungs and subpleural areas achieved a PFS of 9 months. Camrelizumab combined with anlotinib/surufatin in a patient (T4N1M1) with bone metastases resulted in a mean PFS of 13.5 months[13]. The tri-modality regimen in a patient with advanced, unresectable URCS (T4N1M0) achieved a PFS of 14 months[14]. Chemotherapy (VAC protocol) combined with pembrolizumab significantly reduced tumor burden, achieving a PR. Subsequent radiotherapy (66 Gy/33 fractions, IMRT) and maintenance therapy (chemotherapy plus immunotherapy) further consolidated treatment efficacy. In the present case, we employed a tri-modality regimen of sequential chemoradiotherapy combined with immunotherapy and targeted therapy in the patient (T2N1M0) with extensive lymph node metastasis based on the NGS result. We also attempted a novel chemotherapeutic protocol with paclitaxel and gemcitabine and reported a PFS and OS at 11 months and 25 months, respectively. In this case, NGS analysis revealed gene amplification of the VEGFR gene with a copy number of 4.0. VEGFR is the receptor of VEGF, a critical regulator of angiogenesis. VEGF/VEGFR can activate various signaling pathways, including the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/alpha serine/threonine-protein kinase (Akt)/ endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) pathway, the phospholipase C γ (PLCγ)/inositol trisphosphate (IP3)/diacylglycerol (DAG) pathway, the rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (Raf)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway, the focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, and the Ras homologous (Rho) pathways, and further promotes the remodeling of the microvascular system and facilitates the growth and metastasis of many cancers[15]. Bevacizumab blocks angiogenesis by blocking the binding of VEGF to VEGFR[16]. The BERNIE study reported that the bevacizumab plus chemotherapy prolonged the median event-free-survival (EFS) in patients with metastatic rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) or non-rhabdomyosarcoma soft tissue sarcoma (NRSTS)[17]. These studies indicate that the VEGF inhibitor bevacizumab may be beneficial as a treatment option for sarcoma.

NGS analysis also showed pathogenic mutations in CHEK1 (deletion), ERCC3 (c.302T>C, p.V101A), and TP53 (c.742C>T, p.V101A), concurrent with CD274 (gene of PD-L1) deletion, microsatellite stable (MSS), and low tumor mutational burden (TMB-L, 3.84 Muts/Mb). A case of URCS with TMB-high, microsatellite instability-high, and positive PD-L1 (100%) demonstrated clinical benefit from immunotherapy[14]. In another case of URCS with negative PD-L1, the patient received combined treatment with anlotinib/surufatin and camrelizumab (anti-PD-1 antibody) as second-line and fourth-line therapy, achieving PFS of 1 month and 26 months (mean PFS: 13.5 months)[13]. These cases imply that immunotherapy might benefit patients independent of PD-L1 expression. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests a favorable response in patients with CHECK1, ERCC3, and TP53 alterations[[18], [19], [20]]. CHEK1 plays a critical role in cell cycle progression triggered by DNA damage[21]. Loss of CHEK1 results in the failure of the DNA damage checkpoint, causing nuclear DNA leakage into the cytoplasm[22]. In the soft tissue sarcomas, tumors with low CHEK1 expression exhibit a higher density of infiltrating CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and regulatory T cells[21]. ERCC3 is a crucial member of the nucleotide excision repair (NER) system, facilitating the repair of damaged DNA[18]. The c.302T>C mutation in the ERCC3 gene impairs its ability to recognize and bind to DNA damage, thereby increasing genome instability. TP53 is a well-known tumor suppressor. The c.742C>T mutation directly affects its DNA-binding capacity to target genes, representing a loss-of-function mutation[23]. Tumor cells with TP53 mutations exhibit characteristics of genomic instability and defective DNA repair[23]. All CEHK1, ERCC3, and P53 are primarily involved in DNA damage repair. Mutations in these genes cause a sharp increase in genome instability and elevated TMB. High TMB enables tumor cells to generate a large number of neoantigens, which activate the cGAS-STING pathway and induce an IFN-I response[24]. This promotes dendritic cell maturation and T cell cross-presentation, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor immune response[24]. These suggest a potential immunotherapy response in the current case.

In this case, combination treatment with gemcitabine, paclitaxel, bevacizumab, and pembrolizumab led to PR of the gallbladder nodule and a reduction in lymphadenopathy after five cycles of treatment. The following radiotherapy further caused the complete remission of the gallbladder nodule, and the PFS of this patient was 11 months. Accumulating studies indicate that radiotherapy potentially improves immunotherapy response by inducing cancer cell autophagy, apoptosis, and necrosis, thereby promoting the release of tumor-specific antigens[25]. Radiotherapy-induced DNA damage in cancer cells also activates the cGAS-STING pathway, upregulating type I interferon expression and boosting T-cell anticancer activity[26]. Besides, chemotherapy shows potential for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy by increasing tumor antigen release and reducing immunosuppressive cells[27]. Therefore, in this case, radiotherapy and chemotherapy might both exert a synergistic effect on the anti-PD-1 antibody.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of gallbladder URCS and summarize its treatment and prognosis by reviewing reported cases. The patient in this case was administered a pioneering treatment regimen, which comprised chemotherapy in combination with anti-angiogenesis targeted therapy (bevacizumab) and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy (pembrolizumab), followed by radiotherapy. This novel regimen enabled the patient to achieve a PFS of 11 months. This study has several limitations. First, as a single-case report, its generalizability is constrained, making it difficult to represent treatment responses and prognostic characteristics in the broader population. The patient in this case received sequential multimodal therapy, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and radiotherapy, which precludes definitive determination of the independent contribution of each treatment modality to the clinical outcome. Furthermore, assessments of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), immune microenvironment profiling, and potential molecular predictors were unavailable, thereby compromising the precision of efficacy prediction and mechanistic interpretation for the therapeutic strategies.
