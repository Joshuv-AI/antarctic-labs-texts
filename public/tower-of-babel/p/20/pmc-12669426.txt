The Effect of Restorative Emergence Angle on Soft and Hard Tissue Around Splinted Implants: A Preclinical Study
Authors: Seung‐Ju Lee, Yuseung Yi, Frank Schwarz, Jungwon Lee, Ki‐Tae Koo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the impact of the restorative emergence angle and splinting configurations on peri‐implant soft and hard tissues.Materials and MethodsThirty implants were placed in the mandibular premolars (P2, P3, P4) of five beagle dogs. In a split‐mouth design, each animal received both narrow (NE) (emergence angle = 30°) and wide (WE) (emergence angle = 60°) abutments on each side of the mandible. The implants were subsequently splinted. Radiographic images were captured at 0, 4, 12, and 24 weeks post‐restoration. Biopsy samples were then collected for histomorphometric analysis and circularly polarized light examination. All analyses were performed based on the splinted positions: either the mesial or distal end of the terminal implant, the splinted zone of the terminal implant, or the splinted zone of the middle implant.ResultsRadiographic evaluation showed more pronounced bone remodeling in the WE group. In histomorphometric analysis, both the vertical distance from the implant shoulder to the alveolar bone crest and the infiltrated connective tissue areas were significantly larger in the WE group. Conversely, the NE group exhibited a longer connective tissue attachment. Analysis using circularly polarized light indicated a significantly reduced area fraction of collagen fibers in the peri‐implant epithelium of the WE group, as well as in the oral epithelium of the splinted zone.ConclusionA wide emergence angle of implant prostheses can compromise connective tissue attachment, hinder the formation of an adequate soft tissue seal, and potentially lead to marked bone remodeling.Keywords: dental implant, emergence angle, histology, polarization, splinted

Introduction

The long‐term success of dental implant treatments is influenced by various factors, with early peri‐implant marginal bone loss being a crucial consideration (Galindo‐Moreno et al. 2022). Early bone loss is considered as a process of bone remodeling to establish a stable soft tissue barrier (biologic width) around implants (Berglundh and Lindhe 1996). Bone remodeling primarily occurs within the first year after loading, and it typically ranges from 0.6 to 1.1 mm (Oh et al. 2002). However, according to Galindo‐Moreno et al. (2022), bone loss > 0.5 mm after 6 months of loading is associated with future bone loss. In other words, early bone loss plays a significant role in the long‐term success of implants.

Recent studies have focused on the impact of implant prosthesis characteristics, particularly the emergence profile and emergence angle, on marginal bone loss (Katafuchi et al. 2018; Yi et al. 2020). These prosthetic features have garnered significant attention in the field of implantology due to their potential to affect both soft tissue health and bone stability. Understanding the relationship between prosthetic features and marginal bone loss is essential for improving peri‐implant health and its long‐term outcomes (Mattheos et al. 2021).

The emergence angle is the angle formed between the implant platform and the margin of the prosthesis. Clinically, this angle significantly influences the response of the soft tissue and the stability of the marginal bone (Yi et al. 2020). Recent research indicates that optimal emergence angles can enhance soft tissue attachment, aid in plaque control, and thereby reduce the risk of peri‐implantitis (Yi et al. 2020). These insights have prompted a shift in prosthetic design, highlighting the need for biocompatible and tissue‐friendly contours.

The correlation between emergence angles and peri‐implantitis continues to be a contentious topic. Some studies indicate a higher incidence of peri‐implantitis with emergence angles over 30° (Katafuchi et al. 2018; Yi et al. 2020), while others propose a threshold of 40° (Strauss et al. 2022). This variation may stem from several confounding factors that influence peri‐implant bone loss, including the positional relationship of implant prostheses when they are splinted (Yi et al. 2020). Splinted prostheses are believed to distribute the load on implants, reducing stress on the surrounding bone (Bergkvist et al. 2008). This contributes to a lower risk of implant failure (de Souza Batista et al. 2019). Despite these advantages, a particular study showed that, in cases where three implants are placed and splinted together, the middle implant tends to have a higher rate of peri‐implantitis, possibly due to reduced accessibility for maintaining oral hygiene (Yi et al. 2020). However, there is still a lack of biological evidence to support this hypothesis.

The inconsistency in findings regarding emergence angles and peri‐implantitis highlights the complexity of implant‐related complications. Further research is needed to clarify the specific relationship between emergence angles, splinting configurations, and peri‐implant health, and to establish a solid biological foundation for the observed phenomena. So, the objective of this study was to explore how the emergence angle of the implant prosthesis affects the peri‐implant soft and hard tissues. Additionally, the impact of the splinting position of implants will be assessed. The null hypothesis was that the emergence angle of the implant prosthesis would not significantly affect the peri‐implant bone remodeling.

Materials and Methods

This preclinical split‐mouth study was conducted at Seoul National University in the Republic of Korea. The study received approval from the Seoul National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (SNU‐210308‐3) and was conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0 (Percie du Sert et al. 2020).

Animals

Five beagle dogs, aged 1–2 years and weighing 10–12 kg, were utilized in this study. All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia, which was induced using 0.1 mg/kg of tiletamine/zolazepam (Zoletil, Virbac, Carros, France), 2.3 mg/kg of xylazine (Rompun, Bayer Korea, Ansan, Korea), and 0.05 mg/kg of atropine sulfate (Jeil, Daegu, Korea). The housing conditions were carefully controlled, maintaining a room temperature of 23°C ± 2°C, relative humidity of 50% ± 10%, a 12‐h light/dark cycle, and 12–18 air changes per hour. The dogs were fed a nutritionally balanced diet (HappyRang, Seoulfeed Company, Gyeonggi‐do, Korea), and a soft diet was provided post‐operation. Mechanical plaque control was carried out biweekly using a toothbrush and ultrasonic scaler until the animals were euthanized.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of the distance between the implant shoulder and the bone crest observed in histological images. A mean difference of 1.5 mm and a standard deviation of 0.5 mm were assumed, drawing on data from a previous study (de Souza Batista et al. 2019). The Type I error rate was set at 0.05, and the statistical power was set at 80%. Consequently, the required sample size was determined to be 5 animals per group. The calculations were performed using G*Power software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany).

Surgical Interventions

The timeline for the surgical procedure is presented in Figure 1. Prior to the study, scaling was conducted to maintain oral hygiene. One week following scaling, bilateral mandibular premolars (P2, P3, P4) were extracted under general anesthesia (Figure 2a,b). The premolars were hemisected using a diamond bur and then removed with elevators and forceps. Post‐extraction, the sockets were meticulously curetted and sutured using 5/0 Monosyn (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). The sutures were removed 1 week later.

Radiographic imaging was performed at 0, 1, 3, and 6 months after prosthesis insertion. Subsequently, the animals were sacrificed, and tissue block samples were harvested.

Representative images of the surgical procedure. (a) Pre‐extraction state. (b) Following the extraction of the mandibular second, third, and fourth premolars, crisscross sutures were performed. (c) 2 months after extraction, three implants were placed at the equicrestal level. (d) After healing abutments were connected, simple interrupted sutures were performed. After a two‐month healing period, implants were placed under general anesthesia (Figure 2c,d). A mid‐crestal incision was made, followed by full‐thickness flap elevation, and then implant osteotomy was carried out. Three implants were positioned at the equicrestal level on each side of the mandibular edentulous area. In total, 30 implants were used in this study, with six implants placed in each beagle dog. The implants, measuring 3.1 mm in diameter and 7 mm in height (NR line, Dentium, Suwon, Korea), were installed, maintaining a minimum inter‐implant distance of 3 mm. Healing abutments (diameter: 2.7 mm, height: 3.5 mm, Dentium, Suwon, Korea) were attached to facilitate non‐submerged healing. Simple interrupted sutures using 5/0 Monosyn were applied and subsequently removed 1 week post‐surgery.

Two months after implant placement, intraoral scanning for custom abutment fabrication was conducted using scan bodies (IOS healing abutment, Dentium, Suwon, Korea). Three months post‐implant placement, the custom abutments and implant prostheses were connected (Figure 2e). The custom abutments, milled from cylindrical titanium grade V abutments, were designed with two different emergence angles: narrow (30°) and wide (60°). These abutments were randomly assigned to each side of the mandible using a simple randomization method generated by software (MS Excel, Microsoft, USA). In each beagle dog, the narrow emergence angle (NE) abutments were inserted into one hemi‐mandible, while the wide emergence angle (WE) abutments were placed in the opposite hemi‐mandible. The abutments were tightened to 30 Ncm, and three splinted resin crowns were cemented using glass ionomer luting cement (FujiCEM 2, GC America, Alsip, IL, USA). Subsequently, occlusal adjustments were made using a high‐speed diamond bur with copious irrigation to avoid occlusal contact.

After 6 months of function, the dogs were euthanized by injecting potassium chloride (Jeil, Daegu, Korea) into the carotid artery. Tissue samples, including the implants and the surrounding soft and hard tissues, were harvested and fixed in a 10% formaldehyde solution (Sigma Aldrich Co. LLC, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 1 week.

Histological Preparation

Fixed tissue block samples were dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol concentrations and infiltrated with Technovit 7200 Resin (Heraeus KULZER, Hanau, Germany). The resin blocks were then bisected using an EXAKT Diamond cutter (KULZER EXAKT 300, Heraeus KULZER, Hanau, Germany) to achieve a cross‐section along the mesiodistal plane. The initial section thickness was set at 300 ± 50 μm and was subsequently polished down to 40 ± 5 μm using an EXAKT grinding machine (KULZER EXAKT 400CS, Heraeus KULZER, Hanau, Germany). Tissue slides were prepared and stained with H&E and Picrosirius red. The histological sections were digitized into image files using a digital slide scanner (PANNORAMIC 250 Flash III, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Hungary).

Analysis

Radiographic, histomorphometric, and circularly polarized light microscopy analyses were conducted by a single calibrated examiner (S.J.L.). To discern the differences attributable to the splinted position of the three implants, the analysis was segmented into three areas: the mesial or distal end of the terminal implant (T‐end), the splinted zone of the terminal implant (T‐splint), and the splinted zone of the middle implant (M‐splint) (Figure 3).

Splinting configuration. To examine the variations associated with the position of splinted implants, the implant areas were divided into three sections: the mesial or distal end of the terminal implant (T‐end), the splinted zone of the terminal implant (T‐splint), and the splinted zone of the middle implant (M‐splint).

Radiographic Analysis

Radiographs were taken immediately after the implant prosthesis was connected (baseline) and subsequently at 4, 12, and 24 weeks. The radiographic analysis utilized ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to measure crestal bone remodeling. It was defined as the distance from the implant shoulder to the most coronal point of bone‐to‐implant contact, observed on both the mesial and distal sides of the implant (Figure 4a). To correct for errors, crestal bone remodeling was expressed as a percentage of the actual length of the implant fixture. Crestal bone remodeling at each time point was adjusted by calculating the difference from the baseline level. Additionally, bone remodeling levels were analyzed by categorizing them into high (> 0.5 mm) and low (≤ 0.5 mm) at 24 weeks after implant prosthesis placement.

Histomorphometric Analysis

Vertical measurements were performed on undecalcified specimens stained with H&E using CaseViewer software (CaseViewer 2.2; 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) (Figure 5). According to the previous research (Valles et al. 2018), five anatomical landmarks were identified: the margin of peri‐implant mucosa (PM), the apical extension of the junctional epithelium (aJE), the implant shoulder (IS), the bone crest (BC), and the first bone‐to‐implant contact (fBIC). Measurements were recorded for six distances: IS to fBIC, IS to BC, PM to aJE, aJE to fBIC, PM to fBIC, and IS to PM. Additionally, the area of the infiltrated connective tissue (ICT) was quantified.

Representative images of histomorphometric analysis. The left image represents the narrow emergence angle group, and the right image represents the wide emergence angle group. aJE, apical extension of junctional epithelium; BC, bone crest; fBIC, first bone‐to‐implant contact; ICT, infiltrated connective tissue; IS, implant shoulder; PM, peri‐implant mucosa.

Circularly Polarized Light Microscopy Analysis

Undecalcified specimens stained with Picrosirius red were examined using a circularly polarized light microscope (OS‐EX30POL, Osun Hitech, Gyeonggi‐do, Korea). Images were captured at ×40 magnification with a microscope camera (JENOPTIK GRYPHAX series, JENOPTIK, Jena, Germany) (Figure 6a). The images were analyzed by dividing them into five regions: oral epithelium (OE), oral connective tissue (OCT), peri‐implant sulcular epithelium (PISE), peri‐implant epithelium (PIE), and peri‐implant infiltrated connective tissue (PICT) (Ikeda et al. 2000). For the quantitative analysis of collagen fibers, hue definition was employed to identify the collagen fibers (2–256) (Ko et al. 2024, 2023; Rich and Whittaker 2017). The area occupied by collagen fibers was measured and calculated as a proportion of the total area.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical unit was the animal specimen (n = 5). Due to the small sample size, nonparametric tests were conducted. The Friedman test was used to assess differences in implant positions (T‐end, T‐splint, and M‐splint) within each group (NE and WE), followed by Dunn's multiple comparison test for post hoc analysis. Additionally, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was utilized to evaluate differences between the two angles (NE and WE) at each position. To examine differences in bone remodeling levels (high vs. low) among the six experimental groups, Cochran's Q test was used, accounting for intra‐subject correlations inherent to the split‐mouth design. Upon finding a significant overall difference, pairwise comparisons were conducted using McNemar's test with Bonferroni correction. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29.0.1.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Radiographic Outcomes: Crestal Bone Remodeling

Representative images and radiographic analysis are presented in Figure 4.

At 4 weeks, a significant difference in crestal bone remodeling was observed only at the M‐splint, with the NE group showing 0.25 ± 0.26 mm and the WE group showing 0.51 ± 0.26 mm. However, by 24 weeks, the WE group exhibited greater bone remodeling at all sites compared to the NE group: 0.90 ± 0.70 mm at the T‐end, 1.4 ± 0.88 mm at the T‐splint, and 1.03 ± 0.28 mm at the M‐splint, versus 0.12 ± 0.35 mm at the T‐end, 0.57 ± 0.37 mm at the T‐splint, and 0.61 ± 0.48 mm at the M‐splint in the NE group.

In the NE group at 24 weeks, the M‐splint exhibited significantly greater bone remodeling (0.61 ± 0.48 mm) compared to the T‐end (0.12 ± 0.35 mm). Additionally, in the WE group, there were significant differences in bone remodeling between the T‐end and the T‐splint at 4 weeks (0.25 ± 0.33 mm at the T‐end vs. 0.55 ± 0.52 mm at the T‐splint), 12 weeks (0.48 ± 0.43 mm at the T‐end vs. 1.09 ± 0.70 mm at the T‐splint), and 24 weeks (0.90 ± 0.70 mm at the T‐end vs. 1.4 ± 0.88 mm at the T‐splint).

The distribution of bone remodeling levels (high vs. low) among the six experimental groups at 24 weeks showed a significant difference (Q = 14.487, p = 0.013). However, post hoc pairwise comparisons using McNemar's test with Bonferroni correction revealed no statistically significant differences between any two groups. Although no statistically significant differences were found in the post hoc comparisons, a pattern was observed in which the WE group showed a greater frequency of high bone remodeling compared to the NE group (Figure 7). Notably, within the WE group, the splinted configuration (T‐splint and M‐splint) exhibited the highest occurrence of high bone remodeling (Figure 7).

Histological Outcomes

Representative histological images are presented in Figure 8.

Representative histological images of the narrow (a) and wide (b) emergence angle groups. (a) In the narrow emergence angle group, a thin junctional epithelium and firm connective tissue attachment were observed in close proximity to the implant. (b) In the wide emergence angle group, plaque accumulation was observed between the supracrestal tissue and the implant, the junctional epithelium extended down to the fixture threads, and extensive infiltrated connective tissue was present.

In the NE group, the peri‐implant soft tissue, which includes the junctional epithelium and connective tissue, was in close contact with the implant abutment, shoulder, and thread. Conversely, in the WE group, the junctional epithelium did not establish proper contact with the implant, with only some of the underlying connective tissue making contact with the implant fixture. Residual plaque was present in the gap between the implant and the surrounding tissue, accompanied by widespread infiltration of inflammatory cells in the adjacent area. Additionally, a thin layer of junctional epithelium was observed in the NE group, while a thickening of the junctional epithelium was noted in the WE group.

When comparing the NE group and the WE group, vertical distance from the IS to the BC was greater in the WE group (0.95 ± 1.04 mm at the T‐end, 1.43 ± 0.82 mm at the T‐splint, 1.33 ± 0.74 mm at the M‐splint) than in the NE group (0.50 ± 1.20 mm at the T‐end, 1.01 ± 1.12 mm at the T‐splint, 0.74 ± 1.10 mm at the M‐splint) at all locations. The ICT area was also significantly larger in the WE group at the T‐splint (1.34 ± 0.69 mm2 in the NE group vs. 2.76 ± 0.73 mm2 in the WE group) and the M‐splint (1.56 ± 0.89 mm2 in the NE group vs. 3.79 ± 2.20 mm2 in the WE group). However, the connective tissue attachment (aJE–fBIC) was significantly longer in the NE group at the T‐end (1.10 ± 0.32 mm in the NE group vs. 0.55 ± 0.21 mm in the WE group) and the T‐splint (1.23 ± 0.36 mm in the NE group vs. 0.83 ± 0.34 mm in the WE group).

Regarding the position of the splint, the NE group exhibited significantly greater distance from the IS to the fBIC at the T‐splint (1.29 ± 0.94 mm) compared to the T‐end (0.80 ± 1.00 mm).

Circularly Polarized Light Outcomes: Quantitative Analysis of Collagen Fiber

The circularly polarized light analysis is illustrated in Figure 6.

When comparing the total amount of collagen fibers, the WE group exhibited lower percentages (34.7% ± 15.12% at the T‐end, 30.23% ± 14.74% at the T‐splint, 29.17% ± 16.83% at the M‐splint) than the NE group (48.09% ± 21.73% at the T‐end, 38.51% ± 15.62% at the T‐splint, 42.66% ± 11.72% at the M‐splint) in the PIE region. This difference was only statistically significant at the M‐splint.

In splinted positions, the difference was only significant in the OE region. The NE group showed significantly lower values at the M‐splint (63.98% ± 21.4%) compared to the T‐end (83.45% ± 8.12%). Meanwhile, in the WE group, a significant difference was noted, with the following order: T‐end (85.46% ± 7.02%) > T‐splint (54.13% ± 27.75%) > M‐splint (33.77% ± 20.45%).

Discussion

In the present study, the effects of the emergence angle on peri‐implant tissues after implant restoration were investigated. The results indicated that a wide emergence angle promotes peri‐implant bone remodeling. This conclusion is supported by histological analysis, which revealed that prostheses with a narrow emergence angle establish a more stable soft tissue seal. Such stability of soft tissue seal helps prevent the spread of infection to the peri‐implant connective tissue.

There are ongoing debates about the impact of the emergence angle on marginal bone loss. Several cross‐sectional studies have indicated that an emergence angle greater than 30° in bone‐level implants is associated with increased marginal bone loss (Katafuchi et al. 2018; Yi et al. 2020). Conversely, another retrospective study has argued that the interproximal contour of the implant prosthesis does not influence marginal bone loss (Volp Junior et al. 2024). Despite numerous clinical studies, there has been limited effort to corroborate these findings with histological evidence through animal research. Among the existing animal studies (Finelle et al. 2015; Souza et al. 2018), most have focused solely on implant abutment configuration, with very few attempting to mimic actual clinical conditions by incorporating prostheses. Consequently, this study is the first animal research to explore the effects of the implant restorative emergence angle on peri‐implant tissues.

In radiographic and histomorphometric analyses, implants featuring a wide emergence angle demonstrated more significant bone remodeling compared to those with a narrow emergence angle. This observation aligns with findings from a prior animal study that performed histological and radiographic analyses on healing abutments with 15° and 45° angulations (Souza et al. 2018). The study indicated that the group with a 45° emergence angle experienced increased bone remodeling, attributed to the apical shift of the peri‐implant biologic width.

Radiographic analysis of bone remodeling levels revealed that, although the difference between groups was not statistically significant, the WE group tended to be more frequently classified into the high bone remodeling category. This suggests a potential influence of the emergence angle on bone remodeling. Especially, it is noteworthy that all splinted zones (T‐splint and M‐splint) in the WE group were categorized as exhibiting high levels of bone remodeling. It aligns with the previous study indicating that, in cases where an implant is splinted to adjacent implants on both the mesial and distal sides, a wide emergence angle is associated with greater bone loss around the middle implant (Yi et al. 2020).

The peri‐implant soft tissue seal, also known as biologic width, is comprised of the epithelial and connective tissue attachment, and is essential for maintaining implant health. It forms a physical barrier between the external oral environment and the underlying bone (Ivanovski and Lee 2018). Inflammation around the implant can disrupt the proper attachment of the junctional epithelium and connective tissue, potentially leading to the down‐growth of the junctional epithelium (Atsuta et al. 2012; Nevins et al. 2008). Consequently, the length of the junctional epithelium around the implant increases, while the connective tissue attachment length decreases (Comut et al. 2001). According to the 2017 World Workshop, the epithelial attachment length was approximately 2 mm, and the connective tissue adhesion was 1–2 mm in an animal study (Araujo and Lindhe 2018). In this study, the NE group exhibited the epithelial attachment length of approximately 2.1 mm (2.14 ± 0.83 mm at the T‐end, 2.10 ± 0.66 mm at the T‐splint, 2.00 ± 0.71 mm at the M‐splint) and a connective tissue attachment of about 1.1 mm (1.10 ± 0.32 mm at the T‐end, 1.23 ± 0.36 mm at the T‐splint, 1.11 ± 0.37 mm at the M‐splint), indicating the formation of an appropriate biologic width. However, in the WE group, the epithelial attachment length slightly increased to approximately 2.7 mm (2.72 ± 0.95 mm at the T‐end, 2.60 ± 1.42 mm at the T‐splint, 2.79 ± 1.11 mm at the M‐splint), while the connective tissue attachment decreased to 0.7 mm (0.55 ± 0.21 mm at the T‐end, 0.83 ± 0.34 mm at the T‐splint, 0.84 ± 0.21 mm at the M‐splint), failing to form an appropriate biologic width. Additionally, circularly polarized microscopy analysis showed that the area fraction of collagen fibers in the PIE region was lower in the WE group, particularly at the M‐splint. This finding aligns with a previous study suggesting that a wide restorative angle compromises the integrity of the junctional epithelium (Strauss et al. 2024). Consequently, inadequate formation of biologic width and junctional epithelium may have facilitated bacterial infection in the peri‐implant tissues, leading to peri‐implant bone remodeling.

The impact of splinting adjacent implants on marginal bone loss has been a subject of debate. Some meta‐analyses (Al Amri and Kellesarian 2017; de Souza Batista et al. 2019) and a retrospective study (Mendonça et al. 2014) suggest that splinting does not significantly affect marginal bone loss. However, a 10‐year randomized controlled trial (Vigolo et al. 2015) and a retrospective study (Yi et al. 2020) reported significantly greater bone loss in splinted implants, particularly when both sides are splinted. In the current study, no distinct differences were observed in areas where implants were splinted, except for some values, including the amount of collagen fibers at the OE region and bone remodeling level. Yi et al. (2020) proposed that the reason for increased marginal bone loss in splinted implants was the limited accessibility for oral hygiene maintenance. Additionally, the contour of the prosthesis and the vertical position of the contact point are primary factors influencing accessibility (Dixon and London 2019). Despite the implants being splinted in this study, an adequate inter‐implant distance was maintained at the time of implant placement, allowing for the creation of a contact point in the incisal region. This suggests that splinting was unlikely to restrict oral hygiene access in this case. Meanwhile, a lower amount of collagen fibers was observed in the OE region at the M‐splint and T‐splint in the circularly polarized light analysis. This may be because these areas were classified as oral epithelium for analysis, but in reality, they were interdental gingiva. Unlike oral epithelium, interdental gingiva consists of parakeratinized or non‐keratinized epithelium rather than keratinized epithelium, making it more susceptible to bacterial invasion (Csiszar et al. 2007). This likely results in the presence of mild to moderate inflammation, which could explain the lower area fraction of collagen fibers in the interdental area.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the analysis was confined to the mesial and distal interproximal regions. These areas were chosen as the primary regions of interest because inflammation typically begins interproximally; however, future studies should also include the buccal and lingual/palatal regions. Second, the focus was solely on the prosthetic emergence angle. Other elements of the implant supracrestal complex, such as the emergence profile, should be explored in subsequent research. Third, a non‐splinting group was not included. The experimental design became complex due to variables such as emergence angle and splint configuration, which also reduced the sample size. In this study, the focus was on the phenomena occurring in splinted restorations, and thus a non‐splinting group was not included. Future research may require a comparison between splinting and non‐splinting groups. Fourth, clinical parameters were not measured. Probing depth, bleeding on probing, and plaque scores are important factors in assessing peri‐implant health. However, since these parameters were not measured in the present study, future research should include these factors for a more comprehensive evaluation. Lastly, the small sample size limited the ability to detect the effects of different splinting configurations. Since the sample size calculation was based on the distance between the implant shoulder and the bone crest concerning the emergence angle, the study could only demonstrate the trend for the splinting configuration. Future studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to assess these effects more effectively.

In conclusion, within the limitations of this preclinical study, the restorative emergence angle may influence crestal bone remodeling. Implant prostheses featuring a wide emergence angle could compromise the connective tissue attachment, adversely affecting the formation of a stable soft tissue seal and potentially promoting bone remodeling around the implant.
