A Neurological Outcome Score for Ventilated Pediatric Intensive Care Unit Patients Should Only Include Neurological, Objective Outcome Parameters

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Tomar et al. on a prospective observational study to validate the self-developed neurological outcome prediction score in ventilated children (NOPS-VC) in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU).1 Some points should be discussed.

The first point is that two of the eight parameters evaluated were not necessarily related to the neurological outcome.1 Cardiopulmonary resuscitation and oxygen supply are not necessarily related to the neurological outcome. If cardiopulmonary resuscitation is performed adequately, cerebral hypoxia is unlikely. Similarly, hypoxic brain damage can only occur if hypoxia persists for an unbearably long period of time. To assess neurological outcome, it is recommended to include outcome parameters that are actually related to neurological disorders. The definition of outcome parameters is crucial, as the outcome can depend heavily on the chosen outcome parameters. In addition to the pediatric cerebral performance (PCPC) score, Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), status epilepticus (SE), intracranial pressure (ICP), focal deficits, and central nervous system (CNS) infections, parameters such as electroencephalography, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination, or cerebral imaging should be included in the score. Also, critical neuropathy should be considered as an outcome predictor.

The second point is that CSF tests must be performed in order to assess whether or not a patient has a CNS infection. Brain imaging may be normal in meningitis and encephalitis, especially when performed without contrast.2 Were all included patients really subjected to lumbar puncture to determine whether or not there was a CNS infection?

The third point is that clinical assessment of ICP can be misleading. Cushing's triad may only be visible in the final stages of elevated ICP and not in the early stages.3 Neurogenic respiration may be misinterpreted, as it can also occur in lesions of the spinal cord or peripheral nerves that innervate the respiratory muscles. The decortication/decerebration position may not be visible, as ventilated patients are usually sedated and relaxed.

The fourth point is that not all included patients routinely underwent CNS imaging. In order to assess whether patients acquired a CNS disorder during their hospital stay, it is imperative to perform CNS imaging, particularly cerebral MRI. Since the neurological outcome can depend heavily on whether a patient has experienced a stroke, hemorrhage, hypoxia, vasospasm, atrophy, demyelination, abscess formation, meningitis, or encephalitis, it would be imperative to perform cerebral imaging on each of the included patients.4

The fifth point is that it is not justified to automatically assign PCPC >3 (poor neurological outcome) to those who have died.1 Since the 61 deceased patients may have died for reasons other than neurological causes, the neurological cause of death must be proven, and only then can a neurological PCPC >3 be assigned.

In summary, the validation of an outcome score should be performed by raters other than those who developed the score and in centers other than those where the score was developed. A score for assessing neurological outcome should only include neurological outcome parameters.
