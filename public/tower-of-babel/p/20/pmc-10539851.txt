Labial artery pseudoaneurysm following preterm vaginal delivery treated with ultrasound-guided thrombin injection: A case report
Authors: Simon Banh, Susan Keating
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case describes the diagnosis and management of a pseudoaneurysm of the right labial artery. A 25-year-old primigravida woman at 33 weeks and 4 days of gestation delivered a live infant vaginally. A large vulvovaginal haematoma was diagnosed and was managed in theatre with an incision and drainage. A recurrence of the haematoma was suspected postoperatively given ongoing pain. Doppler ultrasonography and computerised tomography angiography confirmed the diagnosis of a vulvovaginal haematoma with an associated pseudoaneurysm. The arterial pseudoaneurysm was successfully managed prior to rupture using ultrasound-guided direct thrombin injection to promote local thrombosis. There were no immediate complications following the procedure.Keywords: Pseudoaneurysm, Vulvovaginal haematoma, Thrombin injection

Introduction

Pseudoaneurysms are caused by damage to the arterial vessel wall which results in a locally contained haematoma [1]. Pseudoaneurysms of the femoral artery following endovascular access has an incidence of up to 4.8% [1]. In contrast, pseudoaneurysms of the labial artery are rare, with few reported cases in the literature.

Ruptured pseudoaneurysms can lead to life-threatening arterial bleeding and so early identification of pseudoaneurysms prior to rupture is imperative. Pseudoaneurysms can be diagnosed via ultrasonography, computerised tomography (CT) angiogram or conventional angiography [1]. The best mode of imaging is currently unknown for labial artery pseudoaneurysms.

Treatment of pseudoaneurysms includes observation, ultrasound-guided thrombin injections, angiographic embolisation and surgical repair, and these techniques are well studied for femoral and uterine artery pseudoaneurysms [1,2,3]. The preferred treatment method for labial artery pseudoaneurysms has not been established.

Case Presentation

A 25-year-old woman, gravida 2 para 0, presented with suspected rupture of membranes at 33 weeks and 4 days of gestation.

Her medical history consisted of asthma, haemorrhoids, depression, anxiety, and migraines. Her BMI was calculated at 32 kg/m2. Her past obstetric history included a medical termination of pregnancy.

On initial assessment she was confirmed to have preterm prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM). Initial management of PPROM included antibiotics and betamethasone administration. Two hours after admission, she began to labour and despite administration of tocolytic medication, progressed to full dilatation. A live 2700 g female infant was delivered vaginally. The delivery was complicated by a postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) of 1500 mL secondary to uterine atony. PPH management was immediately initiated with resultant cessation of vaginal bleeding.

Four hours following delivery, the patient reported increasing pain in her vulvovaginal region. A right 10x15cm vulvovaginal haematoma was diagnosed. The patient underwent an incision and drainage (I&D) of the vulvovaginal haematoma in the operating theatre and a 200 mL clot was removed. There was ongoing ooze from the lateral vaginal wall and so a suture was placed for haemostasis. A vaginal pack was inserted for 24 h to promote pressure haemostasis.

Day 2 after I&D, the patient reported increasing vaginal pain. Examination revealed right labial ecchymosis with an associated haematoma extending into the lateral vaginal wall measuring 10x5cm. There was also an associated drop in the level of haemoglobin, from 85 to 77 (g/L).

Day 4 after I&D, an ultrasound scan was performed due to ongoing pain. It revealed a 126 cc right vulvovaginal haematoma with an associated 19x7mm pseudoaneurysm of possibly the right posterior labial or perineal artery (Fig. 1). A CT angiogram was performed and confirmed the ultrasound findings of a 10x5x9cm right vulvovaginal haematoma with a 16x10mm pseudoaneurysm of the right posterior labial artery. There was no contrast extravasation to suggest active bleeding at the time of scanning.

Day 5 after I&D, the patient underwent Doppler ultrasound-guided percutaneous thrombin injection into the right labial artery pseudoaneurysm with a 23G spinal needle (Fig. 2). There was immediate thrombosis with no residual Doppler flow seen after the injection of 0.3 mL thrombin (Fig. 3). No immediate complications were seen.

After injection of thrombin into pseudoaneurysm. (Ultrasound, transcutaneous, Doppler flow).

The patient was discharged home on day 8 after I&D (day 2 after the thrombin injection) with clinic follow-up planned for six weeks post-delivery but was unfortunately lost to follow-up as she moved residence.

Discussion

There is only one other reported case of a labial artery pseudoaneurysm in the literature but two cases involving the internal pudendal artery and vaginal artery. These cases have all been described in term vaginal deliveries. The occurrence of pseudoaneurysms in this region is rare and so the aim of this case report is to document another occurrence of a labial artery pseudoaneurysm but in a preterm vaginal delivery. This case report also confirms the successful management of a labial artery pseudoaneurysm with targeted thrombin injection.

The best imaging modality for diagnosis has not been established. Diagnosis of pseudoaneurysms within the pelvis reported in the literature thus far involved a combination of selective angiogram, CT angiogram and Doppler ultrasonography. In the presented case, the right labial pseudoaneurysm was diagnosed through ultrasonography and confirmed with CT angiogram. Doppler ultrasonography is a useful minimally invasive radiation-free method of investigation and should be considered first to characterise the size, location and origin of the pseudoaneurysm. Doppler ultrasound is often the first-line investigation for other types of pseudoaneurysms in the uterine and femoral arteries [ 1,2,3].

Pseudoaneurysms can rupture and result in life-threatening arterial bleeding. The three cases of pseudoaneurysms involving the internal pudendal, vaginal and labial arteries presented in the literature all had a consistent clinical finding of increased vaginal bleeding after a vaginal laceration repair. This warranted further investigation and subsequently confirmed rupture of the pseudoaneurysms. All the patients required emergency treatment for their bleeding. Two cases utilised embolisation of the feeding artery to treat the ruptured pseudoaneurysm [4,5] and one case [6] used a combination of both embolisation and direct thrombin injection.

Thrombin, also known as factor IIa, converts fibrinogen to fibrin. Fibrin creates a meshwork which provides the base for platelet activation and aggregation which subsequently forms a thrombus [7]. This thrombus blocks off the pseudoaneurysm and reduces the risk of rupture. Targeted thrombin injection into pseudoaneurysms in the femoral artery after femoral catheterisation is well described in the literature. This same technique was applied for the treatment of the right labial pseudoaneurysm described in the case, given similar principles could be applied but in a different location.

The procedure was done in the operating theatre. Intravenous sedation was administered to the patient. 2% lignocaine was infiltrated subcutaneously into the right labium. A 23G spinal needle was introduced into the right labial pseudoaneurysm under direct ultrasound visualisation with aseptic technique. Doppler ultrasonography was used to show direction of flow within the pseudoaneurysm from the neck. 0.3 mL of thrombin (Tisseel) was injected through the spinal needle into the pseudoaneurysm to promote thrombosis. Doppler ultrasonography showed immediate cessation of flow into the pseudoaneurysm. There were no immediate post-procedure complications.

This case demonstrated early diagnosis of an unruptured labial artery pseudoaneurysm. Conservative management was not recommended given the high risk of rupture which would result in severe bleeding. (All three cases described in the literature resulted in rupture of the pseudoaneurysm and required emergency management.)

Direct thrombin injection to the pseudoaneurysm was the preferred treatment option as it is less invasive than embolisation. Surgical intervention of this pseudoaneurysm was discussed with the vascular surgeons but was not recommended as the culprit vessel would be very difficult to identify.

Timely management of this unruptured pseudoaneurysm prevented a potential severe haemorrhage, which would have been catastrophic for this patient as she was already anaemic from her postpartum haemorrhage.

Postpartum vaginal pain and vaginal bleeding should prompt examination and subsequent investigation for vulvovaginal haematomas with or without arterial pseudoaneurysms.
