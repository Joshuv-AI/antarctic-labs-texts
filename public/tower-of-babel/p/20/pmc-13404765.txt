Shared Decision‐Making During Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Therapy Selection: A Patient and Clinician Survey
Authors: Paresh C Giri, Jeanette Merrill‐Henry, Tiffany Rajah, Donald R Sullivan, Udochukwu Oyoyo, Katrina Barry, Jean M Elwing, Vijay P Balasubramanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSelection of therapy for pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) requires tradeoffs among disease severity, therapeutic benefit, adverse effects, treatment burden, quality of life, and patient values. Although professional societies endorse shared decision‐making (SDM) in this context, empirical data describing SDM in PAH are limited. We conducted a survey to characterize SDM during PAH treatment selection from both patient and clinician perspectives. Using the Ottawa Decision‐Making Framework, we administered parallel surveys to patients and clinicians at the Pulmonary Hypertension Association's 2022 International PH Conference (Atlanta, GA). Survey items assessed decisional characteristics including role preference, confidence, self‐efficacy, decisional support and the factors considered most important during PAH therapy selection. Surveys were administered electronically and analyses were performed using Python. A total of 89 participants (51 patients, 38 clinicians) completed the survey. Preference for SDM was nearly universal among patients (96%) and clinicians (95%). However, priorities differed significantly. Patients more frequently emphasized quality of life (81% vs. 54%) and efficacy of therapy (42% vs. 29%), whereas clinicians prioritized risk of disease progression (67% vs. 30%; all p < 0.01). Although 92% of clinicians reported eliciting patient values, only 75% of patients perceived that this occurred (p = 0.20). Decisional conflict, defined by a SURE score of less than 4, was reported in 39% of patients. Despite widespread support for SDM, there is a discordance in treatment priorities between patients and clinicians and a high level of decisional conflict among patients suggesting a gap between intent and execution of SDM.Keywords: patient's perspectives, pulmonary arterial hypertension therapy, shared decision‐making

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a chronic, progressive disease for which median survival has improved from 2.8 years in the 1980s [1] to 5–7 years with contemporary therapy [2], owing in part to the availability of 16 FDA‐approved medications. These therapies encompass a broad range of administration routes including oral, inhaled, intravenous, subcutaneous injections and continuous formulations but also carry substantial burden to patients and caregivers [3]. Adverse drug effects – including hypotension, diarrhea, and hepatotoxicity – together with the considerable demands of parenteral therapy, such as strict infection‐prevention practices, frequent care of indwelling catheters, home preparation and mixing of medications, and limitations on swimming and showering, can impose significant physical, psychological and emotional strain on patients [4, 5]. Caregivers often experience emotional stress, logistical strain, and burnout s they help manage complex regimens to support patients [6]. PAH management also poses significant administrative strain on healthcare teams due to intensive multidisciplinary coordination, frequent monitoring, extensive hands‐on education, and navigation of insurance hurdles and high therapy costs [7]. Given these far‐reaching consequences for patients, caregivers and the healthcare team, selection of PAH therapy is well‐suited to a shared decision‐making (SDM) approach between patients and clinicians [8] and SDM is recommended by current PAH treatment guidelines [9, 10].

SDM is defined by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) as a “collaborative process in which patients and clinicians work together to make healthcare decisions informed by evidence, the care team's knowledge and experience, and the patient's values, goals, preferences, and circumstances. Family members and caregivers also play an important role in SDM” [11]. SDM is a meeting of two experts, where the clinician is the expert on medical matters and treatment options, while the patient is the expert on their own values and preferences. SDM has been codified in the 2010 Affordable Care Act and endorsed by the 7th World Symposium for Pulmonary Hypertension [10, 12] and the American Heart Association [13] to improve patient‐centered as well as clinical outcomes. Although many clinicians believe they engage in SDM, evidence suggests that high quality SDM is seldom practiced [14, 15]. Time spent, communication skills, or rapport with patients alone do not equate to SDM [16]. In analyses of video‐recorded clinical encounters, both clinicians and patients consistently overestimated the occurrence of SDM, reporting rates of approximately 70% to 90% and 60% to 80%, respectively, as compared to 20% to 40% among independent observers, suggesting limited understanding of SDM among both groups [17]. Most clinicians have not received formal training in SDM to support effective patient engagement and implementation of this approach [18]. As a result, many patients ‐ including those with PAH ‐ remain unaware of SDM and may experience uncertainty or apprehension about how to participate in the process [19, 20]. Studies focusing on patients with PAH have identified several domains in which care could be improved, including clinician‐patient communication [21], the use of patient reported outcome measures [12], multi‐disciplinary approaches [22], patients' perception of the information they receive, coping strategies [23] and overall patient engagement [8]. A recent expert consensus on patient engagement in PAH identified an unmet need for improved understanding of the SDM process [24]. While cross‐sectional work has explored role and preferences in decision‐making among patients with PAH [21, 25, 26], studies directly evaluating SDM in PAH remain limited. To address this gap, we conducted a survey of patients and clinicians to characterize the SDM process during PAH therapy selection; the analyses were hypothesis‐generating.

Methods

We conducted a questionnaire survey of patients and clinicians at the Pulmonary Hypertension Association's (PHA) International PH Conference and Scientific Sessions, Atlanta, Georgia (June 9–11, 2022). The PHA conference is structured to include parallel scientific sessions for health care professionals and patients, with shared sessions during meals. Many patients were supported by the PHA to attend this conference, creating a distinctive environment that enabled simultaneous surveying of both patients and clinicians. Conference attendees (clinicians and patients) were encouraged by the PHA to visit and explore the “Research Room”, a designated area within the conference center featuring stations that highlighted ongoing research studies for potential participation. A total of 65 patients and 10 clinicians voluntarily approached the research table to inquire about study participation. Surveys were completed on study‐provided laptops by 52 patients and 10 clinicians after verbal consent was obtained in accordance with IRB guidelines; participants received a $5 gift card upon completion. Because of limited on‐site clinician participation, additional clinician responses were obtained electronically through the PHA email listserv, yielding 28 additional clinician responses. In total, 89 respondents completed the survey, including 51 patients (response rate, 78.4%, all on‐site, with one incomplete survey) and 38 clinicians (response rate, 6.8%, combination 10 on‐site and 28 electronic). To ensure inclusion of clinicians actively engaged in pulmonary arterial hypertension care, eligibility was restricted to respondents who affirmed both current clinical involvement in PAH management and prior experience discussing initiation or modification of PAH therapy. Using the Ottawa Decision Support Framework (ODSF) [27, 28] guidelines, multiple aspects of SDM were surveyed. A 5‐point scale was employed to assess preferences for decision‐making roles, ranging from complete physician to complete patient autonomy, with intermediate options reflecting varying degrees of control preferred during shared decision‐making. Assessment of participants' values and preferences involved two methods: (i) selection of the three most critical factors from a 12‐item list related to PAH therapy decisions, and (ii) responses to elements of the Decision Support Analysis Tool (DSAT‐10). The DSAT‐10, developed within the ODSF [28], was originally designed for observer‐based analysis of audio‐ and video‐recorded interactions between health professionals and patients and was therefore not directly applicable to the present study design. Accordingly, selected DSAT‐10 elements were adapted and reported descriptively to inform hypothesis generation. Aggregate DSAT‐10 scores were not calculated or reported because the instrument was used in a modified and incomplete form.

Decisional conflict among patients was analyzed using the four questions validated by ODSF [29] as the SURE instrument which were: (i) Do you feel sure of the best choice? (Sure), (ii) Do you know the benefits and risks of each option? (Understand), (iii) Are you clear about which benefits and risks matter most to you? (Risk‐benefit ratio), (iv) Do you have enough support and advice to make a choice? (Encouragement). Each “Yes” answer was scored 1, and each “No” answer was scored 0, and a score < 4 indicated existence of decisional conflict [29]. The Decision Self‐Efficacy Scale [28] was used to assess self‐efficacy of decisions for patients. It utilizes 11 questions rated “Confident”, “Neutral” or “Not confident”, using a 3‐point Likert scale, an adaptation from the 5‐point Likert scale used by ODSF with scoring as follows: 1 for “Not Confident”, 2 for “Neutral” and 3 for “Confident” and a maximum score of 33. Mean DSE scores were scaled to 100 (Mean Score/33*100) for comparability with published results; with higher scores indicating higher efficiency in decision‐making. The efficacy of decision‐aid strategies pertinent to PAH therapy selection was evaluated in both patients and clinicians using 16 questions created by study authors, and rated as “Effective”, “Neutral” or “Ineffective”. The study was reviewed and approved by the Loma Linda University Health IRB# 5220080.

Statistical Analysis

Questionnaire responses were conducted using Qualtrics (Qualtrics XM, Pro Core Edition, 2022), a secure web‐based platform hosted by the institution, to design, distribute, and manage patient and clinician responses. All branching logic and adaptive questioning were implemented using Qualtrics' built‐in logic and randomization modules. Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and response variables. Continuous variables were reported as means and 95% confidence intervals. Between‐group comparisons (patients and clinicians) were conducted using Welch's t‐tests assuming unequal variances. For categorical outcomes, proportions with 95% Wilson CIs were reported and the groups were compared with χ 2 or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (v3.11.5), with the pands (v2.1.4) and SciPy (v1.11.4) libraries and R (v4.3.2), with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Overall 89 participants responded to the survey in completion, 51 patients and 38 clinicians. Most patients (n = 44, 86%) and clinicians (n = 29, 76%) were between 41 and 80 years of age. Women represented 86% of patients (n = 44) and 45% of clinicians (n = 17). Furthermore, 80% (n = 41) of patients and 53% (n = 20) of clinicians were affiliated with an accredited pulmonary hypertension center. Values and Preferences

The top three factors (from a list of 12 options) influencing PAH therapy selection, as reported by patients were (i) quality of life, (ii) efficacy of medication and (iii) survival, and as reported by clinicians were (i) risk of disease progression, (ii) efficacy of medication and (iii) quality of life, Figure 2. Significant differences were observed (all p < 0.01): patients emphasized quality of life (81% vs. 54%) and side effects (42% vs. 29%) compared to clinicians, while clinicians placed greater weight on disease progression risk (67% vs. 30%) compared to patients. On the adapted DSAT‐10, clinicians reported high rates of asking patients to specify their preferred role in decision‐making (35 of 38 clinicians [92.1%], Figure 3), whereas patient reports were lower (38 of 51 patients [74.5%], p = 0.20). Similarly, higher rates of affirmative responses were obtained from clinicians when compared to patients regarding preferences for involvement of others in the decision‐making process, 22 of 38 clinicians (57.8%) vs 25 of 51 patients (49%), p = 0.02. An example of patient engagement is reflected in this quote by a patient responding to an open‐ended question on how they were involved in the decision‐making process: “When having to decide on pump therapy, I was given the different drugs, side effects and cost before making the decision. Also, when deciding to come off pump therapy for tablet medication I was also involved in the decision with cost, side effects, and how I would be taking it”.

Additional Results

Clinicians responded that their approach to decision‐making varied when selecting parenteral vs oral therapies, 76% Yes vs 24% No, p = 0.015, stating in free text that parenteral therapy decision‐making required more patient teaching aids, more specialty pharmacy services, more clinical resources and was more time consuming. There was no variation in any of the responses by patient education level, current use of parenteral vs non‐parenteral therapy, presence of care‐giver support or affiliation with an accredited PH Center of Excellence (data not shown).

Discussion

In this exploratory study, both patients and clinicians reported a strong preference for shared decision‐making (SDM) during the selection of PAH therapy. Yet, meaningful discrepancies emerged between expressed preference and clinical practice. Approximately one quarter of patients reported that their values and preferences were not elicited by clinicians, and 40% of patients experienced considerable decisional uncertainty. These hypothesis‐generating results highlight a gap between intent and execution of SDM in PAH and suggest a need for additional research.

Our study contributes new and important insights into the evolving field of SDM by focusing on a complex, high‐stakes therapeutic landscape, PAH management, where patients face not only a life‐threatening disease but also an evolving range of treatments with variable routes of administration, side‐effect profiles, and potential lifestyle burdens. This study is among the first to assess SDM in PAH using the Ottawa Decision‐Making Framework tools. Patients and clinicians were largely aligned in preferring a collaborative role in treatment decisions. Similar to this study, a national survey found that 96% of US adults want to discuss their medical options and have their clinician consider their opinion before selecting which option to pursue [30]. This sentiment is well captured in the succinct SDM catchphrase: “Nothing about me without me.”

However, a discrepancy arose in the valuation of the top priority during therapeutic consideration: patients prioritized quality of life while clinicians prioritized the risk of disease progression. Consistent with our findings, prior work has demonstrated discordant treatment priorities among PAH stakeholders: patients emphasize improvements in daily function, symptom burden and psychosocial well‐being, while clinicians focus on objective clinical outcomes such as right ventricular function, 6‐MWD, hospitalization and validated risk scores predictive of disease progression and mortality [31]. Clinicians have acknowledged that such conventional end points frequently fail to capture outcomes most salient to patients [31]. This misalignment has been associated with diminished perceived treatment benefit [32] and underestimation of symptom burden [33] in PAH care. Recognizing the value of patient perspectives, the 7th WSPH guidelines recommended incorporating health‐related quality‐of‐life and patient‐reported outcome measures such as emPHasis‐10, CAMPHOR and PAH‐SYMPACT in routine clinical care and clinical trials [12]. Differences between patient and physician priorities are not uncommon in other chronic disease management. As an example, Sidorkiewicz et al. found that in 153 patient–primary physician pairs, almost 30% of patients' top‐priority conditions did not appear in their physicians' list [34]. In a Canadian survey [35], fewer than half of participants (40%) reported that their views were routinely solicited during decision‐making. In the present study, clinicians reported a higher degree of patient involvement in decision‐making than patients reported experiencing (92% vs 75%). Although these rates exceed those observed in the above‐mentioned Canadian survey, the discrepancy underscores a discordance between clinicians' and patients' experience of shared decision‐making, a gap that may be even more pronounced in everyday clinical practice beyond research‐engaged, conference‐based populations [14, 15].

Perhaps most revealing were the findings regarding decisional conflict, decisional self‐efficacy and decisional quality. Approximately 40% of patients indicated substantial decisional conflict using the SURE instrument, reinforcing earlier observations that clinicians often underestimate the uncertainty patients feel during decision‐making [36, 37]. It is known that decisional conflict leads to decision delay, anxiety, regret, suboptimal outcomes and blaming attitudes [38, 39, 40]. The mean decisional self‐efficacy score of 60.9/100 is considered low when compared to scores of 85.9/100 [41] and 85.4/100 [42] reported in studies evaluating patients with diabetes and geriatric conditions. Higher decisional self‐efficacy is associated with improved self‐management adherence [43]. Decisional support, as assessed by the adapted DSAT‐10 instrument, appeared suboptimal. In particular, elicitation of patients' preference regarding the involvement of family members or others was infrequent, despite its designation as a core element of SDM in the AHRQ definition [11].

Taken together, these findings suggest that there may be a mismatch in the perception and practice of SDM, as is well known in extant literature [35, 44, 45]. Studies using objective measurements, such as video analysis of patient‐clinician dyad interactions, have consistently shown that the rate of SDM is significantly lower than clinicians perceive [17, 46]. A recent expert consensus [24] on patient engagement strategies specific to PAH identified several strategies to enhance SDM. These included, among several recommendations [1]: promoting patient accountability [2], engaging caregivers [3], allowing longer appointment durations [4], simplification of medical information [5], appropriate use of patient‐to‐patient interactions and [6] tailoring needs to individual patient circumstances. A survey of patient preferences suggested that patients with PAH have strong preference for the language their clinicians use when discussing their disease and treatment options e.g. favoring “We want you to have fewer symptoms” over “We want you to feel more normal” [21].

Despite growing research in SDM, most clinicians have not received formal education or training in SDM approaches during their medical education or clinical training [44, 47, 48]. To the contrary, current clinical training, the board‐certification processes and health‐care system structure, reimbursement and policies emphasize clinician‐led, evidence‐based decision‐making with nearly not enough regard for patient preferences and values [49, 50]. As clinicians, we may be adept at asking “what is the matter with you?” but it seems we underutilize asking “what matters to you most?” [51]. Overall, the findings of this study suggest that there is a critical need to expand on research focusing on patients with PAH similar to studies that have identified domains for improvement in SDM, for example in clinician‐patient communication [21], the use of patient reported outcome measures [12], multi‐disciplinary approaches [22], patients' perception of the information they receive, coping strategies [23], overall patient engagement [8] as well as evidence‐based interventions to improve decision‐making, including the development of decision‐aid models analogous to those used in lung cancer [52, 53], asthma [54] and COPD care [55]. In addition, there is a critical need to expand SDM research and training focused on clinicians.

Encouragingly, the present study found that both patients and clinicians highly value personal counseling by clinicians, providing a foundation for the development of effective SDM strategies in the clinic visit.

Limitations of this study include potential selection bias due to a relatively small and self‐selected sample of patients and clinicians who attended a large medical conference. This may reflect a cohort of patients with a greater level of independence and autonomy regarding their disease management. An example of this self‐motivation is reflected in this quote by a patient from the survey: “I come with research already done, both from reading studies and product publications and from talking to patients already on those therapies”. Notwithstanding this limitation, the study design leveraged the distinctive format of the PHA meetings, which feature concurrent patient and clinician conferences. Because participants were highly engaged and research‐oriented, the observed differences likely underestimate those that would be present in more representative clinical populations. The predominance of white, female respondents among patients may limit generalizability. The low response rate among clinicians may lead to nonresponse bias. Although DSAT‐10 tool is intended to analyze audio and video‐taped interactions between health professionals and patients, we solicited self‐reported responses in this study, recognizing their potential value for hypothesis generation. The interpretability of survey findings would have been enhanced by the inclusion of patient‐clinician dyads; however this was not feasible given the study design.

In summary, this study highlights a gap between patients and health care professionals in shared decision‐making during pulmonary arterial hypertension therapeutic selection. Despite consensus regarding the value of shared decision‐making, patients and clinicians differ in perception and practice of the process. These differences appear to reflect divergent priorities – patients emphasizing quality of life and medication efficacy, and clinicians focusing on the risk of disease progression. In addition, patients perceive low decisional support, low decisional confidence and high decisional conflict.
