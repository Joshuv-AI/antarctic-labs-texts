Gene Expression Insights into Cytarabine Resistance in Acute Myeloid Leukemia: The Role of Cytokines
Authors: CHIEH-MING LEE, YIN-HWA SHIH, I-CHEN CHEN, TZONG-MING SHIEH, CHUNG-YU YEH, YU-HSIN TSENG
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimCytarabine is the main chemotherapy agent used to treat acute myeloid leukemia (AML), but drug resistance remains a major challenge. Imbalances in cytokine secretion are known to play a role in the survival and proliferation of AML blast cells. While numerous studies have investigated cytokine secretion in AML, the precise role of cytokines in the pathogenesis of AML remains unclear. This study aimed to compare cytokine-related gene expression in parental and resistant HL60 cells and assess their prognostic relevance in AML.Materials and MethodsThis study compared gene expression profiles of parental HL60 and R-HL60 cells by analyzing gene expression arrays. Further, the correlation between the differential expression genes (DEGs) and overall survival in patients with AML was obtained from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, and the expression of genes was validated by quantitative PCR (QPCR).ResultsOur results showed that twenty-six genes involved in cytokine regulation were significantly different between HL60 and R-HL60 cells. The DEGs associated with cytokine production between parental HL60 and R-HL60 was subjected to a functional enrichment analysis by ShinyGO. The expression of BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, NFKB2, CLEC9A, and OAS2 involved in the regulation of cytokine production was associated with survival probability of AML patient in the TCGA database. Among them, the expression of BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, and NFKB2 genes in R-HL60 cells was higher than that in HL-60 cells.ConclusionThis study identified key genes involved in cytokine dysregulation in cytarabine-resistant HL60 cells, providing potential targets for overcoming drug resistance in AML. These findings offer new avenues for the development of more effective therapies for relapsed or refractory AML patients.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the uncontrolled clonal proliferation of abnormal myeloid progenitor cells in the blood and bone marrow. Chemotherapy is the first-line treatment for AML, however, the drug resistance is one of the most intractable problems in AML treatment (1). Approximately 50% of patients with AML do not respond to induction therapy or relapse within 6 months. Advances in high-throughput next-generation sequencing have revealed the remarkable molecular heterogeneity of AML, spurring efforts to develop new targeted therapies. However, multiple driver mutations and the coexistence of multiple competing tumorigenic clones makes this disease highly complex and increases the challenge of new drug development (2).

Several studies have shown that chronic immune stimulation and abnormal cytokine signaling are triggers for AML initiation and progression (2,3). Under homeostatic conditions, pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines appear broadly balanced. Imbalances in cytokine secretion contribute to AML blast cell survival and proliferation. For example, anti-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β inhibit leukemia cell proliferation and control the expression and function of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6, IL-1β, and GMCSF. Once the anti-inflammatory mediators are down-regulated, the cytokine equilibrium is disrupted (4). IL-10 is another anti-inflammatory cytokine, and increased IL-10 plasma levels in AML patients are associated with higher survival rates and better response rates to chemotherapy. These protective functions might in part be explained by IL-10-induced release of IL-1Ra (5). The anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-35, which belongs to the IL-12 cytokine family, has the ability to suppress CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell proliferation and to decrease T-cell cytokine expression (6). In addition, IL-35 increased the percentage of CD4+ Tregs, which showed a strong ability to suppress effector T cells, suggesting that IL-35 contributes to immune escape of AML blasts by actively suppressing T cell effector functions while promoting Treg activity. IL-35 is also thought to promote the growth of adult AML blasts, as pre-stimulation with IL-35 leads to reduced apoptosis and enhanced proliferation of AML cells (7). A study also indicates that the association between immune-infiltrating tumor microenvironment (TME) and resistance to cytarabine-based chemotherapy but responsiveness to flotetuzumab, as salvage immunotherapy for refractory AML. The most common adverse events were infusion-related reactions and cytokine storm. AML patients with immune infiltration compared with immune depletion in the TME are enriched in interferon gamma (IFN-γ)-related messenger RNA (mRNA) profiles and display resistance to cytotoxic chemotherapy but increased probability of response to immunotherapy (8).

Several studies have explored diverse molecular mechanisms and gene alterations contributing to leukemia development and treatment response (9,10). Although the secretion of cytokines in AML is actively investigated, the exact functions of cytokines in AML pathogenesis are not fully understood. Elucidating the molecular basis of complex cytokine networks in AML is a prerequisite for the development of new therapeutic regimens based on targeting cytokines and their receptors. This study identified DEGs associated with dysregulated cytokine production that may be potential targets for new chemotherapy drugs in patients with relapsed or refractory AML.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture and drug treatment. R-HL60 cells were established from parental HL60 cells (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA) by continual treatment with increasing concentrations of cytarabine from 1 nM to 1 μM. HL60 and R-HL60 cells were both cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Amarillo, TX, USA) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic reagents (Gibco). Cells were cultured at a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37˚C, and subcultured when they reached 80% confluency covering most of the dish.

Gene expression array. Total RNA was extracted from parental HL60 and R-HL60 cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and submitted to Welgene Biotech for gene expression array analysis. A Low Input Quick Amp Labeling Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to amplify and label target RNA to generate cRNA with fluorescent Cy3. Cy3-labled cRNA was fragmented to the average size of the fragmented nucleic acids is between 50 and 100 nucleotides by incubation with fragmentation buffer at 60˚C for 30 min. Correspondingly fragmented labeled cRNA was then pooled and hybridized to Agilent SurePrin Microarray at 65˚C for 17 h. Subsequently, the Cy3 microarray was scanned at 535 nm with a microarray scanner (Agilent Technologies) and data was analyzed using Feature Extraction 10.7.3.1 (Agilent Technologies). The raw signal data were subjected to quantile normalization to identify differential gene expression. The differential expression genes (DEGs) were subjected to a functional enrichment analysis by ShinyGO 0.76.

Expression data from TCGA. The expression profiles of 163 AML samples were obtained from the TCGA data portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html). The correlation between the gene expression of BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, NFKB2, CLEC9A, and OAS2 as well as the poor survival curves in patients with AML were collected and analyzed.

RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was isolated using a TRIzol® total RNA extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), and cDNA was synthesized using a qScriberTM cDNA Synthesis kit (highQu GmbH, Kraichtal, Germany). The reaction steps of reverse transcription were as follows: incubation at 42-50˚C for 30 min to synthesize cDNA, followed by incubation at 85˚C for 10 min to inactivate the enzyme. Amplification reactions of PCR were set up in 10 µl reaction volumes with amplification primers and Fast SYBR Green Master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and then detected by an ABI 7500 system (Applied Biosystems; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). The reaction steps of PCR were as follows: denaturation at 95˚C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95˚C for 15 s, annealing at 60˚C for 60 s and extension at 72˚C for 15 s. Each condition included three independent biological replicates (n=3), and each sample was analyzed in technical triplicates. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the internal control. Functional enrichment analysis. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the ShinyGO 0.76 software to identify functional terms of DEGs between parental HL60 and R-HL60 cells. Protein-protein interaction analysis in the cluster associated with regulation of cytokine production pathways was performed using STRING 11.5.

Statistical analysis. Data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Student’s t-test was used to determine the differences between experimental and control groups. Data are represented as mean±standard deviation of three independent experiments and p<0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference in all comparisons of the experimental and control group.

Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes between parental HL60 and R-HL60 Cells. Total RNA was extracted from parental HL60 and R-HL60 cells and used for gene expression array analysis. Genes whose expression difference more than 2 times between HL60 and R-HL60 cells were selected as candidate genes for this study. The results indicated 274 candidate genes, including 185 genes exhibited over 2 times higher expression and 89 genes exhibited over 2 times lower expression in R-HL60 than they did in the parental HL60 cells, respectively. The top 10 significantly enriched GO_BP terms among the 274 candidate DEGs are shown in a histogram (Figure 2B), and the genes involved in these GO_BP terms are listed in Table II. The correlations among these enriched GO_BP terms were summarized in a hierarchical clustering tree (Figure 2C) and an interactive graph (Figure 2D). Furthermore, three GO_BP terms, including cytokine production, regulation of cytokine production, and negative regulation of cytokine production, shared high percentage of gene members, so were clustered into the same cluster in hierarchical clustering tree and were connected in an interactive graph. In this study, these three GO_BP terms were collectively referred to ‘’regulation of cytokine production pathways’’ (Figure 2C).

Discussion

This study uncovers critical insights into the cytokine dysregulation in cytarabine-resistant HL60 cells, highlighting key genes - BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, and NFKB - that are pivotal in the resistance mechanism. The association between these genes and AML patient survival underscores their potential as biomarkers for prognosis and therapeutic targets. These findings provide a promising foundation for the development of novel treatment strategies aimed at overcoming drug resistance in relapsed or refractory AML, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, and NFKB2 have been reported in past studies to be associated with cancer progression or survival probability. Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) consists of five members, including RelA, RelB (encoded by RELB), c-Rel, NF-κB1and NF-κB2 (encoded by NFKB2). NF-κB is a key transcriptional factor and is known to be constitutively activated in most patients with AML, which contributes to the resistance to apoptosis (11). BCL3 is a candidate proto-oncogene whose expression is inducible by NF-κB, forming an autoregulatory loop controlling nuclear p50/NF-κB1 residence (12). Aberrant expression of OAS3 occurs in almost all TCGA cancer types and subtypes. High expression of OAS3 is associated with poorer overall survival in patients with AML has been reported (13). AIM2 plays a key role in tumorigenesis, either in inflammasome-dependent or inflammasome-independent manner, and is considered to predict the prognosis and recurrence of childhood AML (14).

Previous studies have also confirmed the role of BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, and NFKB2 in regulating cytokine production. RELB and NF-κB2 are the most widely studied members and constitute the most prominent dimer of the alternative NF-κB pathway, likely due to the stability of their binding (15). The NF-κB signaling pathway is widely recognized for its role in mediating the innate immune response and controlling the transcriptional activation of its downstream target genes, including TNF-α (15,16). This pathway is activated by a range of stimuli that trigger a diverse set of cytokine receptors and pattern recognition receptors located on the cell membrane (16). Whether in liver injury (16), kidney injury (17), or LPS-triggered cytokine storm (18) the NF-κB signaling pathway plays a key role to drive immune response. A study indicates that OAS3 can enhance the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. OAS1, OAS2, and OAS3 can restrict mycobacterial growth within cells through an unidentified mechanism and appear to promote the secretion of cytokines/chemokines. It is speculated that the expression of OAS in human macrophages may contribute to the activation of host defense mechanisms against viral and mycobacterial infections (19). In addition, AIM2 is a key element of the innate immune system, acting as a cytoplasmic sensor for double-stranded DNA. It plays a critical role in antiviral and antibacterial defense, as well as in autoimmune diseases associated with self-DNA. The expression of AIM2 plays a crucial role in the inflammatory response by triggering the inflammasome signaling cascade in various conditions, including cholesteatoma tissues (20), joint in rheumatoid arthritis, kidney in systemic lupus erythematous (21), and tumorigenesis (22).

In physiological conditions, the interaction between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines plays a crucial role in regulating cellular activation profiles. However, in AML, these interactions are often severely dysregulated and chemoresistance is often mediated interaction between leukemic cells and the cytokines-altered microenvironment (23). Major pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL6 have been shown to have tumor-promoting properties, while certain anti-inflammatory cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, can counteract leukemogenesis and lead to better clinical outcomes. Therefore, restoring cytokine homeostasis could potentially offer significant benefits for AML patients (24). TNF-α, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, exerts both cytotoxic and cytostatic effects on cancer cells and high-dose cytarabine treatment has been shown to induce the release of TNF-α, followed by the sequential release of other pro-inflammatory cytokines (25). Our data revealed significant differences in the expression of 10 genes (IL1B, CCL2, CCL5, BCL3, CXCL10, ICAM1, MMP9, BIRC3, TNFAIP3, NFKBIA) associated with the TNF signaling pathway between HL60 and R-HL60 cells, as identified through KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (data not shown). This suggests a potential intricate relationship between cytarabine-induced chemoresistance and the activation of TNF-α signaling pathways.

Conclusion

Our study provides compelling evidence that the regulation of cytokine production plays a significant role in the survival and resistance of AML cells to chemotherapy. The identification of genes such as BCL3, OAS3, RELB, AIM2, and NFKB2 as key modulators in cytokine regulation suggests their potential as biomarkers for prognosis and therapeutic targets in AML. Our findings emphasize the importance of further exploration into how these cytokine-related pathways contribute to chemoresistance and immune evasion in AML, offering new avenues for targeted treatment strategies that could improve patient outcomes.
