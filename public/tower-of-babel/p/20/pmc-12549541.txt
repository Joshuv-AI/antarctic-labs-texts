Physicochemical characteristics and key aroma evolution in sauced duck curing
Authors: Guangfa Xie, Weiyi Liu, Xiaomin Zheng, Qi Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSauced duck is valued for its distinctive aroma, yet the physicochemical drivers and key odorants formed during curing remain unclear, limiting process control and quality standardization. We sought to determine when and which odorants define sauced-duck aroma and how they relate to measurable physicochemical changes. We combined physicochemical assays (pH, WHC, protein/fat, color, texture), E-nose, HS-SPME-GC-MS, multivariate modeling (OPLS-DA), odor-activity values (OAVs), and aroma recombination/omission tests across curing stages. We identified 82 volatiles; 23 had VIP > 1 and OAV > 1. Recombination–omission verified 10 core odorants—anethole, heptanal, nonanal, phenylacetaldehyde, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal, trans-2-decenal, 2-heptenal, 1-octen-3-ol, and 1-octen-3-one—as necessary for the characteristic aroma. E-nose and multivariate analyses showed that most aroma reconfiguration occurred within the first five days, coinciding with rapid shifts in WHC, color (↑a*, ↑b*, ↓L*), and texture. By linking stage-specific physicochemical transitions to verified key odorants, this work provides actionable markers and a time window to optimize curing. The integrated sensomics workflow offers a practical basis for flavor evaluation, curing-time optimization, and quality control in industrial sauced-duck production.Keywords: Electronic nose, HS-SPME-GC-MS, Volatile compounds, Physicochemical properties, Sensory analysis

Introduction

Meat is a major source of high-quality proteins, amino acids, vitamins, and minerals, playing a vital role in human diets and nutrition (Guo et al., 2024). Poultry meat, in particular, is a significant component due to its high production volume and rich nutritional value (Jalal, et al., 2024). To enhance the flavor and texture of meat, various curing techniques such as braising, salting, and smoking are commonly used. Studies have shown that braising improves the texture and mouthfeel of meats, imparting signature aromas and flavors. For example, Deng et al. demonstrated how braising enhances the texture of chicken, while Song et al. found that it deepens the flavor profile of pork. Similarly, Ji et al. reported that braised sausages are highly popular among consumers due to their distinctive taste (Deng, et al., 2024; Ji, et al., 2024; Song, et al., 2024). Among braised meats, Chinese sauced duck stands out for its tender texture and rich umami flavor. The flavor of food is a complex interaction of aroma, taste, and sensory perception, with volatile organic compounds (VOCs) playing a crucial role in shaping overall flavor. VOCs are profiled using GC-MS, GC-IMS, and electronic noses (E-nose) (Alfieri, et al., 2024; He, et al., 2024; Yu, et al., 2025). Among these, HS-SPME-GC-MS is widely used for its sensitivity, small sample size, and reproducibility (Jiang, et al., 2022). Hao et al., for instance, used SPME-GC-MS to analyze the flavor profile of Inner Mongolian dried meat, identifying 111 volatile compounds, while Jung et al. identified 144 flavor compounds in Korean fried chicken. Similarly, Li et al. evaluated the volatile compounds in cured pork and identified 47 key components (Hao, et al., 2024; Jung, et al., 2024; Li, et al., 2024). Electronic noses, which simulate the human olfactory system, have emerged as a reliable alternative for analyzing food aromas. E-nose is advantageous for its operational simplicity, cost-efficiency, and high sensitivity (Fan, et al., 2024). They are increasingly applied in flavor characterization and comparison studies. For instance, Feng et al. employed E-nose, GC-MS, and GC-IMS to investigate the volatile profile of pepper fruits at different stages, while Su et al. used E-nose and GC-MS to analyze and compare the flavor of butter from different pastures. Additionally, Wang et al. characterized the flavor profile of Chinese carp species using E-nose (Feng, et al., 2024; Su, et al., 2024; Wang, et al., 2025).

Previous studies on cured meats have mainly focused on either physicochemical properties or volatile compounds, but comprehensive investigations integrating both aspects remain limited. Moreover, few reports have validated the sensory relevance of the identified volatiles through recombination and omission experiments. To address these gaps, we systematically investigated physicochemical changes, aroma evolution, and sensory validation during sauced-duck curing. By combining E-nose, HS-SPME-GC-MS, and aroma recombination/omission experiments, this study not only identified the key volatile compounds responsible for the characteristic aroma but also linked them with specific physicochemical changes. These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms of flavor formation in sauced duck and offer a scientific basis for optimizing traditional processing and promoting product standardization.

Materials and methods

Preparation of sauced duck

For this study, Shaoxing shelduck (weighing 1.50 ± 0.20 kg) were purchased as post-mortem retail products from Yonghui Supermarket, Shaoxing City, Zhejiang Province, China. Labels identified licensed processing plants, and products were purchased post-slaughter from retail channels. We retained purchase receipts and product labels to ensure traceability. However, we did not conduct on-site audits, and therefore we refrain from making animal-level welfare claims.

Ethics statement: Samples were obtained post-mortem from retail channels; no procedures were performed on live animals, and slaughter-ethics approval was not required. Samples were obtained from retail channels where processing plants are required to comply with applicable national animal welfare and food safety regulations. A limitation of this study is that upstream animal welfare practices could not be independently verified by the authors.

To ensure experimental consistency, only duck legs with similar size, shape, and weight were used as the experimental samples. The seasoning ingredients, including bay leaves, star anise, cinnamon, salt, soy sauce, dried chili peppers, scallions, ginger, and rock sugar, were purchased from Yonghui Supermarket, while Shaoxing rice wine was sourced from Zhejiang Gu Yue Long Shan Shaoxing Wine Co., Ltd.

The traditional marinade for the curing process was prepared as follows: 3 g of bay leaves, 7 g of star anise, 7 g of cinnamon, 45 g of salt, 1300 mL of soy sauce, 100 mL of Shaoxing rice wine, 11 g of dried chili peppers, 215 g of rock sugar, 50 g of ginger, and 10 g of scallions were boiled together and simmered for 10 minutes. The marinade was then cooled and filtered to remove the spices. Dried duck legs were submerged in the marinade and cured at 18°C for three days, followed by air drying for another three days. In traditional sauced-duck curing, no additional water was added to the marinade. Samples were taken daily during the curing process. After deboning (while leaving the skin intact), the duck legs were minced using a meat grinder, then dried to remove moisture. The ground samples were stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Analysis of physicochemical changes during the curing of sauced duck

The pH values of sauced duck samples were measured using a pH-01 penetration pH meter (Eutech Instruments, USA). After calibration with pH 4.0 and pH 7.0 buffers, the probe was inserted approximately 10 mm into the core of the minced duck gel samples at various curing stages. Once the reading on the digital display stabilized, the pH value was recorded. For each sample, measurements were taken at three randomly selected points, and the average was calculated as the final result. WHC, protein, and fat were measured per national standards. WHC was measured using the direct drying method according to GB 5009.3-2016, protein content was assessed using the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method (GB 5009.5-2016), and fat content was analyzed via the acid hydrolysis method (GB 5009.6-2016).

Changes in L*, a*, and b* values during curing of sauced duck

Color measurements of sauced duck samples were conducted using a colorimeter equipped with a D65 light source, a 10° standard observer, and an 8 mm aperture setting. The instrument was calibrated with a white standard tile before use. Samples were thawed at 4°C for 30 minutes and placed on a white background for measurement. The colorimeter was positioned vertically to take readings of the color parameters. Results were reported in CIE Lab (L*, a*, b*). Each group of samples was measured three times, and the average values were used as the final result.

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Texture was measured using a texture analyzer. Samples were cut into cubes of 2 cm × 2 cm × 1 cm. The texture analyzer was set with the following parameters: P36/R probe, full TPA mode, pre-test speed of 5.0 mm/s, test speed of 0.5 mm/s, post-test speed of 5.0 mm/s, compression ratio of 50 %, and a trigger force of 5.0 g.

E-nose analysis

The portable electronic nose (PEN 3.0, Airsense Analytics GmbH, Germany) was equipped with a 10-sensor array of metal oxide semiconductors (MOS), each designed to respond to specific volatile compounds. The sensors included W1C (S₁), W5S (S₂), W3C (S₃), W6S (S₄), W5C (S₅), W1S (S₆), W1W (S₇), W2S (S₈), W2W (S₉), and W3S (S₁₀). The array targets aromatic compounds, nitrogen oxides, ammonia, hydrogen, short-chain alkanes, methane, sulfur compounds, alcohols/aldehydes/ketones, alkanes, and organosulfur (Tian, et al., 2019). The detailed specifications of the sensor performance are shown in Table 1. Data acquisition and refinement were carried out using pattern recognition software (WinMuster v.1.6.2).

Sensory evaluation

A descriptive sensory evaluation was performed by a trained panel (n = 13; 6 F/7 M; 20–35 y). Panelists were recruited from Shaoxing University and had prior experience in food aroma evaluation. Before the formal tests, two 1-h training sessions were conducted in a sensory laboratory maintained at ∼20°C and 50–70 % relative humidity. During training, aroma attributes of sauced duck samples were presented and discussed, leading to a consensus lexicon. Reference standards were provided to calibrate sensory terms: trimethylamine (fishy), 3-methylthiopropionaldehyde (sauce aroma), 2,4-decadienal (fatty), eugenol (spicy), ethyl acetate (ester), and a mixture of 3-methyl-1-butanol plus 4-ethylguaiacol (alcoholic). Coded samples were presented in randomized, balanced order under odor-free, quiet conditions. First, triangle tests assessed the similarity between sauced duck samples and aroma models. Panelists then rated the intensity of six aroma attributes and color appearance on a 10-point scale (0 = not perceived, 10 = extremely strong)（Table 2）. Each sample was evaluated in triplicate.

HS-SPME-GC-MS analysis

To analyze the volatile flavor compounds of the sauced duck samples, HS-SPME-GC-MS was employed. A 1 g portion of the minced sauced duck sample, along with 10 μL of internal standard (2-octanol, 4 mg/L; 3-heptanone, 10 mg/L; octyl propanoate, 12 mg/L), was added to a 25 mL headspace vial. A DVB/CAR/PDMS (50/30 µm) fiber extracted volatiles at 80°C for 30 min. The fiber was then inserted into the GC-MS injection port for desorption at 230°C for 5 minutes. Analysis was carried out using a QP 2020 NX (SHIMADZU) gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer, equipped with a DB-5 column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm) and helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The GC temperature program was as follows: an initial temperature of 40°C for 3 minutes, followed by an increase of 3°C/min to 120°C, held for 5 minutes, then ramped up at 10°C/min to 250°C and maintained for 5 minutes. The ion source temperature was set at 250°C. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing the mass spectra with the NIST 17 spectral library, with a match factor of ≥95 %. Quantification of the volatile compounds was performed using an internal standard method.

Calculation of OAV (Odor Activity Value)

OAVs were calculated as OAVi=Ci/ OTi, where C is the measured concentration and OT is the odor threshold. The concentration of each VOC (C) was measured through GC-MS analysis, and the odor threshold (OT) values were obtained from reference sources, such as the FEMA flavor library https://www.femaflavor.org/flavor-library. OAVs greater than 1 indicate that the compound contributes significantly to the aroma perception.

Aroma recombination and omission

To identify the key aroma compounds in Sauced Duck with OAV > 1, an aroma recombination and omission model was employed. This method was used to verify the core aroma compounds contributing to the characteristic flavor of the product. Each odor-active compound was quantified using the internal standard method. Calibration curves (R² > 0.995) were constructed in ethanol–water solution (12 %, v/v), and concentrations were expressed as mean ± SD from triplicate analyses and OAV results of each compound. A recombination model was created by spiking an odorless matrix with target compounds at measured concentrations. Sensory evaluation was then used to assess the validity of this model. The omission test involved systematically removing specific compounds or groups of compounds from the recombination model to create omission models. Significant differences between omission and full models indicated that the omitted compound(s) were essential to the core aroma profile. Prior to conducting the experiment, a neutral base matrix was prepared by eliminating the odor from Sauced Duck samples. This was achieved by shaking the sample for 8 hours in a mixture of diethyl ether and pentane (2:1, w/w). The mixture was then filtered, and the extraction process was repeated three times until GC-MS analysis showed no volatile compounds. The odorless matrix consisted of the treated Sauced Duck and ultrapure water (Liu, et al., 2023).

The identified aroma compounds with OAV > 1 were then recreated at their original concentrations in the odorless matrix to form a recombination model. For the omission model, solutions were prepared that systematically omitted one aroma compound at a time, added to the odorless matrix. Triangle tests (three-alternative forced-choice) were conducted by a trained sensory panel (n = 13). Each test included two complete recombination models and one omission model, presented in randomized order. Significant differences were assessed using chi-square tests (p < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

All experiments used ≥3 independent replicates. Data are mean ± SE. Single-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with Tukey's multiple comparison test was employed to assess differences between groups. Graphs and statistical representations were created using Origin 2018 and SIMCA 14.1 software for comprehensive data visualization and multivariate analysis.

Results and discussion

Changes in physicochemical indexes during the curing process of duck in soy sauce

Changes in pH during the curing of sauced duck

Meat pH is a critical quality factor. Deviations from the optimal pH range can lead to spoilage, discoloration, and undesirable flavors. Generally, the pH of meat tends to stabilize within a slightly acidic range (Choi, et al., 2023). In this study, the pH values of sauced duck samples were observed to range from 5.42 to 5.91, showing no significant differences across the groups. These findings align with Chang et al. (Chang, et al., 2021). Interestingly, the pH values of the samples showed a gradual decrease with an increase in curing time, exhibiting a rapid decline in the early stages followed by a slower decrease. This trend may be attributed to changes in protein structure, where charged ion groups are released into the extracellular environment, affecting the overall pH (Guo, Hong, Wang, Zhang, Chen, Chen, Ashokkumar and Ma, 2024). Additionally, the breakdown of fats, leading to an increase in free fatty acid content, may have contributed to the pH decline (Mao, et al., 2023). Several factors may be responsible for this phenomenon: high-salinity brine could induce excessive water loss in cells, leading to membrane damage and the release of acidic compounds from within the cells. Moreover, brine could potentially alter protein structures, facilitating the release of acidic groups into the tissue. The metabolic activity of microorganisms may also diminish under high salt concentrations, further limiting the production of alkaline compounds (Mao, Xia, Zeng, Xia, Zhou, Cao, He, Pan and Wang, 2023). High concentrations of saturated brine can effectively inhibit microbial metabolism, which helps in reducing the formation of alkaline by-products. (Fig. 1A)

Changes in water-holding capacity (WHC) during the curing process

Water-holding capacity (WHC) is a critical indicator of the juiciness of meat products and is a key factor in determining their overall quality (Park, et al., 2021). Throughout the curing process, the WHC of the sauced duck samples exhibited a declining trend from day 0 to day 6. The initial drop in WHC was rapid from day 0 to day 2, after which it stabilized between days 2 and 3. A significant decrease in WHC was observed between days 3 and 4, and by days 5 and 6, the WHC of the sauced duck samples plateaued.

Water in meat exists mainly as bound, immobilized, and free water, immobilized water (80–85 %) dominates WHC (Jiao, et al., 2022). Immobilized water is typically located in the myofibrillar network or between actin and myosin filaments (Jiao, Zhang, Chen, Yang, Shi, Liu, Wang, Lu and Hui, 2022). During the first three days of curing in brine, the high ionic concentration in the brine increased the ion concentration within the duck samples. In such a high-salt environment, the gel network facilitates better solubilization of myosin and the formation of chemical bonds, thereby enhancing the WHC of the samples (Guo, Hong, Wang, Zhang, Chen, Chen, Ashokkumar and Ma, 2024). From day 4 onwards, as the samples were sun-dried, partial evaporation of water led to a reduction in WHC. During days 5–6 (air-drying, shaded/ventilated), WHC stabilized. These findings indicate that increasing the salt concentration during curing effectively enhances the WHC of meat products, thus contributing to improved tenderness. (Fig. 1B)

Changes in protein content during the curing process

The protein content in the sauced duck decreased from 76.81 g/100 g on day 0 to 72.68 g/100 g by day 6. A significant decline in protein content was observed during the first three days, followed by a slower rate of decrease from day 4 onwards, stabilizing around day 6. The high-salt curing environment (8 g/100 g) is known to inhibit the activity of tissue proteases, which can suppress the hydrolysis of muscle proteins (Jiao, Zhang, Chen, Yang, Shi, Liu, Wang, Lu and Hui, 2022). However, endogenous enzymes and exogenous enzymes secreted by microorganisms continue to degrade proteins, leading to a reduction in protein content (Guo, Hong, Wang, Zhang, Chen, Chen, Ashokkumar and Ma, 2024). From day 4, when the sauced duck was exposed to air drying, the increased exposure of proteins to oxygen promoted protein oxidation. This process was further accelerated by the shift from the low-temperature curing environment at 18°C to a higher ambient temperature of around 30°C, which enhanced enzyme activity and accelerated protein degradation. Additionally, lipid oxidation in the duck meat likely triggered further protein oxidation, reducing the overall protein content (Bao and Ertbjerg, 2019). As the drying process progressed, the reduction in moisture content and the decline in microbial activity led to a decrease in protease activity, thus slowing the rate of protein degradation and stabilizing the protein content during the later stages of curing (Mediani, et al., 2022) (Fig. 1C). Although free amino acids (FAAs) were not directly quantified in this study, the observed protein degradation trends, together with the detection of nitrogen-containing volatiles (e.g., phenylacetaldehyde), provide indirect evidence of proteolysis contributing to flavor formation. Future targeted FAA analysis will be necessary to validate these mechanistic inferences.

Changes in fat content during the curing process

Fat content decreased overall during curing. From day 0 to day 2, the rate of fat reduction was minimal, with the fat content remaining almost unchanged. However, starting from day 3, a more pronounced decline in fat content was observed, which stabilized between days 5 and 6. During the early stages of brining, which occurred at a lower temperature (approximately 18°C), the oxidation of fats proceeded at a slower rate (Liu, et al., 2019b). At this stage, the curing process had a more noticeable impact on protein degradation than on fats, leading to only slight changes in fat content. As the curing time progressed, fat breakdown became more significant, with a marked reduction beginning on day 3. The transition to air drying under natural conditions at room temperature (around 35°C) on day 4 further accelerated this process. The increased temperature likely elevated lipase activity, which intensified enzymatic reactions, and the heightened microbial activity contributed to the enhanced fat degradation, leading to a sharp decrease in fat content (Liu, Liu and Chen, 2019b).

Lipid oxidation is a critical factor influencing the quality and acceptability of meat products. It includes enzymatic oxidation, photooxidation, and autoxidation, with autoxidation being regarded as a key mechanism for improving the flavor profile of dry-cured meats (Mediani, Hamezah, Jam, Mahadi, Chan, Rohani, Che Lah, Azlan, Khairul Annuar, Azman, Bunawan, Sarian, Kamal and Abas, 2022). This oxidation process is responsible for generating important flavor-active compounds such as aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones, which contribute significantly to the unique aroma and taste of cured meat products (Fig. 1D). While free fatty acids (FFAs) were not directly determined, the declining fat content and concurrent increases in aldehydes and ketones (such as nonanal, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal) strongly suggest lipid oxidation pathways are active. Direct FFA profiling will be included in future work to substantiate these mechanisms.

Color

During the curing process of sauced duck, the changes in L*, a*, and b* values indicate a steady decrease in L* (lightness) and a significant increase in a* (redness) and b* (yellowness) values (p < 0.05). This demonstrates that, as the curing time progresses, the duck meat gradually darkens, and its red hue becomes more pronounced. Myoglobin, a heme protein responsible for the red coloration of meat products, plays a pivotal role in this color change (Mancini and Hunt, 2005). The color of meat is primarily influenced by the three chemical states of myoglobin: deoxymyoglobin (purplish-red in its reduced state), oxymyoglobin (bright red when oxygenated), and metmyoglobin (brownish in its oxidized state) (Poveda-Arteaga, et al., 2023). Various factors such as pH, temperature, and oxygen availability affect these transitions, ultimately impacting the meat’s color (Ramanathan, et al., 2020). The decline in L* value during the curing process may be attributed to fat oxidation, which leads to a darker and redder meat color, thereby reducing lightness (Gomez, et al., 2020). The increase in a* (redness) can be linked to the oxidation of deoxymyoglobin into oxymyoglobin. Hydroxyl radicals promote this oxidative process, leading to enhanced redness in the meat (Guo, Hong, Wang, Zhang, Chen, Chen, Ashokkumar and Ma, 2024). Additionally, during the curing process, the partial dissolution of muscle proteins and the reaction between ferrous myoglobin and other cellular components—combined with the hypertonic environment causing cellular disruption—can result in the outward migration of myoglobin, further intensifying the a* value (Guo, Hong, Wang, Zhang, Chen, Chen, Ashokkumar and Ma, 2024). The rise in b* (yellowness) reflects an increase in the yellow hue of the cured duck, which is likely associated with Maillard reactions and enzymatic browning that occur during the curing process. The production of soy sauce, used in the brining process, involves caramelization and enzymatic browning, resulting in the formation of compounds such as pyrrothiazole salts, melanoidins, and 2,4-dihydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-thiazolone, which contribute to the brownish color of soy sauce(Furusawa, et al., 2013; Noda, et al., 2020). These compounds may impart additional brown and yellow tones to the duck meat during curing, enhancing its overall appearance. (Fig. 1E)

Texture analysis during the curing process of sauced duck

The texture of sauced duck is primarily characterized by adhesiveness, chewiness, hardness, elasticity, cohesiveness, and resilience. As shown in Fig. 2A, the adhesiveness of sauced duck decreases as the curing process progresses. The protein in the muscle forms the foundation of gel formation, and during curing, NaCl penetrates the duck meat, leading to the dissolution and hydration of muscle fibers. This alteration in muscle fiber structure directly impacts the adhesiveness of the sauced duck (Kim, et al., 2019). In terms of chewiness, the sauced duck initially experiences a decrease in chewiness, followed by an increase. Chewiness is influenced by the myofibrillar network and collagen content—the more compact the muscle structure or the higher the collagen content, the higher the chewiness. Between Day 0 and Day 3 of curing, the duck meat retains more water, and the protein gel network is more developed. However, as water retention increases, the muscle structure becomes relatively loose, leading to a reduction in chewiness. From Day 4 to Day 6, the moisture in the duck meat evaporates, reducing the water-holding capacity of the protein, and the muscle structure tightens, resulting in an increase in chewiness, which peaks on Day 6 (Chen, et al., 2010) (Fig. 2B). The hardness of the duck meat shows a trend of initial decrease followed by an increase over the curing period. During Days 0 to 3, the high-salt brine causes protein hydrolysis in muscle fibers, increasing protein solubility, which loosens the structure and reduces the hardness of the duck meat. The release of acidic groups during protein hydrolysis lowers the pH, which further disrupts muscle fibers and reduces hardness (Guo, Hong, Wang, Zhang, Chen, Chen, Ashokkumar and Ma, 2024). From Day 4 onwards, protein hydrolysis weakens, protein content stabilizes, and the reduced water content between muscle fibers tightens the structure, thereby increasing the hardness of the sauced duck (Yalçın and Şeker, 2016) (Fig. 2C). Elasticity decreases steadily during the curing process. Brining induces water and NaCl migration within the muscle tissue (Hansen, et al., 2008). The high NaCl concentration in the brine promotes protein hydrolysis, weakening the muscle fiber structure. A well-formed myofibrillar network helps retain water, but as the structure loosens and muscle fibers expand due to water uptake, the elasticity of the duck meat decreases (Fig. 2D). Cohesiveness initially increases during curing and then decreases. As the pH of the brine lowers, proteins—particularly myofibrillar proteins—undergo deformation and crosslinking, forming a network structure that enhances the cohesiveness of the meat. However, as water and other substances from the brine permeate the duck meat, the cohesiveness decreases. Beginning on Day 4, the sample undergoes air-drying, which leads to moisture loss and slight drying of the meat, causing a reduction in cohesiveness (Fig. 2E). Resilience decreases with prolonged curing time, though it shows a slight increase during the drying process (Days 4–6). The reduction in resilience is attributed to the loss of protein content as curing progresses. However, during the air-drying phase, the muscle fiber structure becomes firmer, and the changes in chemical composition stabilize, resulting in a slight recovery in resilience. (Fig. 2F)

Electronic nose analysis

Sensors W1C, W6S, W2S, and W1S showed the highest responses, indicating a strong presence of aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and acids in the sauced duck samples, with significant changes in these compounds over time. Previous studies have also observed significant responses in W1S and W2S sensors when analyzing meat products (Qu, et al., 2024). These results suggest that the concentrations of aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones increase rapidly during the curing process. (Fig. 3A)

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to further analyze the electronic nose responses for samples taken at different stages of sauced duck curing. The first principal component accounted for 77.7 % of the variance, while the second accounted for 11.7 %, effectively capturing most of the variation in the data and distinguishing the different samples. Color-coded regions in the PCA plot summarize odor profiles across curing times. The distances between these regions reflect the differences in the samples' aroma profiles. As shown in Fig. 3B, the major components of the electronic nose responses successfully distinguished the four sauced duck samples at different curing stages.

Sensory evaluation of sauced duck during curing

Sensory scores increased overall across curing stages. From Day 0 to Day 3, the scores rose rapidly, while the increase slowed and stabilized from Day 4 to Day 6 (Fig. 4). In terms of color, the sauced duck showed significant changes during the initial curing stage. The color transformation was most pronounced between Day 0 and Day 1, followed by a slower yet steady enhancement from Day 2 to Day 6. Initially, the duck meat had a pale, glossy appearance. As soy-sauce components (e.g., melanoidins, Maillard pigments) permeated the meat over time, the surface took on a deep reddish-brown hue (Furusawa, Goto, Satoh, Nomi and Murata, 2013; Noda, Amano, Shimamura and Murata, 2020). Consequently, the duck's color became more vibrant as the curing time increased.

Sensory evaluation of sauced duck samples at different curing times across seven attributes: Color and lustre, Fishy smell, Sauce aroma, Fatty aroma, Spicy aroma, Ester aroma, Alcohol aroma.

Aroma is a crucial quality factor for sauced duck. The sensory profile of the duck includes six primary aromas: Fishy smell, soy sauce aroma, fat aroma, spice aroma, ester aroma, and alcohol aroma. The results indicated that Fishy smell decreased continuously with curing time, with a rapid decline during the soaking phase and a slower reduction during the air-drying stage. The soy sauce aroma intensified during soaking but slightly diminished during air-drying. The fat aroma showed a declining trend during soaking but increased slightly during drying. The spice aroma increased as the spices infused the meat during soaking but showed a slight decrease during drying. The ester aroma continuously increased during curing, with the sharpest rise during drying. The alcohol aroma intensified throughout soaking and slightly decreased during air-drying. Initially, the duck's aroma was dominated by its natural scent, which included mild fat and fishy notes, with no significant additional aromas, leading to lower sensory scores. As the duck soaked in the brine, flavors from the soy sauce, rice wine, sugar, salt, star anise, and cinnamon infiltrated the muscle fibers and myofibrillar network (Jiao, Zhang, Chen, Yang, Shi, Liu, Wang, Lu and Hui, 2022), enriching the duck's soy sauce, spice, and alcohol aromas while reducing the fishy odor. The fat aroma declined due to lipid hydrolysis and the breakdown of fats by enzymes and microorganisms, releasing aroma precursors that weakened the perceived fat intensity (Liu, et al., 2019a; Mediani, Hamezah, Jam, Mahadi, Chan, Rohani, Che Lah, Azlan, Khairul Annuar, Azman, Bunawan, Sarian, Kamal and Abas, 2022). During the air-drying phase, the duck was exposed to higher temperatures and oxygen, which accelerated lipid oxidation. The formation of ester compounds increased as lipid hydrolysis intensified over time (Qu, Xi, Li, Yang, Chen, Jin and Yan, 2024), particularly during drying, when lipid oxidation accelerated, further promoting esterification reactions and the creation of complex aroma profiles.

HS-SPME-GC-MS analysis of key aroma compounds during the curing of sauced duck

The analysis of volatile compounds in the sauced duck samples during curing was conducted using HS-SPME-GC-MS. A total of 82 volatiles were identified, including aldehydes (17), alcohols (15), ketones (7), acids (13), benzenes (8), esters (12), alkanes (8), alkenes (1),and furans (1). Retention indices were calculated using C7–C30 n-alkanes under identical conditions (Table S1). Among these, n-hexadecanoic acid showed the highest content at the end of curing, followed by 2,4-decadienal and nonanal. Compounds such as oleic acid, 2(3H)-furanone, 9-octadecenoic acid, and 2-methylhexacosane exhibited a decreasing trend during the curing process, while n-hexadecanoic acid, 2,4-decadienal, nonanal, 9-octadecenoic acid ethyl ester, and 1-octen-3-ol showed an increasing trend. To further investigate the differences in aroma profiles over various curing durations, orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to analyze the signal responses of the volatile compounds. OPLS-DA (supervised) enhanced group separation and identified discriminatory volatiles. This model also allowed a more intuitive comparison of the samples at different curing times, helping us visualize stage-dependent changes in aroma composition. As shown in Fig. 5A, samples from the same curing time clustered closely, while those from different curing times exhibited significant differences. It was also observed that the samples from Day 5 and Day 6 clustered more closely, which aligns with the earlier observation that sauced duck curing was largely completed by Day 5. Additionally, permutation tests were used to validate the OPLS-DA model, confirming its reliability and stability. The R2 value was greater than the Q2 value, with the R2 regression line having a positive intercept and the Q2 line having a negative intercept, indicating a well-fitting model (Fig. 5B). Although OPLS-DA provided useful visualization and ranking of discriminating volatiles, we acknowledge that this supervised method has limitations, such as potential risk of overfitting and dependency on sample size. Therefore, we combined it with permutation testing and univariate statistics to reduce bias and improve reliability.

The primary differential metabolites (VIP>1) across the curing process were 9-octadecenoic acid, dotriacontane, oleic acid, n-hexadecanoic acid, tetradecanoic acid, sorbic acid, 9-octadecenoic acid ethyl ester, octadecanoic acid, 2,4-decadienal, phenylethyl alcohol, anethole, 2,3-octanedione, phenylacetaldehyde, octadecanal, 2-nonenal, hexadecanal, decanal, palmitoleic acid, nonanal, 2-undecenal, furan, 2-pentyl-, 2-methylhexacosane and 2-decenal (Fig. 5D). Oleic acid showed significant reductions, likely due to their high susceptibility to oxidation, as they are unsaturated fatty acids (Schmid, et al., 2006). Oleic acid, a common fatty acid, is found in the fat and muscle tissues of animals. Tetradecanoic acid, a saturated fatty acid, significantly influences the flavor and quality of sauced duck, and its variation is associated with the breakdown of muscle tissue fats during curing, particularly during the air-drying phase (Liu, Liu and Chen, 2019b; Qu, et al., 2022).

Alcohols in sauced duck mainly result from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. These alcohols, especially unsaturated ones, have a low odor threshold and contribute to the characteristic aroma of sauced duck. Saturated alcohols, on the other hand, contribute less to the flavor. 1-Hexanol, 1-octanol, and 1-octen-3-ol are important components in sauced duck, and their significance in meat products has also been confirmed by Domínguez et al. (Domínguez, et al., 2019). 1-Octanol is a straight-chain saturated alcohol produced through the oxidation of oleic acid esters (Benet, et al., 2015), while 1-hexanol is formed via the reduction of hexanal (Montanari, et al., 2018). 1-Octen-3-ol, known for its low odor threshold, is generated through lipase-catalyzed reactions and the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids. Its presence in sauced duck may stem from both the duck itself and the added Chinese rice wine (Huangjiu) (Gómez and Lorenzo, 2013; Yuan, et al., 2023). Although present in relatively low concentrations, these compounds play a crucial role in shaping the overall flavor profile of sauced duck. The rise of alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol and phenylethanol in the later curing stage explains the increasing mushroom-like and floral nuances. Aldehydes, major products of lipid and amino acid degradation, are key aroma compounds in meat products, including duck meat (Domínguez, Purriños, Pérez-Santaescolástica, Pateiro, Barba, Tomasevic, Campagnol and Lorenzo, 2019). They are present at high concentrations with low odor thresholds, making a substantial contribution to the sauced duck's aroma (Mediani, Hamezah, Jam, Mahadi, Chan, Rohani, Che Lah, Azlan, Khairul Annuar, Azman, Bunawan, Sarian, Kamal and Abas, 2022). Unsaturated aldehydes like 2,4-decadienal, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal, and 2-undecenal increased throughout the curing process, adding to the duck’s unique flavor profile. Benzaldehyde and phenylethyl aldehyde, which impart bitter almond and hyacinth-like aromas, respectively (Martinez-Onandi, et al., 2017; Petricevic, et al., 2018), were likely derived from spice ingredients, such as star anise and cinnamon, used during curing. Benzaldehyde may also be produced through the Strecker degradation of phenylalanine or its metabolites (Lorenzo and Carballo, 2015), while phenylethyl aldehyde is likely produced from tyrosine via similar degradation pathways. These aldehydes, with low odor thresholds, were especially important in shaping the green, fatty and fried notes of sauced duck during curing. Esters, which are typically formed by reactions between free fatty acids and alcohols, are important for the fruity and floral aromas in sauced duck. A total of 13 esters were detected, including octanoic acid ethyl ester, decanoic acid ethyl ester, and 9-octadecenoic acid ethyl ester, which contribute significantly to the fruity notes of the product. Ethanol, added through rice wine during the curing process, served as an essential precursor for the formation of ethyl esters. The formation of ethyl esters from ethanol and fatty acids contributed fruity and pleasant notes that balanced the fatty aldehydes. Ketones were detected in lower concentrations due to their generally high odor thresholds and limited production under the curing conditions of sauced duck. The curing temperature of 18°C and lower humidity during air-drying likely inhibited the microbial activity responsible for ketone formation. Although acids were relatively abundant, many had higher thresholds, limiting impact on the sauced duck’s aroma. Additionally, heterocyclic compounds were detected in low concentrations and made minimal contributions to the overall flavor profile.

Overall, the curing process led to clear stage-dependent differences in aroma composition. In the early curing period, aldehydes such as hexanal, nonanal and heptanal accumulated rapidly, giving grassy, fatty and slightly fishy notes. With longer curing, unsaturated aldehydes (2,4-decadienal, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal) increased, contributing more intense fatty and fried aromas, while alcohols such as 1-octen-3-ol and phenylethanol, together with ketones (2-octanone, 1-octen-3-one), imparted mushroom-like, floral and buttery nuances. Spices added in the marinade (e.g. anethole, benzaldehyde, eugenol) remained detectable or were transformed, enhancing clove- and herbal-like notes. At the same time, some fishy amines decreased, which improved the sensory balance. These changes can be explained by several factors: (i) marination and seasoning—salt and spices promote lipid oxidation and release of spice-derived volatiles; (ii) enzymatic activity—lipoxygenase and microbial enzymes catalyze degradation of unsaturated fatty acids into aldehydes, alcohols and ketones; (iii) time and moisture loss—progressive dehydration concentrates volatiles and favors oxidative pathways. Together, these processes transform sauced duck aroma from raw/meaty and slightly fishy in the early stage into a layered profile with fatty, spicy, floral and umami-like characteristics at the later stage.

The aforementioned research primarily focused on the raw aroma characteristics of uncooked duck meat. In subsequent experiments, we will further investigate how additional cooking processes influence aroma profiles to more accurately reflect consumer perceptions.

OAV analysis of aroma compounds in sauced duck

OAVs assessed each compound’s contribution to aroma. A total of 23 compounds with OAV values >1 were identified, including acids (2), aldehydes (11), esters (3), ketones (2), alcohols (4), and benzenes (1) (Table 3). Among them, 9 compounds, including heptanal, Phenylacetaldehyde, nonanal, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal, 2-decenal, 1-octen-3-one, and 2-heptenal, exhibited OAVs >10, suggesting that they may play a key role in defining the sauced duck's core flavor profile.

Aldehydes, known to be key contributors to meat aromas, played a predominant role in the flavor of sauced duck, in line with findings from studies on other cured meats such as dry-cured ham (Purrinos, et al., 2012), smoked duck (Lou, et al., 2018), and ham (Marusic, et al., 2014; Petricevic, Radovcic, Lukic, Listes and Medic, 2018). These compounds mainly originate from the oxidation of lipids and fatty acids, as well as from Maillard-induced amino acid degradation (Qu, Xi, Li, Yang, Chen, Jin and Yan, 2024). Aldehydes with OAVs >10, such as heptanal, phenylacetaldehyde, nonanal, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal, and 2-decenal, significantly contributed to the sauced duck’s flavor. For example, heptanal and nonanal, formed from the oxidation of oleic acid, play a vital role due to their low odor thresholds, imparting rich, fatty, and slightly citrusy or floral notes to the product (Benet, Guàrdia, Ibañez, Solà, Arnau and Roura, 2015; Nunes, et al., 2008). Aldehydes were primary contributors to roasted/fatty notes in sauced duck, with additional plant-like aromas such as cucumber, melon, and orange peel, as well as floral hints like lavender and rose, enhancing the complexity of the flavor. Alcohols were also crucial contributors to the flavor, particularly 1-octen-3-ol, which is commonly referred to as "mushroom alcohol" due to its distinct mushroom aroma, along with lavender and licorice undertones. Its low odor threshold makes it a significant flavor contributor in duck meat (Qu, Xi, Li, Yang, Chen, Jin and Yan, 2024). Other key alcohols with OAV >1 included 1-octanol, 2-octen-1-ol, and phenylethyl alcohol. These alcohols impart diverse aroma profiles, with 1-octanol contributing a sweet, waxy, fatty scent, 2-octen-1-ol offering camphor, citrus, and mushroom-like notes, and phenylethyl alcohol contributing a rose-like fragrance. Acids, esters, and ketones made relatively minor contributions to the overall flavor of the final sauced duck. Compounds such as octanoic acid ethyl ester, 9-octadecenoic acid, octadecanoic acid, and 2-octanone were primarily found during the curing process rather than in the finished product. Anethole, a key component of the sauced duck flavor, is a monoterpene compound with a sweet, licorice-like aroma, and is characteristic of star anise, a major spice used in the curing process. This finding aligns with previous studies on meat products and spices (Domínguez, Purriños, Pérez-Santaescolástica, Pateiro, Barba, Tomasevic, Campagnol and Lorenzo, 2019). As the curing process progresses, anethole penetrates the duck meat, becoming a key contributor to its distinctive flavor.

In summary, this study demonstrated that some of the flavor compounds in sauced duck increased significantly during the curing process, particularly aldehydes and ketones. These compounds are closely related to lipid oxidation, indicating that the sauced duck flavor evolves mainly through alterations in its lipid profile. The lipid-related reactions include both lipid oxidation and lipid hydrolysis, which are fundamental to shaping the flavor of the final product.

Our HS-SPME (80°C) conditions align with established practice in meat and cured-meat aroma studies (Domínguez, Purriños, Pérez-Santaescolástica, Pateiro, Barba, Tomasevic, Campagnol and Lorenzo, 2019; Jiang, Zhang, Ye, Qian, Li, Zhao and Bai, 2022; Lou, Zhang, Sun, Wang, Pan and Cao, 2018). Importantly, no excessive formation of pyrazines or other heterocyclics was detected, suggesting that “re-cooking” artefacts did not occur under these conditions. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that HS-SPME at elevated temperature may overestimate certain reactive aldehydes compared with low-temperature, high-vacuum techniques such as Solvent-Assisted Flavor Evaporation (SAFE). SAFE is particularly advantageous for thermolabile and very volatile compounds, and future comparative studies employing SAFE alongside HS-SPME would be valuable for expanding the coverage of aroma-active compounds in Sauced Duck.

Aroma recombination analysis of sauced duck

The importance of the flavor compounds in sauced duck is determined not only by their concentrations but also by their OAV (Odor Activity Value). While OAV values provide insight into the key compounds influencing the duck's flavor, the overall aroma cannot be solely explained by OAV. Matrix effects, aroma release, and interactions between compounds must also be considered. The distinct aroma of sauced duck is likely the result of the complex interplay between core flavor compounds rather than the influence of any single compound. To evaluate the contribution of each key flavor compound to the overall aroma, we selected sauced duck samples from the fifth day of curing as the control group (CK) and conducted aroma recombination and omission experiments.

GC-MS and panel results indicated dominant floral, citrus, meaty/fatty, waxy, and cucumber-like nuances. These are likely key contributors to the overall aroma profile. We constructed Recombination Model 1 by adding all detected aroma-active compounds from the fifth-day sample to a flavorless matrix prepared from sauced duck meat. This model was then evaluated by a trained sensory panel, comparing the reconstructed aroma (Model 1) with that of the CK sample. Recombination Model 2 included only 17 aroma-active compounds with OAVs >1, which we hypothesized to be the primary contributors to the characteristic flavor of sauced duck. These compounds included pentadecanoic acid, benzaldehyde, anethole, heptanal, 1-octanol, nonanal, phenylacetaldehyde, 2-octen-1-ol, 2,4-decadienal, 2-octenal, 2,4-hexadienoic acid, ethyl ester, 2-nonenal, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-decenal, 1-octen-3-one, 2-heptenal, and decanal. Sensory evaluation revealed that the aroma of Model 2 closely matched the overall aroma impression of the sauced duck, indicating that these 17 compounds are likely key contributors to its distinctive flavor. Further narrowing the focus, we selected 10 core flavor compounds for additional recombination experiments (Model 3): anethole, heptanal, nonanal, phenylacetaldehyde, 2-octenal, 2-nonenal, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-decenal, 1-octen-3-one, and 2-heptenal. Model 3 retained high similarity scores, albeit with fewer nuances. This suggests that these 10 compounds can effectively reproduce the core flavor of sauced duck (Fig. 6). Previous studies have confirmed that hexanal, nonanal, and 1-octen-3-ol are core flavor compounds in meat products, particularly in duck, chicken, and lamb (Liu, et al., 2022, 2019a). It has been shown that while many volatile compounds may be present in food, the key aroma compounds responsible for defining the characteristic flavor are typically concentrated in a small subset of these compounds (Liu, Li, Hamid, Li, Sun, Wang, Liu, Ma, Sun and Gong, 2023). This study demonstrates that sauced duck's distinctive flavor is primarily driven by a limited number of core aroma compounds, emphasizing the complexity and importance of aroma interactions in flavor formation.

Conclusion

This study systematically dissected the curing process of traditional Chinese sauced duck through physicochemical analysis, E-nose, sensory evaluation, HS-SPME-GC-MS, and aroma recombination–omission models. Significant changes in pH, water-holding capacity, protein, fat, color, and texture were observed, along with notable shifts in aroma profiles during the first five days. A total of 82 volatile compounds were identified, among which 10 (including anethole, heptanal, nonanal, and 1-octen-3-ol) were confirmed as the key contributors to the characteristic flavor. These findings clarify the mechanisms of flavor formation and provide a scientific basis for optimizing curing time, enhancing product quality, and promoting the standardization and industrial application of sauced duck. A limitation is the lack of direct FAA/FFA measurements, and future studies incorporating targeted metabolite analyses will provide deeper mechanistic insights.
