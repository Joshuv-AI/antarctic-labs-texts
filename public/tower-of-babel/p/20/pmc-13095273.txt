Effect of caudal block injection speed on optic nerve sheath diameter in pediatric surgeries: A prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Açelya Toprak Karaoğlu, Rasim Onur Karaoğlu, Volkan Özen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perioperative pain management in children is challenging due to limited communication and the risk of long-term psychological effects. Regional anesthesia, particularly the caudal block (CB), is frequently used in pediatric surgery for lower abdominal and urogenital procedures. However, the influence of injection speed during CB on intracranial and hemodynamic responses remains unclear. This prospective observational cohort study included 64 pediatric patients (American Society of Anesthesiologists I–II, aged 1–7 years) undergoing elective lower abdominal or urogenital surgery under general anesthesia. All patients received a CB with 0.8 mL/kg of 0.125% bupivacaine. Injection speed was not assigned by the study protocol but reflected routine clinical practice, and patients were categorized according to the injection speed used (0.25 mL/s or 0.5 mL/s). The primary outcome was the change in optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) over time and between injection-speed groups. Secondary outcomes included heart rate and mean arterial pressure, recorded at baseline and at predefined time points after the block. Baseline ONSD values were similar between groups. ONSD increased transiently after caudal injection in both groups, with a significantly greater increase in the fast-injection group immediately after the block. These differences were short-lived, and ONSD values returned toward baseline within 10 to 20 minutes, with no persistent intergroup differences. Heart rate and mean arterial pressure showed mild, transient reductions in both groups, which were slightly more pronounced in the fast-injection group but remained within physiological limits. Different caudal injection speeds were associated with distinct transient changes in ONSD and hemodynamic parameters. Slower injection was associated with more gradual physiological responses during the early postinjection period.

1. Introduction

Perioperative pain management in pediatric patients presents unique challenges due to difficulties in pain communication and the need to prevent long-term psychological consequences.[1,2] Regional anesthesia plays a critical role in pediatric surgery, with caudal block (CB) being one of the most commonly preferred techniques for lower abdominal, urogenital, and lower extremity surgeries.[3,4] First described by Campbell in 1933, the CB has been widely adopted as a safe method due to its effectiveness in providing intraoperative and postoperative analgesia.[5] The prominent sacral anatomy in children allows easy identification of the sacral hiatus, contributing to a high success rate of CB; even beginners can achieve roughly an 80% success rate after about 32 attempts.[6] CBs are often combined with general anesthesia; depending on the volume of local anesthetic, the analgesic effect can extend from the sacral level up to T11 to T6 dermatomes.[7–9]

Despite its efficacy, the CB is not completely free of risk in terms of intracranial pressure (ICP). Epidural injection can cause displacement of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cranially, leading to a transient rise in ICP. According to the Monro-Kellie doctrine, the intracranial compartment has a fixed volume; an increase in the volume of 1 component (brain, blood, or CSF) must be compensated by a decrease in one or both of the others. Injecting local anesthetic in the caudal epidural space can exert pressure on the dural sac, displacing CSF toward the cranium and thereby causing a temporary ICP increase.[10]

Although this ICP increase is usually minor and brief, it could pose a risk to patients with conditions associated with reduced intracranial compliance, such as intracranial masses, hydrocephalus, or traumatic brain injury. In otherwise healthy children, this risk is considered minimal. It is recommended to monitor surrogate markers of ICP during procedures that could raise ICP.[11]

Ultrasound measurement of optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) has emerged as a noninvasive method for real-time assessment of ICP in both adult and pediatric patients.[12] The optic nerve sheath expands in response to acute increases in ICP, and numerous studies have shown that changes in ONSD correlate with ICP fluctuations.[13,14] Because the subarachnoid space of the optic nerve is continuous with intracranial CSF, an elevated ICP causes distension of the nerve sheath; the region about 3 mm behind the optic disc is the most sensitive to these changes.[15] ONSD ultrasonography is often preferred over invasive methods such as intracranial catheters or lumbar puncture. However, ONSD measurement can be operator-dependent, and variability in measurement technique and image quality can affect results.[16] For this reason, standardized protocols including dual-plane measurements and color Doppler (for example, the color Doppler ONSD evaluation protocol) have been recommended to improve consistency.[17,18]

With the increasing use of ONSD monitoring in anesthesia and intensive care, it has become important to investigate how procedures like CB affect ONSD and, therefore, ICP. In particular, the speed of local anesthetic injection during a CB may influence the degree of CSF displacement and the magnitude of ICP change. Previous studies have mostly focused on injection volume, and the effect of injection speed on ICP dynamics has not been sufficiently investigated. Rapid injections may cause a sudden cranial shift of CSF and a sharp transient peak in ICP, whereas slow injections may allow for more gradual pressure equilibration and lower ICP fluctuations.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of CB injection speed on changes in ONSD in pediatric patients. Secondary objectives included the assessment of hemodynamic changes (heart rate [HR] and mean arterial pressure [MAP]), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and the incidence of adverse events, including nausea, vomiting, urinary retention, and motor block.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and ethics approval

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary pediatric anesthesia center in SBU Prof. Dr. Cemil Taşcioğlu City Hospital between July and November 2023. The study protocol was approved by the İstanbul Medipol University Ethics Committee under number E-10840098-772.02-3946 (23.06.2023) and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all participants.

2.2. Patient selection

Eligible participants were children aged 1 to 7 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I to II who were scheduled to undergo elective lower abdominal or urogenital procedures suitable for caudal anesthesia. Only pediatric patients who received a CB with a standardized local anesthetic volume of 0.8 mL/kg as part of routine clinical practice were eligible for inclusion in the study. Cases in which different caudal volumes were used were not considered for enrollment. Exclusion criteria included preexisting intracranial or ocular pathology, contraindications to caudal anesthesia, and incomplete intraoperative data.

2.3. Anesthesia protocol and CB

A standard anesthesia protocol was applied to all patients. All patients received oral midazolam 0.5 mg/kg (maximum 15 mg) 20 to 30 minutes before transfer to the operating room for premedication. This allowed smooth separation from parents and facilitated intravenous cannulation, reducing the need for inhalational induction in younger children. In the operating room, standard monitoring was established, and an intravenous line was placed. General anesthesia was induced with propofol 2 mg/kg and fentanyl 1 µg/kg; rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg was given before intubation. Patients were mechanically ventilated (tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, FiO2 40%, sevoflurane inhalational anesthesia) with end-tidal CO2 (EtCO2) maintained between 35 and 40 mm Hg. Positive end-expiratory pressure was set at 3 cmH2O, and core body temperature was kept within normal limits. To minimize physiological confounding, ventilation parameters (tidal volume, positive end-expiratory pressure, and respiratory rate) and EtCO2 were kept constant throughout the measurement periods. Sevoflurane concentration (0.8–1.2 minimum alveolar concentration) was adjusted only to maintain an adequate depth of anesthesia and remained stable during ONSD measurements.

CB was performed using a standard landmark-based technique with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. After identification of the sacral hiatus, a single-shot caudal injection was administered using a 22-gauge short-bevel needle. Correct needle placement was confirmed by negative aspiration for blood or CSF before injection. No ultrasound guidance was used.

In our operating room, CBs are routinely supervised by 1 of 2 attending anesthesiologists, each of whom follows a consistent personal practice regarding injection speed during caudal anesthesia. Although 2 attendings were involved, the technical performance of all CBs, including needle placement and local anesthetic injection, was carried out by the anesthesia resident on duty in all cases, following the supervising attending’s usual instructions. Importantly, injection speed was not assigned, modified, or influenced by the study protocol, and no attempt was made to standardize or alter individual practice patterns for research purposes. Patients were therefore grouped retrospectively according to the injection speed that naturally occurred as part of routine clinical care: approximately 0.25 mL/s (group S) or 0.5 mL/s (group F). The concentration and total volume of local anesthetic were identical in all patients, and injection speed represented the only procedural variable that differed between groups. Notably, these injection speeds fall within the range of caudal injection rates previously reported in pediatric anesthesia literature, supporting their relevance to routine clinical practice.[19,20]

2.4. ONSD measurement protocol

The ONSD was measured at 4 predetermined time points (T0, T1, T2, and T3) using a 5 to 10 MHz linear probe on a Mindray DC-7 (Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) ultrasound device. All ONSD measurements were performed by a single experienced operator who was blinded to group assignment to prevent interobserver variability. A standardized scanning protocol was applied for accuracy and reproducibility. With the upper eyelid closed and adequate gel to avoid excessive pressure on the eye, the probe was placed on the eyelid. At each measurement, the diameter was measured 3 mm behind the optic disc in both the transverse and sagittal planes; this region is the most sensitive to ICP changes. The average of the 2-plane measurements was calculated, and consistency was enhanced by using the color Doppler ONSD evaluation protocol. To test measurement reliability, images from a randomly selected subset of 10 cases were measured again by a second operator who was blind to the original measurements. The intraclass correlation coefficient between these paired measurements was 0.78, indicating excellent interobserver agreement. The measurement time points were as follows:

At each time point, ONSD was measured for both eyes, and the mean value was used for analysis. HR and MAP were recorded at the same time points. Respiratory parameters (oxygen saturation, peak inspiratory pressure [PIP], and EtCO2) were continuously monitored throughout the procedure to assess patient stability and identify any respiratory changes related to the block.

3. Outcomes

3.1. Primary outcome

The primary outcome of the study was the change in ONSD over time in response to the CB. ONSD values were compared between the 2 injection-speed groups (0.25 mL/s and 0.5 mL/s) at 4 predefined time points (T0–T3).

4. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Python (version 3.9.12; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington). Continuous variables (ONSD, HR, MAP, etc) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were summarized as frequency or percentage. Independent-sample t tests were used to compare continuous variables between group S and group F, and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess within-group changes over time (T0–T3); if ANOVA was significant, Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For repeated-measures ANOVA, partial eta squared (ηp2) was calculated and reported as an estimate of effect size to enhance transparency in statistical reporting. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Results were reported with the corresponding P values. In addition, 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the mean ONSD at each time point. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion (only cases with complete data were included in the analysis). Listwise deletion was used because missingness resulted from incomplete intraoperative recordings, occurred completely at random, and affected fewer than 10% of patients; therefore, imputation was not necessary for the primary outcome analysis.

5. Results

5.1. Patient flow and baseline characteristics

Of the 76 patients enrolled, 64 (32 per group) completed the study and were included in the final analysis. Twelve patients were excluded due to failed block or incomplete intraoperative data. The 2 groups were comparable in age, weight, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, and surgical distribution, with no significant baseline differences (Fig. 1). The majority of children underwent inguinal hernia, hydrocele, or hypospadias surgery.

Patient flow and baseline demographic and surgical characteristics of the study population. No significant differences were observed between groups for any baseline demographic or surgical variable (all P > .05). ASA = American Society of Anesthesiologists, SD = standard deviation.

5.2. Primary outcome: ONSD changes

Baseline ONSD values were nearly identical between the groups. Immediately after the CB (T1), ONSD increased in both groups; however, the increase was greater in the fast-injection group (group F), resulting in a significant between-group difference at T1 (P = .042). This difference resolved by T2 and T3, when ONSD values converged and returned toward baseline in both groups (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that the effect of injection speed on ONSD was transient and limited to the immediate postinjection period.

Changes in optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD, mm) over time at 4 measurement points (T0–T3). Data are shown as mean ± 95% CI for group S and group F. CI = confidence interval.

5.3. Hemodynamic parameters

Both groups remained hemodynamically stable. HR and MAP decreased mildly after the block in both groups, reflecting the expected sympathetic blockade. At T2, group F showed slightly greater reductions in HR and MAP compared with group S; although statistically significant, these differences remained within normal physiological ranges and required no therapeutic intervention. By T3, hemodynamic values were similar between groups (Fig. 3).

Mean HR and MAP values measured at T0 to T3. Values are expressed as mean ± SD. HR = heart rate, MAP = mean arterial pressure, SD = standard deviation.

5.4. Respiratory parameters

Respiratory parameters remained stable throughout the study period in both groups. SpO2 was consistently maintained at ≥96% under controlled ventilation. EtCO2 values were kept within the predefined target range of 35 to 40 mm Hg, and no significant between-group differences were observed at T1, T2, or T3. PIP similarly remained stable, without clinically relevant changes or intergroup variation. Importantly, no episodes of hypoventilation, respiratory depression, airway obstruction, desaturation, or ventilation-related complications occurred during the monitoring period.

6. Discussion

The observed transient increase in ONSD with faster injection rates is consistent with previous experimental and clinical observations. Triffterer et al[19] demonstrated that faster caudal injections were associated with more abrupt cranial spread of local anesthetic in pediatric patients, suggesting a rapid CSF displacement mechanism. Similarly, Gönen et al[10] reported greater ONSD expansion with higher CB volumes, further supporting the relationship between injection dynamics and transient ICP changes. However, the long-term clinical significance of these transient ICP elevations remains unclear, particularly in patients with normal intracranial compliance.

The larger ONSD expansion observed with the faster injection rate suggests a measurable physiological response, although the clinical significance of such transient changes remains uncertain. Children have higher brain water content and more compliant skulls, making them both more adaptable to volume changes and more sensitive to sudden ICP increases. Previous studies have highlighted that small ICP increases, as measured by ONSD, can adversely affect cerebral perfusion or increase the risk of complications in vulnerable patients. Our findings are consistent with those of Yayik et al,[21] who observed a similar transient ONSD increase without hemodynamic changes following CB in children, and with Castillo et al,[22] who demonstrated that high-volume caudal injections can measurably alter cerebral blood flow and oxygenation, supporting the concept that epidural injection dynamics can influence cerebral hemodynamics even in the absence of overt neurological signs. Our findings confirm these concerns: the 0.5 mL/s injection likely caused a more abrupt CSF shift and a larger instantaneous rise in ICP. In contrast, the 0.25 mL/s injection delivered the same volume more gradually, mitigating this effect. These findings suggest that injection speed may influence the magnitude of transient ICP-related changes. Lee et al[23] similarly reported that larger CB volumes produced greater ONSD increases in pediatric patients, highlighting that both injection volume and speed can influence transient ICP changes. Although caudal or epidural anesthesia is generally contraindicated in patients with hydrocephalus, intracranial tumors, or traumatic brain injury, these conditions illustrate how reduced intracranial compliance can exacerbate ICP rises following rapid epidural injection in experimental or theoretical contexts.[24] A slower injection may allow more gradual redistribution of CSF and reduce this risk. From a technical perspective, injection speed represents a modifiable procedural factor that may influence short-term physiological responses during caudal anesthesia.

Hemodynamic responses also showed slight differences related to injection speed. While both groups remained stable, slow injection resulted in more gradual decreases in MAP and HR, whereas fast injection caused a sharper decline at 10 minutes. These differences were small and clinically insignificant, but they suggest that rapid injection might transiently accelerate the sympathetic block effect and anesthesia spread. Avoiding abrupt hemodynamic changes may be desirable in pediatric anesthesia, particularly in vulnerable patient populations. Therefore, in these subgroups, choosing a slower injection rate could allow more controlled hemodynamic effects. Overall, our data indicate that although both speeds are acceptable, the slower speed better modulates physiologic responses in children.

Notably, the ONSD differences disappeared within 10 to 20 minutes. By T2 and T3, ONSD had equalized between the groups, indicating that the ICP increase from the CB was short-lived. Our findings align with previous observations that the effects of caudal anesthesia on ICP in pediatric patients are transient. Once the initial pressure wave from the injection subsides, compensatory mechanisms likely restore normal CSF distribution within minutes. However, even a brief ICP peak can be significant in patients with reduced compliance; thus, minimizing the peak ONSD (and ICP) by using a slower injection is a reasonable precaution. These results support the principle of using gentle techniques to increase safety margins in pediatric anesthesia.

Our study has limitations. First, the follow-up period was short; ONSD and physiological changes were observed only for the first 20 minutes after the block. Potential delayed effects in the postoperative or postanesthesia period were not evaluated. Future studies should extend the observation period to examine late effects of injection speed on ICP and neurological outcomes. Second, the sample included a broad age range (1–7 years), but we did not perform subgroup analyses by age or weight. Neonates and older children differ in brain water content and cranial compliance, which can affect CSF pressure dynamics.[25] The responses of very young children (particularly <3 years) may differ. Our study was not powered to detect age-specific effects, so larger future studies should analyze outcomes by age or weight. Third, some confounding factors were not controlled. Factors such as arterial CO2 tension and depth of anesthesia can influence cerebral blood flow and ICP. Future studies with strict control of these parameters could more clearly isolate the effect of injection speed. Although EtCO2 and ventilation settings were kept within predefined targets, arterial CO2 tension and the exact depth of anesthesia were not recorded, which may have influenced cerebral hemodynamics to a minor degree. ONSD measurements were performed by a single experienced operator using a standardized protocol, which minimized measurement variability. Although a limited interobserver assessment was conducted in a subset of patients, future studies including multiple operators could further strengthen reproducibility. In addition, our population was limited to children without intracranial pathology; children with hydrocephalus, brain tumors, or brain injury may experience more pronounced ICP increases with epidural injections. Special studies are needed to determine safe injection practices in these high-risk groups. Finally, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed during the study design phase, which represents a limitation of this observational study. Although our sample size was sufficient to detect the observed differences, it was still limited. A larger study would increase statistical power, allow identification of rare adverse events, and enable analysis of injection speed effects by age or type of surgery. Therefore, further studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and inform clinical guidelines. However, the number of studies directly comparing different injection speeds is limited. Our findings also align with recent large-scale safety analyses. Xu et al reviewed pediatric regional anesthesia data from over 100,000 cases and confirmed that CBs are safe and reliable, with no permanent neurological deficits reported and a very low overall complication rate.[26] These results reinforce that the transient ONSD changes and the absence of hemodynamic instability observed in our study are consistent with the broader safety profile of caudal anesthesia in children. Integrating data from both volume-based[10,21] and speed-based[19] investigations may provide a more comprehensive understanding of how the CB technique influences ICP-related parameters in pediatric patients.

7. Conclusions

In conclusion, different caudal injection speeds were associated with distinct but transient changes in ONSD and hemodynamic parameters in pediatric patients. Slower injection was associated with more gradual early physiological responses, while faster injection produced a greater immediate increase in ONSD. These findings highlight injection speed as a modifiable technical factor that may influence short-term physiological responses during caudal anesthesia.
