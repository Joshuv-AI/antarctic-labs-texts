Longitudinal trajectories of stroke risk among Chinese adults: a four-year community screening cohort study
Authors: Xiaoci He, Shuping Zhao, Xiaomeng He, Feng Li, Qian Wang, Xu Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeStroke remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, with a growing burden in China. Traditional stroke risk assessments are often static, relying on single-time point measurements. This study aimed to identify heterogeneous longitudinal stroke risk trajectories and examine factors influencing these trajectories in a Chinese community cohort.MethodsWe used data from the National Stroke Screening and Intervention Program, which followed 1,376 participants across three waves (2018, 2020, 2022). Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was applied to identify stroke risk trajectories based on cumulative risk scoring. We evaluated associations between baseline characteristics and trajectory group membership using multinomial logistic regression.ResultsThree distinct stroke risk trajectories were identified: (1) Stable low-risk (41.42%); (2) Increasing-risk (41.21%); (3) Stable high-risk (17.37%). Significant baseline differences in demographic, lifestyle, and health-related factors were observed across the three groups, including drinking habits, taste preference, dietary pattern, fruit intake frequency, waist circumference and homocysteine levels. Multinomial logistic regression revealed that older age, frequent heavy drinking, vegetarian diet preference, larger waist circumference, and higher homocysteine levels were significantly associated with higher odds of progressing to the increasing-risk and stable high-risk trajectories.ConclusionsThis study identified heterogeneous stroke risk trajectories among Chinese adults over time. The identification of individuals on an increasing-risk trajectory may provide an opportunity for earlier intervention. These findings suggest the potential utility of trajectory-based approaches for improving stroke risk stratification and identifying individuals who may benefit from earlier and more targeted prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27939-4.

Introduction

Stroke remains the third leading cause of death and the fourth leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide, according to the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study [1]. A substantial proportion of this burden occurs in low- and middle-income countries, with China accounting for nearly one-quarter of global incident cases and almost one-third of stroke-related DALYs [2, 3]. Over the past three decades, the burden of stroke disease in China is on an explosive growth trend, largely driven by urbanization, population aging, unhealthy lifestyles, and inadequate control of major risk factors [4]. It is estimated that the incidence of cerebrovascular disease events in China in 2030 will increase by about 50% compared with 2010 [5].

In response, China launched the National Stroke Screening and Intervention Program in 2009 to identify stroke risk factors and provide early intervention across the country. The program has screened millions nationwide, integrating risk assessment with health education and follow-up interventions [6, 7]. However, despite biennial re-screenings generating extensive longitudinal data, current assessments still rely on static classifications in community practice. Each round of screening typically updates categorical risk labels (low, moderate, or high) without integrating prior results or monitoring individual risk trajectories over time. As a result, the program’s potential for continuous, individualized risk management remains underutilized, and control of modifiable risk factors is suboptimal [8].

This limitation is also reflected in analytical approaches to stroke risk assessment. Traditional static risk assessments, which capture risk at a single time point, implicitly assume the temporal stability of individual risk status. However, stroke risk is inherently dynamic, influenced by the cumulative and interacting effects of behavioral, metabolic, and environmental factors [9, 10] Recent studies suggest that trajectory-based or cumulative exposure analyses may outperform static models by capturing temporal variation and identifying individuals whose risk escalates over time. For instance, a population-based cohort study found that cumulative exposure to an inflammatory-metabolic index predicted stroke incidence more strongly than baseline values (HR = 1.90, 95% CI 1.30–2.78) [11]. Similarly, time-series modeling improved predictive accuracy for stroke compared with conventional static assessments (HR = 3.85, 95% CI 3.26–4.56) [12]. These findings underscore the need for dynamic approaches to capture evolving risk patterns and detect early transitions from low- to high-risk trajectories before clinical onset, thereby enabling earlier and potentially more effective interventions.

To address these issues, group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) offers a powerful alternative by identifying latent subgroups following distinct longitudinal risk patterns [13, 14]. Yet its application in stroke prevention research, particularly within large community cohorts in China, remains scarce. Harnessing GBTM may provide a useful approach for moving stroke prevention beyond static snapshots toward more dynamic and data-driven risk stratification. Therefore, this study used a four-year community-based cohort with three waves of follow-up data to (1) identify distinct longitudinal trajectories of stroke risk among Chinese adults and (2) examine the sociodemographic, behavioral, and physiological determinants associated with these trajectories. These findings provide empirical evidence to inform precision prevention and may help improve the long-term effectiveness of community-based stroke control strategies in China.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the National Stroke Screening and Intervention Program conducted in a rural community of Shijiazhuang, Hebei Province, between 2018 and 2022. The program, launched in 2009 as part of the China National Stroke Screening Survey (CNSSS), aimed to identify stroke risk factors and provide early intervention across the country. This initiative, which serves as the foundation for the current study, is supported by the China Ministry of Health and the China Ministry of Finance. For more details on the organization and implementation of the CNSSS, please visit the official website (http://csp.ncmi.cn/).

At baseline in 2018, a total of 2,680 residents aged ≥ 40 years were invited to participate in the baseline survey. After excluding 435 individuals who were ineligible, refused to participate, or had incomplete data, 2,245 participants completed the baseline survey. For the present analysis, 50 participants with a history of stroke at baseline were further excluded, leaving 2,195 eligible participants. Among them, 1,794 and 1,376 completed the follow-up surveys in 2020 and 2022, respectively. Thus, 1,376 participants who completed all three survey waves were included in the final analysis. The participant selection and follow-up process is shown in Fig. 1. Baseline characteristics of eligible participants who completed both follow-up surveys and those who did not are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the local institutional ethics committe.

Stroke risk assessment

Stroke risk was assessed using the Rapid Stroke Risk Screening Chart, a nationally implemented tool widely used in large-scale stroke prevention and control programs in China, including the China National Stroke Screening Survey (CNSSS) [15, 16]. This tool is commonly referred to as the “8 + 2” stroke risk assessment model, in which eight major vascular risk factors are used for risk stratification, and a history of transient ischemic attack (TIA) or prior stroke is also incorporated into risk classification.

The eight risk factors included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, current smoking, overweight or obesity, physical inactivity, and family history of stroke. The definition of each factor followed the standardized criteria of the CNSSS. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported history of hypertension, use of antihypertensive medication, or an average of two resting systolic blood pressure measurements ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure measurements ≥ 90 mmHg during the field survey [17]. Diabetes mellitus was defined as self-reported diabetes, use of insulin and/or oral hypoglycaemic medications, or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L [18]. Dyslipidemia was defined as self-reported use of lipid-lowering medication or the presence of at least one of the following: triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L, total cholesterol ≥ 5.18 mmol/L, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 3.37 mmol/L [19]. Atrial fibrillation was defined as self-reported physician diagnosis or diagnosis by electrocardiography during the field survey. Current smoking was defined as self-report of smoking at least one cigarette per day. Overweight or obesity was defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m², calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared [20]. Physical inactivity was defined as engaging in physical exercise fewer than three times per week for less than 30 min per session, including industrial and agricultural labor [21]. Family history of stroke referred to stroke occurring in first-degree relatives.

Based on the CNSSS criteria, participants were classified into three stroke risk groups. The high-risk group included individuals with three or more of the above risk factors, or a history of TIA or prior stroke. The moderate-risk group included individuals with fewer than three risk factors but with at least one of the following core chronic conditions: hypertension, diabetes, or atrial fibrillation. The low-risk group included individuals with fewer than three risk factors and none of these core chronic conditions.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and stroke risk factors were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables with non-normal distributions were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the χ² test.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify longitudinal patterns of stroke risk across the three survey waves (2018, 2020, and 2022). The outcome variable was the three-category ordinal stroke risk level, coded as low-risk = 1, moderate-risk = 2, and high-risk = 3, consistent with the standardized risk stratification used in the Chinese National Stroke Screening and Intervention Program. Although the outcome variable was ordinal, a censored normal distribution was applied for model estimation within the PROC TRAJ framework, which has been widely used in group-based trajectory modeling [22, 23].

Trajectory models with one to six latent classes were fitted. The optimal model was selected based on the following criteria: (1) better model fit, indicated by larger (less negative) AIC and BIC values in the PROC TRAJ output; (2) class size of at least 5% of the total sample; (3) adequate classification accuracy, defined as an average posterior probability (AvePP) ≥ 0.70 for each class; and (4) clinical interpretability and parsimony.

After data verification, outcome and covariate data were complete for all included participants. Therefore, analyses were conducted on the full analytic sample without imputation, and the effective sample size was fixed at 1,376 for all models. Associations between baseline characteristics and trajectory group membership were examined using multinomial logistic regression, and results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using SAS software, with trajectory models estimated using the PROC TRAJ procedure. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Most participants were married (98.98%) and lived with family members (94.62%). In terms of education, 41.42% had senior high school education, and 63.15% reported an annual income ≥ 20,000 yuan.

Regarding lifestyle factors, 85.17% reported no alcohol use, 12.79% reported frequent heavy drinking, and 2.03% reported occasional light drinking. Dietary patterns were classified as balanced (19.77%), meat-preference (23.76%), and vegetarian-preference (56.47%), and 51.38% consumed fruit daily. The median waist circumference was 82 cm (IQR, 75–88), and the median homocysteine level was 10.5 µmol/L (IQR, 8–13). At baseline, 59.23% of participants were classified as low risk, 18.60% as moderate risk, and 22.17% as high risk according to the “8 + 2” stroke risk classification. The distribution of participant characteristics and stroke risk factors across the three survey waves (2018, 2020, and 2022) is shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Stroke risk trajectories

Fit statistics for candidate trajectory models are presented in Table 2. In the PROC TRAJ output, both the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values were negative, and larger (less negative) values indicated better model fit. From the one-class to the three-class model, both criteria became less negative, indicating improved model fit. For models with four or more classes, model performance was less satisfactory. The four-class model included a class with 0% of participants, and the six-class model contained two classes with proportions below 5%. Although all class proportions in the five-class model exceeded 5%, some classes showed low average posterior probabilities (AvePP), indicating suboptimal classification accuracy. Overall, the three-class model provided the best balance of model fit, class size, classification accuracy, and clinical interpretability.

Figure 2 shows the three distinct stroke risk trajectories identified over the four-year follow-up. Trajectory 1 (stable low-risk, 41.42%) included participants who maintained consistently low stroke risk levels across all three survey waves. Trajectory 2 (increasing-risk, 41.21%) included participants who started with relatively low risk levels at baseline but showed a progressive increase over time, reaching substantially higher risk levels by 2022. Trajectory 3 (stable high-risk, 17.37%) included participants who maintained persistently high stroke risk levels throughout the study period.

Additional analyses

To further examine the association between vegetarian diet preference and stroke risk trajectories, we compared the distribution of the stroke risk factor components across dietary patterns (Supplementary Table S3). Although the vegetarian-preference group had a lower prevalence of overweight/obesity, it showed a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and family history of stroke.

Stratified analysis by annual income showed that vegetarian diet preference remained associated with the increasing-risk and stable high-risk trajectories in both income subgroups (Supplementary Table S4). Sensitivity analysis excluding BMI from the “8 + 2” risk factors yielded similar results (Supplementary Table S5).

Consistent with general drinking patterns in China, the prevalence of frequent heavy drinking was lower among females than males (4.05% vs. 27.54%), and no female participants reported occasional light drinking (Supplementary Table S6). Sex-stratified regression analyses and formal testing of the sex-by-alcohol interaction further showed no significant interaction between sex and alcohol consumption for either the stable high-risk or increasing-risk trajectory (Supplementary Table S7). These findings suggest that the higher proportion of females in the increasing-risk trajectory group was unlikely to be explained by a higher prevalence of drinking among women.

Discussion

Heterogeneity of stroke risk trajectories

This study identified three distinct stroke risk trajectories in a community-based Chinese cohort over four years: stable low-risk, increasing-risk, and stable high-risk. These findings indicate that stroke risk is not static, but instead follows heterogeneous patterns over time within the population. While a substantial proportion of participants remained in a persistently low-risk group, others showed either sustained high-risk status or a clear upward transition in risk over time. This heterogeneity highlights the limitations of relying solely on single time-point risk classification in routine community screening.

Among the three trajectories, the increasing-risk group is particularly important from a public health perspective. Participants in this group did not have the highest risk profile at baseline, but their risk increased progressively during follow-up and reached a markedly less favourable level by 2022. This suggests that a considerable proportion of individuals who appear relatively low-risk at one screening point may still experience meaningful risk deterioration over time. Such individuals may be overlooked if stroke prevention strategies focus only on those already classified as high risk at baseline. In this sense, our findings challenge the assumption that individuals initially classified as low risk remain stable over time and are consistent with previous cohort studies showing that stroke-related risk can accumulate progressively under the influence of modifiable factors such as blood pressure, abdominal obesity, and lifestyle behaviours [24, 25].

These findings have important implications for community-based stroke prevention. Traditional screening strategies are largely based on risk classification at a single time point and may therefore overlook individuals whose risk is gradually increasing but who have not yet reached a conventionally defined high-risk threshold [26, 27]. In contrast, the trajectory-based approach used in the present study captures temporal changes in risk status and may help identify individuals who are moving toward a less favourable risk profile. This may provide an opportunity for earlier behavioural intervention, targeted lifestyle counselling, and closer risk monitoring.

From a public health perspective, our findings support the potential value of incorporating longitudinal monitoring into community-based stroke prevention. This interpretation is broadly aligned with international public health efforts that emphasise repeated risk assessment and earlier identification of individuals at rising vascular risk [28–31]. However, these initiatives provide contextual support rather than direct evidence for trajectory-based stroke risk modelling. Further research in larger and more diverse populations, with longer follow-up and validation against incident stroke outcomes, is still needed before broader conclusions can be drawn regarding implementation in routine practice.

Factors influencing stroke risk trajectories

Our study identified several key modifiable factors—including drinking habits, dietary patterns, waist circumference, and homocysteine levels—that significantly shaped the evolution of stroke risk trajectories. These findings highlight the multifactorial and interactive nature of stroke risk, where behavioral and metabolic factors jointly drive the transition from stable low-risk to increasing- or persistently high-risk trajectories.

Frequent heavy drinking was associated with both the increasing-risk and stable high-risk trajectories, consistent with previous evidence identifying alcohol consumption as an important modifiable risk factor for stroke [32–34]. In our additional analyses, the prevalence of frequent heavy drinking was markedly lower among females than males, and no female participants reported occasional light drinking. Sex-stratified analyses further showed that the association between alcohol consumption and higher-risk trajectories was present in both males and females, and no significant sex-by-alcohol interaction was observed. These findings suggest that the higher proportion of females in the increasing-risk trajectory group is more likely to reflect the sex composition of that subgroup rather than a stronger association of alcohol in women. Given that alcohol use is modifiable, reducing frequent heavy drinking may represent an important target for community-based stroke prevention.

Vegetarian diet preference was also independently associated with both the increasing-risk and stable high-risk trajectories [35, 36]. Further analysis showed that although the vegetarian-preference group had a lower prevalence of overweight/obesity, it had a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and family history of stroke. These findings suggest that the observed association was not driven by BMI alone, but rather reflected the combined contribution of multiple vascular risk factors. This interpretation was further supported by the sensitivity analysis excluding BMI from the “8 + 2” risk factors, in which the association remained significant. In addition, income-stratified analysis showed that the association was evident in the low-income subgroup and showed a similar trend in the high-income subgroup, suggesting that the observed relationship was not solely explained by socioeconomic disadvantage. In this rural northern Chinese population, vegetarian preference may reflect local dietary habits and dietary structure rather than a nutritionally planned plant-based diet. This finding should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the BMI-based definition used in the “8 + 2” framework does not capture potential risk related to underweight status, and unmeasured factors such as nutritional status and access to healthcare may still contribute to residual confounding. Taken together, these findings may indicate the importance of overall dietary quality and dietary balance, rather than vegetarian preference per se, in stroke prevention.

Larger waist circumference was associated with higher-risk trajectories, highlighting the role of central adiposity in stroke risk progression [37–39]. Waist circumference is a practical marker of cardiometabolic risk and may provide additional value in community-based screening beyond conventional risk classification. This finding supports the inclusion of abdominal obesity management in stroke prevention strategies.

Elevated homocysteine levels were also associated with less favourable trajectories. In China, homocysteine levels are frequently elevated due to widespread folate deficiency, and this finding has been corroborated by local research [40, 41]. Given the relatively high burden of hyperhomocysteinemia in China, this finding may have particular relevance for community prevention. Routine identification and management of elevated homocysteine may help improve risk stratification in populations with similar backgrounds.

Overall, these findings suggest that stroke risk trajectories are influenced by multiple interacting factors. From a public health perspective, early identification of individuals with high-risk profiles and intervention on modifiable factors—particularly alcohol use, dietary imbalance, abdominal obesity, and elevated homocysteine—may help delay progression to higher-risk trajectories.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only participants who completed all three survey waves were included in the trajectory analysis. Although this ensured a consistent longitudinal dataset, it may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the analytic sample. The baseline comparison between completers and non-completers suggested that loss to follow-up was not entirely random; therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the findings to the broader population. Second, several lifestyle-related variables, including diet and alcohol consumption, were self-reported, which may have introduced some degree of recall bias or misclassification.

Third, the stroke risk classification was based on the nationally used “8 + 2” screening framework. Although this tool has substantial practical value in community screening, it was originally designed for categorical risk stratification rather than longitudinal modelling. Its discrete and bounded structure may impose methodological constraints, and the equal weighting of its individual components may not fully reflect the true heterogeneity of risk contribution across factors. Fourth, although the present study focused on longitudinal trajectories of stroke risk, the multinomial regression analysis used baseline characteristics to predict subsequent trajectory membership and did not model major modifiable exposures as time-varying covariates. Therefore, the analysis did not fully capture dynamic changes in individual exposure factors over time.

Finally, this was an observational study based on stroke risk classification rather than validated stroke outcomes. Causal inferences cannot therefore be made, and the clinical relevance of the identified trajectories requires further confirmation in studies with larger samples, longer follow-up, and validation against incident stroke events.

Conclusion

In this community-based Chinese cohort, three distinct longitudinal stroke risk trajectories were identified: stable low-risk, increasing-risk, and stable high-risk. Older age, frequent heavy drinking, vegetarian diet preference, larger waist circumference, and elevated homocysteine levels were associated with less favourable trajectories. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of stroke risk over time and suggest the potential utility of trajectory-based approaches for improving risk stratification and identifying individuals who may benefit from earlier and more targeted preventive intervention.
