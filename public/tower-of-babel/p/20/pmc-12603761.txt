The role of community leaders on rational drug use literacy: A structural equation modeling
Authors: Thunpitcha Sakpirom, Kusawadee Maluangnon, Chaoncin Sooksriwong, Apinya Ingard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIrrational drug use remains a global health challenge, particularly in underserved communities with limited healthcare access. In Thailand, despite the Rational Drug Use (RDU) initiative, problems such as antibiotic misuse and selfmedication persist. Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL)—the ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply drug information—is vital for improving medication behaviors. While most efforts focus on health professionals, the role of community leaders has been less explored.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 328 participants in Pathum Thani Province, including 168 community members and 160 community leaders selected through stratified random sampling. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and a validated RDUL tool. Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was used to assess direct and moderating effects of situational and socioenvironmental factors on RDUL.ResultsCommunity leaders exhibited higher Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) than community members (M = 41.90 vs. 39.48, p = .003), particularly in accessing, appraising, and applying drug information. Structural Equation Modeling revealed that both situational factors (β = 0.357, p < .001) and socio-environmental factors (β = 0.168, p = .021) positively influenced RDUL, indicating that favorable circumstances and supportive environments enhance individuals' medication literacy. Importantly, leadership played a nuanced role: it diminished the influence of situational factors on RDUL (β = −0.634, p < .001) while moderately amplifying the effect of socioenvironmental factors (β = 0.327, p = .058). These results highlight that community leaders not only enhance overall medication literacy but also alter how contextual factors contribute to informed drug use.ConclusionsCommunity leaders play a complex but crucial role in shaping RDUL. While they strengthen certain literacy dimensions, their influence also alters contextual effects. RDUL strategies should leverage leaders as trusted figures while enhancing their capacity to promote informed medication use. Incorporating community leadership into health programs may increase the effectiveness and sustainability of RDU efforts in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Irrational drug use remains a significant global public health challenge, contributing to treatment failures, antimicrobial resistance, and increasing healthcare costs. Despite Thailand's national Rational Drug Use initiative launched in 2016, inappropriate drug behaviors such as antibiotic misuse, hoarding of leftover medication, and self-medication without professional consultation persist, particularly in rural and underserved areas where access to accurate medical information and healthcare services is limited.1,2 To address this problem, the concept of Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) has emerged as a critical focus. Defined by Phodha T et al.3 as the ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply drug-related information to make appropriate health decisions, RDUL is essential for promoting safe and effective medication use and serves as a foundation for sustainable health behavior change at the community level.

While formal healthcare providers and trained community health workers are traditionally viewed as the primary agents in promoting RDUL, growing international evidence highlights the vital role of informal and semi-formal community leaders. In countries such as Kenya, India, and Indonesia, village chiefs, religious leaders, and local council members have been instrumental in improving vaccine uptake, encouraging maternal health practices, and advancing community health education by leveraging their trusted social legitimacy.4,5 The COVID-19 pandemic further underscored the importance of local leadership in health promotion. In Sierra Leone and Liberia, community chiefs successfully adapted national infection prevention measures into culturally relevant local actions.6 Similarly, ward and barangay leaders in Nepal and the Philippines played key roles in disseminating accurate health information, enforcing lockdowns, and supporting public health campaigns.7 Despite such evidence, many RDUL initiatives remain narrowly focused on formal health professionals, often overlooking the strategic influence of community leaders, especially in rural and low-resource settings where residents frequently rely more on elders, religious figures, or local authorities than on distant healthcare personnel.8

Research from sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America suggests that integrating community leaders into health literacy efforts enhances health outcomes, fosters community ownership, Addressing this gap, the present study focuses on Pathum Thani Province, Thailand, which provides an appropriate and strategic context for RDUL research. Pathum Thani represents a transitional zone between urban Bangkok and semi-rural central Thailand, characterized by a diverse population that includes migrant workers, industrial employees, and long-established local communities. The province has a mix of well-equipped healthcare facilities and areas with limited access to professional medical services, reflecting both urban and rural health system dynamics. This socioeconomic diversity makes Pathum Thani an ideal setting for examining how situational and socio-environmental factors interact with community leadership to shape rational drug use behaviors. Furthermore, Pathum Thani has been a pilot area for several Ministry of Public Health community health literacy campaigns, providing a relevant foundation for assessing RDUL within Thailand's evolving public health context. and increases cultural relevance, which are vital for long-term behavior change.9 Specifically, the study aims to: (1) assess the RDUL levels among community leaders and residents; (2) identify situational and socio-environmental factors influencing RDUL in both groups; and (3) examine whether community leadership moderates the effects of these factors on RDUL. (H1) Situational factors such as social support and access to health resources create an enabling environment for rational drug use, as individuals with strong support systems and available resources are more likely to engage in informed medication practices. This aligns with the Health Belief Model (HBM), which highlights the role of perceived benefits and cues to action, and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), which associates perceived behavioral control with health-related intentions.,1011 Similarly, (H2) socio-environmental factors—including access to social services and communication with health influencers—positively influence Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL). Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) emphasizes the importance of observational learning and social interaction, while Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory underscores the impact of interconnected social systems on health behavior.,1213 (H3) Community leaders further play a moderating role by shaping community norms and legitimizing health behaviors, thereby strengthening the influence of situational and socio-environmental factors on RDUL. Their roles are supported by the Diffusion of Innovations Theory and Empowerment Theory, which identify them as opinion leaders who enhance behavioral adoption and collective agency.,1415 Moreover, (H4) Community Organization Theory and Transformational Leadership Theory explain how leaders promote communication, trust, and collective health awareness, moderating the relationship between socio-environmental factors and RDUL.,1617

Method

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional, quantitative research design to examine factors influencing Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) in Pathum Thani Province, Thailand. Two target groups were included: the community members and the community leaders.

Population and sample

The target population for this study consisted of residents of Pathum Thani Province, Thailand, categorized into two primary groups: community members (general population) and community leaders. The community leader group specifically included village headmen and subdistrict chiefs, who play a vital role in supervising and promoting health-related initiatives at the local level. To determine the minimum required sample size, the G*Power program was employed using a power of 0.95, a significance level of 0.05, and a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15). The calculation indicated that at least 146 participants were needed to achieve sufficient statistical power.

To ensure representativeness across all areas and improve the precision of statistical estimates, a stratified random sampling method was used. The population was stratified by district, followed by simple random sampling within each stratum. The sampling frame was constructed based on the most recent and comprehensive population database available for Pathum Thani Province. The stratification included all seven districts of Pathum Thani Province: Mueang Pathum Thani, Khlong Luang, Thanyaburi, Lam Luk Ka, Lat Lum Kaeo, Sam Khok, and Nong Suea. As a result, a total of 328 participants were selected for inclusion in the study, consisting of 168 community members and 160 community leaders. All selected individuals participated in the survey and were included in the final data analysis.

Prior to data collection, all research assistants underwent structured training conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the principles of human research ethics. The training covered the study's objectives, confidentiality protocols, and standardized data collection procedures. Emphasis was placed on obtaining informed consent, safeguarding participant privacy, and administering questionnaires in a neutral manner to minimize potential bias. Research assistants were instructed to ensure independent response completion and maintain secure handling of all research materials. A pilot test was performed to evaluate procedural clarity and instrument reliability, after which the principal investigator provided continuous supervision throughout the data collection phase to ensure methodological rigor, accuracy, and data integrity.

Part 1 focused on situational factors, organized into three domains: distance from health services, availability of health resources, and social support (13 items). These items were designed to assess how external circumstances may impact individuals' health literacy behaviors. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

This section also used a 5-point Likert scale format.

The questionnaire was subjected to content validity assessment using the Index of Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) method, with evaluations conducted by three subject-matter experts. All items received IOC scores above 0.50, indicating acceptable content relevance. Data collection

Data collection was conducted between September and November 2024, following a systematic and coordinated approach. Initially, the researcher coordinated with district public health officers in all districts within Pathum Thani Province to plan the distribution of questionnaires to a randomly selected sample. During this phase, the objectives of the study and the rights of participants were reiterated to ensure informed and voluntary participation.

Participants were then asked to complete a self-administered Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) assessment along with a structured questionnaire. Upon collection, the researcher reviewed each questionnaire to verify completeness and accuracy, ensuring the integrity of the data. Finally, the validated data were systematically entered and transferred into computer software for statistical analysis, thereby enabling a rigorous and reliable examination of the study variables.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses included both descriptive statistics, such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation, and inferential statistics, such as independent sample t-tests and Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM).

Results

Participant characteristics

Data collected from 328 participants indicated that the majority were female (58.2 %) and primarily between 41 and 60 years of age (48.8 %). In terms of educational background, 26.8 % had completed Grade 12 or vocational education, while 26.2 % held a bachelor's degree. Regarding income, 50.9 % reported a monthly income ranging from 10,000 to 20,000 Thai Baht. Most participants (57.9 %) reported no chronic health conditions. Furthermore, a significant portion (73.5 %) was employed in occupations characterized by unstable income.

Rational drug use literacy scores

The findings of this study indicate significant differences in Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) between community leaders and general community members in Pathum Thani Province. Community leaders reported significantly higher overall RDUL scores (x̄ = 41.90, SD = 5.73) than community members (x̄ = 39.48, SD = 8.45; t = −3.03, p = .003). Structural equation modeling

The findings from a Variance-Based Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) approach identified causal factors significantly influencing Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) through measurement and structural models. The measurement model was evaluated for construct and convergent validity using established criteria, including statistically significant outer loadings (λ ≥ 0.70), Cronbach's alpha (α ≥ 0.70), composite reliability (ρA and ρC > 0.70), and Average Variance Extracted (AVE ≥ 0.50), with some allowance for AVE values below 0.50 if composite reliability exceeded 0.60.20

Situational factors (SITU) were initially specified with three indicators: distance from healthcare services (DIS), resource availability (RES), and social support (SS). However, DIS was removed due to a non-significant outer loading (−0.092), low internal consistency (α = 0.298), and inadequate composite reliability (ρC = 0.669).This exclusion may reflect that perceived accessibility such as convenience, availability, and affordability often influence health behavior more than physical proximity.21 Furthermore, the growing use of digital health tools, telemedicine, and local outreach programs has reduced the impact of physical distance as a barrier to healthcare.22 Limited variation in participants' access to services may also have weakened the item's discriminatory power. In line with construct refinement principles,23 DIS was removed to improve model validity. The revised model retained RES and SS as valid indicators of SITU.

Socio-environmental factors (SENV), consisting of social services (SER), communication with health influencers (COM), and consultation with health influencers (ASK), met all thresholds and were retained. The RDUL construct, measured by six indicators—access (ACC), understanding (UND), appraisal (APP), decision making (DEC), application (APY), and sharing (SHA)—also satisfied reliability and validity criteria, with composite reliability values above 0.60, justifying inclusion despite some AVE values slightly below 0.50. In addition, one item with a relatively low loading (0.352) was retained due to its theoretical importance in capturing a critical dimension of the construct. Although both effects are statistically small, their implications are meaningful for public health policy and behavioral development. Small effect sizes often represent consistent, cumulative influences that, when sustained across populations, can generate substantial long-term impact. In this context, enhancing accessibility to community health resources, strengthening social support mechanisms, and promoting engagement with credible health influencers can progressively improve rational drug use behavior. These findings highlight the importance of continuous, system-level strategies that foster supportive environments for responsible medication practices, rather than relying solely on short-term educational interventions.

SEM structural model: direct and moderating effects

The results of structural equation modeling (SEM) tested the moderating effects of community leaders on the relationship between situational and socio-environmental factors and Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL). The direct effect of situational factors (SITU) on RDUL remained statistically significant (β = 0.540, p < .001), supporting H1 and indicating that access to health resources and social support continued to be a critical predictor of rational drug use. However, a significant negative interaction between community leaders and situational factors (Leader × SITU; β = −0.634, p < .001) was observed, suggesting that the presence of community leaders weakened the positive relationship between situational support and RDUL, which is consistent with H3. Community leaders may attenuate the influence of situational factors on RDUL due to over-reliance by community members. When strong leadership is present, individuals may defer health decisions and information-seeking to leaders, reducing personal engagement with available resources and social support. This dependency can weaken the direct positive effects of situational factors on rational drug use literacy. This pattern is consistent with our SEM findings, which show that the presence of community leaders significantly weakens the positive effect of situational factors on RDUL.

In contrast, the direct effect of socio-environmental factors (SENV) on RDUL became non-significant (β = 0.084, p = .422), failing to support H2, although it was significant in the direct effects model (Table 4). The moderating effect of community leaders on socio-environmental factors (Leader × SENV) was marginally non-significant (β = 0.327, p = .058). Finally, the moderating effect of community leaders on the relationship between socio-environmental factors and RDUL, thus H4 was not supported. Discussions

This study demonstrates that community leaders play a distinctive role in shaping Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) within Thai communities. Leaders scored higher than general members in access, appraisal, and application of drug information, a finding consistent with Phodha T et al.3 and Nutbeam's conceptualization of interactive and critical health literacy.27 These differences may be explained by leaders' broader social networks, more frequent engagement with healthcare professionals, and their established role as trusted figures within the community. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in understanding, decision, and sharing, suggesting that both leaders and community members face comparable challenges in comprehending drug information, making informed choices, and disseminating knowledge. This aligns with Sørensen K et al.18 who emphasized that even individuals with social influence may struggle with advanced health literacy tasks without targeted support.

Importantly, this study is among the first to quantify the moderating role of community leadership on RDUL pathways in Thailand, providing empirical evidence on how leadership dynamics intersect with individual and contextual factors in promoting rational drug use. While prior research has qualitatively acknowledged the influence of leadership in health behavior change, this study advances the field by statistically modeling its moderating effects—a novel contribution to health literacy research in the Thai context.

Situational and socio-environmental factors were also found to exert significant, albeit modest, effects on RDUL. This aligns with Sørensen's model, which emphasizes the interaction between individual capacities and contextual enablers, and with Greenhalgh T et al.,8 who noted that health literacy outcomes are shaped by multiple mediating influences. The relatively small effect sizes observed are consistent with global evidence that complex health behaviors rarely hinge on a single determinant. Nevertheless, as Funder DC and Ozer DJ28 argue, even small effects can yield meaningful cumulative impacts when scaled, underscoring the relevance of these findings for community health promotion. For example, modest improvements in RDUL facilitated by better access to resources and social support could reduce antibiotic misuse and improve medication safety over time.

Moderation analysis further revealed the nuanced role of community leaders: their presence attenuated the direct effect of situational supports while rendering the socio-environmental pathway non-significant. This suggests that in contexts where trusted leaders are active, individuals may rely less on formal structures and more on interpersonal guidance, a pattern consistent with findings on reliance on interpersonal trust in health decision-making.29 The non-significant moderation of socio-environmental factors could reflect the collectivist and hierarchical nature of Thai culture, where people place strong trust in community figures and personal relationships rather than institutional systems. In such contexts, health information and behavioral norms are often transmitted through social bonds and moral authority rather than through impersonal organizational mechanisms. Thus, when community leaders act as intermediaries, the added value of broader socio-environmental resources—such as public health campaigns or community infrastructure—may be less pronounced.

However, the lack of a significant moderating effect also indicates that leaders may not substantially enhance socio-environmental influences, echoing perspectives that such factors tend to operate similarly across groups regardless of leadership.30 This interpretation aligns with Social Cognitive Theory, which stresses self-efficacy and direct experiences,12 and Diffusion of Innovations Theory, which underscores the importance of communication effectiveness and credibility.14 The marginally non-significant moderation effect for socio-environmental factors further suggests that while leaders can reinforce communication and service access, their impact may not be distinct enough to differentiate outcomes from settings without strong leadership.

Policy and practice implications

Overall, these findings affirm that community leaders are not passive elements in the health ecosystem but instead actively reshape how individuals engage with health resources and social supports. By leveraging their strengths and addressing gaps in critical competencies, policymakers and public health practitioners can design multicomponent interventions that integrate leadership with other contextual supports. Such strategies could enhance the sustainability and effectiveness of RDUL promotion, ultimately fostering more rational and informed medication use at the community level. Moreover, empowering leaders through capacity-building programs and aligning their efforts with digital platforms and formal health influencers could increase the credibility and reach of drug literacy initiatives. Even modest effects, when scaled through community-wide interventions, can generate substantial long-term public health benefits, reducing inappropriate medicine use and strengthening trust in health systems.

Conclusion

This study enhances understanding of how situational and socio-environmental factors shape Rational Drug Use Literacy (RDUL) and underscores the moderating role of community leaders in this relationship. Leaders exhibited stronger competencies in key literacy dimensions and moderated the impact of situational factors on outcomes. These findings highlight the value of leader-focused RDUL training modules that equip trusted figures with practical skills to communicate accurate drug information and model safe medication practices.

Integrating such training with broader community initiatives—improving access to reliable resources and social support—can foster more equitable literacy gains. Future studies should further examine how leadership engagement influences RDUL mechanisms and assess the effectiveness of structured leader development programs. By combining leadership capacity-building with supportive community environments, policymakers and practitioners can design sustainable, culturally grounded interventions that promote rational, safe, and informed drug use across diverse populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the RDUL construct showed suboptimal convergent validity, with some indicators presenting low factor loadings and average variance extracted values below accepted thresholds. Although these items were retained based on theoretical relevance, their inclusion may affect measurement accuracy.

Second, the moderating variable of community leadership was treated as a simple binary category. This approach does not reflect the complexity of leadership roles, such as trust, influence, or degree of engagement, which may influence outcomes differently.

Third, the observed effect sizes for situational and socio-environmental factors were small. While statistically significant, these findings suggest that other unmeasured variables may contribute more substantially to rational drug use literacy.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, as the observed relationships represent associations rather than temporal effects.

Finally, the study was conducted in Pathum Thani Province, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other Thai regions or populations with different socio-cultural characteristics.

Ethics approval

Following ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Pathum Thani Provincial Public Health Office (Approval No. PPHO-REC No. 2566/44), the researcher implemented rigorous protocols to protect the rights, safety, and well-being of all participants. Prior to data collection, the study's purpose and procedures were clearly explained, emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were assured of their right to decline or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. These measures ensured transparency, autonomy, and ethical integrity throughout the research process.
