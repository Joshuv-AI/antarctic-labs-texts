Brain metastases converge on shared geometric architecture and transcriptomic landscape yet remain distinct from gliomas
Authors: Neha Yadav, Jayshaan Baibhav, Seemadri Subhadarshini, Saroj Kumar Das Majumdar, Mohit K. Jolly, Vivek Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBrain metastases (BMs) and gliomas arise from distinct biological origins yet frequently present with overlapping radiological features, particularly within peritumoral regions. By quantifying fractal dimension (FD3D) and lacunarity (Lac3D) across enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous tumor subcomponents, we demonstrate that BMs from diverse primary cancers converge on a conserved structural phenotype within the brain-microenvironment, whereas gliomas retain distinct subcomponent complexity and geometric profiles. Enhancing subcomponents of BMs exhibited intermediate structural complexity between low- and high-grade gliomas (LGGs and HGGs), while non-enhancing and edematous compartments were structurally smoother. The integration of subcomponent complexity and volumetric fractions enabled accurate discrimination across independent imaging cohorts, and edema fractality uniquely predicted survival in BMs. Independent transcriptomic analyses revealed pathway-level convergence consistent with brain microenvironment-driven remodeling, yet molecular programs remained distinct from gliomas. These findings establish a quantitative neuroimaging-based geometric framework with clinical utility in neuro-oncology settings, reducing reliance on immediate biopsy for diagnostic and prognostic stratification.

Introduction

Brain metastases (BMs/Brain-Mets) and gliomas represent the two most common categories of adult brain tumors, yet they arise from fundamentally distinct origins: BMs result from secondary spread of extracranial malignancies, while gliomas are primary neoplasms of the central nervous system. Despite these biological differences, they often exhibit overlapping radiographic features, creating a persistent diagnostic challenge in neuro-oncology. Conventional MRI remains indispensable, yet it frequently fails to disentangle the morphological overlap between high-grade gliomas (HGGs) and BMs.1 HGGs and BMs both can present with contrast enhancement, necrosis, extensive peritumoral hyperintensities, and comparable intratumoral texture,2,3 limiting diagnostic specificity and often necessitating invasive biopsy for confirmation.4 This challenge is most acute in the peritumoral regions: vasogenic edema surrounding BMs and infiltrative, tumor-rich edema in gliomas appear radiologically similar, despite arising from fundamentally different biological processes.5 Such ambiguity compromises diagnostic accuracy and risks delays in treatment planning.

We reasoned that tumors should be considered mosaics of biologically distinct compartments: contrast-enhancing (ET), non-enhancing + necrotic (NET), and peritumoral edematous (ED), each reflecting unique microenvironmental pressures such as neovascularization, necrosis, infiltration, and stromal remodeling.6 The geometry of these compartments may encode disease-specific signatures. A key question is whether BMs of diverse extracranial origins converge toward a common morpho-architectural state once established within the brain, and how distinct such a state is from gliomas, which originate within the brain itself.

The conventional neuroimaging investigations either neglect subcomponent resolution or treat tumor geometry in simplistic, Euclidean terms. To address this gap, we implemented a novel neuroimaging framework that quantifies the structural complexity and spatial heterogeneity of brain tumor subcomponents using non-Euclidean geometric descriptors, three-dimensional fractal dimension (FD3D), and lacunarity (Lac3D). FD3D captures scale-invariant structural complexity and self-similarity, while lacunarity quantifies spatial heterogeneity or “gappiness” within a region.7,8,9,10 These metrics, when applied separately to the enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous regions, offer a refined quantitative lens into tumor growth architecture and microenvironmental interaction. In large multi-institutional cohorts, we found that BMs, regardless of primary origin, converge to a conserved geometric profile: The fractality of BMs enhancing components lies intermediate between LGGs and HGGs, while the non-enhancing and edematous compartments of BMs are smoother, with lower fractality than gliomas. In contrast, gliomas displayed greater heterogeneity across compartments, reflecting diffuse infiltrative growth and lineage-specific remodeling.

This morpho-architectural convergence in BMs was paralleled by transcriptomic convergence in independent datasets. Despite originating from diverse primaries, BMs clustered tightly and were unified by low-variance hallmark pathways, including TNFα/NF-κB, TGF-β, and p53 signaling. Gliomas, by contrast, exhibited fractality-linked correlations with gene programs involved in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), solute carrier (SLC) transport, angiogenesis, and cytoskeletal remodeling. Thus, fractal geometry may not be a purely morphological readout, but rather a reflection of distinct underlying biology in BMs and gliomas.

Clinically, combining FD3D and Lac3D with subcomponent volumetric fractions enabled highly accurate, machine learning-based classification of BMs versus gliomas across independent cohorts. Importantly, fractal geometry of the edema subcomponent emerged as a prognostic marker in BMs: patients with FD3D < 1.83 in edema showed significantly poor survival. In gliomas, by contrast, edema geometry was not prognostically informative.

Together, these findings establish a subcomponent-aware, geometry-driven, and biologically informed neuroimaging framework that not only distinguishes BMs from gliomas but also provides prognostic insight. By quantifying geometric complexity as a surrogate for molecular convergence, this approach reduces reliance on immediate biopsy and supports earlier, more confident decision-making in radiologically ambiguous neuro-oncology cases.

Demographic and clinical data

Brain Metastases cohort

A total of 200 patients diagnosed with brain-mets in Pretreat-Mets cohort (mean age: 63.4 ± 11.8 years) were included in the study, all of whom underwent preoperative MRI acquisitions (T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), T2w-fluid attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR), and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1Gd)), followed by detailed segmentation of tumor subcomponents. The distribution of primary tumor origins among BM cases was as follows: gastrointestinal cancer (BM-GI, N = 14), breast cancer (BM-BC, N = 26), renal cancer (BM-RC, N = 16), melanoma (BM-M, N = 41), small cell lung cancer (BM-SCLC, N = 17), and non-small cell lung cancer (BM-NSCLC, N = 86) (Table 1). Consistent with the Pretreat-Mets cohort, BMs exhibited intermediate fractal complexity in the enhancing regions, whereas the non-enhancing and edema subcomponents showed markedly reduced FD3D relative to both LGG and HGG (Figure S6, Table S3).

Lacunarity comparisons between brain-mets, LGG, and HGG

Lac3D, a measure of spatial heterogeneity, also revealed significant differences among the three tumor groups across all subcomponents (Kruskal-Wallis tests: enhancing: H = 154.7, p = 2.6×10−34, df = 2; non-enhancing: H = 102.9, p = 4.5×10−23, df = 2; edema: H = 91.5, p = 1.3×10−20, df = 2).

For the enhancing subcomponent, BMs exhibited significantly higher Lac3D values than both LGG and HGG (p(BM-vs-LGG) = 8.8×10−6, p(BM-vs-HGG) = 2.5×10−34, and p(LGG-vs-HGG) = 0.0002) (Figure 3C, Table S2). The non-enhancing + necrosis component in BMs also significantly elevated Lac3D values than both LGG and HGG (p(BM-vs-LGG) = 4.9×10−15, p(BM-vs-HGG) = 1.2×10−17, and p(LGG-vs-HGG) = 1.0) (Figure 3E, Table S2). Edema in BMs was significantly more heterogeneous than in LGG or HGG, as reflected by higher Lac3D in BMs (p(BM-vs-LGG) = 4.1×10−13, p(BM-vs-HGG) = 6.8×10−15, and p(LGG-vs-HGG) = 1.0) (Figure 3G, Table S2).

Further, the BMs in the UCSF-BMSR cohort showed similar lacunarity patterns, with higher Lac3D values than both LGG and HGG across the enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous compartments (Figure S6, Table S3).

Integrating geometric measures and fractional volumetry with machine learning for brain-metastases vs. glioma differentiation

Supervised ML models trained on the FD3D values of the enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous tumor subcomponents achieved a classification accuracy of 84.8 ± 4.2% in distinguishing BM from gliomas (Figure S8, Tables S4 and S5). Notably, the integration of FD3D with the fractional volumetric contributions of subcomponents markedly enhanced the model’s discriminative performance, achieving an accuracy of 91.9 ± 2.8%. Among the evaluated algorithms, the SVM classifier demonstrated the highest overall performance, achieving a balanced accuracy of 91.9 ± 2.8%, sensitivity of 92.0 ± 4.3%, specificity of 93.5 ± 3.4%, and an AUC-ROC of 0.97 ± 0.02. Feature importance breakdown identified FD3D of the non-enhancing and edema subcomponents as the top two discriminative features for distinguishing BMs from gliomas (Figure 5A (III)). Comparison of fractal dimensions derived from manual and automated tumor subcomponent masks

In the 29 patients with BM (from the Pretreat-Mets cohort), used as test data for the trained nn-Unet model to generate automated segmentation masks, we compared the FD3D values derived from automated segmentation masks with those obtained from manually segmented masks. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed no significant differences between the two approaches (Figures S13A–S13C). These findings indicate that as long as the tumor subcomponents are precisely segmented using the required expertise, the method itself does not significantly alter the geometric metrics.

Passing-Bablok regression further confirmed strong concordance and the absence of systematic or proportional bias between the manual and automated segmentation. For the enhancing component, the slope was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.78–1.01) with an intercept of 0.07 (Figure S13D). The non-enhancing component showed a slope of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.81–1.04) and an intercept of 0.0 (Figure S13E). For the edema subcomponent, the slope was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.61–1.30), with an intercept of 0.16 (Figure S13F). Notably, the 95% confidence intervals for all slopes included 1, confirming the absence of proportional bias for any tumor subcomponent.

These findings collectively indicate that the observed differences in FD3D across tumor subcomponents are not attributable to methodological bias, thereby establishing the robustness and reproducibility of the quantitative FD3D measurements and their clinical utility.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that BMs, despite originating from diverse extracranial primaries, adopt a conserved geometric complexity distinct from gliomas, with FD3D and Lac3D providing robust, non-Euclidean descriptors of tumor subcomponents. By resolving enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous compartments, we showed that BMs converge to a uniform morpho-architectural phenotype, whereas gliomas retain heterogeneity shaped by lineage-specific growth and diffuse infiltration. Importantly, this convergence was observed consistently across independent imaging and transcriptomic cohorts, with BMs unified by low-variance pathways (TNFα/NF-κB, TGF-β, p53), while gliomas displayed fractality-linked associations with solute transport, EMT, angiogenesis, and cytoskeletal remodeling. Clinically, the inclusion of peritumoral edema fractality not only improved BM vs. glioma discrimination but also identified it as a prognostic marker unique to BMs, where low FD3D was associated with significantly poor survival. Together, these findings establish a clinically relevant, geometry-informed neuroimaging framework that addresses one of the most persistent diagnostic challenges in neuro-oncology: distinguishing BMs from gliomas in radiologically ambiguous cases.

BMs, despite originating from biologically distinct primary tumors, exhibit conserved fractal-complexity (FD3D) and broadly similar lacunarity (Lac3D) across tumor subcomponents. This geometric consistency suggests that once metastatic lesions colonize the brain microenvironment, they may adopt a common morpho-architectural phenotype that is largely independent of their tissue of origin. The enhancing regions of BMs were structurally more complex than LGGs but less than HGGs, placing BMs between these primary brain tumor classes along a morphological complexity axis. The FD3D of the enhancing subcomponent in BMs was closer to HGGs, suggesting that BMs, despite being secondary lesions, exhibit microstructural patterns of proliferation and vascularization that resemble the infiltrative and chaotic growth characteristic of HGGs. This observation extends prior evidence linking FD with glioma grade and proliferative potential, suggesting that fractal geometry may also serve as a non-invasive biomarker of tumor aggressiveness in BMs, regardless of their primary origin.8,9,10,11 However, the internal architecture of enhancement in BMs is more organized and smoother than HGGs, suggesting that enhancement may arise primarily from leaky neo-vasculature at the tumor-brain interface rather than the diffuse, chaotic vasculature seen in glioblastomas.

Conversely, the lower FD3D in the non-enhancing and peritumoral edematous regions of BMs than LGGs and HGGs indicates that these regions in BMs predominantly represent vasogenic fluid accumulation without significant infiltrative tumor cells, unlike gliomas, particularly in HGGs, which are marked by infiltrative tumor spread, reactive gliosis, and vascular remodeling.12,13 It further establishes that the internal architecture and spatial complexity of these subcomponents in BMs are structurally simpler and present with well-defined geometry and boundaries. Furthermore, BMs exhibit the highest lacunarity among the three groups across all three compartments: enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous, reflecting a disordered tumor microenvironment marked by architectural breakdown and spatial chaos, features typical of poor prognosis and therapeutic resistance. The high lacunarity observed in the peritumoral edema of BMs further confirms a predominantly vasogenic, non-cellular fluid accumulation, as opposed to the cell-rich, structurally heterogeneous infiltrative edema seen in gliomas, especially in HGGs, in line with the observations from the fractal complexity.

Upon integrating the FD3D or Lac3D estimates in ML models, it was evident that a combination of FD3D (or Lac3D) of all three subcomponents (enhancing, non-enhancing + necrosis and edema) provided the maximum accuracy in delineating BMs from gliomas. However, in our prior study delineating between LGG and HGG, the fractal measurements of only enhancing and non-enhancing + necrosis subcomponents were sufficient and provided the highest accuracy.7 Inclusion of geometric complexity of edema provides enhanced sensitivity for delineating brain-mets and gliomas. Including fractional volume together with fractal measures of all three subcomponents provided increased sensitivity and accuracy (accuracy ∼91.9% and 97.4%, AUC ∼0.97 and 0.99) for differentiating brain-mets and gliomas (Figures 5 and S9–S11). Importantly, feature importance breakdown revealed that the FD3D of the edema and non-enhancing subcomponents were consistently among the top two features differentiating BMs from gliomas across cohorts. A few prior studies have employed advanced MRI techniques to differentiate gliomas from BMs, focusing predominantly on either the tumor core, the peritumoral zone, or signal intensity-based features. For instance, Lu et al. (2003) and Holly et al. (2018) employed diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to interrogate tumor and peritumoral regions, achieving moderate performance (accuracy ∼80%, AUC ∼0.81).14,15 Perfusion imaging and MR spectroscopy have also shown promise in distinguishing BMs from gliomas.16,17 A study by Zeng et al. reported high accuracy (88%) and AUC (0.95) of discriminating BMs from gliomas using radiomic features derived from tumor-core, but did not account for peritumoral edema.18 Our study integrates FD3D and fractional volume across all three major tumor subcomponents, enabling a more holistic assessment of tumor morphology and its biological implications.

Among tumor subcomponents, FD3D of edema subcomponent not only provided enhanced diagnostic discrimination between BMs and gliomas but also served as a prognostic marker for patients with BM, with lower FD3D values (<1.83) associated with a 2-fold increase in mortality risk (HR = 2.03; Figure 6). This prognostic relevance of edema subcomponents in BMs is an interesting signature, given our prior work in gliomas, wherein it is the fractality of enhancing and non-enhancing subcomponents that is deterministic of survival in gliomas, not the edema subcomponent.7 This highlights the geometric complexity of distinct subcomponents, which may serve as a potential radiological signature definitive of tumor-type-specific prognostication for a given patient.

By quantifying subcomponent-specific geometric complexity, our method captures structural heterogeneity that is not accessible through whole-tumor morphology or conventional radiomic features. Importantly, while expert neuroradiologists demonstrate high proficiency in distinguishing BMs from gliomas, prior studies have consistently shown that diagnostic accuracy based solely on conventional MRI varies widely in radiologically ambiguous cases. Reported human-reader performance typically ranges from 75 to 82% sensitivity and specificity. For instance, Stadlbauer et al. (2022) observed 81% sensitivity and 76% specificity for differentiating solitary metastases from glioblastoma.19 Fordham et al. (2021) showed that qualitative MRI features such as edema-to-tumor ratio or FLAIR signal patterns yield highly imbalanced sensitivities and specificities (84%/45% and 44%/91% depending on presentation),2 and Teunissen et al. (2022) reported pooled MRI-based values of 79% sensitivity and 76% specificity across 16 studies.20 These findings underscore the inherent variability of visual interpretation, especially in lesions with overlapping radiological characteristics.

Our quantitative, subcomponent-resolved geometric approach provides a standardized and reproducible adjunct to expert review. The method generates noninvasive, field-strength-independent non-Euclidean metrics (FD3D, Lac3D) directly from standard-of-care MRI, requiring no additional sequences or invasive procedures. Its integration potential is high, as the approach is compatible with automated segmentation frameworks and can be incorporated into existing radiology workflows without altering imaging protocols. By providing objective, quantitative measurements, the framework reduces inter-observer variability and strengthens diagnostic confidence, particularly in radiologically ambiguous cases where visual assessment alone becomes challenging. Collectively, these features position the framework as a practical, biologically informed, and clinically adaptable tool that enhances diagnostic interpretation and supports decision-making in neuro-oncology.

The immediate and anticipated clinical significance of this study are: (1) Neuroimaging-based quantitative estimates of fractal geometry as a precise diagnostic measure of radiographical discrimination of BMs and gliomas. This approach provides a tumor subcomponent-aware, geometry-driven framework that leverages standard-of-care MRI for non-invasive tumor classification, not merely using the overall tumor geometry. (2) Prognostication based on the FD3D of the edema subcomponent in BMs. By computing the geometric metrics immediately upon imaging, here, we provide a strong foundation claiming that, by integrating quantitative geometry estimations of tumor subcomponents in radiographic and neuro-oncologic assessments, a precise and informed diagnosis and prognosis will be possible, bypassing the need for immediate tumor biopsy for clinical decision making.

Moreover, our pathway-level transcriptomic analysis reveals a striking molecular segregation between gliomas and BMs. Despite the diverse extracranial origins, the PCA demonstrated that BMs form a cohesive and discrete cluster, suggesting a convergent adaptation to the brain microenvironment that is distinct from the molecular programs driving primary brain tumors (Figures 7 and S14). The PCA loadings clarify that the dominant transcriptomic divergence between gliomas and BMs is driven by pathways with high PC1 weights, whereas pathways contributing to PC2 reflect intra-group biological heterogeneity. This joint PCA across both tumor classes therefore identifies the pathway-level programs that underlie both inter-tumor type separation and within-group variation. It was observed that the Hallmark EMT pathways exhibited significantly higher activity in HGG compared to both LGG and BMs, suggesting a more mesenchymal, invasive phenotype in HGGs. This enrichment was corroborated by elevated activity in TNFα signaling via NF-κB and TGF-β signaling, both of which are canonical regulators of EMT and associated with tumor aggressiveness and immune modulation.21 Other key pathways driving the divergence included KRAS Signaling, angiogenesis, hypoxia, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, and apoptosis processes critical for metastatic colonization and survival in the CNS niche. In contrast, BMs were characterized by a convergent set of low-variance hallmark pathways, including TNFα signaling via NF-κB, TGF-β, apical junction, IL2/STAT5, p53, xenobiotic metabolism, and estrogen response programs. This convergence indicates that BMs adopt common molecular programs, reflecting selective pressures imposed by the brain-microenvironment.22 The low variance in immune-related pathways, such as TNFα/NF-κB and IL2/STAT5, suggests that BMs follow similar molecular remodeling to evade destruction by the brain’s unique immune landscape.23 Concurrently, the low variance across the TGF-β and apical junction pathways suggests a shared process followed by BMs to enter and sustain themselves in the brain-environment. The low variability in these pathways points to a convergent strategy of losing cell adhesion and adopting a sustained EMT-like program, which enhances the invasive and survival properties.24 Interestingly, a recent transcriptomic study (Sanghvi et al.) has shown that metastatic tumors tend to adopt molecular signatures resembling those of the target tissue, rather than retaining those of their tissue of origin.25 We also observe a common cluster of molecular pathways in BMs and conserved fractal-complexity and lacunarity in BMs, suggesting a shared morpho-architectural adaptation within the brain-microenvironment. This biological convergence at both the geometric and transcriptomic levels pinpoint an interesting biology, establishing the brain’s dominant role in reprogramming metastatic tumor behavior.

The enhancing, non-enhancing, and edematous compartments in HGGs exhibited distinct sets of uniquely correlated transcripts, suggesting compartmentalized molecular programs driving local tumor morphology. Subcomponent-specific gene sets showed no overlap between whole-tumor FD3D-associated transcripts and those linked to the enhancing or non-enhancing regions, suggestive of unique geometric-molecular profiles. Edema shared only a small number of transcripts with whole-tumor FD3D, and these were more strongly associated with edema geometry. The radiogenomic associations from tumor segmented subcomponents and bulk-RNA seq potentially capture molecular programs that preferentially influence the geometry of individual subcomponents rather than global tumor morphology.

Functionally, the FD3D-correlated gene sets in HGGs were enriched in pathways central to glioma pathophysiology, including cytoskeletal remodeling, EMT, hypoxia response, angiogenesis, and edema regulation processes previously implicated in tumor invasion, microenvironmental remodeling, and therapeutic resistance. Particularly striking was the peritumoral edema zone, which showed the highest number of FD3D-correlated transcripts, suggesting that morphological irregularity at the tumor margins may be driven by active, spatially encoded molecular processes rather than passive fluid accumulation.

For the edema component in HGGs, positive correlations between FD3D and transporters such as SLC39A9 (ZIP9), SLC12A2 (NKCC1), and SLC25A23/42 suggest that increased structural irregularity in this zone may be linked to ion transport, cytoskeletal signaling, and mitochondrial function (Figure 8). This aligns with prior evidence implicating NKCC1 in promoting cell volume changes and invasion at the tumor margin, and supports the idea that metabolic and osmotic adaptations contribute to peritumoral tissue remodeling.26,27 Conversely, in the enhancing compartment, SLCs such as SLC4A3, SLC43A3, and SLC28A3 were negatively correlated with FD3D. SLC28A3, involved in nucleoside salvage and drug uptake, may mark metabolically active yet morphologically uniform tumor clusters, whereas reduced complexity may reflect focal expansion zones of clonal tumor growth with limited architectural branching. Given the high vascularity and proliferative index of enhancing zones, this inverse relationship may indicate that the higher expression of ion exchangers and nucleoside transporters is associated with structurally simpler, more cohesive tumor niches. In the non-enhancing region, a distinct set of SLCs showed a negative correlation with FD3D.28 These SLCs are transporters, predominantly associated with glycosylation (SLC35 family) or organic anion transport (SLCO family), highlighting an intriguing inverse association between structural heterogeneity and cellular secretory or detoxification capacity in non-enhancing subcomponents as a feature of HGG (Figure 8).29 Together, these subcomponent-specific correlations highlight that SLC transporters are not merely passive facilitators of solute movement but active molecular architects of tumor geometry. Their expression patterns track closely with the fractal irregularity of tumor tissue, offering mechanistic insights into how metabolic, ionic, and biosynthetic programs are spatially organized in GBM.

The unique geometric complexity shared by BMs, yet distinct from gliomas, was consistently replicated across two independent BM cohorts and four glioma cohorts, providing strong evidence for the reproducibility and generalizability of fractality- and lacunarity-based quantitative approaches in neuro-oncological settings. Concordance between manual and automated segmentation further confirmed that these measurements capture genuine biological distinctions rather than being biased by segmentation methodology, supporting their ease of integration across sites. FD3D thus emerges as a non-invasive quantitative neuroimaging biomarker that can be seamlessly incorporated into standard MRI workflows to distinguish BMs from gliomas.

In gliomas, where imaging and transcriptomic analyses were performed in the same cohort, we observed paired transcriptome-geometry coupling, with fractal complexity correlating with gene expression programs involving solute transport, EMT, angiogenesis, and cytoskeletal remodeling. In contrast, for BMs, geometric convergence was demonstrated in multi-institutional imaging cohorts and transcriptomic convergence in independent datasets (50 MSigDB hallmark and 196 PID canonical pathways). Transcriptomic profiling in BM cohorts, distinct from the imaging cohort, reflected shared molecular pathways, for hallmark gene sets such as TNFα signaling via NF-κB, TGF-β signaling, apical junction, IL2/STAT5 signaling, p53 pathway, xenobiotic metabolism, early estrogen response, late estrogen response, spermatogenesis, and myogenesis, irrespective of primary origin. Although the imaging and molecular datasets in BMs were not paired, their convergence provides a substantial claim that transcriptomic remodeling underlies the uniform geometric phenotype of BMs independent of tissue of origin. Collectively, these findings highlight that BMs and gliomas each possess unique geometry-molecular relationships, with gliomas exhibiting heterogeneity linked to lineage-specific biology, and BMs displaying cohesive remodeling upon adaptation to the brain.

Limitations of the study

In this work, imaging and transcriptomic analyses for BMs were obtained from distinct cohorts, whereas matched MRI-genomic data were available for gliomas. In gliomas, paired analyses demonstrated clear transcriptome-geometry coupling, while in BMs, the observation of geometric convergence in imaging datasets and transcriptomic convergence across large pathway-level analyses strongly suggests that similar coupling underlies their uniform morpho-architectural phenotype. Although TCGA requires tumor sections to contain ≥60% tumor nuclei and <20% necrosis, the bulk RNA-seq samples are not spatially annotated. Consequently, the radiogenomic associations presented here may gain substantially greater biological and clinical interpretability when future studies incorporate single-cell or spatial transcriptomics with anatomically mapped tissue sampling. Further, future development of paired BM cohorts will strengthen this foundation by generating paired datasets at two levels: (1) Neuroimaging and transcriptomic measures in the same patients with BM, together with preoperative MRI/MRS and (2) paired molecular and imaging features of the primary tumor of origin (e.g., HER2, ER, and PR status in breast cancer) alongside matched brain metastasis imaging and genomics. These integrative resources would enable deeper insights into tumor-host adaptation and accelerate the clinical translation of geometry-informed neuroimaging biomarkers. Moreover, combining in vivo 1H MRS-based metabolic mapping with geometric complexity may provide an even more powerful non-invasive platform for diagnosis, prognosis, and clinical decision-making in neuro-oncology.

By demonstrating that fractal geometry of the edema compartment uniquely improves both diagnosis and prognosis in BMs, our study establishes immediate clinical utility while laying the foundation for future paired imaging-genomics datasets to refine geometry-molecular coupling.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study investigated tumor subcomponent geometry using preoperative and pretreatment MR images of Brain-Mets from breast cancer (BM-BC), lung cancer (BM-LC), renal cancer (BM-RC), gastrointestinal cancer (BM-GI), melanoma (BM-M), low-grade gliomas, and glioblastomas (Table 1). The imaging data utilized in the study were obtained from tumor repositories, namely Pretreat-MetsToBrain-Masks (Pretreat-Mets),37 BraTS-TCGA-LGG (Low-Grade-Glioma),30 BraTS-TCGA-GBM (Glioblastoma Multiforme),31 and provided by TCIA (The Cancer Imaging Archive)38 of the National Cancer Institute. Additionally, this study also utilized imaging data from the University of California San Francisco Preoperative Diffuse Glioma MRI (UCSF-PDGM),32 UPENN-GBM,33 and BraTS-Africa cohorts available in the TCIA repository and the UCSF-BMSR39 brain metastasis cohort, upon request to the UCSF dataset of Imaging (https://imagingdatasets.ucsf.edu/dataset/1). The TCIA is a publicly accessible database containing comprehensive radiological, genomic, and clinical data pertinent to various cancer types. This study included only those subjects who had preoperative MRI (T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), T2w-fluid attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR), and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1Gd)) along with a pathologically proven diagnosis of brain-mets and glioma. All patients included in Pretreat-Mets, UCSF-BMSR, TCGA-LGG, TCGA-GBM, UCSF-PDGM, UPENN-GBM, and BraTS-Africa cohorts were recruited with written informed consent in accordance with the approved IRBs at the participating sites of the TCGA and UCSF research networks (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/). We obtained the de-identified imaging and molecular data using the NBIA data retriever from the TCIA portal and UCSF portal upon request.

Method details

Tumor segmentation and mask generation

The preoperative scans were initially skull-stripped and co-registered before the tumor segmentation. The tumor mass segmentation into subcomponents for all Pretreat-Mets cohort BM patients was performed manually and was approved by two neuroradiologists.37 For glioma patients and the UCSF-BMSR BM patients,40 the tumor mass was segmented into subcomponents using an automated segmentation ensemble model that consists of BraTS challenge-winning segmentation algorithms, which have been top-ranked in the BraTS challenge. The segmentation labels were manually corrected and endorsed by an expert neuroradiologist, and they were made available for clinical analysis, model training, and evaluation.30,31,32,33,34,40,41 The segmentation included three major tumor subcomponents: enhancing component (ET), non-enhancing + necrotic component (NET), and peritumoral edema (ED).

Fractal dimension and lacunarity measurement for tumor subcomponents

A Python-based in-house pipeline was developed to quantify the fractal dimension (FD) using the 2D box-counting function from IMEA42 and lacunarity via the gliding-box algorithm combined with the binned probability distribution method43 for each tumor subcomponent.7 The overall workflow of calculating fractality and lacunarity measures from MRI-based tumor subcomponents in BMs and Glioma patients is described in Figure 1. The tumor regions were extracted from each slice by binarizing the whole tumor mask, followed by contour detection and bounding-rectangle generation (Figure 1B). Within these bounding boxes, tumor subcomponents were annotated (ET, NET, and ED), and subsequently, FD and lacunarity were measured across each subcomponent in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes using the box-counting method and the gliding-box algorithm, respectively.

The mean fractal dimension (FDAxial, FDCoronal, FDSagittal) and mean lacunarity (LacAxial, LacCoronal, LacSagittal) were estimated for the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, respectively. The 3D fractal dimension (FD3D) for each subcomponent was then determined by averaging the planar FD values (Figure 1A; Equation 1).(Equation 1)FD3D=FDAxial+FDCoronal+FDSagittal3

Similarly, the 3D lacunarity (Lac3D) was calculated by averaging the planar lacunarity values (Equation 2).(Equation 2)Lac3D=LacAxial+LacCoronal+LacSagittal3

The median FD3D and Lac3D of the enhancing, non-enhancing, and edema subcomponents were compared between all the Brain-Mets and gliomas to estimate the extent of structural irregularities and complexity. The geometric measures were also compared across the BM arising from different origins. Moreover, we also estimated the volume of each tumor subcomponent using the segmentation mask. Subsequently, the fractional volume of each tumor subcomponent relative to total tumor volume was estimated (Figure 4A).

Supervised machine learning model for discriminating brain-mets and glioma

FD3D and Lac3D measures were integrated into supervised machine learning (ML) models for precise discrimination between BMs and gliomas. The ML algorithms employing FD3D measures of the tumor subcomponents (both individually and in combination) were trained and tested in compliance with the Pareto principle,44 using FD3D values from tumor subcomponents as features and tumor type (BM vs. glioma) as the target variable.

We tested four supervised ML models to rule out any chance of methodological bias, mainly support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and XGBoost (XGB).45 In order to identify the most effective and specific combination of features for accurately distinguishing Brain-Mets from Gliomas, the ML models were independently trained and tested for various combinations of features: a. FD3D, b. Lac3D, c. fractional volume, d. FD3D with Lac3D, and e. FD3D with fractional volume.

Furthermore, a repeated-stratified k-fold (k=5) cross-validation technique (with five repetitions) was performed to ensure the robustness of the models.46 The data were partitioned into five-folds (5-subsets randomly clustered from the total dataset) such that each fold maintained a similar distribution of Brain-Mets and Glioma. Performance was assessed by estimating balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Also, the confusion matrices were generated for all the cross-validations, and an average normalized confusion matrix was calculated. Additionally, feature importance was estimated for the feature set combination of the best-performing model using permutation importance. Given the substantial class imbalance between BMs and Glioma patients, four data imbalance handling strategies were incorporated: (i) Cost-sensitive learning, (ii) synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE), (iii) majority-class undersampling (NearMiss), and (iv) hybrid approach combining oversampling and undersampling (SMOTE + NearMiss).47

Evaluation of effects of manual and automated segmented tumor subcomponents on fractality and lacunarity estimates

The impact of the segmentation approach (Manual vs Automated) on FD3D estimation was tested in the Pretreat-Mets cohort (n = 200). We used an automated segmentation method to generate masks of subcomponents, based on a nnU-Net model,48 which we trained on multimodal MR (T1w, T2w, T2-FLAIR, and T1Gd) images from 171 BM patients with corresponding manual masks annotated and verified by the radiologists. The trained model was then applied to an independent set of 29 BM patients not included in training, to generate automated tumor masks of the tumor subcomponents. FD3D was computed for the enhancing, non-enhancing + necrosis, and edema subcomponents. FD3D estimates from automated segmentation masks were compared with the estimates obtained from manual segmentation masks using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p < 0.01).

To further assess potential systematic or proportional bias, Passing-Bablok regression49,50 was performed, with separate models fitted for each subcomponent, and 95% CIs for the slopes were determined (Figure S13).

Molecular profiling in gliomas and brain metastases

Processed and normalized RNA-seq gene expression for gliomas, including low-grade glioma (LGG; n=530) and glioblastoma multiforme (GBM; n=171), were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).51,52 BM samples (n=107) were curated from Zhang et al., including only intracranial metastases from extracranial primary tumors: BM-BC (n=70), BM-M (n=27), BM-LC (n=8), and BM-RC (n=2).35 To enable downstream analyses, only genes common across all datasets were retained.

Gene set enrichment was quantified at the single-sample level using AUCell,53 a rank-based method. AUCell scores were calculated for all 50 Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB),54 capturing key biological processes relevant to tumor behavior. To assess global pathway activity patterns across tumor types, we performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the enrichment score matrix (samples × pathways), enabling visualization of shared and distinct biological pathways in gliomas and BMs. The pathway loadings (eigenvector coefficients) for PC1 and PC2 represent the weighted contribution of each Hallmark pathway to the variance captured by the corresponding principal component. To deduce the shared common pathways within the BMs, a variance analysis of AUCell scores was performed by computing the variance of the pathway activity across 50 hallmark pathways. The top 10 Hallmark pathways with the lowest variance were identified for BMs. Similar variance analyses were conducted for the gliomas.

Additionally, to further test the molecular convergence in brain metastases and its uniqueness from the gliomas, we have conducted pathway-level transcriptomic analysis for 196 Canonical Pathways from another database ‘Pathway Interaction Database (PID)’, a collection in MSigDB. The AUCell scoring and PCA analyses were performed for these 196 Canonical pathway-specific gene sets.

Furthermore, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using z-normalised activity scores derived from multiple curated gene set collections, including Hallmark pathways, Gene Ontology metabolic processes,55 Reactome,56 and the Human Phenotype Ontology.57 The analysis was conducted across primary gliomas: LGG and HGG, as well as BMs (Figure S15). Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, with p-values < 0.01 considered significant.

Transcriptional signatures associated with geometric complexity of tumor regions

TPM-normalized bulk RNA-seq profiles from TCGA-LGG (n = 61) and TCGA-GBM (n = 19) cohorts were obtained for the patients with matched preoperative MRI. Only protein-coding genes (19,981) were retained for transcriptomic analyses, while pseudogenes were excluded. Associations between imaging-derived geometric metrics (FD3D and Lac3D) and gene expression levels were assessed separately for the enhancing (ET), non-enhancing + necrosis (NET), and edema (ED) subcomponents, using Spearman correlation. Genes exhibiting strong correlations (|r| > 0.5, p < 0.05) were considered significant. To delineate subcomponent-specific transcriptional programs, significantly correlated genes were further filtered to retain only those uniquely associated with a single tumor subcomponent. Subcomponent-specific genes with strong positive (r >0.5) or negative (r <-0.5) correlations were then identified independently in GBM and LGG, enabling the characterization of transcriptional programs unique to each tumor subcomponent (Figure 8A). Gene correlation analysis was additionally performed using whole-tumor FD3D to identify transcripts associated with global tumor geometry. Genes correlating significantly (|r| > 0.5, p < 0.05) with whole-tumor FD3D were compared with subcomponent-specific FD3D-associated genes to identify any overlap.

To further pinpoint the genomic status of the unique genes associated with glioma subcomponents from BMs, we used RNA-seq data of BM-M patients, reported in the study by Biermann et al., from the NCBI-GEO (GEOID: GSE200217) database.36 Differential expression analysis was conducted for FD3D-associated genes between gliomas and BMs.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using in-house scripts and publicly available packages in Python (v3.11) and R (v4.1.2). The Kruskal-Wallis H-test was performed to evaluate the differences in FD3D, Lac3D, and fractional volume of the three tumor subcomponents, both across the brain-mets of different primary origins and between LGG, HGG, and BMs. For all post-hoc pairwise comparisons, Dunn’s test, with Bonferroni correction, was applied. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p <0.01 (Figures 2, 3, 4, and S1–S7; Tables 1 and S1–S3).

To develop a reliable and accurate ML model for predicting the brain-mets or glioma, four supervised machine learning models (SVM, KNN, RF, and XGB) were trained and tested using scikit-learn Python library.58 Model performance was evaluated for each tumor subcomponent (and their combinations) using ROC-AUC, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and normalized confusion matrices (Figures 5 and S8–S11; Tables S4–S10).

Optimal cutoff values for overall survival based on the fractal dimension and fractional volume of tumor subcomponents were determined using maximally selected rank statistics implemented in the maxstat R package (v0.7.25).59 Cutoff selection was constrained to ensure that each stratified group contained at least 10% of the total cohort, thereby maintaining adequate sample size for survival analysis. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were performed in Python using the scikit-survival library (v0.24.1) to compare overall survival between groups stratified by values lower or higher than the optimal cutoff for each subcomponent, with statistical significance evaluated using the log-rank test (p<0.05). Furthermore, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards (CPH) model, including all tumor subcomponents and age as covariates, was fitted to evaluate the independent prognostic value of fractal dimension and fractional volume (Figures 6 and S12).

All transcriptomic analyses were conducted in R (v4.1.2). AUCell was used to compute single-sample gene set enrichment scores for the 50 MSigDB Hallmark pathways and 196 PID pathways (Figures 7, S14, and S15). Spearman correlation was applied to identify genes significantly associated (|r| >0.5, p<0.05) with FD3D and Lac3D metrics across tumor subcomponents. Differential expression analysis between glioma and BM samples was performed using the DESeq2 package, focusing on genes correlated with geometric complexity (Figures 8 and S16–S23; Table S11).
