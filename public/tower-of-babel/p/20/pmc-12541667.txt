An Unusual Presentation of Erysipelas: Involvement of the Abdominal Wall
Authors: Rova Malala Fandresena Randrianarisoa, Scholastique Ngo Souck M, Mostefa Koroghli, Ismail Meziane, Catherine Letrillard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a case of uncomplicated erysipelas of the abdominal wall in a 39‐year‐old immunocompetent man. Prompt recognition of this atypical presentation is essential to prevent diagnostic delays and complications. Abdominal wall erysipelas is rare and may be overlooked. Careful clinical evaluation ensures accurate diagnosis and timely management.

Case Presentation

A 39‐year‐old man was admitted with fever and painful erythematous lesions on his abdomen that developed over 2 days. The lesions initially appeared in the hypogastric region and rapidly spread to both iliac fossae, becoming increasingly painful. His medical history included hypertension, obesity, a surgically treated abdominal aortic dissection 4 years earlier, and a supraumbilical hernia. On admission, he was febrile at 39°C, with normal hemodynamic parameters. There were no systemic symptoms. Examination revealed a well‐defined, painful, erythematous plaque on the lower abdomen, extending from the left to the right iliac fossa (Figure 1A). Two small punctiform lesions were noted in the periumbilical region, which the patient frequently manipulated. The abdomen was soft, and the rest of the examination was unremarkable. Biological tests showed a C‐reactive protein level of 120 mg/L and a procalcitonin level of 14.53 ng/mL. Lipase, liver, renal, and coagulation function tests were within normal limits. The HIV serology was nonreactive. The blood and urine cultures were negative. To rule out pancreatitis or hernia‐related complications, an abdominopelvic CT scan was performed, which revealed no abnormalities. A diagnosis of uncomplicated abdominal wall erysipelas was made. The patient was treated with amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid at 75 mg/kg/day for 7 days. The skin erythema resolved (Figure 1B), and inflammatory markers decreased. The clinical course was marked by the disappearance of skin erythema (Figure 1B) and normalization of inflammatory markers, with no recurrence observed during the 6‐month follow‐up.

Photograph of the patient's abdomen. (A) Erythematous plaque of the lower abdomen, involving the hypogastric region and both iliac fossae, 2 days after erysipelas onset. (B) Disappearance of erythema on Day 7 of treatment.

Erysipelas is an acute dermal infection often caused by Group A Streptococcus. Diagnosis is clinical, based on well‐demarcated inflammatory erythema and fever. The lower limbs are most commonly affected, followed by the face [1]. Erysipelas of the abdominal wall is rare but has been reported [1, 2]. Its causes are similar to those of classic erysipelas, including skin lesions and postoperative infections. Some cases are associated with perforation of an intraperitoneal hollow organ [3]. The main reported risk factors are obesity and previous abdominal surgery. Our patient was both obese and had a history of abdominal surgery. The lesions in the periumbilical region likely served as the entry point for the infection. Clinical manifestations of this entity, particularly the cutaneous lesions, resemble those of erysipelas affecting the lower limbs. The treatment of erysipelas is based on antibiotic therapy targeting streptococci. Penicillin monotherapy remains the first‐line treatment, including for erysipelas of the abdominal wall [1]. In cases presenting with systemic signs of infection, some authors have reported using cefazolin, clindamycin, or piperacillin‐tazobactam [2, 3]. In our case, due to the atypical location of the erysipelas, empirical antibiotic therapy with amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid was initiated to provide broader‐spectrum coverage. The favorable clinical outcome confirmed the appropriateness of this therapeutic choice.

This case highlights an atypical erysipelas presentation, underscoring the need for careful clinical assessment to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.
