Fluorescently engineered KRAS-mutant organoids as versatile tools for in vitro and in vivo cancer modeling
Authors: Alireza Savadipour, Krishna S. Tummala, Eric S. Muise, Mark Buchanan, Vania Vidimar, Mike Veling, Jun Liu, Johnny Kopinja, Charlie Schneider, Brian Pak Ho Chan, Richard Carr, Scott Norton, John M. Gaspar, Jennifer Piesvaux, Erica Leccese, Eunsil Park, Lily Y. Moy, Brian J. Long, Nicolas Solban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organoids have emerged as advanced models in cancer research, offering superior physiological relevance compared to traditional 2D cell cultures. They mimic the three-dimensional architecture and tumor clonal heterogeneity, providing more accurate insights into tumor biology and drug responses.Despite these advantages, organoid-based studies pose significant technical challenges. In in-vitro studies, imaging and drug screening—particularly in co-culture systems—can be difficult. In in-vivo models, assessing tumor characteristics and organoid populations becomes challenging when using heterogeneous organoid mixtures with different mutation profiles. This challenge is further compounded when organoids are not engineered to express imageable markers. To address these limitations, we developed organoids that express green fluorescent protein (GFP) or red fluorescent protein (RFP). We found that factors such as cell seeding density, medium composition, and dissociation methods significantly affect organoid growth and IC50 values in response to chemotherapy drugs in in-vitro systems. Furthermore, we demonstrated that engineering organoids to express GFP or RFP did not significantly alter their growth, drug response, or gene expression profiles. Lastly, in in-vivo studies, we observed no significant changes in tumor growth, and morphology between engineered organoids and their parental lines. In conclusion, our findings suggest that GFP- and RFP-expressing organoids retain the key characteristics of their parental lines and can serve as robust tools for both in-vitro and in-vivo drug screening studies.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most formidable challenges in contemporary medical research, necessitating the development of advanced models that can accurately mimic the complex biology of tumors [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Traditional two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures, while invaluable for many decades, fall short in replicating the intricate microenvironment and heterogeneity of tumors found in-vivo [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. This gap has spurred the emergence of three-dimensional (3D) cell culture systems, among which organoids have gained significant attention. Organoids are self-organizing 3D structures that can recapitulate key aspects of the organ from which they originate, making them powerful models for studying cancer [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

The advent of organoids as advanced models in cancer research marks a pivotal shift in our approach to understanding and combating this multifaceted disease. Organoids offer a more physiologically relevant context compared to traditional 2D cultures, as they closely mimic the architecture and functionality of actual tumors [10,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. They provide a unique opportunity to study the tumor microenvironment, cellular heterogeneity, and complex cell-cell interactions in a controlled laboratory setting [4,15,[17], [18], [19], [20]]. This capability is particularly crucial for cancer research, where the tumor microenvironment plays a pivotal role in disease progression and response to therapy. By enabling the study of these factors in a more realistic context, organoids hold the promise of accelerating the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and improving the predictability of drug responses [9,11,[21], [22], [23], [24]].

Despite their promise, working with organoids presents several significant challenges across various research areas [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Generating organoids that faithfully replicate the characteristics of their tissue of origin requires precise control over differentiation and self-organization. This involves optimizing culture conditions, growth factors, and extracellular matrix components to guide cells into forming organoids with the desired structure and function. Additionally, maintaining the stability and reproducibility of organoids over extended culture periods can be difficult, posing challenges for longitudinal studies and high-throughput screening.

Imaging organoids presents another set of difficulties [30,31]. The 3D nature of organoids, while advantageous for mimicking in-vivo conditions, complicates the application of traditional imaging techniques. High-resolution imaging of organoids often requires advanced microscopy methods, such as confocal or multiphoton microscopy, which can penetrate deeper into 3D structures. Moreover, the dense cellular organization and the presence of extracellular matrix components can cause light scattering and absorption, further complicating imaging efforts. Developing and optimizing imaging protocols that can provide clear, high-resolution images of organoid structures and cellular interactions is crucial for leveraging the full potential of these models in cancer research.

In-vivo and in-vitro studies with organoids also face significant hurdles. Translating findings from organoid models to in-vivo systems necessitates careful consideration of how well the organoid recapitulates the in-vivo tumor environment [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36]]. Organoids may not perfectly mimic the complex interactions between tumor cells and other components of the tumor microenvironment, such as immune cells, stromal cells, and extracellular matrix [15,37,38]. This limitation underscores the need for rigorous validation of organoid-based findings in animal models. Additionally, establishing robust in-vitro assays that can leverage the unique properties of organoids while providing reproducible and interpretable results remains an ongoing challenge [[39], [40], [41], [42]].

Before running assays, significant optimizations are required to ensure that organoids provide reliable and reproducible results. This involves standardizing culture conditions, growth medium, and extracellular matrix components to reduce variability between experiments [[43], [44], [45], [46]]. Additionally, optimizing the seeding density and growth period can help in achieving organoids that are consistent in size and cellular composition [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. These optimizations are essential to minimize experimental variability and enhance the reliability of assays conducted using organoids.

One of the exciting frontiers in organoid research is the engineering of organoids to express specific markers, such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) and red fluorescent protein (RFP) [[51], [52], [53]]. These engineered organoids can facilitate a wide range of studies, including tracking cellular dynamics, studying cell-cell interactions, and monitoring the effects of therapeutic interventions in real time [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55]]. However, introducing such modifications necessitates ensuring that the engineered organoids retain the characteristics of the parental line. Any changes in cellular behavior, growth patterns, or response to stimuli introduced by genetic modifications need to be carefully evaluated to ensure the validity of experimental results [[51], [52], [53], [54], [55]].

Engineered organoids offer particular promise for co-culture studies, where different cell types are grown together to study their interactions within a 3D context. Co-culturing organoids with immune cells, stromal cells, or other relevant cell types such as organoids with different mutation profiles can provide deeper insights into the complex interplay between tumor cells and their microenvironment [51,[56], [57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65], [66], [67]]. This approach can help elucidate mechanisms of immune evasion, metastasis, bispecific effects, and response to therapy, offering valuable information that can guide the development of more effective cancer treatments.

In this study, we aimed to address these challenges by utilizing patient-derived pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) organoids (PDOs). PDAC is one of the most lethal forms of cancer, characterized by a dense stromal environment and significant cellular heterogeneity [[68], [69], [70], [71]]. By focusing on PDAC PDOs, we sought to optimize media and culture conditions, including seeding density and dissociation methods, to achieve robust and reproducible organoid cultures. We also compared the parental and engineered lines to ensure that the engineered organoids retained the characteristics of the parental line. Additionally, we optimized drug screening protocols to enhance the reliability of assays conducted with these organoids. Our goal was to create robust, reliable organoid models that can provide deeper insights into PDAC biology and serve as effective platforms for drug testing and therapeutic development. The introduction of GFP and RFP expressing organoids aimed to facilitate real-time tracking and co-culture studies, providing valuable tools for studying cellular dynamics and interactions within the tumor microenvironment.

In conclusion, while organoids represent a significant advancement in cancer research, their successful application requires addressing several technical challenges. Optimizing media and culture conditions, developing advanced imaging techniques, validating findings in-vivo, and ensuring the stability of engineered organoids are critical steps in harnessing the full potential of these models. As researchers continue to refine these methods, organoids are poised to become an indispensable tool in the quest to unravel the complexities of cancer and develop more effective therapeutic strategies [16,28].

Methods

Organoid culture and harvesting

Organoids were harvested using cold base medium (DMEM F12 with 1% Glutamax, 1% HEPES, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% BSA). After the first centrifugation, the medium and Matrigel were removed, and the organoids were washed one more time with base medium and centrifuged. Then, the medium was removed, and the organoids were resuspended in growth-factor reduced (GFR) Matrigel and plated. After placing the plate in incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) for at least 30 min upside down, the appropriate medium for each organoid was added to the plate and the plate was put back in the incubator. To culture PDO1 and PDO3 organoids, PTMNic medium (DMEM F12, 1% Glutamax, 1% HEPES, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 5 ng/mL EGF, 100 ng/mL FGF10, 10 nM Gastrin, 100 ng/mL recombinant human Noggin, 10 mM Nic, 1.25 mM N–Ac, 50 μg/mL Primocin, 250 ng/mL Rspo-3, 1x B-27, and Rho inhibitor (Y-27632)) and for PDO2, PTMWe medium (DMEM F12, 1% Glutamax, 1% HEPES, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 500 nM TGFβ inhibitor (A083-01), 100 ng/mL FGF10, 10 nM Gastrin, 100 ng/mL recombinant human Noggin, 10 mM Nicotinamide (Nic), 1.25 mM N-Acetylcysteine (N–Ac), 50 μg/mL Primocin, 250 ng/mL R-spondin 3 (Rspo-3), 1x B-27, Rho inhibitor (Y-27632), and 50% Wnt3a condition medium) were used. To prepare organoids for assays, after washing the organoids and removing medium after centrifugation, the organoid pellet was resuspended in 0.05% trypsin. The suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 3 min. Afterwards, the organoids were mechanically dissociated and returned to the incubator for an additional 3–5 min. The trypsin was then neutralized with medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and cells were counted using a Vi-Cell machine. Next, the cells were centrifuged, washed with cold base medium, and centrifuged again. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in the appropriate medium based on the cell count. The desired number of cells was taken and resuspended in a mixture of Matrigel and medium to achieve the final cell and Matrigel concentrations required for plating. All organoids were procured from a third-party company under a license and expanded following the company's Organoids' proprietary protocols.

High-throughput drug screening using CTG

Organoids were harvested, washed, and dissociated into single cells using 0.05% trypsin. The cells were counted and resuspended to achieve a final concentration of 83.33 or 416.66 cells/μL in 80% GFR Matrigel and 20% base medium. Then, 12 μL of the cell suspension was added to each well of a 384-well plate, resulting in a final cell number of 1000 or 5000 cells per well. After solidifying the Matrigel in an incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 30–60 min, 50 μL of complete medium of each organoid was added to the wells. After 3 days of culture, the medium was removed, and chemotherapy drugs, including gemcitabine and SN38, were added using an Echo automation system. The highest concentration used for each drug was 10 μM, with a 10-point, 1:3 serial dilution to prepare additional concentrations. For controls, organoids were treated with either DMSO (negative control) or 10 μM staurosporine (positive control). The organoids were cultured in the presence of chemotherapy drugs for 6 days, with a fresh dose added after 3 days of the initial treatment (total culture duration is 9 days).

On day 9, the medium was removed, and 50 μL of base medium mixed with CellTiter-Glo 3D (1:1 ratio) was added to each well. The mixture was pipetted 20 times using a liquid handler (BRAVO) to ensure even distribution. The plates were then centrifuged, sealed, and shaken for 30–45 min. Finally, the plates were centrifuged again, bottom of the plate was taped, and luminescence signals were measured.

Organoid confocal imaging

Organoids were imaged using Operetta confocal microscope. Briefly, the cells were put inside the operetta after pre-incubating the microscope to reach 37 °C and 5% CO2. Then, a Z-stack image of each well was taken to have a 3D image of the organoids. Afterwards, the images were transferred to SImALoader and processed in the software.

Preparing organoids for Incucyte imaging

For Incucyte imaging, organoids were harvested, dissociated with 0.05% Trypsin, counted, and resuspended in 80% Matrigel and 20% base medium to reach a final concentration of 150 organoids/μL. Afterwards, 50 μL of the resuspended organoids were added to each well of a 96 well plate to have 7500 cells in each well. The plate was centrifuged and incubated for 30–60 min to allow the Matrigel to solidify. Subsequently, 250 μL of culture medium was added to each well, and the plate was transferred to the Incucyte for imaging.

Incucyte imaging

After seeding the organoids in 96-well plates, letting the Matrigel solidify, and feeding the organoids, the plate was transferred into the Incucyte. Imaging began 2 h after placing the plate in the Incucyte to allow condensation to dissipate. Images were taken every 6 h for 10 days and the medium was refreshed every 3 days. The data acquired from the Incucyte were analyzed using Sartorius software, following the company's instructions. The processed data was then transferred to GraphPad Prism for further analysis and visualization.

In-vitro organoids growth screening to evaluate the effect of Matrigel components

To evaluate the effect of different Matrigels on organoids’ growth rate, the harvested, dissociated, and counted organoids were resuspended in either 80% growth-factor reduced (GFR) or normal Matrigel (NMG) and 20% base medium to have a final concentration of 150 organoids/μL. Plating and imaging using the Incucyte were performed as described previously. Total organoid area on day 10 was reported.

In-vitro organoids growth screening to evaluate the effect of seeding density

After harvesting and counting the organoids, the cells were resuspended in either 80% growth-factor reduced (GFR) or normal Matrigel (NMG) and 20% base medium to achieve final concentrations of 100, 200, 500, and 1000 organoids/μL, Then, 50 μL of the resuspended organoids were added to each well of a 96-well plate to have final concentrations of 5k, 10k, 50k, and 50k cells per well. The rest of preparation protocol and imaging were performed as described previously. Normalized organoid area on day 10 (total organoid area on day 10 divided by total organoid area on day 0) was reported.

Organoid dissociation and growth screening

To dissociate organoids for growth screening, 0.05% trypsin, TrypLE, and mechanical dissociation were utilized. After harvesting the organoids and removing the medium and Matrigel, TrypLE or trypsin was added to the organoids, and the mixture was gently mixed 15–20 times and incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 min. After the first incubation, organoids were mechanically dissociated, followed by a second incubation for another 3–4 min, and further mechanical dissociation. For the mechanical dissociation, the organoids were broken into single cells by repeat pipetting with P200 or P1000 tips depending on the organoid pellet size.

Following dissociation, organoids were counted, and the rest of plating and imaging using the Incucyte were performed as described previously. The automated cell counter does not register large aggregates as live cells; therefore, plated cell numbers primarily represented viable single cells. Residual clumps were minimized through repeated pipetting, although small aggregates were occasionally visible. Future optimizations such as filtering or large-particle sorting may further improve single-cell uniformity. Normalized organoid area on day 10 (total organoid area on day 10 divided by total organoid area on day 0) was reported.

Growth screening of parental and engineered organoids

To assess the effect of lentiviral transduction on organoids’ growth, the dissociated and counted engineered and parental organoids were plated and imaged as described in the previous sections. Total organoid area on day 10 was reported.

RNA extraction

For RNA extraction, organoids from one well of a 6-well plate were harvested using cold base medium and centrifuged to pellet the cells. The medium and Matrigel were removed, and the pellet was washed once with cold PBS, followed by centrifugation. After discarding the PBS, the pellet was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer's protocol after thawing the cell pellets. Briefly, organoid pellets were lysed with the provided lysis buffer, mixed, and homogenized using a QIAshredder. The lysate was combined with 70% ethanol, passed through an RNA-binding filter, washed several times, and eluted in 35 μL of RNase-free water. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

RNA-seq process (DRUG-seq)

Isolated RNA was diluted to 15 ng/μL, and 50 μL of the diluted RNA was transferred to each well of a 96-well plate, with 8 replicates prepared for each sample. The Drug-Seq procedure was adapted from Ye et al. (2018) and Li et al. (2022). Reverse transcription (RT) master mix was added to individual wells of 96-well PCR plates with a Certus Flex liquid dispenser. Barcoded RT primers were dispensed into each well with an Echo liquid handler and 15uL of RNA was transferred to each well using a Bravo liquid handler. After incubation at 50 °C for 45 min, material from each PCR plate was pooled into a reagent reservoir using a Bravo liquid handler and purified using a 1:1 ratio of sample to SPRI select beads (Beckman). Material was treated with Exo1 and purified using SPRI select beads (0.6X), followed by cDNA pre-amplification using cDNA primers and KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix formulation. Library construction was conducted with a Hamilton STAR Plus equipped with an On Deck Thermal Cycler and a Magnum FLX magnet (Alpaqua) following the 10× genomics 3’ Single Cell reagent kit protocol. Samples were sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 (Illumina), utilizing dual index primers (10x genomics).

RNA-seq analysis

The DRUG-seq pipeline trims and filters reads and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) using an optimized reimplementation of ST Pipeline v1.7.6's quality control filters [72] and demultiplexes reads based on well barcodes. The reads were mapped to the GRCh38 GENCODE v32/Ensembl 98 reference genome using STAR v2.7.11b [73] and assigned to annotated genes using featureCounts from the Subread package v2.0.6 [74]. Duplicate UMIs were removed based on mapping and assignment information. Unique UMIs were then counted per gene. Samples with fewer than 900,000 reads were excluded from downstream analysis. Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were computed from raw read counts. Protein-coding genes were identified using BioMart (version 2.60.1). Differential expression analysis and variance-stabilizing transformation were performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.44.0) in R (version 4.4.1). All plots were generated in R (version 4.4.1).

Protein extraction

Two wells of a 6-well plate containing organoids were harvested and combined using cold base medium. The organoids were centrifuged, and the medium and Matrigel were removed. The pellets were resuspended in organoid harvest solution and placed on ice on a shaker for 30–45 min to remove residual Matrigel. The tubes were then centrifuged, the organoid harvest solution was discarded, and the pellets were washed with cold PBS, followed by another centrifugation. After discarding the PBS, the organoid pellets were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. For protein extraction, the organoid pellets were thawed and resuspended in lysis buffer containing Per buffer, 0.1% SDS, protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and Benzonase. The pellets were mixed thoroughly in the buffer and incubated on ice for 15–20 min. The lysate was passed through QIAshredder to maximize protein extraction and homogenization. The lysate was then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C.

Protein quantification and western blotting (JESS)

Protein quantification was performed using a BCA assay. For Western blotting, the JESS system was used following the manufacturer's protocol to measure protein levels of the genes of interest. Briefly, the protein lysate was thawed, and the required volume based on protein concentration was transferred to a new tube. The lysate was mixed with 5X mastermix and 0.1X sample buffer to achieve a final concentration of 2 μg/μL, vortexed, and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The lysate, primary (Supplementary Table 1) and secondary antibodies, antibody diluent, ladder, and 1:1 mixture of luminol-s and peroxide were added to the JESS plate. The plate was spun down and placed in the system for fluorescence and chemiluminescence readouts. Data from JESS was analyzed using Compass for SW and ImageJ software.

Organoid fixation

Organoids were harvested as described previously. After centrifugation, the pellets were resuspended in organoid harvest solution, placed on ice on a shaker for 30–60 min to dissolve the Matrigel, and centrifuged again. The organoids were then washed with cold PBS and fixed in 10% formalin.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue/organoid pellet processing was performed using the Leica ASP 300 (Leica Biosystems). Routine processing involved sequential exposure of formalin fixed Tissue/organoid pellet samples to graded ethanol solutions (100%, 90%, and 70%) and paraffin wax. Following processing, Tissue/organoid pellet cassettes were removed and embedded in paraffin wax for sectioning. Tissue/organoid pellet sections were cut using a microtome at a standard thickness of 5 μm. Sections were placed in a water bath, mounted onto glass slides, and baked for 1 h prior to staining. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using the Leica Bond automated staining system (Leica Biosystems).

Lentiviral transduction

Organoids were harvested, and Matrigel was completely removed by multiple washes with base medium and organoid harvest solution as previously described. The organoids were then resuspended in TrypLE to promote gentle dissociation for more efficient transduction. Following dissociation, TrypLE was removed, and the cell pellet was washed again with base medium. Next, 500 μL of base medium containing polybrene was added to each viral vial (GFP and RFP), each containing 5 μL of virus. The contents of the vials were combined with the organoid pellet, mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The organoid-virus mixture was centrifuged at 0.9 rcf for 90 min at room temperature, followed by an additional centrifugation at 800×g for 5 min. After centrifugation, the medium was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in Matrigel. The organoids were then plated in a 6-well plate and incubated upside down for 1 h to allow the Matrigel to solidify before adding culture medium. After 2–3 days of recovery post-lentiviral transduction, organoids were subjected to selection by adding 10 μg/mL of blasticidin to the culture medium. The medium was replaced every 3 days, and the selection process was completed after 7 days. Following selection, the culture medium was switched to normal medium to promote faster growth of positively transduced organoids. Finally, the organoids were expanded in large quantities and cryopreserved for future experiments.

Subcutaneous organoid inoculation in mice

Organoids were harvested, resuspended in organoid harvest solution, and incubated on ice on a shaker for 30 min to completely dissolve the Matrigel. The organoid harvest solution was then aspirated, and the organoids were resuspended in base medium. To determine organoid concentration, 5% of the total volume was set aside in a separate tube, centrifuged, and the medium was aspirated. These organoids were dissociated using 0.05% trypsin, neutralized with FBS-containing medium, and counted. The resulting count was used to calculate the appropriate concentration for the remaining, non-dissociated organoids. For subcutaneous injection, organoids were resuspended in a 50:50 mixture of base medium and Matrigel (NMG) to achieve a final concentration of 10 × 106 cells/mL. All animal experiments were conducted with prior approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Merck & Co., Inc., Rahway, NJ, USA. (Animal Procedure Statement # 2028-200585-Oct). Throughout the study, animal care and handling adhered to the guidelines set by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC). Female NSG mice (5–8 weeks old) were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME) and housed in a pathogen-free environment. All animals had unrestricted access to food and water. Each mouse received 1 × 106 cells in 100 μL of the mixture subcutaneously in the lower right flank (10 mice/group). Two weeks post-injection, tumor size and body weight were measured twice weekly. Tumor volume was calculated using the 0.5 × L × W2 equation. In this equation, L refers to the length and W refers to the width of the tumor. Mice were euthanized once the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3 - 2000 mm3or after 8 weeks post inoculation. Harvested tumors were divided into three sections for histological analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Depending on the data format, t-test student or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test were performed to evaluate the significant differences of the data.

Results

Effect of Matrigel components and seeding density on organoids growth

We first evaluated the effect of different Matrigel types on organoid growth by comparing organoids cultured in growth factor-reduced (GFR) Matrigel to those in normal Matrigel (NMG). Our data revealed that the type of Matrigel did not significantly impact the growth of the PDO2 organoid line (Fig. 1B). However, the PDO1 organoid line exhibited slightly enhanced growth in NMG compared to GFR Matrigel, while the PDO3 organoid line displayed the opposite trend, with reduced growth in NMG (Fig. 1A and C). Despite these differences, the overall growth trends for all three organoid lines were similar, regardless of the Matrigel type. We next examined the effect of cell seeding density, a critical factor that can significantly influence organoid growth by affecting spatial constraints and nutrient availability. Organoids were seeded at densities ranging from 5000 to 50,000 cells per well in both GFR Matrigel and NMG. Our results showed that increasing the seeding density progressively reduced the final total normalized organoid area in all three PDAC lines, irrespective of the Matrigel type (Fig. 1D–I, Supplementary Fig. 1A). This effect was consistent across both growth conditions, emphasizing the importance of optimizing seeding density to promote robust organoid growth. Interestingly, growth kinetics revealed that within the first three days of culture, organoids seeded at different densities showed similar normalized areas. However, after this initial period, organoids seeded at lower densities began to significantly outgrow those at higher densities. This separation in growth trends suggests that organoids in sparsely populated wells encounter less spatial competition, allowing them to expand and achieve larger sizes over time. These findings highlight the importance of carefully balancing seeding density to maximize organoid growth without compromising their size or proliferation rate. To further assess whether 5000–10,000 cells per well represents an optimal seeding density, we examined organoid growth at lower densities (2500–250 cells per well) in GFR Matrigel. While organoids seeded at lower densities exhibited progressively larger normalized areas, the variability in growth also increased (Supplementary Fig. 1B–C). Moreover, excessively sparse cultures produced fewer and less heterogeneous organoids, which could limit the representativeness of responses in subsequent drug assays.

Effect of different dissociation methods on organoids growth

A critical step in preparing organoids for screening assays is dissociating them into single cells to enable accurate counting and consistent plating. To determine the impact of dissociation methods on organoid growth, we compared mechanical dissociation (to pipetting the organoids 15-20 times up and down using a p200 or p1000 pipette), TrypLE, and 0.05% Trypsin digestion across three organoid lines (PDO1, PDO2, and PDO3). Organoids were cultured in both GFR Matrigel and NMG.

Among the three dissociation methods, trypsin digestion consistently produced the most effective single-cell suspension, yielding higher viability, higher cell counts, and fewer residual aggregates compared to TrypLE digestion or mechanical dissociation. We also observed fewer pre-formed organoids or cell clumps with trypsin treatment. The incubation time was kept constant (6–8 min total) across all methods to avoid damaging cells, but dissociation efficiency varied by organoid line, with some lines breaking down more easily than others. To improve dissociation, both enzymatic and mechanical steps were applied to maximize single-cell yield while maintaining cell health.

Equal numbers of cells were plated based on automated counts, which primarily reflect viable single cells, as large clumps are not detected by the counter. Therefore, observed growth differences likely reflect the intrinsic effects of the dissociation methods on cell health and re-aggregation capacity rather than major differences in plating uniformity.

Our results demonstrated that for the PDO1 organoid line, the normalized total area of organoids increased slightly with TrypLE dissociation compared to Trypsin, whereas mechanical dissociation resulted in a significant reduction in organoid growth compared to both enzymatic methods (TrypLE and Trypsin, Fig. 2A and D). In the PDO2 and PDO3 organoid lines, organoid growth was significantly reduced in both the mechanical and TrypLE dissociation groups compared to Trypsin, with PDO3 showing the most pronounced decrease in growth (Fig. 2B–C, 2E–F). These findings suggest that 0.05% Trypsin provides the most effective balance between achieving a single-cell suspension and maintaining viability, resulting in more robust and reproducible organoid growth across all lines. Effect of different medium components on organoids growth

Organoid growth depends on key medium components, particularly growth factors such as EGF and FGF10, which are crucial for maintaining viability but can interfere with drug screening by inducing resistance through the activation of cancer-associated pathways. To optimize conditions for drug screening, we evaluated multiple medium compositions to identify a protocol that minimizes growth factor influence while preserving robust organoid growth.

Across all three organoid lines tested, organoids cultured in full medium for 10 days exhibited the most robust growth, establishing a baseline for comparison (Fig. 3A–F, Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2A–F). Several alternative conditions were assessed, with the “3-day full medium + 7-day reduced medium” protocol emerging as the most promising. This condition consistently supported organoid growth comparable to full medium, even in the PDO3 line, which demonstrated greater dependence on growth factors. More aggressive reductions in growth factors, such as prolonged culture in base medium or base medium with 5% FBS, led to significantly reduced growth, indicating that these conditions are insufficient to support organoid viability. Notably, these trends were consistent across both GFR and NMG Matrigel (Fig. 3A–F, Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2A–F), reinforcing the robustness of the “3-day full medium + 7-day reduced medium” condition. This protocol effectively maintains organoid health while mitigating the impact of growth factor-driven resistance, making it a suitable approach for future drug screening assays. Effect of lentiviral transduction on organoids gene expression and growth

Engineering organoids to express specific markers such as GFP and RFP is important for in-vitro assays, including drug screening and co-culture studies. Moreover, it is crucial to evaluate the effect of lentiviral transduction on organoid growth and gene expression to ensure the general characteristics of the organoids remain intact and that they respond similarly to the parental line under different conditions. Therefore, we established GFP- and RFP-expressing organoids using lentiviral transduction and evaluated their growth and gene expression to identify any significant changes between the parental and engineered groups.

Using high-throughput confocal microscopy, we confirmed the success of lentiviral transduction and selection, as GFP and RFP expression were clearly detectable via imaging (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 4D–F). Further analysis revealed that the growth rate of the organoids was not significantly affected by the lentiviral transduction in the PDO2 and PDO3 lines (Fig. 4C–D, Supplementary Fig. 4B–C). However, there was a slight increase in the growth rate of the PDO1 engineered organoids compared to their parental line (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 4A). This slight increase may result from minor changes in metabolism or cellular signaling induced by GFP or RFP expression. Histological staining of these different organoid lines for markers such as vimentin, HER3, HER2, EGFR, and Ki67 showed that the expression patterns of these markers were largely consistent between the parental and engineered organoids (Supplementary Fig. 5A). EGFR, HER3 and HER2 expression remained localized and similar across all conditions, while Ki67, a proliferation marker, showed comparable activity between the parental and engineered lines. This further supports that the engineered lines retained their proliferative capabilities and did not undergo significant phenotypic changes due to the transduction process in-vitro. Moreover, histological examination using H&E staining showed comparable morphology in the parental, GFP, and RFP groups across all organoid lines.

Protein expression analysis showed that key proteins involved in cell signaling and growth (RAS, p-AKT, p-ERK, HER2, HER3, and VEGFR2), apoptosis regulation (c-Casp3, Casp3, Casp6, Casp9, PARP, BAD, BAX, Bcl-xL, BAK, BID, p-BAD), adhesion and epithelial integrity (E-cadherin, EPCAM, β-catenin), cell cycle control (p21, CDK4), tumor suppression (p53), and transcriptional regulation (p-YAP) exhibited similar expression levels in GFP- and RFP-expressing organoids compared to their parental lines (Fig. 4F). While some engineered organoids showed altered expression of certain genes, the majority of genes maintained stable expression levels.

RNA-seq data for the PDO1 and PDO2 organoid lines revealed that lentiviral transduction did not significantly affect the gene expression profile (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 5B–D). PCA, heatmap, and volcano plot analyses showed that the PDO1-parental, PDO1-GFP, and PDO1-RFP lines clustered very closely together, and similar results were observed for the PDO2-parental, PDO2-GFP, and PDO2-RFP lines. However, the PDO3 engineered lines exhibited more significant differences in their gene expression profiles compared to their parental line. As shown in the PCA plots, PDO3-parental samples clearly separated from both the PDO3-GFP and PDO3-RFP samples, with the PDO3-RFP samples clustering furthest away. This indicates that the PDO3-RFP line underwent more pronounced transcriptomic changes than PDO3-GFP. Similar findings were observed in the heatmap and volcano plots, where the PDO3-RFP samples exhibited the highest number of differentially expressed genes compared to the parental line.

In summary, lentiviral transduction did not significantly affect the growth, histology, or gene expression profiles of the engineered organoid lines, except for some transcriptomic changes observed in the PDO3-RFP line. These results suggest that GFP and RFP expression can be introduced into organoids without major alterations to their overall characteristics, making them suitable for further in-vitro studies.

Together, these results demonstrate that while seeding density profoundly impacts IC50 values, lentiviral transduction introduces only minimal variability that does not affect overall drug response patterns. This underscores the importance of maintaining consistent cell density conditions when performing drug sensitivity assays, as differences in plating density can have far greater effects on IC50 outcomes than lentiviral modification itself.

Discussion

The development and characterization of fluorescently engineered organoids have significantly advanced cancer research by bridging the gap between simplistic 2D culture models and the complex in-vivo tumor microenvironment. By incorporating fluorescent markers such as GFP and RFP through lentiviral transduction, we have created tools for real-time monitoring of organoid behavior, including proliferation, differentiation, and therapeutic responses. These innovations address longstanding challenges in non-invasive tracking of dynamic processes, representing a substantial step forward in preclinical cancer research.

Our study demonstrates that fluorescent engineering does not alter the physiological or molecular characteristics of organoids, as evidenced by comparable growth rates, gene expression profiles, and responses to chemotherapeutic agents between engineered and parental organoids. This ensures that the biological fidelity of organoids in recapitulating primary tumor biology is maintained, making them robust models for cancer research. Previous studies [29,[75], [76], [77], [78], [79]] have highlighted organoids’ ability to capture patient-specific tumor heterogeneity. By adding fluorescence capabilities, our models enhance their applicability in imaging, co-culture systems, and drug discovery without compromising their biological relevance. Our findings are consistent with earlier reports demonstrating that biomaterial composition, such as Matrigel concentration and growth factor content, can subtly influence epithelial organoid proliferation and morphology in a line-dependent manner [[80], [81], [82], [83]].

These results also parallel nanomaterial-based 3D culture studies, where differences in hydrogel stiffness and bioactive components affected cancer cell growth and drug penetration [[84], [85], [86], [87], [88], [89]]. Such parallels suggest that the microenvironmental context, whether derived from Matrigel or synthetic nanomaterials, critically modulates organoid growth dynamics and therapeutic responses.

Fluorescent-labeled organoids provide unprecedented opportunities to study spatial and temporal dynamics in cancer research. They enable visualization of processes such as cell migration, invasion, and apoptosis, which are challenging to capture using traditional methods. For example, live-dead transitions during drug treatments can now be tracked in real time, offering valuable insights into therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms of action. Furthermore, fluorescence-based multiplexing facilitates the simultaneous tracking of different cell populations within organoids, shedding light on intra-tumoral heterogeneity and clonal evolution under selective pressures such as drug treatments or immune challenges [35]. [25].

The translational potential of our engineered organoids is underscored by their retention of tumorigenic properties in-vivo, demonstrated through comparable growth patterns and histological profiles between fluorescently labeled and parental organoids. Fluorescent organoids are compatible with advanced imaging techniques, including multiphoton and light-sheet fluorescence microscopy, enabling high-resolution tracking of tumor dynamics over time. This capability is critical for studying metastasis, angiogenesis, and stromal interactions, offering new perspectives on cancer progression [[90], [91], [92], [93], [94], [95], [96], [97]].

Our systematic optimization of culture conditions addresses critical challenges in organoid research. By adjusting variables such as Matrigel composition, growth factors, dissociation methods, and seeding density, we established robust protocols that ensure experimental reproducibility. Notably, our findings on excluding growth factors like EGF and FGF during drug screening assays mitigate their confounding effects on drug efficacy studies by preventing the activation of pro-survival pathways. Minimal medium conditions were shown to maintain organoid viability and yield reproducible drug responses, offering a more clinically relevant framework for preclinical studies [98,99]. Comparable optimization efforts have been reported in high-throughput nanomedicine screening, where matrix composition and cellular density were found to strongly influence nanoparticle uptake, diffusion, and cytotoxicity profiles [55,[100], [101], [102]]. Together, these observations highlight the shared need across organoid and nanomedicine platforms for microenvironment standardization to improve screening reproducibility.

Seeding density emerged as a key factor influencing drug screening outcomes, with higher densities correlating with increased IC50 values due to enhanced cell-cell interactions and reduced drug penetration. These findings align with previous studies [103,104] and emphasize the need for standardized plating conditions to minimize variability. Additionally, our scalable protocols for high-throughput drug screening demonstrate their potential to accelerate drug development pipelines.

Beyond drug screening, fluorescent organoids hold immense potential in co-culture systems. Fluorescent labeling simplifies the study of tumor-immune and tumor-stromal interactions within the tumor microenvironment. For instance, co-culture models incorporating immune cells (e.g., T cells, macrophages, and natural killer cells) or stromal components (e.g., cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and endothelial cells) can elucidate mechanisms of immune evasion, stromal contributions to tumor progression, and drug resistance. This capability opens new avenues for studying the complex interplay between cancer cells and their microenvironment [[1], [2], [3],105,106].

The clinical relevance of patient-derived organoids has been well-documented in predicting responses to chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies. Fluorescent markers enhance these models by enabling real-time therapeutic response assessments, facilitating biomarker identification for drug sensitivity and resistance. This advancement paves the way for precision oncology by supporting the development of more tailored and effective treatment strategies [107].

Incorporating fluorescent organoids into large-scale drug screening platforms further enhances their utility. Automated systems for organoid handling, imaging, and analysis can now leverage fluorescence to improve throughput and reliability, expediting the identification of promising therapeutic candidates [82]. The organoids we developed will also be beneficial for studies exploring the interactions and mutual influence of different fluorescently labeled organoids, each with distinct characteristics, in response to various drugs.

The in-vivo applications of fluorescent organoids are equally promising. Orthotopic transplantation of fluorescently labeled organoids allows for real-time, longitudinal studies of tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance in animal models. Advanced imaging modalities enable visualization of these processes with unparalleled resolution, providing insights beyond endpoint analyses. Additionally, fluorescent organoids offer a platform for evaluating novel therapies, such as CAR-T cells or bispecific antibodies, by monitoring treatment efficacy and mechanisms of action in-vivo [35,36].

Looking forward, the integration of fluorescent organoids into emerging technologies such as organ-on-a-chip systems and bioprinting represents an exciting frontier. These platforms allow researchers to explore the effects of mechanical forces, nutrient gradients, and drug distribution on tumor behavior. Fluorescent markers further enhance these systems by enabling precise monitoring of these variables, advancing organoids as versatile and physiologically relevant models [26].

In conclusion, our work establishes fluorescently engineered organoids as a transformative tool for cancer research, retaining the essential features of parental organoids while offering unparalleled advantages for imaging, drug screening, and translational applications. By addressing key challenges in organoid culture and characterization, we provide a versatile platform with vast potential to impact co-culture studies, immune-oncology, and high-throughput drug discovery and screening. These advancements bring us closer to translating preclinical findings into meaningful clinical outcomes, ultimately improving cancer treatment and patient care.
