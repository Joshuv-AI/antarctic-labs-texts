Comparison of six nutritional screening tools for identifying BMI‑defined malnutrition in patients with Crohn’s disease: a cross‑sectional study
Authors: Zhihui Yu, Rongfei Duan, Qing Liu, Wan Mei, Danlei Chen, Ting Pan, Yan Chen, Fang Kong, Chengliang Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is currently debate in clinical practice regarding the selection of nutritional screening tools for patients with Crohn’s disease. This cross-sectional study aimed to compare the ability of six nutritional screening tools to identify malnutrition defined by a body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m2 in patients with Crohn’s disease.MethodsA total of 163 patients with Crohn’s disease admitted to the Gastroenterology Treatment Centre of a tertiary-level hospital in Nanjing from September 2023 to December 2023 were enrolled. Their nutritional status was assessed using the NRS2002, PG-SGA, MNA-SF, GLIM criteria, MUST, and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of the six tools for identifying BMI-defined malnutrition.ResultsBased on BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 as the operational definition, the prevalence of malnutrition in Crohn’s disease patients was 28.83%. An enterostomy was associated with malnutrition. All six tools showed significant agreement in identifying malnutrition, with significantly higher identification rates in the high-risk (or positive) group (p < 0.05). The AUCs were 0.978, 0.720, 0.868, 0.983, 0.957, and 0.716, respectively.ConclusionsIn this BMI-based analysis, the GLIM criteria showed higher agreement with the malnutrition definition than the other five tools. The combination of the GLIM criteria with phase angle (PA) further improved discriminative ability. However, because BMI is an imperfect reference for malnutrition in Crohn’s disease, these findings should be considered exploratory. Background

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic, nonspecific inflammatory disease of the intestinal tract with an uncertain etiology [1]. Patients with CD often suffer from malnutrition due to reduced absorption, intestinal dysbiosis, and diminished food intake caused by loss of appetite, nausea and vomiting [2]. Several studies have shown that malnutrition is an important factor contributing to hospital readmission, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs in CD patients, and is also associated with poor prognosis, a high incidence of postoperative complications, and reduced quality of life [3–5]. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) measures whole-body impedance and calculates body composition parameters, such as body water, fat mass, and skeletal muscle mass-using built-in regression equations [6]. BIA has been widely used for body composition analysis, nutritional status assessment, and evaluation of therapeutic efficacy [6–8]. Phase angle (PA), derived from BIA, is a metric that reflects cellular integrity and can be used to assess nutritional status [6]. Early identification of nutritional deterioration and timely nutritional interventions are crucial for improving clinical outcomes in CD patients. Although numerous malnutrition assessment tools are available, conclusive evidence to guide tool selection specifically for patients with CD remains lacking. Therefore, this study compared several commonly used clinical tools for nutritional assessment and analyzed their discriminative ability for malnutrition defined as BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 in patients with CD, with the aim of informing clinical decision-making.

Methods

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Research subjects

A convenience sampling method was used to enroll 163 patients with CD who were admitted to the Gastroenterological Treatment Centre of a tertiary hospital in Nanjing between September 2023 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for Crohn’s disease issued by the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Group of the Society of Digestive Diseases of the Chinese Medical Association in 2018 [9]; (2) age ≥ 18 years; and (3) availability of complete clinical data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cognitive or communication disorders preventing cooperation; (2) presence of other chronic wasting diseases (e.g., malignant tumors, hyperthyroidism); (3) limb dysfunction (e.g., disability), edema, or presence of a cardiac pacemaker, rendering the patient unsuitable for BIA. Research methodology

Data collection

(1) General information questionnaire: This questionnaire was designed by the researcher and included questions about sex, age, education level, history of use of biological agents and surgical history.

Nutritional status identification tool

Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 (NRS 2002) [10]

Proposed by Kondrup et al. in 2002, comprises three components: a nutritional score (0–3 points), a disease severity score (0–3 points) and an age score (0–1 point). The total score is the sum of the three components, and a total score ≥ 3 indicates nutritional risk.

Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) [11]

First proposed by Ottery in 1994, the PG-SGA is a nutritional assessment tool specifically designed for cancer patients. It comprises two parts: a patient-reported part (weight loss, nutritional impact symptoms, food intake, and functional capacity) and a clinician-rated part (disease and age, metabolic stress, and physical examination). The total score is interpreted as follows: 0–1 indicates well-nourished status, 2–3 indicates suspected malnutrition, 4–8 indicates moderate malnutrition, and ≥ 9 indicates severe malnutrition.

Mini Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA-SF) [12]

Developed by Rubenstein et al. in 2001 as a simplified version of the MNA, the MNA-SF comprises 6 items: body mass index, mobility status, presence of acute disease or stressful condition in the preceding 3 months, weight change over the last 3 months, eating status, and neuropsychiatric disorders. The MNA-SF has a total score of 14, with scores interpreted as follows: ≥11 indicates normal nutritional status, and < 11 indicates poor nutritional status.

Global Leaders Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria [13]

The GLIM criteria comprise 3 phenotypic indicators (involuntary loss of body mass, low BMI, and loss of muscle mass) and 2 etiological indicators (reduced food intake or impaired digestion/ absorption, disease burden, or inflammation). According to the GLIM framework, malnutrition is diagnosed when at least one phenotypic criterion and one etiologic criterion are met. All patients with CD included in this study were considered to fulfill the etiological criteria. For the phenotype criterion, this study selected involuntary loss of body mass, defined as loss > 5% within 6 months, or > 10% over 6 months.

Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) [14]

The MUST was developed in 2004 by the Multidisciplinary Malnutrition Information Group of the British Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition. The MUST score comprises BMI, recent weight loss, and food intake, which are summed to form a total nutritional risk score: a score of 0 indicates low risk, 1 indicates moderate risk, and ≥ 2 indicates high risk. In this study, a MUST score of ≥ 1 was defined as indicating risk of malnutrition.

Body composition analysis

Patients were measured by staff who passed the training and assessment on the morning of the second day after admission using InBody770, and their height, weight, and PA were recorded. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using BMI ≤ 18.5 kg/m2 as the state variable, and Youden’s index was calculated. Youden’s index was greatest when the PA was 4.85. Therefore, the optimal cutoff value for the PA was 4.85. When the PA was ≥ 4.85, the patients were in the nonmalnourished group, and vice versa.

Operational definition of malnutrition used in this study

Body mass index (BMI) is a widely used and reliable indicator of body fatness and thinness, and can reflect, to some extent, protein and energy malnutrition in patients [15]. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2). In this study, patients with BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 were classified as malnourished according to this operational definition. However, it is important to emphasize that BMI is not a true diagnostic gold standard for malnutrition in CD, as it does not capture sarcopenia, inflammation-driven nutritional impairment, or altered body composition without weight loss. Therefore, our analyses should be interpreted as the agreement of each tool with this BMI-based classification rather than as true diagnostic accuracy.

Quality control

Before collecting the patients’ data, the researcher obtained cooperation and signed an informed consent form after explaining the purpose, content and significance of the study to them. The researcher collected subjective patient data using a standardized script derived from the questionnaire items, which prevented leading questions and enabled on-site completeness checks immediately after questionnaire completion. Strict adherence to operational procedures was maintained when performing body composition measurements. The patient data collected were entered, collated, and analyzed simultaneously by two researchers.

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 was used. Measurement data are expressed as mean ± SD, and categorical data as counts and percentages (%). Chi-square test was used for group comparisons. Binary logistic regression was used to analyze factors associated with malnutrition. Kappa test assessed agreement between the six tools. ROC curves were plotted using MedCalc 22.0. AUCs were calculated for the NRS2002, PG-SGA, MNA-SF, GLIM criteria, MUST, and PA to identify BMI-defined malnutrition. A difference of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Forty-seven (28.83%) patients were classified as malnourished according to the BMI-based definition. Discussion

Current status of malnutrition in CD patients and influencing factors

Malnutrition is a common complication in patients with CD, and according to previous studies, the prevalence of nutritional risk or malnutrition ranges from 16.0% to 67.5% [16, 17]. In the present study, 28.83% of patients with CD were malnourished. Multivariable analysis revealed that the presence of an enterostomy was associated with malnutrition (OR = 3.371, 95% CI: 1.421–9.798). Surgery is also an important modality in the comprehensive treatment of CD, a disease that requires lifelong treatment. The results of a retrospective study by Blackwell [18] revealed that 15% of patients with CD who underwent their first bowel surgery had temporary stomas and 16.5% had permanent stomas. The literature suggests [19, 20] that enterostomies can cause a range of mental health problems and related complications in patients, but there are no reports of enterostomies causing malnutrition in patients with CD. Most of the 26 patients with an enterostomy had undergone temporary stoma formation shortly after surgery. Surgical stress, insufficient intake, and impaired postoperative nutrient absorption would have an impact on the nutritional status of the patients [21]. Further studies could be carried out to confirm the relationship between the intestinal stoma and the nutritional status of patients with CD. The results of this study suggest that attention should be given to the nutritional status of patients with enterostomal CD to enhance their nutritional management and reduce stoma-related complications.

The GLIM criteria have good discriminative ability for malnutrition in CD patients

The kappa consistency test revealed significant agreement among the six tools (p < 0.05), but the magnitude of kappa values varied, possibly because their assessment criteria are not uniform. The AUCs for NRS2002, PG-SGA, MNA-SF, GLIM criteria, MUST, and PA were 0.978, 0.720, 0.868, 0.983, 0.957, and 0.716, respectively. These results indicate that the PG-SGA, MNA-SF and PA had fair discriminative ability (AUC = 0.7–0.9), whereas NRS2002, MUST, and GLIM criteria had good discriminative ability (AUC > 0.9). The AUC for GLIM criteria was 0.983, exceeding those of the other five tools, with a sensitivity and specificity of 100% and 96.55%, respectively, as well as a PPV of 92.16% and an NPV of 100%, suggesting that the GLIM criteria had optimal efficacy in identifying BMI-defined malnutrition. Previous studies have also confirmed that the diagnostic accuracy of the GLIM criteria is significantly better than that of other nutritional status identification tools [22, 23]. The GLIM criteria are more comprehensive, simple to use, and easy to use clinically [24]. The results of this study revealed that the NRS2002 and MUST had AUC values of 0.978 and 0.957, respectively, which were second to those of the GLIM criteria in terms of sensitivity and specificity but had a lower PPV, suggesting that the screening results for malnutrition in patients with CD had a more general concordance with the BMI-based classification. This may be because the MUST is more applicable to outpatient CD patients, and there may be differences between outpatients and inpatients in terms of confounders of malnutrition [25]. The NRS2002 considered several serious disease factors, whereas 84.66% of the patients in this study were mildly ill in remission. The results of this study suggest that the MNA-SF may not be the best tool for identifying the nutritional status of CD patients. It has poorer sensitivity (80.85%) and better specificity (85.34%) in identifying malnutrition in CD patients, suggesting that its ability to identify malnutrition in CD patients has a high rate of underdiagnosis. The possible reason for this may be that the MNA-SF is applicable mainly to older patients, whereas 92% of the patients in this study were under 60 years of age. The PG-SGA was designed primarily as a nutritional assessment method for patients with malignant tumors, but the Chinese expert consensus on inflammatory bowel disease states that its use is recommended for assessing the nutritional status of patients [26]. The PG-SGA showed low specificity (68.97%) and moderate sensitivity (72.34%), indicating a high rate of misdiagnosis. Consequently, nonmalnourished patients were often classified as malnourished, leading to unnecessary nutritional interventions and waste of medical resources. This finding is in agreement with the findings of Kurban [27]. BIA is widely used in clinical practice because of its advantages of noninvasiveness, low cost and reliable results [6]. The results of this study showed that the discriminative ability of PA as a single indicator for malnutrition in CD patients was moderate (AUC = 0.716, specificity 71.55%, sensitivity 61.7%). Using the exploratory cutoff derived from this cohort, the combination of GLIM criteria and PA marginally improved its discriminative ability (AUC = 0.989, sensitivity 100%, specificity 96.6%), though this should be interpreted cautiously. This suggests that clinical use of GLIM criteria to evaluate the nutritional status of CD patients can be accompanied by bioelectrical impedance measurement, thereby improving the accuracy of identification.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 is an imperfect reference for malnutrition in CD, potentially inflating the performance of tools containing weight components. Our findings represent agreement with a BMI-based classification, not true diagnostic accuracy. Second, the single-center cross-sectional design and modest sample size limit generalizability and causality. Third, the PA cut-off was derived and tested in the same dataset, risking overfitting. Fourth, the logistic regression lacked adjustment for disease activity and medication use. Fifth, recall bias and the use of tools developed for other populations may affect applicability. Finally, the absence of a comprehensive nutritional reference standard limits clinical interpretability.

Conclusion

The prevalence of malnutrition defined by BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 in patients with CD was 28.83%, and clinical attention should be paid to nutritional problems, early identification, and early intervention to improve patients’ nutritional status. All six tools showed utility in this cohort of CD patients, but in this BMI-based analysis, the GLIM criteria showed higher agreement with the malnutrition definition than the NRS2002, PG-SGA, MNA-SF, MUST, and PA. The combination of GLIM criteria with PA further improved discriminative ability. However, because BMI is an imperfect reference for malnutrition in CD, these findings should be considered exploratory. Independent validation using a comprehensive nutritional assessment is required before clinical implementation.
