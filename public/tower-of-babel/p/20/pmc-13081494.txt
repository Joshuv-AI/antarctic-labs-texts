Surgically avertable deaths in a rural Ethiopian community: a verbal autopsy study
Authors: Alemayehu Bekele, Amanuel Belay, Andualem Deneke, Amezene Tadesse, Girmay Medhin, Abebaw Fekadu, Charlotte Hanlon, Martin Prince
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgically avertable deaths are high in low- and middle-income countries. However, there is a paucity of evidence about the contribution of surgical conditions to mortality in Ethiopia particularly from community settings.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to assess surgically avertable deaths in the Butajira Health and Demographic Surveillance Site (HDSS), Central Ethiopia.MethodsRecords of deceased persons between 2014 and 2019 of Verbal Autopsy (VA) assessments at the Butajira HDSS were used. We employed World Health Organization(WHO) VA procedures to review and assign probable causes of death using the International Classification of Diseases 10 codes and were reviewed by two surgical residents and a senior surgeon to determine if the deaths were potentially avertable with surgical intervention. The magnitude of surgically avertable conditions was estimated using descriptive analysis. Factors associated with surgically avertable causes of death were investigated using a Poisson regression model with sandwich variance estimator.FindingsFrom this community-based dataset (n = 636), approximately one quarter of all deaths, (26.2%) were reported as definitely or probably surgically avertable. Primary causes of death that definitely or probably could be avertable by surgical interventions were neoplasms, external causes (e.g. injuries), renal disorders, gastrointestinal disorders and perinatal causes. Deaths in men were 1.62 times more likely to have been surgically avertable compared to women (adjusted Risk Ratio 1.62; 95% CI: 1.16, 2.24).ConclusionSurgically avertable causes of death in the current study were considerable, comprising a higher proportion of deaths in men than women. Strengthening the health system to improve access to surgical care is expected to reduce mortality and provide evidence for Ethiopia’s Saving Lives Through Safe Surgery (SaLTS) initiative.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03610-y.Keywords: Surgically avertable causes, Death, Verbal autopsy, Butajira, Ethiopia

Introduction

Surgically treatable conditions are those where surgical procedures (operative or non-operative) can improve the outcome and reduce disability and premature death [1]. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face a huge annual health burden of 401 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), of which about 116 million are avertable. This figure significantly exceeds the global burden of HIV/AIDS and malaria combined [2]. Surgical care is highly effective and cost-effective, demonstrating significant potential for saving lives and improving global health outcomes [1]. High-quality obstetric surgery alone could prevent 21.1 million lost years of healthy life, predominantly due to neonatal encephalopathy (16.2 million) and abortion (2.1 million) [3]. Basic surgical care alone could avert 52.3 million lost years from injuries (21% of the total injury burden). The reduction is higher for deaths (23%) than for non-fatal burdens (20%), indicating that injury-related surgical care is more effective at saving lives than at reducing long-term disability [4].

As one of the most cost-effective health interventions, essential surgical procedures could avert an estimated 1.5 million deaths annually in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), which is six to seven% of all avertable deaths [5]. Furthermore, the surgical platform of first-level hospitals is capable of delivering 28 out of 44 essential procedures, highlighting its cost-effectiveness as an investment [6]. Scaling up surgical systems to meet a target of 20 to 40 surgery/anesthesia/obstetric (SAO) procedures per 100,000 population per year could prevent over 600,000 deaths in children under the age of five [6]. Ultimately, achieving universal health coverage and the health-related United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) is impossible without dedicated investment in surgical, obstetric, and anesthesia care [7]. The Ministry of Health, Ethiopia introduced a Saving Lives Through Safe Surgery (SaLTS) initiative to address lack of appropriate human resources, support staff, equipment, and supplies, which prevent health facilities in predominantly rural Ethiopia from providing surgical and anesthesia services. The initiative emphasizes on availing a package of essential and emergency surgical and anesthesia care at all levels of the Ethiopian health care delivery system, including strengthening primary care to provide essential surgical care [8].

Predictors of surgically avertable causes of death have been reported in various studies. In India, death due to an acute abdomen was strongly associated with the physical inaccessibility of hospitals where surgical interventions are available [9]. In Ethiopia, factors considered to be fueling surgically avertable causes of death included the absence of surgical care in primary healthcare facilities, delays in patients making the decision to undergo surgery, lack of family and social support, and inability to afford hospital fees [10]. Furthermore, an assessment in India indicated that surgically avertable causes of death, including trauma due to road traffic injury, were higher in men, working age groups, and urban residents [11]. In response to bottlenecks like lack of appropriate human resources, support staff, equipment, and supplies, which prevent health facilities in predominantly rural Ethiopia from providing surgical and anesthesia services, the Ministry of Health introduced a Saving Lives Through Safe Surgery (SaLTS) plan. This plan emphasizes availing a package of essential and emergency surgical and anesthesia care at all levels of the Ethiopian health care delivery system, including strengthening primary care to provide essential surgical care [8]. Despite the known burden, evidence on avertable mortality from community-based studies in low-income countries is scarce. For example, a community-based study in Sierra Leone reported that surgically avertable causes of death accounted for 25% [12]. However, this study did not use verbal autopsy data, nor were surgeons engaged in making assessments to confirm surgically avertable causes of deaths among the study participants. To understand the potential impact of major national programs like SaLTS, it is imperative to have accurate data on current avertable mortality from surgical conditions in community settings. Therefore, the aim of this study was to estimate the proportion of deaths that are potentially surgically avertable in a predominantly rural population in south-central Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study design

Secondary analysis of Verbal Autopsy (VA) data collected by the Butajira Health and Demographic Surveillance Site (HDSS) from 2014 to 2019.

Study setting

Butajira HDSS (formerly known as the Butajira Rural Health Program) was established in 1986 [13]. Its geographical coverage encompasses nine Kebeles (smallest administrative areas) around Butajira town and one urban Kebele in the Butajira town. The site has served to establish a system of demographic surveillance with continuous registration of vital and migratory events (birth, death, marriage, new household, out-migration, in-migration and internal move) at the household level since its establishment.

The Butajira HDSS site is located in the Meskan, West Meskan, Mareko and Silti districts in the Central Ethiopia Region, one of the most densely populated parts, of Ethiopia. These districts are located around 100 to 130 km south of the capital city, Addis Ababa [13]. The people that reside in the HDSS site have access to four hospitals (1 public, 1 private and 2 not-for-profit), seven health centres and 10 health posts. Surgical care such as Caesarean section and abdominal surgery are provided at the hospital level only [13, 14]. The total population size of the Butajira HDSS by the end of 2019 was estimated at 56,781.

Source population

All deaths that occurred in the households of HDSS site in the study period from 2014 to 2019.

Study population

All deaths in the households of HDSS site from 2014 to 2019 for which verbal autopsy data on cause of death had been collected.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Deaths with duplication of personal identifiers, and deaths whose causes were discordant when assigned by three physicians were labelled as indeterminate and dropped from the analysis.

Sample size and selection procedure

The required sample size to estimate avertable deaths due to surgical conditions was computed taking into account the death reports from the HDSS, which ranged from 180-200 deaths per year. Hence, we aimed for a sample size of 700 (though the final analyzed sample size was 636, this size remained sufficiently powered), which would yield precision of +/-1.4% around the percentage of deaths, which could potentially could have been averted with access to surgical care, assuming that 25% of deaths were potentially avertable.

Assignment of cause of death

Butajira HDSS routinely collects the data on death every six months. The HDSS employed the WHO verbal autopsy procedure, questionnaire [15, 16]. Brief narratives on the circumstances of death and symptoms/signs experienced by the deceased person were collected from a family member of the deceased by lay data collectors who visited the home of the deceased person between 45 days and five months after death [15–17].

We requested the HDSS leadership to provide us the death data which were collected from 2014-2019. The quantitative data (e.g., person’s age, gender, educational status, occupation, place of residence and marital status, signs and symptoms of the deceased and other variables) were available in the HDSS database with Stata statistical software version 15 and the brief narratives on the circumstances of death (qualitative data) were available in verbatim.

After cleaning and ensuring completeness of records on causes of death, two senior surgical residents were trained on how to assign cause of death. The surgical residents initially assigned cause of death for 25 records independently and whether the death was potentially surgically avertable. The classification of whether a death was surgically avertable was rated using the following scale:

No, there was already an appropriate surgical intervention;

No, there was already an appropriate surgical intervention;

Yes, definitely a surgical intervention could have prevented the death;

Yes, definitely a surgical intervention could have prevented the death;

Yes, probably a surgical intervention could have prevented the death;

Yes, probably a surgical intervention could have prevented the death;

Yes, possibly a surgical intervention could have prevented the death, and

Yes, possibly a surgical intervention could have prevented the death, and

No, it is unlikely that a surgical intervention could have prevented the death.

No, it is unlikely that a surgical intervention could have prevented the death.

Ratings of these 25 records were compared and discordant assignments of the cause of death were discussed with a consultant surgeon to ensure consistency of ratings.

The surgical residents used WHO Verbal Autopsy (VA) and international classification of diseases 10 tools and codes to indicate the probable underlying cause of death. After the surgical residents completed assigning the causes of death and identifying surgically avertable conditions, discordant causes of death assigned by the surgical residents were sent to a third physician (a senior surgeon) for arbitration. The senior surgeon was given a brief orientation and assessed data of 25 records with discordant diagnoses already reported by the two physicians. Feedback was provided to the surgeon on any ambiguities. Finally, concordance of findings on cause of death between either of the first two surgical residents and the senior surgeon was checked. In this process, only six records had discordant ratings of cause of death and these records were labelled as indeterminate and excluded from the final data analysis.

Measurements

The outcome variable was surgically avertable cause of death (dichotomized to definitely or probably avertable vs. possibly or not avertable. The independent variables included age, gender, educational status, occupation, place of residence, marital status and place of death.

Data management and analysis

Stata version 15 was used for recoding and analysis of data. Descriptive analysis was employed to estimate summary values of the available socio-demographic characteristics, cause of death, and location of death and judgement of whether surgically avertable. Poisson regression model using a sandwich variance estimator [18] was employed to calculate Risk Ratios for factors associated with surgically avertable causes of death. The Poisson regression analysis was conducted taking age of the deceased persons into consideration i.e., with and without inclusion of those under 15 year of age. In addition to this, the regression analysis was done adjusting for key background characteristics of the deceased (i.e., age, gender, residence, marital status, educational status, and place of death) to control for potential confounding.

Ethical considerations

We obtained ethical approval for this study from Institutional Review Board of the College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University. We secured permission to use the anonymized HDSS data for this study from Addis Ababa University, School of Public Health. Informed consent to participate was obtained from the families of the deceased for the original Verbal Autopsy data collection, as it is s standard procedure for the HDSS. Personal identifiers were used to anonymize the data. We adhered to the ethical principles mentioned in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Population characteristics

There were 833 deaths registered in the database of Butajira HDSS, of which 680 records had complete data on causes of death. Of these 680 records, 37 were excluded, as they were duplicates. Seven study participants had discordant causes of death as assigned by the physicians and were excluded from the final analysis. Hence, 636 records were used to estimate causes of death and to identify surgically avertable causes of death (Fig. 1).

Surgically avertable causes of death

Of all the deaths with complete information, (26.2%; n = 167) deaths were rated as definitely or probably surgically avertable. The remaining causes of death were not surgically avertable (67%), possibly surgically avertable (6%) and there already was an appropriate surgical intervention was already done (0.8%). Of these surgically avertable deaths, neoplasms (37.1%; n = 62), external causes (22.2%; n = 37) and renal disorders (17.4%; n = 29) were the causes of death with the highest reported proportion. The commonest surgically avertable causes of death among females were neoplasms (49.2%; n = 32), external causes (18.5%; n = 12) and perinatal causes (9.2%; n = 6). The commonest causes of surgically avertable deaths in males were neoplasms (29.4%; n = 30), renal disorders (28.4%; n = 29) and external causes (24.5%; n = 25). All the surgically avertable deaths due to renal disorders were only reported in males and they were majorly due to bladder outlet obstruction secondary to benign prostate hyperplasia. See Fig. 2.

Factors associated with surgically avertable death

In the adjusted regression model, there was no statistically significant association between age category and surgically avertable death. There was a significantly higher risk ratio for having died from a surgically avertable condition in males compared to females (IRR = 1.94; 95% Confidence Interval (CI) 1.24, 2.14) after adjusting for age and gender. Discussion

Using a large, population-based verbal autopsy dataset from a predominantly rural population in south-central Ethiopia, we found that over one quarter of all deaths were probably or definitely surgically avertable. Important contributors to surgical avertable deaths were neoplasms, external causes and renal disorders.

Our finding is in line with the percentage of surgically avertable deaths reported using a survey-based designs in Sierra Leone [12] and Malawi [19], a systematic review from low income countries [5] and a hospital based assessment conducted in Nigeria [20]. Our study finding implies that access to surgical care is limited and a considerable proportion of the community are dying from surgically avertable causes. The primary causes of death in our study are similar to those reported from Malawi [19] and in a review of surgical conditions in low- and middle-income country settings [5, 21].

Of all the surgically avertable deaths in this study, neoplasms, external causes of death and renal disorders were the causes with the highest proportion. This finding is in agreement with the systematic reviews conducted in 36 countries for neoplasms [5], trauma related assessment in India and Malawi [9, 19].

In our study, men were more likely to have a surgically avertable cause of death, which is line with Indian study [11]. Moreover, we found out that, age at death, residence, marital status, educational status and place of death were not significantly associated with surgically avertable conditions. This is dissimilar to the study finding reported from Malawi disclosing surgically avertable causes of death were more likely to occur outside a health facility [19]. The differences could partly be attributed to better healthcare access and utilization in our study setting than in Malawi.

Considering the sizeable magnitude of surgically avertable causes of death in the current study, it has paramount importance to acknowledge and prioritize the significant barriers currently hindering effective care and prevention of surgically avertable deaths. These obstacles include deeply ingrained community beliefs of delaying access to surgical care [21], the overwhelming issue of catastrophic out of pocket expenditure for surgical care [3, 22], and insufficient leadership engagement at all levels of the health systems [23] that weakens access. Only by addressing these fundamental barriers can we warrant efforts to strengthen the health system to deliver more accessible and quality surgical care. Specifically, this intervention must involve offering further attention to the early identification of surgical problems and establishing a robust linkage to the appropriate level of care needs special attention. To achieve this, the Ministry of Health, Regional Health Bureaus and other actors have a direct responsibility to mobilize resources to improve access to surgical care, and simultaneously, referral linkage needs to be improved. Crucially, the integration of basic surgical care into the primary healthcare system needs to be fundamentally enhanced.

The need for critical analysis as to why some surgically avertable conditions i.e., neoplasms, external causes, renal disorders dominate avertable deaths is a necessity. Such findings need to be streamlined to inform and support the surgical initiatives for further priority setting, resource allocation, improving access through innovative approaches such as instituting mobile health services and subsequent interventions to enhance surgical care in primary healthcare and beyond in Ethiopia. From the global surgery perspective, it would serve in aligning findings with the need for investment in primary level surgical care to achieve Universal Health Coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals.

The strengths of this assessment were that it was most comprehensive and community based VA data set available in the study setting over the study period. Potential recall and social desirability biases from the family of the deceased and physicians’ subjectivity in assigning causes of death might have affected the quality of the data. In addition to this, data incompleteness for the records of many deaths and data on distance to nearest health facility, household income, community health insurance and data age were the limitations that we encountered in assigning causes of death and subsequent surgically avertable conditions, which might have affected the magnitude of various estimates reported in this paper.

Conclusion

This study indicates that a substantial proportion of deaths in the Butajira HDSS were potentially surgically avertable. Neoplasms, external causes (injuries), and renal disorders specifically bladder outlet obstructions in men emerged as the primary surgically avertable conditions. Notably, men were significantly more likely to die from surgically avertable conditions than women were. In the contrary, factors such as residence and educational status did not show statistical significance in this study, the high burden of avertable deaths highlights a critical need to strengthen surgical care access within the primary healthcare system. Future efforts should focus on integrating surgical-specific indicators into routine surveillance to monitor the impact of national initiatives including SaLTS.
