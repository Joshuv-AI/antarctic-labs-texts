Maternal Malignancies Identified Through Non-invasive Prenatal Testing: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Eduardo Teixeira, Gustavo Yano Callado, Daryane Rezzuto, Carolina Josué Vilella Waideman Puga, Henrique Provinciatto, Antonio Braga, Gabriele Tonni, Edward Araujo Júnior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveNon-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) may incidentally reveal chromosomal abnormalities of maternal origin. Although specific chromosomal aberrations have been associated with maternal malignancy, reported risks vary widely. This meta-analysis aimed to quantify the magnitude and consistency of these associations.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies reporting maternal malignancy among pregnant individuals with abnormal NIPT results was conducted. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using a random-effects model. Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool.Results Six studies met the inclusion criteria. Multiple chromosomal aberrations (MCA), although representing a minority of abnormal NIPT findings, accounted for a substantial proportion of confirmed maternal malignancies. MCA were strongly associated with cancer (OR 239.98; 95% CI 80.16–718.44; I 2 = 0%). In studies that further characterized genomic patterns, complex gain–loss profiles were also associated with increased risk (OR 188.64; 95% CI 34.17–1041.51; I 2 = 9.5%). Heterogeneity was low across analyses. ConclusionMultiple chromosomal aberrations and complex gain–loss patterns on NIPT were strongly associated with maternal malignancy. These findings support the use of structured diagnostic pathways and targeted clinical evaluation when high-risk genomic patterns are identified.

Introduction

Although designed to assess fetal chromosomal abnormalities, the non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) can detect atypical copy-number variations originating from the pregnant individual 1 . Previous studies suggest that discordant or complex NIPT results may reflect maternal malignancies 1 2 .

Pregnant women may not exhibit overt clinical symptoms of malignancies during gestation, which can delay the diagnosis 3 4 . Therefore, NIPT may serve as an early indicator, enabling timely oncologic evaluation. In pregnant women with abnormalities identified by NIPT, a single chromosomal aberration (SCA) refers to the detection of an alteration involving only one chromosome, such as a single trisomy 5 . This finding generally reflects an isolated chromosomal alteration of fetal, placental, or, less commonly, maternal origin. In contrast, multiple chromosomal aberrations (MCA) indicate the simultaneous detection of alterations affecting two or more chromosomes. These may include multiple trisomies, multiple monosomies, or a combination of chromosomal gains (trisomies or duplications) and losses (monosomies or deletions) across different chromosomes 6 . An abnormal pattern is defined as the presence of multiple gains and losses in different chromosomes (e.g., trisomy in one chromosome and monosomy in another).

Emerging evidence indicates that specific genomic patterns, particularly MCA, are strongly associated with the presence of maternal cancer, likely reflecting tumor-derived cell free DNA (cfDNA) released into the maternal bloodstream 6 . Consequently, our study aimed to evaluate pregnant women with abnormalities detected through NIPT, assessing the likelihood of identifying an underlying maternal malignancy in cases with MCA compared with those with a SCA. In addition, we aimed to analyze the likelihood of identifying an underlying maternal malignancy in cases with an abnormal pattern compared with a normal pattern in MCA.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines 7 and the methodological recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions 8 . We prospectively registered the research protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD420251243854).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were required to provide extractable data for comparisons between multiple and SCA or, when available, between abnormal and normal gain–loss copy-number patterns among cases with multiple aberrations.

We excluded case reports without systematic follow-up, studies addressing solely fetal outcomes, incomplete datasets, and overlapping populations, defined as those sharing the same study period, institutions, and study inclusion criteria.

Information sources

A systematic search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to December 2025. Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary and free text related to “non-invasive prenatal test”, “chromosomal aberrations”, and “maternal malignancy”. The full search strategy is available in Appendix 1. Reference lists of included studies, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses were screened for additional reports.

Study selection

Search results were imported into Rayyan, and duplicate records were removed 9 . Two independent reviewers (E.T. and H.P.) screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then assessed for inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with the other reviewers (C.J.V.W.P. and D.R.).

Data extraction and assessment of risk of bias

Two authors (E.T. and H.P.) extracted data from selected studies, including: study design, country, sample size, number of maternal malignancies, and NIPT patterns. Two independent authors assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane tool for Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies (ROBINS-I) 10 , wherein each study was scored as high risk, high, moderate, or low risk of bias. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus after discussing reasons for discrepancy.

Data synthesis

We computed the dichotomous outcomes using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A random-effects model using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator was applied to account for between-study variability. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test. P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

We classified I 2 values of < 25%, 25–75%, and > 75% as representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Our meta-analysis was conducted using the meta package for RStudio version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Our initial search resulted in 936 records for study screening. After eliminating duplicate results, 427 studies were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 386 were eliminated based on title and abstract screening. Finally, 41 remained and were fully reviewed based on predefined eligibility criteria ( Fig. 1 ). Maternal age and gestational age were inconsistently reported across the included studies. While three studies specified a minimum gestational age for NIPT (typically ≥ 11–12 weeks) 12 15 16 , none provided detailed distributions or comparative analyses. Maternal age was largely unreported, with only one study mentioning advanced maternal age as a risk category rather than a descriptive variable 16 .

Most studies identified distinct but overlapping patterns of chromosomal aberration associated with maternal malignancy. In the study by Bianchi et al. 11 , maternal cancers were primarily associated with multiple aneuploidies accompanied by genome-wide copy-number gains and losses across several chromosomes. Similarly, Heesterbeek et al. 12 reported that multiple chromosomal aberrations, particularly when identified through genome-wide NIPT, were strongly associated with malignancy. Lenaerts et al. 13 described both genome-wide copy-number alterations and isolated findings such as trisomy 8, although the former demonstrated a higher predictive value for cancer detection. Turriff et al. 14 further refined these observations by distinguishing between patterns, showing that combinations of chromosomal gains and losses affecting three or more chromosomes were highly predictive of malignancy, whereas isolated gains or losses were less specific. Likewise, van der Meij et al. 15 identified complex genome-wide abnormal profiles as indicative of possible maternal cancer in a population-based screening setting. Finally, Yin et al. 16 reported that multiple chromosomal aberrations (≥ 4), often classified as non-reportable results, may be associated with maternal malignancy or other maternal conditions, highlighting the clinical complexity of interpreting such findings.

Discussion

Main findings

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we included 4245 participants from six observational studies evaluating the occurrence of maternal malignancy in the context of abnormal NIPT results 11 12 13 14 15 16 . Our main findings were twofold:

the presence of MCA was associated with a markedly increased likelihood of maternal cancer compared with SCA; and

the presence of MCA was associated with a markedly increased likelihood of maternal cancer compared with SCA; and

among cases with multiple aberrations, abnormal gain-loss patterns were associated with a higher probability of malignancy compared with more uniform patterns.

The maternal malignancies most frequently reported in this context include hematologic cancers (particularly lymphomas and leukemias), as well as solid tumors such as breast and colorectal cancer. These findings are biologically plausible, as tumor-derived cell-free DNA may contribute to complex and discordant genomic signals detected by NIPT. These large effect sizes should be interpreted with caution, given the small number of included studies, potential sparse data bias, and the wide confidence intervals observed in the pooled analyses.

Interpretation in the context of current clinical evidence

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of evidence demonstrating a strong association between complex NIPT abnormalities and occult maternal malignancy. Current clinical guidance from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recognizes that the presence of multiple aneuploidies or discordant chromosomal findings on cfDNA screening should raise suspicion for an underlying maternal cancer 17 . This is supported by the IDENTIFY study, in which Turriff et al. 14 showed that specific genomic patterns, particularly the combination of chromosomal gains and losses across three or more chromosomes, were associated with a markedly high likelihood of malignancy (95.9%), whereas more uniform patterns were more frequently linked to non-malignant conditions. Similar findings have been reported in earlier and large-scale studies, including those by Bianchi et al. 11 and Heesterbeek et al. 12 , which demonstrated that multiple chromosomal aberrations and genome-wide copy-number alterations are strongly enriched in patients with maternal cancer.

However, variability across studies remains substantial, particularly regarding the definition and interpretation of abnormal NIPT patterns. While some studies define abnormality based on the presence of multiple aneuploidies, others rely on genome-wide copy-number alterations or classify results as non-reportable when complex genomic signals preclude fetal interpretation. Consequently, the reported predictive value of abnormal NIPT findings varies widely across the literature, ranging from approximately 40% to over 90%, depending on the genomic pattern, sequencing platform, and study population. We believe that this heterogeneity is primarily driven by differences in analytical approaches and definitions of abnormality. Importantly, our findings support the emerging consensus that not all multiple aberrations carry the same risk, and that complex patterns involving both chromosomal gains and losses represent the most specific genomic signature of underlying maternal malignancy.

Clinical implications

Our results suggest that risk stratification can be further refined by distinguishing between different genomic patterns. Complex profiles characterized by combined chromosomal gains and losses appear to confer a substantially higher risk of malignancy than more uniform alterations, supporting a more tailored approach to post-test evaluation.

From a practical standpoint, our findings reinforce the need for a structured and multidisciplinary diagnostic pathway when high-risk NIPT patterns are identified. Initial evaluation should include detailed clinical history, physical examination, and basic laboratory testing, followed by targeted imaging and oncology referral when appropriate 17 . Emerging evidence suggests that whole-body magnetic resonance imaging may be an effective first-line modality for detecting occult malignancy, whereas routine laboratory tests and tumor markers may have limited diagnostic yield 4 14 . Recent expert-based recommendations further suggest that when no clear explanation for aberrant NIPT findings is identified during pregnancy, postpartum NIPT reassessment may serve as an additional surveillance tool, helping to distinguish transient pregnancy-related abnormalities from persistent maternal genomic alterations. In cases of persistent postpartum abnormalities, continued multidisciplinary follow-up, including repeat imaging, hematologic evaluation, and consideration of benign maternal conditions such as uterine fibroids, may be warranted. Importantly, current recommendations emphasize cautious interpretation and individualized assessment to avoid unnecessary investigations, given the rarity of these findings and the potential psychological burden for patients 18 . As genome-wide NIPT becomes more widely implemented, the development of standardized reporting criteria and evidence-based management algorithms will be essential to optimize clinical decision-making and ensure appropriate counseling.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first meta-analysis to address the association between MCA and abnormal patterns on NIPT with maternal malignancy. Furthermore, although our findings are based mostly on observational studies, they demonstrate low heterogeneity, supporting the consistency of the observed associations across different populations, sequencing platforms, and study designs.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all included studies were observational, which introduces inherent risks of selection bias, particularly as most cohorts included only patients with abnormal or non-reportable NIPT results. Second, the small number of included studies and limited number of malignancy events substantially restrict the precision and robustness of pooled estimates. Although the observed associations were strong, the wide confidence intervals indicate considerable statistical uncertainty, suggesting that effect sizes may be overestimated due to sparse data bias. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and larger prospective studies are needed to confirm the magnitude and reproducibility of these associations. Third, there was substantial heterogeneity in how studies defined and reported abnormal NIPT patterns, including differences between targeted and genome-wide sequencing approaches, thresholds for chromosomal imbalance, and classification of non-reportable results. Fourth, important maternal and pregnancy characteristics, such as age and gestational age, were inconsistently reported, precluding subgroup analyses. Finally, publication bias cannot be excluded, as studies reporting positive associations between abnormal NIPT findings and malignancy may be more likely to be published.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the presence of MCA and abnormal patterns on NIPT were associated with an increased likelihood of maternal malignancy. Although the incidence of this finding is rare, when multiple aneuploidies are detected on NIPT and the fetus has a normal karyotype, investigation for maternal malignancy should be considered, as up to two-thirds of these patients may have a confirmed cancer diagnosis. Early detection of these neoplasms may positively impact maternal prognosis by allowing treatment to begin at less advanced stages of the disease.
