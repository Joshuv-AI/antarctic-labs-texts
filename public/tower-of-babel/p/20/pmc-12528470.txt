GaN/WS2 trilayer heterostructure with tunable band alignment via polarization
Authors: Mubashir A Kharadi, Ibrahim Mahariq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe functional potential and charge-carrier dynamics in van der Waals heterostructures are critically influenced by their electronic band alignment. This alignment can be reconfigured by exploiting spontaneous out-of-plane polarization-induced shifts in the surface energy bands of individual layers. Using density functional theory (DFT), we systematically study how polarization orientation in a two-dimensional buckled GaN layer governs the electronic structure of GaN/WS₂ heterostructures. Simulations reveal that reversing the GaN layer’s polarization direction directly modifies the relative band edge positions of the constituent materials, enabling controllable transitions between type-I and type-II band alignments. Such inherent adjustability, when synergized with externally applied electric fields, provides a dual mechanism for on-demand bandgap and alignment tuning with reduced energy consumption. Enhanced interlayer coupling in these heterostructures also enhances light absorption efficiency, exhibiting higher absorption coefficients and a wider spectral response range than isolated WS₂ and GaN monolayers. These tailored optoelectronic properties highlight GaN/WS₂ heterostructures as adaptable candidates for advanced photonic and electronic devices.Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

In recent years, the scientific community has shown significant interest in two-dimensional (2D) materials for use in electronic and optoelectronic devices due to their unique features, such as surfaces without dangling bonds and their atomic-level thickness1–3. The continuous development of synthesis processes of 2D materials has resulted in many novel 2D materials beyond graphene4. Researchers have explored materials such as transition-metal-dichalcogenides (TMDs)5, hexagonal boron nitride (hBN)6, and III-V binary compounds7 for their potential in optoelectronic applications. Over time, the category of 2D materials has expanded significantly, encompassing substances with diverse electrical characteristics, including metallic, semiconducting, and insulating properties. Each type of 2D material possesses unique attributes, and their van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures offer a distinctive method for combining the varied properties of these 2D materials8. The weak van der Waals bonding between layers in heterostructures maintains their intrinsic material properties while enabling emergent phenomena through interlayer coupling—features absent in isolated 2D materials9–18. The behavior of the carriers and application prospects of a vdW heterostructure depend primarily on the alignment of bands in the heterostructure. The alignment of the bands depends on the work function of the constituent layers of the vdW heterostructure. Different band alignments are shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic band alignments of vdW heterostructures.

Schematic illustration depicting the different types of band alignments (type I, II, and III) possible in van der Waals heterostructures, determined by the relative positions of the conduction and valence band edges of the constituent 2D materials.

The straddling-type band alignment (type-I) confines carriers to a single layer by localizing the valence band maxima (VBM) and conduction band minima (CBM) in the narrow-bandgap material, creating ideal conditions for exciton generation in light-emitting systems. Contrastingly, in type-II band alignment, electrons and holes reside in distinct layers owing to staggered band edges, creating built-in charge separation that optimizes photocurrent generation in light detecting devices. Type-III heterostructures exhibit a broken-gap alignment where one layer’s valence band maximum lies above the other’s conduction band minimum, inducing metallic conductivity through direct band overlap. The alignment of bands in van der Waals heterostructures is determined by the material composition of the layers and how they are stacked17,18. Although band alignment in vdW heterostructures can be adjusted via electric fields, their energy inefficiency necessitates alternative tuning strategies for optimized performance16.

Gallium nitride (GaN) with several layers has been successfully fabricated using graphene encapsulation19. 2D GaN has received considerable attention both theoretically20–22 as well as experimentally19,23–25 by exhibiting characteristics like variation of bandgap with thickness and improved exciton behaviour. Based on theoretical studies, 2D GaN (passivated with hydrogen (H) atom) with a buckled configuration is thermodynamically more stable than the planar version19. Besides, 2D buckled GaN shows a direct band gap of 4. 10 eV, which is suitable for optoelectronic applications26. After the initial theoretical predictions, 2D buckled GaN was Successfully prepared in 201924. The 2D buckled GaN portrays intense out-of-plane polarization due to the ionic nature of the Ga-N bond27. The spontaneity of this polarization results in a strong electric field, thereby providing an alternative for tuning the alignment of the bands in a vdW heterostructure. While prior literature provides valuable understanding of polarization effects in GaN-based heterostructures28–31, our work advances the field by focusing on monolayer buckled GaN with switchable intrinsic polarization in a dual-interface sandwiched configuration. Several aspects of GaN/TMD heterostructures have been studied. The authors in28 have explored the influence of intrinsic polarization on band alignment and carrier dynamics in GaN/TMD heterostructures using DFT focusing primarily on multilayer GaN thin films and their interactions with TMD layers. Furthermore, strain- and polarization-induced modification of electronic structures in GaN/TMD heterostructures have been explored in29. Besides, the effect of number of layers of GaN on the optical and electronic properties of GaN/WS2 heterostructure has been presented in31. The current work advances the understanding beyond previous studies by focusing on the monolayer buckled GaN limit, where intrinsic polarization can be switched via stacking orientation. Unlike prior works that center on thick or few-layer GaN films and usually single-interface heterostructures, this manuscript explores a sandwiched GaN/WS₂/GaN heterostructure providing dual-interface control over polarization-induced electric fields.

Polarity switching in GaN, including transitions between Ga-polar and N-polar orientations, can be experimentally achieved through several strategies32–34. Substrate engineering—such as selecting specific substrates (e.g., SiC, sapphire), employing buffer layers like AlN, or modifying substrate surfaces via oxidation or nitridation—critically influences the polarity. Epitaxial strain and growth conditions during techniques like metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) further enable polarity control by stabilizing desired polarities and suppressing inversion domains. Although direct polarization reversal by external electric fields is challenging in bulk GaN due to its non-ferroelectric nature, electric fields can assist switching in ultrathin layers or heterostructures where depolarization effects are reduced. Additionally, interface and surface modifications, such as inserting oxidized AlN, provide effective polarity inversion points through controlled oxidation and regrowth. These approaches collectively offer practical pathways for dynamic or static polarity control in GaN-based systems.

This study investigates a van der Waals heterostructure combining 2D WS₂ and buckled 2D GaN with engineered polarization states. Using density functional theory (DFT)35, we demonstrate that intrinsic polarization in GaN enables precise band alignment control, driven by interfacial potential differences that generate substantial polarization-induced electric fields. By reversing the GaN layer’s polarization direction, we achieve tunable transitions between type-I and type-II band alignments. Comparative analysis reveals that this polarization-driven band engineering outperforms conventional external electric field methods in efficiency and precision. The heterostructure exhibits enhanced optical absorption across polarization configurations compared to isolated WS₂ and GaN monolayers, positioning it as a versatile candidate for next-generation optoelectronic technologies.

Models and methods

All simulations were performed using the Atomistix Toolkit software package36. Structural relaxations and energy computations employed the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) framework, incorporating van der Waals interactions via the DFT-D3 correction scheme37–39. The computational parameters included a density mesh cut-off of 125 Hartree and a Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid of 15 × 15 × 1 for Brillouin zone sampling. Structural optimizations utilized the LBFGS algorithm with convergence thresholds of 0.0001 eVÅ³ for stress and 0.01 eVÅ⁻¹ for atomic forces40. A 20 Å vacuum layer was implemented to mitigate periodic boundary interactions. For all the calculations spin-orbit coupling (SOC) has not been considered. In GaN/WS₂ heterostructures, the modulation of internal electric fields and band alignment is more precisely attributed to interfacial dipole compensation rather than generic “field screening” by free carriers. When the GaN and WS₂ layers are brought into contact, electrons transfer from WS₂ (lower work function) to GaN (higher work function), resulting in the WS₂ acquiring a net positive charge and GaN a net negative charge at the interface. This interfacial charge redistribution forms an electric dipole, whose field directly opposes and partially cancels the intrinsic polarization field of the buckled GaN monolayer. The effect of this dipole is a reduction in the overall electrostatic potential difference, thereby tuning the band offsets and the net built-in field across the heterostructure. This physical picture of interfacial dipole compensation provides a more accurate and microscopic understanding of how charge transfer neutralizes polarization-induced potential differences, and is especially significant for heterostructures exhibiting type-II band alignment, where the spatial separation of electrons and holes enhances the dipole effect across the interface.

The interlayer charge transfer induced by work function differences and spontaneous polarization in GaN/WS₂ heterostructures leads to spatial separation of electron-hole pairs across the interface, particularly in the type-II aligned GaN/WS₂ ‘Up’ configuration. This spatial separation reduces exciton binding energy by weakening Coulomb interactions, thereby facilitating exciton dissociation into free carriers—an effect advantageous for photovoltaic and photodetection applications. Moreover, the resulting interfacial dipole modifies excitonic transitions and optical properties, enhancing absorption and emission tunability. Conversely, in the type-I aligned GaN/WS₂ ‘Down’ configuration, excitons remain more localized, favoring radiative recombination suitable for light-emitting applications.

The GaN/WS₂ ‘Up’ polarization produces a smaller bandgap (~ 0.84 eV) due to the type-II band alignment, where electrons and holes reside in different layers (WS₂ and GaN, respectively). This reduces the effective optical bandgap, leading to an earlier onset of absorption around 0.85 eV, enabling the heterostructure to absorb photons from a broader spectral range, especially in the infrared to visible region. In contrast, the GaN/WS₂ ‘Down’ polarization exhibits a larger bandgap (~ 2.07 eV) with type-I alignment confining both charge carriers to the GaN layer. The absorption onset occurs at a higher photon energy (~ 2.10 eV), restricting absorption to higher energies (near-ultraviolet), consistent with the intrinsic properties of GaN. The type-II alignment in the ‘Up’ state promotes spatial separation of photogenerated electrons and holes, enhancing interlayer coupling and facilitating exciton dissociation. This results in broad, enhanced optical absorption over a wide spectral range, but with a moderate maximum absorption coefficient. Conversely, the type-I alignment in the ‘Down’ state concentrates excitons within the WS2, increasing the overlap of electron and hole wavefunctions. This leads to stronger, sharper intralayer optical transitions with a slightly higher maximum absorption coefficient (5.28 × 10⁵ cm⁻¹) but a higher absorption threshold.

The optical absorption of the two heterostructures is relatively higher than the standalone GaN and WS2 layers. For GaN/WS2 ‘Up’ heterostructure, the absorption peak is around an energy value of 3.52 eV with an absorption coefficient (αa) of 4.82 × 105 cm−1. However, for GaN/WS2 ‘Down’ heterostructure, the peak of the absorption spectrum is located at an energy value of 3.43 eV with an absorption coefficient (αa) of 5.28 × 105 cm−1. Besides, both the heterostructures show a wider range of energies they can absorb. Elevated αa values in the heterostructures arise from coupled electronic state overlap and enhanced optical transitions introduced by layer interactions44.

The large difference in bandgap response between the GaN/WS₂ ‘up’ and GaN/WS₂ ‘Down’ heterostructures originates from the interplay between the intrinsic polarization field of buckled GaN and the direction and magnitude of the externally applied electric field. In the “up” configuration, the out-of-plane polarization of GaN is oriented such that its built-in field is aligned (additive) with the externally applied field (when the field direction is positive). As a result, the total field across the heterostructure is monotonically strengthened or weakened depending on the field direction, causing a smooth and nearly linear tuning of band edges. This manifests as a monotonic increase or decrease in bandgap with the applied field, with no abrupt changes or reversals in trend. In “down” configuration, the intrinsic polarization of GaN is oriented opposite to that in the “up” configuration, so its built-in field is initially opposed to the externally applied field (for positive field direction). As the external field increases from negative to positive values, the external field counteracts the intrinsic polarization field, which screens the polarization-induced band offsets. This results in an initial increase in bandgap because the band edges are “flattened,” reducing interlayer potential difference. Once the external field surpasses and reverses the intrinsic polarization, the net field direction flips. Now, the effective field across the heterostructure is in the same direction as the applied field—but opposite to the original intrinsic field—causing the GaN bands and WS₂ bands to shift in energy in a way that narrows the bandgap. Thus, the bandgap starts to decrease with further increase in applied field. This non-monotonic (increase-then-decrease) variation is due to the competition and eventual reversal between the intrinsic polarization field and the external field. The transition point, where the bandgap reaches its maximum, corresponds to the field strength at which these two fields precisely cancel each other out.

Unlike external fields, optimizing the polarization direction of GaN/WS₂ heterostructures achieves band edge offsets with minimal energy consumption, enabling proficient band alignment control. Moreover, strategically designing the heterostructure’s initial band edges through built-in polarization allows precise regulation of the bandgap to a predetermined value under smaller applied fields. Such a fact could be significantly beneficial for optoelectronic applications. Intrinsic polarization provides a strong, stable built-in field that sets the baseline band alignment and electronic structure of GaN/WS₂ heterostructures with low power consumption and distinct type-I vs. type-II regimes. External electric fields offer a dynamic tuning knob to further modulate band edges and bandgap around these intrinsic states, enabling real-time control but at the cost of continuous energy input. Combining both effects yields highly versatile polarization-engineered vdW heterostructures with tailored optoelectronic properties for next-generation low-power, tunable devices.This comparison clarifies that intrinsic polarization establishes primary device functionality and energy-efficient baseline control, while external fields provide complementary, real-time adjustment capabilities.

GaN/WS₂ heterostructures with intrinsic polarization exhibit unique electronic and optical properties that make them highly promising for a range of advanced applications. The strong out-of-plane polarization in the buckled GaN layer enables tunable band alignment, facilitating efficient spatial separation of electrons and holes in type-II configurations, which is beneficial for high-performance photodetectors and solar cells through enhanced charge carrier extraction and reduced recombination. Conversely, switching the polarization can induce type-I alignment, localizing carriers to promote strong radiative recombination for light-emitting diodes and lasers. The intrinsic polarization also creates stable interfacial dipoles that can modulate excitonic behavior—such as binding energy and lifetime—paving the way for excitonic devices applications. Additionally, the built-in electric fields support efficient charge separation critical for photocatalysis, including water splitting. Combined with 2D van der Waals stacking, these heterostructures offer mechanical flexibility and environmental stability, making them ideal candidates for flexible, low-power optoelectronic and nanoelectronic devices with dynamic, polarization-driven functionality.

Conclusion

This work explores the tuning of the band alignment in 2D GaN/WS2 heterostructure by in-built polarization of the 2D GaN layer via DFT calculations. The GaN layer’s polarization direction determines the electronic behavior of the heterostructures, as demonstrated by the GaN/WS₂ ‘Up’ structure’s type-II band alignment and 0.84 eV indirect bandgap. The GaN/WS₂ ‘Down’ configuration demonstrates a direct bandgap measuring 2.11 eV (with type-I band alignment). By strategically adjusting GaN’s built-in polarization, targeted band alignments and bandgaps can be achieved in GaN/WS₂ heterostructures with low external fields. The GaN/WS₂ ‘Up’ heterostructure demonstrates significantly boosted absorption and broader range compared to its individual monolayer constituents. This improvement arises from interlayer coupling, which facilitates strong electronic interactions and additional optical transitions, making the heterostructure highly efficient in utilizing visible light for optoelectronic and photocatalytic applications. Buckled 2D GaN’s strong built-in polarization enables tunable electronic/optoelectronic van der Waals heterostructures, as highlighted by these findings.
