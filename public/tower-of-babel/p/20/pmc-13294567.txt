Prevalence and Discrimination of On-going Fibrillation in Isolated Pulmonary Vein in Non-paroxysmal AF Ablation
Authors: Ling-cong Kong, Tian Shuang, Jian-feng Huang, Hai-feng Wang, Zhi-guo Zou, Xin-hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:It is unclear whether on-going fibrillation in isolated pulmonary veins (OFIP) may coexist with AF in patients undergoing ablation for non-paroxysmal AF (PAF). This study explored the prevalence of OFIP in non-PAF ablation with the aim of developing an algorithm to distinguish between OFIP and isolation failure, thereby minimising excessive ablation.Methods:Non-PAF patients undergoing first-time ablation were divided into a retrospective induction cohort (IC; n=300) and a prospective validation cohort (VC; n=263). In the case of on-going pulmonary vein (PV) fibrillation after ablation, the completeness of PV isolation was assessed based on the change in PV potential and after additional ablation.Results:Among 563 patients, 20 (3.55%) had OFIP (9 in the IC; 11 in the VC). No significant predictors of OFIP were identified in terms of baseline characteristics or procedural factors. Based on three right-sided, five left-sided and one bilateral case of OFIP in the IC, the disappearance of PV potential (either spontaneous or mechanical bumping-induced) or a combination of bipolar voltage reduction >80%, local activation time later than PV recordings and no response to carina ablation were adopted as major criteria in the algorithm to discriminate between OFIP and isolation failure. Among the 20 patients in the VC with on-going PV fibrillation, the algorithm identified true OFIP in 11, false OFIP in three and true incomplete PV isolation in six. The sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values of the algorithm were 100%, 66.7%, 78.6% and 100%, respectively.Conclusion:The rate of OFIP was not negligible in non-PAF patients undergoing ablation procedures to achieve efficient and safe PV isolation. A novel four-step algorithm was developed to discriminate OFIP from isolation failure, with good predictive value.

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the cornerstone for AF ablation.1 PVI frequently results in termination of episodes of paroxysmal AF (PAF) without termination of fibrillation in the isolated pulmonary vein (PV).2 However, PVI usually results in the abolition of PV potentials (PVP) without concomitant termination of non-PAF due to the complex substrate involved in the maintenance of non-PAF.3,4 Therefore, on-going rapid electrical activity within the circular lesion lines (CLLs) usually indicates incomplete PVI in non-PAF ablation.

PV entrance and exit block are two requisite criteria for confirming PVI, typically after a 20-minute observation time.3,5,6 On-going PV fibrillation can be readily recognised as a sign of PV exit block if AF termination is achieved. However, it is technically difficult to evaluate PVI status in the setting of on-going PV fibrillation concomitant with persistent AF. Theoretically, on-going rapid electrical activity (fibrillation) in isolated PVs, referred to as on-going fibrillation in isolated pulmonary veins (OFIP), may occur if the autonomic rhythm of PVPs in isolated PV is highly active. It is unclear whether OFIP may coexist with persistent AF after ablation for non-PAF. If the operator is unaware of OFIP and repeatedly applies ablation to ‘gaps’ to ‘isolate’ the PVs, the duration of the procedure will be greatly prolonged, and the risks of severe complications, such as cardiac tamponade and PV stenosis, will increase. Although electrical cardioversion is typically used to evaluate bidirectional blockade in sinus rhythm, the presence of residual rapid PV fibrillation on electrograms (EGMs) may lead to cardioversion failure. In addition, cardioversion poses a risk of patients’ location dislodgement and errors in 3D navigation. Therefore, prompt differentiation between OFIP and PVI would prevent unnecessary electrical cardioversion.

This clinical study aimed to identify and explore the prevalence of OFIP in a consecutive cohort of non-PAF patients undergoing ablation, and to develop an algorithm to distinguish between OFIP and isolation failure, thereby minimising excessive ablation and associated risks.

Methods

Study Design

Patients were divided into two cohorts, a retrospective induction cohort (IC; n=300), used to refine the OFIP identification algorithm, and a prospective validation cohort (VC; n=263), used to assess the accuracy of the algorithm and its utility in an external independent dataset.

Study Participants

All consecutive patients scheduled to undergo first-time catheter ablation at Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, between September 2020 and August 2023 were considered for inclusion in the study. Prior to ablation, patients underwent transoesophageal echocardiography to exclude left atrial (LA) thrombus. The study’s inclusion criteria were persistent AF (PeAF) or long-standing PeAF (LPeAF), age 18–75 years, and LA diameter <55 mm on transthoracic echocardiography. The definitions of PeAF and LPeAF conformed to the European Society of Cardiology AF guidelines.1 Specifically, PeAF was defined as continuous AF lasting for >7 days without intermittent restoration of normal sinus rhythm (NSR), and LPeAF was defined as AF that was sustained for >12 months.1 Patients with LA thrombus, prior catheter or surgical ablation, moderate to severe valve insufficiency or stenosis, decompensated heart failure or uncontrolled hyperthyroidism were excluded. Prior to ablation, all antiarrhythmic drugs were withdrawn for at least five half-lives or >1 month in the case of amiodarone.

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All patients provided written informed consent.

Electrophysiological Study

Oral anticoagulation (direct oral anticoagulants or warfarin) was not suspended prior to ablation. Intraprocedural heparin was used to maintain an activated clotting time of 300–350 seconds. A decapolar mapping catheter (Abbott Medical) was positioned in the coronary sinus via left femoral vein puncture. Access to the LA was obtained by two transseptal procedures. A duo-decapolar mapping catheter (PentaRay, Biosense Webster) was used to reconstruct LA geometry and to map PVP. After reconstruction of the LA geometry, the LA volume value was calculated using the built-in CARTO 3 software (Biosense Webster). Intracardiac bipolar EGMs were recorded (bandpass filter 30–400 Hz).

Radiofrequency Ablation Protocol

Circumferential PV ablation (CPVA) was performed in all patients as the first step; adjunctive linear ablation was used if substrate modification was deemed necessary. A porous contact force-sensing ablation catheter (Thermocool SmartTouch SF, Biosense Webster) was used to create contiguous lesions (with an interlesion distance ≤4 mm) encircling first the right-sided PVs (RPVs) and then the left-sided PVs (LPVs) in high-power mode (45–50 W), with a target ablation index of 350–380 at the posterior wall (350–360 for the oesophagus, typically identified by an intracardiac echocardiogram at the inferior or the posterior–inferior antrum of LPVs) and 450–500 for the remaining areas. ‘First-pass PVI’ refers to achieving PVI once the CLL was complete; otherwise, touch-up gap ablation in the lesion line was performed. If it was considered that complete PVI had been achieved in the context of on-going PV fibrillation according to the criteria detailed below, no additional PV ablation was applied, and linear ablation was subsequently performed. Linear ablation included ablation of the LA roof line and the lateral mitral isthmus line, with a radiofrequency power of 40 W and a target ablation index of 500–550. The procedural endpoint of ablation was defined as achievement of PVI and bidirectional conduction block across the lesion lines.

Responses to Circumferential Pulmonary Vein Ablation

The PVP responses to CPVA were described in terms of changes in PVP cycle length and the disappearance of PVP. Commonly, three types of PVP response were observed:

type 1: all ipsilateral PVPs disappeared or exhibited extremely slow (~10-20 BPM) autonomic rhythm, which indicated PV entrance block but warranted further PVI verification after NSR was restored;

type 1: all ipsilateral PVPs disappeared or exhibited extremely slow (~10-20 BPM) autonomic rhythm, which indicated PV entrance block but warranted further PVI verification after NSR was restored;

type 2: PVPs were obviously slowed but did not disappear; this was deemed incomplete PVI, and the residual gaps in the CLL were closed by touch-up ablation; and

type 2: PVPs were obviously slowed but did not disappear; this was deemed incomplete PVI, and the residual gaps in the CLL were closed by touch-up ablation; and

type 3: there were minimal changes in the PVP cycle length after completion of CPVA;7 in this scenario, the PVI status could not easily be assessed.

Gap Mapping and Pulmonary Vein Isolation Assessment in Induction Cohort Patients With Type 3 Responses

Unlike type 1 and type 2 responses (indicating complete and incomplete PVI, respectively), PVI status was not easily determined in patients exhibiting a type 3 response. Thus, in this study, we focused on patients with a type 3 response to investigate a practical means for assessing the completeness of PVI.

Rare and unique PVP phenomena needed to be noted before the mapping of gaps, specifically spontaneous or mechanical bumping-induced disappearance of PVP during the subsequent ablation process, which strongly indicated the presence of PV entrance block (Figures 1A and 1B).

Otherwise, the status of PVI was evaluated systematically according to observations from previous pilot studies. First, the quality of radiofrequency-induced lesions was evaluated by checking the amplitude of bipolar EGMs with a reduction of >80% of initial values.8 Second, the lesion sites with a local activation time preceding PVP recordings during PV fibrillation were considered potential conduction gaps and would be a target for touch-up ablation; thus, the activation time at each lesion site along the CLL was checked to determine if it fell behind PV recordings or not (Figure 2). Third, epicardial connection had to be ruled out by empirical linear ablation at the intervenous carina before PV exit block could be suspected (Figure 1C).9–11

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± SD, whereas those with a skewed distribution are presented as the median with interquartile range. Normally distributed and skewed continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test and the Mann–Whitney U-test, respectively. Categorical variables are presented as counts or proportions and were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. To explore significant factors predicting OFIP, variables with p<0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM). Two-tailed p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 563 consecutive non-PAF patients were enrolled and divided into a retrospective IC (n=300; from September 2020 to July 2022) and a prospective VC (n=263; from August 2022 to August 2023). OFIP coexisting with persistent AF was observed in 9 and 11 patients in the IC and VC, respectively, with an overall prevalence of 3.55% among patients undergoing de novo catheter ablation for non-PAF. The baseline demographic characteristics of patients included in this study are presented in Table 1.

Procedural Findings

The procedural findings are presented in Table 2. The proportion of mitral isthmus ablation was lower in patients with than without OFIP. The RPV ablation time was significantly shorter in the IC than in the VC. Among all patients without OFIP, the proportion of first-pass RPV and LPV isolation was approximately 80% and 60%, respectively. Intergroup comparisons (both between IC vs VC groups, and groups with versus without OFIP) of other procedural parameters revealed no significant differences.

Development of an Algorithm to Identify On-going Fibrillation in Isolated Pulmonary Veins

The discrimination results in the IC are shown in detail in Figure 3. Briefly, a type 3 PVP response was observed in 11 patients, including fibrillation in the LPVs and RPVs in five patients, respectively, and in bilateral PVs in one patient. In one patient, the fibrillation rhythm in the RPV disappeared spontaneously during the ablation procedure. In another patient, rapid PVP in the RPV disappeared by inadvertent mechanical bumping. Both patients were deemed to have OFIP, and PVI was confirmed after restoration of NSR. In the remaining nine patients, revisiting the PVI lesion confirmed a >80% reduction in the amplitude of the bipolar EGMs. Seven patients had an activation time at the lesion sites that was later than that of the PVPs, whereas the remaining two patients failed to meet this criterion. In these two patients, the PVPs did not respond to touch-up ablation in the CLL. However, after linear ablation at the intervenous carina, RPV isolation was achieved in another two patients, neither of whom was considered to have OFIP. The remaining seven patients did not receive any further gap ablation. After direct current (DC) electric cardioversion, PVI was verified in all nine patients. A comparison of parameters between patients with and without OFIP is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Based on these observations, we developed a four-step algorithm combining mapping and intervention to discriminate between isolation failure and PVI completion in the setting of on-going PV fibrillation coexisting with persistent AF (Figure 4). Briefly, PVI completion was considered if either of the confirmation criteria was met or all three suspected criteria were fulfilled. Spontaneous PVP disappearance (Figure 1A) or PVP disappearance caused by inadvertent mechanical bumping (Figure 1B) were adopted as the confirmation criteria in this study, because both items are strong evidence of PV entrance block. The suspected criteria aimed to exclude conduction gaps along the CLLs and epicardial connection and included: a >80% reduction in the amplitude of the bipolar EGM compared with initial values, which was a prerequisite for the adequacy of radiofrequency lesions; a local activation time that was later than that of neighbouring PV recordings in case of transient/intermittent PV fibrillation (Figure 2), which helped exclude the possibility of residual gaps; and no change in the PVP in response to diagnostic linear ablation at the intervenous carina (Figure 1C). PVI completion during persistent AF was validated after restoration of NSR (Figure 1D and Supplementary Figure 1).

Performance of the Algorithm in the Validation Cohort

The performance of the developed algorithm was tested in the subsequent prospective VC by comparing the predicted results with the exact PVI status evaluated in NSR. A type 3 PVP response to CPVA was observed in 20 of 263 patients in the prospective VC.

During the procedure, RPV fibrillation disappeared spontaneously in three patients and as a result of mechanical bumping in two; thus, these five patients met the confirmation criteria (with PVI validation in all five patients after DC cardioversion). The remaining 15 patients met the amplitude reduction criterion. However, the activation item was not met in three patients, and PVI was achieved in these patients by touch-up gap ablation. Intervenous carina ablation resulted in RPV isolation in two patients and LPV isolation in one. Nine patients met all three criteria for suspected OFIP and were deemed to have complete PVI. After DC cardioversion, complete PVI was validated in six of the nine patients meeting the criteria for suspected OFIP (isolated fibrillation in the LPV in three patients, in the RPV in two patients, and in the bilateral PVs in one patient). In three patients with a false positive prediction of OFIP, PVI was obtained by inferior ostial ablation in the right inferior PV in two patients and by posterosuperior ostial ablation in the left superior PV in one patient. The discrimination results are presented in Supplementary Figure 2.

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value of the algorithm for discriminating between isolation failure and on-going fibrillation in isolated PVs in the VC were 100%, 66.7%, 78.6% and 100%, respectively.

Procedural Complications

Two patients had femoral artery pseudoaneurysm and three patients had groin haematoma. All five patients were treated with conservative compression and recovered without any sequela. There was no cardiac tamponade, thromboembolic event or PV stenosis during the 1-month periprocedural period.

Discussion

One of the main findings of this study is that on-going fibrillation in isolated PVs could coexist with sustained AF after ablation for non-PAF, with a prevalence of 3.55% among a cohort of 563 non-PAF patients. Another notable outcome of this study was the development of a novel four-step algorithm for distinguishing OFIP in the setting of on-going PV fibrillation and persistent AF, which demonstrated good predictive accuracy. To the best of our knowledge, no similar studies have been reported.

Clinical Significance of Timely Identification of On-going Fibrillation in Isolated Pulmonary Vein

CPVA in patients with non-PAF typically results in the elimination of PVP rather than concomitant termination of AF.3 However, PV fibrillation may be self-sustaining independent of afferent rapid impulses from the LA. Hence, it is possible that PV fibrillation and AF coexist despite the completion of PVI in ablation procedures for non-PAF. In this scenario, it is technically difficult to accurately determine the PVI status. However, failure to recognise OFIP and repeated touch-up ablation may prolong the duration of the procedure and increase the risks of PV stenosis, cardiac perforation, oesophageal injury and other severe complications. Traditionally, another valid approach for patients with on-going AF at the beginning of an ablation procedure is to perform electrical cardioversion after first-pass circumferential PV ablation to facilitate gap mapping if no obvious entrance block is achieved; however, residual rapid PVP, some of which turned out to be epicardial connections in the present study and was successfully treated with ablation of the carina region, could fail to maintain NSR and additional repeated electrical cardioversions.

In this sense, the timely recognition of PVI completion in the context of on-going PV fibrillation and AF is of considerable clinical significance to ensure an efficient and safe PVI procedure. In our study, the prevalence of on-going fibrillation in isolated PVs was 3.55% in the non-PAF cohort, which is not clinically negligible.

Discrimination of On-going Fibrillation in Isolated Pulmonary Veins after Ablation

Neither the prevalence nor the algorithm for the identification of OFIP has been reported in the literature. This study presents a novel four-step algorithm developed on the basis of data for the IC (n=300 patients). As outlined in the Methods section, three types of PVP responses to CPVA were defined, with the type 3 response requiring that one confirmation criterion or all three suspected criteria be met. We observed two specific indicators of PV isolation: termination of PV fibrillation and the disappearance of PVP, either spontaneously or as a result of mechanical bumping. These indicators are robust evidence for PVI completion and serve as confirmation criteria, although they failed to reach statistical significance due to the limited sample size of this study.

In terms of the three suspected criteria, the first, a reduction in the amplitude of local bipolar EGMs of >80%, is a well-recognised indicator for a quality radiofrequency lesion.8 Despite uniform settings for the ablation index value, an inadequate lesion may form in the case of poor catheter stability, as a result of the cessation of ablation due to body movement or other causes. For the second criterion, we observed isolated PV fibrillation exhibiting an activation pattern from within the PV to the antrum and included this as a suspected criterion (Figure 2), which, to be noted, is not as clear as in NSR.

Moreover, epicardial connection is not uncommon in patients without first-pass PVI, with intervenous carina breakthroughs being the dominant type of epicardial connection that hampers PVI.10 Other sites, like epicardial connections within the inferior ostium of the right inferior PV or within the posterosuperior ostium of the left superior PV, have been reported in the literature.12,13 In the prospective VC, the four-step algorithm demonstrated favourable predictive accuracy to discriminate between non-isolated PV fibrillation and isolated PV fibrillation. This suggests its potential utility in detecting this infrequent occurrence during routine non-PAF ablation procedures.

Possible Impact of On-going Fibrillation in Isolated Pulmonary Veins on Non-paroxysmal AF Ablation Effectiveness

Rapid PV electrical activity is a well-known driver of the initiation and perpetuation of AF in both PAF and non-PAF. With rapid technological advances, there has been a marked decrease in the PV reconnection rate after ablation index-guided high-power CPVA.6 In a recent study, the most recent novel pulsed field ablation catheter was reported to produce the lowest long-term PV reconnection rate among four ablation energy sources.14 However, although acute pulmonary vein isolation success rates with pulsed field ablation are high, lesion durability over time can be affected by subthreshold electroporation and anatomical variability.15

Theoretically, if durable OFIP is achieved, its impact on clinical effectiveness may be insignificant. Moreover, differences in patients’ baseline characteristics, such as age, AF duration and LA size, may have a greater impact on ablation effectiveness. In addition, the approaches for non-PAF ablation vary markedly between different cardiac electrophysiology laboratories, and this could significantly affect ablation results. Thus, future large-scale prospective cohort studies should evaluate the impact of OFIP on the effectiveness of non-PAF ablation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the limited sample size restricts our ability to explore factors associated with OFIP and to evaluate its impact on the effectiveness of non-PAF ablation. In addition, the single-centre study design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of our findings. Multicentre validation is warranted to determine the true prevalence of OFIP across different populations. Second, although the discrimination algorithm exhibited good sensitivity and specificity in the prospective VC, the main items selected as the suspected criteria are based on limited observations in an IC consisting of 300 patients. Due to the instability of PVP activation and cycle length during AF, judgements regarding the local activation sequence in each patient may be ambiguous. Therefore, the algorithm may need refining in future studies. However, it is advisable for an operator to withdraw prolonged gap ablation and check PVI status in NSR if repeated touch-up ablation fails to eliminate PVP. Third, intervenous carina breakthrough is the major, but not only, source of epicardial connection. Other epicardial connections have been reported in rare cases. Hence, linear ablation at the intervenous carina breakthrough is not sufficient to preclude other epicardial connections to the PV myocardial sleeve and may yield false-positive prediction results when judging PVI achievement. However, epicardial connections other than the carina area are even rarer and are difficult to localise before ablation. Finally, the PVI energy we used in this study was a traditional thermal approach (i.e., radiofrequency ablation); it is not clear whether a similar OFIP phenomenon would be present with pulsed field ablation. Further studies are needed after pulsed field ablation to verify the conclusions of the present study.

Conclusion

OFIP coexisted with persistent AF and a rate that was not negligible in patients undergoing ablation for non-paroxysmal AF. A novel four-step algorithm was developed to discriminate between OFIP and isolation failure, with good predictive value.
