Post-traumatic osteoarthritic-mediated changes in condylar shape do not covary with changes in the internal microstructure of the bone
Authors: Zach Skabelund, Dakshina Acharya, Jonathan Banks, Minahil Chaudhry, Chun-Chieh Huang, Christina Nicholas, David Reed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is associated with remodeling of the subchondral bone. This remodeling changes both the external appearance of the condylar bone and the internal bony microstructure. The external geometry can be quantified using shape, a multivariate mathematical measurement that contains all of the structure’s geometric information with location, scale, and rotation effects removed. There is an important gap in knowledge related to how TMJ PTOA affects the shape of the mandible and if the external shape covaries with the internal bony microstructure. To evaluate these gaps, TMJ PTOA was induced in male and female skeletally mature mice using a surgical destabilization procedure. After four weeks, tissues were collected and characterized using a high-resolution μCT scanner. Shape was calculated from surface reconstructions of the mandibular condyle, and the internal bony microstructure was characterized by the region of interest including the subchondral trabeculae. The covariance of shape with and without corrections for allometric scaling and internal bony microstructure was calculated using a Procrustes ANOVA. The data illustrate that PTOA significantly alters the shape of the condyle in a sex-independent manner. PTOA does alter some aspects of the internal bony microstructure in a sex-dependent manner. Allometric scaling was a significant factor in the variance of shape. Shape including the effects of allometric scaling significantly covaries with some internal bony microstructure variables in both sexes. Shape scaled to remove the effects of allometric scaling does not covary with internal bony microstructure in either sex. These findings indicate that PTOA progression is associated with changes in the size and shape of the condyle but variance in trabecular bone remodeling is only associated with size related shape change. Thus, the allostatic response of subchondral bone is multimodal, coordinating two independent biological processes controlling size and shape. Since subchondral bone participates in and guides the progression of PTOA, these findings have implications for identifying select and specific mechanisms contributing to the progression and pathophysiology of the PTOA in the TMJ.

Introduction

Dysfunction of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is one of the most common musculoskeletal disorders in the craniofacial skeleton, and is associated with pain, limited mobility, facial asymmetry, malocclusion, growth disturbance, ankylosis, and diminished quality of life [1–3]. Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) of the TMJ occurs in 38.3 % of patients with a history of trauma to the mandible [4]. There are no current therapeutic interventions for PTOA and the etiology has yet to be fully resolved. Early intervention is in many cases beneficial to the patient as pain and dysfunction can be attenuated through conservative, non-surgical approaches. Thus, there is an emphasis on preclinical investigations that focus on understanding the genetic, biochemical, and structural changes that occur in the TMJ during this critical period.

One of the defining clinical diagnostic criteria of PTOA progression is to change the appearance of the mandibular condyle as assessed through cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and/or radiographs. Human patients typically present with 1) flattening of the condylar head, 2) osteophytes/osteophytic lipping, 3) erosion/pitting and 4) changes in the subchondral microarchitecture of bone [3,5–7]. Changes in the subchondral bone during the progression of TMJ OA are complex and manifest as a loss of bone mass in the early stages of the disorder and thickening of the bone at the osteochondral interface during end-stage disorder. These dynamic changes in subchondral bone are more than simple diagnostic criteria, actively guiding and participating in the pathophysiology of TMJ OA by regulating matrix and bone remodeling and osteoclast recruitment [8,9]. Thus, there is a critical need for understanding how and why the subchondral bone remodels and changes during the progression of TMJ OA.

There are a variety of preclinical animal models that permit mechanistic investigation of TMJ OA. TMJ OA can be induced in these models through surgical manipulation of the articular disc, biochemical injections, transgenic manipulation, occlusal overloading, and/or injury to the joint. In nearly all animal models, bony changes are observed in both the gross appearance of the condyle and in the microarchitecture of the trabecular bone and is a hallmark of the early stages of TMJ OA [6,10]. Surprisingly, this pattern of change in the trabecular bone is mostly consistent across multiple different models of osteoarthritis induction in the TMJ [11] including chemical [12,13], occlusion [14–19], simulated disc derangement and joint overloading [20], some transgenic [21], and orthodontic manipulations [22]. However, there are some studies with diverging trends in the trabecular response to PTOA progression [23].

Some preclinical animal models have also quantified changes in the size and dimensions of the condyle using univariate measures of condylar height, width, and length [14,17,20]. While these univariate measures of the condyle have documented preclinical and experimental value, they only capture a fraction of the complexity of three-dimensional, multivariate changes in the geometry of the bone. With location, scale, and rotation effects removed, this geometric information represents a mathematical term called shape [24]. Mathematical definitions of shape have been leveraged to significant effect for the clinical diagnosis of TMJ OA from CBCT [25].

The analysis of shape in preclinical TMJ animal models of OA has been less well-studied but is an emerging high context target for the study of mandibular development [26], comparative anatomy [27,28], and TMJ OA pathophysiology [14]. Following TMJ OA induced through unilateral extraction of the maxillary molars, there are changes in both univariate measures of mandibular dimensions such as width and multivariate measures of external geometry such as shape [14]. Thus, remodeling of the subchondral bone during TMJ OA progression affects more than the scale/size, location, and rotation of the mandibular condyle, but the multivariate measure of the geometry of the tissue that occurs concomitant with changes in the subchondral architecture.

There is an important gap in knowledge related to whether shape change in the mandibular condyle is a universal response of the tissue to OA progression, if changes in the organization, composition, and dimensions of the underlying trabecular bone occur independently of this higher-level geometry of the tissue, or if both changes are instead biologically coupled as an osteogenic tissue level response. We hypothesize that variance in the trabecular microstructure of the bone occurs independently of the shape of the condyle during PTOA progression since changes in shape are regulated by anabolic mechanisms and changes in the trabecular bone are regulated by either osteoclast-mediated catabolic mechanisms or the neoangiogenesis at the osteochondral junction during the early stages of PTOA. Here we test this hypothesis using the unilateral partial discectomy murine model of PTOA [29–33]. This PTOA model presents with evident changes in the appearance of the condyle ipsilateral to the discectomy mimicking the human presentation of condylar flattening, osteophytic lipping, and erosions. Changes in mathematical shape and subchondral microarchitecture of the trabecular bone have not been described in the literature for this preclinical model to our knowledge. Beyond characterizing changes in the shape of the condyle and the subchondral microarchitecture, the objective of this work is to determine whether PTOA-mediated changes in condylar shape covary with those in the internal bony microstructure of the trabeculae. We carry out all analyses in male and female mice to determine if both male and female mice have similar bony responses.

Results

PTOA alters the shape of the condyle independent of biological sex

Induction of TMJ PTOA through unilateral partial discectomy changed the appearance of the mandibular condyle, with a flatter and wider appearance (Fig. 1.a–d). Measuring the maximal mediolateral width of the bone condyle illustrated that PTOA results in the dimensions trending wider. However, this difference was not significant in either male or female mice (Fig. 1.e). To determine if the shape of the condyle was different, landmarks and semi-landmarks were super-imposed over three-dimensional reconstructions of the mandibular condyles from control and PTOA mice (Fig. 2; Table 1). A principal component analysis (PCA) of the landmarks after superimposition identified a total of 27 principal components (PCs). The first five PCs individually accounted for at least 5 % of the total variation each, and were chosen for further analysis. Shapiro-Wilk normality test confirmed all PCs analyzed exhibited normal distribution, except for PC4. Welch two-sample t-tests demonstrated that the means are normally distributed across PC1, PC2, PC3 and PC5.

The PC analysis illustrated significant disparity in shape between the NSC and PTOA groups (Fig. 3.a). This disparity is driven through a significant difference in PC1 between the NSC and PTOA groups (Figs. 3.a–b; p < 0.0001; male and females). No statistical differences were seen between NSC and PTOA with other PCs. The PTOA group displayed higher PC1 scores, while the NSC group was characterized by lower PC1 scores (Fig. 3.b). No statistically significant differences were found between the sexes for PC1 (Fig. 3.b). Raw shape (i.e. not scaled for allometry) change along PC1 max illustrated that the PTOA group is wider mediolaterally and flatter superoinferiorly (Fig. 3.c). Similarly, the raw shape change associated with PC1 min illustrated that the NSC group is characterized by more rounded condyles that are taller in the superoinferior direction and thinner in the mediolateral direction (Fig. 3.d). To improve rigor, we repeating the analysis using a Procrustes ANOVA that accounts for multivariate shape change and allows for the analysis of overall shape. This analysis was in agreement, PTOA but not sex was significantly associated with variance in shape (Table 2).

Condyle shape change is allometric but allometry does not explain all of the variance in PTOA-dependent shape change

To determine the effect of size on shape, we tested the sample for allometry by regressing centroid size on our Procrustes-transformed coordinates. Allometric scaling explained 21.5 % of the variance in shape across all groups. When the shape data was adjusted to remove the effects of size/allometry, disparity in shape scaled to remove the effects of allometry was still observed between the NSC and PTOA groups (Fig. 4.a). However, the PTOA mediated difference in PC1 was no longer significant (Fig. 4.b). No statistically significant differences were found between the sexes for PC1 (Fig. 4.b). Similar to the raw shape trends, allometrically scaled shape change along PC1 max illustrated that the PTOA group is wider mediolaterally and flatter superoinferiorly (Fig. 4.c). Allometrically-scaled shape change along PC1 min illustrated that the NSC group is characterized by more rounded condyles that are taller in the superoinferior direction and thinner in the mediolateral direction (Fig. 4.d). Procrustes ANOVA regression illustrates that PTOA is still significantly associated with shape adjusted for allometry (p < 0.001) but sex was not (p = 0.510; Table 3).

The internal bony microstructure of the bone varies in both a sex- and PTOA-dependent manner

Quantification of the subchondral bone using μCT found significant differences associated with both the progression of PTOA and sex. PTOA was associated with substantial increases in bone volume (females), total volume (both), trabecular spacing (male) and a significant decrease in bone volume/total volume (both), trabecular thickness (female), bone mineral density (both), and tissue mineral density (male). Sex differences were found in bone volume/total volume, trabecular spacing, trabecular thickness, and bone mineral density in non-surgical NSC tissues. In PTOA tissues, sex differences were found in trabecular spacing (Fig. 5). These changes are illustrated in heat maps scaled to HA density in the condyles (Fig. 6).

PTOA-dependent changes in shape and internal bony microstructure do not covary

Since PTOA altered both the external shape and the internal bony microstructure of the condyle, we next sought to determine if the internal and external geometric properties of the bone covary. A series of Procrustes ANOVAs were carried out on raw shape with no allometric scaling and illustrated that total volume (females), bone volume/total volume (both), bone mineral density (males), and tissue mineral density (males) all significantly covary (Table 4; p < 0.005 after Bonferroni corrections). When shape is adjusted for allometric scaling, none of the internal bony microstructural measured were significant in either sex (Table 5). To improve experimental rigor, we replicated the analysis using a two block partial least squares (2B-PLS) approach. Raw shape data illustrated a strong, statistically significant correlation between external shape and internal microstructure for both males (r-PLS: 0.962, Z, 2.44, p < 0.01) and females (r-PLS: 0.936, Z, 2.13, p = 0.013). For shape adjusted for allometric scaling, there was no statistically significant correlation between external shape and internal microstructure for either males (r-PLS: 0.678, Z, −1–12, p = 0.871) or females (r-PLS: 0.83, Z, 0.43, p = 0.340).

Discussion

The bone of the mandibular condyle has internal and external geometric properties, with internal geometry characterized by trabecular bone and external geometry characterized by the mathematical construct of shape. Here we mathematically correlate variance in condylar shape with the internal bony microstructure variables from a μCT analysis to determine how both variables change during the progression of PTOA and if they covary. Key findings from this study demonstrate that the progression of TMJ PTOA through unilateral partial discectomy is associated with changes in both the internal microarchitecture and external shape of the mandibular condyle. The PTOA-mediated external shape data illustrate that allometric scaling has a significant effect on shape but when the effects of allometric scaling are removed, the shape differences between the non-surgical control and PTOA samples are still significantly different. Biological sex does explain variance in some of the internal bony microstructure variables during TMJ health and disease but does not explain a significant portion of the change in the shape with and without allometric scaling. There is covariance between shape and internal bony microstructure when the effects of allometric scaling are retained. Removing allometric scaling illustrates that PTOA mediated shape changes independently of internal bony microstructure and that internal bony microstructure covaries with size.

Understanding why shape change occurs in the condyle after injury has yet to be fully resolved. The shape and morphology of a biological structure is dependent on both canalized genetic factors favoring integration with the skull and plastic epigenetic factors favoring adaptation to the local mechanical microenvironment of the joint [27]. In the TMJ, mechanical loading has a large impact on both the anabolic and catabolic response of the cartilage and bone. Masticatory hypofunction impacts bone mineral density in the mandibular condylar process [34] and OA-mediated changes in trabeculae [13], with myotomy or myectomy affecting proliferation rates in the condylar cartilage [35]. Some have hypothesized that this response is due to the embryological origins of the tissue from secondary cartilage [36]. In our preclinical model of PTOA, the articular disc has been excised. Due to the torus-like nature of the articular disc, this geometry would equilibrate forces across the surface of the hemisphere-like geometry of the condyle head. When the articular disc is removed, the hemisphere-like shape of the condyle head is in direct contact with the relatively flat glenoid fossa. This could lead to elevated contact stress due to the smaller contact surface area. We hypothesize that these higher contact stresses would necessarily be associated with high ploughing and/or tractional forces that are contextually linked with the development of osteoarthritis [37]. Thus, equilibration of the stress field on the condylar surface would require shape changes that match the glenoid fossa or alternatively the surface of both could flatten in a coordinated manner as has been reported in some cases [38,39]. In support of this hypothesis, hypofunction secondary to discectomy leads to lower levels of cartilage degeneration in this model [33].

Another important consideration in understanding this PTOA-mediated change in shape is the mechanism of post-natal growth of the mandibular condylar. The mandibular cartilage undergoes ossification through endochondral mechanisms, with growth occurring through the proliferation and differentiation of osteochondral progenitor cells. One of the sources of these progenitor cells is the fibrous periosteum around the peripheral edge of the joint [40,41]. This study focused on early stage PTOA because this timepoint is associated with dysfunctional remodeling of the articular cartilage and the expansion of a neomatrix rich is type VI collagen and other fibrogenic matrix components [42,43]. The recruitment of this cell population may disproportionately manifest itself along the periphery of the condyle near the medial and lateral poles of the condyle. Since the articular disc is excised through a posterior and lateral approach, the destabilization of the joint here would both disrupt the mechanical microenvironment of the tissue, causing an acute inflammatory response, and the release of mitogenic growth factors.

The trabecular response from the unilateral partial discectomy model is largely consistent with other preclinical murine models that induce TMJ OA through joint destabilization [20] and orthodontically induced joint overloading [15,17]. All models trend with a decrease in bone and tissue mineral density, total volume, and bone volume/total volume, representing a loss of subchondral bone. In longitudinal studies, this trend continues, with the highest rate of change occurring in the early stages of the disorder [17]. Osteoclast levels are found to be highest in this early stage of dysfunction, contextually linking osteoclast activation with the loss of subchondral bone [9,15,17,20]. We predict that a longitudinal study of the unilateral partial discectomy model would demonstrate a similar tissue level response, with continued loss of the subchondral bone as PTOA progressed to the end stage disorder at 16-weeks post-operatively [32,42].

Given that the external geometric properties of the bone are scaf-folded by the internal geometry of the trabecular bone, it would be reasonable to hypothesize that changes in the shape would necessitate significant correlative changes in the underlying internal geometry. However, our findings illustrate that phenotypic variance in the trabecular microstructure is associated only with size-related shape change. Thus, the molecular mechanisms regulating shape adjusted for allometric scaling and those regulating size may represent biologically de-coupled tissue-level responses. This could indicate a transition in remodeling favoring a catabolic tissue response. In orthodontically induced joint overloading models of TMJ PTOA, the transition from early to advanced stage dysfunction is associated with a decrease in new bone formation, an increase in biomarkers for bone turnover, and an increase in osteoclast number [17]. Here we illustrate that allometric scaling during early stage TMJ PTOA is associated with an increase in both bone and total volume, and that this accounts for a significant portion of shape change. This tissue response is indicative of anabolic bone growth. We predict based on prior research that the transition to advanced stage TMJ PTOA would be characterized by predominantly catabolic tissue level activities such as those described in Zhang et al. [17]. Future studies will determine the extent to which this transition regulates the covariance of the external and internal geometric properties of the condyle.

There are several important limitations of this study that should be considered, particularly with reference to the implications of this study on human health. The first is that mice are an imperfect model for the study of human-like PTOA presentations due to the lack of an articular eminence and post-glenoid process and a predominant propalinal masticatory cycle. The motivation for the use of mice in this study was that we seek to leverage the preclinical unilateral partial discectomy model to study the molecular mechanisms regulating PTOA pathophysiology. Thus, mice permit robust transgenic approaches that are limited in other preclinical animal model systems. The second limitation of the study is that discectomy procedures are typically used clinically to alleviate the symptoms of TMJ dysfunction. In the mouse, the presentation mirrors that of clinical osteoarthritis. Surgical access of the joint space can cause confounding effects including an acute inflammatory response. Age-matched sham controls illustrate that the impact on cartilage health is not significant up to 16-weeks post-operatively and there is no change in the appearance of the condylar head [32]. However, the response of the bone and cartilage to unilateral partial discectomy is likely a combination of many factors.

Despite these limitations, the conclusions from this study underscore the utility of using shape as a high context variable for the diagnosis of early stage TMJ PTOA. Quantifying the TMJ bone by three-dimensional superimposition of condylar landmarks has demonstrated clinical value for the diagnosis and classification of TMJ dysfunction [44,45]. Bone imaging biomarker discover can be improved through validated correlation with disease stage, gender, disc position, condylar relations with the skull, mineral density, and trabecular microstructure [46–48]. The findings from this study help advance the clinical pipeline.

In sum, we illustrate that the progression of PTOA is associated with both changes in the external shape of the mandibular condyle and the internal bony microstructure. Our findings are significant as they demonstrate that the shape of the condyle is a high context target for subchondral remodeling in TMJ PTOA pathophysiology. The lack of covariance in internal and external measures of condylar bone during PTOA progression when allometric scaling in controlled illustrates that the injury response of the tissue following trauma is complex and multimodal. The anabolic and catabolic processes guiding bone remodeling could be simultaneously directing changes in the external shape and internal geometry through distinct molecular pathways. Thus, dysfunction related to signaling from the subchondral bone could be equally complex and multimodal.

Materials and methods

Preclinical animal model of PTOA

TMJ PTOA was induced in the mouse model (C57 BL6/J mice) by performing unilateral partial discectomy on the right TMJ as previously described [30–32,43]. In short, 16-week-old C57 BL6/J mice are used because they are skeletally mature and correspond to about thirty-eight-year-old skeletal age of human beings. This ensures that measured changes in bone are not spontaneous due to old age and are not influenced by rapid growth. Male and female mice were used, and sex was treated as a biological variable. The mice are anesthetized using ketamine (100 mg/kg, Henry Schein, Dublin, OH, USA) and Xylazine (5 mg/kg, Akorn, Lake Forest, IL, USA). The skin over the TMJ was shaved and sterilized using betadine and 70 % ETOH. The articular disc is excised through a 3–5 mm incision using a posterior approach through the lateral capsule. The wound was irrigated with sterile saline and closed with the help of a 5–0 suture. Tissues were collected 4-weeks after the surgery for analysis. Only the joint ipsilateral to injury was included in the study. This timepoint was selected because it represents early stage TMJ PTOA, characterized by dysfunctional remodeling of the articular cartilage and the formation of a neomatrix rich is type VI collagen and other fibrogenic matrix components [42,43]. Non-surgical control tissue represents condyles scanned from age-matched mice with no surgical intervention. Sham controls were omitted from this study since no significant changes were observed in age-matched control samples [32]. The sample size used in the study was determined with a sample size and power analysis calculation using values from the trabecular bone analysis, using a probability (power) of 0.8 and type I error probability to test of this null hypothesis of 0.05. All vertebrate animal work was approved by the UIC Animal Care Committee under ACC #23–042.

μCT scanning, segmentation, and analysis

TMJ tissue was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight, and then stored in 70 % ethanol. The collected skulls were scanned in microcomputed tomography (μCT) while submerged in 1xPBS. The scanning parameters for the skulls were 90kVP, 88 μA, and 7.4 μm. To contour the region of interest (ROI), the lower threshold was set to 450 while the upper threshold was 10,000 with the unit of density. Bone superior to a plane through the medial and lateral condylar poles was used as the ROI. After contouring the region of interest, the data was analyzed using a trabecular analysis package in the Scanco Medical application. The variables of interest in this study were bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume (BV), total volume (TV), BV/TV ratio, trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and tissue mineral density (TMD). The data was analyzed using Two-Way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey corrections (α = 0.05).

Landmarking

μCT scans were exported as DICOM files, then loaded into 3D Slicer. Mandibular condyles were segmented using 3D Slicer that isolates the condyle from the surrounding glenoid fossa. STL files of the isolated mandibular condyle were generated. These newly generated three-dimensional STL reconstructions were loaded into 3D slicer and landmarks and semi-landmarks were placed. There are several approaches to landmarking on the mandibular condyle [28]. We used a slightly modified version of Chen et al. [14]. A total of 18 landmarks and semi landmarks were used (Fig. 3) and are defined in Table 1. Landmark points were placed at the medial and lateral pole of condyle, as well as the most anteroinferior and posteroinferior surface of the condyle. Seven semi-landmarks were placed in the mesiodistal center of the condyle, from anterior to posterior on the superior surface of the condyle. Three semi-landmarks were placed in the anteroposterior center in the medial-to-lateral direction. Four semi-landmarks were placed around the periphery of the condyle.

Geometric morphometrics

Geometric Morphometrics (GM) allows statistical analysis of the shape of an object based on landmarks that have associated Cartesian coordinates [24]. Procrustes superimposition was performed to account for discrepancies in translation, rotation, and scale between different objects. A principal component analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the dimensionality of data while retaining variation.

Statistical analysis of geometric morphometric analysis

Following principal component analysis (PCA), Shapiro-Wilk normality test was performed on principle components (PCs) to determine if data was normally distributed. Of the PCs selected for further analysis, parametric statistical analysis was performed on data that followed a normal distribution. The data that did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric statistical analyses were performed. For normally distributed data, Welch two sample t-tests were performed to test the difference in means between the NSC group and PTOA group. Welch two sample t-tests were also performed to compare means between the PTOA male and PTOA female group. Conversely, for non-normally distributed data, Wilcoxon rank sum exact test was used to compare means.

Testing and correcting for allometry

To test for the potential effects of size-related shape change (allometry) we regressed centroid size on the Procrustes-transformed shape coordinates. Allometrically-scaled shape is defined by the residuals from this regression [24]. To evaluate the role of PTOA and sex, we carried out a Procrustes ANOVA on raw shape with no allometric scaling and shape adjusted for allometric scaling. PTOA and sex were used as grouping variables. This statistical test determined the effect of these groups on shape with and without correction for allometric scaling.

Testing for covariance of shape and internal microstructure

To examine the relationship between external shape and internal trabecular microstructure, we first ran a series of linear regressions, regressing our internal microstructure variables against the first 5 principal components of shape. We secondarily confirmed our findings using a Procrustes ANOVA, a variable inclusive of the full range of shape data. Procrustes ANOVA was corrected with post-hoc Bonferroni corrections. Finally, to account for our small sample size and the multi-collinearity across our variables, we ran two block partial least squares (2B PLS) analysis, with the Procrustes-transformed for multigroup comparisons. All analyses were run first on the raw shape variables with no allometric scaling and then on allometrically-scaled shape variables. All tests were run on variables either pooled or separated by sex.
