Optimization of biomass production, morphology, architecture, and nutrient removal by Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 cultivated on potato starch processing effluents as a fungal cell factory
Authors: Miguel Anchundia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe valorization of agro-industrial effluents through filamentous fungi represents a strategic pillar of the circular bioeconomy. Liquid residues generated during potato starch processing contain substantial amounts of carbon and nitrogen, making them suitable substrates for fungal biomass production. Despite this potential, few studies have integrated statistical optimization, morphological characterization, and nutrient assimilation efficiency in a single bioprocess using defined Rhizopus oryzae strains. This study evaluated and optimized biomass production, growth morphology, and nutrient removal efficiency of Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 cultivated in potato starch processing effluents under submerged fermentation.ResultsBiomass production reached 15.70 g/L after optimization using response surface methodology at 35 °C, pH 4, and 150 rpm, representing approximately a 15% increase compared to the initial conditions. The quadratic model showed strong predictive performance (adjusted R² = 89.67%), adequate precision (18.19), and a non-significant lack of fit (p = 0.089). Nutrient removal efficiencies were 85.85% for total sugars, 80.49% for total starch, 74.29% for total nitrogen, and 70.90% for reducing sugars. Morphological analysis revealed predominant pellet formation (85%) with a higher surface-to-volume ratio (1.58 ± 0.69) than clumps, suggesting improved mass transfer conditions.ConclusionsRhizopus oryzae M10A1 efficiently converted starch-rich effluents into fungal biomass under statistically optimized conditions while substantially reducing nutrient load. The integration of statistical modeling and morphological control with physical and chemical parameters highlights its potential as a fungal cell factory within sustainable waste valorization and circular bioeconomy approaches.

Background

Filamentous fungi of the genus Rhizopus have long been used for the production of enzymes, organic acids, and secondary metabolites. Rhizopus oryzae, in particular, has been widely used for the biosynthesis of amylases, proteases, cellulases, xylanases, fumaric acid, lactic acid, and l-carnitine [1–5]. In many of these processes, fungal biomass is generated as a byproduct and frequently discarded, despite its intrinsic biotechnological value.

Over the past decades, interest in filamentous fungi has expanded considerably due to their metabolic versatility and capacity to convert complex substrates into high-value products. More recently, fungal biomass itself has emerged as a valuable biotechnological resource under the concept of fungal cell factories [6, 7]. Within this approach, biomass is no longer viewed as a residual fermentation output but rather as a functional material that can be integrated into the food industry, biotechnology, and materials value chains [8].

Filamentous fungal biomass is characterized by a heterogeneous and nutritionally rich composition, including structural polysaccharides such as chitin, chitosan, and β-glucans, along with high-biological-value proteins, lipids, minerals, and dietary fiber [9–11]. Rhizopus oryzae has been reported to contain chitin ranging from 1 to 2% of dry cell weight, with values exceeding 40–45%. Chitosan content may range from 1.7 to 14.3%, while β-glucans have been reported at 21–65% under certain cultivation conditions [12, 13]. The concentration of these structural components is strongly influenced by cultivation parameters, physiological state, and metabolic regulation during fermentation.

From a food engineering and green technology perspective, fungal biomass may serve as a single-cell protein [14], dietary fiber, a structuring agent, a biopolymer precursor, and a matrix for bio-based packaging or edible coatings. Nevertheless, in many industrial processes, biomass remains underutilized, resulting in significant value loss within sustainability and circular bioeconomy paradigms [14].

In parallel, potato processing industries, particularly starch extraction, generate large volumes of liquid effluents rich in macro and micronutrients, such as magnesium, potassium, sodium, calcium, sulfur, and phosphorus, as well as vitamins such as C, B1, B2, B6, and E. Reported macronutrient concentrations include protein levels between 0.93 and 1.57%, sugars between 0.5 and 0.8%, and total fat near 0.2% [15].

Due to their high biodegradable organic content, these effluents may generate elevated chemical and biochemical oxygen demand (COD and BOD) when discharged into water bodies or soils [16]. Improper disposal, therefore, poses environmental risks to aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems. However, their nutrient-rich composition makes them suitable culture media for filamentous fungi capable of assimilating soluble carbohydrates, nitrogen compounds, and other organic constituents.

From a biotechnological standpoint, such residues are low-cost substrates that simultaneously enable environmental mitigation and nutrient recovery through fungal biomass generation, aligning with waste valorization and circular economy strategies [18]. Filamentous fungi are particularly well suited for this application because of their ability to hydrolyze complex polymers such as starch, tolerate compositional variability, and grow efficiently in media derived from agro-industrial wastes [17].

Within this group, Rhizopus oryzae is widely recognized as a Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) microorganism, owing to its long-standing use in traditional fermentations such as tempeh and in the production of miso, shoyu, tane-koji, fermented soy products, seasonings, vinegar, and sake [18, 19]. Its robust growth on complex substrates and ability to produce metabolites with technological relevance make it a strong candidate for integrated biomass production and effluent treatment processes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that cultivating filamentous fungi in potato starch processing effluents enables not only metabolite production but also simultaneous removal of organic load via metabolic assimilation of carbohydrates, nitrogen, and soluble compounds [20–25]. This integrated approach offers environmental and economic advantages by reducing conventional wastewater treatment costs while generating fungal biomass as a value-added bioproduct.

A critical factor influencing submerged fungal fermentations is growth morphology. The formation of pellets, clumps, or dispersed mycelium directly affects mass transfer, nutrient availability, and overall process efficiency [26–29]. Pellet size, density, and internal structure can influence both biomass productivity and the removal of medium components, making morphological characterization essential for understanding and optimizing fungal bioprocesses [30–33].

In the context of effluent valorization, this study aimed to optimize biomass production and to evaluate growth morphology and nutrient utilization by Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 cultivated in potato starch-processing effluent under submerged fermentation conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Liquid effluents generated during potato starch processing were obtained from the Pilot Starch Processing Plant at Universidad Politécnica Estatal del Carchi (UPEC), Tulcán, Ecuador. The effluents were collected prior to the experimental phase and used as the basal substrate for submerged fermentation assays without prior dilution unless otherwise specified.

All reagents used during the experimental procedures were of analytical grade. Sucrose, glucose, molasses, and starch were purchased from the local market.

The fungal strain Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 was originally isolated from cereal grain samples and identified at the Molecular Microbiology Laboratory. The strain was preserved for subsequent experimental use. ITS sequences were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under accession numbers OR775420 and OR775425 and can be found at the following links: (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/OR775420; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/OR775425).

Inoculum preparation

The inoculum was prepared following previously described methodologies [34, 35]. Spores were harvested from potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates incubated at 27 °C for 5–7 days. Six milliliters of sterile distilled water were added to the culture surface, and spores were gently dislodged using a sterile inoculation loop to obtain a homogeneous suspension. The final spore concentration used for fermentation inoculation was 5 × 10⁶ spores/mL.

Optimization by response surface (RSM) for biomass production

Submerged fermentations were conducted in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, covered with cotton wool, containing 50 mL of liquid residue from starch processing, without dilution or supplementation. The undiluted effluent served as the basal medium, while supplemented treatments were designed to evaluate the effect of nutrient content on biomass production. Flasks were cotton-plugged and sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min. After cooling, each flask was inoculated with 1 mL of the prepared spore suspension [36].

Initial optimization was performed using the one-variable-at-a-time (OVAT) approach [37] to identify factors that significantly affect biomass production and to define their effective experimental ranges. The independent variables evaluated included cultivation time, temperature, initial pH, carbon source and concentration, and nitrogen source.

Temperature levels tested were 25, 30, 35, and 40 °C. Initial pH values (2–7) were adjusted with HCl (0.5 N) or NaOH (0.5 N). Carbon sources evaluated were glucose, sucrose, molasses, and starch, with concentrations of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1, 2, and 3 g/100 mL. Nitrogen sources included ammonium chloride, ammonium sulfate, yeast extract, and urea, tested at concentrations of 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1.2, and 2 g/100 mL.

After identifying significant variables through OVAT screening, biomass production was optimized using response surface methodology (RSM) with a central composite design.

Glucose was selected as the carbon source and ammonium sulfate as the nitrogen source for model development. A quadratic regression model was fitted to the experimental data using Design-Expert software (trial version, Stat-Ease Inc., USA). Model adequacy was evaluated based on analysis of variance (ANOVA), adjusted coefficient of determination (R²adj), adequate precision, F-test, and lack-of-fit statistics.

Chemical composition of the culture medium

Total solids, suspended solids, and soluble solids were determined gravimetrically.

Total solids were measured by drying 10 mL of the homogenized sample at 105 °C to constant weight. Suspended solids were obtained by centrifuging 50 mL of homogenized sample at 3000×g for 10 min, followed by drying the pellet at 105 °C to constant weight. Soluble solids were calculated as the difference between total and suspended solids. Ash content was determined by incinerating total solids at 575 °C for 5 h [35].

Total nitrogen was quantified using the Kjeldahl method [38]. Ten milliliters of culture medium were digested with 15 mL H₂SO₄ and two Kjeldahl catalyst tablets at 420 °C for 1 h. Distillation was carried out using 25 mL of 4% (w/v) boric acid with the Shiro -Tashiro mixed indicator, and titration was performed with HCl (0.1 N).

The total starch content was determined using the iodine method [39]. The samples were treated with 52% perchloric acid, and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm. A standard curve was prepared using pure starch. Total sugars were determined using the phenol-sulfuric acid method, and reducing sugars using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [20, 40]. Absorbances were measured at 490 nm and 540 nm, and concentrations were calculated from glucose standard curves.

Biomass determination

Biomass concentration was determined as described by Osadolor et al. [41]. Mycelial biomass was harvested, washed repeatedly with deionized water to remove residual medium components, and dried at 60 °C until a constant weight was reached. For kinetic analyses, biomass was collected every 2 days.

Morphological characterization of biomass

Morphological analysis was conducted at the end of fermentation using a stereomicroscope (Stemi 305, ZEISS, Germany) equipped with an AxioCam camera (MRc5, ZEISS, Germany). Five milliliters of fermentation broth containing pellets were transferred to Petri dishes containing 40 mL of saline solution (0.9%). Images were captured under top illumination on a dark background. For each sample, 100 pellets were analyzed [42]. Biomass morphology was classified as dispersed mycelium, pellets, and clumps. Pellets were further categorized as soft, densely compact, compact with protruding hyphae, loosely compact, core-shell, or filamentous structures [43].

Surface area, equivalent volume, surface-to-volume ratio (A/V), and solidity were calculated according to Wucherpfennig et al. [30]. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ (open-source software). Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, the gray-level threshold was then automatically calculated using the Otsu algorithm, and the images were analyzed using the particle analysis function.

Microscopic structures (hyphae, spores, reproductive structures) were evaluated using a trinocular optical microscope (Primostar 3, ZEISS, Germany) at 400× magnification, coupled with an Axiocam color camera (208 color, ZEISS, Germany) [32].

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare biomass production under different cultivation conditions, culture medium components before and after fermentation, and morphological parameters. Mean differences were evaluated using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 95% confidence level [44]. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using Statgraphics version XVI. Graphical representations were generated using RStudio version 2025.05.1 + 513.

Results

Optimization for biomass production

Effect of cultivation time and temperature on biomass production

Biomass production by Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 was significantly influenced by temperature and incubation time (Fig. 1A). The maximum biomass concentration (6.29 g/L) was achieved at 2 days of cultivation at 35 °C, showing statistically significant differences compared with the other temperatures evaluated (p ≤ 0.05). Beyond this point, biomass progressively declined at all tested temperatures. The lowest concentration (1.26 g/L) was recorded at 14 days at 40 °C. These findings indicate that short incubation periods at moderate temperatures favor rapid biomass accumulation under submerged conditions.

Effect of pH on biomass production

Biomass production exhibited a clear pH-dependent pattern (Fig. 1B), with distinct behavior, showing optimal biomass production at acidic pH values. Complete growth inhibition was observed at pH 2, where no biomass was detected. Maximum biomass production (6.96 g/L) occurred at initial pH 4. Above this value, biomass concentration gradually decreased, although values remained above 5 g/L across the pH range tested. Statistically significant differences were observed among pH treatments (p ≤ 0.05). The results indicate that Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 performs optimally under moderately acidic conditions.

Effect of carbon source and carbohydrate concentration

The carbon source influenced biomass production in Rhizopus oryzae M10A1, revealing a metabolic preference for monosaccharides such as glucose (Fig. 2A). This carbon source supported the highest biomass concentration (9.99 g/L), significantly exceeding that of the control and other carbon sources tested (p ≤ 0.05). Sucrose resulted in the lowest biomass production (8.88 g/L).

When glucose concentration was evaluated (Fig. 2B), maximum biomass production (9.69 g/L) was achieved at 2 g/100 mL. Increasing glucose concentration beyond this level led to reduced biomass production, suggesting substrate-associated inhibition. Significant differences were detected among glucose concentrations and relative to the control (p ≤ 0.05).

Effect of nitrogen source and ammonium sulfate concentration

Nitrogen source significantly affected biomass production (Fig. 3A). Ammonium sulfate yielded the highest biomass concentration (8.35 g/L), whereas urea resulted in the lowest (3.47 g/L). Differences in biomass production among nitrogen sources were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05). Regarding ammonium sulfate concentration (Fig. 3B), maximum biomass production (8.31 g/L) was obtained at 1 g/100 mL. At higher concentrations, biomass production declined but remained above 4 g/L (4.47–6.69 g/L). Differences among treatments were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05).

Morphological characterization of biomass

Morphological analysis revealed that Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 predominantly formed pellets (85%) and clumps (15%) under optimized submerged fermentation conditions (Table 4; Fig. 6). Pellets and clumps were classified as filamentous and slightly compact. Pellet diameters ranged from 1.10 to 5.50 mm, while clumps ranged from 5.60 to 15.10 mm, indicating greater structural heterogeneity in clumps (Fig. 6D and F).

Pellets exhibited solidity values between 0.85 and 0.95, closer to 1 than those of clumps (0.54–0.62), indicating more compact structures. Although clumps displayed larger surface areas due to their size (98.52–716.31 mm²), pellets showed significantly higher surface-to-volume ratios (0.91–5.45), with a mean A/V of 1.58, compared to 0.61 in clumps. Equivalent volume values were also higher for clumps (91.95–1802.72 mm³), whereas pellets exhibited lower, more uniform values.

Discussion

The findings demonstrate that Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 functioned effectively as a fungal cell factory, converting nutrient-rich potato starch processing effluents into fungal biomass while simultaneously reducing the organic load. This performance reflects coordinated physiological, metabolic, and morphological traits that collectively determine process efficiency under submerged fermentation [6, 17].

Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 demonstrated a strong and favorable response in biomass production to variations in key nutritional factors, particularly ammonium sulfate and glucose as nitrogen and carbon sources, respectively. A substantial increase in biomass concentration following optimization indicates the strain’s efficient substrate utilization and metabolic responsiveness under controlled fermentation conditions. This pattern aligns with previous reports describing Trichoderma harzianum and Mucor hiemalis as highly competitive organisms when fermentation systems are precisely optimized for carbon and nitrogen concentrations and operational parameters, underscoring the critical role of nutritional balance in enhancing fungal growth performance [45].

Effect of cultivation time and temperature on biomass production

Biomass production reached its maximum at 2 days at 35 °C, indicating rapid metabolic activation and efficient substrate assimilation. Compared to Huang et al. [20], who reported 0.99 g/L at 30 °C after 2 days, the present biomass levels were higher, likely reflecting differences in starch concentration and nutrient composition of the effluent. Elevated starch concentrations may induce enzymatic limitations or substrate inhibition, thereby affecting nutrient uptake efficiency [46].

The short optimal cultivation time is particularly relevant from a bioprocess perspective. Faster biomass accumulation reduces operational costs and increases reactor turnover compared with other filamentous fungi, which require up to 14 days for maximum production [11, 37]. This rapid growth capacity enhances the industrial attractiveness of R. oryzae M10A1 for biomass-oriented fermentations. Moreover, this mold is recognized for its lack of mycotoxin production and its suitability for food-related fermentations [19], reinforcing its applicability in sustainable biotechnological systems.

Effect of pH on biomass production

Biomass production was strongly pH-dependent, with complete inhibition at pH 2 and maximum production at pH 4. This behavior confirms that Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 requires moderately acidic conditions to sustain enzymatic and metabolic activity.

The optimal pH range aligns with previous reports (pH 4–7) describing efficient starch utilization and lactic acid production in similar systems [20]. pH values different from the optimum may compromise membrane transport, enzyme stability, and intracellular metabolic regulation. This parameter is species-specific, and controlled pH conditions are known to enhance biomass production by ensuring optimal enzyme activity and the activation of nutrient transport systems. Variations in pH can alter protein and enzyme functionality, ultimately leading to reduced biomass concentration [47, 48].

Reported biomass values for Rhizopus oryzae grown on starch-processing effluents vary widely, from 0.62 g/L [22] to 25.20 g/L [37]. Such variation likely reflects differences in substrate composition and nutrient concentration [48].

Effect of carbon source and carbohydrate concentration

Glucose supported the highest biomass production, while starch also enabled substantial growth. This observation is metabolically consistent with the ability of Rhizopus oryzae to encode diverse CAZymes, including amylases, xylanases, cellulases, and pectinases [18], enabling efficient degradation of agro-industrial residues.

The preference for glucose can be explained by its direct entry into glycolysis, thereby reducing the energetic investment required for extracellular hydrolysis. However, increasing glucose concentration beyond optimal levels reduced biomass production, suggesting catabolite repression mechanisms and downregulation of carbohydrate-active enzymes [37, 49].

The biomass values obtained are within the range reported by Asadollahzadeh et al. [22], who observed 1.23–7.33 g/L at glucose concentrations between 1.43 and 3.96 g/L. This confirms that carbohydrate availability is a primary driver of biomass synthesis, influencing cytoskeletal development, metabolite formation, and structural growth.

From a bioprocess standpoint, glucose represents an economically viable supplement for effluent-based media. Its integration into starch-rich wastewater systems may provide competitive advantages over fungi that require more complex carbon sources [37, 50–52].

Effect of nitrogen source and ammonium sulfate concentration

Ammonium sulfate at 1% (w/v) resulted in the highest biomass production. Nitrogen concentration plays a central role in amino acid synthesis, nucleic acid formation, and cell wall assembly, directly influencing biomass concentration and composition. Therefore, nitrogen could be a critical nutrient that influences both biomass production and composition, and its effects are modulated by several factors, including the type of microorganism, its metabolic characteristics, the chemical nature of the nitrogen source, and its concentration.

While yeast extract has been reported as an efficient nitrogen source (4.30–7.14 g/L biomass at 0.5–0.7%) [37, 49], ammonium sulfate supplementation has also been shown to enhance biomass production in Rhizopus oryzae at concentrations around 1.2%, with inhibitory effects at higher levels [18].

These findings indicate that nitrogen concentrations are critical. Under nitrogen-limited conditions, fungi may redirect metabolic flux toward carbohydrate utilization and autolytic recycling mechanisms [45]. Conversely, excess ammonium may disturb intracellular pH homeostasis and nitrogen metabolism.

Optimization by response surface (RSM) for biomass production

Biomass production is influenced by multiple interacting variables, including agitation, pH, temperature, medium composition, and nutrient balance, among others [20, 53]. The response surface methodology integrated these variables to achieve approximately a 15% increase in biomass.

From a statistical perspective, the p-value and F-test results indicate that the quadratic model for predicting biomass production is statistically significant. Furthermore, the adjusted R² value indicates strong agreement between the experimental data and model predictions, indicating a high degree of correlation and confirming the model’s reliability in describing the observed response [44].

The results indicate that 89.67% of the variation in biomass concentration produced by Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 is explained by the second-order predictive equation. The precision index exceeded 10, demonstrating that the model possesses an adequate signal-to-noise ratio and can be reliably used to explore and navigate the proposed experimental domain. Moreover, the lack-of-fit p-value was greater than 0.05 and therefore not statistically significant, confirming that the quadratic model adequately represents the experimental data [54].

The optimized combination of glucose and ammonium sulfate (coded levels 1.41 and 1.41) yielded a predicted biomass concentration of 15.77 g/L, experimentally validated at 15.70 g/L. The close agreement confirms model robustness and reliability within the experimental space.

NTherefore, the statistical parameters obtained indicate that the model is robust, stable, and accurate, with strong predictive capability across the experimental domain, thereby confirming the appropriateness of the selected quadratic model.

Chemical composition of the culture medium

Reductions in total sugars (85.85%), starch (80.49%), and total nitrogen (74.29%) differed from values reported by Ibarruri and Hernández [18], Freitas et al. [55], and Souza et al. [35]. The strong carbohydrate assimilation observed here is consistent with prior carbon-source screening, confirming glucose and starch as primary metabolic drivers. Nitrogen levels did not appear limiting under optimized conditions, supporting efficient biomass synthesis.

Importantly, the fermentation process did not merely accumulate biomass; it transformed the chemical composition of the effluent. This metabolic conversion aligns with fungal-based biological treatment strategies that combine pollutant removal with resource recovery [22, 23, 56].

These findings may have significant practical implications. Liquid waste generated from potato starch processing could support the production of substantial amounts of biomass, a value-added product that can be reintegrated into the production chain to recover proteins, α-glucans, β-glucans, chitin, chitosan, and dietary fiber—key biomolecules widely used as raw materials in the food industry. These components may be further investigated in future studies.

Simultaneously, nutrients in liquid waste were significantly reduced, providing an environmentally sustainable alternative for waste valorization and minimizing potential ecological impacts associated with disposal into water bodies or soil. Furthermore, due to its high metabolic efficiency, Rhizopus oryzae M10A1 showed strong potential for application in treating other agro-industrial liquid effluents, thereby contributing to nutrient removal and environmental mitigation.

Morphological characterization of biomass

The optimized conditions predominantly promoted pellet formation (85%), with only a minor proportion of clump structures observed. In submerged fungal cultures, pellet morphology is widely associated with enhanced metabolite productivity [57].

Pellet diameters (1.10–5.50 mm) were smaller than those reported for Rhizopus stolonifer [34] but larger than some Rhizopus oryzae systems [56]. Differences may stem from substrate composition, hydrodynamics, and the presence of synthetic morphology-inducing agents such as triethanolamine.

Pellets exhibited higher solidity values and greater surface-to-volume ratios than clumps, indicating improved mass transfer characteristics [30]. Although dissolved oxygen was not measured, smaller pellets typically exhibit lower internal diffusion gradients, lower viscosity in the medium, and enhanced metabolic homogeneity [58]. In contrast, larger clumps or oversized pellets tend to develop hypoxic cores, resulting in reduced effective growth and decreased metabolite production.

The coexistence of pellets and clumps may result from agitation speed, fermentation volume, electrostatic interactions, and spore surface properties [26, 59]. In this system, the mixed morphology may have contributed to approximately a 15% increase in biomass production.

From a bioprocess engineering perspective, morphological control is critical, as it directly influences productivity and significantly affects mixing efficiency and mass transfer within the system [30]. Fungal morphology is governed by intrinsic genetic characteristics of the species, as well as by extrinsic factors such as the composition and viscosity of the culture medium and operational conditions, including pH, temperature, and mechanical agitation intensity [60].

In studies on metabolite production by Rhizopus oryzae, several conditions have been reported to favor biomass formation in pellet form, including the presence of glucose and ammonium salts as key nutritional factors [58], a pH range of 3–4 for Neurospora intermedia and Rhizopus oryzae [31, 60], an agitation speed of 200 rpm, and a cultivation temperature of 30 °C [61].

Hyphal extension was greater in pellets, potentially facilitating enhanced carbon flux and structural development. The presence of chlamydospores at the end of fermentation suggests nutrient depletion and metabolic transition toward survival structures [61–63].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that starch-rich effluents can be effectively valorized through an integrated strategy that combines statistical optimization, morphological control, and nutrient recovery. Within this framework, effluents are reconceptualized as resource streams rather than waste, serving as substrates to produce fungal biomass with promising applications in food science, biotechnology, and biomaterial development.

This integrated approach aligns with circular bioeconomy principles by converting nutrient-rich residues into value-added biomass while simultaneously mitigating the environmental impact associated with effluent discharge.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that Rhizopus oryzae M10A1, as a fungal cell factory, can produce fungal biomass from potato starch processing effluents under submerged fermentation conditions. The strain showed substantial assimilation of soluble nutrients, including total sugars, starch, and nitrogen, with reductions ranging from 40.63% to 85.85%, indicating efficient metabolic activity on the substrate.

Statistical optimization using response surface methodology resulted in an approximately 15% increase in biomass production, reaching 15.70 g/L under optimal conditions (35 °C, pH 4, 150 rpm, with glucose and ammonium sulfate supplementation). The quadratic model exhibited good predictive performance (R²adj = 89.67%) and a non-significant lack of fit, supporting the applicability of design-based optimization to improve biomass concentration.

Morphological analysis revealed a predominance of pellet structures (approximately 85%), which were associated with higher surface-to-volume ratios compared to clumps. This structural organization suggests improved mass-transfer conditions that may favor nutrient uptake and biomass accumulation in the evaluated cultivation system.

From a circular bioeconomy perspective, integrating nutrient-rich effluents into culture media and applying statistical optimization strategies highlights the utility of this system as a biomass-oriented fungal cultivation approach within waste valorization frameworks.

The study was conducted at the laboratory scale under shaken-flask conditions; therefore, hydrodynamic behavior, shear forces, and oxygen transfer characteristics may vary with bioreactor type. Oxygen transfer and mass transport were inferred from morphological parameters but were not directly quantified. Additionally, although significant nutrient reduction was observed, global organic load indicators such as COD and BOD were not measured, limiting the assessment of overall environmental performance. Furthermore, the biochemical and functional composition of the produced biomass was not characterized, and therefore, its industrial applicability remains to be validated. Therefore, these aspects should be further investigated in future studies.

Despite these limitations, the observed nutrient consumption patterns, particularly the reductions of 85.85% in total sugars, 80.49% in starch, and 74.29% in nitrogen, provide a relevant baseline for future scale-up studies. These results reflect consistent substrate assimilation under controlled conditions and offer valuable insights into nutrient utilization dynamics in effluent-based cultivation systems. Such information may support the design of feeding strategies and process control during scale-up, where substrate availability and metabolic demand become critical factors for biomass production.
