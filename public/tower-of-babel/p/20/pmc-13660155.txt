Effectiveness of stepped versus usual care for mental health service delivery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Raphael Ohms, Jerneja Sveticic, Megan H. Ross, Nicole Hartley, Peter Worthy, Trevor Russell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStepped models of care provide a sequential approach in the treatment of mental health conditions, with the level of care prescribed varying in intensity according to the severity of mental health concern for the individual. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluate the effectiveness of stepped care compared to usual care for mental health-related clinical outcomes, quality of life, cost-effectiveness and satisfaction across mental health conditions.MethodsA systematic literature search across eight databases (February 2024) identified randomised controlled trials of stepped care models for diagnosed mental health conditions. Standardised mean differences (SMDs), risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random effects models. Effect sizes were interpreted using Cohen’s d (d < 0.5; small, 0.5 < d < 1.2; medium and d > 1.2; large).ResultsForty-one studies were eligible and 31 included in the meta-analyses. Stepped care showed large and statistically significant effects from baseline to post-treatment that was greater when compared to usual care for mental health-related quality of life and PTSD symptoms (SMD [95% CI] 3.13 [2.08 to 4.18] and 1.39 [0.61 to 2.17], respectively). Further, compared to usual care, stepped care had an increased likelihood of depression remission (RR [95% CI] 1.64 [1.24 to 2.16]) and response (RR [95% CI] 1.78 [1.35 to 2.35]). For general health-related quality of life, stepped care showed a statistically significant, medium effect from baseline to post-treatment compared to usual care (SMD [95% CI] 0.62 [0.19 to 1.06]). From baseline to post-treatment, stepped care showed medium, but non-significant improvements for depression and anxiety (SMD [95% CI] 0.73 [-0.26 to 1.72] and 0.92 [-0.20 to 2.03], respectively) when compared to usual care. There were no significant differences in direct costs (MD [95% CI] 195.16 [-137.96 to 528.28], however, indirect costs were significantly lower with stepped care (MD [95% CI] -962.19 [-1412.55 to -511.83]).ConclusionsStepped care demonstrates better outcomes than usual care and is more cost-effective for indirect costs. Future research should standardise and compare outcomes, populations, and conditions to ensure meaningful results and reduce heterogeneity. More research is needed to develop personalised stepped care models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13033-026-00717-w.

Background

Mental health conditions are among the most prevalent causes of disability in the world with more than one in ten people affected [1]. The global prevalence of mental health disorders has increased over the last 30 years from 654.8 million (1990) to 970.1 million (2019) [1]. The rising prevalence of mental health conditions places significant strain on healthcare systems, increasing demand for treatments and support. At the individual level, these conditions contribute to substantial disability, while at the population level, they account for a significant burden, ranking 7th globally in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among all diseases [1]. Economically, mental health conditions generate considerable direct healthcare costs, and indirect costs due to productivity losses, with these impacts expected to grow alongside rising prevalence [2]. To deliver more resource-efficient treatments for mental health conditions, stepped care approaches were developed in the 1990s. In these approaches, mental health interventions are grouped into ‘levels’ of care, from low-intensity early intervention to high-intensity care, and matched to the individual’s preferences, needs, and the severity of the condition [3]. The intention is to refer into the appropriate level of care, offer the appropriate treatment(s) and step up or down in the level of care, according to the response to treatment [4]. Models of stepped care can vary substantially in design due to their complex nature, with at times, several individual interventions combined. Each model is also likely to differ in terms of the triage and referring system, the number of steps, the treatment intensity within each step, and the rules for stepping up or down a level of care [5, 6]. This heterogeneity of stepped care models means the evaluation of treatment benefits and interpretation of findings from systematic reviews of treatment effects is complex [5, 7–11]. Although stepped care models vary across settings, their shared organising principles justify pooled analyses as indicative system-level effects rather than uniform intervention effects.

For depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), systematic reviews have attempted to evaluate the impact of stepped care. These reviews indicate that stepped care may be clinically effective compared to usual care for depression, anxiety and depression-related quality of life [7, 9, 10]. Further, the cost-effectiveness of stepped care treatments has been explored for PTSD, with narrative syntheses suggesting that stepped care treatments may be twice as costly as usual care [8]. However, the current body of evidence is limited by exploring only depression, anxiety and PTSD, with the effectiveness of stepped care for other mental health conditions unknown.

In addition to the examination of the broad spectrum of mental health conditions, previous studies are limited in the scope of outcome variables assessed. One salient variable that appears to have received limited attention is patient satisfaction in relation to mental health treatment. Patient satisfaction is a key metric for evaluating care models, reflecting service quality and its effect on clinical outcomes [12, 13]. It encompasses perceptions of accessibility, communication, empathy and the overall experience of the healthcare system. As stepped care models explicitly match treatment intensity to individual need and response through structured monitoring and step-up processes, they are expected to directly shape patient experience of care, making satisfaction a theoretically relevant outcome rather than a secondary or incidental measure. Higher satisfaction levels are associated with greater trust in healthcare providers, encouraging adherence to treatment plans, regular attendance at appointments, and active involvement in care decisions [14]. Such behaviours are vital for achieving better health outcomes and reducing complications or hospital readmissions [13]. Conversely, dissatisfaction stemming from issues such as long waiting times or poor communication can lead to disengagement, reduced adherence and ultimately poorer outcomes [15]. It can also place additional strain on healthcare resources due to preventable complications. Therefore, patient satisfaction should not be viewed in isolation; it is closely tied to the effectiveness and sustainability of care delivery. Recognising this is essential when evaluating healthcare models [16].

Models that promote accessibility and personalisation are more likely to foster trust and participation which is essential for sustained recovery. One such model that aims to deliver accessible and scalable care is stepped care. Stepped care models for mental health has been implemented in countries such as the United States (US), the United Kingdom (UK), the Netherlands and Australia for a broad range of mental health conditions [5]. However, substantial resources are required to develop and implement these models, highlighting the need for a robust evidence base to justify resource allocation [5]. While existing reviews suggest potential clinical and quality of life benefits for certain conditions, evidence remains limited, particularly for cost-effectiveness and patient satisfaction.

Stepped care and usual care are service-led models rather than clinical interventions, and their implementation varies across health systems, settings and conditions. This systematic review does not assume uniform definitions across studies but examines whether systems organised around stepped care principles show consistent directional differences compared with usual care. Pooled estimates reflect indicative system-level trends rather than precise intervention effects across settings.

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of stepped care compared to usual care for mental health outcomes in patients with diagnosed mental health conditions. The secondary objective is to evaluate the cost effectiveness of and satisfaction with stepped care models compared to usual care.

Methods

Search strategy and selection process

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (12) and registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews: CRD42024491432. Eight databases (PubMed, PubMed Central, Embase, PsycINFO, CINHAL, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane) were searched from inception to February 2024 for relevant articles that examined the effect of stepped care compared to usual care for patients with diagnosed mental health conditions using the search terms and keywords presented in Supplementary Table 1. Titles and abstracts of potentially relevant articles were uploaded to Covidence (www.covidence.org), and duplicates removed. Two authors (RO and JS) independently screened against predetermined eligibility criteria and any disagreements resolved by discussion with a third author (TR). Disagreements for title and abstract screening and full text screening were interpreted using Cohen’s Kappa, with d < 0.6 considered moderate, 0.61 < d < 0.8 substantial, 0.81 < d < 0.99 near perfect and d = 1 perfect agreement [17].

Eligibility criteria

We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that compared stepped care with usual care for people of all ages with a diagnosed mental health condition (i.e., using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [18] or International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) [19] and evaluated clinical, quality of life, cost-effectiveness or satisfaction outcomes. Although minor operational differences exist between manuals, diagnostic categories were considered conceptually comparable at the mental health condition level for inclusion. Non-sequential treatment models, prevention studies, waiting lists treatments, pharmacology treatments, complementary treatments, non-randomised controlled trials and studies reported in a language other than English were excluded.

Data extraction

Data were extracted by two independent authors (RO and JS) using a pre-determined spreadsheet that was piloted prior to formal data extraction. The data extraction spreadsheet included the authors’ names, year of the publication, sample size, study design, study setting, population, interventions, outcome measures and results (at baseline, post-treatment and follow-up). If any of the selected studies did not report the data needed to calculate effect sizes, we attempted to contact the authors to obtain the required information [20]. Intervention descriptions were reviewed to assess whether classifications of stepped care and usual care reflected conceptually consistent system‑level approaches across studies.

GRADE and summary of findings table

The two authors (RO and JS) independently conducted risk of bias assessment using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2 (RoB2) [21]. Agreement for risk of bias were interpreted using Cohen’s Kappa, with d < 0.6 considered moderate, 0.61 < d < 0.8 substantial, 0.81 < d < 0.99 near perfect and d = 1 perfect agreement [22]. The GRADEpro GDT software (https://www.gradepro.org/) was used to classify the overall quality of the included studies in the meta-analyses as high, moderate, low or very low. Initially, all studies were graded as high quality and later downgraded based on the assessment [23, 24].

Data synthesis and analysis

Outcomes were synthesised as either continuous or dichotomous measures, depending on how treatment effects were reported in the original studies and analysed separately as complementary indicators of treatment effects. Meta-analyses were conducted in RevMan Web (https://www.revman.cochrane.org/) when at least three studies reported outcome data that could be included. For continuous outcomes, standardised mean differences (SMDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mean changes were calculated using a random effects model to compare treatment effects from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up between stepped care and usual care models. Mean change between timepoints was obtained by subtracting the baseline mean from the post-treatment mean and the baseline mean from the follow-up mean. Standard deviation (SD) of the change was obtained using the formula in Supplementary Fig. 1. Effect sizes were interpreted using Cohen’s d, with d < 0.5 considered small, 0.5 < d < 1.2 medium and d > 1.2 large (21). Negative values for scale instruments were standardised by multiplication with -1 to ensure that favourable outcomes (improvements) were represented by positive numbers. For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RR) and 95% CIs were calculated using a random effects model (inverse variance) to compare post-treatment and follow-up effects between stepped care and usual care models. Relative risks > 1 indicate increased risk, and RRs < 1 indicate decreased risk. An increased risk indicates a higher probability of an event occurring in the stepped care group, An increased risk indicates a higher probability of an event occurring in the stepped care group, whereas a decreased risk indicates a lower probability of an event occurring [25]. Data not able to be included in the meta-analysis were reported narratively. Cost data were converted to a common currency (US dollars), without adjustment to a common price year or standardised for purchasing power.

Results

Study selection

A total of 7098 articles were identified by database searching, 6922 articles were removed based on title and abstract screening, which resulted in 176 full texts being screened and 41 included in the qualitative synthesis, with 31 included in the quantitative synthesis. The selection process is outlined in Fig. 1. Characteristics of included studies

The studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis were published between 2003 and 2023 and included a total of 12,330 study participants (6649 participants were randomised to stepped care and 5681 to usual care). Most studies were conducted in the United States of America (n = 14) and the Netherlands (n = 8) and were primarily RCTs (n = 30, n = 11 cluster RCTs) (Table 1). Stepped care models were primarily trialled in adults with a diagnosis of depression (n = 21), followed by anxiety (n = 5), depression and anxiety (n = 5), alcohol dependence (n = 3), PTSD (n = 2), eating disorders (n = 4) and obsessive–compulsive disorder (n = 1). The stepped care intervention varied in the number of steps, treatment intensity, step-up decision and triage concept, while usual care comparators also differed across settings and in some cases incorporated elements resembling stepped care (Table 2), underscoring the system-level nature of the comparison. Although stepped care models in some studies incorporated components similar to usual care, the defining elements across included studies were the presence of defined step‑up rules, which consistently distinguished stepped care as a system‑level approach from usual care comparators as defined by the original study authors. Quality of included studies

Based on RoB2, 35 studies had a moderate risk of bias, and six studies had a high risk of bias. The Cohen’s kappa for risk of bias was 0.32, indicating low agreement, as the two reviewers initially disagreed on low risk and moderate risk assessments before reaching a consensus. A summary of the RoB2 and a traffic plot is presented in Fig. 2 for all included studies, and alongside the forest plots for the meta-analyses.Fig. 2Risk of bias summary and risk of bias traffic plot

Risk of bias summary and risk of bias traffic plot

The certainty of the findings for each meta-analysis using the GRADE assessment and the overall quality of the meta-analyses is provided in Supplementary Table 3. The certainty of findings for all meta-analysis outcomes were downgraded one level for serious risk of bias and two levels for very serious risk of bias. For high inconsistency, the outcomes were further downgraded one level for a heterogeneity of 50% > I2 < 75% and two level for a heterogeneity of I2 > 75%. For high imprecision, the outcomes were further downgraded one level for non-significant outcomes. Based on the GRADE assessment, seven outcomes were of very low certainty (Depression, anxiety and quality of life related general health mean changes from baseline to post-treatment and from baseline to follow-up and direct healthcare costs), two outcomes were of low certainty (depression remission post-treatment and depression remission follow-up), four outcomes were of moderate certainty (depression response post-treatment, PTSD mean change from baseline to post-treatment and quality of life related mental health from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up) and two outcomes were of high certainty (depression response follow-up and indirect healthcare costs).

Main outcomes

Given substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%), pooled estimates are presented as indicative system-level effects reflecting average directional trends rather than uniform intervention effects.

Depression

The meta-analyses for the effectiveness of stepped care compared to usual care for depression related outcomes included 24 studies (n = 15 with continuous measures and n = 9 with dichotomous outcome measures). Findings from 5 studies are reported narratively.

The meta-analysis for continuous measures of depression demonstrated moderate but not significant effects for standardised mean changes from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up in favour of stepped care (SMD [95% CI] 0.73 [-0.26 to 1.72] and 0.92 [-0.20 to 2.03] respectively) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Depression mean change from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up

Depression mean change from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up

The meta-analysis for depression remission demonstrated a significantly higher (64%) probability for remission at post-treatment in favour of stepped care (RR [95% CI] 1.64 [1.24 to 2.16]) (Fig. 4). At follow-up, the probability of remission was 55% higher in stepped care, although this was not significant (RR [95% CI] 1.55 [0.94 to 2.57]) (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Depression remission post-treatment and follow-up

Depression remission post-treatment and follow-up

Meta-analyses for depression response (defined as a 50% reduction in depression symptoms) also demonstrated significantly higher probability at post-treatment and follow-up in favour of stepped care (RR [95% CI] 1.78 [1.35 to 2.35] and 1.46 [1.04 to 2.04] respectively) (Fig. 5). This indicates a 78% and 46% higher probability of treatment response in the stepped care group, at post-treatment and follow-up, respectively.Fig. 5Depression response post-treatment and follow-up

Depression response post-treatment and follow-up

Findings from studies reporting depression outcomes unable to be included in meta-analyses are provided as raw post-intervention scores in Supplementary Table 4. Four studies found stepped care to be significantly more effective than usual care in reducing adverse outcomes at 12 months. The mortality rate was 0.6% in the stepped care group compared to 3.3% in usual care (RR [95% CI] 0.20 [0.07–0.65]), (p = 0.007), and the rate of self-harm events was 2.2% versus 7.8% respectively, (RR [95% CI] 0.28 [0.15–0.55]), (p < 0.001). At 3 months, remission of minor or major depression was significantly higher in the stepped care group (41.4%) than in usual care (23.6%), (p = 0.015), as was response to treatment (56.6% versus 38.9%), (p = 0.022). This effect persisted at 6 months, with response rates of 69.7% in stepped care compared to 54.2% in usual care (p = 0.038). Additionally, one study found a significantly lower prevalence of ICD-10 diagnoses at 6 months in the stepped care group (12.7%) compared to usual care (25.0%), (RR [95% CI] 0.52 [0.29–0.96]), (p = 0.04).

Anxiety

The meta-analyses for the effectiveness of stepped care compared with usual care for anxiety related symptoms included outcomes from eight studies. Findings from four studies unable to be meta-analysed are reported narratively.

For continuous measures of anxiety, meta-analyses demonstrated moderate effects for standardised mean changes from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up in favour of stepped care, but these were non-significant (SMD [95% CI] 0.60 [-0.64 to 1.84] and 0.92 [-0.08 to 1.92] respectively) (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Anxiety mean change from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up

Anxiety mean change from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up

Clinical outcomes for anxiety from the four studies unable to be included in meta-analyses [26–29] are presented as raw post-intervention scores in Supplementary Table 4. Two studies reported dichotomous outcomes related to symptom recurrence and reduction in social stressors, with significant differences favouring stepped care over usual care [28, 29]. One study [29] found that reduction in anxiety symptoms was significantly greater in the stepped care group at 6 months (56.5% versus 36.4%), at 12 months (63.3% versus 41.5%), and at 18 months (64.6% versus 41.6%), (p < 0.001). Anxiety remission was also significantly higher in the stepped care group at 6 months (38.1% versus 27.8%), (p = 0.01) and at 18 months (42.3% versus 33.3%), (p = 0.04). A second study [28] reported anxiety symptom reduction at 4 months (stepped care = 21.0% versus usual care = 12.5%), at 8 months (23.0% versus 18.5%), and at 12 months (24.0% versus 22.0%). Anxiety remission in the same study was 16.0% versus 7.0% at 4 months, 16.0% versus 7.0% at 8 months, and 22.0% versus 18.0% at 12 months, (p = 0.002).

For the outcomes of Social Phobia Improvements, Panic Disorder Improvements, Anxiety Improvements and Fear of physical symptoms, there were no significant improvements in the stepped care group over usual care, (Supplementary Table 2) [26, 27].

PTSD

The meta-analysis for the effectiveness of stepped care for PTSD related outcomes included three studies and demonstrated large significant effects for means changes from baseline to post-treatment in favour of stepped care compared to the changes from baseline to post-treatment for usual care (SMD [95% CI] 1.39 [0.61 to 2.17]) (Fig. 7). One study reported PTSD-related outcomes that were unable to be meta-analysed (Supplementary Table 4). This study reported the prevalence of PTSD cases at 6 and 12 months for stepped care compared to usual care, with no difference between groups [30].

Alcohol dependence

Studies reporting alcohol dependence related outcomes (n = 4 studies) used discrete scales that could not be meta-analysed. Findings are reported as raw post-intervention scores in Supplementary Table 4. One study [31] found significantly reduced alcohol intoxication in the stepped care group compared to usual care. At 12 months, the percentage of days abstinent was higher in the stepped care group (mean = 77 days, SD = 29) than in usual care (mean = 60 days, SD = 39), (p = 0.03). The proportion of participants reporting no heavy drinking days at 12 months was also significantly greater in the stepped care group (69.44%) compared to usual care (35.90%), (p = 0.008). Another study [32] reported a continuous outcome measuring readiness to actively change drinking behaviour, with a significantly greater improvement in the stepped care group (mean difference [MD] = 2.7, standard error [SE] = 0.7) compared to usual care (MD = 0.8, SE = 0.7), (p = 0.023). The remaining two studies [30, 33] had outcomes with no difference for stepped care compared to usual care, (Supplementary Table 2).

Eating disorder

Three studies reported outcomes for eating disorder related symptoms using discrete scales that could not be meta-analysed and reported narratively as raw post-intervention scores [34, 35] (Supplementary Table 4). The studies compared the effects of stepped care and usual care on changes in binge eating and body measurements. Only one outcome (number of eating binges) demonstrated, after 12 months, significantly fewer episodes with stepped care = 13.6% compared to usual care = 40%, p = 0.028 [34]. All other outcomes showed no significant differences: The Binge Eating Remission Rate at post-treatment was 66.5% for stepped care and 74.4% for usual care, at 6 months it was 38.2% for stepped care and 33.3% for usual care and at 12 months it was 44.7% for stepped care and 41.0% for usual care, showing non-significant improvements for stepped care at 6 and 12 months [35, 36]. Outcomes for physical parameters (Cholesterol reduction, waist reduction, weight reduction or BMI reduction) showed no difference between stepped care and usual care [34–36]. Outcomes for eating disorder severity (EDE 16, 3-F-E and YBC-EDS) demonstrated no differences for stepped care compared to usual care [34–36].

Insomnia & obsessive–compulsive disorder

Two studies reported insomnia & obsessive–compulsive disorder related outcomes that could not be meta-analysed (37, 40) (Supplementary Table 4). On the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; a measure of the severity of sleeping problems), there were no differences between stepped care and usual care at 18 or 24 months [37, 38]. For the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale(Y-BOCS; a measure of the severity of OCD symptoms), there were no differences between stepped care and usual care at post-treatment or 3 months [39].

Quality of life

Meta-analyses for the effectiveness of stepped care for quality of life related outcomes included a total of 17 studies (n = 11 reporting mental health related quality of life, n = 10 reporting general health quality of life). An additional 20 studies reported quality of life outcomes unable to be meta-analysed.

The meta-analyses for mental health-related quality of life demonstrated large and significant effects for standardised mean changes from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up in favour of stepped care (SMD [95% CI] 3.13 [2.08, 4.18] and 3.60 [2.15, 5.05] respectively) (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Quality of life mental health mean change from baseline to post-treatment and baseline to follow-up

General health-related quality of life from the three studies unable to be included in meta-analyses [40–42] are presented as raw post-intervention scores in Supplementary Table 5. One study [42] reported that significantly more participants in the stepped care group compared to the usual care group (49.3% versus 8.0%, p < 0.001) achieved a World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL) score > 3 (using 3 as the cut-off for clinically meaningful quality of life) at 12 months. Another study reporting other measures of general health related quality of life reported no differences between stepped care and usual care (CGAS, CGI-S, CGI-I) [40, 41].

The additional 17 studies reported quality of life outcomes using discrete scales that could not be meta-analysed and reported narratively as raw post-intervention scores social health (n = 3), physical health (n = 4), environmental health (n = 1), fatigue (n = 3), functional impairment (n = 5) and emotional health (n = 1); Supplementary Table 5).

Three studies [40, 43, 44] explored social health domain with these outcome measures (SF-36 Social Functioning, WHOQOL Social Domain, SAS-SR). The SF-36 One study [43] reported significant improvements in social functioning (impact of physical and emotional health on social activities) in favour of stepped care. At 3 months, the mean score on the SF-36 Social Functioning scale was 63.8 (SD = 30.2) for stepped care compared to 44.0 (SD = 26.9) for usual care (p < 0.0001). At 6 months, scores remained significantly higher in the stepped care group (mean = 70.1, SD = 26.7) compared to usual care (mean = 51.2, SD = 28.9), (p < 0.0001). In contrast, no significant differences were found between groups for the WHOQOL Social Domain (assessing relationships, social support, and sexual activity) or the SAS-SR (assessing performance in social roles) [40, 44].

Four studies [32, 44–46] explored the physical health domain. One outcome (SF-12 Physical Health) which measured the impact of physical health on daily activities at 12 months was significant higher for stepped care (MD = 46.38, SD = 9.99) compared to usual care (MD = 41.66, SD = 10.91), (p = 0.001) [46]. Other outcomes (SF-12 Physical Health, WHOQOL Physical Domain, SF-36 Physical Health) that measured an impact of physical health on daily activities showed no difference between stepped care and usual care [32, 44, 45].

One study explored the environmental health domain using the WHOQOL Environmental Domain outcome (a measure of an individual’s perception of their environment), and demonstrated no difference between stepped care and usual care [44].

For the fatigue domain, the SF-36 Vitaliy outcome, which assessed energy level and fatigue, demonstrated significantly higher rates in favour of stepped care compared to usual care at 3 months for stepped care (MD = 53.7, SD = 28.5) compared to usual care (MD = 34.8, SD = 26.1) MD (SD), (p < 0.0001) and 6 months for stepped care (MD = 62.8, SD = 25.7) compared to usual care (MD = 38.8, SD = 27.0), (p < 0.0001) [43]. For the FACIT-F outcome, that measured the impact of fatigue on daily activities, no differences between stepped care and usual care were found [37, 38].

Five studies explored the functional impairment domain [29, 41, 42, 44, 47, 48] with different outcome measures (WHODAS 2.0, CGI-S, SDS functional impairment). The WHODAS 2.0 outcome, which measured an individual’s level of functioning in major life domains, showed a significant improvement at 9 months in favour of stepped care (MD = 7.35, SD = 8.36) compared to usual care (MD = 11.31, SD = 10.76), (p = 0.04) [47]. The SDS functional impairment outcomes, which measured an individual’s impairment in work/school, social life and family life, showed a significant improvement at 6 months in favour of stepped care (MD = 3.07, SD = 2.93) compared to usual care (MD = 3.55, SD = 2.90), (p = 0.01) [29]. Other WHODAS outcomes [42, 44, 48], showed no differences between stepped care and usual care.

One study explored the emotional domain (SF-36 Emotional Role) which measured the impact of emotional problems on daily functioning. It demonstrated a significant improvement at 3 months for stepped care (MD = 47.4, SD = 41.0) compared to usual care (MD = 28.4, SD = 35.1), (p < 0.0001) and at 6 months for stepped care (MD = 57.1, SD = 41.2) compared to usual care (MD = 34.6, SD = 40.9), (p < 0.0001) [43]. The final two studies [37, 38] explored general health domain in patients with cancer, with no differences between stepped care and usual care.

Nine studies were included in meta-analyses for the cost-effectiveness of stepped care versus usual care, and an additional two studies are synthesized narratively. For indirect healthcare costs (costs associated with absenteeism, productivity loss), the meta-analysis demonstrated a large, significant effect in favour of stepped care (MD -962.19 [-1412.55, -511.83]) (Fig. 10). For direct healthcare costs (treatment associated costs), there were small, but non-significant effects (MD 195.16 [-137.96, 528.28]) (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Direct healthcare costs and indirect costs in US-dollar

Two additional studies [29, 32] reported cost-effectiveness outcomes that were unable to be meta-analysed and are presented as raw post-intervention scores in Supplementary Table 6. One study [29] reported a statistically significant improvement in self-reported financial situation at 6 months for stepped care (MD = 0.15, SD = 0.35) compared to usual care (MD = 0.28, SD = 0.45), (p < 0.001). Service utilisation outcomes from one study (related to accident, criminal justice, health care and social care) showed no difference between stepped care and usual care [32]. Another study explored the total health care costs for patients with HIV treatments, which showed a saving of 840 US-Dollars in favour of stepped care compared to usual care, but without a statistical significance [49].

Satisfaction

Only study reported patient satisfaction with stepped care compared to usual care (45). Satisfaction, measured using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire-8 (CSQ-8) at 12 months, was 0.96 points higher with stepped care compared to usual care, but the difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the clinical effectiveness of stepped care compared to usual care for mental health outcomes in people of all ages with diagnosed mental health conditions and evaluated the cost effectiveness and satisfaction of stepped care models compared with usual care.

The meta-analyses showed that stepped care models resulted in significantly greater improvements in depression, PTSD and anxiety outcomes, mental and general health related quality of life compared to usual care in people with mental health conditions, and that cost-effectiveness for indirect (but not direct) costs were significantly lower with stepped care. Variation in costing methods, economic perspectives, and valuation of productivity loss limits the interpretability of pooled estimates as transferable savings across settings. While meta-analyses were unable to be conducted, our narrative synthesis suggests that stepped care may also provide significant improvements in alcohol dependence, eating disorder outcomes, and other domains of quality of life (e.g. social health, fatigue, functional impairment and physical health). Evidence for patient satisfaction was extremely limited, with only one included study assessing satisfaction and reporting no statistically significant difference between stepped care and usual care. This represents a critical gap in the stepped care evidence base, given the conceptual relevance of satisfaction to service-level care models.

Interpretation of heterogeneity and pooled effects

Substantial heterogeneity across pooled analyses was anticipated, given the complex nature of stepped care models. Variability in treatment structure, progression criteria, populations and usual care comparators limits the interpretation of pooled estimates as precise effects. Subsequently, pooled estimates are best interpreted as indicative system-level trends reflecting average directional effects, consistent with GRADE downgrading for inconsistency. We considered excluding potential outliers, but heterogeneity reflected system‑level differences, and post‑hoc exclusion risked bias. We therefore prioritized transparent reporting and cautious interpretation. Restricting analyses to narrowly comparable health-care systems would limit the available evidence and undermine the aim of examining stepped care models as a scalable service-delivery approach across real-world contexts, supporting the decision to retain pooled analyses with strengthened interpretive caution.

Implications for practice and health systems

Interpretation of findings should be guided by GRADE ratings, which ranged from moderate to very low across many pooled estimates, reflecting heterogeneity, risk of bias and imprecision. The findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive. Stepped care models may be prioritised within health systems where step-up criteria are clearly defined and access to evidence-based low-intensity interventions is assured. Effective implementation also depends on organisational structures, workforce capability and systems that support appropriate progression through stepped care steps.

Findings in the context of existing evidence

Our findings are consistent with previously published research that demonstrates the clinical effectiveness of stepped care models compared with usual care models for depression and anxiety symptoms [7, 9–11]. For depression and anxiety, we included a larger number of studies in the meta-analyses than in previous reviews, and for depression we included dichotomous outcomes for the first time. As a result, our findings evaluate a larger body of evidence that supports the clinical effectiveness of stepped care compared to usual care for depression and anxiety. For PTSD, our findings extend the previously published works and demonstrate that stepped care may be more clinically effective than usual care. Previous studies on PTSD treatment outcomes (not included in meta-analyses) and meta-analyses on PTSD prevention outcomes consistently found no significant difference between stepped care and usual care [8].

Existing reviews [6–11, 50] have predominantly examined stepped care for the outcomes of depression, anxiety and PTSD. These reviews generally conclude that stepped care is more clinical effective than usual care. Our review supports this conclusion but incorporates a broader spectrum of mental health conditions, including alcohol dependence, eating disorder, insomnia and obsessive-depressive disorder [6–11, 50]. This expanded scope allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of stepped care’s utility across diverse diagnostic categories. For these conditions, our review highlights a significant gap in the literature, in that there are currently too few randomised controlled trials to support meta-analytic synthesis. This underscores an urgent need for future research to focus on evaluating stepped care in the context of those conditions.

Our findings are consistent with previously published (non-meta-analysis) research that demonstrates the cost effectiveness of stepped care models compared to usual care models. Our meta-analysis of cost-effectiveness demonstrated that stepped care is on average US $195 more expensive than usual care. The 95% confidence interval ranges from US $137 less expensive to US $528 more expensive, which is consistent with the literature that stepped care incurs higher direct costs than usual care (US $2,743 for stepped care versus US $989 for usual care) [8]. Stepped care models generally incur higher direct costs than usual care, likely due to their greater complexity. These models involve multiple stages and require close monitoring to ensure timely progression through the steps, which contributes to the increased costs [5]. Despite the higher direct cost of stepped care to cover mental health treatment, stepped care models are ultimately more cost-effective when we look at the total cost. The total costs or indirect costs (e.g. absenteeism from work) of stepped care models have not been meta-analysed in previous literature. The existing literature reports that the indirect costs are significantly lower for stepped care compared to usual care (51.3% lower and 62.4% lower) [8]. We provide stronger evidence with a meta-analysis that indirect costs are significantly lower for stepped care models compared to usual care models, with an average saving of approximately US $962.19. The 95% confidence interval suggest that the true cost difference could reasonably range from US $ 511.83 to US $ 1412.55, reinforcing the economic advantage of stepped care. The significantly lower costs might result from better clinical outcomes and a resulting shorter sickness period. However, it is challenging to analyse the cost-effectiveness of stepped care models compared to usual care models as the trials included heterogeneous populations, conditions and outcome measures. This heterogeneity limits the meaningfulness of our cost-effectiveness analysis. Therefore, subgroup cost-effectiveness analyses should be performed including health systems with similar structures and similar conditions to ensure that there is a high standard of comparability. A high standard of comparability is achieved by adopting the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) [51] checklist for achieving meaningful and actionable outcomes in health economic evaluations. By promoting standardised reporting of costs, outcomes and context, CHEERS ensures transparency and comparability across studies. This comparability is required for more meaningful outcomes which are needed as public funded health systems and low-income countries health systems have limited resources and stepped care models are beneficial in optimising resource allocation.

Future research

We experienced difficulties synthesising the evidence on stepped care models due to the high level of heterogeneity in the outcome measures used by the included studies. The high heterogeneity of different outcome measures is a source of inconsistency that can reduce confidence in the meta-analyses. Future research should adopt core outcome sets (COS) that are an agreed, standardized collection of outcomes which should be measured and reported in randomised controlled trials for a specific condition. COS are developed in a standardised process among stakeholders to ensure clinical meaningful outcome measurement. This would enhance comparability of randomised controlled trials and facilitate more reliable meta-analyses. One example of a core outcome set is discharge from acute mental health services, which includes four criteria: readmission; quality of life; completed suicide; and psychological distress reported by service users [52, 53]. The World Health Organization and the Core Outcome Measures in Effective Trials Initiative (COMET) also emphasized the usage of core outcome sets for clinical trials to ensure meaningful outcome measurements [54, 55].

There were difficulties in analysing studies that involves heterogeneous populations as the populations assigned to stepped care models differ in terms of age, gender, sex, profession, socioeconomic status, education level and other demographic characteristics. The included trials provide limited information about population details and restrict a detailed subgroup analysis with comparable demographic cohorts. Such an analysis would elevate more meaningful outcomes and better tailored interventions. This is also important regarding marginalised and underserved populations with limited access to mental health services [56]. Future research should investigate whether stepped care models elevate or reduce existing barriers to stepped care models and how those models must be adapted to ensure inclusivity that reduces health disparities.

Randomised controlled trials often have different primary and secondary outcomes that makes it challenging to combine data for meta-analyses. When applying the same stepped care model to different mental health conditions, trials often overlook the fact that interventions tailored to one condition may have limited or no impact on another. Such outcomes that are not the intervention’s primary focus are likely to limit the meaningfulness. A meaningful comparison can only be made between studies with similar aims. Eventually, it undermines the validity of conclusions and may lead to misinterpretations about the effectiveness of stepped care models compared to usual care models. Future research should select trial outcomes based on the focus of the intervention and distinguish between primary and secondary outcomes, as well as whether each outcome was designed to be addressed. This will mitigate the risk of misinterpreting the outcomes and inappropriately implementing interventions in ineffective clinical settings.

Research on stepped care models must shift to implementation science to gather evidence about how stepped care models can be adopted, adapted and sustained in clinical settings. Even though existing research and our research demonstrates the effectiveness of stepped care models compared to usual care, its implementation into clinical settings and mitigating challenges remain widely unexplored. Challenges relate to its implementation cover numerous contextual factors, such as clinician training and perception, organizational support and commitment, patient engagement and satisfaction. For example, we could only identify one trial with a patient satisfaction outcome. The same might apply to other contextual factors. Future research should explore implementation challenges to identify barriers and facilitators of successful delivery. Mixed-methods studies that provide insights from clinicians and patients will help to identify those barriers and facilitators.

Stepped care models are predominantly designed for depression, anxiety and PTSD with well-established, evidence-based and low intensity interventions that can be delivered early and scaled up if necessary. Patients with these conditions are often likely to have an early positive response that will not require high-intensity treatment. This structured scaling up approach makes stepped care models effective, efficient and clinically appropriate for those conditions [5]. In contrast to that are mental health conditions, such as obsessive–compulsive disorder or insomnia that require a more intensive treatment approach. For those conditions, it would be necessary to develop tailored stepped care models with a high-intensity approach. This is supported by our findings that there are no significant differences between the analysed stepped care models and usual care models for outcomes related to insomnia, obsessive–compulsive disorder and quality of life (environmental domain). Therefore, a one-size-fits-all approach for all mental health conditions is not appropriate. Clinical guidelines should recommend adaptations specific to each condition, aligning the interventions of stepped care models with the characteristics of the condition in question. In the next step, future research should identify predictors of response to different steps in the stepped care model, such as symptom severity or patient preferences that could inform a more personalised stepped care strategy [57]. Adapting stepped care models on specific mental health conditions and on a personal level will enhance its effectiveness.

Research on stepped care models mainly focuses on people with existing mental health conditions rather than on preventing them [7]. Prevention of mental health conditions is an important aim as the least intensive treatment option should be selected at the earliest stage when people can still self-manage mental health symptoms [58]. The earliest stage is when a mental health condition is not manifested, and prevention strategies or treatments might stop further manifestation. This strategy is beneficial on an individual level but also on a system level, including potential financial savings. This would also require aiming for outcome, population, mental health condition and mental health system standardization to minimize heterogeneity and to ensure meaningful outcomes. Future research should explore stepped care models in terms of mental health prevention.

Limitations

Overall, the quality of the included studies was moderate, as six out of 41 had a high risk of bias. The high risk of bias in these studies was found to be due to bias in the randomisation process, as there were significant differences in baseline characteristics between the stepped care groups and the comparison groups (Fig. 2). The overall quality of the included studies in the meta-analyses was variable, with seven forest plots reported with very low certainty. The low certainty was based on the high risk of bias and the inconsistency of the studies included in the meta-analyses. The reason for this inconsistency is based on the high heterogeneity of the results. This is probably due to the different stepped care models in terms of number of steps, treatment components, duration of steps, rules for stepping up and triage options. Our findings of high heterogeneity are consistent with previous published research.

The low rate of agreement between reviewers on risk of bias (initial disagreement on low/moderate risk of bias) may be the result of insufficient pilot testing of the assessment. There is a risk that we may not have identified all the relevant literature because the search strategy only included English-language articles from electronic databases, which is a potential limitation of this review. Another limitation is that we were only able to meta-analyse depression, anxiety, and PTSD outcomes. As a result, findings related to other mental health conditions could not be synthesized quantitatively and were interpreted narratively. In addition, cost data were converted to a common currency (US dollars) but were not adjusted to a common price year or standardized for purchasing power. While inflation adjustment is methodologically feasible where base years are consistently reported, incomplete and heterogeneous cost reporting across studies limited the reliability of such adjustments, which may reduce comparability of cost estimates across settings and require cautious interpretation of pooled cost-effectiveness results. Most of the included studies were conducted in high-income countries, such as the United States of America and those in Europe. This raises concerns about whether the findings can be generalised to low- and middle-income countries, where healthcare infrastructure, cultural contexts and resource availability may differ significantly. Consequently, the feasibility and effectiveness of stepped care models in those areas remain uncertain and require further investigation.

Conclusions

The review demonstrated that stepped care models have better outcomes than usual care models for outcome measures related to depression, PTSD, quality of life (mental health and general health) and cost-effectiveness (indirect costs), highlighting their potential to improve mental health outcomes. More research focus is necessary to explore health disparities among marginalised cohorts in accessing stepped care models. Future research should also explore implementation associated factors to optimise its facilitation in clinical settings. Evidence on patient satisfaction is limited, as only one study assessed this outcome, highlighting a key research gap. There is also a need to develop condition-specific stepped care models that allow each treatment step to be personalised, which could enhance its clinical effectiveness. Above all, it is essential to standardise and compare only similar outcomes, populations and conditions to ensure meaningful results and avoid heterogeneity.
