Ulnar artery reconstruction in hypothenar hammer syndrome with heterotopic placement in carpal tunnel—A strategy for improved patency
Authors: Madeleine Charlotte Young, Calver Pang, Charles Yuen Yung Loh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ulnar artery thrombosis from hypothenar trauma is an uncommon cause of digital ischaemia, most often associated with Hypothenar hammer syndrome (HHS). Standard treatment includes segmental excision and autologous vein grafting along the Guyon's canal, but this may be suboptimal in patients with occupational risk factors. We report a case where the vein graft was rerouted through the carpal tunnel to avoid subsequent compression at Guyon’s canal. To our knowledge, this is the first report of this anatomical approach. The patient achieved excellent graft patency and functional recovery at 6 months, suggesting a promising surgical alternative in select cases.

Introduction

HHS results from repetitive trauma to the hypothenar eminence, leading to ulnar artery thrombosis or aneurysm formation.1 The artery’s superficial course over the hook of the hamate within Guyon’s canal makes it vulnerable to mechanical stress.2 Patients often present with cold intolerance, pain, or paraesthesia in the ulnar digits. Diagnosis is supported by duplex ultrasound or cross-sectional imaging.3

Surgical intervention is indicated when ischemia progresses. The standard approach involves vein grafting along the ulnar artery’s anatomical path.4 However, this route may be prone to recurrent pressure in patients with persistent mechanical exposure. We describe an alternative reconstruction using the carpal tunnel to protect the graft.

Case report

A 43-year-old right-handed mechanic presented with a four-week history of cold sensation and paraesthesia in the ring and little fingers of his non-dominant hand, following an isolated episode of forceful torque using a spanner. Examination revealed delayed capillary refill in the ulnar digits and sensory changes. Duplex ultrasound confirmed a 4.5 cm thrombosed ulnar artery segment at the base of the hand with distal reconstitution. Due to progressive ischaemic symptoms, surgical reconstruction was performed.

Surgical technique (Fig. 1)

Under general anaesthesia, without a tourniquet, the ulnar artery was exposed via a curvilinear incision over the hypothenar border. A 4.5 cm thrombosed segment was identified within Guyon’s canal. Notably, the artery was confined within dense fascial tissue. A 6 cm segment of superficial vein from the ipsilateral forearm was used, with an acknowledgement of the increased risk of thrombosis when compared with arterial grafts. Following standard carpal tunnel release, the proximal anastomosis was performed with 10-0 nylon proximal to the thrombosis. The graft was then tunnelled through the carpal tunnel and anastomosed distally. Intraoperative Doppler confirmed strong pulsatile flow. The total length of procedure was under 90 min.

Outcome

Postoperatively, the hand remained well-perfused. The patient received low-molecular-weight heparin, a 2-week course of aspirin (75 mg), and hand therapy from week one. At 6-month follow-up, he reported full resolution of ischaemic symptoms and had returned to unrestricted work duties. Duplex ultrasonography confirmed excellent graft patency (supplementary video). Minor numbness near the incision site was improving with therapy.

Discussion

This case illustrates the importance of tailoring vascular reconstruction to individual patient risk factors. While repetitive trauma is classically implicated in HHS, isolated blunt trauma may also result in ulnar artery thrombosis.¹ Surgical reconstruction via vein grafting along the native path is standard,⁴ but in patients with ongoing mechanical stress, such as manual labourers, this route may not offer long-term durability. Vein grafts have lower donor site morbidity than arterial grafts but require more careful placement and protection.

Routing the vein graft through the carpal tunnel provided a protected anatomical space for the conduit and, to our knowledge, has not been previously described. Carpal tunnel release is occasionally performed to relieve median nerve compression in the setting of HHS³, but not as a conduit route. This approach may reduce graft trauma and improve longevity, particularly in high-risk patients, by exploiting the tunnel’s deep protection between the palmar pillars.

Although other innovative techniques such as thrombolysis, endovascular interventions, or arterial interposition grafts have been reported,⁵ these remain under evaluation and are often limited by accessibility or long-term data. Further comparative studies are warranted.

Conclusion

This report presents a novel technical modification in the surgical management of HHS: routing the vein graft through the carpal tunnel. This approach may offer superior graft protection in patients with high mechanical stress to the palm. The favourable outcome in this case supports consideration of this technique in select cases.
