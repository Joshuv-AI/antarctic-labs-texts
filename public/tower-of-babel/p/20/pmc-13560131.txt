A 3D-printed extravascular stent containing sirtuin-3 engineered human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells maintains venous graft patency
Authors: Kexun Liu, Xin Sun, Xuan Jiao, Limeng Chao, Bohao Zhang, Chang Liu, Jie Wu, Mingli Huang, Peian Cai, Xin Li, Yuanzhong Zhao, Xin Tian, Ren-Ke Li, Wei Chen, Hai Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) reconstructs the blood supply for treating coronary heart disease. One of the most used conduits is the great saphenous vein, but its effect is limited, owing to lower long-term patency versus arterial grafts. This study devised a 3D bio-printed stent, comprising of genetically modified human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), to improve venous graft patency.MethodsBMSCs and endothelial cells (ECs) were obtained from sternal bone marrow and discarded saphenous veins, respectively. BMSCs were transduced with lentivirus overexpressing sirtuin-3 (SIRT3) and seeded on a 3D bio-printed matrix stent, comprising of hyaluronic acid methacryloyl and gelatin methacryloyl (HAMA/GelMA). A rat CABG model was established, via generating a jugular vein-common carotid artery arteriovenous graft. The SIRT3-BMSC-seeded stent was “wrapped” around this venous graft, serving as an extravascular stent. An in vitro model was also devised, in which lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-pre-treated ECs were co-cultured with SIRT3-BMSCs, followed by evaluating mitochondrial transferal and tunneling nanotube (TNT) formation-related functional changes. Immunoprecipitation was used to examine SIRT3-vasodilator-simulated phosphoprotein (VASP) interactions.ResultsRat arteriovenous graft model found that SIRT3-BMSC+stent, compared to Control, Stent, and BMSC+stent groups, had the greatest graft vessel diameter, maximum blood flow velocity during systole and CD31+ EC area, along with the lowest cell proliferation and inflammatory cell infiltration; therefore, SIRT3-BMSC+stent had the greatest inhibitory effects on venous graft neointimal formation. In vitro, LPS-pre-treated ECs, after co-culture with SIRT3-BMSCs, restored endothelial, along with lowering mesenchymal marker expression. Furthermore, mechanistic analyses revealed increased SIRT-BMSC-to-LPS-pre-treated EC mitochondrial transfer, facilitated by TNTs formed between SIRT3-BMSCs and ECs. This mitochondrial transfer was mediated via SIRT3-VASP interactions, in which SIRT3 deacetylates VASP to promote TNT.ConclusionSIRT3-BMSC/HAMA/GelMA extravascular stent could effectively lower arteriovenous graft dilation and inhibit neointimal formation, possibly via SIRT3 deacetylation of VASP, thereby promoting TNT formation, and subsequently, mitochondrial transfer from BMSCs to ECs to improve EC function. Thus, the extravascular stent was able to provide external support, along with facilitating BMSC therapeutic effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05127-w.

Background

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is an important treatment for coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, but an undesirable side effect is venous graft restenosis. It involves 3 stages: early ischemia-reperfusion injury and endothelial cells (ECs) damage, followed by venous graft intima hyperplasia and atherosclerotic lesion formation. Ischemia-reperfusion injury occurs during graft acquisition and transplantation, in which increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production leads to over-expanded and leaking vein grafts, ultimately resulting in ECs damage. This yields reduced nitric oxide synthase and nitric oxide production, subsequently weakening vasodilation and anticoagulation [1]. Endothelial damage also exposes the extracellular matrix (ECM) to the circulation, promoting fibrin and platelet aggregation, all of which accelerates inflammatory factor release and thrombosis [2]. Furthermore, inflammatory factor release leads to immune cell infiltration in the venous graft, stimulating vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) transformation from quiescent to proliferative types, as well as migration to the intima layer, resulting in vessel wall thickening. Additionally, ECs also undergo endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT) [3, 4], resulting in increased mesenchymal cell proliferation and abnormal endothelial function. Overall, VSMCs and mesenchymal proliferation, along with ECM deposition and macrophage infiltration, yields venous graft intima hyperplasia. Later, atherosclerotic lesion formation and plaque rupture are the main causes of venous graft failure [5]. It is also noted that compared to natural atherosclerotic coronary arteries, atherosclerotic venous grafts have more inflammatory cell infiltration and foam cells, rendering them more prone to thrombosis and rupture [6]. Therefore, effective strategies to mitigate venous graft failure are an urgent need.

Arterial grafts, though, have been noted to be more successful than venous ones, in that they tend to have superior long-term patency rates, and are less susceptible to atherosclerosis, thrombosis and graft failure [7]. This is owed to arterial grafts being able to withstand the higher pressure of arterial circulation and adapt to different blood supply demands; by contrast, venous grafts merely act as conduits. Arterial grafts, such as those from internal mammary or radial arteries, have thicker walls, with greater elasticity, which, along with higher nitric oxide production, contributes to them being more reactive to vasoconstrictors, as well as greater resistance to spasm and atherosclerotic, all of which reduces atherosclerosis risk [8]. On the other hand, venous grafts, such as from the saphenous vein, have thinner, less elastic walls, rendering them more prone to developing intimal hyperplasia, and resulting in higher rates of occlusion over time. In fact, arterial grafts have higher patency rates than venous ones, such as 92% for radial arteries, compared to 86% for saphenous veins [8]. Ultimately, patients receiving arterial grafts tend to have better long-term survival, fewer cardiac events, and lower need for future interventions. Therefore, arterial grafts are generally recommended as the first-line treatment for coronary bypass, especially in younger patients, or those with life expectancies > 10 years, with saphenous veins being used if no suitable arteries are available. However, the technique of arterial grafting is more challenging and time-consuming. This has resulted in venous grafting still being the most frequently used, particularly in emergency situations and for patients with higher operative risks.

One strategy to mitigate the shortcomings of venous grafts involves bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), which possess multi-differentiation potential, low immunogenicity, no ethical controversies, as well as being easy to obtain and expand. BMSC transplantation therapies provide paracrine support, as well as being able to transfer mitochondria to damaged cells to improve their function. Mitochondria also constantly change their morphologies, as well as intracellular locations and distributions, in response to cell stress. In recent years, intercellular mitochondrial transfer has been observed, such as among somatic, cancer, and stem cells, where intact mitochondria, rather than merely mitochondrial DNA or fragments, have been transferred, resulting in improved functioning of damaged recipient cells [9–14]. Multiple mechanisms are involved in this process, including tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), extracellular vesicles (EVs) [15], and cell fusion [16]. For TNTs formation, 2 processes are involved, 1 of which entails the extension of multiple filamentous pseudopodia-like protrusions from the cell membrane. One protrusion contacts a neighboring cell, leading to other protrusions being retracted, leaving only 1 TNTs [17]. The other process is cell translocation, in which 2 cells directly contact each other, and membrane fusion occurs at the contact site. Upon the separation of these cells, the contact site is stretched and deformed to form TNTs [18]. TNTs are mainly composed of filamentous actin (F-actin), microtubules (Tubulin), as well as other cytoskeleton and cell membrane components. Their formation is fostered by vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein (VASP), which promotes globular actin (G-actin) monomer aggregation to form F-actin. TNTs mediate intercellular transfer of mRNA, non-coding RNA, and DNA to affect recipient cell gene expression and physiological function [19], as well as organelles, such as mitochondria, Golgi bodies, and lysosomes [20]. One factor involved in mitochondrial functional regulation is sirtuin-3 (SIRT3), a highly conserved NAD+-dependent deacetylase. Upon mitochondrial oxidative stress damage, SIRT3 is activated via cleavage into 28 kDa proteins, where it enters the mitochondria and deacetylates a large number of functional proteins, including those associated with regulating the tricarboxylic acid cycle, lipid metabolism, redox balance and mitochondrial dynamics [21–23]. SIRT3 has been found, including in our previous study, to be more highly expressed in young, versus aging BMSCs. Additionally, restoring SIRT3 expression in old BMSCs significantly improved their oxidative stress resistance and anti-apoptotic capabilities [24, 25]. Therefore, we devised a 3D bio-printed matrix stent, comprising of hyaluronic acid methacryloyl and gelatin methacryloyl (HAMA/GelMA) that was seeded with SIRT3-overexpressing BMSCs (SIRT3-BMSC). Both gelatin and hyaluronic acid are ECM materials and can be conjugated with methacryloyl groups to form, respectively, GelMA and HAMA. The combination of GelMA and HAMA yielded a biomaterial matrix, possessing the favorable structural stability of HAMA [26–30], and the good biocompatibility of GelMA [31–33]. This SIRT3-BMSC-seeded HAMA/GelMA stent was “wrapped” around a jugular vein-common carotid artery arteriovenous graft, forming an extravascular stent, which provided good mechanical support for the venous graft, along with preventing its dilation and inhibiting neointimal formation. This is possibly through SIRT3 deacetylation of VASP, leading to increased TNT formation and mitochondrial transfer from BMSCs to ECs to improve ECs function, ultimately improving long-term venous graft patency.

Materials and methods

Obtaining and culturing BMSCs and ECs

Sternal BM blood and great saphenous vein (GSV) tissues were collected from 22 patients (14 males, age 61.07 ± 10.94, and 8 females, age 64.33 ± 5.61), who underwent CABG in the Department of Cardiovascular Surgery, Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Patient diagnoses were summarized in Table S1. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (REB#: YJSKY2022-464; Title: The effects and mechanisms of 3D bioprinting HAMA/Gelatin combined with SIRT3 BMSCs extravascular stent on improving the long-term patency of venous bridges; Approval date: August 23, 2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. To obtain BMSCs, 3–4 mL of sternal bone marrow blood was taken during the CABG operation, and placed into a sterile 15 mL centrifuge tube with 1 ml heparin (1000 IU/mL). An equal volume of human peripheral blood lymphocyte separation solution (LTS107, Tbdscience) was slowly added, and the resulting mixture was centrifuged at 600×g, in 4 °C, for 25 min. The lymphocyte layer was transferred into another 15 mL centrifuge tube, mixed with 10 mL PBS, and centrifuged at 250×g, in 4 °C, for 5 min, to obtain BMSCs. These BMSCs were resuspended in 5 mL of low-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS, inoculated in a 25 cm2 flask, and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 (Fig. S1a). BMSCs from individual patients were expanded until passage 3 to a total cell number of 5 × 106. BMSCs from passage 3 were used for identification by flow cytometry (Bio-Rad C6). The markers (all from Biolegend) used were CD90 (328107), CD73 (344003), CD105 (323205), CD45 (982304), CD14 (4343519), HLA-DR (327005) of which BMSCs were incubated on ice with the antibodies for 25 min, and those incubated with isotype-identical IgG control was the negative control. Data showed that these cells were positive for mesenchymal stem cell markers CD90, CD73 and CD105, but negative for hematopoietic lineage cell markers CD45, CD14 and HLA-DR (Fig. S1b).

To assess BMSC differentiation potential, they were cultured to 80% confluency. Adipogenic differentiation was induced by culturing BMSCs in adipogenic supplement-enriched media (Stem Cell Technologies) for 14 days, followed by Oil Red O staining. For osteoblastic differentiation, BMSCs were cultured in osteoblastic differentiation media (10% FBS, 100 nM dexamethasone, 50 µM L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate) for 21 days, then stained with Alizarin Red S. For chondrogenic differentiation, BMSCs were cultured in chondrogenic differentiation medium (Sigma-Aldrich) for 21 days, then stained with Alcian blue. BMSCs were able to be stained by Oil Red O for lipid droplets, Alizarin Red S for extracellular calcium crystal deposition, and Alcian blue for cartilage spheroids, indicating that they could differentiate into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages (Fig. S1c-e).

For obtaining ECs, discarded GSV tissue was repeatedly rinsed with sterile PBS, until no blood clots were present. One end was ligated shut with 4 − 0 silk suture, and 0.2% collagenase type II solution infused from the other end. After filling, the open end was ligated shut, and incubated for 30 min, at 37°C and 5% CO2. Afterwards, both ligated ends of the GSV were opened, and EC-containing collagenase digestion solution was neutralized with high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS. To obtain additional ECs, GSV was longitudinally cut with ophthalmic scissors, vascular intima scraped several times and rinsed multiple times with neutralized EC-containing collagenase digestion solution. The resulting solution was centrifuged at 800×g for 5 min to obtain a cell pellet, which was resuspended in 5 mL high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS, inoculated in a 25 cm2 flask, and cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 (Fig. S2a). Like with BMSCs, ECs from passage 3 were used for identification, in which they were seeded into 6-well plates, at 2 × 104 cells/well. ECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin for 30 min, then incubated with von Willebrand factor (vWF, ab6994, Abcam) or CD144 (MA5-44146, Thermo Fisher) primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Afterwards, ECs were incubated with fluorescence-labelled secondary antibody for 1 h, at room temperature, and nuclei counter-stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma) for 3 min. Bright-field and fluorescence images were obtained under an inverted fluorescence microscope (LEICA Dmi8, Fig. S2a, b). Western blot analyses showed higher eNOS protein levels in ECs versus BMSCs (Fig. S2c, d).

Preparing and characterizing the HAMA/GelMA material

To prepare 0.25% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) photo-initiator, 0.025 g LAP (EFL-LAP, Engineering for Life) was added into 10 mL PBS, dissolved in a 40–50℃ water bath for 20 min, then stored at 4 °C post-dissolution. 0.5, 1.0, or 1.5% HAMA (EFL-HAMA-150k, Engineering for Life) and 5% GelMA (EFL-GM-60, Engineering for Life) were added into the LAP solution, and the resulting mixture was stirred at 50 °C for 30 min. The 5% GelMA was selected based on previous studies demonstrating that it was more bio-compatible, compared to higher concentrations [34–36]. In particular, 5% GelMA, compared to 10–15%, was found to better support Schwann cell proliferation, viability, and myelination [35], as well as promoting adipose tissue-derived stromal cell migration [36]. As for the HAMA concentrations, they were chosen based on obtaining the suitable mechanical properties for the resulting scaffolds, in which a too high compressive modulus yields poor compliance, rendering them unsuitable for shaping. Overall, these scaffolds should be strong, yet elastic enough to endure physiological arterial blood pressures [37, 38].

After dissolution, the resulting solution was filtered with a 0.22 μm sterile filter and stored at -20 °C in darkness. For 3D printing (REGENOVO, Bio-Architect® WS), the following parameters were set: 0.2 MPa air pressure, 10 mm/s printing speed, 1 mm spacing, 23 °C nozzle temperature, 5 °C platform temperature, and 200 μm layer thickness for each of the 3 layers. Furthermore, a 27G steel needle, with its barrel adapter, was inserted into the low-temperature nozzle. Afterwards, HAMA-GelMA mixture was added to the light-proof barrel, pre-cooled for 5 min at 4 °C, and 3D printing was conducted through the 27G needle. After the resulting stent was printed, it was cured for 45 s under 405 nm blue light.

To characterize HAMA/GelMA, the stent was placed in -80 °C for 24 h, then freeze-dried for 48 h. The resulting freeze-dried sample was attached to a conductive adhesive with double-sided tape and prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) by spraying gold for 45 s, using a Quorum SC7620 sputtering coater at 10 mA. SEM was then carried out using a HITACHI SU8010 scanning electron microscope, with accelerating voltage 5 kV and a SE2 secondary electron detector.

As the 3D-printed HAMA/GelMA stent is thin (600 μm thickness), the compression modulus cannot be directly measured using the general biomechanical tester (EFL-MT5600, Engineering for Life). Therefore, the material was filled into a standard cylindrical mold, with an inner diameter of 10 mm and ground height 8 mm, then placed into the general biomechanical tester. Additionally, for the swelling test, the HAMA/GelMA stent was placed in a 6-well plate with PBS, and incubated for 24 h, at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for swelling. Surface moisture was then wiped with sterile absorbent paper and the stent weighed as W (wet). After weighing, it was immediately frozen at -80 °C for 24 h, freeze-dried, and weighed again as W (dry). Swelling rate was calculated, based on W (wet)/W (dry). As for the degradation test, after swelling for 24 h post-freeze-drying, the stent was wiped, and weight recorded as the 1st day weight (W1). PBS was replaced every 2 days, and the stent was weighed on days 3, 7, 10, 14, and 21 (W3, 7, 10, 14, 21). Degradation weight was calculated based on (W1-W3, 7, 10, 14, 21)/W1 × 100%.

Characterizing HAMA/GelMA stent cell compatibility

To identify HAMA/GelMA cell compatibility, BMSCs were mixed with HAMA/GelMA bio-ink solution, at 1 × 106 cells/mL, which was then 3D printed. The Calcein/propidium iodide (PI) live-dead cell staining kit (C2015S, Beyotime) was used to detect cell survival at 1, 4, and 7 days, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the 3D printed BMSC-HAMA/GelMA stents were cut into 0.75 × 0.75 cm pieces, placed into a 12-well plate, incubated with 500 µL/well of Calcein/PI working solution at 37 °C for 30 min, and cell survival observed under confocal microscopy (Zeiss, LSM980). For measuring cell proliferation, cells were also seeded onto HAMA/GelMA stents, and the CCK-8 kit (BS350C, Labgic) was used, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lentiviral transduction of BMSCs to overexpress SIRT3 and in vivo evaluation of cell survival

BMSCs from passage 3 were plated into 12-well plates, at 5 × 104 cells, cultured overnight., and transduced with lentivirus vector, in which each well had 460 µL fresh culture medium, 20 µL 25×HiTransGA infection (REVG004, Genechem) enhancement solution, and 20 µL lentivirus at 5 × 105 particles/well (multiplicity of infection = 10); these lentiviruses carried SIRT3 or blank control, with or without green fluorescent protein (GFP) tagging (LV-SIRT3 [18621-0] for SIRT3, LV-SIRT3 [18621-1] for SIRT3-GFP, LVCON228 for control and LVCON238 for control-GFP lentiviruses, all from Genechem). Transduced BMSCs were incubated for 12 h, and their statuses observed under an inverted optical microscope. Afterwards, medium was changed and 5 µg/mL puromycin was added to select for transduced BMSCs. Successful SIRT3 overexpression was confirmed by both reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) for mRNA (Fig. S3a), as well as Western blot for protein expression (Fig. S3b).

Furthermore, to evaluate cell survival in vivo, BMSCs were transduced with lentivirus carrying the luciferase gene (LVCON101 for luciferase lentiviruses, Genechem). They were mixed with HAMA/GelMA and 3D bio-printed to obtain luciferase-labeled BMSCs-HAMA/GelMA extravascular stents, which were implanted into a rat arteriovenous transplantation model, as described below. In vivo bioluminescent imaging was performed at 1, 2, and 4 weeks after surgery to evaluate the survival of transplanted stem cells, using the IVIS optical imaging system (Perkin Elmer, B916).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

SIRT3 overexpression was confirmed by isolating total RNA from BMSCs with Trizol reagent (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. One µg total RNA served as the template for cDNA synthesis with SuperScript TM Reverse Transcriptase IV (Invitrogen). TB Green® Premix EX Taq II (TaKaRa) was used for tracking PCR product accumulation during cycling with ABI Real-time PCR Step One Plus (Applied Biosystems). All reactions were carried out in triplicate. The fold differences in the expression levels for each gene were calculated using CT values, and normalized to the housekeeping gene β-Tubulin. Primer sequences are the following: SIRT3-F: 5’-GAAGTGGAGGCAGCAGTGACAAG-3’; SIRT3-R: 5’-CGGCGATCTGAAGTCTGGAATGC-3’; β-Tubulin-F: 5’-CAACGCCACCTGTCCATCC-3’; β-Tubulin-R: 5’-GCGGAAGCAGATGCTGTAGAGC-3’.

Establishing a rat CABG model and implanting the SIRT3-BMSC-seeded HAMA/GelMA extravascular stent

All animal procedures approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (REB#: YJSDW2022-250; Title: The effects and mechanisms of 3D bioprinting HAMA/Gelatin combined with SIRT3 BMSCs extravascular stent on improving the long-term patency of venous bridges; Approval date: August 23, 2022) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH, 8th Edition, 2011). All the animal work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (200–220 g) were all obtained from the Animal Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, and intra-peritoneally injected with 5 mL/kg cyclosporine A daily, for 3 days pre-operation. Afterwards, rats were initially anesthetized with 4% isoflurane in O2 (flow rate 1 L/min) and intubated. Anesthesia was maintained by ventilating 2.5% isoflurane in O2, at 0.5 L/min. A 2 cm incision was made longitudinally at the middle of the neck, the right jugular vein freed, its branches ligated with 6 − 0 sutures, and a 6 mm length was cut from the vein to serve as the venous graft. The cut vein segment was flushed with heparin saline (200 U/mL) until no blood was present. The left common carotid artery was then freed, its proximal and distal ends clamped with hemostatic clips, and cut off. The venous graft was anastomosed to both ends of the severed common carotid artery with 11 − 0 sutures, and 6 stitches were sutured on each side. The hemostatic clips were loosened, and bleeding checked for both proximal and distal ends; if bleeding was present, it was compressed with a cotton swab, with no additional stitches to prevent anastomosis stenosis. Extravascular stents were then implanted, in which they comprised a 1.2 × 1.2 cm 3D-printed stent, containing 2.5–3.3 × 105 BMSCs, which were wrapped around the venous graft. After surgical recovery, all rats received daily subcutaneous heparin saline injections (800 U/kg).

To evaluate graft vessel status, Doppler color ultrasound (Esaote, MyLabSixVET) was performed to measure its patency, inner diameter, and peak systolic velocity (PSV). In short, an 18 MHz probe (Esaote, SL3235) was used at the neck, with ultrasound depth 15 mm to examine the graft vessel. Its inner diameter was based on the average of the measurements taken at the 2 anastomotic sites, as well as the middle of the graft vein, while for PSV, it was based off of the average value of 3 repeated measurements taken from the middle of the graft vein. All measurements were carried out by a researcher blind to the surgery or treatments.

Establishing the in vitro model of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-pre-treated ECs co-cultured with SIRT3-BMSCs and measuring mitochondrial transferal

To establish the in vitro model, ECs were first seeded into 6-well plate, at 2 × 105/well, and incubated with 500 µg/mL LPS for 24 h. These ECs were co-cultured, at a 1:1 ratio with either SIRT3-BMSCs, or BMSCs, in F12-DMEM containing 5% FBS for 24 h. EC phenotypic changes were then identified by immunofluorescence staining for CD31 (ab231084, Abcam), CD144 (MA5-44146, Thermo Fisher), and α-SMA (CL-594-67735, Proteintech), and nuclei stained with DAPI. GFP+ cells were identified as BMSCs, and GFP− as ECs.

The occurrence of mitochondrial transfer between ECs and BMSCs were identified using Mito Tracker™ Deep Red FM (M22426, Invitrogen) to stain BMSCs, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, transduced BMSCs were incubated with 100 nM Mito Tracker™ Deep Red FM solution for 30 min, then co-cultured with ECs at a 1:1 ratio for 24 h. Flow cytometry was then carried out, in which BMSCs were GFP+Deep Red+, while ECs with mitochondria transferred from BMSCs were only Deep Red+. F-actin (ab176759, Abcam) staining was also used to detect TNTs post-BMSC/EC co-culture, and TNT/50 cells were calculated.

Protein extraction and western blot

Cells were lysed in 300 µL RIPA buffer (SW104-02, SevenBio) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (SW106-01, SevenBio) for 20 min in 4 °C, followed by placement on ice for 30 min, to extract total protein. After centrifugation at 8000×g for 10 min, the supernatant was obtained; 25 µg of protein was loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE for electrophoresis separation, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, and incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: SIRT3 (10099-1-AP, Proteintech), VASP (ab47268, Abcam), and eNOS (85020-4-RR, Proteintech). Afterwards, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein expression was detected by chemiluminescence substrate, using the BIO-RAD ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System.

Identifying SIRT3 interactions with VASP by immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation was conducted by obtaining 3 × 105 passage 3 transduced BMSCs and SIRT3-BMSCs, then lysing them, as described in the protein extraction and Western blot procedure above. Lysates were cleared with centrifugation at 500×g for 1 min, and supernatant was obtained for immunoprecipitation, in which 5 µL anti-SIRT3 or VASP antibodies (experimental groups), or 5 µL IgG (control group) were added, and incubated overnight, with gentle inversion, at 4 °C. Afterwards, 50 µL protein A/G magnetic beads were added to each group, and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. The resulting immunoprecipitation-bead complexes were collected by centrifugation at 2000×g for 5 min in 4 °C, then washed 5 times with 500 µL pre-cooled immunoprecipitation lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation at 2000×g for 5 min in 4 °C. Subsequently, an equal volume of reduced loading buffer was added to the complexes, and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min for eluting. The resulting elution was obtained by centrifugation at 2000×g for 5 min in 4 °C, and was used for SIRT3, VASP or acetylation (pan-acetylation antibodies, 66289-1-Ig, Proteintech) detection by immunoblotting, following the Western blot procedure above.

Histological analyses

For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining, rats were euthanized with 5% isoflurane in O2 (flow rate 1 L/min), and an intravenous injection of 10% KCl (1 ml), followed by excision of the venous grafts, immediately fixed with 10% formalin at room temperature, dehydrated in increasing percentages of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Coronally sectioned tissues (5 μm) were deparaffinized, hydrated, then stained with either H&E or Masson’s trichrome. These sections were washed in DI water, dehydrated in increasing percentages of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted onto slides. Each slide was examined by light microscopy (LEICA, DMi1).

For evaluating cell proliferation, infiltration, and CD31, venous graft tissue sections were de-paraffinized, hydrated, and stained with proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA; ab29, Abcam), CD11b, (ab133357, Abcam) or CD31 primary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Afterwards, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine, and examined by light microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean±standard deviation. GraphPad Prism 10 software was used for statistical analysis. Comparisons between 2 groups were analyzed with unpaired Student’s t-test (2-tailed), while comparisons among 3 or more groups were carried out using either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or repeated measures ANOVA for repeated measures over time, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Establishing the jugular vein-common carotid artery arteriovenous graft for a rat CABG model

We selected the rat jugular vein as the venous graft, which was anastomosed to both ends of the severed common carotid artery to simulate venous graft restenosis in CABG. Restenosis development in the venous graft was evaluated over an 8-week study period by Doppler color ultrasound to measure venous graft patency, inner diameter, and PSV. It was observed that throughout the study period, venous graft diameter decreased up to ~ 54% relative to the proximal and distal native arteries (Fig. 1a, b), signifying the progressive narrowing of the vessel. On the other hand, there was a concomitant stepwise increase in PSV throughout the study period, which indicated increased resistance to blood flow (Fig. 1c, d). Furthermore, histological analysis with H&E staining found that the lumen/vascular area ratio significantly decreased over the 8-week period, with the neointima accounting for 22.33 ± 3.02% of the venous graft area at 8 weeks (Fig. 1e, f). Additionally, Masson’s trichrome staining for collagen deposition showed significant increases in neointimal/vascular collagen, with neointimal collagen deposition accounting for 36.922 ± 6.5% of the venous graft area at 8 weeks post-CABG (Fig. 1g, h). All this evidence thus illustrates the successful establishment of the rat CABG model, with a venous graft that developed restenosis over the 8-week period.

3D bioprinting enabled the construction of the SIRT3-BMSC-seeded HAMA/GelMA extravascular stent

HAMA and GelMA were chosen as the materials to construct the extravascular stent, owing to the favorable structural stability of HAMA [26, 28–30], as well as the good biocompatibility of GelMA. To identify the optimal proportions for these materials, 0.5, 1, or 1.5% HAMA were mixed with 5% GelMA (0.5% HAMA + 5% GelMA, 1% HAMA + 5% GelMA, 1.5% HAMA + 5% GelMA) for 3D bioprinting, and all stent formulations demonstrated a uniform mesh-like structure (Fig. S4a, b). However, the stent with the highest HAMA proportion, at 1.5%, had the lowest swelling rate (Fig. S4c). As for material degradation, all 3 stent formulations tested had degraded to varying degrees, though none of them lost 35% of the original weight over 4 weeks of degradation (Fig. S4d). With respect to mechanical properties, the materials must be able to endure the rat arterial blood pressure of 85–115 mmHg (about 11.3–15.3 kPa). We found that the compression modulus was 13.80 ± 0.148 kPa for 0.5% HAMA + 5% GelMA, 16.55 ± 0.178 kPa for 1% HAMA + 5% GelMA, and 23.68 ± 0.268 kPa for 1.5% HAMA + 5% GelMA; therefore, it significantly increased with increasing HAMA concentrations (Fig. S4e), and both 1% HAMA + 5% GelMA and 1.5% HAMA + 5% GelMA met the requirements of rat arterial blood pressure. However, considering possible deviations between the compression modulus in a cylinder, versus that of the manufactured extravascular stent, 1.5% HAMA + 5% GelMA, possessing a higher compression modulus, was selected for subsequent experiments.

BMSCs or SIRT3-BMSCs were mixed with HAMA and GelMA, and 2 extravascular stents were made with 3D bioprinting (Fig. 2a). Calcein/PI was used to detect cell viability (Fig. 2b, c), and CCK-8 for cell proliferation (Fig. 2d), on days 1, 4, and 7. For cell viability, we found > 90% cell survival for both stents throughout the period, though SIRT3-BMSC had significantly higher survival, compared to BMSC, on days 4 and 7 (Fig. 2b, c). As for cell proliferation, it increased over the 7-day study period, and SIRT3-BMSC had significantly higher proliferation than BMSC on days 4 and 7 (Fig. 2d). We also carried out live-cell tracking in vivo, via luciferase labeling of BMSCs, and the extravascular stent with these BMSCs was applied to the venous graft. Even though bioluminescence signaling had decreased from weeks 1 to 4, it was still relatively strong during the 4-week study period, indicating good BMSC survival over time (Fig. 2e, f). All these findings thus confirmed that the extravascular stent, both in vitro and in vivo, provided a biocompatible matrix to support BMSC survival.

SIRT3-BMSC-seeded HAMA/GelMA extravascular stent prevented neointimal formation and restenosis in vivo

To evaluate the effect of the extravascular stents on long-term venous graft patency, we established 4 experimental groups, of which rats were randomly assigned, based on a computer-generated random number sequence: Control (with only graft), Stent, BMSC+stent, and SIRT3-BMSC+stent. Doppler color ultrasound showed that inner diameters decreased throughout the 8-week period for all groups. However, from week 4, SIRT3-BMSC+stent had the greatest inner diameter, compared to the other 3 groups. Additionally, in week 8, BMSC+stent had a significantly greater inner diameter, compared to Control (Fig. 3a, b), but still smaller than that of the proximal and distal native arteries (~ 0.82–0.93 mm); however, SIRT3-BMSC+stent had a similar diameter to those arteries. On the other hand, PSV increased throughout the 8-week period for all 4 groups. Nevertheless, from 4 weeks, Control had the highest, and SIRT3-BMSC+stent, the lowest PSV (Fig. 3c, d). These findings were further supported by histological analyses with H&E staining, in which the lumen/vascular area ratio was significantly higher in Stent, compared to Control; additionally, BMSC+stent and SIRT3-BMSC+stent were significantly higher than Stent and Control, throughout the 8-week period (Fig. 4a, b). As for neointimal/vascular collagen, Control had the highest amount of collagen deposition, compared to the other 3 groups, from 4 weeks (Fig. 4c, d). Moreover, in 8 weeks, Control had the greatest amount of collagen deposition, followed by, respectively, Stent, BMSC+stent, and SIRT3-BMSC+stent (Fig. 4c, d). Therefore, the SIRT3-BMSC-seeded HAMA/GelMA extravascular stent was the most effective in preventing venous graft restenosis and maintaining patency, as it was associated with the greatest inner diameter, lowest PSV and collagen deposition, as well as a high lumen/vascular area ratio.

As surgical trauma and arterial blood flow could trigger venous graft inflammation, we conducted immunohistochemical staining for CD11b, representing inflammatory cell infiltration, on days 1, 4, and 7 post-CABG. We found that on days 4 and 7, Control had a significantly greater CD11b+ intimal area, compared to BMSC+stent and SIRT3-BMSC+stent groups (Fig. 5c, d). Additionally, on day 7, CD11b+ was significantly greater for Stent than for SIRT3-BMSC+stent (Fig. 5c, d). Therefore, SIRT3-BMSC+stent was able to lower inflammatory cell infiltration, which likely contributes to the subsequent reduction in neointimal proliferation and increase in long-term venous graft patency.

Surgical trauma and arterial blood flow could also cause venous graft endothelial damage, leading to increased neointimal proliferation, and subsequently, restenosis. Therefore, we investigated whether the extravascular stents could alleviate endothelial damage, via immunohistochemical analyses for endothelial marker CD31. We found that on day 14 post-CABG, Control had the lowest CD31+ intimal area, followed by Stent, BMSC+stent, and SIRT3-BMSC+stent (Fig. 5e, f), indicating that the SIRT3-BMSC-seeded HAMA/GelMA stent could promote the re-endothelialization of the venous graft, which, along with lowering inflammatory cell infiltration, could contribute to reducing neointimal formation and preventing venous graft restenosis.

SIRT3-BMSC co-culture with LPS-treated ECs alleviated EC damage in vitro

To further confirm whether SIRT3-BMSCs could alleviate endothelial damage, we established an in vitro model, in which ECs were pre-treated with LPS to induce EC injury. Immunofluorescence staining showed that LPS-treated (damaged) ECs, compared to untreated, had significantly lower CD31 and CD144, along with higher, though not statistically significant, α-SMA expression levels, representing EndMT (Fig. 6a, b). These damaged ECs were then co-cultured with BMSCs and SIRT3-BMSCs, in which damaged ECs alone had the lowest CD31 and CD144 expression levels, while ECs co-cultured with SIRT3-BMSC had the highest, and those co-cultured with BMSC in-between (Fig. 6c, d). Conversely, α-SMA expression levels were the highest for damaged ECs alone, and the lowest for ECs co-cultured with SIRT3-BMSCs, with those co-cultured with BMSCs in-between (Fig. 6c, d). Therefore, SIRT3-BMSCs were the most effective in restoring LPS-treated ECs towards that of a normal EC phenotype, and counteracting against EndMT, represented by the highest CD31 and CD144, along with the lowest α-SMA expression levels.

Mitochondrial transfer from SIRT3-BMSCs to LPS-treated ECs was facilitated by SIRT3 deacetylation of VASP to promote TNT formation

One potential underlying mechanism behind SIRT3-BMSC co-culture with LPS-treated ECs being able to improve EC phenotypes may be mitochondrial transfer from SIRT3-BMSCs to damaged ECs. To confirm whether this has taken place, mitochondria in BMSCs and SIRT3-BMSCs were pre-labelled with Deep Red before co-culture with damaged ECs. Therefore, under immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry analyses, GFP+Deep Red+ represented BMSCs or SIRT3-BMSCs, while Deep Red+ alone (GFP−Deep Red+) represented ECs that had BMSC mitochondria transferred to them. Indeed, Fig. 7a provides a representative immunofluorescence image depicting such BMSC mitochondrial transferal to ECs. This was further confirmed by flow cytometry analyses, in which ECs co-cultured with SIRT3-BMSCs had significantly higher mitochondrial transfer from BMSCs, in the form of greater numbers of GFP−Deep Red+ ECs, compared to ECs co-cultured with BMSCs (Fig. 7b, c).

We then examined whether TNTs, which enable intercellular mitochondrial transfer, were formed between co-cultured BMSCs/SIRT3-BMSCs and damaged ECs, via immunofluorescence staining for F-actin; TNTs were indicated by the presence of cellular extensions that were F-actin+Deep Red+ (White arrows, Fig. 7d). We quantified the number of TNTs per 50 cells and found that the SIRT3-BMSCs-damaged ECs co-culture group (SIRT3-BMSCs) had significantly more TNTs from BMSCs than the BMSC-damaged EC co-culture group (BMSCs; Fig. 7e). From these observations, we then further investigated the role of SIRT3 on TNT formation, namely via its interactions with VASP. Western blot analysis showed that SIRT3-BMSCs, compared to BMSCs, not only had SIRT3 overexpression, but also increased VASP expression (Fig. 7f, g). Furthermore, immunoprecipitation analyses found that SIRT3 co-precipitated with VASP in SIRT3-BMSCs (Fig. 7h). Moreover, SIRT3-BMSCs had greater deacetylation of VASP, as shown by lowered pan-acetylation co-precipitation with VASP (Fig. 7i, j). All these findings thus suggested that SIRT3-BMSCs were able to alleviate endothelial damage, via SIRT3 deacetylating VASP, leading to TNTs formation and mitochondrial transfer from SIRT3-BMSCs to damaged ECs.

Discussion

The main therapies to treat coronary artery disease include drug therapy, percutaneous coronary intervention, and CABG. However, each method has its own limitations, such as drug therapy being less able to alleviate established vascular lesions, percutaneous coronary intervention causing mechanical damage to blood vessels, leading to intimal hyperplasia and stent thrombosis, as well as venous graft restenosis in CABG. Owing to these limitations, stem cell therapies have become a topic of great interest. Some of the most effective potential therapies involves BMSCs, as they not only have paracrine capabilities to participate in immune regulation, inflammatory response, and other cell processes, but are also able to deliver organelles to other damaged cells, such as lysosomes and mitochondria [39, 40]. BMSCs applications, however, have been limited by the shortcomings of existing delivery routes, including intravenous and in situ injections, all resulting in insufficient cell survival. Consequently, tissue engineering approaches, using ECM materials and 3D printing, have been devised, as they are able to improve the microenvironment for stem cell survival, as well as providing a 3D growth space and mechanical support for stem cells [41]. On this basis, we produced, by combining HAMA and GelMA, followed by seeding with SIRT3-BMSCs, a 3D bio-printed matrix stent. With this stent, our study made multiple innovations: (1) Applying this SIRT-BMSCs-seeded HAMA/GelMA matrix as an extravascular stent, (2) Demonstrating the effectiveness of this stent for inhibiting neointimal formation and promoting long-term venous graft patency, and (3) Highlighting the potential underlying mechanisms, which may involve SIRT3 deacetylating VASP, leading to increased TNT formation and mitochondrial transfer from SIRT3-BMSCs to damaged ECs to improve their function. Therefore, forming this extravascular stent could serve as a novel approach for maintaining long-term venous graft patency, as it combines the benefits of stem cells and tissue engineering.

A number of studies have previously been conducted regarding stem cell transplantation to maintain venous graft patency, as well as 3D bioprinting to produce extravascular stents. One study involved promoting venous graft ECs functional recovery, via intravenous human umbilical cord vein MSCs injection, which was able to improve venous graft long-term patency. The underlying mechanism behind such an improvement is through paracrine signals, which activate the ERK1/2 and AKT pathways, in turn enhancing ECs proliferation, migration and tube formation [42]. Furthermore, intravenously injected stem cells could migrate to the injured site and differentiate into functional ECs [43]. Additionally, these stem cells could exert directional differentiation and paracrine effects to inhibit venous graft neointimal formation [44, 45]. Therefore, applying stem cells to maintain venous graft patency could serve as a viable treatment approach in CABG. However, intravenous stem cell injections have multiple limitations, one of which is that they could accumulate in tissues and organs outside the target of interest; another is the low survival rate of those cells at the injury site [46]. As a result, the optimal effects of stem cell therapies are unable to be achieved, and developing matrices to directly situate stem cells at the injury site has become an attractive approach.

In this regard, the usage of 3D bioprinting to devise a carrier for delivering BMSCs has become a potential strategy for improving long-term venous graft patency. Therefore, HAMA and GelMA were used as an ECM scaffold for BMSCs, as it was able to provide a suitable survival microenvironment, along with mechanical properties for supporting the venous grafts. This cell-seeded extravascular stent not only was able to protect venous grafts from continual endothelial damage, stemming from arterial pressure, but also provided the beneficial effects of BMSCs in a stable, continuous manner, ultimately improving long-term venous graft patency. It is also worth noting, though, that the selection of matrix materials for 3D bioprinting is crucial for successful stem cell therapy. Currently, natural matrix materials able to be used for 3D bioprinting include sodium alginate, gelatin, fibrin/fibrinogen, hyaluronic acid, agarose, and silk fibroin [47, 48]. Furthermore, combining different matrix materials with different characteristics could yield a material possessing the advantages of each component. Additionally, the natural matrix materials could be chemically modified with different chemical groups to obtain desirable properties. Indeed, in this study, we modified hyaluronic acid and gelatin, by photopolymerization and methacryloylation, to obtain, respectively, HAMA and GelMA. These materials not only possess the favorable biocompatibility of hyaluronic acid and gelatin, but are also able to be cured under 405 nm wavelength blue light [49]. Moreover, their mechanical properties were able to be adjusted, via altering blue light irradiation times and material concentrations, to render them suitable for application as an extravascular stent. Furthermore, compared to synthetic materials, such as polylactic acid (PLA), HAMA/GelMA have excellent biocompatibility and low cytotoxicity [28]. GelMA has also been found to possess cell binding motifs, such as RGD sequences, allowing cell attachment, proliferation and differentiation; these processes are further augmented by adding HAMA, as it is able to provide a hydrated environment for these cells [28, 50]. The addition of methacryloyl to both gelatin and hyaluronic acid enables precise control over pore sizes, stiffness, and elasticity of these materials, allowing them to more closely mimic vascular tissue properties [28, 50]. Such properties are not available for other potential synthetic polymer matrix materials, such as polyglycolic acid, PLA, and polycaprolactone, which are the 3 most commonly used [51], as they are less able to support cell attachment [50].

The usage of an extravascular stent is based on previous observations that continual pressure from arterial blood flow was the primary cause of venous graft restenosis. The chosen materials for those stents have evolved from metals to biomaterials, such as polycaprolactone, silk protein, and sodium alginate [52–56]. However, extravascular stents constructed from simple biomaterials, even though they are able, to a certain extent, to limit neointimal formation, are unable to alleviate venous graft damage that is already present. For instance, a recently developed technology is the Venous External Support (VEST™, Vascular Graft Solutions, Tel Aviv, Israel), which provides external support to saphenous vein grafts (SVG). It has been found, in a meta-analysis of 4 randomized clinical trials, to lower intimal hyperplasia, compared to non-stented SVG controls [57], but it did not lower graft occlusion or repeat revascularization after up to 1.5 years of follow-up post CABG [58]. This may be owed to VEST, comprising of an external cobalt-chrome support, being more oriented towards mitigating disease progression, rather than preventing early graft failure [57]. Therefore, alternative strategies to avoid early graft failure have been examined, such as tissue-engineered vascular grafts (TEVGs). Traditional methods for TEVGs, using natural/synthetic polymers, or de-cellularized tissue scaffolds that could be seeded with cells to form vessel-like conduits, have shown promising long-term outcomes for medium- and large-diameter arterial grafts, but have poor patency for smaller-diameter vessels, such as coronary arteries [59]. Moreover, these scaffolds could trigger complications, such as inflammation, thrombosis and rejection, which, along with the challenges of emulating the properties of native ECM components to obtain optimal cell-cell interactions and alignments, have resulted in the development of cell-based, scaffold-free techniques. These techniques are mainly in the form of tissue engineering by self-assembly (TESA) [60, 61], such as devising cell sheets that could be assembled around a rod to form small-diameter vessel-like structures [62]. One such scaffold-free technique was pioneered by L’Heureux et al. [63], based on replicating the 3 layers of the blood vessel: functional endothelium seeded onto an “internal membrane” (IM), “media” made of SMC, and “adventitia” made of human skin fibroblasts. The IM was first devised by rolling a fibroblast sheet around an inert cylindrical support to form a homogeneous tissue. It was devitalized via dehydration, followed by rolling a SMC sheet around the IM to form the medial layer. The adventitial layer was formed by rolling a sheet of fibroblasts around the SMCs. Following the maturation period, the cylindrical support was removed, and lumen lined with endothelial cells [63]. The resulting TESA conduit was used as an arterio-venous (AV) shunt for hemo-dialysis access in a clinical trial, which yielded promising results, with 78% of patients maintaining primary patency after 1-month-, and 60% after 6-months post-implantation [62].

Our work thus builds upon previous findings by developing a 3D bio-printed matrix stent, comprising of HAMA/GelMA seeded with SIRT3-overexpressing BMSCs. Its advantages are multi-fold, in that HAMA provides favorable structural stability that could resist the continual pressure from arterial blood flow, which is the primary cause of venous graft restenosis, while GelMA provided an ideal matrix for cell attachment. Furthermore, BMSCs could alleviate venous graft damage, and their effects were augmented by overexpressing SIRT3, which has been found in our previous study to yield BMSCs with better resistance to oxidative stress and apoptosis. This is mostly due to SIRT3 being involved in multiple biological processes via deacetylating histones. Furthermore, SIRT3 can be localized to the mitochondria, as it can possess a mitochondrial targeting sequence, where it is involved in almost all aspects of mitochondrial biology, including tricarboxylic acid cycle, lipid metabolism, redox balance, and mitochondrial dynamics regulation [64, 65]. In fact, in this study, we found that SIRT3 deacetylated VASP, which promoted TNT formation, mitochondrial transfer from BMSCs to ECs, and ultimately, improved ECs function.

There are a number of limitations in this study, one of which is that this is a proof-of-concept study. Therefore, the usage of the lentiviral vector should take into consideration the biosafety and regulatory requirements for clinical applications. Moreover, as genetically modified BMSCs are used, caution should be taken for potential secondary side effects. Another limitation is that the presence of the SIRT3-VASP-TNT axis is only tentatively and indirectly identified, via in vitro co-culture analyses between SIRT3-BMSCs and ECs. A third limitation is that unlike the typical CABG procedure, which applied saphenous vein grafts to the coronary arteries, this study used a jugular vein graft on the common carotid artery and only analyzed an 8-week follow-up period. The usage of the rat jugular–carotid model, which is surgically difficult to perform, along with limited patient numbers for BMSCs, also resulted in relatively small sample sizes in this study. Therefore, future studies will use larger sample sizes, with longer follow-up periods beyond 8 weeks, to validate our findings. Additionally, rescue experiments, such as VASP knockdown, should be carried out to directly validate the presence of the SIRT3-VASP-TNT axis. For potential future clinical applications, the lentiviral vector should be replaced with the FDA approved adeno-associated vector and the potential risks of genetically modified MSCs (ex. tumorigenicity, off-target effects) should be addressed. Studies should also be conducted to examine the applicability of this extravascular stent in a large animal model, such as a bovine or porcine model.

Conclusions

We successfully constructed an extravascular stent, comprising of HAMA and GelMA seeded with SIRT3-overexpressing BMSCs. This stent was able to inhibit venous graft restenosis, in the form of increasing the graft inner diameter, lower PSV, and inhibiting neointimal formation. This may be due to the anti-inflammatory effects of the stent, along with inhibiting nucleated cell proliferation and restoring damaged endothelium integrity. All of these effects likely stem from SIRT3 deacetylating VASP, leading to TNT formation, BMSC-to-EC mitochondrial transfer, and overall, ECs functional improvements. This extravascular stent could thus serve as a novel intervention to improve long-term CABG venous graft patency.
