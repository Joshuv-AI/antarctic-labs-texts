Multi-omics insights into the microbiota-gut-brain axis and cognitive improvement post-bariatric surgery
Authors: Qiaoyuan Xiang, Minhua Yu, Qi Cai, Mengjie Hu, Bo Rao, Xin Liang, Zhenxing Liu, Yu Xie, Kuan Cen, Renwei Zhang, Haibo Xu, Yumin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough numerous studies have shown that bariatric surgery results in sustained weight loss and modifications in gut microbiota composition and cognitive function, the exact underlying mechanisms are unclear. This study aimed to investigate the effects of bariatric surgery on cognitive function through the microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA).MethodsDemographic data, serum samples, fecal samples, cognitive assessment scales, and resting-state functional connectivity magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) scans were obtained from 39 obese patients before and after (6 months) laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG). PCA analysis, OPLS-DA analysis, and permutation tests were used to conduct fecal 16 S microbiota profiling, serum metabolomics, and neuroimaging analyses, and a bariatric surgery-specific rs-fMRI brain functional connectivity network was constructed. Spearman correlation analysis and Co-inertia analysis were employed to correlate significant alterations in cognitive assessment scales and resting-state functional connectivity difference networks with differential serum metabolites and 16 S microbiota data to identify key gut microbiota and serum metabolic factors.ResultsLSG significantly reduced the weight of obese patients, with reductions of up to 28%. Furthermore, cognitive assessment scale measurements revealed that LSG enhanced cognitive functions, including memory (HVLT, p = 0.000) and executive function (SCWT, p = 0.008). Also, LSG significantly altered gut microbiota composition (p = 0.001), with increased microbial abundance and diversity (p < 0.05). Moreover, serum metabolite levels were significantly altered, revealing intergroup differences in 229 metabolites mapped to 72 metabolic pathways (p < 0.05, VIP > 1). Spearman correlation analysis among cognitive assessment scales, gut microbiota species, and serum metabolites revealed correlations with 68 gut microbiota species and 138 serum metabolites (p < 0.05). Furthermore, pairwise correlations were detected between gut microbiota and serum metabolites (p < 0.05). Functional neuroimaging analysis revealed that LSG increased functional connectivity in cognitive-related frontotemporal networks (FPN, p < 0.01). Additionally, normalization of the default mode network (DMN) and salience network (SN) connectivity was observed after LSG (p < 0.001). Further canonical correlation and correlation analysis suggested that the cognitive-related brain network changes induced by LSG were associated with key gut microbiota species (Akkermansia, Blautia, Collinsella, Phascolarctobacterium, and Ruminococcus, p < 0.05) and neuroactive metabolites (Glycine, L-Serine, DL-Dopa, SM (d18:1/24:1(15Z), p < 0.05).ConclusionThese findings indicate the pathophysiological role of the microbiota-gut-brain axis in enhancing cognitive function after bariatric surgery, and the study provides a basis for clinical dietary adjustments, probiotic supplementation, and guidance for bariatric surgery, but further research is still needed.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2100049403. Background

Obesity is a chronic condition characterized by a body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 kg/m², according to the World Health Organization. Obesity has become a global epidemic with increasing prevalence. More than 51% of the global population, or over 400 million people, may be overweight or obese in the next 12 years, according to the World Obesity Federation’s World Obesity Atlas 2023. Besides, obesity and its associated comorbidities are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in developed countries [1]. Besides the well-known metabolic complications, obesity can lead to structural brain changes, cognitive impairment, and neurodegenerative diseases [2]. Studies have demonstrated that weight gain is directly related to cognitive impairment [3]. Therefore, achieving sustainable long-term weight loss is crucial for reducing the incidence of obesity, cognitive impairment, and neurodegenerative diseases [4]. The present mainstream treatments for obesity include dietary modifications, lifestyle changes, pharmacotherapy, and bariatric surgery. However, bariatric surgery is one of the few effective methods widely used to attain significant and sustained weight loss. Bariatric surgeries, such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) and laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) [5], can improve immediate and delayed memory and enhance executive function [6, 7] in obese patients in both the short and long term. However, the exact mechanisms underlying the improvement of neurocognitive function following weight loss surgery are unclear.

The bidirectional interaction between the brain and the gut microbiota, the microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA), has attracted much attention in the fields of obesity and neuropsychiatry, explaining the improvement in cognitive function following weight loss surgery. Recent studies have shown that bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract can interact with the brain through the gut-brain axis, thus significantly impacting cognitive function [8]. The microbiota and the brain communicate through various pathways, including the immune system, tryptophan metabolism, vagus nerve, and enteric nervous system. This communication involves various microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, branched-chain amino acids, and peptidoglycans [9]. Recent reviews have also summarized some bacterial metabolites with known or suspected neuroregulatory activity, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as organic acids, tryptophan, and its derivatives, precursors of neurotransmitters (glutamate), hydroxy fatty acids and sphingolipids involved in lipid metabolism [10, 11]. Chen et al. found that weight loss surgery alters the composition of the gut microbiota and regulates cognitive function, possibly through the mediation of the gut microbiota-brain axis by increasing neurotransmitters and modulating inflammatory cytokines [12, 13]. Furthermore, blood acts as a transporter for metabolites, serving as a bridge for cross-talk between the brain and the gut microbiota. Studies have indicated that weight loss surgery changes serum metabolites, including branched-chain amino acids and choline metabolites. These changes are associated with alterations in the microbiota composition [14, 15].

Furthermore, several studies investigating the resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) studies have utilized cognitive scales such as the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test (HVLT) and the Stroop Color and Word Test (SCWT) in conjunction with differential analysis of resting-state signals within regions of interest (ROI) to detect obesity-related cognitive impairments, such as impaired decision-making, inhibitory control, learning and memory, and attention [16–19]. Obesity negatively impacts brain function and structure, especially in the fronto-limbic circuitry. Bariatric surgery significantly affects cognitive function by altering the function and structure of the fronto-limbic circuitry [20]. The aforementioned studies suggest that weight loss surgery, including LSG, can result in sustained and significant changes in gut microbiota, cognitive function, and brain signaling. However, the precise relationship between these changes and brain signaling is unknown.

While the MGBA is acknowledged for its potential influence on cognitive function, the precise mechanisms remain poorly understood. To elucidate these mechanisms, further multidimensional research and validation in larger, more diverse populations are necessary. This study employs a multi-omics approach aimed to comprehensively explore the components of the MGBA and elucidate how the gut microbiota influence host metabolism and neural networks through various analyses, including the use of cognitive assessment scales, gut microbiota analysis, serum metabolome analysis, and resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) analysis, thus showing the pathophysiological basis of cognitive changes in obesity.

Materials and methods

Patient screening and study design

A total of 39 obese patients who underwent LSG at the Weight Loss Center of Zhongnan Hospital between August 19, 2021, and November 5, 2021, were enrolled in this prospective observational study (Supplementary Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients aged between 18 and 55 years scheduled to undergo weight loss surgery; (2) patients meeting the criteria specified in the guidelines for obesity surgery; (3) right-handed patients; (4) patients willing to undergo neuropsychological testing and neuroimaging examinations. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with a history of neurological disorders or severe mental illness; (2) patients with a history of clear secondary and drug-induced obesity and diagnosed genetic obesity; (3) pregnant or lactating women; (4) individuals who could not cooperate with examinations; (5) patients with a history of use of antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics within three months before the study or at any time during the study. The samples and clinical data were collected after obtaining informed consent from each patient. The outcomes of obese patients before and after surgery were compared based on multiple parameters. This study followed the Helsinki Declaration guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Zhongnan Hospital, Wuhan University.

Patient information and cognitive questionnaires

Height, weight, BMI, and circumferences (neck, chest, abdominal, and hip circumferences) were recorded at baseline and 6 months post-surgery. Various cognitive assessment scales, including the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test (HVLT) [21], Verbal Fluency Test (VFT) [22], Digit Span Test (DGS) [23], Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) [24], and Stroop Color and Word Test (SCWT) [25] were used. These assessments were conducted by trained cognitive testing personnel strictly following established guidelines in a controlled environment free from external interference. The personnel underwent extensive training by experienced clinical psychologists, which included multiple practice sessions with non-participants to ensure the accuracy and consistency of cognitive assessments.

HVLT [21] comprises three learning trials, recall trials, and delayed recall trials (recording the number of correct responses), and it is widely used to assess verbal learning and memory abilities. The testing procedure involves word reading and repetition, as well as recall and recognition tasks at various time points. VFT [22] assesses language abilities, semantic memory, and executive functions. In VFT, participants are instructed to verbally produce as many words as possible within a given time frame for three categories: vegetables, fruits, and animals. The number of words generated in each category is scored individually and then summed to obtain a total score. DGS is used to assess verbal short-term memory [23]. In DGS, participants are required to memorize a sequence of digits and repeat them in either the same (subtest A) or reverse (subtest B) order. The maximum length of digits successfully repeated determines the final score. SDMT [24] is a cognitive assessment based on symbol-digit matching. SDMT evaluates visual and spatial working memory, processing speed, and executive function, where participants are instructed to pair digits with corresponding symbols within a given time frame. The total number of correct pairings is the final score. SCWT [25] assesses cognitive resistance to interference, and it involves sequentially presenting three cards (A, B, and C) with increasing difficulty levels. Manual timing and counting are used to quantify the completion time and accuracy of each card type. Key measures for assessing Stroop interference effects include single-word time, single-color time, double-word time, and double-color time. This evaluation was based on the average number of correct responses and the average completion time across all tests.

Sample collection

The fecal and serum samples were collected one week before surgery and six months post-surgery. Briefly, the patients were instructed to collect mid-segment fecal samples using a sampling tube. The samples were transported to the laboratory on dry ice, divided into two parts, and stored in 2 ml cryovials. Blood samples were collected in heparinized vacutainer tubes in the early morning after an overnight fast, with samples exhibiting hemolysis or lipemic interference excluded. The blood samples were allowed to settle, then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min (room temperature). The supernatant was then aliquoted into two separate tubes. Any serum samples showing any signs of hemolysis were excluded. The fecal and serum samples were immediately placed in liquid nitrogen after collection for 15 min and subsequently stored at -80 °C.

DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the MagPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MAGEN, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR products were purified with AMpure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA). To minimize potential contamination, blank controls were included in the preparation of DNA extraction and library, and all procedures were performed with nucleic acid-free reagents. The samples were quality-checked using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) and analyzed using the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, California, USA), but technical replicates were not performed.

Sequencing data analysis

Paired-end reads were generated after Illumina sequencing. The reads were merged based on overlapping information using FLASH software (Fast Length Adjustment of Short Reads, v1.2.11) to generate specific tags. Low-quality tags were removed using Trimmomatic software (v0.33), and about 75,000 clean tags were obtained per sample. Chimeric sequences were identified and excluded by comparing them with the existing chimera database, the gold database (v20110519). The merged tags with a similarity of over 97% were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) using USEARCH (v7.0.1090). The chimeric sequences were further filtered using UCHIME software (v4.2.40) against the gold database. Species annotation was performed by aligning with the Silva database (www.arb-silva.de). OTUs were then assigned to different phylogenetic levels. Alpha diversity and the richness and diversity of bacteria in each sample were determined using R (v3.2.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). This software was also used to plot rarefaction curves and rank abundance curves for structural and cluster analysis. Additionally, beta diversity (diversity between samples) was calculated using the ggplot package in R (v3.4.1). The differences in diversity before and after surgery were analyzed using Analysis of Similarity (ANOSIM). Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSE) analysis at the genus level generated cladograms, while Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) effect size analysis produced dendrograms, both aimed at identifying and quantifying the impact of key gut microbes before and after surgery.

LC-MS serum processing

First, 100 µL of serum was transferred into an Eppendorf tube, followed by the addition of 400 µL of an extraction solvent (methanol in a 1:1 ratio, V/V) containing an isotopically labeled internal standard mixture. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, then subjected to ultrasonication in an ice-water bath for 10 min. The sample was subsequently incubated at -40 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C (centrifugal radius of 8.6 cm) for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred into a sample vial for further analysis. Equal volumes of supernatant from all samples were combined to form a quality control (QC) sample.

LC-MS metabolomics data acquisition

The samples underwent chromatographic separation using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase A comprised an aqueous solution containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia, whereas phase B consisted of acetonitrile. The sample tray temperature was maintained at 4 °C, with an injection volume of 2 µL. Mass spectrometry data, including primary and secondary data, were acquired using the Thermo Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer through Xcalibur software (Thermo). The detailed parameters were as follows: Sheath gas flow rate: 30 Arb, Aux gas flow rate: 25 Arb, Capillary temperature: 350 °C, Full MS resolution: 120,000, MS/MS resolution: 7500, Collision energy: 10/30/60 in NCE mode, Spray Voltage: 3.6 kV (positive) or -3.2 kV (negative).

LC-MS metabolomics data analysis

The raw MS data were converted into mzXML format using ProteoWizard software and processed with XCMS. Data preprocessing involved peak identification, alignment, extraction, retention time correction, and integration to generate a three-dimensional data matrix. The data were then matched against a custom-built secondary mass spectral database in BiotreeDB (V2.1) for substance annotation (cutoff value for algorithm scoring: 0.3). To ensure stability in data acquisition, the peak height variations of internal standards across QC samples were assessed, along with their distribution in PCA plots and the presence of peaks in blank samples. Data with missing values exceeding 50% in any group were excluded. For remaining data with missing entries, imputation was conducted using half the minimum value observed across the dataset. Finally, normalization was performed on each sample under both positive and negative ion modes using the internal standard (IS) method.

The normalized data were imported into SIMCA (V16.0.2, Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden) for multivariate statistical analysis. Principal Component Analysis (PCA), an unsupervised multivariate pattern recognition analysis, was used to examine the intrinsic variation in the dataset. Classification discrimination was conducted using supervised projection to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). A permutation test (200 times) was conducted to avoid overfitting. Variables of Importance in Projection (VIP > 1) and Student’s t-test (p < 0.05) obtained from the OPLS-DA model were used to assess the significance of differences in metabolite abundance. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was used for pathway analysis.

High-resolution magnetic resonance imaging data acquisition and processing

Acquisition

Eleven of the 39 participants underwent MRI examinations 20 min before and six months after surgery, 25 participants did not attend the six-month post-surgery MRI examination, and 3 were excluded due to head movement and inadequate registration. The subjects underwent cranial MRI, including a 3DT1 sequence and a 10-minute rs-fMRI. Briefly, the subjects were instructed to remain still, keep their eyes closed, avoid falling asleep, and refrain from engaging in cognitive activities. A MAGNETOM Trio 3.0 T MR scanner (Siemens, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel head coil was used for baseline imaging before surgery. Resting-state functional images were acquired using a gradient echo planar imaging protocol with the following parameters: TR (repetition time) = 2000 ms, TE (echo time) = 30 ms, Flip Angle (FA) = 90 °, voxel size = 3.75 × 3.75 × 3.8 mm3, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2, number of slices = 33, slice thickness = 3.8 mm. The slices were interleaved, starting with odd slices. The same parameters were used during the examination after six months of surgery using 3.0 T GE Signa HDxt scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA) equipped with an eight-channel phased-array head coil except for the number of slices (34 in this case). In addition, the slices were interleaved, starting with even slices. High-resolution 3D T1-weighted images were acquired using BRAVO (Brain Volume Imaging) with the following parameters: TR = 8.5 ms, TE = 3.2 ms, Flip Angle (FA) = 12 degrees, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 isotropic, FOV = 240 × 240 mm2, number of slices = 288, slice thickness = 1 mm.

Data preprocessing and denoising

Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and the Rs-fMRI Data Analysis Toolkit (Gretna) were used for data preprocessing. The first 10 volumes of functional images were discarded to eliminate data acquired before equipment stabilization and account for signal equilibration and participants’ adaptation to scanning noise. Time correction for the remaining 229 volumes was conducted by aligning the signal acquisition timing of each slice with the middle acquisition time of each TR. Head motion correction was performed on the time series of each subject’s images using a six-parameter (rigid body) linear transformation based on the least squares method [26]. Notably, data from two participants were excluded from further analysis due to excessive head motion (> 3 mm or 3°). Individual structural images (T1-weighted MPRAGE) were then co-registered with the average functional image [27]. Data from one subject were excluded from further analysis due to poor registration results. A unified segmentation algorithm [28] was used to segment the transformed structural images into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. The functional volumes, after motion correction, were spatially normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute space using the normalization parameters estimated during the unified segmentation and resampled to 6 mm isotropic voxels. Spatial smoothing was then applied to the functional images using a [6 6 6] mm smoothing kernel. Finally, linear detrending and temporal filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) were applied to the time series of each voxel using the RS-fMRI Data Analysis Toolkit (REST plus, http://rest.restfmri.net) to reduce the effects of low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise [29, 30].

Regions of interest (ROI)

A whole-brain ROI-based resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) analysis was conducted, as described by Zhiming Li et al. [31]. This approach, which goes beyond the use of predefined ROIs from prior studies, provides a more comprehensive depiction of brain signal alterations following surgical intervention. The brain was divided into 136 different regions of interest (ROIs), excluding the cerebellum. This analysis included 91 bilateral cortical ROIs defined in the FSL Harvard-Oxford Atlas maximum likelihood cortical atlas and 15 bilateral subcortical ROIs defined in the FSL Harvard-Oxford Atlas subcortical atlas. Moreover, 30 additional ROIs were added based on the CONN algorithm network in the rsFC analysis, which were used for subsequent functional network construction. These ROIs were defined from the analysis of the Human Connectome Project dataset using independent components from CONN.

Neuroimaging analysis

First, Amplitude of Low Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF), Fractional Amplitude of Low Frequency Fluctuations (fALFF), and Regional Homogeneity (ReHo)were determined using REST plus, as previously described [32, 33]. A single-sample t-test was then conducted on the ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo maps voxel-wise for the two groups. ROI-based resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) analysis was conducted using CONN-20.b toolbox [34]. A pairwise analysis of the average Blood Oxygen Level Dependent time series of the predefined ROIs was conducted, and the Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients between each pair of ROIs were determined. The inter-sample functional connectivity (FC) connections were examined and compared using a t-test, with statistical correction applied using False Discovery Rate (FDR). The entire connectivity network for each subject was characterized using pairwise ROI-to-ROI connectivity (RRC) matrices and categorized into 20 brain functional integration networks for subsequent correlation analysis with gut microbiota and serum metabolites.

Statistical analysis

R (v3.6.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS (v22.0, Statistical Product and Service Solutions, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) were used for statistical analyses. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Two independent samples with non-normal distributions were compared using rank tests. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method and the FDR. Following standardized post-surgical dietary and exercise guidelines, major confounding variables such as diet and physical activity were maintained consistently across participants. Pairwise correlations among clinical cognitive scales, serum metabolites, and gut microbiome composition were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis [35], p < 0.05, |r| > 0.24 for gut species and serum metabolites were considered to be associated with cognition. Additionally, the abundance spectra of gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and brain function RRC matrices were analyzed using Co-inertia analysis (CIA) [36] to evaluate the relationships among gut microbiota, serum metabolome, and rsFC. The potential impacts of each cognition-related serum metabolite and gut species on significant rsFC following LSG were investigated using Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) [37]. Adjusted p < 0.05, R2 > 0.12 for gut species and serum metabolites were considered to be closely associated with changes in rsFC.

Results

Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) decreases weight and improves cognitive function

The average age of the enrolled patients was 34.74 ± 6.53 years. Most patients were females (27 females and 12 males). The educational background of the patients was as follows: middle school (4 individuals), high school (6 individuals), and above high school (29 individuals). The mean preoperative weight and BMI were 100.76 ± 15.22 kg and 36.75 ± 4.46 kg/m2 (Table 1). The baseline and 6-month postoperative CRF data for participants are presented in Table 2 (Supplementary Table 1). LSG significantly decreased weight at 6 months (100.76 ± 15.22 vs. 72.04 ± 11.70 kg, p = 0.000), with an average percentage of total body weight loss of 28.34 ± 5.9%. Additionally, LSG significantly decreased neck, chest, waist, and hip circumferences at 6 months post-surgery (40.87 ± 3.75 vs. 36.17 ± 2.91 cm, p = 0.000; 118.75 ± 10.11 vs. 99.44 ± 8.95 cm, p = 0.000; 116.23 ± 11.85 vs. 92.80 ± 11.50 cm, p = 0.000; 117.74 ± 8.63 vs. 101.02 ± 9.37 cm, p = 0.000), respectively.

Cognitive function before and after bariatric surgery was evaluated using cognitive scales (p < 0.05) to assess memory and executive functions (Table 3, Supplementary Table 1). Memory was assessed using HVLT and DGS. Immediate memory test scores 1, 2, 3, and total post-surgery scores were significantly higher compared with baseline scores (6.56 ± 1.55 vs. 8.18 ± 1.50, p = 0.000; 8.36 ± 1.60 vs. 9.97 ± 1.39, p = 0.000; 9.85 ± 1.62 vs. 10.95 ± 1.17, p = 0.003; 24.79 ± 4.11 vs. 29.10 ± 3.50, p = 0.000, respectively). Compared with baseline, LSG significantly improved the delayed recall test scores, including 3–5 min delayed recall, 20 min delayed recall, and delayed recognition scores (8.64 ± 2.01 vs. 10.08 ± 1.93, p = 0.003; 7.97 ± 2.12 vs. 9.85 ± 2.06, p = 0.001), with a higher 20-minute retention rate post-surgery (81.12 ± 17.54% vs. 89.37 ± 15.14%, p = 0.039). Furthermore, LSG slightly increased the Digit Span Test scores. Executive function assessment comprised SCWT, VFT, and SDMT. Compared with baseline, LSG improved the SCWT Card C performance with shorter completion time (31.03 ± 0.85 vs. 27.03 ± 7.99, p = 0.023), increased the number of correct responses (22.87 ± 1.08 vs. 23.44 ± 1.02, p = 0.008), and decreased the number of corrections (1.13 ± 1.08 vs. 0.56 ± 1.02, p = 0.008), indicating higher accuracy. Results for SCWT Cards A and B demonstrated slightly shorter completion times and slightly increased accuracy post-surgery. These improvements are consistent with findings from other studies [38, 39], demonstrating that patients who undergo bariatric surgery experience significant cognitive enhancements. However, VFT and SDMT showed no significant differences at baseline and post-surgery. These results indicate that LSG may reverse obesity-related cognitive decline, enhancing overall quality of life considering the well-established negative effect of obesity on cognitive health.

Gut microbiota profile

16 S rRNA sequencing was performed on gut fecal samples from 36 preoperative and 30 postoperative obese patients due to preoperative constipation and postoperative follow-up attrition. A total of 3,697,219 high-quality sequences were obtained (about 56,018.47 sequences per sample, range: 51,385–61,061). A total of 13,751 usable OTUs were identified after species annotation (about 208.35 OTUs per sample, range: 86–373). Structural analysis of the gut microbiome was conducted based on these OTU data. The rarefaction curves of both groups flattened or stabilized, indicating that the sequencing depth could identify most bacterial features in the samples for subsequent structural analysis (Fig. 1A-E). Rank abundance distribution curves indicated that the abundance and dysbiosis of microbial communities were significantly reduced in the postoperative group compared with the preoperative group (Fig. 1F).

Sequence analysis was conducted to estimate α and β diversity and determine changes in microbial diversity between the two groups. Alpha diversity analysis revealed that Shannon index (2.66 ± 0.56 vs. 3.30 ± 0.36, p < 0.001), observed species (189.75 ± 54.68 vs. 230.67 ± 48.93, p < 0.001), Chao1 index (225.90 ± 59.43 vs. 263.79 ± 54.68, p < 0.001), and Simpson index (0.15 (0.1, 0.26) vs. 0.09 (0.06, 0.1), p < 0.001) were significantly different at baseline and six months post-surgery. These results indicate that the abundance and diversity of the gut microbiota were significantly higher post-surgery compared with the preoperative group (Fig. 2A). Beta diversity describes the inter-group differences in species. PoCA analysis showed also that the composition and abundance of the gut microbiome were significantly different between the groups (Fig. 2B). Similarity analysis found that inter-group differences were significantly greater than intra-group differences (Fig. 2C).

LSG changes gut microbiota composition

Species abundance tables at various taxonomic levels were generated based on OTU annotations and abundance tables for the analysis and comparison of species composition between the two groups. LSG significantly influenced the abundance of 4 phyla, 5 classes, 12 orders, 18 families, 31 genera, and 41 species levels (Supplementary Table 2). Bacteria belonging to 11 phyla were identified in the preoperative group at the phylum level, whereas 14 phyla were identified in the post-surgery group. Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Candidatuas, and Verrucomicrobia were the dominant phyla in both groups (Fig. 3A). However, inter-group differences were observed in major phyla in the gut microbiota. Notably, Firmicutes composition showed the highest difference (Fig. 3B). Specifically, Firmicutes composition was higher in the postoperative group (49.30%) than in the preoperative group (34.52%). Conversely, Proteobacteria composition was higher in the preoperative group than in the postoperative group (53.24% vs. 41.28%).

Further assessment at different taxonomic levels showed that the abundance of Firmicutes (p < 0.001), Verrucomicrobia (p < 0.001), and Synergistetes (p < 0.05) was higher in the postoperative group than in the preoperative group. Conversely, the abundance of Proteobacteria (p < 0.001) was significantly higher in the preoperative group than in the postoperative group (Fig. 3B). At the genus level, significant inter-group differences were observed in 31 genera (FDR, p < 0.05). Specifically, the abundance of Streptococcus (p < 0.001), Phascolarctobacterium (p < 0.001), Oscillibacter (p < 0.001), Blautia (p < 0.001), and Veillonella (p < 0.001) was significantly higher in the postoperative group than in the preoperative group. In contrast, the abundance of Megamonas (p < 0.001), Clostridium_XVIII (p < 0.001), and Selenomonas (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the preoperative group than in the postoperative group (Supplementary Table 2).

LEfSE analysis was conducted at the genus level to generate a cladogram, while LDA effect size analysis was conducted to produce a dendrogram. These visualizations depicted all layers of species differences between the two groups, identifying specific microbial communities associated with the postoperative group (Fig. 4A). A total of 101 microbial species exhibited statistically significant differences (LDA score log10 > 2) between the two groups. The abundance of Firmicutes, Clostridia, Clostridiales, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, Phascolarctobacterium, Acidaminococcaceae, Blautia, Veillonella, and Faecalibacterium were higher in the postoperative group than in the preoperative group (LDA score log10 > 4). Specifically, the upregulated gut microbiota post-surgery have been shown to be positively related to cognitive function [40, 41]. In contrast, the abundances of Bacteroidetes, Bacteroidia, Bacteroidales, Megamonas, and Escherichia were higher in the preoperative group than in the postoperative group (LDA scores log10 > 4). These results suggest that the abundance levels of gut microbial communities are different between the two groups (Fig. 4B).

Functional analysis of the gut microbiome was also conducted before and after surgery using KEGG Pathway database. The differences in functions between the two groups were compared at Level 3 (Fig. 4C, FDR q < 0.05). Results revealed that 72% (36/50) of the pathways were upregulated post-surgery. These pathways were involved in biological functions, such as the biosynthesis of valine, leucine, and isoleucine, the biosynthesis of steroids and carotenoids, and pathways related to ribosomes. Additionally, increased activity was observed in metabolic functions, such as butyrate metabolism, D-glutamine, and D-glutamate metabolism. These results suggest that LSG may regulate critical neuroactive metabolites, including amino acids and branched-chain amino acids, neurotransmitters, and their precursors, by inducing functional changes in the gut microbial communities.

General characteristics of serum metabolomics

Blood transports metabolites and facilitates crosstalk between the brain and the gut microbiota. Therefore, the changes in gut microbiota induced by bariatric surgery might influence serum metabolic pathways, thus potentially affecting cognitive functions by modulating brain functional activities. Therefore, LC-MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics was used to identify and quantify 707 metabolites in 78 serum samples from the two groups (Supplementary Table 3).

Serum metabolites

The OPLS-DA model revealed significant differences in metabolic profiles between the two groups, suggesting distinct metabolic characteristics in post-surgery group (R2X(cum) = 0.152, R2Y(cum) = 0.938, Q2(cum) = 0.837, p < 0.05) Fig. 5A-B). The results of the differential metabolite screening were visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 5C). The matchstick plot of differential metabolites indicated that LSG significantly decreased Linoleamide, gamma-L-Glutamyl-S-(2-carboxy-1-propyl) cysteinylglycine, Isopulegone caffeate, and PE (14:1(9Z)/18:0) / Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE or GPEtn) levels. Conversely, LSG significantly increased Sphingosine 1-phosphate, PC(16:0/P-16:0), PS (22:2(13Z,16Z)/15:0), SM(d18:1/16:0), p-Cresol sulfate, and p-Anisic acid levels (Fig. 5D).

Differential metabolites

Hierarchical Clustering Analysis (HCA, Fig. 6A) revealed that LSG upregulated various metabolites, including glycerophosphocholines (GPCs) (PC(16:0/16:0), PC(18:0/20:5), PC(16:1(9Z)/P-18:0)), sphingolipid metabolites (SM(d18:1/24:1(15Z)), SM(d18:1/16:0)), amino acid metabolites (betaine proline, pyroglutamate, L-serine), lipids and lipid-like molecules (decanoyl carnitine, lutein), organic acids and derivatives (p-cresol sulfate, eicosadienoic acid, succinic semialdehyde), and carbohydrate metabolites (xylose, ascorbic acid). Conversely, LSG downregulated some metabolites, including amino acid metabolites (L-leucine, DL-phenylalanine, and L-norleucine), organic acids and derivatives (ketohexose, 2-pentanone, alpha-hydroxyisobutyrate), lipids and lipid-like molecules (butyryl carnitine, deoxycholate, estrone), glycerophosphocholines (2-acetyl-1-alkyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, PC(18:3/18:0), PC(22:2/16:1(9Z))), and carbohydrate metabolites (alpha-D-glucose, L-Gulose). Notably, the postoperative upregulation of GPCs and sphingolipid metabolites may contribute to enhanced cognitive recovery and function [42, 43]. Moreover, LSG significantly altered the serum metabolome, especially lipid metabolism (27.07%, 62/229; Fig. 6A). The differential metabolic pathways involved in post-LSG surgery were identified through KEGG annotation. Metabolic pathway analysis results were expressed as bubble chart (Fig. 6B). The results indicated that LSG significantly altered amino acid metabolic pathways related to cysteine and methionine metabolism, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, α-linolenic acid metabolism, as well as biosynthetic pathways, including aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and the biosynthesis of valine, leucine, and isoleucine (p < 0.05; Fig. 6C).

The relationship among microbial Taxa, differential serum metabolites, and cognitive function

A total of 101 key gut microbial genera (LEfSe LDA > 2 and p < 0.05) and 229 differentially expressed metabolites (VIP > 1 and p < 0.05) were utilized to validate the correlation between gut microbial genera and serum metabolites after LSG. Results showed that gut microbiota was significantly associated with serum metabolites (Fig. 7A, |r| > 0.24 p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 8). Moreover, serum metabolites were significantly associated with cognitive scores (Fig. 7B, |r| > 0.24 p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 7). Notably, 229 serum metabolites were correlated with at least one type of gut microbiota. Furthermore, key gut microbiota were significantly correlated with cognitive scales (Fig. 7C, |r| > 0.24 p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 6). Specifically, 67.33% (68/101) of gut microbiota and 79.91% (183/229) of serum metabolites were associated with at least one cognitive scale score. Cluster analysis results indicated that cognition-related gut microbiota and differential serum metabolites were primarily related to scores of the HVLT scale and Stroop Color and Word Test Card C, consistent with significant differences observed in scores pre- and post-surgery.

LSG affects resting-state neural activity and functional connectivity patterns related to cognition

The effects of LSG on brain activity were evaluated using rs-fMRI data from 11 patients. The changes in resting-state activity (ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo) were characterized at baseline and six months after LSG using rs-fMRI (Fig. 8A-C). Post-bariatric surgery, compared to baseline, there was increased in resting-state activity in the bilateral middle frontal gyrus, left middle temporal gyrus, left inferior temporal gyrus, and left middle occipital gyrus, while a decrease was observed in the left lentiform nucleus and bilateral hypothalamus. These findings align with previous studies by Zeighami Y, and Li, P. et al. [44, 45]. Notably, the enhanced imaging signals in the DLPFC, a key component of the FPN network involved in cognitive function regulation, may account for the observed improvements in memory and executive function. Hierarchical clustering was then conducted on 640 significant pairwise connections among 136 regions of interest (ROIs), identified based on ROI-to-ROI spatial proximity and functional similarity metrics, and 20 distinct networks were formed by CONN (Fig. 8F, Supplementary Table 4). PCA analysis found that the RRC matrices were significantly different between the post-surgery and pre-surgery groups (Fig. 8D, multivariate permutation test, p = 0.087). T-tests were performed to explore significant changes in functional connectivity before and after surgery (Fig. 8E, t-test, p < 0.05).

Cognitive-related serum metabolites and gut microbiota are closely related to brain functional connectivity

The effect sizes of serum metabolomics, gut microbiota, and rs-MRI (including differential brain regions and rsFC) were assessed by CIA to further explore how cognition-related gut microbiota and metabolites may influence brain activity. The effect sizes of serum metabolomics and rs-MRI (Pearson r = 0.352, p = 0.45) were greater than those of gut microbiota and rsFC (Pearson r = 0.431, p = 0.04) (Fig. 9A, B). This suggests that neural signals can alter gut sensory-motor and secretory functions through complex neurohumoral pathways. Besides, gut microbiota derivatives can regulate brain function via visceral and endocrine signaling. Furthermore, the correlations between cognition-related serum metabolites and cognition-related gut microbiota with differential brain regions and ROI-based FC were analyzed by PERMANOVA test. About 41.54% (100/194) of cognition-related serum metabolites were associated with at least one FC (PERMANOVA test, p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 9). Furthermore, only 18% (18/100) of cognition-related serum metabolites were related to both pre- and post-surgery FNC networks, 38% (38/100) were related to the pre-surgery FNC network, and 44% (44/100) were associated with the post-surgery FNC network, suggesting that changes in serum metabolites may regulate brain network signaling transitions. Serum metabolites related to post-surgery FNC networks included tryptophan and its derivatives, amino acids and branched-chain amino acids (BCAA), neurotransmitters (DOPA, GABA), precursors, and short-chain fatty acids, which are involved in MGBA [46] and regulation of mood and cognition [47]. Notably, amino acids Glycine and L-Serine are related to the language areas (network-3, network-4, network-8), salience network (network-6), visual areas (network-11, network-17), FPN (network-13), and sensorimotor areas (network-15). Short-chain fatty acid Valeric acid is related to DAN (network-12, network-13), the visual network (network-17), and reward pathways (network-20). Neurotransmitter DL-Dopa is related to the visual areas (network-17). Sphingomyelin SM(d18:1/24:1(15Z)), found in the myelin surrounding neural axons, is associated with the auditory areas (network-1), FPN (network-9), limbic hippocampal areas (network-18), and reward pathways (network-19). Sphingomyelin (SM(d18:1/24:1(15Z))) is also related to auditory areas (network-5), visual areas (network-12), salience network (network-2, network-6), dorsolateral attention network (network-18), and frontoparietal network (network-6), suggesting that the dysregulation of identified cognition-related metabolites in serum may impact specific brain areas associated with language, emotion, reward processing, and executive functions (Fig. 9C).

Similarly, 58.82% (50/85) of gut microbes were significantly associated with at least one individual connection (PERMANOVA test, p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 10). Specifically, microbes related to serum neuroactive metabolites were also associated with specific connectivity networks, with most gut microbes (post-surgery) related to language areas (network-3), visual networks (network-5, network-17), salience networks (network-6), default mode networks (network-5, network-6), sensorimotor networks (network-16), limbic hippocampal areas (network-19), and hypothalamic reward pathways (network-20), suggesting that gut microbiota may control weight loss and cognitive functions by regulating these networks. Short-chain fatty acid-producing microbes, such as Verrucomicrobia spp. (Verrucomicrobiae, Verrucomicrobiales and Verrucomicrobiaceae, Akkermansia) are related to reward networks (network-20), Coriobacteriia spp. (Coriobacteriaceae and Collinsella) is related to language networks (network-3), Ruminococcaceae spp. (Ruminococcus) are related to language areas (network-3) and limbic systems (network-18, network-9), Phascolarctobacterium is related to salience networks (network-6) and sensorimotor networks (network-16), and Blautia is related to language areas (network-3) and salience networks (network-7). Amino acid and branched-chain amino acid-producing microbes, including Eubacteriaceae are related to reward networks (network-20) (Fig. 9D). These results suggest that bariatric surgery may affect brain cognitive functions by altering gut microbiota and neuroactive metabolites.

These results suggest that bariatric surgery not only facilitates weight loss but may also influence cognitive brain networks. This effect is potentially mediated by alterations in cognition-related gut microbiota and neuroactive serum metabolites, which could enhance executive functions by upregulating the FPN and normalizing the DMN and SN. The latter networks are often negatively correlated with working memory [48] and task performance [49]. Consequently, bariatric surgery may significantly improve cognitive function in patients. Further research into the long-term cognitive effects of LSG and the causal relationships between microbiota changes and brain is warranted.

Discussion

Although studies have suggested that bariatric surgery can induce changes in the gut microbiota composition and improve cognitive impairments, the underlying mechanisms are unclear. In this study, LSG induced changes to gut microbiota composition and function, including increased microbial diversity, altered metabolic profiles, and modifications in signaling connections between functional brain networks.

LSG and cognitive enhancement

This study further confirms that LSG improved cognitive function in obese patients. Studies have also shown that obese individuals are more prone to cognitive impairment or cognitive disorders. Rochette et al. [50] found that 53.8% of severely obese patients had mild cognitive impairment (χ2(3) = 1.45,p = 0.69), and only 15.56% (95% CI: 13.24–18.03) [51] of the general population had mild cognitive impairment. Even though some studies have not reported changes in cognitive function following bariatric surgery [52], most studies have shown that bariatric surgery can improve cognitive function in obese patients, especially memory and executive functions [53], possibly due to changes in gut microbiota, neurotransmitter secretion, and central nervous system signaling [13, 17].

LSG and brain functional connectivity

Li, G. et al. [45] also showed that obesity negatively affects brain function and structure, whereas bariatric surgery positively impacts the cognitive function of obese patients. In this study, LSG increased resting-state activity between the DLPFC and visual cortex, consistent with Li, G. et al.’s studies. Moreover, DLPFC, a core region of the PFN, is primarily involved in the regulation of executive functions [54], which may explain the observed postoperative improvements in executive function. Additionally, LSG increased rsFC between the temporal pole and language network, as well as between the fusiform gyrus (temporal lobe portion), and the dorsal attention network. The temporal pole is involved in the maintenance and consolidation of long-term memory [55], the language network regulates language learning and memory [56], and the DAN guides goal-directed attention [57], potentially indicating enhanced memory and executive function following LSG.

Notably, LSG decreased rsFC in the middle temporal gyrus (MTG, [DMN]), middle frontal gyrus (MFG, [DMN]), and the angular gyrus and supramarginal gyrus (SMG, AG, [DMN]), and putamen (PUT, [SN]), as well as the anterior cingulate gyrus and paracingulate gyrus (ACG, [SN]). At rest, DMN shows higher activity which decreases during externally oriented cognitive tasks [58]. Similar findings by Cerit et al. [59] and Heinrichs et al. [60] suggest that bariatric surgery may normalize DMN connectivity, restoring negative inter-network connections, reducing task-unrelated thoughts, and improving task performance [48]. Preoperative SN hyperconnectivity in obese patients may underlie excessive appetite integration and cognitive decline [61]. Previous studies have documented attenuation and subsequent normalization of SN functional connectivity (FC) following surgery [62, 63], which aligns with our findings. These results suggest a decrease in focus on internal appetite states and cognitive recovery after surgery. Interestingly, rsFC changes observed in obese patients after non-surgical interventions, such as reduced DMN activity following treadmill walking [64], resemble those seen after surgical interventions. In summary, the increased FC observed in obese individuals [65] may contribute to the memory and executive functional recovery post-surgery.

Gut microbiota, neuroactive metabolite and cognitive function

The gut-brain axis describes how gut microbes communicate with the brain through multiple mechanisms. For instance, gut microbes regulate global immunity and brain function by activating immune cells and producing short-chain fatty acids, thereby influencing mood and cognition [9]. The metabolic products of gut microbes also stimulate enteroendocrine cells to release neuropeptides and hormones, thus affecting appetite and mood via the vagus nerve [66]. Additionally, these microbes can indirectly act on the neural pathways between the gut and brain by regulating hormone precursors, modulating stress and mood [67]. These microbes can also adjust the availability of micronutrients, directly impacting brain development and health through the blood-brain barrier [68]. This study showed how post-surgical cognition-related neuroactive microbes and metabolites participate in these gut-brain communication pathways, providing a significant addition to the existing body of scientific evidence.

The increased levels of Akkermansia are linked to the reward network (network-20). For example, Akkermansia muciniphila, a member of this genus, has been shown to combat obesity by modulating fat metabolism [69]. Moreover, its outer membrane protein Amuc_1100 stimulates the production of the gut neurotransmitter 5-hydroxytryptamine through the TLR2 signaling pathway [70], and they can also regulate cognitive functions by protecting the gut mucosa and microbial metabolites [71] within the gut-brain axis. Similarly, the upregulated Blautia is associated with the language areas (Network-3) and salience network (Network-7). The abundance of Blautia is reduced in the gut microbiota of obese children [72]. Moreover, Hou et al. [73] found that a decrease in acetic acid, a metabolic product of Bluatia, is associated with cognitive decline in children with Down syndrome. Collinsella is associated with language areas (network-3, network-8) and visual areas (network-18). Phascolarctobacterium is associated with the salience network (network-6) and language areas (network-8). Phascolarctobacterium produces propionate, a short-chain fatty acid [74] that can affect the synthesis and release of neurotransmitters, such as serotonin and GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid), indirectly regulating mood and cognitive processes. Ruminococcus is associated with language areas (network-3) and visual areas (network-8). Salas-Pérez and others have shown that the abundance of Ruminococcus is correlated with methylation differences in the MACROD2/SEL1L2 gene regions associated with BMI [75]. Therefore, Ruminococcus gnavus may alter gut-brain communication, enhancing working memory in mice by regulating PSA-NCAM immunoreactive granule cell polysialylation thereby regulating synaptic plasticity and memory consolidation [76].

Similarly, the cognition-related neuroactive metabolite DL-Dopa, as a precursor to dopamine, plays a critical role in cognitive processes, including attention, learning, and memory [77]. For instance, DL-Dopa can modulate signaling pathways involved in neurogenesis, such as the Ras-ERK pathway, which influences the survival and integration of newly formed neurons [78]. The amino acids glycine and serine regulate cognitive growth by modulating neuronal gain and depolarization regulation via different synaptic and extrasynaptic NMDA receptor glycine sites [79]. Studies have shown that glycine and serine can improve cognitive functions in both animals and humans [80, 81]. Sphingomyelins participate in the brain development process [82] and can influence synaptic and memory functions by modulating NMDA receptor subunit expression in the hippocampus. Moreover, they enhance synaptic plasticity through the lipid raft localization of signaling proteins [83].

In this study, KEGG functional pathways analysis showed that LSG upregulated pathways associated with the synthesis and metabolism of neuroactive metabolites, including the biosynthesis of steroids, carotenoids, and the metabolism of D-glutamine and D-glutamate post-surgery. These findings are consistent with changes in serum metabolomics, characterized by increased circulating levels of phosphatidylcholine, sphingolipids, lutein, and succinic semialdehyde. Moreover, LSG enriched the metabolism of short-chain fatty acids (butyrate metabolism) despite not being reflected in the serum metabolites. Butyrate has anti-inflammatory effects, promotes neural growth, modulates gene expression, and impacts neurotransmitter levels, and thus regulates cognitive functions [84]. Herein, LSG enriched ribosome biogenesis and downregulated lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis. Notably, LSG enhances biosynthesis functions of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan, as well as valine, leucine, and isoleucine [85]. However, LSG decreases the circulating levels of tryptophan, tyrosine, and leucine, indicating that factors not related to gut microbiota may also influence these changes.

Limitations and future research directions

This study has some limitations. For instance, we enrolled a small sample size, did not identify other factors that can potentially influence cognition (e.g., dietary changes), and did not use advanced methods, such as metagenomic shotgun sequencing. Moreover, the results were not validated in other data sets. In addition, the present results cannot be generalized to patients using antibiotics, probiotics and non-surgical interventions for weight loss, because these patient groups may differ in their microbiome and metabolic profiles. In future research, we will address this issue by employing comprehensive metagenomic sequencing and targeted metabolomics in larger, more diverse, and gender-balanced cohorts. In addition, we shall investigate the mechanistic pathways associated with weight loss, cognitive improvement, changes in gut microbes and metabolites. Moreover, clinicians need to explore the potential cognitive benefits of LSG and incorporate cognitive assessments into standard pre-and postoperative care. And the study also provides a basis for clinical dietary modifications or probiotic supplements aimed at enhancing beneficial gut microbiota to support cognitive function.

Conclusion

In summary, this innovative study explores the effects of bariatric surgery on cognitive functions using a thorough multi-omics approach. It identified specific microbiota related to cognition, including Akkermansia, Blautia, Collinsella, Phascolarctobacterium, and Ruminococcus, as well as neuroactive metabolites such as Glycine, L-Serine, DL-Dopa and SM(d18:1/24:1(15Z)). The study also observed changes in functional connectivity networks, including the normalization of memory and executive function networks, and the enhancement of language and interoceptive networks. These findings suggest that bariatric surgery may improve cognitive functions by influencing gut-brain interactions. This includes modulation of gut microbiota, production of neuroactive metabolites, and normalization of brain network connectivity. However, these results should be confirmed through larger cohort studies. Future research should investigate the mechanisms underlying gut-brain-microbiota interactions following weight loss surgery. This should involve the use of advanced sequencing techniques and the exploration of potential therapeutic interventions.
