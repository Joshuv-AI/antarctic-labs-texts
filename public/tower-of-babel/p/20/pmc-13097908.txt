Comparative transcriptome analysis identifies key players associated with downy mildew resistance in foxtail millet variety JG21 and its resistant mutant rdm12
Authors: Yanqing Han, Anqi Wei, Yongchao Zhang, Hengbo Xiao, Xinyan Liang, Wen Zhang, Lei Su, Yuxin Fan, Haili Han, Mansen Jin, Zehua Zhang, Yuanhuai Han, Siyu Hou, He Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFoxtail millet downy mildew, caused by the obligate parasite Sclerospora graminicola, is a highly devastating disease for foxtail millet. S. graminicola infection often damages buds, leaves, and spikes, significantly compromising its quality and yield. However, resistant varieties can effectively reduce susceptibility to pathogen attacks.ResultsIn this study, we investigated Jingu21 (JG21) and the resistant mutant rdm12, which was generated by ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis. Phenotypic observations showed that rdm12 did not differ significantly from JG21 in agronomic and quality traits. Notably, rdm12 exhibited enhanced disease resistance, along with increased activities of defense enzymes and higher levels of osmoregulatory substances. Transcriptome analysis of rdm12 mutants and JG21 revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly enriched in pathways such as plant-pathogen interaction, MAPK signaling, diterpenoid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism. The differential expression of several key genes—including receptor protein kinase genes, WRKY transcription factors, pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins, calmodulin, glutathione S-transferase—contributed to improved downy mildew resistance in the mutants. In particular, WRKY transcription factor 53 (encoded by Seita.3G139400), pathogenesis-related protein PRMS (encoded by Seita.3G175100), and G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine protein kinase (encoded by Seita.7G095600) played an essential role in resistance during S. graminicola infection.ConclusionsThis study identifies key genes and important products involved in resistance, along with their associated metabolic pathways, thereby elucidating the resistance mechanism of foxtail millet against S. graminicola. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for resistance screening in foxtail millet.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08520-y.Keywords: Foxtail millet, Downy mildew, Sclerospora graminicola, Transcriptome, Differentially expressed genes, Resistance mechanism

Introduction

Foxtail millet [Setaria italica (L.) P. Beauv.] is one of the important coarse cereals in northern China, characterized by its strong drought resistance and wide adaptability [1]. However, changes in cropping systems have increased the risk of infection by various pathogens, which not only hinders normal foxtail millet growth, but also adversely affects yield and quality [2, 3]. Downy mildew is one of the most significant diseases affecting foxtail millet and can occur throughout the growth cycle, presenting a range of symptoms. Pathogenic oospores germinate and infect young buds, leading to "bud rot". In cases of mild infection, seedlings may exhibit "gray back" symptoms on the abaxial side of leaves. This may be followed by a series of other symptoms, such as "white tip", "straighthead", "white hair" and "hedgehog". The disease can cause substantial reduction in both yield and quality, posing a serious threat to foxtail millet production [3, 4]. Given the harm caused by downy mildew, the selection and promotion of resistant varieties is an economical and environmentally friendly way to control the disease [5, 6]. JG21 is an excellent variety, known for its high yield and quality, and is widely planted in the major production areas of Shanxi [7]. Unfortunately, it has become highly susceptible to S. graminicola.

Posing a significant threat to productivity, downy mildew causes substantial yield losses for farmers. Infection by this pathogen generally leads to yield losses of 20–30%, with losses exceeding 70% under severe epidemic conditions [8]. These observations are supported by physiological studies on the susceptible cultivar JG21, where infection markedly inhibits plant growth, as evidenced by reduced plant height and panicle length, and impairs photosynthetic capacity, including a decreased net photosynthetic rate. Moreover, JG21 exhibits significantly weaker defense-related enzyme activity and osmotic adjustment capability compared to resistant cultivars [9].

So far, several methods have been used to control downy mildew, including spraying chemical fungicides, selecting coated seeds, and application of various agricultural regulation measures [10, 11]. However, cultivating and utilizing disease-resistant varieties is the best way and environmentally sustainable strategy [12].

Genetic improvement of elite varieties and breeding of disease-resistant varieties are particularly urgent. Traditional breeding methods can take seven to eight years or more to screen resistant varieties. Fortunately, FAO and the IAEA have developed more than 3,200 new crop varieties through mutagenesis, approximately 80% of which were obtained via physical mutagenesis [13, 14]. Mutation breeding is a promising strategy to develop resistant varieties. The resistant mutant WYB-18, generated by ethyl methylsulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis, showed resistance to black spot disease and provided a theoretical basis for breeding resistance varieties in the future [15]. Similarly, embryogenic cell suspension (ECS) of banana were mutagenized with EMS and screened for Fusarium wilt resistance, which may be involved in enhancing resistance to this disease [13]. Metabolome and transcriptome studies of the roots of Cuibi-1 (CB-1) and its mutant KCB-1 revealed that key metabolites in the phenylpropanoid pathway, and the hormone signals pathway, including indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and abscisic acid (ABA), were involved in the response to R. solanacearum infection in both CB-1 and KCB-1 [16].

Foxtail millet, as an important coarse cereal crop, with the improvement of people's quality of life and health awareness, its domestic market demand has also been increasing. Disease resistance varieties are an internal factor influencing the occurrence of disease. Therefore, this study focused on the inherent disease resistance of foxtail millet and applied a relatively common and efficient breeding method (EMS mutagenesis) to obtain the mutant plants. Combined with the field resistance evaluation, the phenotype, agronomic, quality and physiological and biochemical characteristics of the mutants were compared. Meanwhile, transcriptome sequencing was used to study the defense mechanism of foxtail millet infected by S. graminicola, in order to obtain the differentially expressed genes and elucidate the underlying resistance or susceptibility mechanism. The aim of this study is to lay a theoretical foundation for resistance breeding in foxtail millet and provide a reference for breeding of other coarse cereals.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

A stable, downy mildew-resistant mutant designated as rdm12 was identified from an ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS, 1% concentration) mutagenesis population of JG21, a widely cultivated foxtail millet variety in northern China, known for its high yield and superior grain quality. JG21 was originally collected and has been maintained by the Coarse Cereal Molecular Breeding Team at Shanxi Agricultural University. To reduce genetic heterogeneity, the rdm12 mutant underwent five consecutive generations of forced self-pollination using the bagging technique under greenhouse conditions. All plant materials were cultivated in experimental fields located in the Taigu District (37°25′13″ N, 112°35′26″ E) of Shanxi Province, China.

Pathogen inoculation and sample preparation

In 2022, each foxtail millet genotype was divided into a treatment group and a control group, with three biological replicates per group. Each plot measured 9 m2 (3 m × 3 m), with a row spacing of 30 cm, plant spacing of 15 cm, and a sowing depth of 3–5 cm. All plots were managed under consistent cultivation conditions and received conventional field practices, including thinning, irrigation, and weeding.

Leaf segments (3 cm in length) displaying early-stage "gray-back" symptoms were collected and rinsed under running tap water to remove surface contaminants. The segments were surface-sterilized in a laminar flow hood using absolute ethanol for 30 s, followed by three rinses with sterile distilled water. After blotting dry with sterile filter paper, the leaf segments were placed on moistened sterile filter paper in Petri dishes and incubated under high humidity to encourage pathogen growth and sporulation. Once abundant mycelium and sporangiophores had developed, sporangia were gently brushed from the abaxial leaf surface with a sterile brush within the hood and suspended in sterile water. The concentration of the sporangial suspension was determined microscopically using a hemocytometer and adjusted to 3 × 105 sporangia/mL. Inoculation was performed during the booting stage under continuous overcast conditions. A 1 mL aliquot of the sporangial suspension was injected into the middle of each panicle using a syringe. A total of 30 uniformly growing panicles were inoculated. At each sampling time point, panicles were collected from three independent plants as three biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted separately from each replicate, and strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced independently. The remaining inoculated panicles were used for disease incidence assessment. Panicles for transcriptome analysis were collected at 12 h and 24 h after inoculation. Control plants were inoculated with sterile water, with three biological replicates per treatment. All collected panicles were labeled, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80℃. Diseased symptoms on panicles were surveyed 15 days after inoculation, and resistance levels were classified according to the criteria provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Investigation of agronomic traits, yield, and quality

At maturity, five plants were randomly selected from each genotype to measure the following agronomic traits. Mean values were calculated for: plant height (PH), number of main stem nodes (NNMS), main panicle length (MPL), main panicle diameter (MPD), main stem diameter (MSD), panicle weight per plant (PWP), grain weight per plant (GWP), and 1000-grain weight (TGW). Data were analyzed for significant differences using Excel and SPSS 26.0 software.

Mature grains were collected from three individual plants per genotype as three independent biological replicates. After drying and threshing, grain color was measured using a colorimeter (X-rite VS50, Washington, USA). For color measurement, 5 g of grains from three separate panicles per genotype were ground in liquid nitrogen and analyzed using the same instrument [17].

Protein content was determined by the micro-Kjeldahl method using an automatic discrete chemistry analyzer (SmartChem 140, Paris, France) [18]. Starch content was measured according to the Association of Official Agricultural Chemists (AOAC) [19], and gel consistency was assessed as described by Cagampang et al. [20]. Amino acid content was analyzed following the method of Hou et al. [21].

Determination of antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), soluble sugar, and proline (Pro) contents

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using commercial assay kits (Boxbio, Beijing, China) with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1200, Shanghai, China). Crude enzyme extract was prepared from approximately 0.1 g of fresh panicle tissue that was ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. The powder was transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube. Mixed with 1 mL of extraction buffer, and vortexed thoroughly. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃. The supernatant was collected on ice for subsequent assays. Enzymes activities were expressed on a fresh weight basis. Soluble sugar content was determined by the anthrone colorimetric method [22]. Proline content was measured according to the method described by Lu et al. [23], with appropriate modifications. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Differences between the rdm12 and JG21 were tested by analysis of variance (ANOVA), and means were compared using Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at the 0.05 significant level. All experiments included independent biological replicates.

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

For RNA extraction, over 500 mg of panicle tissue was collected from wild-type JG21 and mutant rdm12. For clarity in subsequent analyses, the susceptible JG21 was designated as "S" and the resistant rdm12 as "R". Total RNA was extracted using the Universal Total RNA Extraction Kit (Newbate, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and its integrity was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Qualified RNA samples were used for transcriptome library construction. For library construction, poly(A) enrichment was performed to enrich mRNA from the qualified total RNA, and rRNA depletion was not required due to the specificity of poly(A) enrichment. The library was constructed as a strand-specific library to retain the strand information of the original RNA transcripts. The specific library preparation kit used was the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, Catalog No. NR604), and all operations were carried out in strict accordance with the kit's instructions. After library preparation, preliminary quantification was performed using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (requiring a concentration ≥ 1 ng/μL), followed by analysis on a Qsep400 high-throughput analysis system to verify that the insert fragment size met expectations. Finally, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was employed to accurately determine the effective library concentration (required to be > 2 nM) to ensure library quality. Libraries passing quality control were subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) read length mode.

Sequencing data analysis

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were subjected to quality control and adapter trimming using fastp (v0.23.2) with parameters set to remove low-quality bases and discard reads shorter than 50 bp [24]. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the Setaria italica reference genome (v2.2, Phytozome) using the splice-aware aligner HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [25]. Gene-level read counts were generated from the aligned BAM files with featureCounts (v2.0.3) based on the corresponding genome annotation [26]. Differential expression analysis was performed to identify the specific transcriptional responses of each genotype and time point. A total of 24 RNA samples were extracted (2 harvest stages × 3 replications × 2 treatments × 2 varieties), Within each genotype-time combination, independent two-group comparisons between S. graminicola-inoculated samples and the corresponding mock-inoculated controls were conducted using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) in R [27]. Genes with |log2(fold change)|≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Meanwhile, gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) only for the visualization of gene expression patterns within individual samples [28]. Functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted through Gene Ontology (GO) terms using Blast2GO [29] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the KOBAS2.0 tool [30], with terms/pathways showing an FDR-adjusted P ≤ 0.05 regarded as significantly enriched. Data organization and visualization (Venn diagrams and heatmaps) were performed with Microsoft Excel and TBtools (v2.025) [31], respectively.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted using a plant RNA extraction kit (Cowin Biotech, Jiangsu, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from the RNA using a commercial cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Japan). The Actin gene of foxtail millet served as the internal reference. Gene-specific primers were designed with Primer Premier 6.0 software, their sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2. qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Green qRT Kit (Takara, Japan) on a CFX96™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 1.0 μL cDNA, 10 μL 2xGS AntiQ qPCR SYBR Fast Mix, 0.5 µL each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), and ddH2O to make up the final volume. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 10 s, 56–60 ℃ for 10 s, and 72 ℃ for 30 s. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [32]. Three technical replicates were performed for each biological replicate.

Results

Comparison of resistance to S. graminicola between JG21 and the mutant rdm12

The resistance of wild-type JG21 and the mutant rdm12 to downy mildew caused by S. graminicola (Fig. 1). After inoculation, JG21 developed various disease symptoms, the most characteristic signs were the "white hair" and "hedgehog" observed during the heading stage. In contrast, the resistant mutant rdm12 remained asymptomatic (Fig. 1A). Disease incidence was quantified under both field and greenhouse conditions, confirming a difference between the two lines. JG21 was highly susceptible, with incidence rates of 38.25% and 47.33%, respectively. In contrast, the rdm12 mutant showed strong resistance, with significantly lower incidence rates of only 0.44% and 2.70% (P < 0.05), respectively (Fig. 1B). These results demonstrate a clear contrast in susceptibility to S. graminicola between JG21 and the rdm12 mutant.

B Statistics of downy mildew incidence rate of JG21 and rdm12. Bars represent means ± SE (n = 10); The * indicates significant differences (P < 0.05, Duncan’s test)

Characterization of agronomic and grain quality traits in JG21 and its mutant rdm12

The agronomic traits of JG21 and the rdm12 mutant were measured at the heading stage to compare plant height, number of internodes, stem diameter, panicle length, panicle diameter, panicle weight, number of grains per panicle, and thousand-grain weight. The results showed that the plant height of JG21 (179.46 ± 4.55 cm) was significantly greater than that of rdm12 (134.52 ± 4.57 cm) (P < 0.05). JG21 also had more internodes than rdm12. In contrast, rdm12 displayed a longer panicle (29.33 ± 2.73 cm) compared to JG21 (23.56 ± 2.73 cm), while its panicle diameter (29.93 ± 2.87 cm) was smaller than that of JG21 (34.2 ± 1.88 cm). No significant differences were observed in yield-related traits (panicle weight, grains per panicle, and 1000-grain weight) between the two lines, although JG21 exhibited slightly higher values (Fig. 2A).

B Comparison of protein content, fat content, gel consistency, starch content and the contents of 17 amino acids. Bars represent means ± SE (n = 3); The * indicates significant differences (P < 0.05, Duncan’s test)

Grain quality was assessed by measuring gel consistency, protein, fat, and amylose content (Fig. 2B). Protein and fat contents were slightly higher in JG21 than in rdm12, but the differences were not significant. Conversely, rdm12 exhibited higher gel consistency and amylose content than JG21, with a significant difference in gel consistency between the two (P < 0.05). Amino acid analysis revealed that, among the 17 detected, glutamic acid was the most abundant (2.56 ± 0.01 in JG21, and 2.47 ± 0.03 in rdm12). While cysteine was the least abundant (0.14 ± 0.01). Overall, rdm12 contained relatively higher levels of nine amino acids and lower levels of six amino acids (Glu, His, Ile, Leu, Pro and Thr) compared to JG21; however, none of these differences reached statistical significance (Fig. 2B).

Physiological and biochemical differences between JG21 and rdm12

We compared the activities of protective enzymes and contents of osmoregulatory substances in JG21 and rdm12 following infection by S. graminicola. Regarding protective enzymes, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity increased in both genotypes after infection, with activity in rdm12 being significantly higher than that in JG21 (P < 0.05), reaching 358.77 ± 6.19 U·g⁻1·min⁻1 (Fig. 3A). In contrast, peroxidase (POD) activity decreased in both JG21 and rdm12 compared to their respective controls. POD activity in JG21 declined from 78,058.82 ± 322.48 to 55,235.29 ± 423.56 U·g⁻1·min⁻1, while in rdm12 it decreased from 82,470.58 ± 512.45 to 51,588.24 ± 409.38 U·g⁻1·min⁻1, with the reductions being significant in both lines (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). Catalase (CAT) activity was significantly higher in rdm12 than in JG21 both before and after inoculation, after inoculation, CAT activity in JG21 decreased significantly to 3,900.78 ± 340.32 U·g⁻1·min⁻1 (P < 0.05), whereas it increased in rdm12, reaching 17,632 ± 190.45 U·g⁻1·min⁻1 (Fig. 3C). We also determined the contents of malondialdehyde (MDA), soluble sugars, and proline. Under uninfected conditions, the basal MDA content in rdm12 was significantly higher than in JG21 (P < 0.05). After inoculation, MDA content in JG21 increased sharply, showing an approximate 3.5-fold significant increase compared with its control group. While, no significant change was observed in rdm12 (Fig. 3D). Indicating distinct oxidative stress response between the two genotypes. Soluble sugars and proline contents were consistently and significantly higher in rdm12 than in JG21 (P < 0.05), regardless of inoculation (Fig. 3E, F). After inoculation, both genotypes showed a decline in soluble sugar contents compared to their controls, with the decrease in JG21 being statistically significant (Fig. 3E).

Sequencing, data processing and differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

A total of 170.47 Gb of clean data were generated, with at least 5.99 Gb per sample. The sequencing depth of each sample was approximately 12 ×, ensuring sufficient coverage for transcriptome analysis. Across all 24 samples, the Q30 base percentage exceeded 93%, the mapping ratio of each sample against the reference genome ranged from 94.41% to 96.19%, the average mapping rate of unique mapped reads was 90.82%, and the GC content was above 52% (Supplementary Table 3). Alignment to the foxtail millet reference genome showed a total read mapping rate over 95% for each sample, indicating high coverage and good quality of the sequencing data, which were suitable for further analysis. The correlation coefficients between samples are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Using an adjusted P < 0.05 as the threshold, we analyzed the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 12 h and 24 h after inoculation with S. graminicola. As shown in Fig. 4A, a large number of DEGs were detected in both JG21 and rdm12 following infection. The most pronounced response was observed in rdm12 at 12 h, which showed the highest total number of DEGs (3386). Furthermore, at both time points, the number of up-regulated genes was higher in rdm12 than in JG21. The overlaps among these DEGs are illustrated by Venn diagrams (Fig. 4B). These mapping results indicated that there were 67 common genes differentially expressed between rdm12 and JG21 (Supplementary Table 4). Heatmap analysis of these genes revealed that 70.8% (47 genes) were induced and up-regulated in rdm12 at 12 h, while no suppression of gene expression was observed at 24 h (Fig. 4C). In contrast, in the susceptible material JG21, these genes were down-regulated at 12 h, whereas 77.6% of them were significantly induced and up-regulated at 24 h (Fig. 4C). Of the 67 common genes, most (61.2%) have been reported to be mainly involved in the regulation of plant defense, cell wall and secondary metabolism, hormone signaling, proteases and hydrolases, antioxidant and detoxification processes, transcription factors, and other defense-related genes during plant disease resistance. Collectively, these DEGs may be associated with the basal disease resistance regulatory processes in foxtail millet.

GO functional enrichment and expression level analysis of DEGs

Using a threshold of P ≤ 0.05, GO enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of JG21 and rdm12. The top 30 enriched GO terms at 12 h and 24 h after S. graminicola infection are shown in Fig. 5A. The results revealed 14‌ common GO terms significantly enriched in rdm12 at both time points. These terms were associated with biological processes including cell surface receptor signaling pathway, defense response to oomycetes, defense response to bacterium, protein phosphorylation, glutathione metabolic process, cell wall macromolecule catabolic process, chitin catabolic process, hydrogen peroxide catabolic process, response to oxidative stress, recognition of pollen, positive regulation of response to salt stress, defense response, cinnamic acid biosynthetic process, and amino acid transport. In contrast, no common GO terms were enriched in JG21 at 12 h and 24 h, suggesting that these shared GO terms in rdm12 may play a role in the plant's defense against S. graminicola. Notably, the GO term "defense response to oomycetes" was specific to rdm12, 13 DEGs were enriched at 12 h, 9 at 24 h, with 9 DEGs common to both time points. All 9 shared genes contain an L-type lectin domain, among which 5 encode receptor kinases with L-type lectin domains. These genes were significantly up-regulated in rdm12 (Fig. 5B).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs

To identify metabolic pathways involved in the response to S. graminicola infection, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis on the DEGs. As shown in Fig. 6, our focus was on significantly enriched metabolic pathways in JG21 and rdm12 at 12 h and 24 h after infection. DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways including diterpenoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, MAPK signaling pathway, plant-pathogen interaction, starch and sucrose metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, zeatin biosynthesis, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, indicating that these pathways are strongly induced upon infection (Fig. 6). In JG21, five pathways were significantly enriched at 12 h and 24 h, with no pathway shared between the two time points. In rdm12, five pathways were significantly enriched, of which three: diterpenoid biosynthesis, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, and plant-pathogen interaction were enriched at both time points; among these, the plant-pathogen interaction pathway contained the most DEGs (38). followed by the MAPK signaling pathway-plant (31 DEGs) and the diterpenoid biosynthesis pathway (12 DEGs). Additionally, the glutathione metabolism pathway was specifically enriched only at 24 h (12 DEGs). These results indicate that S. graminicola infection activates these pathways to mediate defense responses. We further analyzed the genes within these key pathways (Fig. 6).

Altered expression of plant-pathogen interaction-related genes in the mutant rdm12

Compared with JG21, the expression of two CDPK genes (Seita.3G128600 and Seita.9G043400) was significantly induced in rdm12 at 12 h after inoculation, reaching levels substantially higher than those in JG21. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) serve as crucial signaling molecules in plant disease resistance, with Rboh being a key enzyme for ROS production. The expression patterns of two Rboh genes in JG21 and rdm12: in rdm12, Rboh expression was up-regulated at 12 h, but suppressed at 24 h, whereas in JG21, it was suppressed at 12 h and then significantly induced and up-regulated at 24 h (Fig. 7). The calmodulin/calmodulin-like (CaM/CML) signaling system has been implicated in plant disease resistance. In rdm12, eight CaM/CML genes were up-regulated, showing expression patterns opposite to those in JG21. As a plant receptor kinase, BAK1 (BRI1-associated kinase 1) perceives extracellular signals to regulate immune responses and disease resistance; in this study, the expression of six BAK1-like receptor kinases genes was significantly up-regulated upon infection (Fig. 7). Extensive studies have confirmed that the primary biological function of the plant WRKY gene family is to regulate resistance responses and mediate signal transduction pathways. Here, 18 WRKY genes were significantly up-regulated in response to pathogen challenge. Additionally, the pathogenesis-related protein PR1, the mitogen-activated protein kinase gene Seita.9G444100 (encoding a MAPK/ERK3 homolog), and Seita.5G175100 (encoding MEKK3, a mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 3) were all significantly up-regulated at 12 h after S. graminicola infection (Fig. 7).

Altered expression of MAPK signaling pathway-related genes in the mutant rdm12

The MAPK cascade participates in PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI), primarily transducing signals from pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to downstream transcription factors. We analyzed DEGs involved in the MAPK signaling pathway and found that in rdm12, several key genes were significantly induced and up-regulated following S. graminicola infection. The only exception was Seita.5g095400 (Fig. 8). The up-regulated genes comprised seven transcription factor genes (four WRKYs, two ERFs, and one other transcription factor), five pathogenesis-related (PR) genes (Seita.2g024600, Seita.2g024800, Seita.2g024900, Seita.9g0435200, and Seita.5g0175100), three calmodulin-like (CML) protein genes (homologous to those in Chinese cabbage), and three rust resistance Lr10 genes. Additionally, several receptor protein kinases were activated in rdm12, including GsSRK homologs (Seita.5g016900, Seita.7g095600, and Seita.7g045600), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinases (MPKKKs), two leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinases (LRR-RLKs), wall-associated kinase 10 (WAK10; Seita.8g152000), and serine/threonine kinase (STK; Seita.8G151600) all of which were up-regulated (Fig. 8).

Altered expression of glutathione metabolism and diterpenoid biosynthesis pathways in rdm12

Both the glutathione metabolism and diterpenoid biosynthesis pathways were enriched following S. graminicola infection. As shown in Fig. 9, among the 12 genes involved in glutathione metabolism, glutathione S-transferase (GST) genes were up-regulated in rdm12 at 12 h after infection but showed decreased expression at 24 h, whereas their expression patterns were opposite in JG21. Expression of deglutathionylation (DGS) genes remained down-regulated throughout the time course, except for Seita.3g0387000. These results suggest that glutathione S-transferase may promote glutathione synthesis to counteract infection during the early stage. In rdm12, the expression patterns of DEGs in the diterpenoid biosynthesis were opposite to those in JG21 at both two stages. Discussion

The destructive downy mildew disease causes major yield losses in foxtail millet, yet resistance mechanisms are not well characterized [2, 3]. This study utilized a downy mildew-resistant mutant (rdm12) derived from the elite variety JG21 via EMS mutagenesis. The rdm12 exhibits a stunted phenotype with shortened internodes (Fig. 2A). Through integrated phenotypic, physiological, biochemical and transcriptomic analyses, we identified key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in rdm12 that are enriched in defense-linked pathways, including plant-pathogen interaction, MAPK signaling, diterpenoid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism. These findings elucidate potential transcriptional mechanisms governing the resistant phenotype of the rdm12.

Plant growth and defense are regulated by antagonistic molecular pathways, leading to the well known "growth-defense trade-off": in which plants transiently suppress growth upon pathogen or pest attack. Due to this inherent antagonism, genetic variations that enhance disease tolerance often reduce growth, whereas those promoting growth tend to compromise resistance [33]. Derbyshire et al. (2024) reported an inverse correlation between yield and disease resistance, noting that highly resistant genotypes usually have lower yields, while susceptible genotypes often show higher yields [34]. Notably, the disease-resistant rdm12 mutant of Jingu 21 exhibited significant phenotypic alterations relative to the wild type, including reduced plant height, fewer internodes, and altered panicle-related traits. Importantly, these phenotypic differences are considered biologically meaningful rather than random or incidental effects caused by EMS mutagenesis alone [35]. Background mutations induced by EMS are generally stochastic and rarely lead to consistent, stable changes in multiple correlated agronomic traits [36]. In contrast, the observed suite of morphological changes strongly suggests pleiotropic effects of the rdm12 mutation, which likely affects shared regulatory networks involved in both plant development and immune activation. This pattern aligns with the growth-defense trade-off theory, as enhanced disease resistance is frequently accompanied by measurable shifts in growth architecture due to resource reallocation and pathway crosstalk [37]. Interestingly, despite the obvious growth defects, such as reduced plant height and shortened internodes-observed in the rdm12 mutant, the typical negative correlation between disease resistance and yield-related traits appears to be partially uncoupled in this line. This finding suggests the potential to optimize the coordination between agronomic performance and disease resistance in foxtail millet, echoing results from gene editing approaches that have generated disease-resistance and high-yielding wheat and rice lines [38, 39]. Thus, the use of mutants can contribute to the genetic improvement of foxtail millet.

When plants detect pathogens, oxidative stress occurs, leading to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Pathogen-induced ROS act as signaling molecules that trigger downstream signaling cascades, thereby activating host basal resistance [40]. However, cellular ROS levels must be dynamically balanced to avoid excessive oxidative stress. Along with the up-regulation of defense-related genes, intrinsic antioxidant pathways are activated to counteract this stress. The increased activities of enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase (PPO), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) are critical for scavenging harmful free radicals and maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [41, 42]. In this study, compared with the susceptible genotype JG21, the rdm12 showed significantly higher SOD and CAT activities, indicating that rdm12 can rapidly degrade free radicals generated during pathogen invasion [43, 44]. In JG21, even with host-produced antioxidants, maintaining optimal ROS levels remains challenging: reduced ROS-scavenging capacity leads to marked ROS accumulation, inducing disease susceptibility [45]. This can damage host DNA and membranes, ultimately resulting in plant death. In addition, we observed that rdm12 exhibited constitutively higher basal levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and soluble sugars than the susceptible JG21 under non-inoculated conditions. This elevated basal MDA may indicate either a pre-activated defense state or underlying stress. Upon pathogen infection, MDA increased sharply in JG21 but remained stable in rdm12, suggesting that the pre-elevated oxidative status in rdm12 functions to suppress infection-induced oxidative burst. Together with its sustained soluble sugar accumulation, this pre-regulated oxidative state likely constitutes a key physiological basis for disease resistance in rdm12. Although soluble sugars have been linked to plant resistance [46, 47], our study highlights the coordinated contribution of a pre-adjusted oxidative status and soluble sugar accumulation to the resistance phenotype in rdm12.

Lectin receptor-like kinases (LecRLKs) constitute a distinct subfamily of receptor-like kinases and are classified into three types (L, G, and C) based on domain architectures [48]. In plants, LecRLKs serve as crucial immune sensors and play indispensable roles in defense against a wide range of microorganisms [49, 50]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Arabidopsis LecRLKs confer effective resistance to Phytophthora infections [51], while LecRLKs in Nicotiana benthamiana and tomato also contribute to strong resistance to Phytophthora [52]. Recent research further indicates that L-type LecRLKs regulate soybean resistance to Phytophthora sojae [53], and a novel lectin receptor kinase gene, AtG-LecRLK-I, enhances Arabidopsis resistance to bacterial pathogens by modulating stomatal immunity [54]. In this study, GO enrichment analysis identified "defense response to oomycetes" as a unique term in rdm12, with nine LecRLKs being significantly up-regulated in rdm12 (Fig. 5). This finding suggests that LecRLKs are strongly induced in foxtail millet upon S. graminicola infection and likely function as core candidate genes associated with downy mildew resistance. However, in the absence of direct functional validation, the specific regulatory mechanisms through which LecRLKs mediate resistance in foxtail millet remain to be elucidated.

Throughout the long-term co-evolutionary process between plants and pathogens, a precisely coordinated defense network composed of key genes is crucial. This study reveals that in the interaction between foxtail millet and the downy mildew pathogen, genes such as BAK1, CaM/CML, CDPK, Rboh, MEKK3, WRKY, and PR1 play central regulatory roles (Fig. 7). An in-depth analysis of the temporal regulation of these genes in the resistant material rdm12 is of great significance for deciphering the unique immune mechanisms of foxtail millet [55].

Firstly, the early signal perception and rapid amplification mediated by BAK1 demonstrate high efficiency in rdm12. Upon pathogen invasion, BAK1 acts as a co-receptor to swiftly initiate immunity [56]. Our data show that in rdm12, calcium signal sensors (CaM/CML) and kinases (such as CDPK) directly downstream of BAK1 are significantly induced as early as 12 h post-inoculation [57]. This suggests that rdm12 can establish initial signals more rapidly [58]. Recent studies indicate that the successful establishment of early calcium signals relies not only on the activation of Rboh by CDPK to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), but also on a sophisticated feedback mechanism to clear excess calcium ions, preventing signal toxicity and maintaining cellular homeostasis [59]. The rdm12 may better coordinate this balance between "burst and clearance" at the 12 h, laying the foundation for effective defense [60]. Secondly, the transition from early signaling to later defense reinforcement determines the success of disease resistance. At 24 h, the expression of MAPK cascade components (such as MEKK3) and downstream WRKY transcription factors continues to intensify in rdm12, accompanied by strong induction of the PR1 gene [61]. This indicates that signals have been systematically transduced from rapid perception at the plasma membrane to the stage of transcriptional reprogramming within the nucleus. It is particularly noteworthy that we identified several foxtail millet WRKY family members specifically highly expressed at 24 h in rdm12, whose expression patterns differ from those in known model plants [62]. For instance, homology analysis suggests that SiWRKY-29 may positively regulate a set of disease-resistance-related genes unique to foxtail millet [63]. This aligns with previous findings that WRKY genes in foxtail millet exhibit specific response patterns under downy mildew stress [64]. Compared to susceptible materials, the pathways in rdm12-involving BAK1 (perception), CDPK/Rboh (early signaling), MAPK/WRKY (signal integration and amplification), PR1 (defense execution)-exhibit stronger temporal coordination and higher expression intensity [65]. Furthermore, rdm12 may harbor additional positive regulators or weakened negative regulation, resulting in a more defense signal flow. For instance, recent studies have revealed that pathogens often attack such core hubs through effector proteins (e.g., proteases targeting BAK1 cleavage) to suppress immunity [66]. Whether rdm12 possesses variations in BAK1 protein stability or interactions to counteract such attacks represents a direction worthy of in-depth investigation [67].

Glutathione (GSH) is a major plant antioxidant involved in resistance to pathogen infections [68]. It is involved in defense responses against various biotic stresses, including fungi, nematodes, viruses, and bacteria [69]. For instance, overexpression of PdbGSTU10 increases salicylic acid (SA) levels and induces SA signaling-related genes, suggesting that PdbGSTU10 enhances poplar resistance to Alternaria by scavenging ROS and modulating the SA pathway [70]. Glutathione also influences plant resistance to nematodes by precisely regulating the balance between cellular redox status and the production of defense compounds [71]. Furthermore, Zhu et al. (2021) reported that GSH regulates plant immune responses against viruses via SA and ROS-dependent signaling pathways [72]. In this study, we identified ten differentially expressed glutathione S-transferase genes between JG21 and rdm12, suggesting their potential involvement in host defense against S. graminicola.

The diterpenoid biosynthesis pathway serves multiple important biological functions: (1) producing direct disease defense substances (e.g., phytoalexins); (2) activating complex signaling networks to induce defense genes; and (3) acting as signaling molecules or participating in signal transduction [73]. Our results indicated that cytochrome P450, momilactone A synthases, ent-kaurene oxidase 2, terpene synthase TPS13, and ent-copalyl diphosphate synthase are differentially expressed in this pathway and are involved in defense against S. graminicola.

Notably, transcriptome analysis revealed that among the co-DEGs in both JG21 and rdm12 at 12 and 24 h after infection by S. graminicola, three genes (Seita.3G139400, WRKY transcription factor 53; Seita.3G175100, pathogenesis-related protein PRMS; Seita.7G095600, G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine protein kinase) were also annotated in the plant-pathogen interaction pathway of the KEGG database and showed significant differential expression. We speculate that these three genes may be involved in regulating downy mildew resistance, and subsequent studies will focus on further function investigation of these candidates.

Conclusions

In this study, comparative analysis of the foxtail millet variety JG21 and its downy mildew-resistant EMS mutant rdm12 showed that rdm12 exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to the pathogen S. graminicola. With no substantial differences in agronomic or quality traits relative to the wild type, this resistance was associated with elevated activities of defense-related enzymes and increased accumulation of osmoregulatory substances. Transcriptome analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to plant-pathogen interaction, MAPK signaling, diterpenoid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism. Key candidate genes implicated in resistance include WRKY transcription factor 53 (Seita.3G139400), pathogenesis-related protein PRMS (Seita.3G175100), and G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase (Seita.7G095600). Together, these results provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying downy mildew resistance in foxtail millet. Future work will focus on functional characterization of these genes to further elucidate the molecular basis of resistance.
