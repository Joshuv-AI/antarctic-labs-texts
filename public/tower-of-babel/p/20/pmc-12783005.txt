Regulatory dendritic cells and their usefulness in mitigating solid organ transplant rejection
Authors: Pawan Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dendritic cells (DCs) are highly efficient antigen-presenting cells located throughout body tissues and surfaces. Initial studies described these cells as potent activators of naïve T lymphocytes; however, subsequent research has demonstrated that DCs can also regulate T cell activation, survival, and effector functions. DCs possessing T cell regulatory properties, known as regulatory DCs (regDCs), are phenotypically immature cells with modified functionality. In humans, regDCs are typically derived from blood monocytes and characterized by the surface marker expression profile CD11cloCD11bhiCD14+HLA-DR+, low expression of major histocompatibility complex class I molecules, adhesion proteins, and costimulatory markers (e.g., CD40, CD80), and high expression of immune checkpoint and apoptosis-inducing ligands (e.g., PD-L1, Fas ligand). Additionally, these cells secrete abundant anti-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-10 and transforming growth factor-β. Preclinical studies using rodent and nonhuman primate models have demonstrated the efficacy of regDCs in mitigating allograft rejection. Likewise, clinical studies evaluating regDCs have confirmed their safety, efficacy, and feasibility for inducing antigen-specific immune tolerance and/or reducing solid organ transplant rejection. In this manuscript, we review the tolerogenic potential of regDCs, the mechanisms by which regDCs mediate immune tolerance, and the findings from preclinical and clinical studies assessing their use in mitigating solid organ transplant rejection.

INTRODUCTION

Organ transplantation is the treatment of choice for patients suffering from end-stage organ failure. Studies have shown transplantation to be effective in prolonging survival and improving the quality of life of affected patients [1,2]. With advancements in surgical techniques, cross-matching methods, and immunosuppressive medications, an increasing number of solid organ transplants are performed worldwide. According to data from the Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation, approximately 157,494 organ transplantations were performed in 2022. Kidney transplantation was the most frequent (102,090), followed by liver (37,436), heart (8,988), lung (6,784), and pancreas (2,026) transplants.

The success of solid organ transplantation depends on multiple factors, including acceptance of the graft by the recipient's immune system. Since most transplanted organs are allogeneic, they face a constant risk of recognition and rejection by the recipient’s immune system. To prevent rejection, recipients receive long-term administration of immunosuppressive drugs (e.g., tacrolimus, mycophenolate, corticosteroids) [3]. However, prolonged use of these medications is associated with numerous adverse effects, including increased risk of hypertension, infectious diseases, and neoplasms [4].

Dendritic cells (DCs) are innate immune cells that play a crucial role in activating and regulating adaptive immunity. They sample tissues for antigenic molecules via phagocytosis and pinocytosis, undergo maturation, and subsequently process and present antigenic peptides to naïve T cells. The nature and intensity of T cell responses are largely determined by the maturation status of DCs [5]. Immature DCs, characterized by low costimulatory molecule expression and/or elevated secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin [IL]-10, transforming growth factor [TGF]-β), induce T cells with suppressed functional capabilities [5]. These DCs, known as regulatory DCs (regDCs), render T cells anergic to specific antigens or induce apoptosis in activated T cells.

Due to their T cell suppressive properties, researchers have explored regDCs as potential biological therapies for inflammatory, autoimmune, and allergic conditions [6]. Additionally, regDCs have emerged as promising tools for mitigating solid organ graft rejection and have been evaluated extensively in both preclinical and clinical studies. In this manuscript, we review studies focusing on the T cell suppressive and tolerogenic properties of regDCs, and evaluating their efficacy against solid organ transplant rejection.

OVERVIEW OF SOLID ORGAN TRANSPLANT REJECTION

Most transplanted organs, being allogeneic, carry a risk of recognition and rejection by the recipient’s immune system. Graft rejection is generally classified as hyperacute, acute, or chronic, based on the timing of occurrence [7]. Hyperacute rejection occurs within minutes to hours of transplantation and is mediated by preformed antibodies. Acute rejection occurs within days to weeks posttransplantation and is predominantly mediated by cell-mediated immunity. Chronic graft rejection occurs months to years after transplantation and involves both antibody- and cell-mediated immune mechanisms. In kidney transplants, acute rejection can also manifest subclinically, characterized histologically by tubulointerstitial mononuclear infiltration without significant concurrent functional deterioration (variably defined as serum creatinine levels not exceeding 10%, 20%, or 25% above baseline values) [8,9]. Due to advancements in human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing, cross-matching techniques, and immunosuppressive therapies, there has been a significant decline in hyperacute and acute graft rejection rates [10]. However, rates of chronic graft rejection have remained relatively unchanged over recent decades.

The risk of solid organ transplant rejection depends on multiple factors, including the organ being transplanted, donor status (living or deceased), donor and recipient ages, and the degree of donor-recipient matching. In lung transplantation, recent data from a multicenter study showed that 53.3% of patients experienced at least one episode of acute rejection within the first year [11]. According to recent data on kidney transplants, acute rejection within 1 year was highest among recipients aged 18 to 34 years (9.3%) and lowest among recipients aged 65 years or older (5.3%, for transplants performed in 2020) [12]. The reduction in acute kidney graft rejection rates within the first year—from 30% to 40% in the 1990s to 10% to 20% in the early 2000s—highlights significant improvements in transplantation outcomes [13].

Studies have shown that both innate (e.g. natural killer [NK] cells) and adaptive (T cells and B cells) immune systems play the important roles in recognition and rejection of allografts [13]. Hyperacute rejection is mediated by preformed donor-specific antibodies, often generated due to previous exposure to identical or similar antigens of human or microbial origin [14]. Most effector antibodies involved in hyperacute kidney rejection are immunoglobulin G isotypes directed against major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I antigens [15]. Graft-bound antibodies trigger complement activation and secretion of Von Willebrand factor by endothelial cells, resulting in platelet adhesion, aggregation, intravascular thrombosis, lesion formation, and eventual graft loss [14]. Routine pretransplant testing for ABO compatibility and donor-specific antibodies in recipient sera has significantly reduced the incidence of hyperacute rejection.

Acute transplant rejection is predominantly mediated by T cells, although roles for B cells have also been suggested [16]. CD4+ T cells can directly react to graft MHC molecules (alloreactivity), become activated, and orchestrate graft rejection by recruiting and activating other immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, macrophages, NK cells, and B cells [14]. Similarly, CD8+ T cells can become activated in response to allo-MHC molecules. Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells can also be activated indirectly by DCs, which sample donor antigens and migrate to draining lymph nodes [14]. Activated CD4+ T cells secrete interferon (IFN)-γ and other TH1 cytokines, mediating graft rejection via mechanisms analogous to delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions [17]. Activated CD8+ T cells differentiate into cytotoxic T lymphocytes capable of lysing graft cells and facilitating graft rejection [18]. Consistent with the time required for T cell priming, the risk of acute rejection is highest in the first weeks after organ transplantation [2,11]. Other helper T cell subsets, including TH9, TH17, and TH22 cells, can also promote acute transplant rejection by participating in inflammatory reactions [19]. Acute rejection rates have substantially decreased for most solid organs, due to advancements in HLA typing, cross-matching techniques, and improved immunosuppressive regimens [20,21].

Chronic rejection, in contrast, has emerged as the leading cause of solid organ transplant rejection. Chronic rejection involves persistent inflammation and graft-directed immune responses, resulting in organ-specific symptoms and complications [22,23]. The presence of tertiary lymphoid structures—organized lymphoid aggregates that function similarly to secondary lymphoid organs—within grafts characterizes chronic rejection [24]. During chronic renal graft rejection, antibody-dependent complement activation and cell-mediated arteritis result in interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. According to Solez et al. [25], minimal rejection signs develop in over 81% of transplanted kidneys by 1 year and progress to severe lesions in more than 50% of grafts within 5 years. Additional contributors to chronic kidney graft rejection may include calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, chronic organ dysfunction, immune responses to minor HLA antigens, and cytokine secretion by endothelial cells [26]. Roles for antibodies and T cells have also been implicated in chronic liver rejection. In heart transplantation, Glinton et al. [27] identified vascular disease as a significant risk factor for chronic complications. Histological evidence suggests that macrophages, monocytes, and subsets of DCs may directly contribute to chronic cardiac allograft rejection or indirectly contribute by activating B and/or T cells [27].

DENDRITIC CELL SUBSETS AND THEIR ROLES IN THE IMMUNE SYSTEM

DCs are a heterogeneous population of innate immune cells specialized in priming naïve T cells. Many DC subtypes have been described based on their origin, location, function, and expression of surface markers. Broadly, these cells are classified into plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) and conventional DCs (cDCs) [28,29]. pDCs originate from both myeloid and lymphoid precursors during hematopoiesis and constitute 0.1% to 0.5% of peripheral blood mononuclear cells [30]. Under a microscope, freshly isolated pDCs exhibit plasma cell-like morphology and acquire DC-like characteristics upon activation [31]. Activated pDCs produce large quantities of type-I IFNs, primarily mediated via endosomal Toll-like receptors [30,32]. Additionally, pDCs produce proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, express costimulatory molecules, and can present or cross-present antigens to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [33]. pDCs have also been implicated in autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus and systemic sclerosis, and therapeutic inhibitors or blocking monoclonal antibodies against IFN-α or type-I IFN receptor subunit-1 (IFNAR1) are currently being evaluated in clinical studies [30].

cDCs are further classified into lymphoid DCs, myeloid DCs (mDCs), and Langerhans cells (LCs) [28,29]. Lymphoid DCs are located primarily within T cell areas of lymphoid organs, including the spleen, lymph nodes, and Peyer's patches. They can be further subclassified based on the expression of CD4 and CD8 markers and may activate or suppress T cell responses against different antigens [28]. mDCs demonstrate the classical functions of DCs—namely, antigen uptake, processing and presentation to naïve CD4+ T cells [28]. In humans, mDCs are subdivided into CD1c/BDCA-1+ cells and CD141/BDCA-3+ cells, which constitute approximately 50% and 5% to 10% of the total peripheral DC population, respectively [34]. Both CD1c+ and CD141+ mDC subsets efficiently uptake and present antigens to CD4+ T cells; however, CD1c+ mDCs promote TH2- and TH17-biased immune responses, while CD141+ mDCs induce TH1-weighted immunity [35]. Additionally, CD141+ mDCs are more efficient in antigen cross-presentation and priming of CD8+ T cells. Recent studies using humanized mouse models have also confirmed the superiority of CD141+ DCs in priming naïve CD8+ T cells [36]. LCs are epidermis-resident DCs characterized by Birbeck granules and CD1a expression. LCs serve as immune sentinels, interpreting environmental signals and determining whether to initiate an immune response or maintain immune tolerance [37]. Interestingly, recent findings suggest these cells represent a unique subset of tissue-resident macrophages with overlapping DC features [38]. It is also possible that LCs constitute a distinct cell type possessing attributes of both macrophages and DCs.

The functional attributes of DCs largely depend on their maturation status. Generally, immature DCs efficiently capture antigens but exhibit limited T cell priming capabilities [39]. Upon exposure to microbial or inflammatory stimuli, immature DCs undergo maturation and migrate through the lymphatic system to lymphoid organs. Mature DCs secrete abundant proinflammatory cytokines and upregulate costimulatory molecule expression (e.g., CD80 and CD86) [40]. While less efficient in antigen capture, mature DCs effectively prime naïve T cells. Depending on their functional activation status, DCs can also suppress T cell responses and induce T cell tolerance. The subsequent sections of this manuscript specifically focus on DC subsets possessing T cell immunoregulatory properties.

REGULATORY DENDRITIC CELLS AND THEIR PROPERTIES

In addition to their role in priming naïve T cells, DCs possess numerous mechanisms to regulate T cell responses. RegDCs are a specialized DC subset with T cell regulatory properties, playing a critical role in peripheral tolerance. Tolerogenic properties of DCs were first described by Simon et al. [41], who showed that TH1 lymphocytes cocultured with keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH)-pulsed, UV-irradiated LCs became unresponsive to subsequent stimulation with nonirradiated LCs and KLH or exogenous recombinant IL-2. Additionally, these lymphocytes exhibited a defective IL-2 production. In 1995, Lu et al. [42] showed that DCs cultured in the presence of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) alone were MHC class-II+, B7-1dim and B7.2-deficient and induced antigen-specific hyporesponsiveness in allogeneic T lymphocytes. In 1996, the same group demonstrated that DCs generated with GM-CSF alone significantly prolonged median graft survival from 9.5 days to 22 days in a mouse heterotopic heart transplantation model [43]. These experiments involved DC generation from murine bone marrow cells cultured ex vivo with recombinant murine GM-CSF alone or in combination with IL-4. In contrast, human DCs are commonly derived from blood monocytes cultured with recombinant human GM-CSF and IL-4. Human regDCs are phenotypically characterized as CD11cloCD11bhiCD14+HLA-DR+ cells, with low expression of MHC class I molecules, adhesion proteins, and costimulatory molecules (e.g., CD40, CD80, CD86, OX40L), and high expression of immune checkpoint ligands (e.g., PD-L1) and apoptosis-inducing proteins [e.g., Fas ligand (FasL)] [44]. Alongside their phenotypic immaturity, regDCs also exhibit altered cytokine production, characterized by increased secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β [45].

DCs providing diminished antigen presentation and weak costimulatory signals induce T cell anergy and promote T cell differentiation into regulatory T cells (Tregs) [46]. Multiple mechanisms have been described for regDC-mediated suppression to T cell responses. It has been demonstrated that insufficient CD28 signaling impairs activation of the activator protein 1 transcription factor, resulting in transcriptional changes that ultimately lead to T cell anergy and Treg induction [47]. Interestingly, phenotypically mature DCs can also induce Tregs when producing anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, especially under conditions with low proinflammatory cytokine (e.g., IL-12p70) production [48,49]. Travis et al. [50] demonstrated that DCs deficient in the TGF-β activating integrin αvβ8 failed to induce Tregs and instead mediated severe inflammatory bowel disease and autoimmunity in an animal model. IL-10 and TGF-β's roles in DC-mediated immune tolerance are further supported by cancer immunoediting mechanisms, where tumor cells secrete or induce secretion of these cytokines to evade T cell immunity [51].

A variety of other mechanisms underlying regDC-mediated T cell suppression have been identified. These include upregulation of FasL and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), leading to T cell apoptosis [52,53]. RegDCs express immune checkpoint molecules such as programmed death ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2 and inhibitory immunoglobulin-like transcripts, which inhibit T cell receptor signaling and suppress T cell priming [54,55]. RegDCs also alter T cell metabolism by producing indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1, which degrades tryptophan into N-formylkynurenine, thus diminishing cellular proliferation through tryptophan depletion [56]. Additionally, regDCs inhibit hemoglobin via heme oxygenase 1 production, leading to local carbon monoxide accumulation and T cell suppression [57,58]. RegDCs can further restrict effector T cell differentiation and proliferation by shedding CD25 (IL-2 receptor), which sequesters IL-2 from T cells [59]. Iwata et al. [60] showed that regDCs can also promote Treg differentiation via retinoic acid production. Clearly, regDCs possess multiple mechanisms for suppressing T cell responses, attracting significant attention for their therapeutic potential against immune-related disorders.

USE OF REGULATORY DENDRITIC CELLS FOR MITIGATING SOLID ORGAN TRANSPLANT REJECTION

Due to their ability to regulate T cell responses, regDCs have been evaluated in various conditions, including autoimmune diseases, allergies, inflammatory disorders, and solid organ transplantation [6,61]. An initial concern regarding regDC therapy was their potential acquisition of immune-stimulatory properties following in vivo administration. However, accumulating evidence from various experimental models has alleviated this concern, demonstrating the safety and efficacy of regDC therapy in conditions driven by exacerbated immune responses [6,61].

The first reports indicating that regDCs could inhibit experimental allograft rejection appeared in the mid-1990s [43,62]. Numerous subsequent studies demonstrated prolonged graft survival and/or the induction of donor-specific tolerance following regDC infusion, either alone or in combination with immunosuppressive agents, in rodent models [63,64]. In an MHC-mismatched nonhuman primate renal allograft model, Ezzelarab et al. [65] showed prolonged graft survival following infusion of donor-derived regDCs conditioned with vitamin D3 and IL-10. The median graft survival in regDC-treated animals was 113.5 days, compared to 39.5 days in control animals that did not receive DC infusions (n=6, P<0.05) [65]. Additionally, regDC treatment suppressed donor-reactive CD95+ memory T cells and reduced the memory T cell-to-Treg ratio without causing host sensitization or other adverse effects [65]. In another study, monocyte-derived regDCs loaded with cell membrane vesicles induced T cell hyporesponsiveness to donor alloantigens both in vitro and in vivo [66]. These cells also improved graft survival when combined with minimal immunosuppressive drug therapy [66]. Further studies by Ezzelarab et al. [67,68] associated regDC therapy with selective attenuation of donor-specific memory T cell responses and the preservation of donor-reactive CD4+CTLA4hi T cells with immunoregulatory capabilities. Recently, systemically infused regDCs were also reported to mitigate acute kidney injury and protect against ischemia-reperfusion injury without altering tissue-infiltrating inflammatory cell populations [69]. The authors further observed reduced tubular epithelial cell injury following regDC therapy. These findings have significant relevance to transplantation medicine, as ischemia-reperfusion injury is a critical factor in deceased-donor allograft rejection.

The first human study evaluating regDCs was reported by Ralf Steinman's group in early 2001 [70]. The researchers administered a single injection of immature DCs pulsed with influenza matrix peptide (MP) and KLH to two healthy volunteers, observing targeted inhibition of MP-specific CD8+ T cell effector functions. Additionally, the authors detected MP-specific IL-10-producing cells and increased frequencies of MP-specific MHC tetramer-binding cells, which produced lower levels of IFN-γ and lacked cytotoxic activity [70]. In a subsequent study, Dhodapkar and Steinman [71] showed that IFN-γ–producing effector cells re-emerged by 6 months in healthy volunteers vaccinated with regDCs. Cell-mixing experiments revealed that CD8+ T cells isolated 7 days after vaccination from these individuals could suppress MP-specific effectors in a dose- and contact-dependent manner. These findings suggested the induction of MP-specific regulatory CD8+ T cells in response to regDC vaccination [71].

The past two decades have witnessed numerous clinical studies evaluating regDCs, primarily targeting autoimmune diseases. The first clinical study assessing the safety, efficacy, and feasibility of donor-derived regDCs (infused 1 week before transplantation) with the goal of withdrawing immunosuppressive drugs in living donor liver transplant recipients was initiated by Macedo and colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA (NCT03164265). Immunological analyses demonstrated that donor regDCs cross-dressed recipient DCs in blood and lymph nodes, leading to a reduction in frequencies of T-bethiEomeshi memory CD8+ T cells at these sites [72]. There was also an increase in the CD25hiCD127−Foxp3+ regulatory to T-bethiEomeshi CD8+ T cell ratio in the intervention group. These findings suggested that donor-derived regDC may induce systemic changes in host immunity and regulate anti-donor immune reactivity at the time of transplantation [72]. Follow-up analyses in these patients revealed that regDC therapy was associated with reduced frequencies of effector CD8+ T cells and NK cells compared to the control group at 12 months [73]. Moreover, there was decreased donor antigen-specific proliferation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the regDC group, a phenomenon not observed in control patients. Additionally, the authors reported reductions in circulating IL-12p40, IFN-γ, and CXCL10 levels, fewer infiltrating CD8+ T cells, and attenuation of TH1/cytolytic pathways in CD8+ T cells and NK cells in the regDC-treated patients compared with controls at 12 months [73]. Initial results from a recent phase I/IIa clinical study evaluating autologous regDCs infused one day before kidney transplantation have also been published [74]. This study enrolled eight patients treated with regDCs (alongside standard steroid, mycophenolate mofetil, and tacrolimus immunosuppression, with an option to taper mycophenolate mofetil) and a control group of nine patients receiving the same immunosuppressive medications with basiliximab induction but without mycophenolate tapering. The intervention group exhibited 100% graft survival, no mortality, and no significant increase in adverse events during 3-year follow-up. Mycophenolate mofetil was successfully tapered in five patients from the autologous regDC group, leading to tacrolimus monotherapy in two cases. Importantly, the autologous regDC-treated patients also exhibited reduced CD8+ T cell activation markers and increased Foxp3 expression [74].

Currently, a few ongoing clinical studies are evaluating the efficacy of regDCs in mitigating rejection of renal and hepatic allografts. In one such trial being conducted at the University of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania (NCT03726307), researchers are evaluating the safety and feasibility of a single infusion of donor monocyte-derived regDCs seven days prior to transplantation in living donor renal transplant recipients. This phase 1, single-center, open-label, dose-escalation study includes 28 subjects (14 recipients, 14 donors). Recipients receive standard-of-care immunosuppressive therapy with two notable modifications: (1) mycophenolate administration is initiated at the time of donor regDC infusion instead of the day of transplantation, and (2) the pretransplant mycophenolate dose is reduced to half the standard post-transplant dose. Another phase I/II study at the University of Pittsburgh (NCT04208919) is evaluating delayed infusion of donor-derived regDCs for safety, preliminary efficacy, and the potential to enable withdrawal of immunosuppressive drugs in low-risk living-donor liver transplant recipients who are 1 to 3 years posttransplant and meet specific inclusion criteria. Patients on standard-of-care immunosuppression receive a single regDC infusion, followed by gradual tapering of immunosuppressive drugs starting 1 week after infusion. Successfully weaned participants remaining rejection-free will be followed for 3 years after discontinuing immunosuppression. Additionally, the &quot;Improving KIdney Transplantation with Cellular Therapy&quot; (i-KITCaT) trial is underway at Westmead Hospital, New South Wales, Australia (NCT06243289). This trial aims to exploit the potential of regDCs to favorably modulate immunological responses during acute kidney injury in transplantation settings. Successful outcomes from this study could significantly increase the donor organ pool by enabling the acceptance of kidneys exposed to medical or surgical factors leading to acute kidney injury. RegDC therapy has significant advantages over conventional immunosuppressive drugs, such as tacrolimus, mycophenolate, cyclosporine, and corticosteroids. These conventional drugs are frequently associated with adverse effects, including an increased risk of hypertension, infections, nephrotoxicity, malignancies, and other conditions [75]. The adverse effects of immunosuppressive medications typically arise due to their broad targeting of biological pathways active in multiple cell types and their general immunosuppressive properties. For instance, calcineurin signaling (targeted by tacrolimus) is crucial in neurons, muscle cells, and developmental processes such as the formation of the heart, bone, cartilage, muscle, and blood vessels [76]. Corticosteroids similarly influence gene transcription across multiple cell types and exert immediate effects by interacting with membrane-associated glucocorticoid receptors [77]. In contrast, regDCs specifically target alloreactive T lymphocytes and facilitate Treg formation. Although long-term data are limited, preclinical and clinical studies with regDCs have demonstrated their safety and feasibility without significant adverse effects.

Despite advantages, regDC therapies have their limitations. These include labor-intensive and complex manufacturing processes, requirements of highly trained personnel and sophisticated facilities, and high treatment costs [78]. Variability in regDC manufacturing protocols may also influence cell characteristics, thus necessitating rigorous quality control measures. Approaches aimed at in situ regDC generation, such as using nanoparticles or biomaterials loaded with growth factors and immunosuppressive cytokines or molecules (e.g., IL-10, rapamycin), could potentially circumvent these limitations. Moreover, continuous in situ generation of regDCs could enhance graft tolerance and further reduce rejection rates. Another critical aspect of regDC therapy is regulatory approval and compliance. The absence of clear policies and regulatory frameworks has led to the proliferation of unregulated and potentially unsafe cell-based therapies globally. Due to increased awareness, many developed nations, including the USA and European Union countries, have implemented stringent regulations for cell-based therapy production and use, with other countries progressively adopting similar standards [79].

CONCLUSION

DCs, as critical regulators of adaptive immunity, hold immense therapeutic potential across a wide spectrum of conditions, including autoimmune, inflammatory, allergic diseases, cancers, infectious diseases, and graft rejection. RegDCs, in particular, suppress T cell responses via mechanisms that induce functional impairment or apoptosis of T cells. Multiple protocols tailored to specific tolerance requirements have been described for generating regDCs. Studies in animal models, including nonhuman primates, have demonstrated the safety, efficacy, and feasibility of regDC use in transplant recipients. Clinical studies evaluating regDCs have also yielded promising outcomes in liver and kidney transplant patients. Currently, ongoing clinical trials are further assessing the safety and efficacy of regDC therapy in liver and kidney transplant recipients.

The field of DC-based therapy is still evolving, with newer methodologies, such as nanoparticle or biomaterial-based strategies, actively being investigated to modulate DC functions. Genetic modification of DCs to improve their survival, stability, and immunoregulatory properties is another promising avenue. Genetically engineered DCs have shown encouraging results for cancer immunotherapy and treatment of autoimmune diseases [80,81]. However, such therapies are likely to undergo increased scrutiny by regulatory agencies. A platform for in situ DC modulation composed of a common component (pharmacologically active agents) capable of generating regDCs combined with donor-derived antigens could provide an efficient and straightforward approach. Additionally, because a large proportion of ex vivo generated DCs undergo apoptosis shortly after infusion, in situ protocols for regDC generation might offer significantly improved outcomes. Given the considerable promise and potential for regDC-based therapies to become standard-of-care treatments in solid organ transplantation, this approach represents a beacon of hope for researchers and patients facing end-stage organ failure.
