A de novo SOX11 mutation causing hypogonadotropic hypogonadism: a case report and literature review
Authors: Shanshan Chu, Xuewen Yuan, Qing Niu, Wei Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSOX11 variants have been associated with Coffin–Siris syndrome and a broader neurodevelopmental spectrum (IDDMOH), but their role in isolated or syndromic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) remains under-recognized.Case presentationWe report a 13-year-old Chinese girl who presented with delayed puberty (Tanner B1, PH1), infantile uterus and low basal gonadotropins. Trio whole-exome sequencing identified a de-novo heterozygous SOX11 c.346_348del (p.Tyr116del) variant. Olfactory and pituitary MRI revealed bilateral olfactory-bulb/nerve hypoplasia and a pituitary height of 3.4 mm, fulfilling Kallmann-syndrome criteria. GnRH-pump therapy restored gonadotropin output within 72 h. A systematic literature review of 32 additional SOX11-related HH cases (total n = 33) showed that 25/26 phenotyped individuals exhibited Coffin–Siris-compatible features, whereas 13/33 met Kallmann-syndrome criteria. Thirty unique variants were identified, 17/30 clustering within the HMG domain and 15/18 proven de-novo.ConclusionsSOX11 should be included in Kallmann syndrome gene panels. We propose “SOX11-related disorder” as an overarching diagnostic framework for all individuals with SOX11 likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants, mandating comprehensive baseline evaluation—including endocrine assessment, neurodevelopmental screening, and MRI of olfactory tracts and pituitary—regardless of initial presentation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06974-5.

Introduction

The SOX11 gene maps to chromosome 2p25.2 and encodes a member of the SRY (sex-determining region on the Y chromosome)-related high-mobility-group (HMG) transcription factor family [1]. The phenotypic entry associated with SOX11 variants is catalogued as OMIM #615,866. Originally designated “Coffin-Siris syndrome 9,” the nomenclature has been revised to “intellectual developmental disorder with microcephaly, with or without ocular malformations or hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (IDDMOH)” [2]. Coffin–Siris syndrome (CSS) is characterized by developmental/cognitive delay, absent or hypoplastic distal phalanges and/or nails of the fifth fingers or toes, generalized hypotonia, and a distinctive craniofacial appearance [3]. Studies have demonstrated marked phenotypic variability among individuals with SOX11 variants, and a subset of patients had never received a clinical diagnosis of Coffin–Siris syndrome prior to molecular testing [4, 5].

This study retrospectively analyzes a child with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) caused by an SOX11 variant, evaluated at our center in May 2025. The aim is to delineate the clinical and molecular spectrum of SOX11-related disease, substantiate the role of SOX11 in HH, and underscore that SOX11 should be considered a candidate gene for Kallmann syndrome (KS).

Clinical information

The patient is a 13-year-old 4-month-old female who was admitted for evaluation of delayed puberty noted four months prior. Her parents reported that she had not developed breast tissue (thelarche), pubic or axillary hair, or experienced menarche by age 13 years. One year earlier, an outside evaluation had revealed estradiol < 21 pg/mL, FSH 1.73 mIU/mL, LH < 0.42 mIU/mL, β-hCG < 0.1mIU/mL (reference:0-5.3 mIU/mL), AFP 1.38 ng/mL (reference: 0.5–10.0 ng/mL), and AMH 13.25 ng/mL (reference: 0.97–11.76ng/mL). Her karyotype was 46,XX. Thyroid function tests were normal, including TSH 1.00mIU/L (reference: 0.51–4.3mIU/L), free T3 4.80pmol/l (reference: 3.93-7.70pmol/l), and free T4 15.5pmol/l(reference: 12.6-21.0pmol/l). No intervention was undertaken. She was subsequently referred to our Pediatric Endocrinology and Genetics Unit for comprehensive evaluation. The patient reported normal olfaction and denied headache, vomiting, visual loss, or polyuria/polydipsia.

Perinatal and developmental history

She was the second child of a gravida 2, para 2 mother, delivered vaginally at term with a birth weight of 4.1 kg. Mild perinatal asphyxia was noted, and the mother had CMV infection during pregnancy. Feeding difficulties and global developmental delay were observed early in life—she achieved independent sitting at 10 months and independent walking at 2 years of age. Following rehabilitation, she began to speak at 5 years; her current IQ is 45 (WISC), and she attends special education. Strabismus surgery was performed at 7 years of age. The family history was negative for genetic disorders, and the mother reported menarche at 14 years.

Physical examination

The patient’s vital signs were as follows: temperature 36.7 °C, heart rate 85 beats per minute, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, and blood pressure 107/66 mmHg. Her weight was 48 kg, height 155.5 cm (50th–75th percentile for age), and arm span 157 cm. Dysmorphic features included full cheeks, fair skin, highly arched eyebrows, epicanthal folds, a broad nasal bridge, thick everted lips, low-set ears, clinodactyly of the fifth fingers (Fig. 1), and impaired convergence. Tanner staging revealed breast stage 1 (B1) and pubic hair stage 1 (PH1), with no axillary hair. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations

Basal sex steroid levels were as follows: estradiol 13.38 pg/mL, progesterone 0.33 nmol/L, and testosterone < 0.34 ng/mL. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulation testing was performed using gonadorelin 100 µg intravenously. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels were measured at 30, 60, and 90 min, yielding the following results: FSH 1.8→3.0→3.9→3.8 mIU/mL and LH 0.45→5.24→5.06→3.99 mIU/mL. Additional hormonal assessments showed anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) 7.87 ng/mL (reference range: 0.65–9.98 ng/mL) and inhibin-B 48.22 pg/mL (reference range: 11–111 pg/mL).

Renal ultrasonography revealed a normal right kidney (106 × 41 mm) and a 56 × 25 mm multiseptate cystic mass occupying the left renal fossa, suggestive of renal dysplasia. Pelvic ultrasonography showed an infantile uterus (17 × 6 × 8 mm) and small ovaries (18 × 6 × 7 mm and 17 × 7 × 8 mm). Bone age was assessed as 12 years using the Greulich-Pyle method, consistent with her chronological age.

Olfactory magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which was limited by motion artifact, demonstrated shallow olfactory sulci, a small and ill-defined left olfactory bulb, a non-visualized right olfactory bulb, and poorly visualized olfactory tracts. These findings were compatible with olfactory bulb and nerve hypoplasia and were suspicious for Kallmann syndrome. Pituitary MRI showed a height of 3.4 mm with normal signal intensity and stalk.

Genetic analysis

Trio-based whole-exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous SOX11 variant (c.346_348del, p.Tyr116del). The variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) per ACMG/AMP guidelines (PM2 + PM4), as the de novo evidence was downgraded from PS2 (Strong) due to lack of genetically confirmed parentage. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant (Fig. 2). The associated phenotype included intellectual disability with microcephaly, with or without ocular anomalies or hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Tyrosine 116 was completely conserved across species (Fig. 3). AlphaFold structural modeling demonstrated a marked conformational change, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 32.014 Å, predicting impaired protein function (Fig. 4). Although chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was not performed, copy number variation (CNV) analysis based on whole-exome sequencing (WES) data did not identify any clinically significant CNVs.

Follow-up and treatment

The patient was initiated on a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse pump at a dose of 10 µg every 90 min. After 3 days of treatment, her estradiol level increased to 54.09 pg/mL, FSH to 4.9 mIU/mL, and LH to 4.92 mIU/mL, indicating a good response; therapy was therefore continued. At 3 months, follow-up assessments showed estradiol 113 pmol/L, LH 9.67 mIU/mL, and FSH 6.0 mIU/mL. The patient progressed to Tanner breast stage 2 (B2) and pubic hair stage 2 (PH2). Repeat pelvic ultrasonography revealed a uterus measuring 33 × 16 × 20 mm and ovaries measuring 30 × 12 × 13 mm and 32 × 10 × 11 mm.

Literature review

We systematically reviewed all previously reported cases of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) attributed to SOX11 variants [5–9]. Together with the present patient, the cohort comprises 33 individuals (14 females, 19 males). Age at diagnosis was unavailable for two; 17 were adults (> 18y) and 14 were children. Phenotypic descriptions were lacking in seven. Among the 26 fully characterized patients, 25 exhibited CSS-compatible features. Digital anomalies were most frequent (10/26), followed by global developmental delay/intellectual disability (8/26) and microcephaly (6/26). Additional findings included neonatal feeding difficulties, high-arched palate, ptosis, epicanthus, amblyopia, strabismus, sensorineural hearing loss, and structural renal defects. Gonadal phenotypes comprised delayed or absent puberty (12/26), cryptorchidism/micropenis/hypospadias (6/26), and uterine hypoplasia/primary amenorrhea (4/26). Thirteen individuals met diagnostic criteria for Kallmann syndrome (13/33). Of the 12 who underwent dedicated neuro-imaging, olfactory-bulb/nerve hypoplasia was documented in five and pituitary hypoplasia in three.

Molecular analysis revealed 30 unique variants across 34 alleles. Missense changes predominated (26/30), with the remainder being nonsense or frameshift mutations; only the current patient harbored an in-frame deletion (p.Tyr116del). Two variants—p.Ser345Alafs13 and p.Gly84Asp—were each identified in two unrelated probands. Parental segregation was available for 18 cases: 15 variants occurred de novo, one (p.Val199Leu) was maternally inherited, and two affected siblings shared p.Asp244Glu inherited from their reportedly asymptomatic father. Importantly, the mother transmitting p.Val199Leu and the affected sibs also carried additional pathogenic variants known to produce HH, leaving the definitive contribution of SOX11 in these kindreds uncertain. Genotype–structure mapping showed preferential clustering of pathogenic alleles within the HMG domain (17/30); detailed variant topography is depicted in Figs. 4 and 5.

Discussion

Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) results from defective development, migration, or function of GnRH neurons, or from impaired synthesis and secretion of gonadotropins [10]. To date, more than 60 genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism [11]. SOX-family proteins constitute a group of transcription factors essential for multiple developmental processes. In humans, SOX11 is expressed in the developing central nervous system and is required for neuronal differentiation and cell proliferation [12]. The SOX11 gene (NM_003108.4) is intron-less and comprises a single exon that encodes a 441-amino-acid polypeptide. As a member of the SRY-related HMG-box family, SOX11 belongs to the SOXC subgroup together with SOX4 and SOX12. SOXC proteins contain a highly conserved N-terminal HMG-box DNA-binding domain and a C-terminal transactivation domain [13]. In addition, the HMG domain governs protein–protein interactions and controls the nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of SOX proteins [14]. Pathogenic variants in SOX2 and SOX10 have been conclusively shown to cause human hypogonadotropic hypogonadism [15, 16].

In 2014, Tsurusaki et al. first reported that pathogenic variants in SOX11 are associated with a mild form of Coffin–Siris syndrome [7]. Coffin–Siris syndrome (CSS) is a multiple congenital malformation syndrome caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in genes encoding subunits of the BAF (BRG1/BRM-associated factor) complex—an ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complex central to epigenetic regulation of gene expression [3]. The classic Coffin–Siris phenotype comprises coarse facial features, sparse scalp hair, thick/long eyebrows and eyelashes, a broad nasal bridge with thick alae, a wide mouth with full and everted lips, generalized hirsutism, absent or hypoplastic fifth finger/toe nails or distal phalanges, developmental delay, and multi-organ malformations [17]. SOX11 participates in a transcriptional network downstream of the BAF complex that drives cellular differentiation; consequently, SOX11 variants produce a milder phenotype than upstream BAF-complex subunit variants and are identified in only ~ 2% of patients with clinically suspected CSS [7]. These data indicate that the phenotypic spectrum of SOX11 variants extends beyond the classic CSS facial gestalt, exhibiting substantial heterogeneity.

Among the genes associated with Coffin–Siris syndrome (CSS), only pathogenic variants in PHF6 had previously been linked to hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) [17]. In 2022, Cho et al. first reported SOX11 pathogenic variants in patients with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, thereby expanding the phenotypic spectrum of Coffin–Siris syndrome to include HH [8]. Al-Jawahiri et al. described 38 additional individuals with SOX11 variants and documented extensive phenotypic heterogeneity, with idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (IHH) emerging as a recurrent feature [5]. Sun et al. screened 1,810 patients with IHH and found that SOX11-variant carriers uniformly exhibited endocrine abnormalities, including IHH with or without anosmia, growth-hormone deficiency, hypothalamic–pituitary structural defects, and hypothyroidism, indicating that human SOX11-related disease may originate from combined hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction [6]. These findings indicate that HH and CSS do not arise from distinct SOX11 allelic lesions, but represent divergent phenotypic expressions within a single SOX11-variant spectrum.

The proband presented with mild CSS-associated features, including global developmental delay, highly arched eyebrows, epicanthal folds, a broad nasal bridge, thick and everted lips, low-set ears, and clinodactyly of the fifth finger, together with abnormal extra-ocular movements and renal malformations. Microcephaly was absent. She was referred for delayed puberty; work-up disclosed a hypoplastic uterus and ovaries, endocrine studies consistent with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH), and MRI evidence of olfactory-nerve hypoplasia, raising suspicion for Kallmann syndrome (KS). The differential diagnosis between constitutional delay of puberty (CDP) and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism in early adolescence is challenging. While the LH peak of 5.24 mIU/mL may represent early pubertal activation, the absence of spontaneous progression, requirement for exogenous estrogen, and presence of olfactory system abnormalities collectively support a diagnosis of HH. Long-term follow-up with gonadotropin levels off treatment would provide definitive confirmation. A systematic literature review revealed that almost all individuals with SOX11-related HH exhibit variable CSS-like features. Among the HH-associated phenotypes, delayed or absent pubertal development is most common, followed by cryptorchidism, micropenis and hypospadias. Thirteen patients fulfilled diagnostic criteria for Kallmann syndrome, and three had pituitary hypoplasia, underscoring that MRI of the olfactory tracts and pituitary should be routinely performed in individuals harbouring SOX11 variants. As early as 2014, Tsurusaki and colleagues documented one individual with a pathogenic SOX11 variant and Coffin–Siris syndrome who exhibited uterine hypoplasia and experienced menarche at the age of 17 years [5]. These observations underscore that hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) may accompany Coffin–Siris syndrome (CSS) due to SOX11 variants; without systematic gonadal-function assessment the diagnosis is likely to be overlooked.

To date, more than 60 individuals harboring pathogenic SOX11 variants have been reported in the literature [9]. Most reported missense variants cluster within the high-mobility-group (HMG) domain, implying a critical role for this region in protein function, while mutations in the C-terminal transactivation domain (TAD) similarly impair transcriptional activity. The tyrosine residue at position 116 demonstrates complete conservation across vertebrate species and absolute conservation throughout the HMG domain of the SOX transcription factor family. This residue is situated in the critical DNA-binding helix, supporting its functional importance. Literature review confirms that SOX11 variants are predominantly located within the HMG domain; however, we observed no correlation between specific mutation position and the presence of co-existing hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

The mechanism by which SOX11 variants precipitate hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) remains unresolved. GnRH neurons—the central regulators of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis—originate in the olfactory placode and subsequently migrate to the hypothalamus. SOX11 haploinsufficiency may disrupt this ontogenetic trajectory, because (i) SOX11 is endogenously expressed in murine hypothalamic GnRH neurons and (ii) in vitro studies demonstrate that SOX11 directly stimulates both GnRH transcription and peptide secretion [18, 19].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, subcloning was not performed to independently sequence both alleles of the 3-bp deletion in the proband. Although the heterozygous status was supported by WES data showing a balanced VAF of 50%, the characteristic deletion signature on Sanger chromatograms, and parental confirmation of de novo origin, subcloning remains the gold standard for definitive allele-specific validation. Second, as a single-case report, the generalizability of our findings is limited, and further functional studies are needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanism by which this SOX11 variant contributes to hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.

Conclusions

This study represents the first mainland-Chinese report of HH caused by an SOX11 variant since 2022. We acknowledge that SOX11 genotype-phenotype correlation remains incompletely defined, with identical variants producing phenotypes ranging from isolated HH to classic CSS. We propose “SOX11-related disorder” as an overarching diagnostic framework mandating comprehensive baseline evaluation— including endocrine assessment, neurodevelopmental screening, and MRI of olfactory tracts and pituitary—regardless of initial presentation, with annual multidisciplinary follow-up. Our proband’s olfactory-nerve hypoplasia supports adding SOX11 to KS candidate genes. However, the variable expressivity evident in our patient’s mild CSS features with prominent HH underscores that neither CSS nor KS labels alone are sufficient. Clinicians must maintain vigilance for both neurodevelopmental and endocrine manifestations to prevent clinical neglect from narrow diagnostic categorization.
