Patient involvement in the development of childhood, adolescent and young adult cancer survivorship guidelines: recommendations from EU-CAYAS-NET ambassadors
Authors: Jikke Wams, Jaap den Hartogh, Hannah Gsell, Carina Schneider, Barbara Brunmair, Anna Zettl, Zuzana Tomášiková, Anita Kienesberger, Katie Rizvi, Ana M. Totovina, Roderick Skinner, Jeroen te Dorsthorst, Helena J. H. van der Pal, Leontien C. M. Kremer, Elvira C. van Dalen, Renée L. Mulder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurvivors of Childhood, Adolescent, and Young Adult (CAYA) cancer can face long-term health complications due to adverse effects of cancer and its treatment, necessitating lifelong follow-up care. Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) provide evidence-based recommendations for late effects surveillance. Despite the recognized value of patient involvement, its integration into CPG development remains inconsistent and underexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify effective strategies for integrating patient involvement across all phases of CPG development in CAYA cancer survivorship care.MethodsThis qualitative study consisted of a half-day workshop that was conducted during the European Network of Youth Cancer Survivors (EU-CAYAS-NET) ambassador training event in November 2023 in Vienna, Austria. Forty-nine project ambassadors, all CAYA cancer survivors, participated in presentations and interactive sessions, including breakout discussions and mind-mapping exercises. Data from plenary discussions and mind-maps were analyzed using qualitative content analysis to extract key recommendations and conditions for successful patient involvement in CPG development.ResultsParticipants formulated 12 recommendations and 11 conditions for effective patient involvement in CPG development, structured across four phases: recruitment, preparation, development, and dissemination. Key areas included clarity, support, collaboration, respect, feasibility, and communication.DiscussionThese findings emphasize the need for a systematic approach to patient involvement, ensuring patient representatives are integrated as active stakeholders throughout CPG development, in a role that fits the goal of each guideline.ConclusionThis study proposes a structured framework for patient involvement in CAYA cancer survivor CPG development, advocating for continuous patient involvement to enhance guideline quality, adoption, and impact. Future efforts should align with our proposed approach to patient involvement in CPG development to establish standardized practices within existing frameworks.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00855-y.

Background

In recent decades, more and more children, adolescents, and young adults (CAYAs) are surviving cancer as a result of the progress made with optimized and targeted therapies. Consequently, the global population of CAYA cancer survivors continues to grow [1, 2]. However, they face a higher risk of experiencing chronic health issues resulting from toxic cancer treatments and the cancer itself [3]. These chronic health issues may impact multiple domains, including all organ systems and psychosocial functioning. Their severity and manifestation can differ widely between individuals and may evolve over time [3, 4].

Addressing these health issues requires life-long surveillance, a need actively advocated for by CAYA cancer survivors within the European Network of Youth Cancer Survivors (EU-CAYAS-NET) project. EU-CAYAS-NET is a patient-driven initiative under Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan, built on a consortium consisting of nine beneficiaries and 32 associated partners across Europe. The network strives to improve the quality of life for CAYA cancer survivors by establishing a pan-European network of survivors from over 20 European countries. It collaborates with healthcare professionals, researchers, and policymakers, and develops resources as well as an interactive platform for survivors to participate in social networking and peer support. This platform functions as an open community, welcoming patients, survivors, caregivers, and professionals without formal membership requirements. The resources, including educational materials and digital tools, are publicly available via the project’s website and are intended for survivors, caregivers, and healthcare providers [5]. The network also focuses on enhancing survivorship care across Europe, for example, by developing clinical practice guidelines (CPGs).

CPGs improve life-long medical and psychosocial survivorship care. They are informed by evidence and based on a transparent process. They report on appropriate practice recommendations to help prevent, detect early, and adequately treat late effects after cancer [6]. A standardized procedure for developing CPGs in CAYA cancer survivorship care has been introduced by the International Late Effects of Childhood Cancer Guideline Harmonization Group (IGHG). The IGHG is a global collaboration in guideline development to harmonize guidelines in order to improve quality of survivorship care and minimize duplication of efforts [7].

Within CPG development, the value of patient involvement is recognized. Patient involvement in research means the research being conducted ‘with’ or ‘by’ patients rather than ‘to’, ‘about’ or ‘for’ them [8]. Its importance is supported by two key arguments: one grounded in ethical principles – patients are directly affected by the outcomes and have a democratic right to influence its direction – and another emphasizing its potential to improve the quality, relevance, and adoption of the research [9]. Additionally, research funders increasingly advocate for and mandate patient involvement in the design, conduct, and dissemination of health and social care research [10]. Patient involvement is often described under the umbrella of patient and public involvement (PPI), where the term”public” may include individuals with relevant experience, such as patients, caregivers, users of health and social care services, and representatives of related organizations [11].

According to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), patients should be involved directly in producing guidelines as formal members of their committees and working groups, and according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), consumers should be involved in all stages of guideline development [12, 13]. Despite these recommendations, current practices often limit patient involvement to the initial and/or final stages of CPG development, and rarely integrate it throughout the entire development process. Moreover, standardized methodologies for patient involvement are lacking [14]. A key barrier to achieving more meaningful involvement is that professionals often lack the knowledge, skills, and experience to engage patients effectively in a way that aligns with the principles of contemporary research practice, making it difficult to implement patient involvement in practice [9]. An additional challenge is ensuring that involved patients adequately reflect the broader patient population. This shows the need for concrete recommendations on how and when patient involvement should be designed and conducted, and who to involve, ideally being based on the input of patients themselves.

The EU-CAYAS-NET project highlights the critical importance of patient involvement and engagement in research and therefore aims to improve patient involvement in developing CPGs for CAYA cancer survivorship care. To advance patient involvement effectively and avoid misconceptions about its complexity or controversy, this study aimed to gather clear insights from the CAYA cancer survivor community on integrating patient involvement across different phases of CPG development.

Methods

Study design: workshop for CAYA cancer survivors

This qualitative study consisted of a half-day workshop held during the in-person EU-CAYAS-NET training event in Vienna, Austria on 24 November 2023 [15]. The overall aim of the workshop was to identify effective strategies for integrating patient involvement across all phases of CPG development in CAYA cancer survivorship care. Main questions were: 1) who to involve; 2) how to recruit; 3) when to involve; and 4) how to involve CAYA cancer survivors in the CPG development process. Our study followed key principles of qualitative inquiry, including transparency of procedures, systematic documentation of data collection and analysis, and reflexivity regarding the researchers’ positionality.

The workshop was organized by three individuals from the Guideline Unit of the Princess Máxima Center for pediatric oncology with recognized expertise in international guideline development (ED, RM, LK), a researcher (JW), and a patient expert who is a CAYA cancer survivor (JH). All five individuals were trained in facilitating workshops of this kind, ensuring they had the necessary skills and experience to guide the session effectively. The workshop featured a presentation introducing CPGs in general, followed by a presentation introducing the various phases of CPG development (Fig. 1). Then, participants were invited to take part in an active session in four breakout groups led by the organizers, practicing various steps in the guideline development phase: formulating clinical questions, selecting evidence, and translating evidence into recommendations. After a short break, another plenary presentation informed the participants about existing methods for collecting patient preferences. This was followed by a live conversational interview with two patient experts who were at the time involved in the development of a CPG, sharing their experiences (Appendix 1) [16]. After that, the participants were again invited to participate in an active session in four breakout groups led by the organizers performing a mind-mapping exercise on best fitting strategies for patient involvement in CPG development, potential barriers and facilitators, and appropriate roles for patients during the CPG development process. The workshop concluded with a plenary discussion where the breakout groups presented their findings and clarified their mind-maps. Participants and recruitment

All participants in this study were EU-CAYAS-NET project ambassadors. Within EU-CAYAS-NET, the ambassador program is designed to empower young cancer survivors to contribute actively to improving survivorship care across Europe. Project ambassadors are adult CAYA cancer survivors diagnosed before the age of 39 years and selected for their motivation to engage in project activities, share lived-experience insights, disseminate results within their member states, and diverse backgrounds. A detailed description of the recruitment and selection procedures, as well as available demographic information of the ambassadors, has been published in the EU-CAYAS-NET Report on the Ambassador Programme [17]. Authors JH, CS and BB were involved in the selection of ambassadors.

Throughout the entire EU-CAYAS-NET project, ambassadors engaged in activities such as peer visits, roundtables, focus group discussions, and workshops [18]. At the time of the workshop, the ambassador program consisted of 56 survivor advocates from 29 countries with diverse backgrounds. All ambassadors were invited via email to attend the two-day EU-CAYAS-NET training event, which included the workshop on patient involvement in CPG development.

Data analysis

The analysis of the workshop was conducted between January and March 2024. It followed a conventional inductive approach to qualitative content analysis [19]. First, the audio recordings of the plenary session were transcribed verbatim for each of the four breakout groups. The audio recordings were deleted as soon as the transcripts were finished. Two persons (JH, JW) studied the transcripts independently and marked texts that appeared to describe recommendations or conditions for successful patient involvement in CPG development. Single or few word(s) codes and a number for each recommendation or condition were written in the transcription margins. Both persons cross-checked the identified recommendations and conditions against the corresponding mind maps to ensure accuracy and completeness. Once all transcripts were checked, they examined the data collaboratively. Based on this process, an initial set of recommendations and conditions were formulated for each group. The recommendations and conditions from all four breakout groups were then combined, with overlapping items removed to create a preliminary consolidated list. This preliminary list was further refined in consultation with three guideline experts, who reviewed the recommendations for clarity, completeness, and consistency. To validate our interpretations, the recommendations that were directly derived from the transcripts and mindmaps were subsequently returned to all workshop participants. They were invited to review the recommendations and provide additions or corrections. Based on the minor feedback received, the recommendations and conditions were adjusted accordingly.

Patient and public involvement

Patient involvement underpinned this study, as EU‑CAYAS‑NET is a fully patient-driven project led by young people living with and beyond cancer. Patients involved in the design and conduct of the study were representatives from the project’s nine beneficiary organizations. These individuals were distinct from the study participants as the workshop participants were project ambassadors who attended the training event, recruited by the coordinating patient organization.

Results

Forty-nine out of 56 project ambassadors, all CAYA cancer survivors, attended the training event and participated in the guideline workshop. The participants represented 27 countries, primarily from Europe (Fig. 2). Based on four mind maps (Appendix 2) and the total group discussion, a total of 12 recommendations and 11 conditions for successful patient involvement in CPG development were formulated. These were structured into the various phases of CPG development: the preparation phase, the development phase and the dissemination and implementation phase (Fig. 1). To complement this, a recruitment phase was added. Recommendations for patient involvement

In the recruitment phase, the focus is on identifying who are suitable candidates and how to ensure an effective recruitment process. The recommendations emphasize inclusion of patient representatives: patient advocates with relevant lived experiences that align with the scope of the guideline, as well as patient experts who possess specific skills or expertise based on their education, profession, or field of interest. To facilitate recruitment, collaboration between CPG developers and patient organizations/communities is essential to identify and engage appropriate candidates. Such collaboration requires sustained and ongoing relationships, ideally initiated before specific recruitment. Additionally, providing clear and accessible information is crucial, including a plain language summary of the guideline’s scope, the significance of patient involvement, a well-defined task description, and an overview of potential personal benefits for involvement.

In the preparation, development, and dissemination and implementation phases, the recommendations address when and how to involve patients. According to the participants, patient representatives should be engaged as stakeholders in every meeting of the guideline panel and should have the opportunity to provide both prompted and unprompted feedback throughout the entire development process. To foster meaningful involvement, the guideline panel chair(s) should appoint a liaison moderator to facilitate communication between the patient representatives and the CPG panel. A liaison moderator should have strong communication skills, an understanding of the guideline development process, and the ability to translate between technical language and patient perspectives. Furthermore, contribution should include formulating relevant clinical questions and highlighting potential gaps in the guideline from a patient perspective. Finally, patient representatives should maintain regular communication with the broader patient community, providing updates on the guideline’s progress and seeking additional input when needed, e.g., collecting firsthand experiences from individual patients to inform the CPG.

Conditions and barriers to patient involvement

In addition to the recommendations focused on the different phases of CPG development, a list with 11 conditions for successful patient involvement in CPG development was formulated (see Table 2). This list emphasizes key principles for fostering effective and respectful involvement. It highlights the importance of clear communication (1), training/education (2), transparency (3), accessibility (4), safety (5), and taking all involvement seriously (6). It also emphasizes the integration of soft skills, e.g., emphatic listening and constructive facilitation (7), ensuring that patients feel well-equipped and emotional prepared (8), and leave the option to stop participating (9). Lastly, taking into account patient representatives’ time constraints (10) and providing financial compensation (if possible) (11) throughout the process are of importance.

Accessibility may include scheduling panel meetings outside office hours. CPG developers should also consider that patient representatives may have specific needs related to conditions such as fatigue, hearing or visual impairments, or individual differences, including those associated with neurodiversity. They may include requirements such as flexible engagement formats or additional time for information processing. Supportive measures, such as assistance with building access or scheduled breaks during sessions, may likewise be necessary.

Moreover, several barriers for successful patient involvement in CPG development were identified, including language difficulties, a lack of human and financial resources, reduced working capacity, inaccessibility, time constraints, financial and psychological burdens, unreadiness to share, and cultural gaps. These potential obstacles hindering successful patient involvement in CPG development were considered with great care and sensitivity when formulating our recommendations and conditions.

Discussion

Our study shows that evaluation of patient involvement does not need to be complex to be useful. The most important principles in the process of patient involvement in CPG development encompass clarity, support, collaboration, respect, feasibility, and communication. To our knowledge, this is the first paper outlining recommendations and conditions for effective patient involvement in CAYA cancer survivorship care guidelines based on direct input from patients.

Our participants identified two key groups regarding who to involve in CPG development: patient advocates with lived experiences who support a broader cancer-affected community, and patient experts who in addition to their lived experience have specialized skills or expertise through training or experience. This aligns with literature emphasizing the involvement of individuals diagnosed with the condition addressed in the CPG as representatives of the treated population contributing experiential knowledge that complements scientific evidence and clinical expertise [14]. For those patient advocates who wish support in developing additional skills, training initiatives such as the European Patients’ Academy on Therapeutic Innovation (EUPATI) offer structured education to help them grow into patient experts [21]. Although not explicitly found in our study, prior research suggests that other representatives, such as parents or siblings, could also be considered for recruitment, depending on the CPG’s focus [22, 23]. Based on the practical experiences of experts in guideline development, we suggest that involving approximately two to seven patient representatives balances diversity of perspectives with practical feasibility.

For meaningful patient involvement, it is important to establish clear inclusion criteria—such as age, language proficiency, and lived experience—tailored to each guideline. Furthermore, accessibility was broadly acknowledged as a critical factor for meaningful patient involvement, particularly in relation to survivors whose involvement may be affected by late effects such as fatigue, cognitive, visual, or hearing impairments. This highlights the need for an inclusive design of involvement that accommodates diverse functional capacities. It requires tailored approaches by offering adapted materials, flexible pacing, and additional support where needed. In this light, discussions with patient representatives should determine whether details like symptom severity at the time of involvement should be reported, as occasionally addressed in other studies[24–26].

A challenge is ensuring diversity within collaborations, preventing the dominance of perspectives of well-educated, white, middle-class individuals from high-income countries. Marginalized groups are less likely to engage in healthcare research and often have limited trust in the healthcare system [27]. To foster their meaningful involvement, proactive efforts are needed. This includes identifying networks where these communities are represented, actively seeking their advice, and addressing practical barriers such as language support and funding constraints to ensure broader inclusion into healthcare research. It is of great value that EU-CAYAS-NET actively promotes equality, diversity and inclusion, involving individuals with a wide range of lived experiences.

Our participants were clear on how patient representatives should be recruited, emphasizing that the process should be organized through patient organizations. Recruiting through patient organizations has the advantage of reaching individuals who are already informed, engaged, and familiar with advocacy work, although it may risk underrepresenting survivors not connected to such groups. We believe alternative strategies through healthcare providers could be considered to broaden reach. Options include, but are not limited to, newsletters, website posts, emails or letters, clinic notes, telephone contact, and in-person outreach [14]. These considerations also relate to the broader challenge of ensuring diversity in patient involvement, as discussed previously, highlighting that recruitment strategies must be adapted to reach individuals beyond the groups that typically engage in research.

Evidence on how to involve patient representatives in guideline development is limited. We propose integrating them as full working group members, actively contributing from their lived experiences, identifying gaps, and providing feedback. They should be valued equally as content experts, healthcare providers, and methodologists. More importantly, they need a role tailored to the goal of each project and guideline topic through consultation between the guideline developers and the patients. As role clarity is crucial for successful patient involvement, tools like the Involvement Matrix and the NIHR role description template have been developed [28, 29]. Such tools were not discussed by our participants but may be helpful.

Even though preferences may vary from case to case when patient representatives wish to be involved, our results indicate that patient representatives in principal wish to be involved at all stages of guideline development. This would ensure their perspectives are integrated throughout the entire process. In the scoping review by Bryant et al. on PPI in the development of clinical practice guidelines, no evidence was found of CPG being developed with PPI members involved in every single stage [14]. The implementation of these recommendations in the transition guideline developed within the EU-CAYAS-NET project has demonstrated that they can be effectively applied and that the involved patient representatives were satisfied with the process [16]. Further validation is needed to assess the generalizability and practical feasibility of the recommendations across different contexts. Our methodology will be applied and tested in future survivorship guideline development within the Youth Cancer Network (YARN) [30]. offering valuable opportunities to evaluate how well the recommendations function in real-world settings and whether they require refinement.

This study has several strengths. Its participatory nature ensured active involvement of participants when generating knowledge, enhancing the relevance and usability of the findings. We responded to the need for greater involvement of patient representatives in research processes to enhance the impact of health services research and facilitate the translation of findings into meaningful clinical changes [31]. The use of both visual and verbal data, through mind maps and transcripts, provided a rich, multidimensional data set. Additionally, the systematic application of content analysis allowed for structural and thematic insights into perspectives on patient involvement.

A potential limitation is that the study was conducted within the unique European context, where collaboration between cancer organizations, patient communities, and researchers is relatively well established. As a result, some recommendations may be less easily transferable to settings or regions with different health system structures or advocacy landscapes. This is further reinforced by the fact that diversity was not explicitly discussed in the workshop, highlighting the need to examine how these recommendations apply across more diverse patient groups in future research.

In line with principles of transparency, we acknowledge that interpretating mind maps inevitably involves a degree of subjective judgment, as inherent to qualitative research. All individuals involved in the analysis and interpretation may have brought a certain pre-existing perspective to the topic. However, they made a conscious effort to approach the data as openly and reflexively as possible. The research team consisted of individuals with experience in qualitative methods, patient involvement, and clinical guideline development, which informed their interpretations but also sensitized them to potential biases. In drafting the recommendations, they aimed to stay as close as possible to the original input provided by the participants, ensuring that their voices remained central throughout the process.

Conclusion

To conclude, up until now there is only limited global guidance on patient involvement in CPG development for CAYA cancer survivorship care. We propose an approach to patient involvement in developing CAYA cancer survivorship guidelines based on patient representatives’ insights. This approach should be tested further for patient satisfaction, and a dedicated chapter on patient involvement in CPG handbooks could help establish it as standard practice. Ultimately, embracing this proposed approach to patient involvement in CPG development for CAYA cancer survivorship care ensures that evidence-based practice translates into real-world impact, fostering truly patient-centered care.
