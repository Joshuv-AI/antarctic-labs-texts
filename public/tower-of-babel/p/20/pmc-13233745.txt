An experimental study on the efficacy of Shatapushpa Churna in managing PCOS symptoms in a rat model: An Ayurvedic approach
Authors: Rutuja S. Gaikwad, Amruta V. Gadade, Manoj V. Gaikwad, Vikas D. Dighe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most prevalent metabolic and reproductive disorders affecting women of reproductive age. It is characterized primarily by menstrual dysfunction and androgen excess, which impact quality of life. PCOS is also associated with various comorbidities, including metabolic syndrome, reproductive abnormalities, psychological stress, and an increased risk of chronic illnesses such as type II diabetes and cardiovascular disease.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of Shatapushpa Churna in managing PCOS-like symptoms using a rat PCOS model. The primary focus was on assessing its effects on estrous cyclicity, glucose metabolism, body weight, hormonal profile, and ovarian histomorphology.MethodsA PCOS-like condition was induced in adult female Wistar rats by administering letrozole (2 mg/kg body weight, orally) once daily for 21 days. Model validation was based on irregular estrous cycles, impaired glucose tolerance, increased body weight, and altered hormonal profiles. PCOS-positive rats were then treated for 45 days with Shatapushpa Churna at three graded doses: low (0.62 g/kg b.w.), mid (1.24 g/kg b.w.), and high (1.85 g/kg b.w.). A standard reference group received metformin (300 mg/kg b.w.). Therapeutic outcomes were assessed through estrous cycle monitoring, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), serum hormonal assays, and ovarian histopathological examination.ResultsRats treated with Shatapushpa Churna showed significant improvements in reproductive hormone levels and regularity of the estrous cycle. OGTT results indicated improved glycemic control in mid- and high-dose groups, with glucose levels at 30, 60, and 120 min comparable to those of normal controls. Histological analysis of the ovaries revealed a reduction in the number of atretic follicles and a restoration of near-normal ovarian morphology in treated groups compared to the positive control.ConclusionShatapushpa Churna demonstrated notable efficacy in managing PCOS-like symptoms in letrozole-induced rat models. Its positive impact on hormonal balance, glucose metabolism, and ovarian histomorphology supports its potential as a therapeutic agent for PCOS management.

Introductions

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), recently also referred to as Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) is a heterogeneous endocrine-metabolic disorder and among the most prevalent conditions affecting women of reproductive age. Earlier epidemiological studies reported a global prevalence ranging from 4 to 12%, while studies in India indicated a broader prevalence range of 3.7–22.5% among reproductive-aged women. [1]. Recent meta-analyses provide more refined estimates, suggesting a global pooled prevalence of approximately 9.2%, with considerable variation depending on diagnostic criteria: National Institutes of Health (NIH) criteria – 5.5%, Rotterdam criteria – 11.5%, and Androgen Excess Society (AES) criteria – 7.1% [2]. Obesity, hormonal imbalance, insulin resistance, inflammation, and oxidative stress are typical causes of PCOS [3]. Menstrual irregularities, weight gain, and problems with reproduction are the main symptoms of PCOS [4]. Obesity is a significant contributor to the pathophysiology of PCOS and is recognized in modern clinical guidelines as a key modifiable risk factor. Hence, weight management via lifestyle modification is recommended as first-line therapy in PCOS regardless of BMI status [5,6]. In addition, PCOS is associated with an elevated risk of metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, cardiovascular conditions, endometrial cancer, and mental health disorders like anxiety and depression [3,4] (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Microscopic image of Shatapushpa seeds: actual part used in churna preparation.Fig. 1

Earlier, PCOS was diagnosed using the Rotterdam criteria, requiring any two of oligo-/anovulation, hyperandrogenism, or polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound [7]. Now, the updated 2023 international guidelines retain this two-out-of-three framework, allow AMH as an alternative to ultrasound, and set stricter rules for adolescents, requiring both hyperandrogenism and persistent menstrual irregularities for diagnosis [2].

In polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), hyperandrogenism and elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) levels disrupt normal ovarian function. Increased gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulse frequency favours LH synthesis over follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). The increased LH concentration induces theca cells to create androgens; however, a relative FSH shortage reduces granulosa cells’ ability to convert androgen into estrogen, affecting follicle maturation and ovulation, resulting in many cysts in the ovary [8].

Clomiphene citrate and metformin have long been standard pharmacological treatments for PCOS, primarily targeting ovulatory dysfunction and insulin resistance. However, both drugs can cause side effects such as nausea, diarrhoea, irregular bleeding, abdominal pain, hot flashes, breast tenderness, psychological disturbances, renal or hepatic impairment, and cardiovascular risks [7]. Current management strategies have expanded to include letrozole as a first-line ovulation inducer, combined oral contraceptives for menstrual regulation and hyperandrogenism, and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) or SGLT2 inhibitors in women with obesity or metabolic dysfunction. These newer options, alongside lifestyle interventions like diet, exercise, and weight management, aim to improve efficacy and tolerability while minimising adverse effects. Consequently, there is growing interest in natural, safe, and well-tolerated alternatives for PCOS therapy.

Nature has gifted us with a wealth of Ayurvedic herbs. Among them, Shatapushpa (Anethum Sowa or Peucedanum graveolens), commonly known as Indian Dill, stands out for its remarkable benefits in women's reproductive health. It helps regulate menstrual cycles, relieve painful periods (dysmenorrhea), and enhance ovulation and fertility by supporting follicular growth. Additionally, it alleviates menopausal symptoms like vaginal dryness, hot flashes, and mood swings, while promoting uterine health in conditions such as fibroids and infertility [9,10].

Shatapushpa Churna, an Ayurvedic powder prepared from the seeds of Anethum graveolens (dill), is traditionally used for menstrual health and hormonal balance. Reviews report its anti-spasmolytic, anti-inflammatory, and phytoestrogenic effects, making it an effective and affordable option for managing menstrual disorders [11].

In addition to regulating menstrual periods and having no adverse effects, it is always safe to use ayurvedic remedies to treat conditions like these. Shatapushpa contains antihyperlipidemic and anti-hypercholesterolemic properties. It also has anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties that help treat genital ulcers [12]. Considering all these characteristics, the approach will be used to treat PCOS patients, with its effectiveness demonstrated through an in vitro study.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This study was conducted at the ICMR-National Institute for Research in Reproductive and Child Health (NIRRCH) in Parel, Mumbai, at the department in the National Centre for Preclinical Reproductive and Genetic Toxicology (NCPRGT). The authentication part was completed at Alarsia Pharmaceuticals, Andheri, Mumbai.

Animals

The National Centre for Preclinical Reproductive and Genetic Toxicology, ICMR-National Institute for Research in Reproductive and Child Health (NIRRCH), Parel, Mumbai 400 012, India, provided the threemonth-old female wistar rats for the experiment, weighing 300 ± 10 g. The animals were housed in a chamber with controlled lighting and temperature, and they had free access to food and water.

Materials

Letrozole was obtained from Sigma (USA). Shatapushpa Churna and cow ghee were supplied by the Department of Prasutitantra Evum Streeroga, R.A. Podar (Ayu) Hospital, Worli, Mumbai.

Authentication of raw materials

Shatapushpa Churna (Anethum sowa Roxb., seeds) was authenticated based on macroscopic and microscopic characteristics, along with analytical parameters as per the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API, Part I, Vol. V, p. 153). The seed powder was light brown, with a characteristic odour and slightly bitter taste. Microscopically, diagnostic features such as oil ducts and vittae were observed. Physicochemical analysis confirmed compliance with pharmacopoeial standards, including moisture content (0.28%), ash values, alcohol-soluble extractive (AIE), water-soluble extractive (WSE), and volatile oil content.

Goghrita (clarified cow's butter) was authenticated as per Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API, Part I, Vol. VI, p. 204). The sample appeared semisolid, white in colour, with a pleasant, characteristic odour and taste. Analytical values such as specific gravity (1.0199 at 25 °C), Reichert Meissl value (26), saponification value (222), iodine value (32), moisture (0.28%), and unsaponifiable matter (1.3%) all complied with standards.

Thus, both raw materials were confirmed to be authentic and of standard quality as per the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India.

Methods

Preparation of test material

The test formulation was prepared by converting the human equivalent dose (HED) of Shatapushpa Churna (10 g/day) into the corresponding animal equivalent dose (AED) for wistar rats using body surface area normalisation [13]. Based on this calculation, three graded doses were selected: low dose (0.62 g/kg b.w.), mid dose (1.24 g/kg b.w.), and high dose (1.85 g/kg b.w.).

For administration, the required daily quantity of Shatapushpa Churna was mixed with 300 μL of cow ghee per animal per day. Cow ghee was chosen both as the Ayurvedic anupana (vehicle) to enhance the absorption of lipophilic phytoconstituents (API, 2010) and because 300 μL represents the minimum amount necessary to create a uniform and stable suspension of the Churna for accurate oral dosing.

Low dose (0.62 g/kg b.w.): 0.1457 g Shatapushpa Churna +300 μL cow ghee.

Mid dose (1.24 g/kg b.w.): 0.2914 g Shatapushpa Churna +300 μL cow ghee.

High dose (1.85 g/kg b.w.): 0.434 g Shatapushpa Churna +300 μL cow ghee.

For the standard reference group, metformin was prepared by dissolving 108 mg of metformin (equivalent to 300 mg/kg b.w.) in 300 μL of distilled water per animal per day.

Test method: authentication and animal husbandry

Before the study was started, the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC)/NIRRH/05/18) of the National Institute for Research in Reproductive and Child Health (NIRRCH) reviewed and approved the study protocol involving animals. The experiments were carried out in accordance with the rules set forth by the Committee for the Control and Supervision of Experimental Animals, India. For the study, three-month-old wistar rats in good health were selected. Polypropylene cages were utilised to house the animals. The animals were kept in a 14-h light/10-h dark cycle with a regulated temperature of 23 ± 1 °C and humidity of 55 ± 5%. Autoclaved corncob was used as bedding in the cages, and it was changed twice a week. Throughout the investigation, the animals were fed rat pellets made in-house that were devoid of soy and contained crude protein, fibre, and nitrogen-free extract, made in the research facility throughout the investigation, and ad libitum filtered drinking water (purified by reverse osmosis and ultraviolet light).

Study design

Two steps were taken in the study's execution. The first involved creating a PCOS model in female wistar rats, and the second involved examining how well Shatapushpa Churna works on the PCOS-developed wistar rats by conducting an its efficacy study.

PCOS animal model development

A total 64 three-month-old wistar rats were split into two groups: sixteen normal control rats and forty-eight PCOS model rats (Table 1). For 21 days, the animals in the PCOS model group were given 2 mg/kg bw of letrozole orally [14], whereas the normal control group rats were given 0.002% gum acacia as a vehicle. All animals received food and water on a free-for-all basis. The daily estrus cycle was monitored while giving letrozole. After 21 days, the animal's oral glucose tolerance was assessed before sacrifice, and eight animals from the letrozole-treated group and eight animals from the control group were sacrificed. Following a 45-day treatment period, the animals were killed via asphyxiation with CO2. The ovary was taken out for histopathological examination. A retro-orbital puncture was used to get blood for the examination of hormones, biochemistry, and haematology.

Treatment and duration

Shatapushpa Churna, the test formulation, was administered orally for 45 consecutive days following the confirmation of PCOS. The treatment duration was selected to allow sufficient time for evaluating improvements in estrous cyclicity, ovarian morphology, hormonal balance, and metabolic parameters, as previous studies indicate that 30–60 days are typically required to observe meaningful therapeutic effects in Letrozole-induced PCOS models [14].

The control group received only the vehicle (cow ghee), while the test groups received the designated doses of Shatapushpa Churna suspended in cow ghee. The dose–volume ratio was adjusted according to each animal's body weight, maintaining a constant factor of 1 mL/kg to ensure uniform administration. Animals in the control group were handled identically to the test groups, except they did not receive the active test formulation.

Clinical signs, mortality, and weekly body weight

Throughout the dosage period, body weight was recorded weekly, and the animals were observed daily for behaviour, pyloric erection, salivary, ocular, and other mucous secretions, as well as any clinical symptoms of illness or mortality.

Estimation of sex hormones

Using an ELISA kit that was commercially available (DBC, Ontario, Canada), the serum concentration of testosterone was determined by following the immuno-enzymatic approach as specified in the kit's protocol. Using ELISA Kits, serum levels of progesterone and estradiol were evaluated once for each group following a 45-day treatment period.

Measurement of complete blood count and serum biochemistry

For biochemistry and haematology, blood samples were drawn from the intra-orbital flexus and placed in non-heparinised and heparinised tubes, respectively.

Estrus cyclicity

Daily estrous cyclicity was monitored via vaginal smear analysis. Vaginal secretions were collected every morning (8:00–9:00 a.m.) using a plastic pipette containing 10 μL of 0.9% normal saline, which was gently inserted into the vagina. From each rat, one drop was collected with a clean tip and put on a glass slide. For every animal, a separate glass slide was utilised. Under a light microscope, unstained material was examined using 10X and 40X objective lenses.

The rat estrous cycle spans 4–5 days and includes four stages: proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus. A large number of tiny, round, nucleated epithelial cells with nucleated and cornified epithelium are present during the proestrus period (less than 24 h). A large number of well-developed nucleated epithelial cells (with cornified epithelium) and a large number of cornified epithelial cells without nuclei characterize the estrous phase (12–48 h). A few neutrophils and mostly cornified epithelium, both with and without nuclei, are characteristics of the metestrus (18–24 h). Neutrophils predominate during the 48–72-h diestrus phase, but cornified epithelial cells are less common. Letrozole administration disrupts the estrous cycle and is marked by an extended diestrus phase [[14], [15], [16], [17]].

Biochemical parameters

Blood samples were taken by retro-orbital puncture into various microcentrifuge tubes prior to animal sacrifice. After 15 min of centrifuging the blood samples at 3000 rpm, the serum was separated for biochemical analysis. The serum was stored at −20 °C in a freezer. The separate serum samples were well mixed before analysis. Low-density lipoproteins (LDL), total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol (HDL-c), triglycerides, and cholesterol were measured using an automated biochemical analyser (EM 200, Erba Mannheim) in accordance with normal methods outlined in the product kit (Erba Mannheim, Germany).

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

All experimental animals were fasted for 12 h before OGTT [18]. All of the experimental animals were given glucose (2 g/kg body weight) orally, and blood samples were taken every 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. Through retro-orbital penetration into vials coated with fluoride to prevent coagulation, blood samples were obtained. The plasma was isolated from the blood after it was spun at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Using kits based on GOD-POD, glucose was calculated. Twice, following 45 days of treatment with test drugs (Shatapushapa Churna and metformin) and after 21 days of letrozole administration to establish PCOS induction, the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test was carried out.

Histology of ovary

The animals were sacrificed by asphyxiation with CO2, and each one underwent necropsy. For enzymatic experiments, one ovary and uterus were excised and stored at −80 °C. An additional ovary was prepared for histopathological analysis. Bouin's fixative was used to repair one ovary. All groups ovaries had their histological exams performed using standardized histology techniques. Cut in a paraffin-embedded block, sections with a thickness of 5 μm were stained with hematoxylin-eosin.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To do statistical comparisons across various groups, one-way ANOVA and Tukey were utilised, and p < 0.05 was always considered significant. GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.0) (GraphPad Software, California, USA) was used to design the graphics of the manuscript, following the guidelines of Talukder et al., 2023 [19].

Results

Body weight

A per cent body weight gain among the experimental groups demonstrated a differential response to Shatapushpa Churna treatment in the letrozole-induced PCOS model. The weight of rats in the group receiving letrozole was significantly higher than in the control group (5.79%), indicating body weight associated with PCOS induction.

Among the treated groups, the high-dose Shatapushpa Churna (SP-HD) group did not show any change in body weight (5.63%) as compared to the control group. The mid-dose (SP-MD) and low-dose (SP-LD) groups exhibited moderate weight gain (2.99% and 3.89%, respectively), comparable to the vehicle control (VC) group (3.21%). Then the Metformin-treated (MET) group showed a body weight gain of 3.11%, aligning with the well-established role of metformin in mitigating metabolic alterations associated with PCOS.

Estrus cyclicity

The estrous cycle in female Wistar rats consists of four phases: proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus. Following letrozole administration, most rats exhibited a prolonged diestrus phase beginning on day 7, indicative of PCOS induction. Over the 45-day study period, the number of days spent in proestrus, estrus, and diestrus was recorded (see Fig. 2).

Rats in the PCOS positive (PC) group spent significantly more days in the diestrus phase compared to the control group (p < 0.001), confirming estrous cycle disruption. Additionally, an increase in estrus days was observed in the PC group. Treatment with Shatapushpa Churna at low and mid doses (SP-LD and SP-MD) effectively reduced the number of diestrus and estrus days, restoring the estrous cycle toward normal patterns. Oral glucose tolerance test

The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) results show that Shatapushpa Churna treatment significantly improves glucose regulation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4). Among the tested groups, the high dose of Shatapushpa Churna (SP-HD) exhibited glucose-lowering effects comparable to the standard antidiabetic drug metformin (MET) and the positive control (PC). Notably, the Shatapushpa Churna -high dose (SP-HD) group showed a marked reduction in blood glucose levels at all measured time points, especially at 120 min, indicating enhanced glucose clearance and potential antidiabetic efficacy. Discussion

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), recently also referred to as Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) due to its multisystem endocrine and metabolic involvement, is a complex metabolic and endocrine disorder characterized by irregular ovulation, abdominal obesity, and hyperandrogenism [1,[2], [7], [20], [21]]. Alterations in circulating sex hormones, including elevated androgens and disrupted gonadotropin secretion, significantly impact ovarian function, impairing follicular maturation and contributing to hormonal imbalances [19,22,23].

In Ayurveda, PCOS may be interpreted as analogous to Artava Kshaya, Nashtartava, and certain Yonivyapad, involving Kapha–Vata dushti, Agnimandya, and Srotorodha, leading to menstrual irregularity and subfertility. Restoration of Artava pravritti and metabolic balance forms the therapeutic focus [24]. The letrozole-induced PCOS model offers a translational framework to examine these concepts. The hyperandrogenic anovulatory state may correlate with Artava Kshaya and dysfunction of Artava-vaha srotas, while obesity and insulin resistance reflect Kapha prakopa and Meda vriddhi. Prolonged diestrus may be viewed as Vata avarana [25]. Thus, the model enables evaluation of classical principles such as Kapha–Vata shamana and Artava-janana in a controlled setting.

In this study, we employed a letrozole-induced PCOS model in female wistar rats to evaluate the efficacy of Shatapushpa Churna. Letrozole, an aromatase inhibitor, reduces estrogen synthesis, leading to elevated testosterone levels and impaired glucose tolerance, thereby inducing a hyperandrogenic and insulin-resistant state that closely mimics human PCOS [14,17,26]. PCOS induction was confirmed through vaginal smear examinations [12,16], serum hormone profiling, and oral glucose tolerance testing [18].

Treatment with letrozole over 21 days significantly disrupted the estrous cycle, prolonging the diestrus phase relative to control [17]. Administration of Shatapushpa Churna progressively restored estrous cyclicity toward normal, likely through modulation of circulating sex hormones and gonadotropins, which are critical for ovarian function and endocrine homeostasis [3,4,8,11]. These results align with previous studies showing that herbal interventions, including polyherbal formulations, Marjoram, Teucrium polium, and Fagonia indica, can normalize estrous cycles by reducing androgen excess and improving ovarian responsiveness [20,27,28].

A hallmark of PCOS in this model was increased body weight, reflecting the metabolic burden of hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance [1,3,5]. The positive control group showed the highest weight gain (5.79%). Shatapushpa Churna attenuated weight gain in a dose-dependent manner: high-dose (SP-HD) reduced weight gain slightly (5.63%), while mid- and low-dose groups (SP-MD and SP-LD) exhibited gains of 2.99% and 3.89%, comparable to vehicle control (3.21%) and metformin-treated rats (3.11%) [3,6,7]. These findings suggest that Shatapushpa Churna may improve metabolic regulation in PCOS through androgen modulation and enhanced insulin sensitivity [[27], [28], [29]], consistent with effects reported for other herbal interventions [27,28].

Letrozole-treated rats exhibited significantly elevated serum testosterone levels, correlating with prolonged diestrus and increased body weight [19,26,30]. Shatapushpa Churna treatment significantly reduced testosterone levels and restored progesterone and estradiol toward normal ranges, with effects comparable to metformin [3,8,11]. This indicates normalisation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. Similar hormonal improvements have been observed with Marjoram, Fagonia indica, and polyherbal formulations in PCOS models [20,27].

Shatapushpa Churna also improved lipid profiles in treated rats, suggesting benefits in managing PCOS-associated dyslipidemia [5,11,27]. This effect aligns with its known hypolipidemic and cholesterol-lowering properties and mirrors findings from other herbal interventions [20,27].

Overall, these findings support the potential of Shatapushpa Churna as an adjunct therapy for PCOS, addressing both reproductive and metabolic dysfunctions. Limitations of this study include the short treatment duration and the use of a single-species model [13,31]. Future research should investigate long-term efficacy, molecular mechanisms, and clinical translation to human populations [6,14].

In conclusion, Shatapushpa Churna demonstrated beneficial effects similar to metformin in the management of PCOS, including restoration of oestrous cyclicity, normalisation of hormone levels, and improvement in ovarian morphology. Its broad spectrum of biological activities positions it as a promising candidate for addressing both clinical and pathological abnormalities associated with PCOS, warranting further exploration as a therapeutic option.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several strengths. It employed a well-established letrozole-induced PCOS model in Wistar rats, which closely mimics the clinical and pathological features of human PCOS. A comprehensive evaluation was conducted, including assessments of estrous cyclicity, hormone levels, body weight, glucose tolerance, lipid profile, and ovarian histology. The inclusion of metformin as a standard reference drug provided a comparative framework to assess the therapeutic efficacy of Shatapushpa Churna. Furthermore, the dose-dependent dose regime allowed for the identification of potential therapeutic ranges and a clear understanding of Shatapushpa Churna potential for PCOS. Importantly, the study also contributes novel insights into the use of an Ayurvedic formulation for PCOS management.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. In this study we did not explore molecular mechanisms, such as gene expression or signalling pathways, that could explain the observed effects. Our study treatment duration was relatively short, and long-term efficacy, safety, and reproductive outcomes were not assessed. Overall, while the results are promising, further in-depth studies are needed to establish the translational potential of Shatapushpa Churna in PCOS management.

Implications

The findings of this study have important implications for the development of alternative and complementary therapies for Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS). The demonstrated efficacy of Shatapushpa Churna in restoring hormonal balance, improving estrous cyclicity, reducing insulin resistance, and normalizing lipid profiles suggests its potential as a multi-targeted therapeutic agent. We state that the given the limitations and side effects associated with conventional treatments like metformin and hormonal therapy, the use of Ayurvedic formulations such as Shatapushpa Churna offers a promising natural alternative that may be better tolerated over long-term use.

Additionally, the dose-dependent responses observed in this study underscore the importance of optimising dosage to achieve therapeutic benefits while minimising adverse effects. The results also contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the integration of traditional herbal medicine in the management of complex endocrine disorders like PCOS. With further validation through molecular studies and clinical trials, Shatapushpa Churna could be developed into a standardized herbal formulation for use in both preventive and therapeutic settings.

Moreover, this study sets the foundation for future research exploring the mechanisms of action, bioactive compounds, and potential synergistic effects when combined with conventional treatments. The implications extend beyond PCOS, potentially contributing to broader metabolic and reproductive health interventions within integrative medicine frameworks.

Conclusion

Shatapushpa Churna demonstrated promising therapeutic potential in managing letrozole-induced PCOS in Wistar rats. It effectively restored estrous cyclicity, balanced hormone levels, improved glucose tolerance, and normalized lipid profiles, with outcomes likely comparable to metformin. These findings highlight its potential as a natural, multi-targeted approach for PCOS management. However, further molecular and clinical studies are warranted to validate its safety, efficacy, and mechanism of action for translational use in human populations.
