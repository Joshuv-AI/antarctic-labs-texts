Solitary Fibrous Tumor of the Pleura Presenting as a Giant Thoracic Mass: A Case Report
Authors: Zahra Sadin, Manouchehr Aghajanzadeh, Mohammadreza Sadin, Arsalan Dadashi, Omid Mosaffaee Rad, Seyed Ali Fakhr Mosavi, Ali Alavi Foumani, Azita Tangestani Nejad, Mohaya Farzin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSolitary fibrous tumors of the pleura are rare and may present as large thoracic masses. Timely imaging and histopathological confirmation are essential for accurate diagnosis and effective surgical management.

Introduction

Solitary fibrous tumors of the pleura (SFTP) are uncommon mesenchymal neoplasms. Their incidence rate is less than 5% of all pleural tumors [1]. Approximately 2000 instances have been reported across published studies [2]. During the past two decades, immunohistochemical investigations have strongly proved that they have mesenchymal origin [3]. They can occur in males and females equally, usually in the sixth to seventh decades of life. They are often benign, but a minority can show aggressive presentation, especially in elderlies [4]. The pleura and mediastinum are the most common locations. Although there have been reported very rare cases of intrapulmonary, peritoneal and less commonly, nasal, upper respiratory tract, or the tunica vaginalis of the testis too. Most thoracic cases are identified as incidental findings on chest imaging, but patients may occasionally present with dyspnea, chest discomfort, or hypoglycemia [5].

We present a case of a giant solitary fibrous tumor of the pleura to emphasize the critical importance of evaluating any unexplained thoracic mass.

Case Presentation

We had a 64‐year‐old woman with exertional dyspnea. She had a history of type 2 diabetes but had never smoked or been exposed to asbestos. During her physical examination, we noticed reduced breath sounds on her right side. The remaining examination was normal. Routine laboratory investigations were normal. CT‐guided core‐needle biopsy results showed a solitary fibrous tumor of the pleura. After consultations with a cardiologist, pulmonologist, and oncologist, a complete resection was chosen. We performed the right posterolateral thoracotomy at the seventh intercostal space, under general anesthesia with single‐lung ventilation while she was positioned in the right lateral decubitus. During the procedure, we discovered a well‐defined mass with strong vascular connections to her lung, diaphragm, and parietal pleura. It originates from the right lower lobe's visceral pleura, so it caused significant bleeding during dissection. The mass was effectively resected from the lung. Due to its firm attachment to the diaphragm, a portion of the diaphragm was removed with the tumor. We repaired the resulting diaphragmatic defect with nylon sutures (Figure 3A–D). Figure 3A shows the intraoperative exposure of the giant mass, which has a pedunculated base and vascularity. Figure 3B illustrates the dissection from adhesions. Figure 3C shows the diaphragmatic defect after resection, and Figure 3D confirms complete resection with clear margins. Her recovery went smoothly, and the patient was discharged in stable condition on the postoperative sixth day. Final histopathological results confirmed a benign solitary fibrous tumor of the pleura (SFTP). Immunohistochemical staining showed CD34, vimentin, and Bcl2 positive. But pan‐cytokeratin (PCK) and smooth muscle actin (SMA) were negative (Figures 4A,B and 5A,B). Figure 4A (H&E stain) reveals low cellularity and lack of mitosis, which confirms a benign mass. Figure 4B (CD34 IHC) demonstrates strong vascular endothelial staining. Figure 5A (vimentin IHC) confirms mesenchymal origin, and Figure 5B (negative PCK/SMA IHC) differentiates it from similar tumors. The right lung re‐expanded fully, and the patient's pulmonary function returned to normal levels following the tumor's removal. In a 12‐month follow‐up interval, no complications or evidence of recurrence were observed.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

When we first saw this patient with a large chest mass, we considered several possibilities. Malignant mesothelioma was a top concern due to its pleural origin, but the lack of asbestos exposure and diffuse spread made it unlikely.

Metastatic cancer from lung or breast was another thought. But because it was a single mass and had no other signs, we ruled it out.

Infections like empyema or old hematomas were dismissed due to the lack of infection or injury history.

A chest X‐ray showed a big shadow in the right lower lung (Figure 1A,B), and a CT scan revealed a 22 cm × 15 cm × 11 cm mass stuck to the pleura, pericardium, and diaphragm (Figure 2A,B). The biopsy showed a solitary fibrous tumor but wasn't definitive. After consulting with specialists, we decided on surgery. We performed a right thoracotomy and found a well‐encapsulated mass from the right lower lobe, which had tightly adhered with vascular connections. It caused some bleeding. We carefully removed it, took part of the diaphragm due to its attachment, and eventually stitched the gap with nylon (Figure 3A–D). Post‐op went smoothly, and she went home on day six.

Tests confirmed a benign solitary fibrous tumor with positive CD34, vimentin, and Bcl‐2 markers, and negative for others (Figures 4A,B and 5A,B). Her lung function was normal, and a 12‐month follow‐up showed no recurrence.

Conclusions

SFTPs are rare and often benign pleural tumors but can grow massively and cause compressive symptoms like chest pain, cough, hemoptysis, and dyspnea. In some cases, large tumors can result in life‐threatening complications due to mediastinal shift. Radiological evaluation is crucial, but preoperative fine needle biopsy usually cannot help to diagnose, and definitive diagnosis is obtained in most cases after the surgical resection of the tumor.

Complete surgical excision is the preferred approach for managing benign SFTPs. If a complete resection of malignant SFTPs was not feasible, we could consider an adjuvant therapy. In some cases, we can do a less invasive procedure like video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). There is always a risk for recurrence, so long‐term follow‐up is crucial.

Discussion

Solitary fibrous tumor of the pleura (SFTP) is a rare tumor with origination from submesothelial cells. It has a benign manifestation in over 80% of cases but can become malignant in 12% [1, 6]. Cases are most frequently diagnosed in the sixth to seventh decades, with no significant difference between males and females [7]. As observed in our patient, over 50% present initially asymptomatic; however, progressive enlargement may result in dyspnea, cough, or compression‐related pain [3, 8]. Paraneoplastic syndromes such as hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy (seen in up to 20%) or hypoglycemia (in 5%) may occur, although they were not present in this patient [9]. Radiographic evaluation often shows solitary fibrous tumors of the pleura as discrete and well‐defined lesions, while pedunculated types may shift position due to their stalk‐like attachment [10]. MRI is useful for assessing local invasion, whereas PET‐CT assists in evaluating malignant potential or detecting metastatic spread [11]. Histologically, SFTPs consist of spindle cells with variable collagen. It can be distinguished by CD34 positivity and negativity for cytokeratin, as seen in our case [12]. Features suggestive of malignancy include increased mitotic activity, cellular pleomorphism, and areas of necrosis [13]. Staging of SFTPs ranges from 0 to IV, based on pedunculation status and histologic characteristics (Table 1). It provides direction for clinical management [3]. However, our case was stage 0 and benign because it was pedunculated. The differential diagnosis included malignant mesothelioma (which can be excluded by the lack of asbestos history and diffuse growth pattern), pleural metastases (which are unlikely in the absence of a known primary cancer), pulmonary sarcoma (which is not supported by imaging findings of invasion), and empyema or hematoma (which can be excluded due to no evidence of infection or trauma) [14]. Intrapulmonary SFTs demonstrate similar clinicopathologic features, although they are very rare and often need surgical excision [15]. Radical and complete resection with free margins is the treatment of choice in all patients. It may necessitate removal of adjacent structures such as the diaphragm, pericardium, or chest wall [16].

In our case, we resected a portion of the diaphragm, and primary repair was performed. Vascular tumor adhesions to visceral or parietal pleurae may cause major bleeding, but it is manageable [15]. Recent studies suggest that targeted therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, can be used for malignant or inoperable cases [17]. Prognosis after resection is excellent, but there is always a recurrence risk. So careful follow‐up is necessary [18].
