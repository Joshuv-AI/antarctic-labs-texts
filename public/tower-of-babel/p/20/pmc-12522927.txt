Population stratified differences between ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism with Crohn's disease risk: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yiyuan Gao, Yuan Zhang, Peidu Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundATG16L1 rs2241880 (T300A), a single-nucleotide variant, plays a controversial role in the development of Crohn’s disease (CD).AimThis meta-analysis aimed to explore the association between rs2241880 and CD among different subgroups by collecting the largest sample size published so far.MethodsWe retrieved articles from China National Knowledge Infrastructure, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases, adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The correlation between the rs2241880 variant and CD was assessed with a combined effect size of the calculated 95% confidence interval (CI) and odds ratio (OR). Heterogeneity was assessed by the I2 value among genetic models. Publication bias was tested using the Egger test and funnel plot.ResultsThe rs2241880 G allele is positively correlated with the risk of CD worldwide, with an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.29–1.37) for the comparison of G vs. A. This variant is identified as a risk factor for CD progression across all racial subgroups, particularly highlighting an association between Mongolians and CD risk (G vs. A: OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.15–1.34). Furthermore, there are statistically significant differences observed across age subgroups, with adults showing an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.29–1.38) and children an OR of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.10–1.47). Gender-wise, the risk remains significant with a male vs. female comparison yielding an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.29–1.37). Additionally, when comparing the overall co-dominant models, GA carriers exhibited a lower risk compared to GG carriers. Specifically, GA vs. AA showed an OR of 1.30 (95% CI: 1.23–1.38), while GG vs. AA revealed a higher risk with an OR of 1.76 (95% CI: 1.65–1.88).ConclusionThe ATG16L1 rs2241880 G allele is associated with an increased risk of CD globally, acting as a risk factor across various demographics, including age (both adults and children), gender, and particularly within Mongolian populations. A gene dosage-dependent effect may enhance the strength of this association, as GG carriers of the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism exhibit a higher risk for CD compared to GA carriers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04050-3.Keywords: ATG16L1, Rs2241880, Crohn’s disease, Gene polymorphism, Racial differences

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a significant chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and its etiology remains incompletely understood. Factors such as an imbalance in microbiota, compromised intestinal mucosal barrier, and genetic predisposition are commonly associated with exaggerated immune responses that may lead to CD development [1]. Autophagy-related 1 6-like 1 (ATG16L1) gene is vital for the completion of autophagosome. Autophagic dysfunction triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress responses, which disrupts normal immune responses, leads to the loss of antimicrobial peptide in Paneth cells, hinders the elimination of invasive pathogens, affect antigen presentation, and promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages, which contribute to inducing the development of CD [1, 2].

Early research indicated that the rs2241880 A > G SNP in the ATG16L1 genetic polymorphisms, which results in a threonine-to-alanine substitution at the C-terminal domain (Thr300 Ala/T300 A), represents a significant genetic variant associated with CD. Those carrying ATG16L1 rs2241880 variant, whether individuals or mice, are more likely to experience impairment in selective autophagy. This variant promotes the degradation of ATG16L1 by activating caspase-3-mediated cleavage, resulting in defects in intestinal cells in the process of cellular stress, apoptotic stimuli, and deficiencies in autophagy linked to CD [3]. In addition, the ATG16L1 rs2241880 variant alters the interactions between motif-containing molecules and the C-terminal WD40 repeat domain (WDD) of ATG16L1. This disruption leads to slowed intracellular trafficking mediated by transmembrane protein TMEM59 and reduces the engagement of ATG16L1 in response to bacterial infections [4, 5].The variant also triggers inflammasome-dependent cytokine production through the activation of the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway [6]. Additionally, it exacerbates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, modifies the mucosal bacterial composition in Paneth cells [7], and disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis [8–10].

Although the ATG16L1 rs2241880 variant is important for patients with CD, the risk of developing CD varies significantly among different populations. To date, numerous researchers have focused on clarifying the role of the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism in genetic susceptibility to CD across various racial subgroups. Zhang et al. [11] and Palomino-Morales et al. [12] demonstrated that the this polymorphism was associated with racial differences in CD prevalence, being more common in Caucasians than in Mongoloids, suggesting that racial factors may influence the development of CD. A meta-analysis conducted by Grigoras et al. [13] supported this notion and suggested that the lack of significant findings in some studies might be attributed to small sample sizes. Additionally, Na et al. [14] found no significant association between the polymorphism and CD in South Korean children, based on a study involving 65 cases and 72 controls (odds ratio [OR] = 1.37, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.69–2.71, p = 0.37). In contrast, Zhang et al. [15] reported the opposite results in South Koreans (OR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.19–1.43, P = 1.69 × 10–8) from a larger sample of 1,809 cases and 2,436 controls. Similarly, studies in China by Lv et al. [16], Pang et al. [17] and Chen et al. [18] found no clear association between the two in Guangdong, Jiangsu, and Guangxi Provinces. However, research by Zhang et al. [19] and Wei et al. [20] indicated that the incidence of the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism served as a risk factor for CD in Jiangsu and Taiwan. Despite these efforts to elucidate the relationship between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism and CD risk across the general population, debates persist, particularly regarding the limited sample sizes of Mongolian and pediatric cohorts [21]. These findings highlight the need for larger sample sizes to facilitate a more in-depth analysis.

Therefore, this meta-analysis sought to incorporate a larger number of patient samples to assess the relationship between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism and the risk of CD across various racial subgroups, age groups, and gender categories.

Methods

Ethics statement

To ensure the quality of our study, the Strengthening the Reporting of Genetic Association Studies and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines were used [22].

Search strategies

Relevant studies were retrieved through the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases, and the cutoff time for retrieval was March 26, 2023. The search terms used were (autophagy OR ATG16L1 OR rs2241880 OR T300 A) AND (Crohn’s disease OR inflammatory bowel disease) AND (polymorphism OR mutation OR variant OR genotype) in English or Chinese.

Data extraction and literature quality assessment

Two reviewers independently screened eligible studies and recorded the available information for each study: the first author, publication year, country, race, age subgroup, number of cases, genotyping methods, and genotype frequency in cases and controls. The chi-square test (p > 0.05) was used to evaluate whether the genotype distribution in controls of each study complied with the HWE law. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to evaluate the quality of the literature (NOS score ≥ 5). Disagreements on the aforementioned processes were discussed and agreed upon by two reviewers. Persistent disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

The association between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism and CD was analyzed using ORs and 95% CIs in four genetic models: allele (G versus [vs.] A), dominant (GG + GA vs. AA), recessive (GG vs. AA + GA), and co-dominant (GG vs. AA; GA vs. AA). The OR was regarded as the expected relative risk index. The HWE law was evaluated in the controls of each study using the Q test.

Based on the Q test, we degraded the heterogeneity of studies by the p-value (statistical significance: p < 0.05). The heterogeneity of the I2 value was classified as low (< 25%), moderate (≤ 50%), or high (≤ 75%) heterogeneity. If the p-value was ≥ 0.05, and the I2 value was ≤ 50%, the fixed effect model was used to assess the low heterogeneous results; otherwise, the random effect model was used to assess the significant heterogeneous results. Sources of significant heterogeneity were analyzed by subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, or meta-regression analysis. Descriptive statistics were used for insufficient samples. The funnel plot and Egger regression test were used to evaluate publication bias (statistically significant: p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 15.1; Stata Corp.).

Results

Basic characteristics of the retrieved results

Based on the aforementioned search strategies, we retrieved 2039 articles published up to March 26, 2023, from the databases and the references of the published articles. After excluding duplicates and irrelevant literature, 59 case–control articles were initially included (5 and 54 articles in Chinese and English, respectively) (Fig. 1). Due to 5 out of the 59 articles containing multiple independent studies, these additional 6 independent studies were included, including 2 studies by Büning et al. [23], 1 study by Van Limbergen et al. [24], 1 study by Fowler et al. [25], 1 study by Gazouli et al. [26], and 1 study by Latiano et al. [27]. Hence, a total of 65 case–control studies were finally included for quantitative synthesis.

Results of heterogeneity analysis

The forest plots of the overall meta-analysis in random effect model indicated large heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 50%, p < 0.1) (Fig. S1). Hence, meta-regression analysis was performed to identify the source of heterogeneity.

Results of meta-regression analysis indicated that none of the individual covariates, including publication year, country, races, NOS scores, age subgroup, genotyping method, and country’s level of development were possible sources of heterogeneity (p > 0.05) (Fig. S2a–g). However, for genders (male vs. female), Gutiérrez et al. [65] (p = 0.016) may be a possible source of heterogeneity (p < 0.05) (Fig. S2h).

To assess the stability of meta-analysis, the merged studies were sequentially excluded. Results of sensitivity analyses showed that the sequential exclusion of each study was not a possible source of heterogeneity (p > 0.05) (Fig. S3a).

To explore the source of heterogeneity, we selected an allelic model (G vs. A) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 > 50%, p < 0.1) for publication bias analysis. The quantitative results showed that the Egger test (p = 0.865) and Begg test (p = 0.666) indicated no publication bias (p > 0.05) (Fig. S4a). However, in the Begg funnel plot, 10 points (Latiano (1) et al. [27], Latiano (2) et al. [27], Wei et al. [20], Dusatkova et al. [50], Dema et al. [49], Wolfkamp et al. [56], Okazaki [44], Lacher et al. [51], Amre et al. [48], and Gutiérrez et al. [65]) were not distributed symmetrically. To investigate the heterogeneity between studies, we excluded these 10 studies and reperformed the forest plots, resulting in a significant decrease in heterogeneity (Fig. S5). Therefore, these multiple covariates of these studies may be the cause of high heterogeneity, and possible resources of the heterogeneity were required to be interpreted cautiously in subsequent analysis. Ultimately, 46 studies were included in the meta-analysis, with 15,489 patients and 20,724 controls.

Results of the overall meta-analysis

The final results indicated that the rs2241880 G allele was associated with an increased risk of CD in the overall population according to the allelic model (G vs. A, OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.29–1.37) (Fig. S5a). When comparing the risk among genotype carriers, those with the GG genotype exhibited a greater likelihood of developing CD relative to carriers of the GA genotype. This was supported by both the co-dominant models (GA vs. AA, OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.23–1.38) and co-dominant models (GG vs. AA, OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.65–1.88) (Fig. S5 d–e). Additionally, results from the heterogeneity tests for each genetic model showed that the removal of 10 heterogeneous studies significantly diminished overall heterogeneity without modifying the association between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A polymorphism and susceptibility to CD (Table 2).

Results of the racial subgroup association analysis

In the analysis of racial subgroups, we observed that the rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism exhibited significant differences (p < 0.001) across most genetic models in Mongoloid populations. Specifically, we found the following OR: G vs. A: OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.15–1.34; GG vs. AA + GA: OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.21–1.68; GG vs. AA: OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.34–1.90; GA vs. AA: OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.11–1.39. However, the dominant model (GG + GA vs. AA) showed low heterogeneity (I2 > 25%, p > 0.05), yielding an OR of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.69–1.05) (Table 3). Similar trends were observed in Caucasian populations, with significant findings across various genetic models (G vs. A: OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.29–1.39; GG vs. AA + GA: OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.38–1.54; GG vs. AA: OR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.66–1.91; GA vs. AA: OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.22–1.40). However, the dominant model (GG + GA vs. AA) demonstrated high heterogeneity (I2 > 75%, p < 0.05), yielding an OR of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.67–1.05).

In Black populations, similar patterns were noted: G vs. A: OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.18–1.71; GG vs. AA + GA: OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.10–2.22; GG vs. AA: OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.26–2.83; GA vs. AA: OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.12–1.96, except for low heterogeneity (I2 > 25%, p > 0.05) with dominant model (GG + GA vs. AA: OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.75–1.50).

Similarly, the mixed population analyzed in the two studies exhibited statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) for the allelic model (G vs. A: OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.04–2.00), the recessive model (GG vs. AA + GA: OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 0.95–3.17), and the co-dominant model (GG vs. AA: OR = 2.35, 95% CI: 1.19–4.64). However, high heterogeneity (I2 > 75%, p < 0.05) was noted in the dominant model (GG + GA vs. AA: OR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.02–1.32) and the co-dominant model (GA vs. AA: OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 0.85–2.36) (Table 3). The corresponding forest plots are presented in Fig. S6.

Thus, the G allele of the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism is linked to an elevated risk of Crohn's disease (CD) across all racial subgroups. Additionally, contrary to earlier controversies surrounding the association of ATG16L1 rs2241880 variants with CD risk in Mongolian populations, our larger sample size lends support to the conclusion that carriers of these variants in Mongolians are indeed at a higher risk for CD.

Results of the age subgroup association analysis

In the analysis of age subgroups, we found that the rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism was significantly different (p < 0.001) across most genetic models in adults. The OR observed were as follows: G vs. A: OR = 1.33 (95% CI: 1.29–1.38); GG vs. AA + GA: OR = 1.46 (95% CI: 1.39–1.54); GG vs. AA: OR = 1.78 (95% CI: 1.66–1.91); and GA vs. AA: OR = 1.31 (95% CI: 1.23–1.39). Notably, a high level of heterogeneity was observed in the dominant model (GG + GA vs. AA), with I2 > 75% and p < 0.05, yielding an OR of 1.31 (95% CI: 1.23–1.39) (Table 4). The corresponding forest plots are presented in Fig. S7.

Discussion

Our findings revealed a significant correlation between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G allele and CD susceptibility globally. Unlike the earlier study by Cheng et al. [21], which included 14,095 cases and 7,531 controls, our meta-analysis initially identified 65 studies, totaling 20,346 cases and 27,642 controls. Ultimately, through NOS quality evaluation, meta-regression analysis, and heterogeneity assessment, we narrowed our focus to 46 studies, comprising 15,489 cases and 20,724 controls, allowing us to achieve a larger overall sample size.

For different racial subgroups, the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism shows a consistent trend in susceptibility to CD. In Caucasians, although the recent study by Simovic I et al. [6] included a larger sample size with 21,270 cases and 34,774 controls, our findings regarding the association between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele and CD susceptibility in the allelic model (G vs. A: OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.29–1.39, p = 0.000) align closely with their results (G vs. A: OR = 1.357, 95% CI: 1.031–1.785, p = 0.029). A comparable trend was observed in Black individuals (G vs. A: OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.18–1.71, p = 0.001) and mixed populations (G vs. A: OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.04–2.00, p = 0.000).

In Mongoloid populations (or Asian populations), the insignificant statistical results reported in some studies remain controversial. For instance, a study conducted by Na et al. [14], which involved 65 cases and 72 controls, found an OR of 1.37 (95% CI: 0.69–2.71, p = 0.37) in allelic model (G vs. A). In contrast, our study included a larger sample of 3,594 cases and 4,325 controls, revealing an OR of 1.24 (95% CI: 1.15–1.34, p = 0.000), thereby supporting the findings of Zhang et al. [19], who examined 420 cases and 450 controls (G vs. A: OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.03–1.35, p = 0.016). However, Simovic I et al. [6] conducted a study with a larger sample size among the East Asian population, which consisted of 3,919 cases and 10,075 controls, and reported an OR of 1.058 (95% CI: 0.932–1.200, p = 0.383). Additionally, they examined the South Asian population, which included 337 cases and 853 controls, and reported an OR of 1.477 (95% CI: 0.894–2.438, p = 0.128). Consequently, the association between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism and susceptibility to CD among Mongoloid populations (or Asian populations) remains a topic of ongoing debate.

A similar association between the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism and susceptibility to CD was observed across different age subgroups, with adults showing an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.29–1.38, p = 0.000) and children exhibiting an OR of 1.27 (95% CI: 1.10–1.47, p = 0.000) in allelic model (G vs. A). Furthermore, this association was consistent across gender subgroups, with males having an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.29–1.37, p = 0.000) compared to females in allelic model (G vs. A). However, these findings are partially inconsistent with the age subgroup results reported by Simovic I et al. [6], which indicated an OR of 1.19 (95% CI: 1.06–1.34, p = 0.003) for adults and 1.47 (95% CI: 1.12–1.95, p = 0.006) for children.

Importantly, a gene dosage dependency effect may influence the strength of the association between ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism and susceptibility to CD. When comparing the two overall co-dominant models between (GG vs. AA) and (GA vs. AA), we found that, both ATG16L1 rs2241880 GG carriers and GA carriers were at an increased risk for CD; however, GA carriers had a lower risk (GA vs. AA, OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.23–1.38, p = 0.000) compared to GG carriers (GG vs. AA, OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.65–1.88, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.000). This perspective is supported by the work of Gaëlle Quiniou et al. [74], who found that dendritic cells (DCs) derived from monocytes of healthy donors gradually reprogram their autophagy flux as they mature. In contrast, the homozygous ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A allele polymorphism impaired the ability of monocyte-derived DCs from patients with CD to reprogram their autophagic flux during maturation. Notably, this impairment was not observed in polymorphic heterozygous CD patients. Thus, the difference in susceptibility between ATG16L1 rs2241880 GG carriers and GA carriers may be attributed to a gene dosage dependency effect.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample sizes for Black and mixed-race populations may not be adequate to accurately represent their true associations; therefore, further research is necessary. Second, the initial results of the forest plot included 56 studies that demonstrated high heterogeneity (I2 > 75%, p < 0.05) across most genetic models. After assessing for heterogeneity, we excluded 10 studies that contributed to this heterogeneity. In our findings, the overall population, as well as the racial, age, and gender subgroups analyzed under the dominant model (GG + GA vs. AA), may represent significant sources of this heterogeneity. Consequently, these results suggest that confounding variables associated with a large sample size may lead to false-positive conclusions in statistical analyses [75]. After final exclusion, the heterogeneity was significantly reduced, but there was no significant publication bias (p < 0.05) or sensitivity analysis, indicating that our results are reliable.

In conclusion, the ATG16L1 rs2241880 polymorphism may be a novel biomarker for CD and other related diseases. With an increase in the sample size, our study is the first to demonstrate that the ATG16L1 rs2241880 G/A gene polymorphism is broadly associated with CD risk in the overall population, and shows racial differences across racial subgroups, particularly demonstrating the CD risk among the Mongoloid populations. ATG16L1 rs2241880 G allele carriers were more likely to be associated with CD than A allele carriers. In addition, males with ATG16L1 rs2241880 G allele carriers have a higher CD risk than females. Furthermore, adults with ATG16L1 rs2241880 G allele carriers have a higher CD risk than children. Nevertheless, its mechanism of action in viral infections, polygenic inheritance, and environmental interactions remains to be elucidated.
