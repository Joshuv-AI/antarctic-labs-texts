A report on the successful sparing of the entire anterior mitral leaflet through the implementation of a customized procedure in the context of mitral valve replacement
Authors: Francesco Nappi, Ibrahim Abdou, Antonio Salsano, Ivancarmine Gambardella, Sanjeet Singh Avtaar Singh, Almothana Alzamil, Camille Bourgois, Thibaut Schoell, Nicolas Bonnet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMitral valve replacement (MVR) in patients with complex mitral valve (MV) pathology often necessitates excision of the anterior mitral leaflet (AML), potentially compromising left ventricular (LV) geometry and function. We describe a customised full chordal-sparing mitral valve replacement (FCS-MVRpl) technique that preserves the AML and subvalvular apparatus, aiming to maintain LV architecture and improve postoperative outcomes in patients unsuitable for MV repair.MethodsBetween 2014 and 2023, 161 consecutive patients underwent FCS-MVRpl. MV pathology included degenerative (n = 94, 57.1%), functional (n = 58, 36.0%), infective (n = 9, 5.6%), and rheumatic (n = 2, 1.2%) disease. Among these, 93 patients (57.8%) exhibited complex MV pathology: massive annular calcification (n = 59, 36.6%), mitral annular disjunction (MAD; n = 25, 15.5%), and posterior annular abscesses due to endocarditis (n = 9, 5.6%). The AML was fully mobilised and translocated, preserving the entire chordal apparatus. In cases with excess leaflet tissue, partial excision of the free edge was performed to facilitate prosthesis seating while maintaining chordal integrity.ResultsAt 12 months, freedom from procedural complications was 98.6% ± 0.97%, with only two adverse events (1.2%) directly attributable to the technique. Freedom from heart failure-related rehospitalisation was 94.2% ± 1.9%. Multivariable Cox regression identified reduced preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) as a predictor of treatment failure (HR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.92–0.99; p = 0.015). NYHA functional class improved in the majority of patients: 112 (81.2%) were classified as Class I at follow-up, while 11 (8.0%) experienced deterioration.ConclusionFCS-MVRpl is a safe and effective alternative for patients with complex MV pathology who are not candidates for repair. Preservation of the AML and subvalvular apparatus supports LV geometry and functional recovery. The technique is particularly beneficial in cases of posterior annular calcification, Barlow disease with excess leaflet tissue, and infective endocarditis involving the posterior annulus. However, it is contraindicated in cases of circumferential MV calcification. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate long-term outcomes and broader applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-025-03831-x.

Introduction

Preservation of the subvalvular apparatus during mitral valve replacement (MVR) is essential for maintaining left ventricular (LV) geometry and function. This principle has been substantiated by numerous experimental and clinical studies [1–4]. Investigations into the impact of partial [3–5] or complete excision of the mitral valve apparatus have demonstrated significant deterioration in postoperative ventricular performance. To mitigate this, techniques such as the use of expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE; Gore-Tex sutures, W. L. Gore, Flagstaff, AZ) have been employed to maintain continuity between the mitral annulus and papillary muscles (PMs) [6–9].

We present a customised surgical approach that preserves the mitral valve’s subvalvular apparatus in both anatomical and physiological terms. The technique involves complete mobilisation and translocation of the anterior mitral leaflet (AML), facilitating full chordal-sparing mitral valve replacement (FCS-MVR) while maintaining LV function over time (Fig. 1A–E) and (Fig. 2).

Methods

Ethical approval and data collection

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Montpellier (IRB No. UM 2023-010, May 17). The requirement for individual patient consent was waived. A retrospective analysis was conducted using a prospectively maintained database of patients who underwent surgical treatment for mitral valve (MV) disease between 2014 and 2023. Data entry was performed prospectively during hospitalisation and updated during follow-up visits. Validation of data integrity was ensured through regular internal and external audits. Survival and reintervention data were obtained via electronic medical records, communication with referring clinicians, and direct contact with patients or their families. Mortality data were cross-referenced with the French national mortality registry (https://www.insee.fr) prior to statistical analysis.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed via median sternotomy with standard bicaval cannulation. MV exposure was achieved through a transatrial incision, as described by Guiraudon et al. [10]. Normothermic (36 °C) cardiopulmonary bypass was employed. Myocardial protection was achieved using antegrade, intermittent warm potassium-rich cardioplegia, or combined antegrade and retrograde delivery in prolonged cases. For patients requiring systemic cooling, rewarming was managed by maintaining a 10 °C gradient between core and internal temperatures until 36 °C.

In cases deemed irreparable by preoperative echocardiography and intraoperative inspection, the anterior mitral leaflet (AML) was detached between the commissures (Fig. 1A, B). Full mobilisation preserved the subvalvular apparatus, typically comprising 4–6 chords per papillary muscle (Fig. 2B). Once fully mobilized this strip of AML was translocated and then reattached to the posterior leaflet and annulus in the corresponding location with the valve sutures. (Fig. 1C-E)

In patients with Barlow’s disease or bivalvular prolapse, 3–5 mm of excess AML tissue was excised to reduce the risk of neocollagenisation and secondary stenosis, and to mitigate LVOT obstruction (Fig. 2A, B). The AML was reattached to the posterior leaflet and annulus using prosthetic valve sutures (Fig. 1C). Preservation of leaflet geometry was more effective when the AML remained intact [11, 12]. In cases of severe anterior annular calcification, FCS-MVRpl was contraindicated. In order to preserve the AML’s structure, elliptical excision was avoided. Where FCS-MVRpl was a viable option, the tissue with the AML and primary chords was reattached to the posterior annulus, after which the prosthesis was carefully positioned in full contact with the annulus. (Fig. 1D, E).

In cases of mitral annular disjunction (MAD) (Supplementary Figs. 1 & 2) or mitral annular calcification (MAC), redundant AML tissue was used for annular reinforcement or sutured to the atrial side prior to prosthesis placement. A 4 − 0 Prolene suture (Ethicon, Sommerville, NJ, USA) was used to secure excess tissue to the left atrial endocardium, preventing leaflet extension over the sewing ring.

AML translocation prevented LVOT obstruction and prosthetic interference (Fig. 1C–E). In double valve procedures, direct inspection via the open aorta confirmed AML positioning. Obstruction risk increased when excessive AML tissue remained near partitions 3–4 of the aortic aspect of the annulus [8, 13].

The posterior mitral leaflet (PML) was retained when pliable, with redundant tissue folded into the annulus. In cases of fibrosis or thickening, wedge resection facilitated prosthesis implantation.

In infective endocarditis (IE) with multileaflet involvement and poor annular integrity, repair was contraindicated. MV replacement with stented xenograft or mechanical prosthesis was performed. Translocated AML tissue was used for posterior annular abscess repair and annular reconstruction, sutured with 4 − 0 polypropylene prior to prosthesis insertion.

AML relocation enabled neoannulus formation, reducing the risk of annular decalcification in posterior MAC with or without MAD. AML was sutured over the PML or encased with atrial sutures around the retracted annulus. This reduced traction between calcified annulus, PML, and subvalvular structures. In diffuse myxomatous degeneration, abnormal systolic superior papillary muscle shift and traction were observed. A potential link between papillary muscle mechanics and MAD warrants further investigation. Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages in text and tables, with Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test used as appropriate. The normality criteria were met for each continuous variable. For normally distributed data, independent sample or paired sample t-tests were employed, with mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range presented in text and tables.

The development of event-free survival curves was facilitated through the implementation of the Kaplan–Meier method. In order to account for the presumed correlation with treatment failure, a multivariable model was performed, incorporating baseline age, gender, diabetes, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and severity of mitral regurgitation (MR).

Treatment effects were estimated as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Derivation of these intervals utilised the Cox proportional hazards model, whilst the proportional hazards assumption was assessed via a graphical method. A p value less than 0.05 was designated as statistically significant. The analyses were conducted utilising R statistical software, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The present study was conducted between January 2014 and November 2023, and its primary objective was to investigate the outcomes of FCR-MVRpl using the AML translocation, with a particular focus on the incidence of adverse events. Indications for FCR-MVRpl included degenerative valve disease in 58.4% of cases (94 patients), functional MR in 36.0% (58 patients), and infective endocarditis in 5.6% (9 patients). The mean age of subjects undergoing FCR-MVRpl was recorded as 72.52 years, with a standard deviation of ± 10.36 years. The most common underlying cause of functional mitral regurgitation was secondary ischemic MR in 72.4% of cases. Furthermore, it is notable that MAD occurred in 15.5% of patients, and 36.6% presented with severe mitral valve calcification.

The majority of subjects (66.5%) underwent concomitant surgical interventions, including 37 coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) procedures, 64 tricuspid valve repair operations, 11 aortic valve replacements, and 2 septal myectomies. Furthermore, 59 subjects exhibited mitral valve calcification, while 25 subjects demonstrated MAD (Table 2). Notably, the vast majority of subjects (96.3%) were administered biological prostheses, whereas a comparatively smaller proportion (only 3.7%) were fitted with mechanical valves for mitral valve replacement.

Within 12 months of FCR-MVRpl, 2 patients (1.2%) had procedure-related complications and were reoperated on for valve thrombosis. At the 12-month post-operative evaluation, freedom from post-operative complications of the FCR-MVRpl procedure was 98.6 ± 0.97% (Fig. 5). The study was completed at the 12 month follow-up period. Postoperative mortalities were observed in 14 patients within 30 days (8.7%) and 23 patients within 12 months (14.3%). The survival rate following FCR-MVRpl was 85.5% at 12 months post-surgery, with a standard deviation of ± 2.8% (Fig. 6). 12 months following FCR-MVRpl, LVEDDI was recorded as 26.07 ± 4.11 mm/m2, LVESDI as 18.48 ± 4.59 mm/m2 and LVEF as 49.93 ± 11.31%. A comparison of the baseline values revealed that LVEDDI and LVESDI had undergone significant improvement following a one-year period of observation (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively). No statistically significant differences were observed in LVEF over the same time period (p = 0.09). Excluding patients who did not reach 12 months of follow-up, 11 (8.0%) patients had a worse NYHA functional class compared to the start of the observation period. However, the majority of patients exhibited benefits from FCR-MVRpl, with 112 patients (81.2%) being in NYHA Class 1 (Fig. 7). Mitral prosthesis dysfunction was observed in a total of 2 patients (representing 1.2% of the study population) who subsequently developed thrombosis.

Discussion

The primary safety endpoint—freedom from procedural-related complications at 12 months—was achieved in 98.6% ± 0.97% of cases, indicating a high procedural success rate for full chordal-sparing mitral valve replacement (FCS-MVRpl). Monitored complications included cardiac rupture, undersizing of the prosthesis, interference from retained subvalvular structures, LVOT obstruction, and systolic/diastolic dysfunction [13–15]. No structural valve deterioration (SVD) was observed at 12 months, although non-SVD complications such as prosthesis mismatch, malposition, and paravalvular regurgitation were considered. Valve sizing was consistent with body surface area, eliminating the risk of undersizing due to retained tissue. The most frequently implanted prosthesis size was 29 mm (32.9%), followed by 33 mm (32.3%) and 31 mm (24.2%).

FCS-MVRpl demonstrated benefit across both organic and functional MV pathologies, particularly in patients unsuitable for repair due to extensive annular calcification. Preservation of annular–papillary muscle continuity was associated with improved ventricular mechanics and reduced risk of adverse outcomes [15–23].

Mitral annular calcification (MAC), present in 36.6% of patients, was not independently associated with increased procedural complications. However, anterior annular calcification was a contraindication for FCS-MVRpl due to the risk of trigonous injury. Excessive decalcification, especially in elderly patients, was avoided to prevent posterior ventricular rupture. In such cases, transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) may be considered [22–25].

The decision to perform extensive subvalvular preservation was guided by intraoperative assessment of calcification distribution and anterior annular integrity. Preservation of the physiological tethering effect was prioritised to maintain LV geometry and function [20, 26–28]. No cases of LVOT obstruction or prosthetic valve dysfunction were observed due to retained leaflet tissue. This outcome underscores the importance of complete AML mobilisation and translocation. Inadequate resection of exuberant AML tissue may result in entrapment and dynamic obstruction [13, 29, 30].

Finite element analysis (FEA) studies have shown that altered valve geometry increases leaflet and stent stress during systole [22, 23, 31–35]. In Barlow’s disease or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, retained AML may contribute to LVOT obstruction via systolic anterior motion [11, 12]. Unlike edge-to-edge or Stanford techniques, the FCS-MVRpl approach involves excision of excess AML tissue and fixation to the posterior annulus, mitigating this risk [11, 12, 22, 23, 31–33].

Two reoperations were required due to bioprosthetic thrombosis in patients with atrial fibrillation who were not anticoagulated. Radiofrequency ablation and left atrial appendage ligation were not performed, as these patients did not meet guideline criteria [16].

Despite the technical complexity of Barlow’s disease, particularly when associated with MAD or MAC, outcomes were comparable to other series [8, 13, 19–21, 36, 37]. Techniques involving transposition of anterior chordal attachments to the posterior annulus have been associated with prosthetic dysfunction due to chordal entrapment [30].

Variability in outcomes may reflect differences in surgical philosophy. Some surgeons limit preservation to the posterior leaflet in complex cases, while others pursue full preservation in younger or lower-risk patients. The lack of consensus regarding the extent of MV disease, annular calcification, and AML involvement contributes to heterogeneity in practice and outcomes [6–8, 20, 21, 26, 30, 36].

The selection of left ventricular end-diastolic dimension index (LVEDDI) as a secondary endpoint was informed by robust evidence linking LVEDDI to clinical outcomes, including New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, hospitalisation rates, and survival [34, 35, 38–40]. In this study, the FCS-MVRpl technique resulted in a reduction in both LVEDD and LVESD at 12 months. However, no statistical comparison was performed between degenerative and functional cohorts. After adjustment for baseline confounders, lower preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was independently associated with increased risk of rehospitalisation for heart failure and treatment failure at one year post-FCS-MVRpl. The underlying causes of suboptimal outcomes in the subset of patients who were readmitted (5.6%) or experienced treatment failure (5.8%) remain unclear. These outcomes are unlikely to be fully explained by variations in LV remodelling, as assessed by changes in indexed LV end-systolic volume (LVESVI) and LVEDVI, or by differential responses to FCS-MVRpl between degenerative and functional MV disease cohorts.

Patients with degenerative MV disease typically exhibit smaller ventricles and preserved mitral valve spatial configuration compared to those with functional disease [17, 18, 34, 37, 41–45]. In functional disease, changes in LVESVI over time have been strongly associated with adverse outcomes, particularly in the context of residual myocardial scar and reduced LVEF [17, 18, 41–45]. This deterioration in ventricular function may limit the efficacy of edge-to-edge transcatheter interventions [22]. In contrast, conservative MVR techniques that preserve the subvalvular apparatus have demonstrated favourable effects on LV mechanics [5, 7, 8, 36, 46].

Although LVESVI and LVEDVI improved over the 12-month follow-up [17, 42], differences in LVESVI percentage change between degenerative and functional cohorts may influence LV end-systolic stress and overall function [27, 28, 36, 37]. These discordant changes reflect the interdependence among LVESS, LVESVI, LVEF, and LVEDVI following MVR, as described in the mathematical model by Goldfine et al. [27]. According to this model, for a given LVEDVI, a lower LVEF corresponds to a higher LVESVI, which in turn increases LVESS via the Laplace relationship.

Additionally, elevated EuroSCORE, advanced age, and concomitant procedures may have contributed to the observed rates of rehospitalisation and treatment failure [41–45, 47]. Further investigation is warranted to delineate the mechanistic basis of these findings and optimise patient selection for FCS-MVRpl.

Limitations

The principal limitation of this study is its single-arm design, which precludes direct comparison with alternative surgical techniques. To validate the generalisability and efficacy of the FCS-MVRpl approach, future multicentre studies with larger patient cohorts are warranted. These groups may yield critical insights into the technique’s applicability and long-term outcomes.

The observational nature of the study introduces potential confounding, necessitating adjustment for baseline differences. Nonetheless, residual confounding from unmeasured or unknown variables may persist. Prior studies have demonstrated no significant differences in the improvement of LVEDDI, LVESDI, and LVEF over short- and long-term follow-up in patients with degenerative versus functional mitral valve disease [4, 36].

Despite rigorous follow-up protocols, echocardiographic data were only collected at the 12-month endpoint, limiting temporal resolution of ventricular remodelling. This constraint may obscure intermediate changes and dynamic trends. Additionally, the possibility of survivor bias cannot be excluded, as patients lost to follow-up or deceased prior to the 12-month mark were not included in the final echocardiographic analysis [46]. Aside from this, the surgeon and centre-specific learning curves and familiarity also need to be accounted for in this study.

While the study occasionally references outcomes from published literature and historical controls, it does not include a direct comparative analysis with patients undergoing conventional mitral valve replacement (MVR) during the same timeframe. This further limits the ability to critically evaluate potential selection bias and procedural efficacy. A retrospective comparison with contemporaneous patients treated with standard MVR at the same institution would strengthen the findings and contextualise the benefits of the FCS-MVRpl technique. Such an analysis could help delineate whether observed improvements in ventricular remodelling, procedural safety, and functional outcomes are attributable to the technique itself or to confounding institutional or patient-level factors. Future studies should incorporate matched control cohorts or propensity-adjusted comparisons to mitigate bias and validate the reproducibility of these results across broader populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, preservation of the mitral subvalvular apparatus during MVR has been shown to be safe and effective in terms of procedure-related complications, providing an early benefit by reducing LV chamber size and diastolic afterload. Therefore, in order to optimise early postoperative and late LV systolic function, when MVR is necessary, we recommend preserving all chordal structures by translocating the AML instead of quadripartitioning the MV. Despite the absence of robust scientific evidence, the field of surgery has adopted a reparative approach to the surgical management of the mitral valve, superseding the conventional replacement approach. According to data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons, spanning the years 2008 to 2012, 66% of mitral valve surgeries performed on patients undergoing coronary artery bypass graft utilised a reparative approach [47]. In cases of severe secondary mitral regurgitation where patients are not candidates for double-level mitral valve repair [41–45], and severe mitral valve prolapse of both leaflets (Barlow’s disease), a reassessment of surgical intervention may be warranted. These patients may be deemed not eligible for surgery to repair the mitral valve. This is due to the fact that the extent and severity of the injury may impede the efficacy of the repair procedure. Although mitral valve replacement using the FCS-MVRpl procedure provides durable solution for mitral regurgitation, it is essential to exercise caution when interpreting the impact of current conditions on future outcomes, as further validation through nuclear magnetic resonance studies comparing different procedures is necessary. In addition, the advantages offered by FCS-MVR should be evaluated in relation to the adverse effects that are commonly associated with prosthetic valve usage. Nevertheless, transcatheter mitral valve therapy has the potential to mitigate this problem.
