China's revised infectious diseases law: a step towards stronger global pandemic preparedness
Authors: Yi Zhang, Jialin Li, Annemieke van den Dool
Published: 2025

Drawing lessons from COVID-19 is crucial to strengthening pandemic preparedness. This is especially important in China, a hotspot for emerging infectious diseases.1 Recognizing the urgent need to address gaps in its legal framework, China recently adopted a major overhaul of its Law on the Prevention and Treatment of Infectious Diseases, which entered into force on September 1.2

Although revisions of the Law on the Prevention and Treatment of Infectious Diseases were suggested earlier—including in response to the 2009 H1N1 outbreak3—it was not until COVID-19 that this law was included in the official legislative plan. As the COVID-19 outbreak unfolded, experts identified multiple legal shortcomings, including delays in disease classification, ambiguity and rigidity in emergency response powers, overly centralized information disclosure rules that restricted local government response, and failure to codify a direct reporting system and mandate independent reporting.4,5

Recognizing these issues, and following President Xi Jinping's speech on the matter,6 China's National People's Congress announced in April 2020 an unprecedented, comprehensive health legislation reform plan, which included the Law on the Prevention and Treatment of Infectious Diseases.7 After multiple consultation processes and alongside the revision of other domestic and international legislation, the revised law was finally adopted on April 30, 2025.

The revised law substantially reforms China's infectious disease governance and enhances alignment with the 2024 amendment to the International Health Regulations (2005) (‘IHR’). The following three aspects are especially important for pandemic preparedness.

Surveillance and early warning system

The revised law emphasizes surveillance far more than before, as it mandates sentinel surveillance for key infectious diseases and those of unknown origin, expands monitoring to include infectious disease symptoms, and requires the collection of information on syndromes and clusters of cases with unknown causes (article 42).8 This approach supports China's reporting obligations under the revised IHR, which require states to notify WHO of ‘clusters of cases of severe acute respiratory disease of unknown or novel cause’, if such events have a serious public health impact or are ‘unusual and unexpected’ (Annex 2 of the IHR).9 Notably, an event may be deemed serious even if few or no human cases have been identified, provided it poses a significant public health risk.

Improved reporting mechanisms

The revised law requires responsible authorities to streamline reporting channels and facilitate timely notification of emerging or unknown infectious diseases. Importantly, the new law explicitly states that entities and individuals who report in good faith are eligible for rewards and are exempt from liability if the event is later ruled out (article 51).8 These provisions promote early, transparent reporting—core principles of the IHR.

Information technology and personal information protection

The revised law explicitly calls for the use of modern information technology in epidemic prevention but also stipulates measures to ensure data security and to protect individual privacy (article 13).8 Excessive collection of personal information is prohibited, and relevant data—such as personal digital travel codes—must not be used for purposes beyond infectious disease prevention and control. Entities that violate personal information protection obligations or infringe on individual privacy are subject to legal penalties.

Despite these promising revisions, the revised law does not clearly specify the content required in disease reporting. In practice, China has established internal mechanisms within the National Health Commission to facilitate both domestic and international notifications. Nevertheless, to enhance legal clarity and ensure consistency with the IHR, such notification duties could be more clearly articulated in accompanying regulations or detailed rules on infectious disease reporting.

The revised law represents an important step towards stronger infectious disease governance. By strengthening domestic disease monitoring and reporting systems, the law enables earlier detection, more effective outbreak response, reducing the risk of cross-border disease transmission. By aligning more closely with the IHR,10 the revised law will contribute to greater coherence in global health governance, facilitating smoother coordination and cooperation among countries during public health emergencies. Moving forward, the law's impact will depend on effective implementation, further regulatory clarification, and sustained political commitment to transparency and accountability in public health—developments that merit close attention from scholars and global health practitioners alike.
