Lymphatic-pulmonary venous anastomosis for plastic bronchitis in a Fontan patient
Authors: Szu-Yen Hu, Min-Yuan Liu, Yi-Chia Wang, Heng-Wen Chou, Yih-Sharng Chen, Shu-Chien Huang
Published: 2026

End-to-side anastomosis of lymphatic duct to left lower pulmonary vein.

Central Message

Magnetic resonance lymphangiography–targeted lymphatic-to-pulmonary venous anastomosis can resolve plastic bronchitis after Fontan.

Plastic bronchitis (PB) is a rare but morbid complication in Fontan circulation, driven by elevated central venous pressure, lymphatic congestion, and aberrant pulmonary lymphatic perfusion. The resulting deposition of fibrinous bronchial casts manifests clinically as expectoration of characteristic rubbery, branching plugs.1

Management combines medical therapy with surgical lymphatic decompression, guided by T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging and dynamic contrast magnetic resonance lymphangiography (DCMRL).2,3 Current surgical strategies are largely thoracic duct (TD)-centric, redirecting central lymph into the systemic atrium or veins.3,4 Preclinical work has demonstrated the feasibility of redirecting lymphatic flow into the pulmonary venous system as an alternative recipient.5 We describe a patient who developed PB 5 years after Fontan completion, in whom DCMRL identified the offending lymphatic channel and guided anastomosis to an adjacent pulmonary vein (PV).

Case Presentation

A 9-year-old girl with right atrial isomerism, double-outlet right ventricle, complete atrioventricular (AV) septal defect, and pulmonary atresia underwent staged single-ventricle palliation with fenestrated extracardiac total cavopulmonary connection at age 3 years. Another AV valve repair and fenestration reduction were performed at age 6 years.

From age 7 years, she developed progressive cough refractory to medical therapy, and by age 8 years she presented with exertional dyspnea and expectoration of bronchial casts (Figure 1, A). Cardiac catheterization revealed mean pulmonary artery pressure 14 mm Hg and systemic ventricular end-diastolic pressure 10 mm Hg. Computed tomography demonstrated lingular atelectasis with chylous plugs (Figure 1, B) within the left bronchial tree. DCMRL (Figure 1, C) demonstrated a bifurcated distal TD draining bilaterally into subclavian veins, with abnormal retrograde lymphatic flow from the midthoracic level into mediastinal nodes, pulmonary interstitium, and bronchial walls on the left side.

A, Expectorated branched bronchial cast. B, Computed tomography demonstrating lingular atelectasis with chylous plugs. The white arrow indicate the cast within the left bronchail tree. C, Preoperative dynamic contrast magnetic resonance lymphangiography showed abnormal lymphatic reflux at the midthoracic level into mediastinal lymph nodes, pulmonary interstitium, and bronchial walls. D, Intraoperative photograph of the end-to-side anastomosis between the aberrant left lymphatic channel and the left lower pulmonary vein.

Surgery was performed via median resternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass. After AV valve repair, the left hilar lymphatic plexus was dissected and the dilated aberrant left lymphatic channel was identified. An end-to-side anastomosis between this channel and the left lower PV was constructed with 7-0 polypropylene under 3× surgical loupe magnification (Figure 1, D, Video 1).

Cast expectoration ceased postoperatively. Oral rivaroxaban was initiated on the second postoperative day and continued thereafter. At 18-month follow-up, the patient remained symptom-free with normal activity. Repeat DCMRL (Figure 2) demonstrated resolution of the previously noted abnormal lymphatic reflux. Computed tomography showed resolution of the left perihilar infiltration and chylous plugging within the bronchial trees. Echocardiography demonstrated only mild residual AV valve regurgitation.

B, Computed tomography revealed improvement of left perihilar infiltration and no residual bronchial cast.

Approval was waived for this single case report. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient's parents on September 22, 2025.

Discussion

Current management for PB includes medical therapy and lymphatic decompression by surgical redirection of TD to the systemic veins4 or percutaneous lymphatic embolization.3

Lymphatic embolization is an effective therapy; however, because lymph may recanalize toward an alternative low-pressure pathway, durability can be a consideration. In our patient, DCMRL demonstrated lymph draining directly into the left lower bronchial tree, and an end-to-side anastomosis was performed to the adjacent PV. Preclinical work supports TD-to-PV anastomosis as a physiologic concept5; the present case applies the same recipient choice through a focal, lesion-specific anastomosis. Directing the offending channel into the PV may provide an alternative low-pressure outlet, although longer follow-up is needed.

DCMRL was central to the approach, enabling identification of the bifurcated TD and localization of the dominant aberrant channel.3 However, DCMRL alone may not fully delineate the central lymphatic anatomy, and central lymphatic catheterization could provide a more complete anatomic roadmap for precise preoperative planning.

In this case, a focal lymphatic-to-PV anastomosis achieved a satisfactory result. We believe lymphatic-to-PV anastomosis offers a viable surgical option for PB in selected patients with Fontan circulation.

Conclusions

Lymphatic-to-PV anastomosis was effective for PB in our patient, with imaging-confirmed resolution at 18 months. DCMRL enabled diagnosis and lesion-specific localization of the offending channel, although further experience is needed to define long-term durability.
