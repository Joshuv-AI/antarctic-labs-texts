Unraveling the role of the western North Pacific circulation anomaly in modulating Indian summer monsoon rainfall variability beyond ENSO
Authors: Jun-Hyeok Son, Christian L. E. Franzke, Han-Kyoung Kim, Jae-Heung Park, Jung-Eun Chu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Indian summer monsoon (ISM) rainfall interannual variability is known to be strongly linked to the El-Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). This linear relationship is the primary factor in controlling the interannual variation in ISM precipitation. However, there are many outlier cases, and such deviations pose significant challenges in seasonal prediction over this region. Here we show that such challenges can be attributed to anomalous atmospheric pressure patterns in the Western North Pacific (WNP) region. The anticyclonic circulation anomaly over WNP region causes the easterly wind toward the Indian subcontinent, leading to positive precipitation anomalies with stronger low-level moist convergence, while the cyclonic circulation decreases ISM precipitation. The linear baroclinic model simulation results further support that the WNP circulation pattern can serve as an independent factor for forecasting precipitation over India. The WNP circulation anomaly play the crucial role generating ISM precipitation particularly for July and September. Our study suggests that the role of the WNP circulation anomaly should be carefully considered as the secondary prevailing mechanism on the subseasonal timescale during the boreal summer in addition to the ENSO signal.

Introduction

The Indian subcontinent, one of the most abundant precipitation regions in the world, receives ~ 80% of its annual total rainfall during the summer season from June to September, with the highest amount of rainfall occurring in July (Fig. 1). Therefore, rainfall variability during the summer monsoon is of particular interest as it can have significant socio-economic impacts. The variability of monsoon rainfall encompasses a broad range of time scales, ranging from intraseasonal to interdecadal time scales. The large-scale drivers of the Indian summer monsoon (ISM) variability include, but are not limited to, monsoon depressions, the Madden-Julian Oscillation, Indian Ocean Dipole, El-Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), and Atlantic Multi-decadal Oscillation1.

One of the most dominant predictors of the ISM rainfall is the El-Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO)2. The strong inverse relationship between ENSO and the Indian summer monsoon (ISM) rainfall has been well documented in previous studies3–6. In general, the ISM rainfall tends to be enhanced during the La-Niña developing summer, while it is weakened during the El-Niño developing summer. This implies that the interannual variability of ISM rainfall is strongly linked to the sea surface temperature (SST) variation over the tropical eastern Pacific via the anomalous atmospheric Walker circulation7. By strengthening the Walker circulation during La-Niña developing phase, the lower-level equatorial easterly winds cause the convergence, deep convection, and upper tropospheric divergence over the ISM region, which is closely associated with the strong precipitation in summer over the Indian subcontinent (Fig. 1d and e), and vice versa during El-Nino developing phase. However, the spatiotemporal complexity of ENSO can lead to a wide range of diverse and far-reaching responses8–17. Moreover, the response to the same ENSO forcing can vary due to regional high-frequency variability, non-linear interactions with other climate factors and pre-existing atmospheric, oceanic, and terrestrial conditions18–26. Previous studies have reported a moderate negative linear relationship between Indian sub-continent rainfall and ENSO index, with correlation coefficients ranging from − 0.34 to − 0.48 based on 1979–2021 period with a 30-year sliding window27.

In order to improve the seasonal prediction of the ISM variability, however, it is important to discern whether the exceptional ISM events, which are not directly attributed to ENSO forcing, are caused by stochastic random perturbations in the global weather system commonly referred to as “noisy”24, or if they are a result of deterministic physical and dynamical processes28. In line with these discussions, Chakraborty and Singhai (2021)29 emphasize that the net moisture convergence driven by the surface pressure gradient around the Indian region plays a pivotal role in regulating ISM rainfall. Furthermore, Chaluvadi et al. (2023)30 highlight the combined effect of reduced western North Pacific (WNP) tropical cyclone activity and the westward shift of the WNP high-pressure system affecting the ISM rainfall during the non-ENSO years. Here the westward shift of the WNP high-pressure system and WNP circulation anomaly may be associated with the equatorial Rossby wave response induced by the precipitation anomaly over the Maritime Continent and impact of the Pacific-Japan pattern31–33. Meanwhile, it is also reported that the ISM precipitation may be a modulator of the WNP circulation anomaly in the previous study34. That means, the causal relationships between ISM rainfall and atmospheric circulation variability in WNP, specifically regarding non-ENSO-related events, have not been thoroughly understood. Here we focus on non-linear outlier events that deviate from the strong linkage between ENSO, and ISM and their causal relationship.

Data and methods

The monthly mean fifth generation of the atmospheric reanalysis dataset from the European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ERA5)35, the Hadley center sea ice and sea surface temperature data set (HadISST)36, and the Global Precipitation Climatology Project version 2.3 (GPCP)37 for the period 1979–2021 are used. All datasets are interpolated to a common 2.5° × 2.5° horizontal resolution grid except for the HadISST which has a resolution of 1.0° × 1.0°.

The rainy season for ISM is usually considered as June to September (JJAS). However, many studies report that the monthly interannual monsoon variability does not show a high correlation coefficient and each of them is strongly influenced by different climate factors38,39. This study focused on July, as this is the peak month of ISM rainfall across the entire Indian peninsula, and the WNP anticyclone is also the active. The ISM rainfall and velocity potential indices are calculated by averaging over the Indian subcontinent domain [5°–25°N, 70°–90°E]40, and the NINO3.4 index is calculated as the SST domain average over the area of 5°N–5°S, 170°–120°W41. To calculate normalized indices, the climatological mean was subtracted, and the time series was divided by the standard deviation based on monthly mean anomaly data for each index (i.e., for the normalized index in July, normalization was performed using the July mean and standard deviation in interannual timeseries over entire period 1979–2021). The regression analysis is performed to show the strong linear relationship between ENSO variation and ISM precipitation anomaly. And the composite analysis method is used to show the average pattern in a sample group. After we check there is a linear relationship between two groups, the composite difference analysis is performed. To show the statistical significance, we used the student’s t-test for the composite analysis, and for the regression analysis we used the critical values for Pearson’s correlation43.

The Linear Baroclinic Model (LBM)44 is used to show a causal relationship between Indian precipitation-related diabatic heating and WNP anti-cyclonic circulation anomaly in July. Based on the linearized atmospheric primitive equations, the LBM diagnoses steady-state atmospheric dynamical response to prescribed forcing. We used the horizontal resolution T42 with 20 vertical sigma levels, and the steady state response (~ 20 day) to the prescribed heat and pressure anomaly forcing is calculated, respectively. The circulation anomaly in the WNP region, the downstream area of the Tibetan Plateau, is known to have a vertically equivalent barotropic structure45. In addition, surface air-sea interaction has long been regarded as an important mechanism controlling the low-level pressure anomaly in the WNP region46. Therefore, surface pressure anomaly forcing is prescribed as 8 hPa per day, and the Indian diabatic heating is given as the middle heavy vertical structures with 2.5°K per day28,47.

Result

Linear relationship between ENSO and ISM

Due to the well-coupled characteristics of the ISM precipitation with the atmospheric circulation, the temporal correlation between ENSO and circulation parameters is much stronger compared to the direct correlation between the ENSO index and ISM rainfall. Figure 1 illustrates the spatiotemporal characteristics of ISM rainfall, and its connections to SST and upper-level velocity potential, with a focus on the peak rainfall month (i.e., July). The velocity potential has a strong negative correlation with the ISM precipitation over the entire Indian subcontinent (CORR=–0.78 ~ − 0.87 based on 30 years moving window) (Fig. 1e). Notably, the correlation between the velocity potential index and NINO3.4 exhibits significantly much higher values of 0.60 ~ 0.70 using 30 years window compared to the direct correlation between precipitation and NINO3.4 (i.e. − 0.27 ~ − 0.45).

However, as shown in Fig. 2, there are still many outlier events that deviate from the ENSO-ISM linear relationship. To identify these outliers, the normalized velocity potential index is subtracted from the normalized NINO3.4 index, and if the differences exceed ± 0.75 standard deviations, these events are classified as outliers. Although the specific threshold for defining outliers is set at ± 0.75 standard deviation, it is important to note that the overall results remain robust regardless of the exact definition of the threshold. We divided the outlier cases into two groups based on the deviation from the normal linear relationship. Cases in which the velocity potential index exceeds the + 0.75 standard deviation of the NINO3.4 index are categorized as the red group. This group can be interpreted as representing an extremely strong positive velocity potential and lower ISM precipitation that is not fully explained by ENSO. On the other hand, Cases in which the velocity potential index is smaller than the −0.75 standard deviation of the NINO3.4 index are classified as the group negatives, representing extremely weak velocity potential and strong ISM precipitation. Table 1 provides a list of specific years corresponding to each group, with the group positives and negatives consisting of 7 and 9 cases, respectively. The 27 black dots, which represent a strong linear relationship between the ISM and ENSO, are defined as the normal state. The historical correlation coefficient between the velocity potential index and NINO3.4 is 0.69 for the entire period; however, black dots only show a correlation value of 0.92.

Figure 3 depicts the strong linear relationship between ENSO and ISM, as well as their dynamical linkage, using 27 black dotted years shown in Fig. 2. During the La-Niña developing summer, the lower tropospheric tropical Pacific experiences strong easterly winds (Fig. 3c), which strengthen the upwelling in the eastern Pacific, resulting in reduced SST and precipitation over the tropical central Pacific (Fig. 3b). Easterly wind anomalies are prevailing over the tropical central Pacific, maritime continents, Bay of Bengal, and finally reach to the eastern part of the Indian subcontinent (Fig. 3c). The low-level convergent winds over the western Pacific maritime continents and Indian subcontinent lead to moisture convergence, which in turn, fosters increased precipitation through strong convection48–50. The enhanced convection over the Indian subcontinent and maritime continent cause upper-level divergence directly, and westerly winds to the Pacific ocean, which subsequently induces upper-level convergence and sinking motion over the eastern Pacific. This process is the well-known Bjerknes feedback for the development of the La-Niña showing strong linear relationship between ISM precipitation and Equatorial surface easterly winds7, which is summarized schematically in Fig. 3d. In El-Niño developing years, to develop the warm SST in central Pacific, all atmospheric and oceanic dynamical-thermodynamical components show opposite signs from cases La-Niña.

The role of the WNP anti-cyclonic circulation anomaly on ISM rainfall

Figure 4 indicates nonlinear outlier cases between ISM and ENSO selected based on the criteria outlined in Fig. 2. The group positives (negatives) in Fig. 2, where the ISM velocity potential index shifts towards the upper-level convergence (divergence) regardless of the ENSO index. To analyze characteristics in the two different sampling groups, a composite analysis is performed. The group positives (negatives) displays reduced (enhanced) precipitation over the Indian subcontinent (Fig. 4a and b), and this signal is even more pronounced after removing the ENSO signal (Fig. 4c and d). One notable difference between the two groups is the presence of cyclonic and anticyclonic circulation anomalies over the WNP region for groups positives and negatives, respectively (Fig. 4e and f). Along the southern flank of the cyclonic (anticyclonic) anomaly, there are noticeable westerly (easterly) wind anomalies that are directed from (towards) the Indian subcontinent. Consequently, the WNP cyclonic circulation anomaly leads to low-level moisture divergence and negative precipitation anomaly around the Indian subcontinent (Fig. 4g). On the other hand, the anticyclonic circulation anomaly results in moisture convergence and a positive precipitation anomaly over India (Fig. 4h). The positive precipitation anomaly is larger than 2 mm/day, which accounts for more than ~ 24 (7) % of the climatological July (JJAS) precipitation. The modulation of ISM precipitation by the WNP circulation anomaly is consistent signal with the previous studies32,48–51.

Earlier studies have suggested that the development of the circulation anomaly over the WNP region can be attributed to various mechanisms, including Indian Ocean basin-wide warming32,48–53, direct ENSO-related SST forcing54–56, summertime Madden-Julian Oscillation57,58, and local air-sea interactions59. However, all these factors are directly or indirectly linked to the ENSO variability. Other studies45,60, on the other hand, have pointed out that the Western North Pacific circulation anomaly is generated or controlled by the only atmospheric dynamical process via the downstream impact from the Tibetan Plateau. This implies that the WNP circulation anomaly can act as an independent factor that is dynamically connected to the ISM rainfall regardless of ENSO variability (Fig. 4e and f).

To understand the causal relationship between WNP circulation and ISM rainfall anomaly, we conducted the LBM experiment by prescribing surface high-pressure anomaly forcing in the WNP region and diabatic heat forcing in the Indian subcontinent, respectively (Fig. 5). We identify that the atmospheric response to WNP surface anticyclonic circulation forcing can effectively generate lower tropospheric anti-cyclonic circulation anomaly with a nearly barotropic structure. This circulation pattern inducing the anomalous ISM is consistent with the result reported in recent previous studies33,49,61. It is important to note that the presence of easterly wind anomalies extending from the Bay of Bengal to the Indian subcontinent (Fig. 5a) aligns with the result from the observational composite analysis shown in Fig. 4h. In observations, circulation anomaly in WNP region has vertically barotropic structure from surface to high altitude, while simulated result displays a slightly tilted structure to the northwest as the height increases. Our result suggests that any local forcings that cause low-level high-pressure anomalies over the WNP region, such as local air-sea interaction46 and Tibetan Plateau forced downstream atmospheric Rossby wave response45, can effectively act as drivers of the ISM rainfall variability. On the other hand, when diabatic forcing is prescribed in the ISM subcontinent with a middle-heavy vertical structure, the resulting circulation response is primarily confined around the forcing region. There is no significant circulation response observed in the WNP or its surrounding areas (Fig. 5b). However, it may enhance the climatological southwesterly monsoonal flows, leading to positive feedback causing the intense precipitation.

Summary and discussion

This study investigates the factors controlling interannual ISM variability, focusing on the peak month of ISM rainfall across the entire Indian peninsula. We apply a novel approach to disentangle the nonlinear relationship between the ISM and ENSO by categorizing the outlier cases those deviate significantly from the linear relationship into two groups: extremely negative (group negatives) and positive (group positives) ISM rainfall anomaly. The analysis reveals that the ISM precipitation is primarily controlled by ENSO variability in a linear manner. However, we highlight that the WNP circulation anomaly can act as an independent climate factor in influencing the relationship between ENSO and ISM rainfall. In order to demonstrate the causality between ISM and WNP circulation anomaly, we conduct the LBM experiments by prescribing surface circulation forcing in the WNP and diabatic heat forcing in the Indian subcontinent, respectively. Our results support that the ISM rainfall can be modulated by the WNP circulation anomaly. However, the ISM precipitation anomaly cannot directly drive significant changes in the circulation over the WNP region.

Moreover, it is important to mention that the outliers particularly in June, August, and September can also be explained by the WNP circulation anomaly62 (Fig. 6). The ISM precipitation response to the WNP circulation anomaly is limited in the eastern part of the Indian subcontinent in June and August compared to July and September due to a slightly different boundary of the WNP circulation anomaly. That means the precipitation response is very sensitive to the shape of WNP circulation anomaly. Indeed, the results averaged over the whole monsoon period from June to September show much smoothed-out or statistically non-significant features (not shown) compared to using only one month data. In addition, WNP circulation anomaly is the most prominent from June to August, but become weaker and exhibits a northwest-southeast tilted structure in September. The specific years grouped in positives and negatives differ between months. For example, there are only two identical years that are classified both in July and JJAS average, in each group. This implies that the WNP circulation anomaly does not maintain during whole monsoon period, and it has strong intra-seasonal variability. Therefore, we need to analyze month-to-month data to reveal underlying mechanisms, and even to predict the ISM precipitation.

Previous studies45,63 have suggested that the development and seasonal zonal progression of the WNP subtropical high is mainly controlled by the Tibetan Plateau inducing atmospheric downstream stationary Rossby waves. While the WNP circulation anomaly influencing the ISM can be partly explained by that mechanism44,63,64, other climate factors such as MJO and ENSO phase changing speed should be carefully considered to understand the variability of the WNP circulation anomaly in each month. Considering that the ISM precipitation forcing can indirectly influence surrounding circulation patterns by non-linear air-sea interaction, land-atmosphere feedback, and radiative feedback with cloud, further study is needed to understand the ISM variability in complicated earth systems.

In this study, the ISM is defined by the upper-level velocity potential index. By using this index, the WNP circulation anomaly inducing tropical easterly wind anomaly and low-level moisture convergence is clearly shown. However, if the ISM rainfall index is directly used, then a low-level negative vortex anomaly forced by the Atlantic Ocean SST anomaly is captured over the Indian subcontinent causing a weakening of the monsoonal westerly wind and drought as shown in Borah et al. (2020)65. This implies that the negative precipitation anomaly is directly linked to the weakness of the monsoonal westerly wind itself; however, the heavy precipitation and deep convection with upper-level divergence may be driven by the low-level moisture convergence caused by both monsoon wind and easterly wind anomaly to the Indian subcontinent.

The current forecasting skill for Indian precipitation remains limited due to the large uncertainty in micro-scale physical processes, including cloud microphysics, small-scale turbulence, and radiational physical processes66–70. With the improvement of physical schemes, the prediction skill for precipitation can potentially improve by approximately 20%71. For example, the potential maximum predictability correlation skill for the ISM precipitation is known as ~ 0.82; however, the highest operational state-of-the-art dynamical model current forecasting skill is ~ 0.6527,67. Present forecasting skill for the ISM precipitation is dominantly contributed by the ENSO variability, and the non-stationary ENSO-ISMR relationship is regarded as one of the fatal reasons causing false alarms67. Therefore, we hope this study provides helpful insights to improve the ISM precipitation forecasting skill.
