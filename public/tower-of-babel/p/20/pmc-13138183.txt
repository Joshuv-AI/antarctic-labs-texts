Development and evaluation of a quitline intervention commenced within an Australian smoke-free residential AOD withdrawal service
Authors: Donita Baird, Catherine Segan, Ron Borland, Amanda L. Baker, Kristen McCarter, Sacha Filia, Jane Rich, Billie Bonevski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveA novel quitline intervention (counseling plus provision of alternative forms of nicotine) was developed for people using a smoke-free, alcohol and other drug (AOD) withdrawal service. The objectives were: to code the intervention manual for behavior change techniques (BCTs) and to evaluate bespoke training, delivery of counseling provided (quality, number of BCTs) and acceptability.MethodsThe manual was coded for 29 BCTs. Training outcomes were measured by counselor self-reported confidence. Delivery of counseling was analyzed by independent rating of the quality (maximum 100, all components delivered) and number of BCTs used in all recorded calls (n = 71) for randomly selected clients (n = 30). Acceptability was measured by uptake, clients' acceptability item ratings (n = 37) and recall of counseling content (n = 38) at six weeks post-baseline.ResultsCoding of the manual identified 25/29 BCTs. Counselor (n = 10) self-reported confidence increased post-training (p = 0.03). High quality calls were delivered with a mean rating of 82.0 (SD13.16) and a mean of 8.50 (SD3.10) BCTs delivered per call. The BCTs assessment, pharmacological support and advising distraction were used frequently. Acceptability of counseling was good, with high uptake (77%; 68/88 clients contacted), counselors were rated as supportive (92%; 34/37), the majority of clients felt encouraged (87%; 32/37) and recalled counseling content for pharmacotherapy (79%; 30/38) and how to manage cravings (76%, 29/38).ConclusionA novel quitline intervention for residents of smoke-free, AOD withdrawal unit was delivered with high quality, had high acceptability, offered continuity of care pre- to post-discharge and facilitated tobacco cessation. Content of skills training to manage cravings, commenced in residence, was recalled by recipients post-discharge to sustain tobacco abstinence.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains high among people who use alcohol and other drug (AOD) services. More than 80% of people with AOD conditions also smoke tobacco and they are more likely to die from a smoking-related illness than from their other substance use [1], [2], [3]. Rates are particularly high in those utilizing residential AOD services [4], [5]. Residential withdrawal services support people to safely withdraw from AOD during a short term stay. A survey of clients of community and residential AOD services found 79% had a desire to quit smoking and 49% were receptive to receiving cessation advice, yet only 15% were offered support [6]. Smoking cessation does not impair AOD treatment and can even improve AOD outcomes [7], [8]. Government-funded, residential AOD services in Australia prohibit tobacco smoking, presenting the opportunity for tobacco treatment by providing pharmacotherapy (e.g., nicotine replacement therapy, [NRT]) and referral to quitline to help people cope with enforced abstinence and potentially try to sustain abstinence post-discharge.

Quitlines have the potential to address the limited smoking cessation treatment capacity in AOD service systems [9]. Quitlines are accessible, free smoking cessation behavioral interventions shown to be effective for the general population [10]. Quitlines are used by people with behavioral health conditions, including AOD conditions. For example, 57% of North American quitline users report a behavioral health conditions [11]. Innovation is needed because despite the over-representation of this group, lower quit rates are reported [12]. For example, one analysis found the 7-months, self-reported abstinence to be 29.5% for those with behavioral health conditions vs 34.1% for those without [12]. Furthermore, research suggests that people who smoke and use AOD services are less likely to call quitlines [13]. While proactive referrals from mental health or AOD services to quitline results in a 13-to-30-fold increase in quitline uptake relative to self-referral [14], [15], [16], there remain questions of acceptability. For example, in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of varenicline versus placebo control for people who smoked tobacco recruited from methadone treatment programs, all participants (n = 112) interested in quitting were offered a free, proactive quitline service [17]. Analysis of quitline utilization found 22% (n = 25) uptake of referral [9]. The four main reasons for declining service included: skepticism of quitline efficacy; aversion to telephone communication; competing life demands (e.g., shelter); and cell phone problems [9]. The study concluded that further efforts could be made to promote routine quitline referral in AOD service systems [9]. Description of the content of the quitline counseling was not provided [9], [17], nor were client views of the acceptability of interventions sought.

One possible way of promoting use of quitline in AOD service systems is to describe and evaluate innovative quitline interventions for this client group. Quitlines in the USA typically offer free tobacco counseling sessions, self-help materials and other resources, with some offering free or discounted NRT [18] and/or tailored counseling protocols for priority groups, such as people living with behavioral health conditions. A survey of quitlines in the USA found 77% used specific counseling protocols for callers with a behavioral health condition [11]. A detailed description of quitline counseling for people attending residential AOD services has not been identified. Without such supports, quitline counselors may not feel confident in meeting the needs of priority populations. Indeed, a qualitative study of nurses working on quitline found lack of training and resources to be a barrier to effectively support clients with mental health conditions [19]. Despite training and subsequent improvement in self-reported knowledge and self-efficacy, after implementing the intervention, nurses were still unsure of their capacity to assist individuals with mental illness to quit smoking [19]. This study points to the importance of exploring training outcomes and use of resources in conjunction with service delivery. Sharing the development of a novel quitline protocol to support people who smoke tobacco and use smoke-free, residential AOD services and the training methods and training outcomes, will be of benefit. Independent ratings and client ratings of the quality of the counseling delivered may increase confidence in and contribute to the knowledge base of how tailored quitlines can meet the needs of people utilizing residential AOD services.

Behavior change techniques (BCTs) are the smallest, identifiable components of an intervention that, in themselves, have the potential to change behavior [20]. BCTs are useful for identifying, characterizing and comparing the content of behavioral interventions [21]. BCTs could be used to quantify how services are tailored (e.g., counseling protocols) and implemented (e.g., what was delivered). Independent rating of content is important as client and health practitioner recall of sessions vary [22]. For tobacco smoking cessation in the general population, some BCTs, such as advice on medication and strengthening ex-smoker identity, are identified as effective [23]. Evidence for BCTs for smoking cessation following a stay in a smoke-free, AOD service setting is emerging and 12 BCTs (e.g. pharmacological support, goal setting, action planning) have been identified as promising [24].

A qualitative study showed AOD service clients highly valued both combination NRT (cNRT) or Nicotine Vaping Products (NVP) provided with behavioral interventions from quitline [25]. To date, an examination of BCTs used by quitline, according to pharmacotherapy used (NVP or cNRT), was not found. This is important because NVPs are becoming increasingly popular, moreover there is some evidence that NRT is not well tolerated by people with AOD and this may be associated with low adherence rates [26]. Knowing if counselors use different strategies, depending on type of pharmacotherapy used, could inform counseling protocols.

The current study was a secondary analysis of a pragmatic two-arm pilot RCT of tobacco cessation intervention for people leaving a smoke-free, residential AOD withdrawal service [27]. Information about the RCT protocol [28] and outcomes [27] are available elsewhere. Briefly, all clients were offered quitline counseling, commenced during admission and, on discharge, were given either a 12-week supply of cNRT or a NVP. In line with our goal to explore the potential of quitlines for this client group, we focused on the quitline counseling component, but also note whether the client used cNRT or NVP. We aimed to: 1) describe the development of a quitline counseling protocol tailored for people attending a smoke-free residential AOD withdrawal service and assess the BCTs present in the intervention manual provided; 2) evaluate counselor training outcomes; and 3) evaluate delivery of counseling: quality and number of BCTs used. We considered BCT use overall and, according to the pharmacotherapy provided (NVP or cNRT); and [4] evaluate acceptability of quitline intervention using client uptake of quitline counseling, client ratings of the quality of the counseling provided and recall of counseling content.

Material and methods

The current paper is derived from data collected during the QuitNic pilot RCT (ANZCTR number ACTRN12617000849392) conducted between 2017 and 2019, which recruited 100 clients who smoked tobacco from a smoke-free, 12-bed, 7-day residential AOD withdrawal service in Melbourne, Australia [24]. Client retention for the RCT was 63% at 6-weeks post-baseline [25]. Development of QuitNic quitline counseling protocol

QuitNic counseling manual

Prior to the study commencing, authors DB (clinical psychologist) and CS (behavioral scientist) wrote the QuitNic quitline counseling manual (detailed below), with guidance from ALB (clinical psychologist). The manual was designed to include information and counseling strategies for the specific context of people using AOD and residing in a smoke-free AOD withdrawal service.

Theoretical framework

Quitline Victoria supports people to achieve the three tasks of quitting: 1) to make a quit attempt; 2) to learn to effectively deal with cravings and other withdrawal symptoms without relapsing; and 3) to learn to enjoy and value a smoke-free lifestyle, including adopting a new self-image as a non-smoker or proud ex-smoker [29]. Given enforced tobacco abstinence during residential AOD withdrawal, key tasks are: 1) to choose whether to remain abstinent from smoking; and 2) to develop skills to do so in their own environment. The intervention goals were to: 1) instill optimism and confidence regarding addressing tobacco smoking; 2) encourage clients to use cNRT or NVP, instead of smoking once discharged; and 3) try to reduce psychological dependence on cigarettes by finding replacements for the functions of smoking and to build confidence to utilize these strategies to stop or reduce their smoking on discharge. Resumption of smoking frequently occurs on discharge from residential AOD services [24]. Thus, quitline counseling was initiated during their residential stay. Initial calls discussed the experience of being smoke-free and elicited thoughts about abstinence post-discharge. A key task was explaining how quitline could support them to reach their goals (e.g., stop or reduce smoking). On discharge from residential withdrawal, a standard relapse-sensitive scheduling of quitline calls was recommended [30]; however, flexibility was allowed to meet the needs of individual clients. For example, offering daily calls for the first 5–7 days post-discharge for clients trying to remain abstinent, to interrupt a return to regular smoking.

The QuitNic counseling manual included examples of tailored psychoeducation, such as quitting smoking would not harm and may assist AOD cessation attempts. It also equipped counselors to continue to offer smoking cessation support, in the context of AOD relapse, by assessing how smoking is related to AOD use and current thoughts/feelings about smoking and strategies to support harm minimization. If AOD specific support was needed, a referral for the state AOD counseling service was offered to compliment the quitline counseling. The QuitNic intervention manual was retrospectively coded by DB and SF, using a checklist of 29 BCTs (see Supplementary Table 1 for definitions) identified as relevant to the Victorian Quitline [31].

QuitNic counselor training

All 12 counselors (10 female and two male) employed at Quitline Victoria attended a one-day training session and were involved in delivery of the intervention. Prerequisites for employment as a counselor included, at minimum, a Certificate IV in counseling (or equivalent) and a minimum of three years' experience in a counseling or health role. In addition, as part of their employment, counselors completed extensive training in smoking cessation and received monthly group and individual supervision. Routine, individual supervision consisted of a structured call-reflection process where the quality of delivery of key components of counseling calls was rated for two randomly selected, routinely recorded (with client and counselor consent) calls per month by a psychologist. Verbal feedback was given on areas delivered well and areas for improvement. Group supervision was conducted by an external provider.

The QuitNic training session (n = 12) included an overview (i.e. research questions, measures, timeframe and materials) of the research project. The Nurse Unit Manager of the residential AOD service presented the therapeutic milieu so counselors could understand the context and reinforce key messages/strategies used by AOD counselors. A didactic session on implementing tobacco cessation for people with AOD was presented by ALB, outlining the scope of the issue and research illustrating the benefits of stopping smoking. Practical content included a presentation and demonstration of the NVP product by a company representative, with products provided for handling. At the time, encouraging use of NVP was beyond the usual scope of practice for Victorian Quitline counselors and an ensuing group discussion focused on ensuring that counselors were comfortable with this, given the research context. Technical support for the NVP was provided by researchers in product review calls. DB delivered a practice-based session on the counseling protocol, including revision of standard practice, such as therapeutic assessment, motivational interviewing, persuasive education, monitoring (nicotine withdrawal, mental health symptoms and medication side effects) [32] and additional cognitive and behavioral techniques.

Counselors were provided resources to support their learning, including a tip sheet on substances and their interaction with nicotine [33]. To aid understanding of the client withdrawal experience, for each substance a description was given, including common names, prevalence and methods of use, acute effects, symptoms of regular excessive use and during withdrawal and interaction with nicotine. Another handout had examples of reframing cognitions. Counselors also engaged in role plays and were given feedback (ALB, CS, DB).

Evaluation of the QuitNic training involved counselors anonymously completing pre- and post- training questionnaires. The pretraining questionnaire presented counselors with 11 facts and asked them to rate: ‘did’ or ‘did not know’. At pre- and post-training, counselors were asked to self-rate their knowledge and confidence about providing smoking cessation advice and support to people with AOD use. Knowledge was measured using a 4-point scale ranging from low to very high, and self-confidence was rated on a 10-point scale from 1 (not at all confident) to 10 (extremely confident).

Delivery of quitline counseling

Of the 85 QuitNic participants (see Table 1) who used quitline, 30 were randomly selected and grouped according to the number of completed counseling calls (low = single call, medium = up to three calls, high = four or more calls). Calls were analyzed for delivery (quality and content) by an independent rater (SF) and 10% were dually rated (DB). There was 100% agreement between raters for all calls.

Call quality

Call quality was evaluated using the Victorian Quitline's routine call-reflection process where overall counseling skills (e.g., establishing rapport) and delivery of key components of the call flow are rated. The key components were: introduction, assessment, psychoeducation, skills building, self-efficacy and plan. For each component, a rating of either yes, partial, no or not applicable (N/A) could be given. A rating of yes was given if the counselor comprehensively delivered the component. A rating of partial was given if the counselor omitted elements of the component. A rating of no was given if there was no attempt to cover key elements for that component. A rating of N/A was used when the component was not relevant for the client. An algorithm calculated a total score for each call, ranging from 0 to 100% (all components delivered satisfactorily).

Call content

The content of counseling calls was rated for the 29 BCTs relevant to Victorian Quitline counseling, using a coding sheet with labels and definitions (see Supplementary Table 1), the absence or presence of the BCT (0 = not used, 1 = used), the time stamp and rated certainty of BCT coding (1 = least certain to 3 = most certain). The BCT pharmacotherapy refers to counselors encouraging use of the cNRT or NVP provided. In addition, raters recorded any additional BCTs observed that were outside the expectation of the QuitNic intervention.

Acceptability of the quitline counseling intervention

Acceptability (see Table 1) was measured by the number of referrals received by quitline from the QuitNic researcher (maximum 100 clients) and uptake of quitline counseling by clients, including whether the first call was initiated during the stay (to help clients reflect on being smoke free and consider continued abstinence post-discharge) and the number of completed counseling calls.

At the 6-week telephone follow up (chosen for its proximity to the quitline intervention), QuitNic researchers asked clients to rate how true nine statements, indicators of a supportive counseling experience were, on a 4-point scale (completely, mostly, somewhat or not at all). Clients were also asked if, during their calls with quitline, they talked about ten items related to potential counseling content by selecting yes/no. These topics were selected to be consistent with the counseling protocol and training provided.

Evaluation of training

All 12 counselors attended the training, but premeasures were missing for two, resulting in complete evaluations (pre- and post-measures) for ten counselors. A mean of nine facts (range 6–11) out of 11 were endorsed as known on the pretraining questionnaire. In pretraining, ‘moderate’ was the most common self-rating for knowledge (n = 6), and post-ratings increased to ‘high’ (n = 7) and ‘very high’ (n = 2). Training also improved ratings of self-confidence to provide smoking cessation advice and support to people with AOD use. The mean rating for self-confidence pretraining was 6.4 and 8.4 post-training (n = 10, p = 0.03).

Evaluation of counseling delivery

Clients were grouped according to number of calls: low (1 call), medium (up to three calls) and high (four or more calls). As there were no significant differences in quality of calls between these groups (low vs med p = 0.54; low vs high p = 0.60; high vs med p = 0.89), results below are presented according to initial and subsequent calls, and pharmacotherapy condition.

Quality of counseling

Analysis of the quality rating by the independent rater showed high quality calls with a mean rating of 83.81 (SD = 13.86) per call overall; 82.0 (SD = 13.16) for the initial call and 84.91 (SD = 14.38) for subsequent calls. High quality calls were observed regardless of type of pharmacotherapy provided, calls for clients with NVP had a mean rating of 82.5 (SD = 13.46; range 57–100) and calls for clients with cNRT had a mean rating of 84.6 (SD = 14.38, range 50–100) (non-significant).

Content of quitline counseling

BCTs observed by the ratings of the calls were consistent with expectations based on the manual. The average number of BCTs used per call overall was 8.50 (SD = 3.10), 9.5 (SD = 2.82) for the initial call and 7.78 (SD = 3.14) for subsequent calls (non-significant). Table 3 presents BCT use according to call type (initial or subsequent) and pharmacotherapy provided (cNRT, NVP) and shows a similar pattern of BCT use. The most frequently used BCTs related to psychoeducation were risks/benefits and change process. The most frequently used BCTs related to skills building were use of distractions, managing environmental cues and stress management. The BCTs rarely used were the three tasks of quitting and commitment to goals, and stories was not used.

Acceptability

Quitline received referrals for 88% (88/100) of clients from the residential AOD service and successfully contacted 77% (68/88) of these clients. Most clients (65%;44/68), received their first call while in residence.

At 6-week research follow up, 62% (37/60) of respondents reported speaking with quitline with no difference by pharmacotherapy provided (cNRT vs NVP). As the counseling protocol was the same for both pharmacotherapy conditions, results are reported overall. Data on measures examining recall of content of quitline calls were obtained from 38 clients and missing from 22 clients who were not asked these questions. For questions related to quality of quitline counseling, data were obtained from 37 clients and missing from 23 clients who were not asked these questions. There were no statistically significant differences on baseline characteristics, smoking behaviors and smoking outcomes between those who participated in the follow up and those with no follow-up data [27].

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

A novel tailored quitline intervention successfully engaged residents of a smoke-free AOD service and provided continuity of care for tobacco cessation post-discharge. Bespoke training equipped counselors to provide tobacco cessation counseling in this context. The counseling provided was highly acceptable and perceived to be useful by most clients. With such supports, recipients felt understood, supported and encouraged by quitline. Together, results showed this innovative approach can provide acceptable smoking cessation intervention for this high-need, but underserviced population.

Sharing an analysis of the novel tailored quitline counseling protocol for this population could be of benefit to other quitlines and AOD services. Other quitlines have found that while self-confidence increased following training, upon implementation, barriers still remained [19]. Here we have demonstrated how a theory-based counseling protocol, coupled with training and supervision, did support the implementation of a tailored service. Skepticism of quitline efficacy was found to be a barrier to accepting the referral in other studies [2], so being able to inform potential clients of such services about the positive evaluations by users, may facilitate uptake and productive use of quitlines.

This study also has practical implications for training, supervision and professional development for quitline counselors. The counselors reported that training increased confidence and high quality calls were delivered. The BCTs observed were consistent with the training and manual provided. Although coding of the manual revealed that four of the 29 BCTs theorized for smoking cessation were missing in the manual, three of the four were used. Mental rehearsal was used once, while mindfulness and weight management were used in 6% and 8% of calls, respectively. This shows counselors were able to tailor the psychoeducation and skills building, as encouraged in the protocol. Interestingly, despite the emphasis during the training on the inter-relation between smoking, AOD and mental health, only around half of clients recalled this content. Similarly, although refusal skills and enlisting social supports was covered in the training, less than half of clients recalled session content related to coping with people in their social network who smoke. This is consistent with the observation that the BCTs ‘social support’ and ‘role models’ were used in up to 33% of calls. It is possible this content was not adequately covered in the training session. Brady and colleagues recommended that quitlines establish quality improvement measures to ensure that counselors are trained to provide high quality BCTs [34]. This study indicates how supervision can incorporate monitoring of BCTs to support quality. Supervisors can provide feedback on quality of delivery and encourage counselor use of BCTs to support skills building (e.g., ‘social support’, ‘role models’) and self-efficacy.

A limitation of this study is the sample size. The small sample limits our analysis of the clients' recollection of sessions and the BCT analysis. However, there is concordance between clients' recollections and frequency of use of BCTs. Being one of the first studies to pilot cNRT and NVPs in this setting, this evaluation brings great insights into acceptability of intervention and BCTs in practice.

Innovation

A key innovation was offering the tailored quitline intervention to all residents in a smoke-free AOD withdrawal unit who smoked tobacco. Other studies offered a quitline referral to participants interested in quitting and found that while 62% accepted the quitline referral, only 22% used quitline [13], [19]. In our QuitNic study, all clients were offered quitline referral, regardless of readiness, and 88% accepted the referral and 77% used quitline. Although a direct comparison of these studies cannot be made, a practical implication is offering quitline and options for nicotine replacement to all, regardless of their readiness to change, could encourage uptake.

In this study, quitline was offered in conjunction with attractive options for nicotine replacement on discharge [27]. This may have helped to initiate and sustain contact with quitline. In contexts where alternate forms of nicotine are not provided on discharge, it may be more difficult for quitline to engage clients. Efforts should be made to make provision of alternate forms of nicotine on discharge routine practice. Feedback from clients using AOD services is that cost limits cNRT use and access to NVPs is complicated in Australia [25]. Although it was an extension of counselors' usual scope of practice to encourage use of NVP, counselors were able to do this well, as indicated by both client feedback and external ratings of the quality of the calls. The BCTs used by quitline followed the same pattern, whether the person was in the cNRT or NVP condition. Clients recalled being invited to share thoughts about using products (cNRT or NVP) as an alternative to smoking and to reflect on their experiences on their use.

The tailored quitline protocol which considered the context of residential withdrawal enabled counselors to provide continuity of care pre- to post discharge. Skills training to manage cravings was commenced while in residence and the content recalled by recipients to encourage continued abstinence from smoking tobacco. This is important, as a perceived barrier to quitline was competing life demands [13]. The ability to recall these strategies at 6-weeks' follow up shows, despite competing demands, strategies were effectively communicated and retained.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates how quitlines can aid tobacco cessation in smoke-free AOD residential withdrawal. Development of tailored protocol and subsequent training enabled high quality intervention delivery. The tailored quitline had high client acceptability and facilitated development of skills to manage cravings. Counseling content was recalled by recipients post-discharge. Quitline, with counseling protocols tailored to the needs of AOD service users and provision of pharmacotherapy, can engage with people during a smoke-free residential stay and offer continuity of care pre-to post-discharge.
