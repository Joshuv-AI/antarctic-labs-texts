Impact of habitat modifications on flea (Siphonaptera) infestation dynamics of non-volant small mammals across wildlife-human interfaces in Tanzania
Authors: Venance T Msoffe, Festo K Mwombeki, Claus A Thomas, Charles M Lyimo, Abdul S Katakweba, Gerald Misinzo, Ladslaus L Mnyone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChanges in natural habitats affect the population density of small mammals, which in turn influences ectoparasite populations. This study explores how human-induced habitat modifications impact flea infestations of non-volant small mammals considering seasonal and agroecological variations. A cross-sectional study focused on two wildlife–human interfaces in the Iringa and Morogoro regions, Tanzania. Small mammals were captured using Sherman®, Havahart, and wire-cage live traps. Fleas were extracted from the animals' fur with a stiff brush and preserved in 70% alcohol. Flea identification was performed morphologically by using standard guidelines and confirmed by sequencing the mtDNA segment of the cox2 gene. Statistical analyses were performed using Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) and Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) to examine variations in flea infestations across different host species, habitat types, seasons, and agroecological zones. Species diversity of small mammals decreased with increasing habitat modification (bush > farm > peridomestic). The most common rat fleas (Xenopsylla cheopis, Xenopsylla brasiliensis), together with chicken flea (Echidnophaga gallinacea) and cat flea (Ctenocephalides felis), were identified. Flea infestation loads on small mammals increased along the habitat modification gradient (bush < farm < peridomestic). The highest prevalence and mean intensity of infestation were observed in Aethomys spp. at 38.5% (95% CI: 33.7- 43.7) and 2.95 (95% CI: 2.18 - 3.72, SE: 0.366), respectively. Despite Mastomys natalensis being the most abundant, they had a lower prevalence of flea infestation. Flea infestations were significantly higher during the rainy season than the dry season (p-value = 0.003) and more prevalent in peridomestic habitats than others (p-value = 0.014). Thus, habitat modification directly impacts flea infestation loads and small mammal species’ diversity, potentially implicating zoonotic disease transmission. This highlights the urgent need for integrated wildlife conservation and public health management approaches in Tanzania.Keywords: Fleas, cox2 sequencing, Non-volant small mammals, Wildlife-human interface, Zoonosis

Introduction

Anthropogenic activities such as crop cultivation, livestock keeping, and settlement expansion near natural ecosystems profoundly alter habitat structure and biodiversity. These processes modify ecosystem composition and influence the distribution and abundance of wildlife species, including small mammals, particularly rodents and shrews. Habitat modification also affects the population dynamics of their associated ectoparasites through the expansion of ecotones and wildlife-human interfaces (Cevidanes et al., 2016; Wale et al., 2023). Increased interactions among wildlife, humans, and domestic animals in these transitional zones create opportunities for pathogen spillover, parasite exchange, and genetic diversification (McLennan and Plumptre, 2012; Namusisi et al., 2021; Morand, 2018). Several zoonotic diseases, including plague, typhus, rickettsiosis, bartonellosis, and leptospirosis, have been linked to ecological disturbances occurring at such interfaces (Bird and Mazet, 2018; Eisen et al., 2009; Tadin et al., 2016; Thomas et al., 2020; Ziwa et al., 2013).

In this study, “non-volant small mammals” refers to non-flying mammals weighing less than 1 kg, including rodents and shrews (Ardente et al., 2016; Munian et al., 2020). These species play important ecological roles but are particularly sensitive to land-use change, habitat fragmentation, and climate variability (Viteri and Hadly, 2022). Agricultural intensification and habitat homogenization may alter host community composition and influence the distribution and aggregation patterns of ectoparasites such as fleas (Cevidanes et al., 2016; Shilereyo et al., 2022). Disturbances including vegetation clearing, bush burning, and soil disruption may further modify microhabitats critical for both small mammals and their associated parasites (Nziza et al., 2019).

Fleas (Order: Siphonaptera) are obligate hematophagous ectoparasites of veterinary and public health importance (Bitam et al., 2010). They act as vectors and reservoirs of several pathogens, including Yersinia pestis, Rickettsia typhi, Rickettsia felis, and Bartonella spp., all of which have been reported in Tanzania (Babyesiza et al., 2023; Billeter et al., 2014; Mhamphi et al., 2024; Theonest et al., 2019; Thomas et al., 2020). Variation in flea infestation among small mammals may be influenced by habitat characteristics, seasonality, host species identity, body mass, sex, and ecological conditions (Cevidanes et al., 2016; Laudisoit et al., 2007; Viteri and Hadly, 2022). Studies have suggested that infestation levels may differ between natural and human-modified habitats, although patterns are often context-dependent (Babyesiza et al., 2023; Krasnov et al., 2006; Shilereyo et al., 2022; Wale et al., 2023).

Despite increasing anthropogenic pressure along wildlife-human interfaces in Tanzania, comprehensive data on how habitat modification influences flea infestation patterns in non-volant small mammals remain limited. Improved understanding of these ecological relationships is essential for assessing zoonotic transmission risks and informing integrated wildlife and public health management strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the diversity of non-volant small mammals and their associated fleas across habitat types representing different levels of anthropogenic modification in wildlife-human interfaces of Iringa and Morogoro, Tanzania. Specifically, we examined variations in flea infestation prevalence and intensity in relation to habitat type, seasonality, agroecological zone, and host demographic characteristics using both morphological and molecular approaches.

Materials and methods

Study area and design

A cross-sectional study design was conducted in the Iringa and Morogoro regions, which are located in two different agro-ecological zones in Tanzania, the southern highland and the eastern agroecological zones, respectively (Suleiman, 2018), from June 2021 to December 2022. The study districts, wards, and villages were purposively selected based on their proximity to national parks or reserved areas, which characterizes greater interaction between wildlife, humans, and domestic animals (Amuguni et al., 2017; Southern Tanzania Elephant Program, 2016). Three villages from three different wards were selected from the Iringa district. The villages were Kitisi, Malizanga, and Mlenge in the Idodi, Mlowa, and Mlenge wards, respectively, located around the wildlife-human interface of Ruaha National Park. Meanwhile, for Morogoro, three villages were selected from two different wards, Mbwade and Dutumi from the Bwakila Chini ward and Kisaki Kituoni from the Kisaki ward, which are located around the wildlife-human interface of the Nyerere national parks in Morogoro district (Fig. 1). The elevation ranges from 900 to 955 M and 150 -175 M above sea level, and the local climate is variable, with mean monthly temperature ranges from 15 °C to 35 °C and 18 °C to 28 °C for Iringa and Morogoro, respectively (Kimaro and Msanya, 2001; Wapalila, 2008).

The map of Iringa and Morogoro districts in Tanzania demonstrates wards and villages where field-sample collections were conducted.

Iringa district experiences a unimodal rainfall pattern (with a wet season from December to April) characterized by a semi-arid climate with mean annual rainfall ranging from 500 to 1000 mm (Kiwango et al., 2018). Morogoro district experiences bimodal rainfall patterns characterized by a sub-humid tropical climate with short rains from mid-September to late December and long rains between March and May, with a mean annual rainfall of 900 to 2600 mm (Kimaro and Msanya, 2001).

According to the Tanzania population and housing census of the year 2022, the total population estimates for the Iringa and Morogoro districts were 315,354 and 387,736 individuals, respectively (National Bureau of Statistics, 2022). Basically, people within the selected study sites are involved in small-scale agriculture activities, including various crop cultivation and livestock keeping proximal to national parks or reserved areas. Nevertheless, occasionally, some of them are engaged in illegal grazing of livestock in the national park and reserved areas and poaching wild animals for consumption and illegal business (Mrosso et al., 2022). These economic activities around wildlife-human interfaces keep humans and domestic animals at a higher risk of contracting zoonotic infections.

Trapping of non-volant small mammals

The field-sample collection was carried out in 18 collection sites (each village was divided into three existing habitat sites characterized as bushland (relatively natural, pristine, undisturbed, reserved area), farmland (crop-cultivated land), and peridomestic (around human settlements) sites with at least a distance of 1 km apart within the single village. In each collection site, 20 to 30 live traps were laid alternately on the ground (depending on the size of an area) along with two established transect lines with at least 200 m distance from one another for bushland and farmland sites and around living houses for peridomestic sites (Sponchiado et al., 2015). A combination of live traps was used for capturing small mammals for each collection site in a ratio of at least 6:3:1 for Sherman® live traps (standard medium-size LFA: 7.6 × 8.9 × 23 cm), wire-cage traps, and Havahart traps, respectively. The traps were baited with a mixture of maize flour and peanut butter, and maize grain, ripened banana, roasted sardines, and meat were added to Havahart traps. The traps were set for catching small mammals overnight and checked early during the morning of the next day (Mgode et al., 2014).

Four collection missions, including two in dry seasons (between June and November) and two in rainy seasons (between January and May), of three to five consecutive trapping nights were conducted (Mulungu et al., 2008). At least 270 trap nights of sampling efforts per collection site made 4980 trap nights of sampling efforts considering all collection sites.

After trapping, the preliminary identification of age, sex, and species of captured rodents and shrews was conducted in the field. Identification was based on physical appearance and morphometric measurements. Sexes were identified based on three morphological features: urogenital distance, nipples, and testicles. The captured mammals’ weight, tail length, and other body measurements were taken to aid in species identification. Captured animals were identified at the species level using published keys of taxonomic nomenclature (Kingdon, 2015; Wilson and Reeder, 2005).

Collection and preservation of fleas from captured small mammals

Fleas together with other ectoparasites, vis. mites, ticks, and lice, were removed gently from the small mammals using a stiff brush and collected in a clean aluminum basin (Sponchiado et al., 2015). Each sample of collected arthropods was preliminary identified using external features (including body morphology and segments, pair and arrangement of appendages, and color) (Gary and Lance, 2019). Collected groups of ectoparasites were counted and separately preserved in Eppendorf tubes with 70% ethanol labeled according to the date and site of collection and the identity of the host animal (Thomas et al., 2020). The collected samples from the field were transferred to the Parasitology Laboratory at the Department of Veterinary Microbiology, Parasitology and Biotechnology of Sokoine University of Agriculture, Morogoro, Tanzania for further identification and molecular analysis.

Morphological identification of fleas’ genus groups

Taxonomic genus identification of fleas from captured animals was performed based on morphological characteristics aided by available taxonomic key references (Gary and Lance, 2019; Mathison and Pritt, 2014). Partial observation was performed with a stereo Microscope after the fleas were gently washed in 1 × phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), temporally mounted in glycerol, and placed on microscopic slides with a coverslip under 4 × objective magnification. Available procedures were employed to clear a few selected flea specimens for detailed morphological observation (Campbell et al., 2018; Gebrezgiher et al., 2023). Briefly, in order to allow distinct visibility of external and internal structures, the specimens were boiled for 10 min in 10% KOH solution and then neutralized with 10% acetic acid for 30 min. The specimens were then allowed to dehydrate for 1 h in a series of 70%, 80%, and 100% ethanol each and transferred to clove oil overnight to rehydrate before mounting the specimens. The process of mounting clear flea specimens in microscope slides was performed using Dibutyl phthalate polystyrene–xylene (DPX) (Campbell et al., 2018).

Sample preparation and DNA extraction

The representative individuals of flea specimens were selected based on morphologically identified similar genera, small mammal species, the sex of the host they were removed from, the identity of the collection site, and season of collection (dry or rainy season). Each individual of flea samples was washed with 1 × PBS, air-dried for 5 min on tissue paper, and separately grinded using a sterile motor and pestle. They were then re-suspended in 100 μL of 1 × PBS in 1.5 mL microfuge tubes and manually homogenized with a sterile micro-pipette tip.

The whole DNA from fleas was extracted using a salt extraction protocol published by Musapa et al. (2013), with some modifications. Briefly, 100 μL of autoclaved 0.5% Tween-20 in 1 × PBS solution was added to the sample homogenate and mixed through gentle momentary vortexing. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 20 min and then centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 2 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Pellets were re-suspended in 100 μL 1 × PBS and centrifuged again at 16,000 rpm for 2 min. The supernatant was then discarded. Through gentle vortexing (5 s), the remaining pellets were re-suspended in a 2:1 sterile deionized water (PCR water) to 20% w/v Chelex-100 resin (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) suspension. The sample suspensions were then boiled at 98 °C in a water bath on a floating rack for 10 min. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was transferred into a prelabeled 1.5 mL microfuge tube.

The mixture was again centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant (the extracted DNA template) was transferred into another labeled 1.5 mL Eppendorf vial and stored at −20 °C until further use. Assessments of DNA concentration and purity were performed by measuring the absorbance at 260 and 280 Abs using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (BIOCHROM Ltd., Cambridge, UK).

PCR assay and DNA sequencing

Identification of fleas’ species was performed through conventional polymerase chain reaction (cPCR) amplification of 727 bp of a mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) fragment of cytochrome c oxidase subunit II gene (cox2) and direct sequencing using primers specific for fleas:

COII-F-leu (5′TCTAATATGGCAGATTAGTGC3′)

COII-R-lys (5′GAGACCAGTACTTGCTTTCAGTCATC3′) (Whiting, 2002).

PCR amplifications included AccuPower® PCR PreMix from Bioneer (Bioneer Corporation, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). A total of 20 μL of the reaction mixture was used for PCR, consisting of 2 μL of template DNA, 1 μL each for forward and reverse primer, and 16 μL of nuclease-free water in a micro-tube containing AccuPower® PCR PreMix concentrate. Cycling conditions were set at initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 42 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. There was a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Gel electrophoresis in 1.5% agarose gel stained with 4 μL of gel red stain was used to check the amplification of specific PCR products. A 100 bp ladder was used for band size visualization. The band size of 727 bp implies that the sample is positive for the cox2 gene of fleas species. PCR amplicons with high quality were sent to Macrogen Europe (Meibergdree 57, 1105 BA, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) for Sanger sequencing using the same PCR amplification primers.

Sequence analysis and genotyping

The raw sequence data were cleaned, edited, and assembled using Geneious Prime (version 2023.1.2) software to obtain consensus sequences for the amplified region of the cox2 gene. Obtained consensus sequences were aligned in the ClustalW program in MEGA 11 software. The aligned sequences were then submitted for Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) in the GenBank National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database to determine fleas' genotypes and species identification. Multiple sequence alignment was performed through query sequences, and we selected DNA sequences with the highest similarity from the NCBI GenBank using ClustalW. Inference of the flea's evolutionary history was made through the Maximum Likelihood method and the Tamura-Nei model (Tamura and Nei, 1993). According to the Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), the best substitution model was GTR + G + I, with an AICc value of 4994.170. A phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA 11 with the robustness of the 1000 bootstraps method (Tamura et al., 2021) to calculate branch support.

Data analysis

The Shannon Weiner index was used to analyze the diversity index of non-volant small mammals in the three existing habitats of natural, relatively undisturbed bush, cultivated farms, and peridomestic areas. The mean intensity and prevalence of flea infestation per individual small mammal host were calculated and presented as suggested by Bush et al. (1997). Considering the limitation in the collected sample size for some small mammal species, only species with at least 20 collected sample replicates were considered for statistical analysis. Thus, only four species (Aethomys spp., Mastomys natalensis, Arvicanthis niloticus, and Tatera vicinus) satisfied the condition and were considered during statistical analysis. Based on the same reason of the limited single-species collected sample size, statistical analyses were conducted per flea-infesting group instead of a one-by-one flea species analysis. The data on abundance and infestation intensity were tested for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test and verified for normality through the Shapiro–Wilk test. The assumptions for the parametric test were not met even after data were log (-Y)- and arcsine [square root (Y/100)]-transformed to improve the homogeneity of variance. Hence, the assumption of non-parametric tests was opted for (Mishra et al., 2019). For the validation of the aggregated distribution of fleas in their small mammals' host population, we tested the goodness of fit with “MASS” and “vcd” packages in R. Maximum Likelihood for goodness of fit to the negative binomial distribution, which was tested to analyze the frequency distribution of fleas. We tested the difference in variation of fleas' infestation load between seasonality, habitat sites, and agroecological zones using the Kruskal–Wallis test. According to Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) value, the zero inflated negative binomial (ZINB) regression model under “pscl” the package was found to be the best-fit model to evaluate the influence of predictor variables compared to Poisson and Negative binomial models. This was due to the nature of the dataset, including the fact that the count data were over-dispersed (variance was relatively larger than the mean) and there were excessive zeros in the dataset (Lee et al., 2023). The model-dependent variables (predictor) considered were small mammal species, sex, weight, habitat sites, and agroecological zone. In contrast, the independent (response) variable was the intensity of fleas' infection to infer variation in the mean intensity of infestation per individual small mammal host.

Also, the generalized linear model (GLM, binomial family) under the “glm2” package in R was the best-fit model to assess variations in small mammals' fleas’ infestation status (presence/absence) as influenced by predictor variables (habitat sites, agroecological zones, weight, and sex) to infer the variation in the prevalence of infestation. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software using specified R packages implemented in version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2021). Statistically significant difference was assumed at the 5% level.

Results

Demographic characteristics of non-volant small mammals

A total of 376 non-volant small mammals representing 11 species (9 rodents and 2 shrews) were captured across the wildlife-human interfaces of Iringa and Morogoro. Mastomys natalensis was the most abundant species, followed by Aethomys spp. and Tatera vicinus (Table 1). Collected small mammals were captured from cultivated farms, peridomestic areas, and from bush areas with a total of 335, 330, and 331 trap nights of efforts, respectively.

Overall flea infestation patterns

Overall, 36% of captured small mammals were infested with fleas with an overall prevalence of 38.5% (95% CI: [33.7–43.7], p-value: 0.00002) and an overall infestation load with a mean intensity of 2.75 (95% CI [2.49–3.0], SE = 0.129) per individual small mammal host. The mean infestation intensity and prevalence for the four representative species are summarized in Table 2. Aethomys spp. exhibited the highest prevalence and mean infestation intensity, whereas Mastomys natalensis, despite being the most abundant species, showed comparatively lower infestation prevalence.

Variation of flea infestation by habitat, agroecological zone, and season

Flea infestation varied across habitat types (Fig. 4). Peridomestic sites showed the highest infestation levels (0.00429 = [174 fleas collected/(123 small mammals captured/330 trap nights of efforts) = relative abundance), followed by farmland sites (0.00271 = 186/205/335), while bushland habitats exhibited the lowest infestation loads (0.00227 = 36/48/331). The incidence rate of infestation was higher in modified habitats compared to bushland. It was 1.82 times in peridomestic habitats as compared with bushland habitats. Likewise, the incident rate was 1.41 times in farmland habitats as compared with bushland habitats. Therefore, human disturbances in the modification of habitat sites (peridomestic and farmland sites) influence more flea infestation loads per individual small mammal host compared to relatively pristine, natural bush habitat sites.

Mean flea infestation intensity across habitat types and seasons in two agroecological zones.

Across agroecological zones, small mammals from Iringa exhibited higher infestation prevalence of 43.83 (95% CI: 37.39–50.43) and relative intensity (0.0039 = 293/235/320) than those from Morogoro with infestation prevalence of 24.82 (95% CI: 17.94–32.79) and relative intensity of (0.0021 = 103/141/343). This difference was statistically significant (Kruskal–Wallis test, H = 8.9369, p = 0.0028).

Seasonal variation of flea infestations was also evident. Both prevalence and mean infestation intensity were higher during the rainy season compared to the dry season. This pattern was generally consistent across habitat types and host species, with minor exceptions (Fig. 5A and B).

Factors associated with flea infestation

Model comparison indicated that the Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) model best explained variation in infestation intensity (Appendix A; Table A1). According to the ZINB model (Table 3), infestation intensity was significantly associated with habitat type, host species, sex, and body mass. Peridomestic habitat sites were significantly more likely to influence flea infestation load than bushland and farmland habitats. The intensity of flea infestation significantly increased with the body mass of small mammals and was more likely influenced by adult males than females. Mastomys natalensis had a higher probability of being infested by more fleas than other small mammal species. The zero-inflation component indicated that the probability of not being infested by flea was significantly increased with a decrease in the mass of small mammals, and it was increased in Morogoro compared to Iringa's agroecological zone (Table 3).

Morphological and molecular identification of fleas

A total of 396 fleas belonging to three genera (Xenopsylla, Ctenocephalides, and Echidnophaga) were identified (Table 5). Xenopsylla spp. were the predominant genus, accounting for the majority of collected specimens. Molecular analysis of the cox2 gene confirmed the presence of four flea species: Xenopsylla cheopis, Xenopsylla brasiliensis, Echidnophaga gallinacea, and Ctenocephalides felis. Sequence data were deposited in GenBank with the following NCBI accession numbers: Oriental rat flea X. cheopis (PP919957, PP919958, and PP928403), X. brasiliensis (PP919959 and PP919960), chicken flea E. gallinacea (PP919961), and cat flea C. felis (PP928404).

Phylogenetic characterization of identified flea species

Evaluation of nucleotide sequence identity revealed that all genotypes of flea species from Tanzania were clustered separately into four clusters (Fig. 6). The X. cheopis samples from Iringa (PP919957 IRKT TANZANIA and PP919958 IRKT TANZANIA) were 100% similar to each other, and they were 99.6% similar to the Morogoro sample (P928403 MRCM TANZANIA) and closely related to X. cheopis from China (accession number MW310242, HM188404 and MF000679), with an identity rate estimated from 97.76% to 98.12% with the highest (100%) bootstrap replicate support. X. brasiliensis samples from Iringa (PP919960 IRML TANZANIA) and Morogoro (PP919959 MRKS TANZANIA) were 99.33% similar to each other, and they are closely related to X. brasiliensis from Rwanda (MH142442) and Madagascar (KY073317), with identity rates of 98.77% and 98.31%, respectively. E. gallinacea from Iringa, Tanzania (PP919961 IRMZ TANZANIA) were closely related to those of Senegal (JF966770), with an identity rate estimated at 98.63% compared to 98.5% of those from Australia (HQ696945 and KT376420). An identity rate estimate of 99.04% was identified between C. felis of Iringa (PP928404 IRKT TANZANIA) with those of South Africa (LR991703) and Hawaii in the United States of America (MG586688) and 98.90% with that from Queensland in Australia (KF684919).

Discussion

The results in the present study revealed a decrease in small mammals' species diversity across a human disturbance gradient in existing habitat sites. This suggests that increasing unsustainable anthropogenic activities and encroachment of humans into natural areas tend to modify the quality of existing natural habitats. In particular, practices such as shifting agriculture, uncontrolled burning of bushes, urbanization, unsustainable livestock keeping, and overgrazing in natural areas, have immense impacts on small mammals’ ecology (Hoffmann and Zeller, 2005). Among the impacts are the reduction of natural habitat heterogeneity, increased risk of predation, loss of natural food resources, especially for habitat-specialist small mammals, and reduction of land-regeneration potentials (Hieronimo et al., 2014). Hence, only small mammals that have favorable variations, including habitat- and resource-generalist (such as Aethomys spp., A. niloticus, M. natalensis, and T. vicinus), have an advantage to adapt and remain. Meanwhile, those that are less adaptive due to habitat or food resource specialization (such as Grammomys spp., Lemniscomys spp., Acomys spp., and Elephantulus spp.) will die, migrate, or fail to reproduce and eventually be eliminated. The consequence often is the reduction of small mammal diversity across the intensity of habitat modification gradient (peridomestic < farmland < bushland), as observed in this study. Indeed, modification of natural existing habitats through human activities has been reported to reduce the diversity of small mammals, especially in relation to the decrease in the richness of habitat-specialist species (Cevidanes et al., 2016; Shilereyo et al., 2022; Viteri and Hadly, 2022).

The intensity of anthropogenic activities' impact on the abundance of small mammals depends on the frequency of practices. For instance, it is heavily implicated in peridomestic compared to farmland, which is often practiced seasonally, particularly in poor resource countries, including Tanzania. This creates the basis of our observation of small mammals’ abundance: farmland > peridomestic > bushland. Although farmland sites had the highest abundance of small mammals, largely driven by Mastomys natalensis, species diversity remained lower than in bushland. The high abundance of M. natalensis likely reflects its high fecundity and ecological plasticity (Leirs et al., 2023).

Flea infestation intensity followed a clear habitat modification gradient pattern (peridomestic > farmland > bushland). Both the ZINB and GLM models confirmed that peridomestic habitats significantly increased infestation load and probability. Higher flea infestation load in peridomestic and farmland habitats as compared to relatively undisturbed bushland habitats probably was due to an increase in abundance of generalists and suppression of specialists in small mammals and high affinity of flea species in and around the human domicile. Modified environments may provide favorable microclimatic conditions for flea survival, including warmer temperatures, increased organic debris, and stable nesting sites around human dwellings. Additionally, dominance by generalist rodent species may enhance ectoparasite persistence due to host availability (Friggens and Beier, 2010). A study investigating the drivers of ectoparasite communities found that host species and environmental factors were important determinants of ectoparasite infestations (López-Pérez et al., 2022). Similar associations between habitat alteration and increased ectoparasite burden have been documented in the Serengeti and West Usambara ecosystems (Laudisoit et al., 2009; Shilereyo et al., 2022). Both explain that a change in the quality of small mammals’ microhabitat accompanied by resource availability suppresses the specialist species and contributes to increasing the average ectoparasite load per individual small mammal.

However, M. natalensis showed lower prevalence and mean intensity despite being the most abundant species compared to other small mammals’ species. This pattern may reflect a dilution effect, where rapid population growth redistributes fleas across more hosts, reducing mean individual burden (Leirs et al., 2023). Furthermore, because many flea species spend considerable time off-host in nests and burrows (Wale et al., 2023), highly active hosts like M. natalensis may dislodge fleas more frequently. Thus, host behavior and life-history traits likely influence observed infestation patterns.

Our study found little support for species sexual size dimorphism (SSD). However, sex-biased infestation was evident, with males harboring significantly higher flea loads. This reflects that sexual size dimorphism, which refers to the differences in size between males and females of a species, was a potential factor contributing to sex-biased flea infestation in small mammals. Adult males in some species are larger than females, and this difference in size may affect their susceptibility to flea infestation. Additionally, the chance of ectoparasite infestation may be higher in individuals with greater mobility, as they are more likely to come into contact with ectoparasites in the environment or carried by other hosts. Several comparative studies (Herrero-Cófreces et al., 2021; Kowalski et al., 2015; Lareschi, 2010; Morand et al., 2004; Soliman et al., 2001) support our study's results that ectoparasites infestation load per small mammal species are male-biased correlated. A male-biased flea infestation may be associated with sexual behavior. Often males have a larger home range than females, which may increase their chance of exposure and contact with fleas. As suggested by “immunocompetence handicap hypothesis” (Roberts et al., 2004) testosterone (a sex hormone) is associated to stress effect and influence male behavior (such as increase in locomotive ability and high energy to engage in sexual competition). However, it has side effects on reducing the immunocompetence of animals. All these increase probabilities of being parasitized (Hughes and Randolph, 2001; Morand et al., 2004).

Seasonality significantly influenced infestation dynamics. Prevalence and mean intensity were higher during the rainy season across habitats and species. Rainfall likely affects flea population dynamics through both direct and indirect mechanisms. Increased humidity enhances egg hatchability, larval survival, and development of off-host stages in soil, nests, and burrows (Kessy and Rija, 2025; Kreppel et al., 2016). At the same time, heavy rainfall may alter host survival and spatial distribution. Flooded burrows may cause mortality or displacement of some small mammals (Mulungu et al., 2011), potentially concentrating fleas on remaining hosts and increasing per-host intensity. Results in this study reflects the different responses of environmental factors and host-parasite aggregate composition of the ecosystem toward seasonal rainfall patterns. Some non-volant small mammals die in burrows and foliage due to high rainfall and some, especially habitat-specialists, may shift to natural existing bushes, such as in protected areas, for shelter and search for food resources. Redistribution of fleas to the remaining small mammal species may increase the prevalence and average flea infestation per host animal. Because the practice is not the same in natural existing habitats (such as protected bushland areas), possibly, this was the reason for the observed inverted variation pattern of infestation prevalence for bushland compared to farmland and peridomestic habitat sites in this study.

In contrast, during the dry season, crop harvesting and improved vegetation cover may increase food availability, leading to higher small mammal abundance. Greater host density may redistribute fleas across more individuals, lowering average infestation intensity. Similar seasonal shifts in small mammal abundance and habitat use have been documented in the Serengeti ecosystem (Shilereyo et al., 2023). Therefore, rainfall likely shapes flea-host interactions through microclimatic effects on flea development and ecological effects on host population structure.

In this study, we examine the presence of common important fleas of rodents: X. cheopis and X. brasiliensis. However, we found the presence of C. felis, which commonly infest cats, and sticktight fleas (E. gallinacea), which infest chickens. At least all of these species are microhabitat generalists who can quickly adapt to new environments and have large host range preference (Enscore et al., 2020; Wale et al., 2023). Rodents are the primary host for Xenopsylla spp., an efficient vector of pathogens, such as Yersinia pestis or Rickettsia typhi. However, when rodent hosts are absent, this flea can easily shift to surrounding available incidental hosts, such as humans, dogs, cats, or wild mammals and birds (Nziza et al., 2019). Similarly, cats are the primary host of C. felis, but they can be found on various species of rodents, dogs, and, incidentally, they can infest humans (Lawrence et al., 2015). Birds, especially chickens, are the primary host of E. gallinacean; however, they have been reported to infest dogs, cats (Cantó et al., 2013; Kumsa et al., 2019; Weluwanarak et al., 2009), and rodents, as reported in the present study. As observed in this study, the occurrence of cat fleas and chicken fleas in rodents illustrates close interaction between wildlife and domestic animals along the interfaces. These domestic animals may act as amplifier hosts in case of spillover and cross-shift of zoonotic disease pathogens to be transmitted to humans. The fleas are responsible for the transmission of various diseases in pet animals and potentially can spillover to human (Calvani et al., 2020). Hence, the presence of these flea species around human domiciles suggests clear potential implications for the transmission of zoonotic vector-borne infection to humans and domestic animals.

Molecular analysis confirmed the circulation of four flea species: Xenopsylla cheopis, X. brasiliensis, Ctenocephalides felis, and Echidnophaga gallinacea. These species are recognized microhabitat generalists capable of exploiting multiple host taxa (Enscore et al., 2020; Wale et al., 2023). Although Xenopsylla spp. are primarily rodent-associated and are known vectors of pathogens such as Yersinia pestis and Rickettsia typhi (Nziza et al., 2019), this study did not assess pathogen presence. However, when rodent hosts are absent, this flea can easily shift to surrounding available incidental hosts, such as humans, dogs, cats, or wild mammals and birds (Nziza et al., 2019). Similarly, cats are the primary host of C. felis, but they can be found on various species of rodents, dogs, and, incidentally, they can infest humans (Lawrence et al., 2015). Birds, especially chickens, are the primary host of E. gallinacea; however, they have been reported to infest dogs, cats (Cantó et al., 2013; Kumsa et al., 2019; Weluwanarak et al., 2009), and rodents, as reported in the present study. As observed in this study, the occurrence of cat fleas and chicken fleas in rodents illustrates close interaction between wildlife and domestic animals along the interfaces. These domestic animals may act as amplifier hosts in case of spillover and cross-shift of zoonotic disease pathogens to be transmitted to humans. The fleas are responsible for the transmission of various diseases in pet animals and potentially can spillover to human (Calvani et al., 2020). Hence, the presence of these flea species around human domiciles suggests the potential risks of transmission for zoonotic vector-borne infection to humans and domestic animals.

Importantly, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of zoonotic transmission. Rather, the coexistence of generalist flea species, synanthropic rodents, and domestic animals in peridomestic environments may amplify ecological conditions that could facilitate pathogen circulation if present. The higher flea burden observed in modified habitats suggests potential risk amplification at wildlife-human interfaces, particularly where close contact between rodents, livestock, companion animals, and humans occurs.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed high nucleotide similarity between Tanzanian flea genotypes and those reported from other African countries. X. cheopis clustered within a Tanzanian clade distinct from Chinese isolates, while X. brasiliensis grouped closely with sequences from Rwanda and Madagascar. Similarly, C. felis and E. gallinacea showed close similarity to African reference sequences. These findings indicate genomic relatedness of flea populations across regions but do not confirm shared pathogen transmission dynamics.

The integration of morphological and molecular approaches strengthened species identification and phylogenetic characterization (Bird and Mazet, 2018). Accurate identification of flea species in wildlife-human interfaces is essential for ecological surveillance. Because certain flea species are competent vectors of specific pathogens (Labarthe et al., 2004), their spatial and temporal distribution may indicate ecological contexts in which pathogen maintenance could occur. However, pathogen screening studies are required to determine actual transmission dynamics.

Conclusions

Habitat modification at wildlife–human interfaces in Tanzania was associated with reduced small mammal species diversity and increased flea infestation load, particularly in peridomestic environments. Seasonal rainfall further influenced infestation dynamics, likely through its effects on flea development, survival, and host population structure. Xenopsylla spp. were the most abundant fleas identified, followed by Ctenocephalides and Echidnophaga, all of which are host-generalist taxa. Although pathogen presence was not assessed in this study, the higher infestation intensity observed in modified habitats suggests ecological conditions that may amplify the potential for flea-borne pathogen circulation if present.

These findings highlight the importance of integrating habitat management, ecological surveillance, and One Health approaches to better understand and mitigate ectoparasite-related risks at wildlife-human interfaces. Future studies incorporating pathogen screening are needed to clarify transmission dynamics and public health relevance.
