Molecular confirmation of Spirometra mansoni (Cestoda: Diphyllobothriidae) in Javanese keelback water snake (Fowlea melanzosta) in Indonesia
Authors: Ryanka Edila, Seongjun Choe, Mustofa Helmi Effendi, Lucia Tri Suwanti, John Yew Huat Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tapeworms of the genus Spirometra are important zoonotic parasites, yet their taxonomy remains unsettled and molecular species-level data from Indonesia are scarce. We examined plerocercoid larvae isolated from Javanese keelback water snakes (Fowlea melanzosta) collected in East Java, Indonesia. Infection prevalence in F. melanzosta was high (84 %), with 143 spargana recovered from subcutaneous tissues, musculatures, and the body cavities of 25 snakes. Morphological and histological features were consistent with Spirometra. Molecular analysis was used for accurate species-level identification. Partial mitochondrial cox1 sequences from five isolates were generated and analyzed phylogenetically. All sequences clustered unambiguously within the S. mansoni clade (PP = 0.99), alongside global reference isolates. Our analysis also reclassified a 2007 Indonesian isolate, previously assigned to S. erinaceieuropaei, as S. mansoni, retrospectively clarifying a misidentification. This study provides the first prospective molecular confirmation of S. mansoni in Indonesia, and underscores the role of semi-aquatic snakes in transmission cycles. These findings highlight the urgent need for expanded molecular surveillance and integrative taxonomy to clarify the epidemiology and zoonotic potential of Spirometra in the region.

Introduction

The genus Spirometra was formally established by Mueller in 1937, who upgraded it from a subgenus originally proposed by Faust et al. (1929). Tapeworms of the genus Spirometra (Cestoda: Diphyllobothriidae) are globally distributed parasites. Adults inhabit the small intestines of carnivorous mammals, while the larval plerocercoid stage (sparganum) infects a wide range of vertebrates, including amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals (Liu et al., 2015; Scholz et al., 2019; Kuchta et al., 2024). These parasites exhibit a complex life cycle involving five developmental stages: egg, coracidium, procercoid, and plerocercoid, and adult, three obligatory hosts: freshwater copepods (e.g. Cyclopidae) as first intermediate hosts, vertebrates as second intermediate or paratenic hosts (such as amphibians, reptiles, chickens, pigs, wild boar and humans), and carnivorous mammals as definitive hosts (Kuchta et al., 2015; Scholz et al., 2019).

Humans act as accidental, dead-end hosts and acquire sparganosis, a neglected foodborne and waterborne zoonotic disease, primarily through consumption of raw or undercooked meat from frogs or snakes infected with plerocercoids, or unfiltered water containing copepods infected with procercoid larvae. Infection can also occur through traditional practices, such as placing raw frog tissue on wounds or eyes (Lescano and Zunt, 2013; Waeschenbach et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2024). Although humans do not contribute to transmission, clinical outcomes can be severe, including subcutaneous, ocular, or cerebral sparganosis (Kuchta et al., 2021).

The taxonomy of Spirometra remains one of the most challenging among cestodes, complicated by the presence of cryptic species and pronounced morphological similarity between congeners (Kuchta and Scholz, 2017). More than 46 nominal species have been described worldwide, with over 20 reported from Asia (Scholz et al., 2019). However, species-level taxonomy remains unresolved, mainly due to the morphological similarity among congeners and the lack of reliable diagnostic traits. Features such as uterine morphology and spiral counts are highly variable depending on fixation methods and host stage, leading to misidentifications that have propagated into molecular databases.

In Indonesia, one of the earliest records of Spirometra dates to a specimen recovered from the civet cat (Viverra tangalunga) in Moearateweh, Borneo, which MacCallum (1921) described morphologically as Dibothrium tangalongi. This represents the first confirmed description from an animal host in the country. The first molecular data emerged much later, when Okamoto et al. (2007) analyzed an isolate from a dog in Samosir, North Sumatra, originally identifying it as S. erinaceieuropaei. More recently, Yudhana et al. (2020) reported S. erinaceieuropaei from wild frogs (Rana rugulosa) collected in Banyuwangi, East Java, although this conclusion was based on PCR amplification of a partial mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene and BLAST comparisons with misannotated GenBank entries, without generating new sequence data. To date, most Indonesian records remain limited to genus-level identifications (Pranashinta et al., 2017; Bagaskara et al., 2023; Edila et al., 2024; Iskandar et al., 2024), and no study has yet provided species-level confirmation supported by integrative taxonomy in accordance with the latest systematic revisions of the genus.

Despite increasing reports of Spirometra spp. across Asia, data on the distribution, host range, and molecular identity of larval stages remain scarce in Indonesia. Species-level identifications are rarely validated, and existing records do not incorporate the most recent taxonomic revisions. Morphological conservatism among plerocercoids hinders reliable identification, making mitochondrial cox1 gene analysis the current standard for resolving species-level taxonomy (Liu et al., 2024). This study focuses on the Javanese keelback water snake (Fowlea melanzosta), a semi-aquatic colubrid widely distributed in freshwater habitats of Java. Due to its ecological behavior, actively foraging in aquatic and terrestrial environments (Priambodo et al., 2021), it is highly exposed to potential intermediate hosts such as amphibians and copepods, making it a suitable candidate for investigating larval Spirometra infections. We aim to provide species-level identification through cox1-based molecular analysis, with morphological observations provided as complementary descriptive data.

Material and methods

Twenty-five F. melanzosta specimens were obtained from local sellers in Sidoarjo District, East Java, Indonesia (7.4478°S, 112.7183°E), between September 2023 and September 2024. All individuals were identified as wild-caught, as no captive breeding programs for this species are known in the region. Snakes were categorized by snout–vent length (SVL) into hatchlings (<45 cm), juveniles (45–60 cm), and adults (>60 cm). Specimens were euthanized with chloroform anesthesia immediately before decapitation and examination for parasites, in accordance with established animal welfare protocols. Spirometra infection was thoroughly examined following the guideline of Wang (Wang et al., 2011). The entire bodies of snakes were skinned and thoroughly examined for plerocercoids in the subcutaneous tissue, musculature, and body cavity. The number of plerocercoids per host was recorded to assess infection prevalence and intensity. Plerocercoids were preserved in 70 % ethanol for molecular analyses and fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin for subsequent morphological examination.

Morphological observations

Plerocercoid specimens were examined using light microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histological staining. For initial observation, specimens were mounted in glycerol jelly and examined under a light microscope. For SEM, larvae were rinsed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde, and post-washed in PBS. Dehydration was carried out through a graded ethanol series (70–100 %), followed by immersion in isoamyl acetate. Specimens were air-dried overnight in a sealed container with silica gel, then sputter-coated with gold using a Quorum Q150T ES Plus coater (Quorum Technologies Ltd., UK), and examined under a GeminiSEM 560 scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). For histology, selected plerocercoids were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E. Prepared slides were examined under a CX-23 light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from five individual plerocercoid larvae, each isolated from a different F. melanzosta specimen. Midbody tissue sections were used for extraction, performed using the Clear-S™ Quick-DNA Extraction Kit (InVirusTech, Gwangju, South Korea) following the manufacturer's protocol with two modifications: (1) 1 μL of glycogen solution was added (instead of 0.5 μL) to enhance DNA yield, and (2) 20 μL of hydration buffer was used (instead of 100 μL) to increase final DNA concentration. DNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

PCR amplification

A partial fragment of cox1 gene was amplified using primers p1f (5′-TGGTTTTTTGGACATCCTGAA-3′) and p1r (5′-ATCACATAATGAAAGTGAGCC-3′), targeting a 440 bp region corresponding to positions 707–1146 of the cox1 gene (Jeon et al., 2015). Preliminary attempts to amplify longer fragments yielded inconsistent results, possibly due to partial DNA degradation. PCR amplification was performed in a 30 μL reaction volume containing 6 μL of EzPCR™ XO 5X PCR Master Mix (ELPIS-BIOTECH®, Daejeon, South Korea), 2 μL of each primer, 2 μL of genomic DNA (>20 ng/μL), and 18 μL of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s, annealing at 46 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 4 min. PCR products were visualized on 1 % agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide, and gel documentation was performed using a Gel Doc™ XR + system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Genetic analyses

PCR amplicons of the cox1 gene were purified and sequenced by Cosmogenetech (Seoul, South Korea). Consensus sequences were assembled using Vector NTI software and compared to reference sequences in GenBank via BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for species-level identification (Baker et al., 2003). Sequence alignments were performed using ClustalW implemented in MEGA 11 (Larkin et al., 2007; Tamura et al., 2021).

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted based on the aligned cox1 sequences. The best-fit nucleotide substitution model was determined using jModelTest v2.1.10 (Darriba et al., 2012) under the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which selected the TrN + G model. Bayesian inference (BI) was performed using MrBayes v3.2.6 (Ronquist et al., 2012), with four Markov chains run for 10,000,000 generations. Dibothriocephalus nihonkaiensis (GenBank accession no. AB821272) was designated as the outgroup. Resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2012).

The evolutionary divergence among isolates was estimated using the p-distance method (Nei and Kumar, 2000) implemented in MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The analysis included all codon positions (1st, 2nd, and 3rd) as well as non-coding sites, with nucleotide substitutions comprising both transitions and transversions. Rate variation among sites was modelled with a gamma distribution (shape parameter = 1.0) and pairwise deletion of gaps, to assess intraspecific variation among the isolates.

Results

Infection status of Spirometra

Out of 25 examined F. melanzosta snakes, 21 (84 %) were positive for Spirometra plerocercoids (spargana). Morphological description

The larvae were whitish (Fig. 1B–D), with body lengths ranging from 65 to 135 mm and widths from 1.46 to 2.22 mm. The scolex was equipped with two distinct bothria (Fig. 1E–G). The anterior region, including the scolex, appeared contracted, measuring 1.87–2.88 mm in width but capable of extending to nearly 5 mm. The body surface exhibited prominent transverse and irregular wrinkles (Fig. 1H), and the junction between the scolex and neck was slightly constricted (Fig. 1J). Coniform spintriches (∼2 μm in length) were strumose and densely distributed across the tegumental surface (Fig. 1I).

Histological examination revealed a reticular parenchymal structure, densely packed with well-developed muscle bundles (Fig. 1K). Calcareous corpuscles of elliptical to oval shape were present throughout the tissue (Fig. 1L). The tegument comprised three distinct layers: a thin outer eosinophilic layer (1–2 μm) bearing abundant spintriches, a sub-tegumental layer containing nucleated cells arranged irregularly in one to two rows, and the underlying parenchyma (Fig. 1K and L).

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

Mitochondrial cox1 sequences obtained from five Spirometra isolates showed high pairwise identity (>98.86 %) with multiple GenBank reference sequences, most commonly annotated as S. erinaceieuropaei (e.g., MK085784), but also including entries labeled as S. decipiens (e.g., MT274582) and S. mansoni (e.g., LC213623). This overlap in top BLAST hits across nominal species highlights ongoing taxonomic inconsistencies in public genetic databases. Pairwise genetic distance (p-distance) analysis showed that four of the five sequenced isolates were genetically identical, while one exhibited slight divergence (∼1.87 %) (Supplementary Table 1). These results were represented by two distinct haplotypes, which were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PV806838 and PV806839.

Bayesian phylogenetic analysis based on cox1 sequences unambiguously placed both haplotypes within the S. mansoni clade with strong support (posterior probability = 0.99), forming a well-supported monophyletic group alongside previously published isolates from diverse hosts and geographic regions (Fig. 2). Notably, the previously reported Indonesian isolate AB278577, originally assigned to S. erinaceieuropaei, clustered within the same clade as PV806838, further confirming its identity as S. mansoni. Other lineages, including S. erinaceieuropaei, S. asiana, S. theileri, and the cryptic Spirometra sp. 2 and sp. 3, were clearly resolved as distinct clades, each with strong nodal support. Sequences of S. decipiens were not represented due to limited overlap with our dataset. Discussion

This study provides molecular confirmation of S. mansoni in Indonesia, based on partial cox1 sequences, thereby clarifying earlier records that were retrospectively reclassified. This finding provides molecular evidence supporting the presence of this zoonotic cestode in Indonesia, which is widely recognized as one of the principal etiological agents of human sparganosis and spirometrosis worldwide (Kuchta et al., 2021). The identification of F. melanzosta as the second intermediate or paratenic host raises important public health concerns, particularly in areas where this snake is commonly handled, consumed, or used in traditional medicine (Utama et al., 2024; Edila et al., 2024). A high infection prevalence (84 %) was observed across all age classes of snakes (hatchlings, juveniles, and adults), with plerocercoids found in the subcutaneous tissue, musculature, and body cavity. This underscores the ecological significance of this semi-aquatic colubrid. Occupying a key trophic position at the interface of aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems (Amarasinghe et al., 2022), F. melanzosta likely facilitates Spirometra transmission across multiple levels of the food web.

Accurate species-level identification of Spirometra is critical for understanding its zoonotic potential and epidemiological importance, especially in Indonesia where taxonomic data remain limited. Macroscopically, spargana are distinguishable from other cestode larvae (except Mesocestoides tetrathyridia) by the absence of a hooked scolex, suckers, or a vesicular body, and by the presence of solid parenchymal tissue without a primary lacuna (Kuchta et al., 2021). The anterior region exhibited a flattened scolex with two longitudinal bothria. However, while these features are sufficient for genus-level diagnosis, they are insufficient for precise species identification, which requires molecular confirmation due to morphological similarities among Spirometra taxa (Sanders et al., 2025).

Phylogenetic analysis confirmed that the spargana isolated in this study belong to S. mansoni. Reanalysis of a 2007 isolate (AB278577), previously assigned to S. erinaceieuropaei, showed zero genetic divergence from our haplotype PV806838, thereby supporting its reclassification as S. mansoni. These results provide a basis for reinterpreting early Indonesian reports of sparganosis that were historically documented but taxonomically uncertain.

The first case occurred in Ambon in 1910, where a sparganum was recovered from the urinary bladder of a navy officer (von Römer, 1910, as cited in Bonne, 1942). A subsequent autopsy in Batavia (Jakarta) described a large sparganum in the pulmonary artery (Bonne, 1937). In another case, spargana from a 45-year-old male were experimentally transferred into kittens, which yielded adult worms initially classified as Diphyllobothrium spp. and later reidentified as D. erinacei (Bonne, 1942). Beyond the human cases, spargana recovered from frogs in rice fields around Batavia (de Hartogh, 1932), monkeys (Bonne, 1942), and pigs (Meijer, 1937) were also used in experimental infections. These larvae developed into adult cestodes in cats that were variably described as D. ranarum, D. raillieti, or D. erinacei. These findings indicate that sparganosis has been present in Indonesia for over a century. Based on our molecular evidence, these cases are likely attributable to S. mansoni, which is consistent with current taxonomic frameworks suggesting a broad Asian distribution for this species (Kuchta et al., 2021, 2024; Bazsalovicsová et al., 2022; Yamasaki et al., 2024). Taxonomic confusion surrounding S. mansoni has been longstanding across East and Southeast Asia (Jeon et al., 2015, 2018; Eom et al., 2018).

S. mansoni was first described by Cobbold in 1883 as Ligula mansoni from plerocercoids recovered at autopsy in Amoy (now Xiamen, China), making it the first Spirometra species formally described from Asia. Among its congeners, S. mansoni is the most widespread and has been introduced beyond its native range to Africa, Australia, Oceania, southeastern Europe (e.g., Romania), and both North and South America (Jeon et al., 2016; Kuchta et al., 2021; Brabec et al., 2022). Consistent with these patterns, the isolates obtained in this study were genetically identical to S. mansoni sequences from multiple continents suggesting a widely distributed lineage found across multiple continents. Sequence divergence across our dataset (0.0000–0.0563) reflects substantial genetic diversity and limited gene flow among geographically distinct populations, in agreement with previous findings from Chinese populations of S. mansoni (Xu et al., 2022). In contrast, S. asiana, the only other currently validated Spirometra species in Asia, displays notably lower genetic variability, with reported cox1 divergence as low as 0.0031 (Yamasaki et al., 2024).

Currently, seven valid Spirometra species-level lineages are recognized. These include S. erinaceieuropaei (Europe), S. mansoni (Asia, America [North, Central, and the northern part of South America], and East Africa), S. asiana (East Asia and probably East Africa; see Eom et al., 2018; Yamasaki et al., 2024), S. theileri (Africa), and S. decipiens (South America), along with two newly recognized lineages: Spirometra sp. 2 (South American lineage) and Spirometra sp. 3 (North American lineage) (Kuchta et al., 2024). The African lineage, previously referred to as S. folium (Kuchta et al., 2021), has been shown to represent S. theileri, originally described from African carnivores (Baer, 1924) and later confirmed by molecular data from leopard and spotted hyena (Eom et al., 2019; Ndosi et al., 2020; Kuchta et al., 2024). Likewise, the former S. decipiens complex (lineages 1 and 2) has been revised into three distinct lineages, resolving long-standing taxonomic ambiguity (Kuchta et al., 2021; Armúa-Fernández et al., 2021; Fredes et al., 2022).

This study was geographically restricted to a single locality in East Java, limiting the extent to which broader conclusions on genetic diversity and spatial distribution can be drawn. Considering Indonesia's archipelagic complexity and ecological heterogeneity, future studies should prioritize multi-island sampling across different vertebrate hosts. Complete mitochondrial genome sequencing will also be valuable for resolving fine-scale phylogenetic relationships and for improving the understanding of population-level structuring in Spirometra.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first prospective molecular confirmation of S. mansoni in Indonesia. The high prevalence of infection in F. melanzosta emphasizes its role as an important intermediate or paratenic host and raises concerns for zoonotic spillover in areas where snakes are consumed or used in traditional medicine. These findings underscore the need for expanded molecular surveillance and integrative taxonomic approaches to better assess the epidemiology and zoonotic risks of sparganosis across Indonesia.
