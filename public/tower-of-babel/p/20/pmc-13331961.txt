Trajectories of distress and threshold-triggered supportive care in head and neck cancer patients undergoing curative radiotherapy: a prospective observational study
Authors: Pranav Nair, Haridas Mohanachandran Nair, Pushpaja Kuttassery Ullattil, Anoop Remesan Nair, Shoba Nair, Haripriya Parapparambil Surendran, Vidya N, Rajiv Nair, Debnarayan Dutta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo prospectively evaluate trajectories of psychological distress, symptom burden, and quality of life longitudinally, in patients with head and neck cancer undergoing curative-intent radiotherapy, while assessing the feasibility of a predefined threshold-triggered supportive care framework in routine radiotherapy practice.MethodsOur prospective observational cohort study, conducted at a single tertiary care-centre, enrolled 100 adult patients, who were diagnosed with head and neck cancers and were subsequently planned for definitive or adjuvant RT between January and December 2023. Psychological distress was assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and NCCN Distress Thermometer (NCCN-DT), the overall symptom burden was measured using the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS), and quality of life using the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy—Head and Neck (FACT-H&N). Assessments were performed at baseline, once weekly during radiotherapy, at the time of RT completion, and at 1 and 3 months post-RT completion. A structured supportive care protocol was activated using predefined screening thresholds. Longitudinal changes were analysed using repeated-measures methods and paired non-parametric comparisons.ResultsMedian HADS increased from 4.0 (2.0–7.0) at baseline to 7.0 (4.0–9.5) at week 2, before declining to 2.0 (0.0–6.0) at 3 months post-RT completion (p < 0.001). NCCN-DT and ESAS scores worsened during treatment and improved after RT completion. FACT-H&N scores declined from 128 (120–138) at baseline to 98.5 (84–117) at treatment completion, with recovery to 126 (117–138) at 3 months post-RT completion (p < 0.001). Enteral feeding support increased from 24% at baseline to 44% by RT treatment completion. Supportive care activation was recorded in 68% of patients. An exploratory analysis using early week-2 screening positivity identified a higher-burden subgroup with greater distress and lower FACT-H&N scores during treatment, but substantial recovery by 3 months.ConclusionsPsychological distress among patients undergoing curative-intent head and neck radiotherapy is dynamic, with peaks early in the treatment course, and is often closely aligned with symptom burden and treatment-related functional decline. The use of a structured, threshold-triggered supportive care framework is feasible in routine radiotherapy workflows, assisting in the identification of patients who require timely multidisciplinary support. Given the observational design and absence of a comparator arm, findings may be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Introduction

Head and neck cancers (HNC) impose significant physical, functional, and psychosocial burdens on patients. Psychological distress is frequently reported across a wide prevalence range, and varies according to tumour site, treatment phase, assessment tools, and cultural context[1, 2]. This burden is further driven by a complex combination of visible or functional disfigurement, pain, dysphagia, altered speech and swallowing, nutritional compromise, social stigma, and fear related to treatment and prognosis [3].

Radiotherapy (RT), with or without concurrent chemotherapy, remains a core component of curative-intent definitive treatment for HNC. However, RT is associated with a multitude of acute toxicities such as mucositis, odynophagia, dysphagia, xerostomia, dermatitis, fatigue, and weight loss [4]. These often begin early during treatment and often intensify over time. Despite contemporary techniques such as image-guided intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), patients may experience substantial symptom burden and quality-of-life deterioration [5].

The aetiology of distress in HNC is attributed to a multitude of factors. Physical contributors include pain, inflammation (acute), nutritional stress, and metabolic derangements during the course of treatment. Psychological factors include anticipatory anxiety, a fear of an altered body image, generalized uncertainty, and a fear of disease recurrence. These are further compounded by social issues, namely isolation, caregiver stress, and financial/logistic burdens [5]. Clinician recognition of distress is often incomplete without systematic screening. Untreated distress is associated with poorer adherence, treatment interruptions, impaired quality of life, and adverse survivorship outcomes [6, 7].

Professional societies, including the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), recommend routine screening and management of distress in oncology [8], using validated tools. HADS provides structured assessment of anxiety and depression symptoms. The NCCN Distress Thermometer allows rapid identification of clinically significant distress [9]. ESAS captures patient-reported symptom burden [10] and FACT-H&N evaluates health-related quality of life across general and head-and-neck-specific domains [9, 11]. However, repeated screening, linked to a predefined supportive care response, remains inconsistently implemented in busy RT departments [12].

Evidence from early palliative and supportive care studies suggests that proactive symptom and psychosocial management can improve quality of life and mood in selected oncology populations [13]. However, HNC-specific evidence describing longitudinal distress trajectories during RT and linking repeated screening to an explicit supportive care escalation framework remains limited, particularly regarding timing of peak distress, symptom drivers, and practical feasibility of embedding threshold-triggered supportive care into routine RT workflows.

This study prospectively evaluated distress, symptom burden, and quality-of-life trajectories in patients with HNC undergoing curative-intent RT using validated screening tools. The objectives were: (1) to characterise longitudinal psychological distress patterns; (2) to identify the timing and symptom drivers of peak distress; (3) to explore recovery patterns among patients managed within a threshold-triggered supportive care framework; and (4) to assess the feasibility of routine repeated screening in a real-world RT setting.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-centre prospective observational cohort study conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital between January and December 2023. Eligible participants were adults (age ≥ 18 years) with histologically confirmed HNC planned for curative-intent RT, either definitive or adjuvant. Exclusion criteria included palliative RT, hypofractionated schedules of ≤ 10 fractions, split-course RT, metastatic disease at presentation, anticipated survival < 6 months, and significant cognitive or communication impairment precluding completion of patient-reported outcome measures.

Of a total of 112 eligible patients, 100 were enrolled after providing written informed consent. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (ECASM-AIMS-2023–072).

Treatment characteristics

All patients underwent CT simulation with thermoplastic mask immobilisation. RT was delivered using image-guided IMRT or VMAT. Definitive RT schedules typically delivered 66–70 Gy in 30–33 fractions, while postoperative RT schedules delivered 60–66 Gy in 30–33 fractions. Concurrent systemic therapy (predominantly cisplatin based) was administered according to standard clinical indications. Acute toxicities were assessed at least weekly using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Baseline clinical variables

Baseline and treatment variables included age, sex, primary tumour site, AJCC 8th edition stage, histopathology, tumour grade, treatment intent, treatment group, RT dose-fractionation schedule, nodal irradiation, concurrent chemotherapy, systemic agent, feeding access before and after RT, treatment completion, and unplanned treatment interruptions.

Assessment tools and schedule

Psychological distress was assessed using HADS and NCCN-DT, symptom burden using ESAS, and quality of life using FACT-H&N. HADS was the primary psychological distress measure and includes anxiety and depression subscales as well as a global score. We employed NCCN-DT as a rapid global distress screening tool and ESAS was used to assess symptom burden, including pain, fatigue, nausea, anxiety, depression, and overall wellbeing. FACT-H&N scores were analysed as total composite scores representing physical, functional, social, emotional, and head-and-neck-specific domains.

Assessments were performed at baseline before RT, weekly during RT (weeks 1–6), at RT completion, and at 1 and 3 months post-RT completion to capture longitudinal changes in patient reported outcomes.

Threshold-triggered early supportive care protocol

All patients received standard supportive care according to institutional practice, including mucositis management, nutritional counselling, analgesic optimisation, skin care, and correction of haematologic or metabolic abnormalities. In addition, we also implemented a structured threshold-triggered supportive care protocol as depicted in Table 1, when patients met any of the following criteria: (1) a ≥ 2-point increase in HADS global or subscale score; (2) NCCN-DT score ≥ 4; or (3) clinically meaningful worsening in ESAS domains relevant to head and neck RT, including pain, dysphagia/odynophagia, mucositis, fatigue, or sleep disturbance. Threshold-triggered early supportive care protocol

Each trigger corresponded to predefined multidisciplinary interventions, including analgesic escalation, intensified mucositis care, nutritional intervention, fatigue and sleep optimisation, and brief psycho-oncology input using supportive counselling and basic cognitive-behavioural techniques. All interventions were delivered according to predefined protocol pathways; however, minor adjustments were permitted at clinician discretion based on individual needs. The novelty of the protocol lies not in the individual supportive care, based on standard components of HNC RT care, but in linking repeated screening thresholds to structured and timely escalation of multidisciplinary supportive care.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was change in HADS global score from baseline to 3 months post-RT. Secondary outcomes included timing of peak distress, longitudinal NCCN-DT and ESAS trajectories, change in FACT-H&N total score, identification of symptom drivers of distress, feasibility of weekly screening, treatment completion, and exploratory comparison of patients who were/were not screened positive early during RT.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (Q1-Q3), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Longitudinal changes were analysed using repeated-measures ANOVA with Greenhouse–Geisser correction where appropriate. Pairwise comparisons were corrected using Bonferroni adjustment. Paired non-parametric comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. While the calculated minimum sample size was 51, to detect a moderate effect size in HADS change with 80% power and α = 0.05, we recruited 100 patients to improve statistical precision, enhance robustness of longitudinal analyses, and account for potential attrition.

For an additional exploratory analysis, the master chart was used to stratify patients according to early week-2 screening positivity, defined as HADS ≥ 7 and/or NCCN-DT ≥ 4 at week 2. This analysis was included to describe clinically meaningful subgroup trajectories, but was not treated as a causal comparison of intervention effectiveness.

Results

Baseline and treatment characteristics

A total of 100 patients were enrolled. Table 2 lists all of the variables that were collected. Mean age was 60.0 ± 13.1 years (range, 26–83 years), and 84% were male. Oral cavity cancers accounted for 50% of our cohort, followed by laryngeal (15%) and oropharyngeal cancers (13%). Most patients presented with locally advanced disease; 53% were stage IVA and 13% were stage IVB, at the time of diagnosis. Squamous cell carcinoma was identified as the histology in the majority of our patient cohort (91%). Treatment-related characteristics demonstrated a clinically high-burden cohort: 64% underwent surgery followed by adjuvant RT or chemoradiation, while 36% received definitive RT or chemoradiation. Concurrent chemotherapy was administered to 48 patients, most commonly cisplatin (40/48; 83.3%). Supportive care activation and exploratory subgroup analysis

The threshold-triggered supportive care protocol was activated in 68% of patients during RT, based on predefined screening criteria and clinical review. The difference between the 68% protocol activation rate and the 73% early screen-positive subgroup reflects the distinction between clinically documented protocol activation and exploratory threshold-based subgrouping. Interventions most commonly included analgesic escalation, mucositis management, nutritional support, and brief psycho-oncology input. As a dedicated binary variable for actual protocol activation was not recorded, an additional exploratory analysis was performed using early week-2 screening positivity (HADS ≥ 7 and/or NCCN-DT ≥ 4) as a clinically transparent screening-based subgroup. This analysis was intended to describe burden and recovery patterns rather than estimate causal intervention effects.

Seventy-three patients met early week-2 screening positivity criteria, as shown in Table 4. Compared with early screen-negative patients, early screen-positive patients had higher baseline HADS and NCCN-DT scores, higher distress during RT, lower FACT-H&N scores during treatment, and persistent but improved distress at 3 months post-RT. These findings suggest that patients with greater early distress burden experienced not only dynamic deterioration during RT, but also meaningful post-RT recovery. Symptom category contributing to distress

According to NCCN-DT problem categorisation, physical symptoms were the most frequently identified contributors to distress (63%), followed by emotional/functional issues (28%) and social issues (9%). These findings support the close relationship between acute treatment-related symptom burden and psychological distress in this cohort.

Treatment adherence

Radiotherapy completion rates were high (97%). Unplanned treatment interruptions of ≥ 3 days were infrequent (12%) according to clinical treatment records. Distress trajectories were not interpreted as causal determinants of interruption risk because treatment adherence is influenced by multiple clinical, social, nutritional, and toxicity-related factors.

Discussion

This prospective observational study shows that psychological distress, symptom burden, and quality of life in patients with HNC, undergoing curative-intent RT, follow dynamic and clinically meaningful trajectories. Psychological distress peaked early during RT, symptom burden increased across treatment, and quality of life deteriorated substantially during RT before recovering during post-treatment follow-up. By linking repeated screening to a predefined supportive care escalation framework, this study provides pragmatic evidence supporting structured distress screening within routine radiotherapy workflows.

Longitudinal HADS and NCCN-DT assessments characterised psychological distress trajectories; the timing of peak distress was identified; ESAS and FACT-H&N helped contextualise symptom burden and quality-of-life changes; and the feasibility of repeated screening was demonstrated through successful implementation in a busy RT department. Importantly, psychological distress (HADS/NCCN-DT) and symptom burden (ESAS) demonstrated closely aligned trajectories, reinforcing their interdependent relationship during HNC RT.

The early HADS peak at week 2 is clinically important. Some prior reports describe distress peaks later during RT; however, the earlier peak in this cohort may reflect the predominance of oral cavity cancers and the high proportion of patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy [7]. Both factors are associated with rapid onset mucosal toxicity, pain, dysphagia, and nutritional compromise. In this cohort, enteral feeding support increased from 24% before RT to 44% by treatment completion, supporting the clinical relevance of evolving nutritional burden. These findings suggest that supportive care needs may emerge earlier than expected in high-toxicity HNC RT populations.

FACT-H&N trajectories provide an important complement to distress outcomes. The quality of life declined progressively during RT and reached its lowest point around weeks 4 and 5. This was consistent with the cumulative burden of mucositis, dysphagia, fatigue, nutritional difficulty, and functional impairment. The subsequent recovery of FACT-H&N scores toward baseline by 3 months post-RT suggests that treatment-related quality-of-life deterioration was largely transient in this cohort. However, a longer follow-up is required to evaluate the persistent effects of late toxicities, a fear of recurrence, continued swallowing dysfunction, and survivorship concerns.

A key contribution of our study is the structured threshold-triggered supportive care framework. The individual interventions used—analgesia optimisation, mucositis management, nutritional support, and psycho-oncology input – are time-tested standard elements of HNC supportive care. The novelty of our approach lies in integrating predefined distress and symptom thresholds with longitudinal monitoring in order to enable timely escalation of care. Our approach therefore, shifts supportive care from a reactive clinician-led recognition toward a structured patient-reported outcome-driven model [24, 25]. Such a model is particularly relevant in settings with limited psycho-oncology staffing [12, 14, 15], as screening thresholds can help prioritise patients who may benefit from additional support.

The exploratory week-2 screen-positive analysis reported in our study, further illustrates that patients with early clinically significant distress form a higher-burden subgroup. These patients had a higher baseline distress, greater deterioration of scores during the period of RT, and lower FACT-H&N scores, but demonstrated substantial recovery by 3 months post-RT completion. This pattern should not be interpreted as proof of intervention efficacy; rather, it supports the clinical value of early screening to identify patients with dynamic distress trajectories who may benefit from intensified multidisciplinary support.

The findings also reinforce the established and central role of physical symptoms as drivers of distress [11, 16–18]. Physical issues were the most frequently reported contributors to distress across the NCCN-DT problem categorisation while ESAS trajectories showed worsening of symptoms relevant to HNC RT. Thus, we suggest that effective distress management for these patients requires an integrated management of pain, mucositis, dysphagia, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and nutritional compromise, as opposed to offering psychological counselling alone.

Clinical implications

The findings have practical implications for routine practice across RT centres, globally. First, routine distress screening using brief validated tools is feasible and clinically informative, and should begin at baseline with repeated assessments during RT. Particular attention should be paid to the first two to three weeks of treatment, when distress may peak in high-burden HNC cohorts [19, 20].

Second, predefined screening thresholds can be used to activate a structured, stepped supportive care model. This allows timely identification of patients at risk and facilitates targeted multidisciplinary intervention rather than reliance on clinician discretion alone [21].

Third, the close relationship between symptom burden and psychological distress highlights the importance of proactive management of pain, mucositis, dysphagia, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and nutritional compromise. In resource-constrained settings, protocolised screening may help prioritise patients most in need of psycho-oncology, nutrition, pain, and palliative care input [22].

Finally, this threshold-triggered model provides a pragmatic framework that can be adapted across RT settings to systematically integrate supportive care into routine practice. Future multicentre studies could evaluate whether such models do indeed, contribute to an improvement in patient-reported outcomes, treatment adherence, and longer-term survivorship measures compared with routine RT and post-RT care [17, 23].

Limitations

The study was conducted at a single tertiary centre, thus limiting generalisability across different health systems and cultural contexts. The absence of a non-intervention control arm precludes causal attribution of observed improvements in distress and quality of life. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating. Potential confounding factors such as concurrent chemotherapy, tumour site distribution, disease stage, baseline nutritional status, feeding access, social support, and treatment-related toxicity may have influenced the identified trajectory patterns. The exploratory screen-positive subgroup analysis was non-randomised and subject to selection bias and residual confounding. Moreover, follow-up was limited to 3 months post-RT completion; longer-term trajectories of depression, anxiety, swallowing dysfunction, nutritional recovery, and fear of recurrence require further evaluation. Finally, the data available at the time of analysis did not contain a dedicated binary field for actual supportive care activation; therefore, the subgroup analysis based on early screening positivity should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort, distress peaked early during RT, while FACT-H&N scores showed maximum deterioration later during treatment followed by substantial recovery by 3 months post-RT completion. A structured, threshold-triggered supportive care framework was feasible within routine RT practice and provides a reproducible model for early identification and escalation of multidisciplinary support. These findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal given the observational design and absence of a comparator arm. Multicentre studies with longer follow-up and formal comparative designs are warranted to validate and extend these findings.
