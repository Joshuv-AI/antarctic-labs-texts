Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, and Immunogenicity of CM313 in Healthy Participants: An Open‐Label/Double‐Blind, Randomized, Placebo‐Controlled Phase 1 Trial
Authors: Shuyan Yu, Junzhen Wu, Junxue Zhao, Jinjie He, Hongyue Yan, Xiang Zhang, Yifan Yang, Han Song, Qiaoyun Hou, Bujing Guo, Yanping Qiu, Bo Chen, Yu Xue, Jing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCM313, a novel humanized anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibody, has demonstrated therapeutic potential in autoimmune diseases. This Phase 1 trial evaluated safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics in healthy participants following single ascending doses of CM313. Fifty‐one participants received CM313 or placebo by SC injection at doses of 150 mg (1:1), 300 mg (2:1), and 600 mg (2:1), or by IV infusion at 8 mg/kg (2:1). All completed the trial. Treatment‐emergent adverse events occurred in 90.9% of CM313‐treated participants and 83.3% of placebo‐treated participants, all mild or moderate. No serious adverse events were reported. SC injections of CM313 demonstrated a notably lower incidence of infusion‐related reactions (600 mg SC: 20%) than IV infusion (8 mg/kg IV: 60%). CM313 exhibited nonlinear pharmacokinetics. Following a single SC dose, the median T max was 2.00–3.00 days, mean C max was 4.75–87.40 μg/mL, mean AUC0‐t was 20.29–1262.50 day*μg/mL, mean V z/F was 2.63–21.20 L, mean clearance was 0.50–7.90 L/day, and mean half‐life was 1.85–3.90 days. The bioavailability of the 600 mg SC dose was 66% of the 8 mg/kg IV dose. CM313 induced rapid and sustained reductions in total IgA, IgE, IgG, and IgM levels by 40%–80% from baseline. Both SC and IV formulations demonstrated potent CD38 binding activity and markedly depleted NK cells by ≥ 90% within 2 days. CM313 also achieved 80%–90% CD38 receptor occupancy on B cells, T cells, and monocytes within 1 week. Overall, single doses of CM313 demonstrated favorable safety, tolerability, PK, and PD in healthy participants. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06285227)

Introduction

CD38 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that is highly expressed on plasma cells and other immune cells. It functions both as a receptor for CD31 and as an ectoenzyme with NAD‐degrading activities [1, 2, 3]. Due to its overexpression on myeloma cells and low levels on normal lymphoid and myeloid cells, CD38 has been identified as a key therapeutic target in myeloma [4, 5]. To date, two anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), isatuximab and daratumumab, have been approved for use as monotherapy and in combination regimens for both relapsed/refractory and newly diagnosed multiple myeloma.

In addition to their well‐established use in multiple myeloma, anti‐CD38 mAbs have also shown therapeutic potential in autoimmune diseases in recent years [6]. Existing evidence suggests that CD38 may play an indispensable modulatory role in human autoimmunity by regulating multiple inflammatory processes including cell migration and activation, cytokine and chemokine release, phagocytosis, and antigen presentation [7, 8, 9]. Autoantibodies produced by plasma cells have been shown to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of many autoimmune diseases [10]. Therefore, it has been hypothesized that CD38 antibodies may represent a therapeutic strategy for autoimmune diseases through the targeted elimination of pathogenic plasma cells, thereby effectively depleting autoantibodies [11, 12]. Preliminary clinical evidence has suggested the potential efficacy of anti‐CD38 mAbs in patients with several autoimmune diseases including rheumatoid arthritis [13], systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [14, 15], lupus nephritis [16], and immune thrombocytopenia [6, 12]. These cases provide a rationale for further exploration of anti‐CD38 therapy in the treatment of autoimmune diseases.

CM313 is a novel humanized anti‐CD38 mAb with potent pathogenic CD38+ cell killing activity through Fc receptor‐dependent mechanisms including ADCC, CDC, ADCP, and apoptosis. Preclinical data have shown that CM313, with a distinct CDR sequence, has comparable killing efficacy to daratumumab and stronger CDC activity than isatuximab, supporting its clinical development [17]. In an open‐label, multicenter Phase 1 trial of CM313 in patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma or lymphoma (NCT04818372), CM313 monotherapy showed promising clinical efficacy with a tolerable and manageable safety profile [18]. CM313 is also currently under clinical investigation for autoimmune diseases including immune thrombocytopenia (NCT06199089) and SLE (NCT05465707). Data from a Phase 2 trial (NCT05694767) demonstrated tolerable safety and preliminary efficacy of CM313 in patients with refractory/relapsed ITP following intravenous injection of CM313 at a dose of 16 mg/kg weekly for 8 weeks [12]. Taken together, these findings suggest that CM313 holds great potential for treating autoimmune diseases. To support its further clinical development, we conducted a Phase 1 trial to evaluate the safety, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and immunogenicity of subcutaneous (SC) or intravenous (IV) CM313 in healthy participants.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a single‐center, randomized, open‐label/double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, single ascending dose, Phase I clinical trial in healthy participants. The objectives were to evaluate the safety, PK, PD, and immunogenicity of CM313 SC injections or IV infusions. Briefly, this trial consisted of a screening period (D28–D2), a baseline period (D1), a dosing and hospital observation period (D1–D8), and a follow‐up period (D9–D57).

The trial planned to enroll 51 healthy male adults at four dose levels: 150 mg SC, 300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, and 8 mg/kg IV. The rationale for dose selection is detailed in Methods S2. The starting dose (150 mg SC) used an open‐label design, whereas the subsequent doses were double‐blind for participants and investigators. At the starting dose of 150 mg SC, three sentinel participants were initially enrolled in the placebo group to assess safety and tolerability for 8 days after administration, and three participants were subsequently enrolled in the CM313 group to assess safety and tolerability for 15 days after administration. Upon completion of the safety evaluation with the 150 mg SC dose, 45 participants were enrolled and randomized in a 2:1 ratio to receive either CM313 or placebo at the 300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, or 8 mg/kg IV dose levels. Following a sequential enrollment design, all participants at a given dose level underwent a 15‐day post‐dose safety evaluation; based on the results, the investigator and sponsor determined whether to continue, adjust the dose, or discontinue enrollment at the next dose level. The schematic of the study design, including the dose escalation process, is shown in Figure S1.

This trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (approval number: 2024035, date: February 7, 2024). All clinical trials were conducted in accordance with the principles and guidelines of Good Clinical Practice of the International Council for Harmonization (ICH GCP), the Declaration of Helsinki, and local laws and regulations.

Participants and Eligibility

Eligible participants were healthy male adults, aged 18–50 years, with a weight ≥ 55 kg and a body mass index between 19 and 27 kg/m2. Key exclusion criteria included: (1) history of any clinically serious disease or history of circulatory, digestive, endocrine, neurological, hematological, immune, psychiatric, or metabolic disorders; (2) known immune system disorders, including but not limited to autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA); (3) history of malignancy; (4) known allergy to mAbs or other related drugs and foods, or allergy to CM313 excipients; and (5) use of mAbs, cell therapy, etc. within 6 months prior to screening, or prior use of any anti‐CD38 drugs. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for this trial are provided in Table S1.

All participants signed the informed consent form prior to the screening period.

Outcomes and Endpoints

The primary endpoint of this trial is the safety and tolerability of CM313 administered by single SC injection or IV infusion, as assessed by treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), physical examinations, vital signs, 12‐lead electrocardiograms, and laboratory tests (including hematology, blood biochemistry, urinalysis, and coagulation, etc.). TEAEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terminology by system organ class and preferred term.

Secondary endpoints were assessed by PK, PD, and immunogenicity. PK parameters included time to maximum concentration (T max), maximum concentration (C max), area under the concentration‐time curve from time 0 to time t (AUC0‐t ), area under the concentration‐time curve from time 0 to infinity (AUCinf), elimination half‐life (t 1/2), clearance (CL), volume of distribution (V z), and mean residence time (MRT). The PD of CM313 was evaluated by the percent change (%) from baseline of immune cells, immunoglobulins, and CD38 receptor occupancy (RO) in peripheral blood. Immune cells were calculated as the percentage of peripheral blood B cells, T cells, NK cells, and plasma cells to total lymphocytes (%). Specifically, CD38+ immune cells were calculated as the percentage of CD38+ B cells, CD38+ T cells, and CD38+ NK cells to total B cells, T cells, and NK cells. Immunoglobulins were calculated as the concentration of IgG, IgA, and IgE (g/L) in peripheral blood. CD38 receptor occupancy (%) was also calculated for B cells, T cells, and monocytes. Immunogenicity endpoints were the incidence of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) and neutralizing antibodies (NAbs). PK, PD, and immunogenicity assays are provided in Methods S1.

Randomization and Blinding

This study adopted a randomized design for all groups except the 150 mg SC, with an interactive web response system (IWRS) to manage participant randomization, drug dispensing, and data collection. A randomization statistician used SAS Version 9.4 software to generate random tables of participants and drugs for each dose level using the block randomization method. These random tables were then imported into the IWRS by the system engineer. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to either the CM313 or placebo group according to the allocation ratio for each dose level, with their random number accessible to the investigator via the IWRS. During drug dispensing, the investigator accessed the IWRS to obtain the drug number and dispense the drugs accordingly.

Statistical Analysis

The Phase 1 trial was designed to enroll 51 healthy participants, including six at the 150 mg SC dose level and 15 each at the 300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, and 8 mg/kg IV dose levels. Based on this sample size, there is an 80% probability of observing at least one adverse event with a 15% incidence rate among 10 participants.

Several analysis sets were generated in this study, including the full analysis set (FAS), safety set (SS), PK concentration set (PKCS), PK parameter set (PKPS), PD set (PDS), and immunogenicity set (IMGS). We summarized participant characteristics in the FAS, which includes all participants who were randomized or enrolled to receive at least one dose of CM313 or placebo. Safety endpoints were evaluated in the SS, which includes participants who received treatment and underwent safety evaluations. PK, PD, and immunogenicity endpoints were assessed in the PKCS, PKPS, PDS, and IMGS datasets, which include participants who received treatment and had valid PK, PD, or immunogenicity results.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant demographics, baseline characteristics, TEAEs, PK characteristics, PD outcomes, and immunogenicity. Means and standard deviations (SDs) were used to describe continuous variables, and counts and percentages were used for categorical variables. Specifically, for PK assessment, we plotted the serum concentration‐time profile on semi‐logarithmic scales for all dose levels by nominal time; Phoenix WinNonlin Version 8.3.1 software was used to calculate non‐compartmental PK parameters; and a Power Model was applied to assess dose‐exposure proportionality between PK parameters (C max, AUC0‐t , and AUCinf) and doses in the SC cohort at doses of 150, 300, or 600 mg. All statistical analyses in this study were performed using SAS Version 9.4 software.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Between March 12, 2024, and August 22, 2024, a total of 51 eligible participants were enrolled and received a single dose of CM313 (n = 33) or placebo (n = 18). Four cohorts received either CM313 or placebo at doses of 150 mg SC, 300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, or 8 mg/kg IV. All participants completed the trial (Figure S2). As summarized in Table 1, participants were healthy male adults, with a mean (SD) age of 28.7 (6.8) years and a mean (SD) body mass index of 23.7 (1.9). Participant characteristics were generally comparable between the SC and IV cohorts, as well as between the CM313 and placebo cohorts.

Safety and Tolerability

The incidence of TEAEs is summarized in Table 2. In this study, single SC or IV doses of CM313 or placebo were well tolerated at all dose levels in healthy participants. No participants withdrew from the trial due to adverse events, and no SAEs were reported. TEAEs were reported in 90.9% (30/33) of participants in the CM313 group and 83.3% (15/18) in the placebo group. Drug‐related TEAEs were reported in 84.8% (28/33) of participants in the CM313 group and 66.7% (12/18) in the placebo group. All TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity. The most common TEAEs were injection site reaction (CM313: 42.4% vs. placebo: 66.7%), infusion‐related reaction (CM313: 27.3% vs. placebo: 0%), upper respiratory tract infection (CM313: 15.2% vs. placebo: 22.2%), alanine aminotransferase increased (CM313: 12.1% vs. placebo: 5.6%), and lymphocyte count decreased (CM313: 12.1% vs. placebo: 0%).

Injection site reactions were frequently reported in both the CM313 and placebo cohorts, with a dose‐dependent trend observed in the SC cohort. Following a single SC dose of CM313, the incidence of injection site reactions increased to 33.3%, 50%, and 80% at doses of 150, 300, and 600 mg, respectively. Additionally, infusion‐related reactions (IRRs) occurred in 27.3% (9/33) of participants in the CM313 cohort, predominantly following IV infusion (6/9). All IRRs were mild and resolved spontaneously. The incidence of IRRs was lower with the SC formulation (300 mg SC: 10%, 600 mg SC: 20%) than with the IV formulation (8 mg/kg IV: 60%). No IRRs were reported in the placebo cohort. No apparent dose‐dependent trend in other TEAEs was detected in this trial.

A single dose of CM313 exhibited nonlinear PK properties in healthy participants, as indicated by the disproportionate increase in CM313 concentration with increasing doses. This disproportionality of drug exposure across the 150, 300, and 600 mg doses was further confirmed by our Power model results (Figure S3).

Following a single SC dose of CM313 at 150, 300, and 600 mg, the median T max ranged from 2.00 to 3.00 days, mean C max ranged from 4.75 to 87.4 μg/mL, and mean AUC0‐t ranged from 20.3 to 1260 day·μg/mL. Pharmacodynamics

Following a single dose of CM313, rapid and sustained decreases in serum IgG, IgA, IgE, and IgM levels were observed across all dose levels in healthy participants compared with the matched placebo (Figure 2). The most notable reductions were in serum IgA levels, which decreased by up to 74.7% (600 mg SC), 69.9% (8 mg/kg IV), 61.2% (300 mg SC), and 60.1% (150 mg SC) from baseline by the end of the trial. For IgM, IgE, and IgG, the maximum reductions were achieved at the 8 mg/kg IV dose level, with percent changes from baseline of −54.3% (IgM on D29), −36.4% (IgE on D57), and −38.2% (IgG on D57), respectively. Overall, a dose‐dependent trend emerged after Day 15, with higher doses generally leading to greater reductions in IgG, IgA, and IgM levels. Notably, in the SC cohorts, the 600 mg SC dose produced declines in immunoglobulin levels of comparable magnitude and duration to those observed in the 8 mg/kg IV group.

Mean (SE) percent changes from baseline in serum IgG, IgA, IgE, and IgM concentrations (g/L) in healthy participants following a single subcutaneous or intravenous dose of CM313 or placebo. SE, standard error.

The median percent change from baseline in immune cells in healthy participants is presented in Figure 3. Following a single SC or IV dose of CM313, the percentage of NK cells decreased significantly from baseline levels compared with placebo, consistent with the declining trend of CD38+ NK cells shown in Figure S4. The greatest decrease of NK cells was observed 2 days after administration, with reductions of up to 94.7%, 95.3%, 96.6%, and 96.2% for doses of 150 mg SC, 300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, and 8 mg/kg IV, respectively. The magnitude and duration of PD responses exhibited dose‐dependent trends. At the end of the trial, the percentage of NK cells at the starting dose (150 mg SC) gradually recovered toward baseline levels (−21.7%), whereas the declines at higher doses (300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, and 8 mg/kg IV) persisted, remaining 50.1%, 74.4%, and 82.1% below baseline, respectively. These results indicate the potent activity of CM313 in depleting CD38+ high‐expressing NK cells.

Mean (SE) percent changes from baseline in immune cells in healthy participants following a single subcutaneous or intravenous dose of CM313 or placebo. NK cells, plasma cells, B cells, and T cells were calculated as the percentage of peripheral blood NK cells, plasma cells, B cells, and T cells in total lymphocytes. SE, standard error.

Similarly, CD38+ B cells and T cells were also largely reduced following CM313 administration, whereas the changes in total B cells and T cells revealed an inconsistent trajectory. Generally, B cells exhibited a slight decline from baseline, whereas T cells showed a transient drop shortly after administration, followed by a sustained increase. In the 150 mg SC dose, the percentage of plasma cells fluctuated intensely after administration, with no apparent decline observed; the higher doses (300 and 600 mg SC) displayed an overall decreasing trend. The most substantial decrease of plasma cells was observed at the 600 mg SC dose, and percent changes from baseline were −57.9%, −75.5%, and −70.8% on Days 22, 29, and 57 of the trial, respectively.

Following a single administration of CM313, a rapid and dose‐dependent increase in CD38 receptor occupancy on B cells, T cells, and monocytes was observed, reaching peak levels of 92.8%, 86.9%, and 95.6%, respectively, after 7 days of 8 mg/kg IV administration (Figure 4). The high receptor occupancy was maintained for the subsequent 3 weeks. In the SC cohort, CD38 receptor occupancy at the 600 mg SC dose was generally comparable to that of the 8 mg/kg IV dose.

Immunogenicity

During the trial, 2 out of 33 (6.1%) participants in the CM313 group developed treatment‐emergent ADAs to CM313, with NAbs detected in both. Participant 1 showed ADA positivity on Day 29 and Day 57 following a single dose administered on Day 1, with NAbs detected only on Day 57. Participant 2 had both ADA and NAb positivity detected on Day 57. No ADAs to CM313 were detected in the placebo group. No treatment‐emergent ADAs to rHuPH20 were detected. In addition, no apparent effect of immunogenicity on PK, PD, or safety was observed during the trial.

Discussion

CD38 antibodies have shown therapeutic potential in autoimmune diseases, and systematic clinical trials are warranted to confirm their safety and efficacy. We conducted a Phase 1 study to evaluate the safety, tolerability, PK, PD, and immunogenicity of CM313, a novel humanized anti‐CD38 mAb, in healthy participants. We found that CM313 induced marked reductions in serum immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgE, and IgM), as well as in NK cells and plasma cells, and demonstrated favorable safety and tolerability, with low immunogenicity in healthy participants. These results provide the basis for further clinical investigation of CM313 in patients with autoimmune diseases such as SLE and ITP.

CM313 demonstrated favorable safety and tolerability across all dose levels in healthy participants. All drug‐related TEAEs were mild in severity. No TEAEs leading to early withdrawal or SAEs were observed. Relatively low incidences were noted in the SC cohort, and all IRRs were mild and resolved spontaneously. These findings suggest the possibility of reducing the dose of glucocorticoids as prophylactic medication in future studies. Additionally, participants receiving SC CM313 experienced mild injection site reactions, with an incidence rate of 42.4% in the CM313 cohort; this phenomenon was also reported with another CD38 mAb, TAK‐079, which noted injection site erythema (incidence rate: 30%) and injection site pain (incidence rate: 25%) in healthy participants [19]. The most common non‐hematological TEAEs, aside from injection site reaction, were IRR and upper respiratory tract infection, which have also been reported with other CD38 mAbs [20, 21] and in CM313‐treated ITP patients [12].

CM313 exhibited nonlinear pharmacokinetics, with drug exposure increasing more rapidly than the corresponding dose escalation from 150 to 600 mg in healthy participants. This property has also been observed with other anti‐38 mAbs such as TAK‐079, isatuximab, and daratumumab [19, 20, 21]. The nonlinear PK profile of anti‐CD38 mAbs is primarily attributed to its target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD). At lower doses, anti‐CD38 mAbs bind to CD38 antigens with high affinity and specificity, leading to rapid internalization and degradation of the antigen–antibody complexes and thus accelerated drug clearance. As the dose increases and CD38 binding sites become saturated, clearance shifts to slower, non‐specific pathways, resulting in nonlinear increases in drug exposure. This saturation mechanism is consistent with the observed decrease in CL, prolongation of t 1/2, and a greater‐than‐dose‐proportional increase in AUC with increasing SC dose (Table 3). By route of administration, the 600 mg SC and 8 mg/kg IV doses exhibited similar terminal elimination half lives. The relative bioavailability of the 600 mg SC dose was estimated to be 66% compared with the 8 mg/kg IV dose.

In terms of pharmacodynamics in healthy participants, CM313 substantially reduced serum immunoglobulin levels and key CD38+ immune cells. Similar findings have also been reported for TAK‐079 [19]. Immunoglobulins are primarily produced by plasma cells and are closely involved in autoimmune responses, playing a central role in the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment of autoimmune diseases. As crucial biomarkers for diagnosing and monitoring autoimmune diseases, elevated levels of immunoglobulins, including IgA, IgE, IgG, and IgM, may indicate active disease in clinical practice. In healthy participants, a single dose of subcutaneous or intravenous CM313 induced rapid and sustained decreases in total IgA, IgE, IgG, and IgM levels by 40%–80% from baseline. A dose‐dependent trend was also observed, with higher doses generally leading to greater reductions in serum immunoglobulin (IgG, IgA, and IgM) levels. Furthermore, both SC and IV formulations of CM313 demonstrated potent CD38 binding activity and achieved near‐full CD38 receptor occupancy on T cells, B cells, and monocytes. In the SC cohort, the 600 mg SC dose produced similar PD responses and a comparable duration of effect as the 8 mg/kg IV dose. Although reductions of immune cells and immunoglobulins were observed in healthy participants following CM313 administration, the lack of functional assessments to confirm their clinical relevance, along with the small sample size, limits the further interpretation of this Phase 1 study. Future large‐scale clinical investigation of CM313 in patient populations is needed to substantiate these findings.

Studies have demonstrated that SC injection is often preferred over IV infusion for patients with chronic immune system disorders due to advantages such as reduced administration time, prolonged effects, and the convenience of self‐administration [22, 23]. In this study, we evaluated both SC and IV formulations of CM313. Although the IV formulation of CM313 is already established, this trial specifically focused on assessing the safety of a newly developed SC formulation. The subcutaneous CM313 was co‐formulated with rHuPH20, an excipient added to enhance the penetration and absorption of CM313 mAb. Dose groups were determined based on prior evidence from clinical trials and animal studies [17, 18], including 150 mg SC, 300 mg SC, 600 mg SC, and 8 mg/kg IV, with the 8 mg/kg IV dose representing the highest dose. The SC doses of 150 mg, 300 mg, and 600 mg approximately correspond to IV doses of 2, 4, and 8 mg/kg, respectively. Our results indicated that, compared with the 8 mg/kg IV dose, the 600 mg SC dose caused fewer IRRs while maintaining comparable PD responses. These findings substantiate the potential for further development and clinical application of the subcutaneous CM313 formulation.

In conclusion, CM313 demonstrated favorable safety, tolerability, and nonlinear pharmacokinetics across all dose levels in healthy participants. A single SC or IV dose of CM313 substantially reduced serum immunoglobulin levels and key CD38+ immune cells and achieved near‐full CD38 receptor occupancy on T cells, B cells, and monocytes. Based on these promising results and the immunomodulatory properties of anti‐CD38 antibodies, Phase II trials are underway to evaluate CM313 in patients with SLE and ITP.
