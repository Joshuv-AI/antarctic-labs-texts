Comparison of body composition between thin and normal-weight sarcopenic children: a single-center, propensity score matching analysis of a large-scale study
Authors: Xiao-lin Zhang, Yi Zhou, Yi-ting Ji, Lu Zhao, Jin-xin Gong, Yan Chen, Ling Shen, Shi-zhong Cai, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the incidence of sarcopenia in thin and normal-weight children and analyze their body composition differences.MethodsBody composition was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) in children who visited the Department of Child and Adolescent Healthcare at the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University between September 2016 and September 2024. Sarcopenia was defined as a skeletal muscle mass index (SMMI) at or below the 3rd percentile, and classified as thin sarcopenia or normal-weight sarcopenia based on international body mass index (BMI) cutoff values. Propensity score matching (PSM) balances baseline characteristics, including age and sex.ResultsA total of 41,872 children underwent body composition assessment, of whom 1,546 (3.70%) were diagnosed with sarcopenia. After PSM, the final analysis included 367 normal-weight children with sarcopenia and 734 thin children with sarcopenia. Compared to children in the normal-weight sarcopenia group, those in the Thin Group were significantly taller (P < 0.05), but had lower body weight and body fat levels (P < 0.001). No significant differences were found between the two groups in protein content, inorganic salts, muscle mass, lean mass, skeletal muscle mass, or bone mineral content (P > 0.05). Children in the Thin Group had a higher percentage of skeletal muscle mass, relative muscle content, and muscle-to-fat ratio than the Normal Weight Group (P < 0.001). Similarly, the proportion of fat-free mass (FFM) in the extremities was significantly lower in the Thin Group compared to the Normal Weight Group (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe coexistence rate of sarcopenia and thinness is high. The normal-weight children also require body composition analysis to identify occult sarcopenia. Sarcopenia of different body types presents two distinct physical phenotypes, which may involve different pathological mechanisms and interventions.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Thin children, Propensity score matching method, Body composition

Introduction

Sarcopenia, characterized by age-related loss of muscle mass, muscle strength, and/or physical performance, has long been a participant in research in adult health and disease, particularly in elderly populations. Recently, sarcopenia has also been identified in younger populations, including children and adolescents. Skeletal muscle mass in children is closely associated with bone mineral density and cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [1, 2]. Moreover, inadequate muscle strength during adolescence has been considered an important risk factor for disability by the age of 30 [3] and all-cause mortality by the age of 24 [4]. Despite growing attention, a universally accepted definition, or standardized cutoff values for diagnosing pediatric sarcopenia remain absent, leading to substantial heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria across studies [5]. Most studies use the skeletal muscle mass index (SMMI) for diagnosis. However, this lack of consensus is further evidenced by the variability in percentile-based measures or z-scores used to define low SMMI, as highlighted in a recent scoping review [5], contributing to considerable heterogeneity in reported prevalence rates.

Sarcopenia is caused by the interplay of clinically complex pathophysiological mechanisms. Many different molecular mediators regulate muscle metabolic homeostasis through intracellular signaling pathways. Associated cellular disorders include oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired tissue perfusion, disrupted neuromuscular transmission, elevated proinflammatory cytokines, and endocrine changes, including decreased hormone levels and insulin resistance. These processes are modulated by genetic predisposition and environmental influences [6, 7]. At present, most studies on pediatric sarcopenia have focused on children with definite underlying diseases or who are hospitalized, such as children with liver transplantation or leukemia [8, 9]. In addition, malnutrition, inactivity, and sedentary behavior are common contributing factors [10].

In terms of nutritional status, patients with sarcopenia exhibit heterogeneous phenotypes, including thin, normal weight, or overweight/obese [11, 12]. Body mass index (BMI), due to its convenience and cost-effectiveness, is the most widely used indicator for assessing nutritional status. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), thinness is a BMI below the 5th percentile of reference values for children. In contrast, being overweight is having a BMI above the 85th percentile. Based on pooled global data, Cole et al. proposed a hierarchical classification system and internationally standardized BMI thresholds for thinness and overweight/obesity in children and adolescents [13, 14].

Relying solely on optimal body weight is insufficient to fully assess the nutritional status of children, as body composition (including bone, lean, and fat mass) plays a critical role in children’s development and health [15]. However, BMI reflects only overall body weight without distinguishing between adipose and non-adipose tissues. Recent studies suggest that body composition more accurately reflects fat and skeletal muscle mass, leading to its recommendation as a standard assessment tool for evaluating nutritional status [16].

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is a noninvasive technique that evaluates body composition by applying a weak alternating current to human segments and measuring their impedance (Z) and reactance (Xc). The principle is that lean tissues rich in water and electrolytes have good electrical conductivity, which is manifested as low impedance. Adipose tissue with high lipid content has poor electrical conductivity and high impedance, so the proportion of each component is calculated [17]. Unlike conventional BIA, direct segmental multifrequency BIA (DSM-BIA) utilizes simultaneous currents across multiple frequencies (1–1000 kHz) to quantify fluid compartments-specifically, low-frequency currents penetrate extracellular water (ECW). In contrast, high-frequency currents penetrate intracellular water (ICW). DSM-BIA calculates body composition via biophysical modeling rather than empirical population-specific equations (e.g., age/sex/ethnicity regression models). This approach has emerged as a feasible large-scale screening tool in pediatric populations due to its radiation-free operation, rapid measurement (3 min), and high reproducibility [18].

Nevertheless, measurement accuracy remains influenced by hydration status, electrode-skin contact integrity, and extreme body compositions (e.g., severe edema); thus, stringent protocol standardization is essential for validity assurance [19]. Body composition is strongly associated with body size, and distinguishing between variability in body size and body composition is critical in examining malnutrition’s immediate or long-term effects [20]. To ensure comparability among children with sarcopenia of different body sizes, our study presented height-and weight-adjusted indices for tissue mass. These adjustments are expected to make precise comparisons between different body compositions, independent of variations in body size.

Previous research on sarcopenia in children has predominantly focused on its association with obesity, leaving the distribution of sarcopenia across other body types unclear. Limited attention has been given to body composition in children with sarcopenia who are either of normal weight or thin. Addressing this gap, our study aimed to investigate the distribution of sarcopenia among children with varying body types (thin and normal-weight). Furthermore, we analyzed differences in body composition between these groups to offer a scientific basis for the improved diagnosis, treatment, nutritional supplementation, and exercise interventions.

Patients and methods

Participants and sample

This study is a cross-sectional investigation conducted on a natural population of children who visited the Department of Child and Adolescent Healthcare at the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University between September 2016 and September 2024. Inclusion criteria: Children aged 4–14 years who were able to cooperate in completing physical and body composition measurements. Exclusion criteria: Children with a history of specific diseases or surgical procedures, such as congenital genetic metabolic disorders, suspected skeletal developmental abnormalities, or severe chronic organ diseases; children with metal or electronic implants such as pacemakers, defibrillators, or atrial pulse generators. A total of 41,872 children underwent body composition assessment. After excluding non-sarcopenia, overweight, and obese children, 1,546 (3.70%) children with sarcopenia were included in the study (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (NO: 2021CS092). Informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians.

Diagnose and study design

Sarcopenia was defined as a SMMI ≤ the 3rd percentile (− 1.88 SD) of an age- and gender-matched reference population of healthy children from East China. This diagnostic threshold aligns with the WHO child growth assessment framework [21] and the Asian sarcopenia consensus guidelines [22]. The following outlines the scientific foundation. Statistical validity: The 3rd percentile (− 1.88 SD) represents the threshold for mild anthropometric deviation under normal distribution, consistent with diagnostic approaches for pediatric growth disorders (e.g., height ≤3rd percentile for short stature) [21]. Developmental appropriateness: Percentile-based criteria account for nonlinear muscle mass changes during childhood, enabling dynamic a evaluation of growth deviations [21]. Regional specificity: Thresholds were derived from a local healthy pediatric cohort (n = 32,690), minimizing cross-population diagnostic bias [23]. The SMMI was calculated as skeletal muscle mass divided by height squared (kg/m²). Participants were divided into two groups based on Cole’s international BMI cutoff points [13]: the Thin Group (n = 1,179) and the Normal Weight Group (n = 367). After propensity score matching (PSM), the Thin Group (n = 734) and Normal Weight Group (n = 367) were included in the final analysis.

Methods

Anthropometry

Children were instructed to stand upright without shoes, socks, hats, or heavy clothing, and wore light attire. Trained researchers performed physical measurements, recording height and weight to one decimal place (0.1 cm, 0.1 kg).

Body composition

Body composition was quantified using an InBody J20 analyzer (InBody, Seoul, Korea), a medical-grade multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis device with direct segmental measurement capability. The device employs eight tactile electrodes: two on each hand (thumb and palm) and two on each foot (forefoot and heel). It is made of medical-grade titanium to ensure a stable signal. To minimize disturbance, the indoor environment was maintained at 20 to 25 °C. After fasting for more than 2 h, emptying the bladder, and resting for 30 min, the children wore simple clothes and pants, took off shoes, socks, and metal objects [19]. During the test, the children stood upright and barefoot with their upper limbs at 15 degrees of abduction to avoid torso contact. The equipment was operated by a specially trained physician or nutritionist. Before the daily test, the instrument was standardized to ensure the accuracy of the data, and the equipment was regularly repaired and maintained by professionals. All participants followed uniform preparation procedures to ensure the reliability and validity of the measured data [18]. The following parameters were measured: total body water, protein, minerals, fat mass (FM), fat-free mass (FFM), soft lean mass (comprising skeletal and smooth muscle), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), bone mineral content (BMC), and basal metabolic rate. The above parameters were determined based on the device’s built-in equations.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the distribution of all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) according to the results of normality tests. However, the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that all continuous variables in this study deviated significantly from normality (p < 0.05). Therefore, all continuous data, including baseline characteristics and other measures, are reported as medians (interquartile ranges, IQR) and were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies, and the χ2 test was used.

To mitigate confounding effects, PSM was utilized to balance the two groups. Propensity scores were calculated based on two key covariates strongly associated with sarcopenia risk and body composition in children: age and sex. PSM was performed using a nearest-neighbor 1:2 matching method (normal weight: thin). All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was p < 0.05. Data analysis was conducted using Python 3.8 and SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Post-hoc power analysis was performed within the Mann-Whitney U framework. The effect size was calculated as .

Representative indicators across the effect size spectrum were analyzed: (1) Fat Mass Index (maximum effect size); (2) Fat-Free Mass Index (minimum body composition effect); (3) Height (smallest significant effect). Power calculations used G*Power 3.1 with one-tailed testing at α = 0.05.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

All participants were non-overweight/obese, per the study inclusion/exclusion criteria. A total of 1,546 children with thin and normal-weight sarcopenia were enrolled in the study (detection rate was 3.70%). Of these, 911 were boys (58.93%) and 635 were girls (41.07%). The median age of all participants was 8.30 years (IQR 6.50–9.90). All 1,546 sarcopenic children were stratified into two phenotypes: thin sarcopenia (n = 1,179, 76.26%) and normal-weight sarcopenia (n = 367, 23.74%). Statistical power verification

Post-hoc power analysis demonstrated robust detection capability for body composition differences. For core body composition indicators, statistical power exceeded 99.8% across the effect size spectrum. Maximum effect: Fat Mass Index (|Z|=25.224, r = 0.76, power = 1.000); Minimum body composition effect: Fat-Free Mass Index (|Z|=7.827, r = 0.24, power = 0.998). All other body composition metrics showed excellent power (≥ 0.998) with effect sizes ranging from r = 0.24 to r = 0.76. For the anthropometric indicator with the smallest significant effect (height, |Z|=2.443, r = 0.074), power was 0.650. Despite this limitation, statistical significance was achieved (P = 0.015), indicating sufficient model sensitivity to detect clinically relevant differences.

Discussion

Early identification of sarcopenia in children is important because it implies that long-term musculoskeletal, metabolic, and functional consequences may be prevented through early intervention [3, 4]. At the same time, understanding its distribution in different body types can help to develop targeted screening and management strategies. Currently, there are no standardized criteria for diagnosing sarcopenia in children. Our study defines children with sarcopenia because SMMI is lower than the third percentile (-1.88 SD) of the average value of normal children of the same age, gender, and race in similar living environments. This definition was based on SMM reference values and growth curves established by Cao et al. [23] for children in Nanjing, China. Considering the similarity in ethnicity and living conditions between populations in Nanjing and Suzhou in China, our study calculated the detection rate of sarcopenia among the study participants to be 3.70%. Diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia in children vary widely across studies, leading to substantial differences in reported prevalence rates. For instance, some studies have defined sarcopenia as “skeletal muscle mass or strength below the 5th percentile“ [24–26] or a “z-score less than − 2“ [8, 27]. Using these criteria, prevalence rates have been reported from 25% to 50%, significantly higher than the findings of our study. This discrepancy may be attributed to studies focusing on pediatric populations with specific diseases. Burrows et al. [28, 29] conducted studies in healthy children, defining sarcopenia by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and the 25th percentile of gender-adjusted FFMI outcomes. In comparison, their reported prevalence rates ranged from 25.1% to 54.1%. One hundred five children diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) were included in the national prospective study conducted by Verwaaijen et al. [30]. Sarcopenia was defined as a standardized deviation score (SDS) of ≤-1.5 in appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASMM), measured using bioelectrical impedance, along with grip strength assessed via the Jamar dynamometer. The prevalence rate of sarcopenia among these children was found to be 2.8%. Additionally, McCarthy et al. [31] and Kim et al. [32] used the MFR to establish diagnostic thresholds. Kim et al. reported prevalence rates of 0.1% in boys and 3.8% in girls when the mean-2SD cutoff was applied; when the cutoff was adjusted to mean-1SD, these rates increased significantly to 32.1% and 24.3%. Systematic reviews by Inoue et al. [5] indicate that ​discrepancies in diagnostic thresholds​ contribute to ​extremely broad estimates of pediatric sarcopenia prevalence​ (0.1%–54.1%) [24–27, 31, 32], ​outcomes. At the same time, the generalizability of the results of the study was questioned. While our SMMI-based definition leveraged ​population-specific normative data from a geographically comparable cohort [23] to improve validity, ​the omission of standardized functional evaluations​ (e.g., grip strength) ​potentially underestimates the disease burden. Critically, inconsistent diagnostic frameworks hinder direct comparisons across studies, especially those using divergent thresholds or muscle function parameters. To address these gaps, ​three priorities emerge for future work: (i) develop internationally harmonized standards for muscle mass (e.g., SMMI/MFR) and functional measures (e.g., grip strength/gait speed); (ii) establishing specific diagnostic cut-off values for children of different nationalities and races; (iii) prospectively validating these criteria against ​long-term health outcomes—ultimately enabling ​timely screening and targeted interventions​ to support healthy development.

Among the children with sarcopenia included in this study, who were not overweight or obese, 76.26% were classified as thin. The high co-morbidity between underweight status and sarcopenia corresponds with previous studies [33, 34], which suggest that insufficient dietary protein or amino acids to support immune function may contribute to decreased muscle mass. Sarcopenia and malnutrition share similar pathophysiological mechanisms, including a low-grade state of inflammation and negative nitrogen balance [35]. Malnutrition not only induces sarcopenia but is also part of its definition. This study further confirmed the importance of nutritional intervention in the treatment of thin sarcopenia in children [36]. Notably, a significant proportion of children (23.74%) with sarcopenia had normal-weight values, consistent with observations by McCarthy et al. [31]. Their study highlighted the relationship between BIA-assessed muscle mass and BMI in children and adolescents, suggesting that BMI may not be a reliable indicator of muscle mass in clinical practice alone. Further research is necessary to apply body composition analysis techniques more comprehensively, such as BIA, to identify children with “masked” sarcopenia, particularly among those within the normal BMI range.

According to the two-component model, body composition comprises FM (adipose tissue) and FFM (non-adipose tissue). FFM includes BMC and muscle, which are critical for bone health and metabolism. On the other hand, FM significantly influences children’s growth and pubertal development. This study compared the body composition characteristics of children with normal-weight and thin sarcopenia. Children with thin sarcopenia showed reduced body weight and FM compared to those with normal-weight sarcopenia, reflecting a lack of fat reserves. Previous studies [37] have similarly reported that underweight children tend to have lower FM, lean mass, muscle mass, and muscle strength compared to normal-weight counterparts. Interestingly, our findings revealed no significant differences in lean weight, FFM, SMM, or BMC between children with sarcopenia in the Thin and Normal Weight Groups. Human development indicators are interrelated. For example, skeletal muscle mass growth is influenced not only by age and sex but also by weight, height, and FM, making it challenging to assess nutritional status using a single indicator comprehensively. To address this issue, two or more indicators have been linked by mathematical formulas to assess nutritional status, body size, and other relevant factors, which can express the proportions and relationships between different components of the human body. SMM% reflects the proportion of skeletal muscle to total body weight and the individual’s muscle level.

The MFR represents the balance between lean body mass and fat mass. At the same time, the relative SMMI, akin to BMI, quantifies skeletal muscle mass per unit area and reflects overall body size development. Torres-Costoso et al. [12] recently described two phenotypes at risk for sarcopenia in healthy children aged 10–18 years, including: a low BMI sarcopenia risk phenotype, a high BMI and FMI sarcopenia risk phenotype (i.e., sarcopenic obesity), However, both sarcopenia phenotypes are characterized by poor musculoskeletal fitness and low muscle strength. Our Table 3 findings reveal analogous phenotypic profiles, with thin sarcopenia exhibiting elevated MFR that reflect adaptive muscle preservation during nutritional stress, mechanistically akin to cachectic adaptations. Conversely, normal-weight sarcopenia demonstrates increased adiposity markers (waist-hip ratio; FMI) coupled with fat-muscle disproportion—characterized by elevated fat mass concurrent with reduced muscle mass—thus phenotypically aligning with pediatric sarcopenic obesity and indicating underlying metabolic dysregulation [28].

Furthermore, age-stratified analysis revealed that the differences in body composition between lean children and those with normal weight sarcopenia were consistently observed across the preschool group (4–6 years), the school-age group (7–9 years), and the pubertal group (10–14 years). Prospective research evidence suggests a link between these conditions and potential health risks. Lower relative SMMI is significantly associated with diseases such as Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [37] and cardiovascular disease [38]. Similarly, low relative muscle mass is a risk factor for poor clinical outcomes in hospitalized patients [39]. Conversely, higher MFR is associated with improved glucose tolerance in prediabetes [40]. Therefore, comprehensive body composition indicators should be prioritized in clinical assessment to assess children’s nutritional status and health risks comprehensively. Future interventions should distinguish between different phenotypes; that is, nutritional supplementation should be paid attention to for children with sarcopenia. At the same time, dietary modification combined with resistance training should be prioritized for normal-weight sarcopenia.

Additionally, this study analyzed the local FM and FFM distribution characteristics in children between different BMI groups. The results showed that children in the Thin Group had significantly lower FM and FFM in the limbs than those in the Normal Weight Group, revealing underdevelopment of muscles and a lack of fat reserves. The proportion of FFM in the buttocks in this study was not significantly different from that of the Normal Weight Group, suggesting that under nutritional deficiency conditions, FFM in adipose tissue and limbs may be depleted first. At the same time, FFM in the trunk is preserved. These results are in line with the study by Wells et al. [41], which revealed that adolescents with anorexia nervosa had reduced FM and FFM compared to the control group by MRI. After standardized height, regional analysis showed that FM was significantly reduced in the limbs and trunk, while the reduction in FFM occurred mainly in the limb region.

This study has the following limitations: First, our single-center design, which recruits participants exclusively from a tertiary university hospital in Eastern China, may limit the generalizability of findings to broader pediatric populations, community settings, or diverse healthcare systems. Second, we could not directly examine the effect of puberty on body composition because of the lack of data on pubertal stages. Although age stratification was substituted, subsequent studies are needed to assess pubertal development directly. Third, the lack of functional measures, such as grip strength, makes it difficult to fully confirm the sarcopenia phenotype in this study. It may underestimate the true prevalence or functional impact. Future studies should incorporate these functional measures. Fourth, while PSM balanced the groups on age and sex, the lack of data on potential confounders like physical activity levels and detailed nutritional intake limits our ability to fully account for all factors influencing body composition differences between the groups. Future studies should incorporate these measures. Fifth, although the DSM-BIA in InBody J20 is noninvasive, inexpensive, and requires no empirical formula, making it suitable for large-scale pediatric studies, it is affected by physiological status and less accurate than DXA or MRI, which may lead to absolute value errors and affect group comparisons. In the future, DXA, MRI, and other technologies should be introduced to verify the key results.

Conclusions

Based on a large sample of children (n = 41,872), this study revealed that the coexistence rate of sarcopenia and thinness was high (76.26%), but 23.74% of the children with sarcopenia showed normal-weight patterns with normal BMI, confirming that sarcopenia was not a disease exclusively for children with thinness. BMI measures have limitations, and children with normal BMI may also require body composition screening. Although the two types of children have similar muscle mass loss, they have different phenotypes: thinness type (low muscle mass and low fat) and normal weight type (low muscle mass and normal/high fat). This suggests that the two types of children may have different pathological mechanisms (such as nutritional deficiency vs. insufficient exercise/metabolic disorder) and need different interventions. Future studies should include functional indicators to improve diagnosis, verify the effects of different intervention programs, and establish a long-term management network of family, school, and medical institutions.
