Structural and functional changes related to cognition in patients with early subcortical ischemic vascular disease: A cross-sectional MRI study
Authors: Xiaoshuang Liu, Runtian Cheng, Li Chen, Junwei Gong, Tianyou Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subcortical ischemic vascular disease (SIVD) is a key contributor to vascular cognitive impairment, yet early-stage SIVD is underexplored despite its link to cognitive decline. This study investigates structural and functional changes in SIVD patients with and without cognitive impairment. Twenty-five individuals of subcortical ischemic vascular disease with normal cognition, 25 individuals with subcortical ischemic vascular cognitive impairment (SVCI), and 23 normal controls were enrolled. Voxel-based morphometry was used to assess differences in gray matter volume (GMV) across 3 groups by one-way analysis of variance. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between voxel-based morphometry-identified seeds and all brain voxels derived from the resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging analyses to evaluate the alterations of functional connectivity (FC). Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between altered GMV, abnormal FC, and cognitive assessments. Compared with normal controls, the SVCI group showed significant reductions in GMV in prefrontal regions (orbitofrontal cortex, gyrus rectus, inferior frontal gyrus), temporal lobe (olfactory cortex, superior temporal gyrus), insula, and subcortical areas (thalamus, caudate). The subcortical ischemic vascular disease with normal cognition group exhibited GMV loss in the right orbitofrontal cortex. FC analyses revealed hypoconnectivity in cortical-subcortical circuits as well as ipsilateral and contralateral cognitive-related regions. Several GMV and FC alterations in the SVCI group were correlated with neuropsychological assessments, including the Stroop Color-Word Test II and Trail Making Test B. Early-stage SIVD patients exhibit structural and functional abnormalities linked to cognitive deficits. The right orbitofrontal cortex may serve as a potential imaging biomarker for early detection of SIVD.

1. Introduction

Subcortical ischemic vascular disease (SIVD) is a major cause of subcortical vascular cognitive impairment (SVCI),[1] which ranges from nearly normal cognition to dementia. Early SIVD may present without obvious deficits but carries a high risk of future impairment.[2] Understanding the pathological mechanisms during this preclinical stage is essential for early diagnosis, timely intervention, and improved prognosis.

The pathogenesis of SIVD involves both neuroanatomical alterations and disrupted functional connectivity (FC).[3,4] Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) detects gray matter (GM) loss with high regional specificity,[5] and frontal cortical thinning is linked to executive dysfunction.[6] The thalamus, middle prefrontal cortex, and caudate nucleus (CAU) – key nodes in frontal-subcortical and cortico-striato-thalamocortical circuits – are particularly vulnerable and may underlie cognitive decline.[7,8]

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) captures low-frequency blood oxygenation level-dependent signal fluctuations and, via seed-based analysis, reveals FC alterations related to spontaneous neural activity.[9] Such changes have been observed in Alzheimer disease, Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, and vascular disorders.[10–12] In SIVD patients, reduced FC in frontoparietal networks and altered thalamus–orbitofrontal connectivity are associated with neuropsychological deficits.[13] Recent studies have extended these findings using multimodal neuroimaging approaches, showing concurrent cortical atrophy and network-level functional alterations in subcortical vascular cognitive impairment.[14,15] These findings highlight the importance of investigating both GM and FC to better understand the neural mechanisms underlying cognitive decline in SIVD.

Although studies using VBM and rs-fMRI have reported structural and functional GM changes in SIVD,[16,17] most focus on moderate-to-severe stages. Early neural alterations may precede overt symptoms, providing a window of opportunity for prevention. Therefore, this cross-sectional neuroimaging study combined VBM and rs-fMRI to investigate structural and functional changes among patients with SIVD and different cognitive statuses – those with subcortical ischemic vascular disease with normal cognition (SVCN) and SVCI – and normal controls (NCs). We hypothesized that early SIVD would exhibit both GM atrophy and FC disruptions in cognition-related brain regions, and that these imaging changes would correlate with neuropsychological performance.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

The patients were recruited from the outpatient and inpatient departments who underwent conventional head MRI that met the inclusion criteria of SIVD. Finally, a total of 76 participants were recruited, including 26 SIVD patients with cognitive normality (SVCN), 27 SIVD patients with cognitive impairment (SVCI), and 23 NCs matched for age, gender, and education. Informed consent was acquired from every participant involved in the study. All methods were carried out in strict compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with SIVD were recruited according to the criteria[1] are as follows: hyperintensities extending into periventricular and deep white matter (WM) range from extending caps or irregular halos to diffusely confluent hyperintensities or diffuse hyperintensities without focal lesions; more than 5 lacunas in the deep GM, accompanied by moderate WM lesions; and absence of hemorrhages, cortical, territorial, and/or watershed infarcts, and specific causes of WM lesions.

Based on the above SIVD criteria, patients with SVCI were included according to the following[18]: participants’ or carers’ admitted subjective cognitive complaints; objective cognitive deficits not meet the dementia defined by the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders, fifth edition; clinical dementia rating = 0.5; and mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score more than 24.

SIVD patients with normal cognition (clinical dementia rating = 0; MMSE ≥ 27) were included in the SVCN group. The NC had normal cognitive function and no brain abnormalities, as determined by conventional MRI.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: metabolic conditions, psychiatric disorders, and other nervous system diseases that influence cognitive functions. MR scanning contraindications were excluded from the study.

2.2. Neuropsychological assessment

Every participant underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, which encompassed MMSE, Stroop Color-Word Test parts I and II (SCWT-I and SCWT-II), digital span test, and Trail Making Tests parts A and B (TMT-A and TMT-B).

2.3. MRI acquisition

The high-resolution 3D-T1 scan parameters included: repetition time of 8.3 ms, echo time of 3.3 ms, slice thickness/gap of 1.0/0 mm, field of view of 240 × 240 mm, flip angle of 15°, and a matrix setting of 240 × 240, with a voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. The rs-fMRI data were captured using an echo-planar imaging pulse sequence, characterized by a repetition time of 2000 ms, echo time of 40 ms, slice thickness/gap of 4.0/0 mm, field of view of 240 × 240 mm, 64 × 64 matrix, and a flip angle of 90°.

2.4. Imaging analyses

2.4.1. VBM analyses

VBM analyses were measured using the VBM8 toolbox in statistical parametric mapping (SPM) software.[19] Firstly, GM, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid of T1-weighted images were analyzed using the standard unified segmentation module in SPM8. Next, a template specific to the group was generated using the diffeomorphic anatomical registration through the exponentiated lie algebra approach. This method was applied for registering the affine-aligned segments of GM and WM. Thirdly, the individual segments of GM and WM were aligned to the Montreal Neurological Institute standard space. In the fourth step, the images underwent modulation to maintain relative volume and were adjusted for brain size. Lastly, the segmented, modulated, and normalized GM images were subjected to smoothing with a 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with age, gender, and education as covariates was used to examine the regions of changed gray matter volume (GMV) among 3 groups with a 0.2 absolute masking threshold to the VBM data. Then, post hoc individual tests were further conducted within a mask made from the ANOVA results to detect differences between each pair of groups. The threshold for statistical significance was established at P < .05, with family-wise error (FWE) correction applied for multiple comparisons. The altered GMV brain regions were applied as the seeds for FC analyses.

2.4.2. Resting-state FC analyses

fMRI preprocessing was conducted using the data processing assistant for rs-fMRI based on SPM8. Steps included: discarding the first 10 timepoints; slice timing correction; head motion correction (exclusion if > 3.0 mm translation or > 3.0° rotation). The mean framewise displacement was also calculated to characterize the instantaneous head motion of each volume, and there was no significant difference in the mean framewise displacement among 3 groups; spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute space (echo-planar imaging template; voxel size 3 × 3 × 3 mm3); 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian smoothing; detrending and bandpass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz); regression of WM and cerebrospinal fluid signals to reduce motion and nonneuronal noise.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the time series of VBM-identified seed regions and all brain voxels, then converted to z-scores via Fisher transformation. Statistical analyses in SPM8 involved one-way ANOVA with age, gender, and education as covariates, followed by post hoc tests within the ANOVA-derived mask. Significance was set at voxel-level P < .001 and cluster-level P < .05 (FWE corrected).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Demographic data, MRI metrics, and cognitive test results were analyzed across 3 groups using SPSS 23.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk). Differences in continuous variables among the groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc multiple comparison tests. Partial eta squared (ηp2) was used to quantify the effect size of group differences in GMV and FC. For nonparametric data, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied, while the chi-square test was employed for the analysis of sex distribution.

Spearman correlation analyses, accounting for age, gender, and education, were conducted to investigate the associations between altered GMV, average values of abnormal FC, and cognitive evaluations. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < .05 established as the threshold for statistical significance. Considering multiple testing, P-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, setting a significance level at Pc < .005.

3. Results

One SVCN patient and 2 SVCI patients were excluded because of excessive head motion for fMRI preprocessing, resulting in 25 SVCN patients, 25 SVCI patients, and 23 NC for further study.

3.2. VBM analyses

Figure 1 and Table 2 present the significant alterations in regional GMV among 3 groups (P < .05, FWE corrected for multiple comparisons). When compared with the NC group, the GM reductions in the SVCI group were detected at prefrontal brain regions, including the right orbital frontal cortex (OFC.R), the right gyrus rectus, and the left inferior frontal gyrus; at the temporal lobe, including the right olfactory cortex and the left superior temporal gyrus (STG.L); at the right insula cortex (INS.R); and at the right thalamus. We also found significantly decreased GMV in the OFC.R of the SVCN group compared with that in the NC group. Additionally, increased GMV was identified in the bilateral caudate nuclei (CAU) in the patient group. There was no significant difference between the SVCN group and the SVCI group.

3.3. Resting-state FC analyses

Figure 2 and Table 3 illustrate significant hypoconnectivity between the left caudate nucleus (CAU.L, Seed 1) and the left supplementary motor area (SMA.L). The INS.R (Seed 2) and STG.L (Seed 3) were found to have abnormal connectivity with the same brain regions, which were the right superior temporal gyrus (STG.R), the bilateral postcentral gyrus (PoCG), and the left median cingulate and the paracingulate gyri (DCG.L). Post hoc pairwise comparisons between the SVCI group and the NC group showed hypoconnectivity similar to that observed in the ANOVA results. When compared with the SVCN group, significantly decreased FC was also identified between the CAU.L and the SMA.L and between the INS.R and the PoCG.L in the SVCI group (P < .001 at voxel level and P < .05, FWE cluster-level corrected for multiple comparisons). We could not find any significant alterations between the SVCN group and the NC group.

3.4. Correlation analyses

The relationship between the neuropsychological assessments, changes in the GMV and altered FC in the SVCI group are presented in Figure 3. We detected that GMV deficits in the INS.R (r = −0.58, P = .005) and STG.L (r = −0.63, P = .002) exhibited a significant negative correlation with the SCWT-II. The FC decreases between the STG.L and STG.R (r = −0.63, P = .002) and between the INS.R and DCG.L (r = −0.61, P = .003) were negatively correlated with the TMT-B. These correlations remained significant after applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (Pc < .005).

4. Discussion

Our study combined rs-fMRI and structural MRI to compare the SVCI, SVCN, and NC groups. We found: GMV reductions in prefrontal, temporal, limbic, and subcortical regions related to cognition; reduced FC between GMV-altered areas and other cognitive-related regions in SIVD; and neuropsychological scores correlated with both GM loss and FC changes. Even at the preclinical stage, SVCN patients showed structural and functional alterations, while SVCI patients exhibited more extensive atrophy and pronounced FC disruptions. The identification of structural and functional alterations in SVCN patients, prior to the onset of overt cognitive symptoms, underscores the critical importance of early neuroimaging detection. Targeting this preclinical phase could open a vital window for initiating timely interventions, potentially slowing disease progression and improving long-term patient management.

In VBM analyses, the SVCI group showed GM deficits in the middle prefrontal cortex right gyrus rectus, OFC.R, and left inferior frontal gyrus compared with the other groups, while the OFC.R also showed GM loss in SVCN versus NC. These frontal regions are critical for executive functions such as working memory and attention.[20,21] Consistent with this, SVCI patients had the greatest GM loss and poorest cognitive scores, in line with reports linking frontal thinning to executive dysfunction in SVCI.[15] Within the frontal-subcortical circuit, the OFC plays a key role in regulating executive, attentional, and memory functions[22]; its atrophy in SVCN suggests it may serve as an early biomarker for SIVD. Its detection could enable the identification of at-risk individuals during the preclinical stage. This early detection is crucial as it would allow for the implementation of management strategies – such as rigorous vascular risk factor control and targeted cognitive interventions – at a time when they are most likely to preserve cognitive function and alter the clinical trajectory of SIVD.

Additional GM deficits occurred in the right olfactory cortex and STG.L. Medial temporal atrophy, a recognized AD marker, has also been observed in SIVD,[23,24] possibly due to strong connections with the prefrontal cortex supporting verbal memory and naming skills. GM loss in STG.L correlated negatively with SCWT-II scores, consistent with prior findings,[15,25] suggesting its role in cognitive regulation processes.

We also found atrophy in the INS.R, a hub integrating sensory, emotional, motivational, and cognitive processes.[26] Its GM loss was negatively associated with SCWT-II performance, potentially reflecting ischemic injury and related cognitive decline. Subcortical changes included bilateral caudate nuclei hypertrophy and right thalamic alterations, implicating cortico-striato-thalamocortical circuit disruption.[27] While neurodegenerative studies report caudate atrophy,[28,29] our finding (caudate hypertrophy) may reflect vulnerability to WMH and neuroinflammation in SIVD.

In the FC analyses, both SIVD groups showed significant reductions – mainly between the CAU.L and SMA.L – compared with the NC group. The supplementary motor area, involved in attention control and motor planning, links cognition to action.[30,31] This decreased FC may reflect severe cognitive dysfunction associated with motor-executive disorders in SIVD. Additional hypoconnectivity was observed in adjacent ipsilateral and distant contralateral regions. The median cingulate and paracingulate gyri (DCG) are part of the default mode network, a significant cognitive network often considered a central hub for cognition. It plays a pivotal role in various cognitive processes.[32] Yu et al[33] reported topological abnormalities in brain regions of the STG, DCG, and PoCG in patients with SVCI and found that the whole network clustering coefficient in the DCG was negatively related to cognitive examinations. In our study, the decreased FC value between the INS.R and the left DCG was significantly correlated with the TMT-B, which was corroborated in previous studies.

Although the superior temporal gyrus (STG) is not a core cognitive hub, it plays roles in attention and information processing.[34,35] We found bilateral STG FC alterations negatively correlated with TMT-B performance, suggesting its contribution to SVCI progression. The insula cortex, which integrates sensory, emotional, motivational, and cognitive systems,[26] is vulnerable to SIVD, and its hypoperfusion relates to cognitive decline.[36] In our study, the right insula showed reduced FC with both sensory regions (bilateral PoCG) and cognitive areas (STG, DCG), with negative correlations to TMT-B, implicating its role in cognitive dysfunction.

This study has limitations. The small sample size and cross-sectional design may limit sensitivity and accuracy. Cognitive impairment in our SIVD cohort was mostly mild, possibly underrepresenting disease mechanisms. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs with larger samples, including patients with moderate-to-severe impairment. Additionally, as WMH are characteristic of SIVD and linked to cortical atrophy[37] and network dysfunction,[38] future work should investigate WMH–SIVD relationships to clarify disease pathogenesis. Moreover, it is important to investigate the interplay between diabetes, hypertension, and cognitive impairment in SIVD. Although these comorbidities are recognized as pivotal risk factors,[39,40] their exact function within the pathological process – whereby SIVD induces brain changes that lead to cognitive deficits – is not yet understood. Our subsequent research will be aimed at uncovering the mechanisms through which these conditions mediate or moderate this relationship.

5. Conclusion

Our study used VBM and FC analyses of fMRI to explore the brain abnormalities of SIVD patients with SVCI and SVCN. GMV alterations and aberrant FC were detected in both cortical and subcortical brain regions. GM deficits of the OFC.R were identified in the SVCN group and might be treated as sensitive biomarkers for the preclinical phase of the disease. We also found brain abnormalities in the bilateral STG, INS.R, and DCG.L, which were associated with cognitive impairment. Our study demonstrates that integrated MRI techniques can detect subtle yet impactful brain alterations in SIVD, even before clinical decline. The correlation between these imaging biomarkers and cognitive performance strengthens their potential utility in clinical practice. While these preliminary findings are promising, their clinical applicability must be rigorously tested. Future studies in larger, independent cohorts are essential to validate these results and confirm the reliability of the identified neuroimaging biomarkers in tracking disease progression.
