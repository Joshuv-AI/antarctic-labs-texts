Biosynthesis and antibacterial activity of selenium nanoparticles using Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Bacillus pumilus RMO6
Authors: Elina Heidari, Morteza Milani, Farhad Bani, Faezeh Milani, Abolfazl Akbarzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial infections are common and challenging to treat due to increasing antibiotic resistance. Research has focused on using various nanoparticles, with studies showing that Selenium Nanoparticles (Se-NPs) have antibacterial effects. This study focuses on the biosynthesis and characterization of Se-NPs using Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Bacillus pumilus RMO6. Bacteria were cultivated on the microbial culture media, followed by the synthesis of Se-NPs through the reduction of sodium selenite in the bacterial supernatant. Characterization techniques, including UV-Vis spectroscopy, FT-IR, XRD, TEM imaging, and DLS, confirmed the successful formation of Se-NPs, revealing their size, morphology, and surface properties. The Biosynthesized nanoparticles exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against four standard bacterial strains: Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis. Notably, Se-NPs synthesized by Bacillus pumilus demonstrated a significantly lower MIC and greater effectiveness in biofilm eradication (MBEC) compared to those produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, with a fourfold reduction in MIC against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. These findings highlight the potential of Se-NPs as effective agents in combating resistant bacterial infections and suggest their promising application in medical treatments.

Introduction

In the early 20th century, microbial infections were the leading cause of death worldwide, and the discovery of antimicrobial drugs was a significant advance in medical science, especially in the field of pharmacy. But, over time, the increased use of antibiotics to control infectious agents led to the emergence of antibiotic resistance, forcing doctors to resort to stronger antimicrobial agents or higher doses of antibiotics to treat infectious diseases. E. coli is responsible for various infections in humans, including urinary tract infections, food poisoning, septicemia, and meningitis [1]. It is crucial to control infections caused by this bacterium due to its potential to develop drug resistance and cause a range of diseases. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an opportunistic bacterium that can cause a variety of diseases, particularly in intensive care units [2]. S. aureus is a leading cause of a range of human diseases, from skin and soft tissue infections to life-threatening conditions like septicemia and pneumonia. Consequently, the effective treatment of these infections is a critical concern in clinical practice [3]. E. faecalis is known for its intrinsic resistance and the possibility of transferring resistance genes to other bacteria has garnered significant attention [4]. The presence of biofilm production and other virulence factors poses a significant challenge in controlling and eliminating pathogenic bacteria. To combat this issue, antimicrobial compounds and drugs that can penetrate the biofilm layer and eliminate it are being considered as a useful strategy [5]. Nanotechnology faces the challenge of synthesizing nanoparticles of varying sizes and shapes. While physical and chemical methods are commonly used, concerns over particle stability and the use of toxic chemicals limit their applicability in various fields, including medicine [6, 7]. Biological methods offer a safer, cost-effective, and eco-friendly alternative, but controlling particle shape, size, and crystallinity is difficult. More recent studies have focused on the biosynthesis of selenium, silver, and gold nanoparticles using bacteria, with increasing attention towards the biological production of semiconductor nanoparticles like selenium, zinc sulfide, and cadmium [8, 9].

Selenium, an essential element in numerous enzymes and proteins, carries out its biological roles mainly through seleno-proteins in the body. To date, over 30 different seleno-proteins have been identified, with more than 20 of them containing seleno-cysteine residues. Selenium is involved in a wide range of biological functions, including antioxidant activity, boosting the immune system, regulating thyroid function, anti-cancer benefits, protecting cardiovascular health, enhancing reproductive function, and exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects [10, 11]. Selenium compounds have been extensively studied due to their low toxicity and anticancer properties, as well as their role as effective antioxidants [12, 13]. Additionally, Se-NPs have demonstrated antimicrobial activity against various types of bacteria [14, 15].

Previous study demonstrated the significant advantages of using bacteria-synthesized nanoparticles, including their high stability, potent antimicrobial properties, and smaller size compared to chemical and green synthesis methods. In that study, we successfully synthesized silver nanoparticles using new strains (NCBI Bank called ROM6 with access number MW440543) isolated from soil [16]. The objective of this study was to synthesize Se-NPs using both a recent strain and a clinical isolate of P. aeruginosa, followed by assessing the antimicrobial effects of these nanoparticles on four pathogenic strains including E., P. aeruginosa, S. aureus and E. faecalis.

Materials and methods

Chemicals, media, and bacterial strains

Nutrient agar, nutrient broth, Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, and Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) were obtained from Merck (Germany). Analytical-grade potassium dihydrogen phosphate, potassium nitrate, absolute ethanol, Tween 80, octanol, ferrous sulfate, and potassium bromide were also purchased from Merck. Dichloromethane, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), chloroform, sodium selenite, crystal violet, and glycerol were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Standard bacterial strains, including E. coli (ATCC 25922), P. aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), S. aureus (ATCC 25923), and E. faecalis (ATCC 33186), were supplied by the Iranian Research Organization for Science and Technology (IROST).

Selenium nanoparticles synthesis

Se-NPs were biosynthesized following an optimized microbial reduction method. Briefly, 20 µL of bacterial suspension adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) was inoculated into 100 mL of LB broth and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under shaking conditions. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was sterilized through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. Subsequently, 50 mL of the cell-free supernatant was mixed with 5 mL of 20 mM sodium selenite solution, and the pH was adjusted to 7. The mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 3–6 days, during which a visible color change from colorless to brick red indicated nanoparticle formation [17]. It is important to mention that Se- Se-NPs were synthesized independently using two isolates, P. aeruginosa and B. pumilus RMO6.

Characterization of synthesized selenium nanoparticles

The optical properties of the synthesized Se-NPs were evaluated using UV–Vis spectroscopy (Jasco, V-730). Functional group composition was examined by Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Inc.), and crystalline structure was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (TONGA XRD, TD-3700). The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS). Morphological characteristics were further examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Zeiss EM900, operated at 80 kV).

Microbial assay

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC of Se-NPs against standard bacterial strains was determined using a broth microdilution assay. In a sterile 96-well microtiter plate, 150 µL of MHB was added to each well. A Se-NP stock solution (80 µg/mL) was serially diluted twofold across the wells. Then, 15 µL of a bacterial suspension (0.5 McFarland) prepared in sterile normal saline was added to each well. The plates were gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Bacterial growth was visually assessed. Negative controls contained medium with nanoparticles but no bacteria, whereas positive controls contained bacteria without nanoparticles. All tests were performed in duplicate for each bacterial strain and nanoparticle type.

Determination of minimal biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC)

Biofilm eradication was assessed using a modified microtiter plate assay. Briefly, 150 µL of cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth (CAMHB) supplemented with 15% glucose was dispensed into each well, followed by 150 µL of Se-NP solution (80 µg/mL) serially diluted across eight wells. Each well received 15 µL of bacterial suspension, and plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. After incubation, wells were gently washed three times with 300 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic cells. Biofilms were fixed with 150 µL of methanol for 20 min and air-dried for 30 min. The wells were stained with 150 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min at room temperature, washed with distilled water, and dried inverted. Bound dye was solubilized with 150 µL of 33% acetic acid and incubated for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (ELISA reader). Wells containing only culture medium served as negative controls, and acetic acid alone was used as a blank. This experiment was duplicated and the results were interpreted as follows: no biofilm formation (0); OD ≤ ODc, weak form (+); OD ≤ 2 ODc, medium (++); 2ODc < OD≤4OD, strong formation (+++) [18].

Result and discussion

The FT-IR spectra of Se-NPs synthesized by B. pumilus and P. aeruginosa are presented in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. The spectra reveal the presence of various functional groups in both bacterial metabolites, which likely contribute to the reduction and stabilization of the nanoparticles. Although the composition and concentration of compounds differ between the two metabolites, similar functional groups were identified in both cases [19]. As shown in Table 1, the FT-IR spectrum of Se-NPs synthesized by B. pumilus displays characteristic absorption peaks at 562, 1072, 1643, 2925, and 3427 cm−1. These peaks correspond, respectively, to C–N–C bending vibrations in amines, C–N stretching vibrations of amines, C=O stretching of carbonyl groups, C–H stretching vibrations, and O–H stretching of alcohols and phenols. Similar functional groups were also observed in the Se-NPs synthesized by P. aeruginosa.

UV-visible spectroscopy analysis (Fig. 3) revealed that Se-NPs synthesized by B. pumilus exhibited a broad absorption peak between 350 and 450 nm after 6 days, which is attributed to the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of the Se-NPs. In contrast, Se-NPs synthesized by P. aeruginosa showed weaker absorbance in this wavelength range at the same dilution, indicating that B. pumilus metabolites facilitate more efficient NPs synthesis compared to those of P. aeruginosa [20].

DLS analysis revealed that the number-weighted mean hydrodynamic diameters of Se-NPs synthesized from B. pumilus and p. aeruginosa cultures were 161 and 305 nm, respectively, with polydispersity indices (PDI) of 0.09 and 0.61. These results indicate that the Se-NPs produced by B. pumilus are smaller and exhibit a narrower size distribution compared to those synthesized by P. aeruginosa. Furthermore, the zeta potentials of the nanoparticles were − 7 and − 5.6 mV, respectively, suggesting that that bacterial metabolites act as negatively charged capping agents stabilizing the nanoparticle surfaces (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Zeta potential and size distribution analysis of the synthesized Se-NPs by theB. pumilus a and the P. aeruginosa b

Zeta potential and size distribution analysis of the synthesized Se-NPs by theB. pumilus a and the P. aeruginosa b

TEM images (Fig. 5) confirmed the spherical morphology of the Se-NPs synthesized by both bacterial strains and demonstrated that the particles were within the nanoscale range. Size distribution analysis, performed using Figi and Origin software, showed that Se-NPs produced by B. pumilus had a mean diameter of 64 ± 8 nm, exhibiting a narrower size distribution compared to Se-NPs synthesized by P. aeruginosa, which had a mean diameter of 146 ± 38 nm. The observed discrepancy between sizes obtained by DLS and TEM is attributed to the differing measurement principles: TEM directly images dry nanoparticles, representing the physical core size, whereas DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes the nanoparticle core along with surface coatings, solvent layers, and hydration shells surrounding particles in solution.

As shown in Fig. 6, the XRD analysis reveals patterns lacking sharp Bragg peaks, indicating that the Se-NPs synthesized by both B. pumilus and P. aeruginosa are predominantly amorphous, which is consistent with previous reports [21, 22]. Nevertheless, the diffraction patterns could be indexed to the trigonal phase of selenium, evidenced by characteristic peaks corresponding to lattice parameters a = 4.366 A˚ and c = 4.956 A˚ (JCPDS file no. 06-362).

The findings of this study indicated that the MIC of nanoparticles synthesized by the B. pumilus RMO6 was lower than those produced by P. aeruginosa strain. This difference may be attributed to the smaller size and better PDI of Se-NPs synthesized by B. pumilus RMO6 in comparison to those produced by P. aeruginosa. Smaller nanoparticles often exhibit enhanced antibacterial activity due to their larger surface area-to-volume ratio, which can facilitate greater interaction with bacterial cells, thereby improving their efficacy against pathogens [23]. Thus, the characteristics of Se-NPs synthesized by B. pumilus RMO6 may play a crucial role in their effectiveness against resistant bacterial strains. Notably, both types of nanoparticles exhibited the highest efficacy against S. aureus and the lowest effectiveness against E. coli (see Fig. 7). In our previous research, we demonstrated that gold nanoparticles synthesized by bacteria effectively inhibited the growth of S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii strains [16]. In their studies, researchers showed that selenium nanoparticles can successfully inhibit bacterial growth [24–26]. Research findings aligned with their study, indicating that Se-NPs can effectively inhibit the growth of bacteria. Because mycobacteria are naturally resistant to antibiotics and disinfectants due to their unique cell wall structure, managing infections related to them poses a significant challenge for healthcare providers. A 2020 study by Hector Estevez indicated that Se-NPs can inhibit the growth of M. tuberculosis and M. smegmatis by damaging their cell walls [27]. These results suggest that Se-NPs play a crucial role in managing resistant bacterial infections, paving the way for promising advancements in medicine. Additionally, research led by Soumya Menon and colleagues in India s demonstrated that synthesized Se-NPs exhibited notable antimicrobial properties against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [28]. It appears that selenium nanoparticles can react with functional groups of bacterial proteins and enzymes, disrupting their normal function. This disrupts energy metabolism and membrane transport in bacteria. In addition, selenium nanoparticles inhibit the formation of biofilms, which are protective structures that play a significant role in antimicrobial resistance.

Similar results were seen in the biofilm eradication assay, demonstrating that the synthesized nanoparticles effectively eliminate S. aureus biofilm at lower concentrations. In contrast, higher concentrations of nanoparticles were necessary to eradicate E. coli biofilm (Table 2). Several studies support our work, showing that Se-NPs inhibit the growth and biofilm formation of pathogens like K. pneumoniae, S. mutans, S. pyogenes, and H. influenzae [29–31]. Notably, one study reported biofilm eradication at 20 µg/ml, a result that closely matches our own. In the recent study, Catherine Thamayandhi demonstrated that Se-NPs markedly decreased biofilm formation in a dose-dependent fashion, that they reported MIC50 and MIC90 values of 60 and 80 µg/ml, respectively [32]. Similar results were reported by Asad Ullah et al. in 2023 regarding the eradication of bacterial biofilms by Se-NPs [33].

Conclusion

Bacterial antibiotic resistance represents one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time, endangering the effectiveness of current treatment options and leading to increased morbidity and mortality rates. Research on the extensive medical uses of nanoparticles has been growing significantly. Se-NPs play a vital role in controlling bacterial infections due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, biocompatibility, and low toxicity. Their nanoscale dimensions increase surface reactivity, allowing efficient interactions with bacterial membranes, proteins, and DNA, ultimately leading to cell wall disruption and metabolic dysfunction. In this study, we successfully synthesized two formulations of selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs) using two different microbial isolates. Characterization tests validated this, with dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging showing that the nanoparticles had a uniform spherical shape at the nanoscale. Notably, the nanoparticles produced by B. pumilus RMO6 were smaller and more uniform in size (177 nm compared to 305 nm). The Se-NPs exhibited antimicrobial properties against four pathogens: E. coli, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and E. faecalis. Additionally, the synthesized nanoparticles were effective in eliminating the biofilm of pathogenic bacteria. The results of our study will contribute to a better understanding of Se-NPs design and assessment, particularly regarding the effects of nanoparticles on antimicrobial activity and biofilm eradication in pathogenic bacteria. The novelty of this work lies in the pioneering use of P. aeruginosa and B. pumilus for the single-step, extracellular biosynthesis of Se-NPs. This study compares the efficiency, physicochemical characteristics, and antimicrobial efficacy of Se-NPs synthesized by these bacterial strains, revealing that the biological source plays a crucial role in determining the nanoparticles’ functional properties and their potency against a range of clinically relevant pathogens. A critical direction for subsequent investigation involves delineating the precise molecular mechanisms through which Se-NPs exert their antibacterial and antibiofilm activity, and evaluating their usefulness in novel therapeutics targeting multidrug-resistant pathogens.
