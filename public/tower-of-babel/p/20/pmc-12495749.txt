Chidamide impedes glycolysis but increases ferroptosis and cisplatin sensitivity of lung cancer cells through downregulating USP35
Authors: Kunjie Wang, Lin An, Aimin Zang, Yue Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChidamide is a histone deacetylase inhibitor playing an anti-cancer role in several cancers. However, the function of chidamide in lung cancer remains to be further elucidated. This study was designed to explore the regulation of chidamide in glycolysis, ferroptosis, and chemosensitivity of cisplatin (DDP) in lung cancer, as well as its action mechanism associated with ubiquitin-specific protease 35 (USP35).MethodsCell viability and half maximal inhibitory concentration were examined using Cell Counting Kit-8 assay. Glycolysis metabolism was assessed through glucose uptake, lactate generation, and intercellular ATP level. Ferroptosis analysis was performed by detection of reactive oxygen species, glutathione and Fe2+ level. DDP sensitivity was evaluated by colony formation assay and flow cytometry. The protein expression was measured by Western blotting. The function of chidamide in mice was investigated using tumor xenograft assay.ResultsLung cancer cell viability was decreased by chidamide. Chidamide resulted in glycolysis inhibition, ferroptosis promotion, and DDP sensitivity elevation of lung cancer cells. Chidamide could bind to USP35 and reduced its protein expression in lung cancer. The effects of chidamide on glycolysis, ferroptosis and DDP sensitivity were attributed to the downregulation of USP35. Chidamide reduced tumor growth in mice, and enhanced the sensitivity of tumors to DDP.ConclusionThese findings proved that chidamide blocked lung cancer cell development and elevated DDP sensitivity via targeting USP35.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14925-z.Keywords: Lung cancer, Chidamide, Ubiquitin-specific protease 35, Glycolysis, Ferroptosis, Cisplatin sensitivity

Introduction

Lung cancer continues to be the leading cause of human death associated with cancers [1]. Most lung cancer patients are diagnosed at the advanced stage, and the clinical prognosis remains relatively poor [2]. Surgical resection offers a curative possibility for cancers, but patients with advanced lung cancer still experience tumor recurrence and chemoresistance [3]. Cisplatin (DDP) is the first-line treatment for various kinds of cancers, but acquired resistance has seriously limited its therapeutic outcomes [4–6]. Thus, exploring potential drugs to enhance DDP sensitivity is necessary for lung cancer patients. The pathogenesis of lung cancer is complicated and involves various types of biological processes. Glycolysis can occur even in an oxygen-rich environment, and suppression of glycolysis directly blocks cancer cell survival [7]. Ferroptosis is a specific type of non-apoptotic cell death, exhibiting as iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [8, 9]. Targeting glycolysis or ferroptosis has become a potential therapeutic approach for lung cancer.

Epigenetic modifications play essential roles in cancer progression, and histone deacetylases (HDACs) are key to the epigenetic regulation of gene expression that further affects tumor development [10]. HDAC inhibitors have been considered as novel treatment options for cancers, and chidamide shows anti-cancer properties as the first oral selective HDAC inhibitor [11, 12]. Chidamide was reported to inhibit TGF-β-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition of lung cancer cells [13]. In addition, chidamide enhanced sensitivity of lung cancer to crizotinib by targeting c-MET [14]. The mediation of chidamide in DDP sensitivity and its downstream target remain to be researched. A previous study has indicated that HDAC inhibitors can blunt glycolysis in glioblastoma cells [15]. In addition, HDAC inhibitors activate lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, thus exerting anti-tumor effects [16, 17]. Chidamide was also reported to induce ferroptosis in laryngeal cancer [18]. However, whether chidamide can impact lung cancer glycolysis and ferroptosis is completely unclear.

Ubiquitination is a crucial protein modification process, which plays a significant role in multiple pathways related to tumors [19]. As key regulators of this process, ubiquitin-specific proteases (USPs) exert crucial roles in modulating cellular functions by removing protein ubiquitination [20]. USP35 is a frequent element of USP family, with important regulation in cancer development. Lv et al.. testified that USP35 contributed to hepatocellular carcinoma cell proliferation, metastasis and glycolysis via targeting pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) [21]. Depletion of USP35 elevated the sensitivity of DDP-induced lung cancer cell apoptosis by overexpressing baculoviral IAP repeat containing 3 (BIRC3) [22]. USP35 knockdown promoted ferroptosis and sensitized lung cancer cells to DDP through interacting with ferroportin (FPN) [23]. These findings reminder that USP35 may be a key target of regulating glycolysis, ferroptosis, and chemosensitivity in lung cancer. More interestingly, chidamide was reported to inhibit aerobic metabolism by promoting Mcl-1 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway [24]. Given that USPs act as important regulators in the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, it is of great significance to explore the relationship between chidamide and USP35 in lung cancer.

Currently, this study evaluated the influences of chidamide on glycolysis, ferroptosis and DDP sensitivity in lung cancer cells. Also, the expression change of USP35 after chidamide treatment and the hypothesis that chidamide targeted USP35 to affect lung cancer cell progression were explored. The objective was to investigate the biological function of chidamide and its USP35-associated mechanism in lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and Chidamide treatment

Human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B and lung cancer cell lines A549, H460 were purchased from Bena Culture Collection (XinYang, China). Following digestion with tyrisin, cells were maintained in DMEM with the complement of 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution. Normal cultivation was carried out under the humid environment at 37℃ with 5% CO2. Cell culture reagents were all bought from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Chidamide (Selleck.cn, Shanghai, China) was added to cells with different concentrations for 48 h.

Cell transfection

The overexpression plasmid pcDNA-USP35 (oe-USP35) was constructed by GENESEED (Guangzhou, China), using pcDNA as the normal control (oe-NC). After A549 and H460 cells were treated with chidamide or not, Lipofectamine™ 3000 Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was employed for plasmid transfection in line with users’ instruction.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was examined by CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well plate for 24 h and treated with increasing concentrations of chidamide or DDP, then incubated with 10 µL/well CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 3 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was read under a microplate reader, and cell viability (%) was recorded at each concentration point. IC50 was expressed as the concentration at 50% cell viability.

In addition, cells were subjected to chidamide treatment with the gradient concentrations (0 µM, 0.05 µM, 0.1 µM, 0.2 µM, 0.4 µM, 0.8 µM, 1.6 µM, 3.2 µM, 6.4 µM) for 48 h, then CCK-8 was added for cell viability detection.

Glycolysis analysis

Cell supernatant from A549 and H460 cells with chidamide treatment or plasmid transfection was harvested for assessment of glycolytic metabolism. Glucose uptake, lactate generation, and ATP level were measured via Glucose Uptake Assay Kit, Lactate Assay Kit, and ATP Assay Kit as per the specific specifications provided by Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Ferroptosis evaluation

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) level was tested through fluorescence probe DCFH-DA using ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime), followed by examination of flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA). Glutathione (GSH) level was determined via Glutathione Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s manual. In addition, Fe2+ level in cell lysates was detected by Iron Assay Kit (Abcam).

Colony formation assay

Cells were inoculated into 6-well plates with 500 cells/well, and then normal cell culture was performed for 14 d. The generated white colonies were stained in crystal violet (Beyotime) after fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime). Finally, colonies were imaged and counted by an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow cytometry

Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Kit (Beyotime) was applied for measuring apoptosis rate. The harvested cells were resuspended in Binding Buffer, then incubated with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL propidium iodide (PI) at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. Subsequently, apoptotic cells were discerned by the flow cytometer.

Molecular mocking and visualized analysis

Autodock Vina software was used for molecular docking of chidamide and USP35, with USP35 protein as the receptor and chidamide as the ligand. PyMOL software was used to visualize the biomolecular interaction between chidamide with USP35 protein in 3D.

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein samples from A549 and H460 cells were obtained using RIPA Buffer (Beyotime), and SDS-PAGE was performed with equal amount of 50 µg proteins. After activation of polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Beyotime), proteins were transferred onto the membranes and immersed in Blocking Buffer (Beyotime). The primary antibodies against USP35 (proteintech, Wuhan, China, 24559-1-AP), PKM2 (proteintech, 15822-1-AP), FPN (proteintech, 26601-1-AP), BIRC3 (proteintech, 24304-1-AP), β-actin (proteintech, 20536-1-AP) were incubated overnight at 4℃. After that, membranes were added with the secondary antibody (proteintech, SA00001-2) for 1 h and then protein signals were measured by Enhanced Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (proteintech).

Tumor xenograft assay

Tumor assay in vivo was approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Hebei University. Five-week-old BALB/c male nude mice (SPF biotech, Beijing, China) were subcutaneously implanted with 5 × 106 A549 cells in 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Beyotime). After one week, mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 5): control (20% DMSO in PBS), chidamide, DDP, and DDP + chidamide. 3 mg/kg chidamide was intraperitoneally injected daily, and 3 mg/kg DDP was injected twice weekly. Tumors were measured every week after drug injection. Tumor volume (mm3) = length × width2 × 0.5. All mice were euthanized through the flowing CO2. Tumors were obtained, weighed, and photographed.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were independently repeated three times and data were expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was completed by SPSS, with difference analyzed by Student’s t-test (two groups) or ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (multiple groups). There was a significant difference when P < 0.05.

Results

Chidamide reduced cell viability of lung cancer

The molecular formula of chidamide is C22H19FN4O2, and its 2D structure was presented as Fig. 1A. CCK-8 assay demonstrated that IC50 of chidamide was decreased in lung cancer cells (A549: IC50 = 0.74 µM, H460: IC50 = 2.67 µM) relative to normal BEAS-2B cells (IC50 = 37.00 µM) (Fig. 1B). After the chidamide treatment of gradient concentration, cell viability of A549 was inhibited by 0.2 µM chidamide compared with 0 µM group (P < 0.01) and 26% decline was measured at 0.4 µM chidamide compared with 0 µM group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Also, H460 cell viability was decreased at 0.8 µM chidamide (P < 0.01) and the difference was more significant (P < 0.001) at 1.6 µM chidamide with 34% decline (Fig. 1D). However, there was 24% reduction of BEAS-2B cell viability (P < 0.001) until treatment of 6.4 µM chidamide (Fig. 1E). Therefore, chidamide suppressed lung cancer cell viability.

C-E Cell viability detection was carried out using CCK-8 after A549 (C), H460 (D), and BEAS-2B (E) cells were treated with gradient concentrations (0 µM, 0.05 µM, 0.1 µM, 0.2 µM, 0.4 µM, 0.8 µM, 1.6 µM, 3.2 µM, 6.4 µM) of chidamide. **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. *: compared to chidamide 0 µM group

Chidamide suppressed glycolysis and induced ferroptosis of lung cancer cells

According to cell viability change, chidamide was used by 0.4 µM/0.8 µM in A549 cells and 1.6 µM/3.2 µM in H460 cells. The effect of chidamide on glycolysis was evaluated by detecting the associated indicators. Glucose uptake (Fig. 2A-B), lactate generation (Fig. 2C-D), and ATP level (Fig. 2E-F) were all inhibited in a dose-dependent way after A549 were treated with 0.4 µM/0.8 µM chidamide and H460 cells were treated with 1.6 µM/3.2 µM chidamide. Chidamide resulted in the accumulation of ROS (Fig. 2G-H) and the repression of GSH level (Fig. 2I-J) in A549 and H460 cells. In addition, Fe2+ level was prominently elevated in chidamide-treated A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 2K-L). Ferrostain-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor) was shown to reverse chidamide-induced cell viability reduction, MDA increase, protein upregulation of pro-ferroptosis marker ACSL4, and protein downregulation of anti-ferroptosis maker GPX4 (Supplementary Fig. 1), implicating that chidamide indeed promoted ferroptosis in lung cancer. These results suggested that chidamide impeded lung cancer cell glycolysis and enhanced ferroptosis.

Chidamide increased sensitivity of lung cancer cells to DDP

The influence of chidamide on DDP sensitivity was explored. Dose-effect curves from CCK-8 assay showed the IC50 of DDP in A549 cells (16.41 µM) and H460 cells (9.918 µM) (Supplementary Fig. 2). More interestingly, 0.4 µM or 0.8 µM chidamide evidently decreased the IC50 of DDP in A549 cells (Fig. 3A), and IC50 inhibition of DDP was also observed in 1.6 µM or 3.2 µM treated H460 cells (Fig. 3B). Colony numbers of A549 and H460 cells were restrained by 5 µM DDP, and the addition of chidamide further enhanced this inhibition of colony formation ability (Fig. 3C-D). Flow cytometry was applied to estimate cell apoptosis. As displayed in Fig. 3E-F, apoptosis rate was increased in 5 µM DDP-treated cells, then this effect was reinforced following co-treatment of DDP and chidamide. Chidamide-induced cell growth inhibition and apoptosis promotion under DDP suggested that chidamide enhanced DDP sensitivity in lung cancer cells.

USP35 protein was down-regulated by chidamide in lung cancer

USP35 was highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD, Fig. 4A) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC, Fig. 4B) from TCGA samples according to UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html). Kaplan-Meier Plotter website (https://kmplot.com/analysis) predicted that high expression of USP35 was associated with a poor prognosis of LUAD patients, and survival curve showed no significance between high and low expression of USP35 in LUSC patients (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). Molecular docking (receptor: USP35, ligand: chidamide) showed that the optimal affinity (kcal/mol) was − 7.4 (Fig. 4C). 3D visualization analysis was performed by PyMOL software, and the binding situation was presented as Fig. 4D-E. Furthermore, various parameters in molecular dynamics simulation confirmed the good docking between chidamide and USP35 (Supplementary Fig. 4). USP35 protein expression of 0.4 µM or 0.8 µM chidamide group was much lower than that of 0 µM chidamide group in A549 cells (Fig. 4F). Likewise, 1.6 µM or 3.2 µM treatment of chidamide lessened the protein level of USP35 in H460 cells by comparison with 0 µM treatment group (Fig. 4G). However, USP35 mRNA level was not affected by chidamide in A549 and H460 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5A-B). All in all, chidamide could suppress USP35 protein expression in lung cancer cells.

Chidamide targeted USP35 to affect lung cancer cell glycolysis and ferroptosis

USP35 was overexpressed by oe-USP35 for exploration of interaction between chidamide and USP35 in lung cancer cells. WB analysis manifested that USP35 protein was up-regulated in oe-USP35 group contrasted to oe-NC group, and chidamide-mediated downregulation of USP35 was reversed with transfection of oe-USP35 in A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 5A-B). Overexpression of USP35 contributed to glucose uptake (Fig. 5C-D), lactate generation (Fig. 5E-F), and ATP level (Fig. 5G-H) in A549 and H460 cells, and oe-USP35 abolished the inhibitory influences of chidamide on these indicators. A549 and H460 cells transfected with oe-USP35 (relative to oe-NC) appeared significant suppression of ROS (Fig. 5I-J) and promotion of GSH (Fig. 5K-L), meanwhile, co-treatment of chidamide and oe-USP35 attenuated chidamide-caused ROS increase and GSH repression. A depressed change of Fe2+ was triggered USP35 expression elevation, which could overturn chidamide-enhanced Fe2+ level in A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 5M-N). Collectively, the functions of chidamide in regulating glycolysis and ferroptosis were attributed to reduction of USP35 expression.

Chidamide promote lung cancer cell sensitivity to DDP via binding to USP35

IC50 of DDP was higher in oe-USP35 group than that in oe-NC group and chidamide treatment declined the IC50 of DDP, but chidamide + oe-USP35 group reversed IC50 of DDP in chidamide + oe-NC group (Fig. 6A-B). USP35 upregulation increased colony number in A549 + DDP and H460 + DDP cells. More interestingly, chidamide-suppressed colony number in DDP-treated cells was recovered following the high expression of USP35 (Fig. 6C-D). Cell apoptosis by flow cytometry indicated the decreased apoptosis rate after USP35 overexpression, and this expression change of USP35 could eliminate apoptosis acceleration induced by chidamide in A549 + DDP and H460 + DDP cells (Fig. 6E-F). Altogether, USP35 inhibition was responsible for chidamide-mediated elevation of DDP sensitivity.

Chidamide regulated the proteins associated with glycolysis, ferroptosis and DDP sensitivity by downregulating USP35

WB was used to analyze the proteins associated with glycolysis, ferroptosis and chemoresistance. The results of protein blots in A549 cells were shown in Fig. 7A. PKM2 (a rate-limiting enzyme for glycolysis) (Fig. 7B), FPN (an anti-ferroptosis factor) (Fig. 7C), and BIRC3 (a gene related to chemoresistance) (Fig. 7D) were up-regulated by overexpression of USP35, but chidamide caused the reverse regulation of three proteins and these inhibitory effects of chidamide on proteins were all counteracted by oe-USP35 in A549 cells. Similarly to A549 cells, WB analysis (Fig. 7E) in H460 cells demonstrated that USP35 also increased the levels of PKM2 (Fig. 7F), FPN (Fig. 7G), and BIRC3 (Fig. 7H), then USP35 upregulation rescued the chidamide-caused protein suppression. In addition, ubiquitination detection showed that overexpression of USP35 reduced the ubiquitinated levels of PKM2, FPN, and BIRC3 (Supplementary Fig. 5C-E). Thus, USP35 promoted glycolysis but inhibited ferroptosis and DDP sensitivity by targeting these targets, and chidamide regulated these processes through inhibiting USP35. The results in squamous cell carcinoma cell line SK-MES-1 also confirmed that chidamide down-regulated USP35 to inhibit glycolysis, promote ferroptosis, enhance DDP sensitivity, and reduce protein levels of PKM2/FPN/BIRC3 (Supplementary Fig. 6). The established mechanism was applicable to lung squamous cell carcinoma.

Chidamide inhibited tumor growth and promoted DDP sensitivity in mice

Xenograft models were established in mice, and tumor images of each group were shown in Fig. 8A. Administration of chidamide or DDP reduced tumor growth, and chidamide enhanced DDP sensitivity to further intensify DDP-induced tumor inhibition, as evidenced by tumor volume and weight (Fig. 8B-C). Hence, anti-tumor chidamide elevated DDP sensitivity in vivo.

Discussion

Chidamide suppresses cancer progression by inhibiting the activity and function of HDAC. Herein, this study disclosed the effects of chidamide on lung cancer cell glycolysis, ferroptosis and DDP sensitivity, as well as its regulatory mechanism targeting USP35.

Firstly, cell viability was found to be inhibited in chidamide-treated A549 and H460 cell lines, suggesting that chidamide reduced lung cancer cell survival. Tumor development is tightly dependent on metabolic reprogramming, and mediating metabolic requirements is possible to improve cancer treatment [25]. Cancer cells can switch to a glycolysis-based metabolism, which contributes to cell survival and meets the demands of cells for energy and macromolecules [26]. Aerobic glycolysis is exhibited as the rapid consumption of glucose, then provides amount of lactate and ATP to maintain cancer cell development [27]. The recent study has demonstrated the role of chidamide in regulating glycolytic metabolism of cancer cells. In pancreatic cancer, chidamide repressed the maintenance of mitochondrial aerobic metabolism via inducing degradation of Mcl-1 expression [24]. The results from glucose uptake, lactate generation and ATP level showed that chidamide impeded glycolysis of lung cancer cells, firstly disclosing the effect of chidamide on glycolysis of lung cancer. In addition to glycolytic metabolism, iron metabolism also plays a critical role in cancers. Iron is the key component of many physiological processes including DNA replication and repair, and overload of iron can lead to oxidative DNA damage even cell death [28]. This process of cell death related to iron metabolism is known as ferroptosis, which is characterized by excessive iron and ROS as well as the depletion of anti-oxidative GSH [29, 30]. HDAC inhibitor epirubicin has been found to evoke ferroptosis via targeting iron metabolism in leukemia [31]. Also, chidamide revealed anticancer activity in laryngeal carcinoma by inducing cell apoptosis and ferroptosis [18]. The obtained results in this study manifested that chidamide increased ROS and Fe2+ levels, and decreased GSH level, suggesting the promoting regulation of chidamide in ferroptosis of lung cancer. The influences on glycolysis and ferroptosis provided more supports for the role of anti-tumor chidamide in lung cancer from the aspect of cell metabolism.

Cancer can easily acquire resistance to therapeutic drugs, and HDAC inhibitors may be the promising treatment strategy against drug resistance [32]. As the typical HDAC inhibitor, chidamide has shown the wide regulation of chemosensitivity in different cancers. The previous researches demonstrated that chidamide promoted cytotoxicity of doxorubicin in breast cancer [33], and it enhanced sorafenib-induced anti-tumor activities in osteosarcoma cells [34]. In the present study, chidamide reduced cell growth and promoted apoptosis in DDP-treated lung cancer cells, implying that chidamide further contributed to DDP-induced tumor inhibition by increasing drug sensitivity.

USP35 served as an oncogene of lung cancer to facilitate cell growth, angiogenesis, and invasion [35]. In addition, USP35 was implicated in the mediation of cell glycolysis in hepatocellular carcinoma and breast cancer [21, 36]. For ferroptosis, silence of USP35 increased ferroptosis in renal clear cell carcinoma cells [37]. In lung cancer, USP35 knockdown promoted ferroptosis and elevated the sensitivity of cells to DDP [23]. Herein, molecular docking and visualized analysis manifested the excellent binding between chidamide and USP35. Furthermore, chidamide down-regulated USP35 in lung cancer, and USP35 overexpression could expedite glycolysis and restrain ferroptosis to reverse chidamide-mediated regulation of the associated indicators. Moreover, DDP sensitivity was enhanced by chidamide via the downregulation of USP35 in lung cancer cells.

Additionally, the protein detection was performed to further validate the results. PKM2 is an important rate-limiting enzyme in the glycolytic process [38], and FPN is considered as an anti-ferroptosis protein [39]. BIRC3 can activate PI3K-AKT phosphorylation to promote trastuzumab resistance in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive gastric cancer [40]. BIRC3 is also responsible for the resistance of lung cancer to DDP induced by USP35 [22]. Thus, BIRC3 acts as a pivotal marker for drug resistance or sensitivity inhibition. PKM2, FPN, and BIRC3 proteins were all down-regulated by chidamide via repressing USP35. In combining with these findings, the functions of chidamide in cell metabolism and DDP sensitivity were considered to be achieved by targeting and downregulating USP35.

In the current study, there are certain limitations. Firstly, the binding between chidamide and USP35 was only simulated through molecular docking, lacking experimental validation. In the future, Co-IP and surface plasma resonance (SPR) will be performed to provide more evidence for the binding of chidamide and USP35. Secondly, the molecular logic of how USP35 simultaneously regulates glycolysis (PKM2), ferroptosis (FPN), and drug resistance (BIRC3) has not been explained. The essence of ferroptosis is iron-dependent oxidative stress, and glycolysis or drug resistance is also tightly associated with oxidative stress. Whether oxidative stress can act as a common signaling pathway regulating glycolysis, ferroptosis, and drug resistance will be used as a new direction. Finally, animal models only affirmed the effects of chidamide on tumor growth and DDP sensitivity. The role of USP35 in vivo remains to be explored in future study.

To sum up, chidamide hindered cell glycolysis but induced ferroptosis and facilitated DDP sensitivity of lung cancer through reducing the expression of USP35. This study clarified a molecular downstream target of chidamide underlying cell metabolism and drug sensitivity. Chidamide may be an excellent drug against lung cancer progression and acquired resistance.
