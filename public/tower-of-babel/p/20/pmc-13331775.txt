Vestibular schwannoma with hemorrhage in a 63-year-old female: A case report
Authors: Omar Daas, Haneen Baydoun, Somaya Al Kiswani, Radwan Shayeb, Ahmad Daas, Mohammed AbdulJabbar Abed, Abdullah Nofal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVestibular schwannomas are benign cranial nerve tumors that typically follow an indolent course. Management strategies are guided by tumor size, growth rate, and neurological compression. However, a small proportion show aggressive behavior characterized by rapid expansion, cystic degeneration, or spontaneous intratumoral hemorrhage, resulting in acute neurological deterioration. We report a 63-year-old woman presenting with progressive cerebellar symptoms and lower cranial nerve dysfunction. Neuroimaging revealed a giant Koos grade IV cystic vestibular schwannoma with intratumoral hemorrhage causing significant brainstem compression. Subtotal microsurgical resection via a retrosigmoid approach achieved brainstem decompression while preserving facial nerve continuity, followed by adjuvant fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy targeting residual disease. This approach resulted in complete neurological recovery, including full restoration of facial nerve function (House–Brackmann grade I) and tumor stability at 8-month follow-up. Giant Koos grade IV vestibular schwannomas with combined cystic and hemorrhagic features are exceptionally rare and pose unique management challenges. Our results support a customized, function-first combined-modality strategy that prioritizes functional outcomes over gross-total resection in pathologically complex but histologically benign tumors.Keywords: Vestibular schwannoma, Hemorrhagic, Cystic, Subtotal resection, Stereotactic radiotherapy

Introduction

Vestibular schwannomas (VS), or acoustic neuromas, are benign Schwann cell tumors arising from the vestibulocochlear nerve at the cerebellopontine angle (CPA), representing roughly 85% of CPA neoplasms and approximately 8%-10% of all intracranial tumors [[1], [2], [3]]. Although mostly benign, VS poses a threat to intracranial structures due to mass effect and carries a small malignant transformation risk [1]. The most reported symptoms include progressive hearing loss and tinnitus (present in over half of patients), while large tumors can cause hydrocephalus and brainstem compression, leading to headache, vertigo, and facial paresthesia [[3], [4], [5]].

Spontaneous hemorrhage of VS produces a more acute presentation with higher facial nerve dysfunction and mortality rates—reaching 10%, compared with less than 1% for nonhemorrhagic VS with only a limited number of cases documented in the literature [5,6].

The majority of VS remain clinically stable and are managed conservatively, with mean growth rates of only 1-2 mm/y and up to 75% demonstrating no growth. For tumors requiring intervention, the translabyrinthine and retrosigmoid approaches are most employed. Large Koos IV schwannomas compressing the brainstem requires aggressive management balancing gross-total resection against cranial nerve preservation [1]. Hemorrhagic VS demands surgical intervention given their high mortality risk and high rates of reversible facial weakness after clot evacuation [5].

We present a 63-year-old woman with a giant hemorrhagic VS measuring 4.0 × 3.8 cm (Koos grade IV) who presented with progressive dizziness, hearing loss, dysphagia, cerebellar ataxia, and facial palsy. Despite the aggressive hemorrhagic phenotype and large tumor size, the patient achieved excellent functional recovery following an integrated surgical and radiation therapy approach. We describe clinical presentation, diagnostic findings, operative strategy, and long-term outcomes.

Case presentation

Patient information and clinical findings

A 63-year-old woman with well-controlled hypertension presented in early November 2024 with a 3-month history of progressive right-sided hearing loss, dizziness, unsteady gait, and difficulty swallowing. She denied any personal or family history of neurofibromatosis. Neurological examination revealed right sensorineural hearing loss and right-sided cerebellar ataxia. Facial nerve function was graded House–Brackmann (HB) grade II preoperatively, with subsequent surgical notes confirming pre-existing facial palsy. Laryngoscopy confirmed absent gag reflex with mobile vocal cords.

Diagnostic assessment

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identified a substantial, heterogeneous mass in the right CPA, spanning approximately 4.0 × 3.8 cm, with expansion into the internal auditory canal and Meckel’s cave. The tumor exerted considerable mass effect on the pons, right middle cerebellar peduncle, and medulla, resulting in effacement of the fourth ventricle and tonsillar herniation. Susceptibility-weighted imaging demonstrated prominent blooming artifacts within the lesion; corresponding phase images confirmed true susceptibility effects rather than calcification, supporting the presence of intratumoral hemorrhage (Fig. 1).

Treatment

The patient underwent a navigation-guided right retrosigmoid craniotomy with intraoperative ultrasound guidance on March 11, 2025. Intraoperatively, a large, encapsulated tumor was found compressing the brainstem. Subtotal resection (STR) was performed to decompress the brainstem; a small residual volume was intentionally left at the internal auditory meatus given its intimate adherence to the facial nerve.

Final histopathology confirmed a WHO grade I schwannoma. S100 and SOX10 immunostains were positive in tumor cells. Gross examination revealed fragments of light orange tissue, and microscopic analysis showed areas of hemorrhagic degeneration and hemosiderin deposition, consistent with the hemorrhagic nature suspected on imaging.

Postoperative course and adjuvant therapy

Immediate postoperative assessment indicated HB grade IV facial paralysis, leading to exposure keratopathy and a small corneal ulcer managed aggressively with lubrication and taping by the ophthalmology team. MRI on postoperative day 1 identified ischemic changes in the right middle cerebellar peduncle, with no lasting neurological abnormalities other than facial palsy. Fractionate stereotactic radiotherapy was administered in April 2025, approximately 4 weeks after surgery, targeting the residual disease.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement. By July 2025 (4 months postoperatively), facial nerve function had fully recovered (HB grade I), and gait instability and dysphagia had markedly improved. MRI in November 2025 (8 months postsurgery) revealed a stable residual tumor with no evidence of growth, alongside an asymptomatic right infratentorial pseudomeningocele measuring 4.3 × 3.2 cm (Fig. 3), which required no intervention.

Discussion

This case demonstrates successful management of a rare and complex vestibular schwannoma characterized by massive size (Koos grade IV), cystic structure, and intratumoral hemorrhage. Spontaneous hemorrhage occurs in under 1% of VS cases and is linked to abrupt neurological decline due to rapid posterior fossa expansion. Our patient’s rapid symptom onset over 3 months correlated with hemorrhagic expansion on MRI (blooming artifact). Hemorrhagic VS carries higher rates of preoperative facial nerve weakness (33% vs 6% in nonhemorrhagic cases) and increased brainstem compression-related mortality (10% vs 0.2% in nonbleeding VS) [6]. Cystic changes are found more frequently in large tumors (15% of Koos grade IV vs 2% of Koos grade I) and independently predict worse postoperative facial nerve outcomes [7,8].

A planned STR was employed given the extensive capsular adherence encountered intraoperatively. The residual tumor along the petroclival region and within the internal auditory canal underscores the surgical challenges inherent to large, cystic, hemorrhagic VS in which peritumoral adhesions obliterate dissection planes. This strategy aligns with contemporary evidence supporting STR combined with adjuvant stereotactic radiosurgery, which achieves comparable tumor control rates of 89.9% while improving facial nerve preservation from 88.0% immediately after microsurgery to 94.4% at final follow-up, and preserving facial and cochlear function in more than 50% of patients even for large VS [9].

The degree of resection critically influences residual tumor behavior. Patients with ≥87% tumor resection have significantly lower progression rates compared to those with less than 87% resection (7.1% vs 39.1%), with extent of resection identified as the only independent predictor of tumor progression. Facial nerve outcomes show optimal results at 70%-80% resection rather than near-total excision, endorsing a balanced strategy that maximizes tumor control while prioritizing functional preservation [10].

Postoperative imaging showed diffusion restriction in the right middle cerebellar peduncle consistent with acute microvascular ischemia, and a right infratentorial subdural fluid collection that enlarged to a pseudomeningocele. Despite these findings, residual tumor remained stable at 8-month follow-up. In a series of 258 patients undergoing VS surgery, 7.4% showed acute microvascular ischemia on early postoperative MRI, yet none developed lasting neurological deficits, underscoring the relative safety of nerve-sparing STR even in high-risk resections [11]. Pseudomeningocele is a recognized complication of retrosigmoid and posterior fossa surgery, occurring in 4%-23% of cases, and is frequently self-limiting [12,13]. The absence of long-term morbidity in our patient supports this favorable natural history.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that prior to surgery her quality of life was substantially impaired by progressive unsteadiness, difficulty eating, and near-complete hearing loss on the right side. She consented to share her experience to help advance understanding of this rare condition. At her 8-month follow-up, she described her functional recovery as remarkable, noting full return of facial movement, improved walking, and resolution of swallowing difficulty. She expressed satisfaction with the decision to prioritize nerve preservation and remains committed to ongoing imaging surveillance.

Conclusion

Hemorrhagic transformation in vestibular schwannomas is a rare occurrence in which the pursuit of gross-total excision may endanger facial and lower cranial nerve function due to bleeding and dense adhesions that frequently obscure safe surgical planes. In this 63-year-old patient, a planned nerve-sparing subtotal resection successfully decompressed the brainstem, and adjuvant fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy ensured durable control of the residual disease. Full neurological recovery—including facial nerve restoration to HB grade I—alongside radiological stability at 8 months supports a function-first, combined-modality approach for high-risk Koos IV hemorrhagic-cystic vestibular schwannomas.
