Effect of Prior Abdominal Surgery on Abdominal-based Free-flap Breast Reconstruction Outcomes: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Noor H. Allababidi, Jawahir O. AlTamimi, Shahad N. AlAnazi, Abdulaziz M. Alghamdi, Abdulaziz F. Samandar, Shahad H. Bassfr, Isra’a Abdulaziz AlZahrani, Nora N. AlMouaalamy, Nawaf H. AlSaadi, Abdullah M. AlKhudeer, Khalid M. Khoshaim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Autologous tissue reconstruction is a key option for breast reconstruction after mastectomy. Although prior abdominal surgery was traditionally considered a contraindication to abdominal-based flaps, advances in microsurgical techniques and preoperative imaging have expanded patient eligibility. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates the impact of prior abdominal scarring on flap viability and complication rates in abdominal-based breast reconstruction.Methods:A PRISMA-compliant systematic review identified 12 retrospective comparative studies (11 cohort and 1 case–control) involving 4574 patients who underwent abdominal-based autologous breast reconstruction. Recipient- and donor-site complications were assessed. Meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager, with subgroup analyses performed according to flap type.Results:Prior abdominal scarring was not associated with increased recipient-site complications, including hematoma, seroma, infection, venous congestion, partial or complete flap loss, wound-healing delay, reoperation, or revision anastomosis. Flap viability was comparable between groups. However, patients with prior scars had a higher risk of donor-site wound dehiscence (risk ratio [RR] 1.68; P = 0.0001) and abdominal wall laxity (RR 4.19; P = 0.04). The risk of wound dehiscence was greater in deep inferior epigastric perforator–only reconstructions (RR 1.81; P < 0.0001).Conclusions:Abdominal-based breast reconstruction remains a safe and effective option for patients with prior abdominal surgery, with preserved flap viability and comparable recipient-site outcomes. However, prior abdominal scarring is associated with an increased risk of donor-site wound dehiscence and abdominal wall laxity, emphasizing the need for careful preoperative planning and optimized donor-site management.

Takeaways

Question: Does prior abdominal scarring affect the safety, flap viability, and complication rates of abdominal-based autologous breast reconstruction?

Findings: In this meta-analysis of 4574 patients, prior abdominal scarring was associated with increased donor-site wound dehiscence and abdominal wall laxity. However, flap viability and most recipient-site outcomes were comparable between patients with and without prior scars.

Meaning: Abdominal-based breast reconstruction remains a safe and reliable option for patients with prior abdominal scars, although increased donor-site complication risks warrant careful preoperative planning and donor-site management.

INTRODUCTION

Autologous tissue reconstruction plays a central role in modern breast reconstruction, particularly following mastectomy. Among available techniques, flap-based reconstruction has become a foundational approach, using autologous skin and fat, and occasionally muscle, to restore breast contour and volume. Free flaps, most notably the deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap, are widely favored because they provide natural-appearing aesthetic outcomes while preserving abdominal muscle function. By harvesting abdominal skin and fat without removing the rectus abdominis muscle, the DIEP flap has become the preferred option for many patients seeking autologous breast reconstruction.1,2

Since its introduction by Dr. Robert Allen in 1994, the DIEP flap has gained widespread adoption due to its advantages over traditional techniques such as the transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous (TRAM) flap, particularly in reducing donor-site morbidity and preserving abdominal wall integrity.2 Ongoing advances in microsurgical techniques and preoperative imaging have further broadened the applicability of the DIEP flap, including in patients with prior abdominal surgery or scarring.3–5 Several studies have demonstrated that careful surgical planning, incorporating detailed vascular mapping and strategic perforator selection, can preserve flap viability and does not inherently increase complication rates in these patients. In parallel, enhanced recovery after surgery protocols have been successfully implemented in abdominal-based breast reconstruction, contributing to shorter lengths of hospital stay and reduced opioid use without compromising outcomes.

Despite an expanding body of literature supporting the safety and efficacy of abdominal-based flaps in patients with prior abdominal scarring, substantial variability in study design, patient selection, and outcome reporting has hampered the ability to draw definitive conclusions. Heterogeneity in patient characteristics, surgical techniques, and scar descriptions further limits the generalizability of existing findings. Given that prior abdominal surgery was historically regarded as a relative or absolute contraindication to abdominal-based reconstruction, a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence remains necessary.5 This need is increasingly relevant as demand for autologous breast reconstruction continues to rise and more patients present with complex histories of abdominal surgery.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to directly compare outcomes of abdominal-based free-flap breast reconstruction in patients with and without prior abdominal surgery, including both DIEP-only and mixed flap cohorts. Although individual studies have reported variable findings, no previous work has systematically synthesized the existing evidence to clarify whether preexisting abdominal scars significantly affect flap viability, donor-site morbidity, or postoperative complications. By pooling and critically evaluating the available data, this review seeks to elucidate the true impact of prior abdominal operations on the safety and reliability of abdominal-based breast reconstruction and to inform surgical planning and decision-making in this increasingly common patient population.

Accordingly, this systematic review aimed to evaluate the outcomes of abdominal-based flap breast reconstruction in patients with and without prior abdominal scars. The primary objective was to assess the impact of abdominal scarring on flap viability, complication rates, and overall surgical success. Secondary objectives included evaluating the influence of scar type, location, and extent on postoperative outcomes, as well as identifying strategies that may enhance surgical planning and patient selection.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate flap-related complications at both the recipient and donor sites as primary outcomes, including hematoma, seroma, surgical-site infection, venous congestion, fat necrosis, partial flap loss, complete flap loss, wound-healing delay, reoperation, and revision anastomosis.

Literature Search Strategy

The systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.6 A comprehensive systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane databases using the following search terms: (DIEP AND abdominal surgeries) OR (deep inferior epigastric perforator flap AND abdominal surgeries) OR (DIEP AND complications) OR (deep inferior epigastric perforator flap AND complications). This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO, with the registration ID CRD420251078906.

Study Selection and Screening, and Data Extraction

All identified records were imported into Rayyan for reference management and the removal of duplicates. The remaining articles were independently screened by 4 reviewers and divided into 2 groups (A and B), using a 2-phase screening process. First, titles and abstracts were screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the second phase, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed in detail for final inclusion. Any disagreements during either phase were resolved by consensus discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Studies were included if they involved patients undergoing abdominal-based autologous breast reconstruction (eg, DIEP and TRAM) and reported outcomes for patients with and without prior abdominal scars. Articles involving animal models, nonbreast reconstructive procedures, or lacking relevant outcome reporting were excluded. Assessment of Quality and Risk Bias

Two authors (A.F.S. and J.O.A.) independently evaluated the quality of each study, and any differences were discussed and resolved by a third author (Noor H. Allababidi). To ensure adherence to systematic reporting standards, PRISMA guidelines were followed in the assessment process, as recommended by Moher et al.7 Two authors also independently assessed the risk of bias in the included articles using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) assessment tool, a validated instrument specifically designed to evaluate the methodological quality of nonrandomized studies. The tool comprises 12 items, each scored on a scale of 0–2, where 0 indicates not reported, 1 indicates reported but inadequate, and 2 indicates reported and adequate. The total ideal score for comparative studies was 24, with higher scores indicating a lower risk of bias and superior methodological quality. For each study, the scores for all 12 items were summed to generate the total MINORS score.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis was conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration). A meta-analysis was performed, including 1 retrospective case–control study and 11 retrospective cohort studies. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess complication and reoperation outcomes associated with abdominal-based flap reconstruction. Studies with zero events in 1 or both arms were entered into Review Manager as originally reported, and effect estimates were calculated using the software’s default Mantel–Haenszel approach. To explore differences between patient populations, subgroup analyses were performed, stratifying studies involving DIEP flap reconstruction from those including mixed abdominal-based flap cohorts. A random-effects model using the Mantel–Haenszel method, with between-study heterogeneity estimated using the DerSimonian–Laird τ2 estimator, was applied to account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05. Assessment of publication bias using funnel plots or Egger test was not undertaken, as fewer than 10 studies were available for each outcome, in accordance with established methodological recommendations.

Assessment of Evidence Strength

Evidence certainty for each outcome was assessed by 1 reviewer using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach through GRADEpro. Certainty was downgraded based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. One- or 2-level downgrades were applied for serious or very serious concerns, respectively.

RESULTS

Literature Findings

Twelve studies were included in this systematic review, all of which were retrospective in design.8–19 These studies were published between 2007 and 2023 and collectively included 4574 patients. Sample sizes varied across studies, ranging from 40 to 818 participants. All studies focused on patients undergoing abdominal-based free-flap breast reconstruction, comparing outcomes between patients with and without prior abdominal scars. Most studies evaluated DIEP flaps, although some also included muscle-sparing TRAM, superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA), and superficial inferior epigastric perforator (SIEP) flaps.

Characteristics of Included Studies

The patient populations across studies were generally comparable in terms of age and body mass index (BMI), with most studies reporting mean ages of approximately 50 years and BMIs ranging from 23 to 29 kg/m2. Several studies reported relevant comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease, as well as prior treatment history, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Various abdominal scars, including Pfannenstiel incisions, laparoscopic port sites, and midline vertical scars, were reported. Most studies presented flap-specific variables, such as flap weight, number of perforators, and timing of reconstruction. Despite some variation in the depth of reporting, the studies collectively provided comprehensive data on surgical outcomes in patients with and without abdominal scarring.

The primary outcome, postoperative complications, was categorized into 2 main groups: flap-related and donor-related complications. A detailed list of each is provided. Recipient-site Outcomes in Mixed Flap Studies (DIEP, TRAM, SIEA/SIEP)

In mixed flap studies, prior abdominal scarring was not associated with an increased risk of recipient-site hematoma (Fig. 2), seroma (Fig. 3), infection (Fig. 4), complete flap loss (Fig. 8), wound-healing delay (Fig. 9), reoperation (Fig. 10), or revision anastomosis (Fig. 11).

Fat necrosis rates were similar between groups (Fig. 6). Notably, partial flap loss occurred less frequently in patients with prior abdominal scars in mixed flap studies (RR 0.20; 95% CI, 0.06–0.69; P = 0.01), resulting in a statistically significant subgroup difference compared with DIEP-only studies (P = 0.004) (Fig. 7).

Donor-site Outcomes in Mixed Flap Studies (DIEP, TRAM, SIEA/SIEP)

Donor-site complication rates in mixed flap studies were not significantly different between patients with and without prior abdominal scars, including hematoma (Fig. 12), seroma (Fig. 13), infection (Fig. 14), wound dehiscence (Fig. 15), and wound-healing delay (Fig. 16).

Abdominal bulge or hernia initially demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 90%) (Fig. 17). Following a sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Wes et al,13 heterogeneity was eliminated (I2 = 0%), and no significant difference between groups was observed.

Overall Summary of Results

Across all included studies, prior abdominal scarring was not associated with an increased risk of flap loss or most recipient-site complications. However, donor-site wound dehiscence and abdominal laxity were consistently more common in patients with prior abdominal scars, particularly in DIEP-only reconstructions (Figs. 15, 18).

Analyzing Biases, Assessing Quality, and Determining the Level of Evidence

The risk of bias in the included nonrandomized studies was assessed using the MINORS tool, as originally described by Slim et al.20 Item-level and total MINORS scores are presented in Supplemental Digital Content 2. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the risk-of-bias assessment of the included studies, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E987.) Total scores ranged from 11 to 20, with the ideal score for comparative studies being 24, indicating that none of the included studies achieved the maximum methodological quality.

Wes et al13 demonstrated the highest methodological quality, with a total score of 20, whereas Mahajan et al14 received the lowest score of 11. Several MINORS items were consistently rated as 2 (reported and adequate) across most studies, including a clearly stated aim, use of an appropriate control group, inclusion of contemporary groups, and adequate statistical analyses (items 1, 9, 10, and 12). This indicates generally strong performance in study design clarity, group comparability, and analytical rigor.

In contrast, important methodological limitations were consistently observed. None of the studies reported a prospective calculation of study size, with all 12 studies scoring 0 for this item. Additionally, 10 of the 12 studies did not report prospective data collection or blinded assessment of study endpoints, indicating a substantial risk of bias in these domains.

The GRADE assessment showed low certainty of evidence for complete flap loss, fat necrosis, donor-site seroma, and donor-site infection. For all remaining outcomes, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low.

DISCUSSION

Autologous breast reconstruction using abdominal-based flaps is a well-established and dependable option following mastectomy, offering excellent aesthetic results and high patient satisfaction. Historically, surgeons have approached patients with prior abdominal scars with heightened caution due to concerns that scarring could compromise perforator reliability or increase the risk of surgical complications. However, several studies have demonstrated that prior abdominal scars do not meaningfully compromise abdominal-based flaps when surgeons use meticulous operative planning. This includes preoperative vascular imaging, such as computed tomography angiography or Doppler assessment, strategic perforator selection, and appropriate modification of flap design or incision placement as needed. These strategies allow surgeons to anticipate altered vascular anatomy associated with scar tissue and avoid compromised zones during flap harvest. This systematic review was undertaken to determine whether these concerns remain valid in the current era of microsurgery.

In this review, we evaluated a comprehensive range of recipient-site complications, including hematoma, seroma, infection, venous congestion, fat necrosis, partial and complete flap loss, wound-healing delay, reoperation, and revision anastomosis, across 12 retrospective studies comprising 4574 patients. Overall, the presence of prior abdominal scars was not associated with a significant increase in most recipient-site complications. These findings are consistent with previous reports by Zavlin et al21 and Gill et al,22 which demonstrated comparable flap viability and complication rates in patients with and without prior abdominal surgery.

Subgroup analysis revealed a significantly increased incidence of fat necrosis in the DIEP-only subgroup of patients with prior abdominal scarring. This may reflect altered perfusion dynamics in abdominal tissues that have undergone previous surgery, particularly in cases involving vertical or midline scars, as suggested by Roostaeian et al.17 Disruption of perforator continuity or collateral perfusion across scarred regions may predispose flaps to localized ischemic changes, despite preserved overall flap survival.

Regarding donor-site outcomes, most complications, including seroma, hematoma, infection, delayed wound healing, and abdominal bulge or hernia, did not differ significantly between patients with and without prior abdominal scars. In contrast, donor-site wound dehiscence and abdominal wall laxity were significantly more common in patients with prior scarring. Wound dehiscence occurred more frequently in the scarred group (RR 1.68; P < 0.0001), with an even higher risk observed in DIEP-only studies (RR 1.81). These findings may be attributed to scar-related anatomical and physiological alterations, including altered perforator trajectories, reduced regional perfusion, and weakening of the abdominal wall, as well as increased technical complexity during dissection due to dense fibrotic planes. Pfannenstiel and subcostal incisions, in particular, have been associated with higher donor-site morbidity in abdominal flap reconstruction.23–25

These observations are supported by Nahabedian et al,26 who reported compromised fascial strength and soft-tissue quality in scarred abdomens, increasing susceptibility to postoperative separation and laxity. Other studies have similarly noted reduced tensile strength of scarred fascia and dermis, which may necessitate reinforced closure techniques or mesh reinforcement in selected cases. Wes et al13 likewise emphasized the importance of tailoring surgical strategies in abdomens that have undergone previous surgery to minimize donor-site complications.

Our findings further suggest that abdominal scarring likely acts in combination with patient-related risk factors, rather than as an isolated determinant of donor-site morbidity. Studies by Laporta et al19 and Mirzabeigi et al27 have shown that elevated BMI and smoking history are stronger predictors of donor-site complications than scarring alone.

Limitations and Recommendations

All included studies were retrospective, which limits causal inference and increases susceptibility to selection and information bias. Although several studies reported baseline characteristics such as BMI, smoking status, and comorbidities, inconsistent reporting and the absence of outcome-linked data precluded adjusted or multivariable analyses. As a result, observed associations, particularly for donor-site wound dehiscence and abdominal laxity, may be partly attributable to unmeasured confounding.

The risk-of-bias assessment demonstrated a generally high risk of bias across studies, most notably due to the absence of prospective sample size calculations and nonprospective data collection. Heterogeneity observed for certain outcomes likely reflects variability in flap type, surgical technique, patient demographics, and scar characteristics. Restriction to English-language publications may also have excluded potentially relevant studies. Despite these limitations, this study represents the most comprehensive synthesis to date evaluating the impact of prior abdominal scarring on abdominal-based breast reconstruction, incorporating data from more than 4500 patients across 12 comparative studies.

Future research should prioritize prospective study designs with standardized reporting of scar characteristics, patient risk factors, and outcome definitions. Where feasible, individual patient data meta-analyses would allow more robust risk adjustment.

CONCLUSIONS

Prior abdominal scarring does not significantly increase the risk of most recipient-site complications following abdominal-based autologous breast reconstruction, and overall flap viability remains comparable to that of patients without prior abdominal surgery. However, prior scarring is associated with a higher risk of donor-site wound dehiscence and abdominal wall laxity, particularly in DIEP-only reconstructions. These findings support the continued use of abdominal-based reconstruction in patients with prior scars while underscoring the importance of focused preoperative planning, donor-site management, and patient counseling. Future rigorously designed prospective studies are needed to further clarify the independent effect of abdominal scarring and refine risk stratification in this growing patient population.
