Recruitment agency-mediated onboarding, acculturation, and contract completion among internationally educated nurses in the United States: a questionnaire survey
Authors: Jasper Erwin Tolarba, Jennifer Orbeso, Reynaldo R. Rivera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternationally educated nurses play a vital role in addressing global nursing workforce shortages and supporting healthcare systems across many high-income countries. As international nurse migration continues to increase worldwide, effective transition, onboarding, and acculturation practices have become important determinants of workforce integration and retention. In the United States, internationally educated nurses comprise a growing segment of the nursing workforce; however, variability in onboarding practices may affect successful integration and retention.AimTo examine recruitment agency–mediated onboarding and acculturation practices and their association with contract completion among internationally educated nurses in the United States.DesignCross-sectional descriptive correlational study.MethodsA 30-item survey was administered using snowball sampling (N = 76). Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations were used to examine relationships between onboarding practices, professional characteristics, and contract completion.ResultsThirty-six percent of respondents reported no pre-arrival onboarding. Key gaps included legal orientation, communication training, and mentorship. No statistically significant associations were found between contract completion and years of experience (r = −.13, p = .24) or contract length (r = .02, p = .86). Modest, non-significant associations were observed between contract completion and ongoing agency engagement (r = .18, p = .11).ConclusionsRecruitment agency practices vary substantially and may influence internationally educated nurses’ transition experiences. Standardized onboarding and sustained support mechanisms warrant further investigation as potential strategies to enhance workforce integration and retention.ImpactFindings highlight recruitment agencies as underexamined actors in workforce integration and suggest opportunities for standardizing policy and practice.

Introduction

The global nursing workforce continues to face significant shortages, prompting many countries to rely on internationally educated nurses to sustain healthcare delivery and meet growing healthcare demands. International nurse migration has become a key workforce strategy in countries such as the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia. While internationally educated nurses contribute substantially to healthcare systems worldwide, they often encounter challenges related to professional transition, workforce integration, and cultural adaptation. These challenges have implications not only for individual nurse outcomes but also for workforce stability, retention, and quality of patient care on a global scale.

The United States has long been a global destination for healthcare professionals seeking career advancement, economic stability, and professional development. Among these professionals, internationally educated nurses play a critical role in addressing persistent nursing shortages, enhancing workforce diversity, and supporting patient care delivery across healthcare settings. Recent workforce data indicate that internationally educated nurses constitute a substantial and growing proportion of the registered nurse workforce in the United States, with thousands migrating annually to meet ongoing staffing demands (Shin et al., 2024; Smiley et al., 2023). This estimate refers specifically to licensed registered nurses and does not include other nursing personnel categories such as licensed practical/vocational nurses or nursing assistants. Despite their essential contributions, many internationally educated nurses encounter significant challenges during their transition into the United States healthcare system, particularly related to onboarding, orientation, and acculturation. Understanding these experiences is essential to developing policies and practices that promote successful workforce integration and long-term retention.

The transition process for internationally educated nurses is complex and multifaceted, requiring both professional and personal adjustments. Professionally, internationally educated nurses must navigate differences in healthcare delivery models, regulatory requirements, and clinical practice standards. The shift from healthcare systems in their countries of origin to the highly regulated, technologically advanced, and legally complex United States healthcare environment can be daunting. Concurrently, internationally educated nurses often face challenges adjusting to new communities, workplace cultures, and organizational norms, which may contribute to stress, reduced job satisfaction, and early attrition (Kokorelias et al., 2025). Without adequate preparation and support, these challenges can undermine both nurses' well-being and workforce stability.

For many internationally educated nurses, entry into the United States workforce begins long before arrival. The migration process often involves credential evaluation, English-language proficiency testing, licensure eligibility determination, visa sponsorship, and employment placement. Many internationally educated nurses secure employment prior to arrival through recruitment agencies or direct employer sponsorship, while others independently pursue licensure and job opportunities. The pathway can be lengthy and complex, requiring navigation of regulatory, legal, and administrative requirements that differ substantially from those in nurses’ countries of origin. Barriers may occur at multiple stages of this process, including delays in credential recognition, immigration processing, licensure requirements, financial costs, and limited access to information regarding professional expectations and workplace culture. These challenges can influence preparedness for practice and shape early workforce integration experiences, highlighting the importance of effective support mechanisms throughout the migration continuum.

Onboarding programs are widely recognized as critical mechanisms for facilitating workforce transition and integration. Effective onboarding extends beyond basic orientation to include mentorship, role clarification, professional socialization, and ongoing support. However, many healthcare organizations rely on traditional orientation models that presume familiarity with the United States healthcare system, leaving internationally educated nurses insufficiently prepared for clinical practice and workplace expectations. Evidence suggests that structured onboarding approaches incorporating extended preceptorships, cultural competence training, and peer mentorship can improve confidence, competence, and retention among internationally educated nurses (Alostaz et al., 2024; Shiju et al., 2024). Organizations that invest in robust, tailored onboarding programs are more likely to foster inclusive work environments and sustain an engaged nursing workforce (Mosquera and Soares, 2025; Djiovanis, 2022).

Acculturation further shapes the internationally educated nurses transition experience by influencing social integration, professional identity, and sense of belonging. Acculturation is defined as the cultural and psychological changes that occur through sustained contact between individuals and groups from different cultural backgrounds (Berry, 2015). For internationally educated nurses, acculturation extends beyond clinical adaptation to include navigating differences in communication styles, workplace hierarchies, interprofessional relationships, and patient expectations (Taylan and Weber, 2022). Language barriers, implicit bias, and experiences of discrimination have been widely documented as factors that impede acculturation and professional integration (Ugiagbe et al., 2022; Iheduru-Anderson and Wahi, 2021). Social support networks, mentorship, and organizational inclusion initiatives have been shown to mitigate these challenges and promote positive acculturation experiences (Pressley et al., 2022; Drayton-Brooks and Patterson, 2022).

Prior studies consistently demonstrate that structured transition and onboarding programs are not merely beneficial but essential to internationally educated nurses’ success. Ghazal et al. (2019) emphasized that the absence of intentional transition support contributes to early dissatisfaction and attrition. Mentorship and cultural competence training function as critical mechanisms for bridging clinical and cultural gaps between internationally educated nurses’ prior experiences and United States practice expectations (Kamau et al., 2022). Similarly, extended preceptorships tailored to internationally educated nurses enhance clinical confidence and mitigate early professional uncertainty, particularly in high-acuity environments (Eriksson et al., 2023). Organizational investments such as supervisor support, professional development opportunities, and attention to work-life balance are strongly associated with retention and successful integration (Roth et al., 2021; Villamin et al., 2023).

Social integration is a dynamic, relational process that requires opportunities to build support networks and communicate effectively within interdisciplinary teams (Foo and Goh, 2024). Communication challenges in patient care and teamwork have been shown to increase anxiety and reduce self-efficacy among internationally educated nurses (Miyata, 2023), highlighting structural gaps in onboarding programs that overlook language proficiency and cultural fluency. Moreover, discrimination and implicit bias introduce additional barriers to integration. Internationally educated nurses frequently encounter discriminatory practices that hinder career advancement and diminish their sense of belonging (Balante et al., 2021). Anti-discrimination policies, cultural sensitivity training, mentorship, and professional networks have been identified as protective factors that promote resilience and engagement (Rovito et al., 2022; Ugiagbe et al., 2022).

While prior studies have examined onboarding and acculturation within healthcare organizations, the role of recruitment agencies in shaping early transition experiences remains insufficiently studied. Recruitment agencies often function as the primary entry point for internationally educated nurses into the United States healthcare system, particularly during pre-arrival and initial placement phases. Although internationally educated nurses are recruited across many high-income countries, the mechanisms supporting their migration and workforce integration vary considerably. Compared with countries that rely more heavily on employer-sponsored transition programs, regulatory bridging pathways, or publicly supported workforce initiatives, the United States frequently utilizes private recruitment agencies to facilitate migration, placement, and early transition support. These structural differences may influence onboarding experiences and should be considered when interpreting and applying the findings across international settings. However, limited empirical evidence exists regarding the consistency, scope, and impact of agency-led onboarding and support practices.

In the United States, recruitment agencies frequently serve as intermediaries between healthcare organizations and internationally educated nurses. Depending on the agency and contractual arrangement, services may include candidate recruitment, immigration and visa support, credentialing assistance, licensure preparation, relocation assistance, housing coordination, onboarding, and ongoing transition support. In many cases, agencies are contracted by healthcare employers to recruit and place nurses in response to workforce shortages, although some agencies also provide direct support services to internationally educated nurses throughout the migration process.

The financial and operational structures of recruitment agencies vary considerably. Costs associated with recruitment, immigration processing, relocation, and onboarding may be borne by employers, agencies, or shared through contractual arrangements. Because agency responsibilities, resources, and levels of engagement differ substantially, the quality and consistency of support provided to internationally educated nurses can vary considerably. These variations may have important implications for transition experiences, workforce integration, and retention outcomes.

This gap is consequential, as early transition experiences may influence integration, job satisfaction, and retention. Therefore, this study examines recruitment agency onboarding and acculturation practices and explores their relationship with contract completion among internationally educated nurses in the United States.

Research objectives

This study seeks to:1.Describe the onboarding, orientation, and acculturation practices provided by recruitment agencies to internationally educated nurses before and after their arrival in the United States.2.Determine the relationship between recruitment agency onboarding and support practices and contract completion among internationally educated nurses.3.Examine the relationships between internationally educated nurses’ professional characteristics (e.g., years of prior nursing experience), recruitment agency practices, and contract completion.

Methods and design

Study design

A descriptive correlational study design was employed to examine the transition, onboarding, and acculturation experiences of internationally educated nurses working in the United States. Exploratory correlational analyses were conducted to examine associations among recruitment agency onboarding and support practices, internationally educated nurses’ professional characteristics, and contract completion outcomes. This design was selected to capture current onboarding and acculturation practices and to explore relationships among variables where limited empirical evidence exists, particularly related to recruitment agency involvement.

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were internationally educated nurses who completed their nursing education outside the United States and had experience with a recruitment agency as part of their transition into the United States nursing workforce. The five-year inclusion criterion was selected to ensure participants’ experiences reflected contemporary recruitment, onboarding, and workplace practices, while allowing sufficient time for nurses to complete their transition and acculturation processes. This timeframe balances recency with depth of experience and enhances the relevance of findings to current workforce conditions.

Instrumentation

Data were collected using the Transition, Onboarding, and Retention of Internationally Educated Nurses Survey Instrument, a 30-item, questionnaire developed by the authors to assess internationally educated nurses’ experiences related to recruitment agency practices, onboarding, institutional support, and acculturation. The instrument includes a combination of closed-ended items and Likert-scale questions addressing domains such as pre-arrival preparation, legal and professional readiness, cultural integration, post-placement support, and perceived adequacy of recruitment agency engagement.

Content validity of the Transition, Onboarding, and Retention of Internationally Educated Nurses Survey Instrument was established through expert review by a panel of five experts, including two nurse researchers with expertise in nursing workforce and migration research and three nurse educators who were themselves internationally educated nurses. The panel reviewed the instrument for clarity, relevance, comprehensiveness, cultural appropriateness, and alignment with the study objectives and key constructs related to onboarding, transition, acculturation, and retention. Feedback from the experts resulted in revisions to item wording, refinement of response options, removal of redundant items, and strengthening of questions addressing onboarding support, cultural integration, and post-placement experiences. The iterative review process enhanced the face and content validity of the instrument prior to survey administration. The Transition, Onboarding, and Retention of Internationally Educated Nurses Survey Instrument is included as Supplementary File 1. Internal consistency reliability of the survey instrument was not formally assessed in this study and represents an area for future validation. As the instrument was designed for exploratory use, findings should be interpreted with consideration of its preliminary psychometric evaluation.

Sampling and data collection

Recruitment was conducted nationally and was not restricted to a specific geographic region of the United States. Participants were recruited through professional nursing organizations with national membership, social media platforms, and electronic flyers distributed through networks serving internationally educated nurses. Recruitment materials specifically targeted internationally educated nurses who had utilized a recruitment agency during their transition to employment in the United States. Recruitment materials were shared across multiple states to maximize geographic representation and capture a broad range of onboarding and acculturation experiences. Although no state-specific recruitment targets were established, participants represented diverse regions of the United States.

A non-probability snowball sampling strategy was used to recruit participants because internationally educated nurses are considered a hard-to-reach population for research purposes. Unlike some professional groups, there is no centralized national registry that identifies nurses based on country of nursing education or migration status. Additionally, internationally educated nurses are geographically dispersed across healthcare organizations and states, making systematic identification and recruitment challenging. Variability in immigration pathways, employment arrangements, and professional affiliations further limits the availability of comprehensive sampling frames. Snowball sampling was therefore selected as an appropriate strategy to reach a diverse population of internationally educated nurses through established professional and social networks. Participants were invited to share the survey with other eligible internationally educated nurses, thereby expanding the sample reach.

Data were collected between August 2024 and December 2024 using a secure, web-based survey platform to enhance accessibility and participation. The survey included sections addressing demographic characteristics, professional background, recruitment agency practices, onboarding experiences, acculturation support, and contract status. While snowball sampling may limit generalizability due to potential selection bias, this approach was appropriate for exploratory research involving a hard-to-reach population.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics version 30. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize demographic characteristics and recruitment agency practices. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to explore relationships among recruitment agency support variables, professional characteristics, contract length, and contract completion. For correlational analyses, contract completion was operationalized as a dichotomous variable. Participants who had completed their contract or were still actively completing their contract were coded as 1, while participants who exited their contract early were coded as 0. Because contract completion was dichotomously coded, the Pearson correlation is equivalent to a point-biserial correlation and was used as an exploratory measure of association. Correlational findings were interpreted as exploratory, given the sample size and study design, and were used to identify patterns and potential associations rather than causal relationships. Given the sample size (N = 76), statistical power to detect small effect sizes was limited. Correlational findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, because multiple correlations were examined, statistically significant findings were interpreted cautiously, with emphasis placed on effect size patterns rather than statistical significance alone.

Ethical considerations

This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the lead investigator’s affiliated institution. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically prior to survey initiation. To preserve participant anonymity and encourage candid responses regarding onboarding, workplace experiences, and contract completion, no identifying information, including home address, employer, or state of practice, was collected. Consequently, geographic distribution by state could not be reported. All data were stored securely and analyzed in aggregate form. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The results provide an overview of the demographic characteristics of the sample, recruitment agency onboarding and support practices, post-placement experiences, and exploratory correlations between study variables and contract completion.

Demographic characteristics

A total of 76 internationally educated nurses participated in this study. The respondent group was predominantly female, consistent with global nursing trends. Participants reported practicing across different states in the country, reflecting the national demand for internationally educated nurses. They received their nursing education from various countries, underscoring the international scope of the workforce. As shown in Fig. 1, the top five countries of origin for nursing education were the Philippines, India, Nigeria, Kenya, and Jamaica, which together accounted for the majority of respondents. This distribution mirrors broader global migration patterns and highlights the concentration of internationally educated nurses’ recruitment from nations with long-standing nursing export histories. Recruitment and onboarding practices of recruitment agencies.

Recruitment agencies also differed in the financial and logistical support they provided. Most funded critical services, such as visa petitions and licensure fees, but support for health exams, travel costs, and post-arrival needs was less consistently offered. While many agencies arranged travel logistics and offered temporary accommodation, only some provided longer-term support such as housing allowances or orientation to community resources.

Orientation programs often included training on clinical systems such as electronic health records and medication safety. However, fewer addressed cultural integration, interdisciplinary communication, or implicit bias despite their central role in internationally educated nurses’ acculturation.

Post-placement support and retention

Ongoing support after placement also varied. While most internationally educated nurses reported regular agency check-ins, the frequency and format varied. Few reported access to dedicated mentors or structured evaluations coordinated with hospital staff, suggesting limited follow-through beyond initial placement. As illustrated in Table 3, linkages with professional organizations and a dedicated internationally educated nurses mentor/support person were less commonly offered, representing a missed opportunity to support internationally educated nurses’ integration.

Most internationally educated nurses were bound by two- to three-year contracts, and while the majority were still completing or had fulfilled their service, a notable 20% exited their contracts early. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in study designs, healthcare systems, and employment arrangements, international studies have similarly identified retention and turnover as persistent challenges among migrant nurses. These findings suggest that contract completion may be influenced by factors extending beyond contractual obligations, including workplace environment, professional support, job satisfaction, and opportunities for social and professional integration (Villamin, 2023; Woodward and Willgerodt, 2022). Reasons included dissatisfaction with pay, toxic work environments, insufficient hours, or the need to relocate to be closer to family. These challenges underscore the importance of supportive work environments and flexible career pathways in retaining internationally educated nurses.

Summary of key findings

Collectively, the results indicate that internationally educated nurses enter the United States workforce with substantial clinical experience but encounter highly variable recruitment agency onboarding and support practices. While many agencies provide essential regulatory and logistical assistance, preparation related to professional expectations, cultural integration, and sustained post-placement support is inconsistently implemented. Contract completion does not appear to be driven by years of experience or contract length alone, suggesting that ongoing support and workplace context may play a more influential role in retention outcomes.

Discussion

This study adds to the growing body of literature on internationally educated nurses by illuminating the role of recruitment agencies in shaping transition, onboarding, and acculturation experiences within the United States healthcare system. While prior research has largely focused on organizational onboarding and workplace integration, the present findings extend existing knowledge by examining recruitment agency practices as a critical but underexplored component of the internationally educated nurse transition continuum. Despite entering the United States workforce with substantial clinical experience, internationally educated nurses in this study reported wide variability in pre-arrival preparation and post-placement support, underscoring systemic gaps that may influence integration and retention outcomes. In addition, although many participants entered the United States workforce with substantial prior clinical experience, the findings suggest that professional experience alone may not be sufficient to facilitate successful integration.

The findings should also be interpreted within the context of the United States nurse migration system, where recruitment agencies frequently play a prominent role in facilitating international nurse recruitment, immigration processes, placement, and transition support. The involvement of recruitment agencies varies considerably across countries. In some jurisdictions, internationally educated nurses are recruited directly by healthcare organizations or through nationally coordinated workforce programs, while in others recruitment agencies serve as key intermediaries throughout the migration and integration process. Consequently, the influence of recruitment agencies on onboarding and acculturation experiences may differ across international contexts. Nevertheless, the broader findings identified in this study, including the importance of structured onboarding, cultural integration, and sustained support, remain relevant to global discussions regarding internationally educated nurses’ workforce integration.

One of the most salient findings was that more than one-third of respondents reported receiving no formal onboarding prior to arrival in the United States. This lack of preparation is concerning given the regulatory complexity, professional accountability structures, and cultural expectations that characterize United States nursing practice. Consistent with prior studies, the absence of structured transition support has been associated with increased stress, professional uncertainty, and early attrition among internationally educated nurses (Ghazal et al., 2019; Kamau et al., 2022). The present findings reinforce the importance of intentional onboarding and suggest that, for many internationally educated nurses, foundational preparation is needed before their first day of clinical practice.

Importantly, this study challenges the implicit assumption in much of the literature that the burden of onboarding rests solely with healthcare employers. By focusing on recruitment agencies, our findings expand the accountability lens. While institutions like hospitals have often been studied for their role in onboarding and integration, this research shows that the groundwork established by recruitment agencies may meaningfully affect how well internationally educated nurses adapt upon arrival. This is particularly evident in the uneven inclusion of key topics such as the scope of practice, accountabilities, and the complete range of nursing responsibilities and clinical activities that an internationally educated nurse is legally authorized to perform under state licensure. These are foundational elements of nursing practice in the United States, and their absence from many onboarding programs suggests a fundamental disconnect between the regulatory complexity of American healthcare and the preparation internationally educated nurses receive abroad.

While recruitment agencies and healthcare organizations play important roles in facilitating transition, accountability for successful internationally educated nurse integration extends beyond these stakeholders. Regulatory bodies, educational institutions, professional organizations, and policymakers also contribute to the preparation, support, and ongoing development of internationally educated nurses. A coordinated, multi-stakeholder approach may be necessary to ensure that internationally educated nurses receive consistent preparation, orientation, and support throughout the migration and integration process.

Although the majority of internationally educated nurses in this study had extensive professional experience, their ability to thrive in United States settings was heavily influenced by the quality of onboarding. This highlights a persistent misconception that experience alone is sufficient for a successful transition. Eriksson et al. (2023) argue that orientation programs tailored to internationally educated nurses, including extended preceptorships, are crucial for building clinical confidence in a new healthcare environment. Our data reinforce this, showing that even highly experienced nurses struggle when system-level orientation is insufficient or absent. This has implications not only for professional performance but also for emotional well-being and long-term retention.

Another important finding was the critical role of acculturation, which was inadequately supported in many cases. Respondents described challenges related to communication, cultural norms, and social isolation – consistent with prior findings by Foo and Goh (2024) and Miyata (2023), who highlighted the importance of language and social integration in internationally educated nurse adaptation. However, in contrast to their findings, which are often situated within hospital or educational contexts, our study demonstrates that these challenges often begin before the first clinical shift. Cultural competence and communication training were included inconsistently, despite robust evidence that these interventions reduce implicit bias and support professional identity formation (Balante et al., 2021; Rovito et al., 2022). The lack of structured cultural immersion programming may contribute to a sense of disconnection and marginalization, particularly for internationally educated nurses who are navigating a new country without family or peer support.

Unlike some countries where orientation and transition programs may be standardized or overseen by regulatory bodies, onboarding and orientation in the United States are typically employer driven. Healthcare organizations are primarily responsible for providing orientation to newly hired nurses, including internationally educated nurses, while state boards of nursing focus on licensure and regulatory compliance rather than workforce integration. Recruitment agencies may supplement employer-based orientation through pre-arrival preparation, relocation assistance, and transition support; however, there are no national standards governing agency-led onboarding activities. Consequently, the content, duration, and quality of orientation experiences may vary considerably across employers and recruitment agencies.

Previous studies have documented that internationally educated nurses may experience subtle and overt forms of discrimination that can affect workplace integration, professional advancement, and sense of belonging (Balante et al., 2021; Ugiagbe et al., 2022). Although discrimination was not specifically examined in the present study, these findings from the broader literature highlight additional factors that may influence internationally educated nurse transition and integration experiences. Future research should explore the relationship between recruitment agency support, workplace experiences, and perceived discrimination among internationally educated nurses.

Retention challenges in this study were pronounced as well. One in five internationally educated nurses reported leaving their contracted role before completion, citing dissatisfaction with pay, geographic isolation, toxic workplace culture, and high patient acuity as common reasons. These findings echo those of Villamin et al. (2023), who highlighted how retention is influenced not only by initial onboarding but also by ongoing support, workplace climate, and the alignment of job expectations with actual working conditions. While contractual obligations tied internationally educated nurses to their employers for an average of two to three years, the lack of consistent agency follow-up meant that many of the stressors leading to premature attrition went unaddressed. Regular agency check-ins and other forms of ongoing support demonstrated small positive correlations with contract completion, but these associations were not statistically significant. Therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the effectiveness of these practices in promoting retention. Nevertheless, the observed patterns may warrant further investigation in future studies.

Although no statistically significant relationships were identified between the examined variables and internationally educated nurse contract completion, small positive correlations were observed for the presence of a travel specialist/coordinator and regular agency check-ins. Furthermore, the single statistically significant correlation observed elsewhere in the analysis should be interpreted cautiously because multiple comparisons were conducted without statistical adjustment. These findings suggest that continuity of recruitment agency engagement beyond initial placement may be relevant to contractual follow-through. The absence of an association between years of prior nursing experience and contract completion further indicates that clinical tenure alone does not predict retention outcomes among internationally educated nurses. Similarly, the lack of correlation between contract length and completion suggests that extended contractual obligations do not independently ensure retention. Collectively, these findings reinforce the importance of viewing recruitment agency involvement as an ongoing support process and highlight the need for longitudinal research to examine how sustained agency engagement interacts with workplace conditions to influence retention trajectories.

The present study did not collect agency-specific characteristics such as geographic location, years in operation, organizational size, business model, or reputation. Therefore, it was not possible to determine whether these factors influenced onboarding quality or integration experiences. Variability in support may reflect differences in agency resources, contractual obligations with healthcare organizations, staffing models, organizational priorities, or regulatory and market considerations. Future research should examine agency-level characteristics and their relationship to internationally educated nurse integration, retention, and workforce outcomes.

In this sample, the absence of consistent pre-arrival and post-placement supports suggests that standardization across recruitment agencies may represent an important strategy to reduce variability in transition experiences. Current models are inconsistent and largely unregulated, placing the burden of successful integration on the internationally educated nurse rather than on the system that recruits them. As such, policy-level interventions may be warranted. National guidelines that establish minimum onboarding requirements, such as legal orientation, cultural competence training, and post-placement support, could reduce disparities in transition outcomes. These should be jointly developed by licensing boards, healthcare institutions, and recruitment agencies to ensure alignment with workforce expectations.

Although recruitment models and regulatory frameworks vary across countries, the broader implications of this study extend beyond the United States. The findings reinforce international evidence highlighting the importance of structured onboarding, cultural adaptation support, mentorship, and sustained organizational engagement in facilitating successful internationally educated nurses’ integration. While the specific mechanisms used to support internationally educated nurses may differ across healthcare systems, the need for coordinated transition support appears to be a consistent finding across the global nursing workforce literature.

This study also highlights several areas for future research. First, longitudinal studies tracking internationally educated nurses from pre-departure to post-integration could illuminate how different phases of transition influence long-term outcomes such as retention, job satisfaction, and career progression. Second, qualitative research is needed to explore the lived experiences of internationally educated nurses who did not complete their contracts, as their perspectives may shed light on critical failure points in the onboarding process. Finally, comparative studies between agency-hired and directly employed internationally educated nurses could help isolate which practices are most effective and scalable.

Findings suggest that successful integration of internationally educated nurses into the United States healthcare workforce requires more than clinical competence or contractual obligation. Recruitment agencies play a pivotal role in shaping early transition experiences, yet their practices remain inconsistent and largely unregulated. Standardizing recruitment agency onboarding and support practices and strengthening partnerships with healthcare organizations represent critical opportunities to enhance internationally educated nurses’ integration, promote retention, and support a stable, culturally responsive nursing workforce.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The use of a non-probability snowball sampling strategy may limit generalizability, and self-reported data are subject to recall and response bias. Because recruitment was conducted using snowball sampling through professional networks and social media platforms, the total number of individuals who received or viewed the survey invitation could not be determined. Consequently, a response rate could not be calculated. Additionally, recruitment through social media platforms, professional organizations, and peer networks may have introduced selection bias. Nurses who remained connected to professional communities and were willing to participate in the survey may have experienced more successful transitions and workforce integration than those who disengaged from the profession, terminated employment early, or experienced substantial onboarding challenges. Consequently, the sample may underrepresent individuals with more negative transition experiences, potentially attenuating observed associations between onboarding practices and retention-related outcomes.

This study did not include a comparison group of internationally educated nurses who were directly employed by healthcare institutions without recruitment agency involvement. Therefore, findings cannot determine whether the variability observed is specific to agency-mediated recruitment pathways or reflects broader challenges experienced by internationally educated nurses during transition into the United States healthcare workforce. Lastly, the exploratory nature of the correlational analyses and the modest sample size limit statistical power. Despite these limitations, the findings contribute preliminary insights into recruitment agency practices.

Conclusion

This study identifies recruitment agencies as an important yet underexamined component of the internationally educated nurse transition process. Findings indicate substantial variability in onboarding and support practices, with potential implications for integration experiences. While clinical experience alone does not appear to predict contract completion, ongoing support mechanisms may warrant further investigation. Future research should examine longitudinal outcomes and evaluate standardized transition models across recruitment and organizational contexts.
