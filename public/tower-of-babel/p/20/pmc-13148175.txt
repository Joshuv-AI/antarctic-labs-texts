Boolean logic links chromatin accessibility states to gene expression variability across cell types
Authors: Seyed Amir Malekpour, Mohieddin Jafari, Mehdi Sadeghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiomic technologies have enabled simultaneous profiling of chromatin accessibility and gene expression, providing a rich foundation for decoding cis-regulatory logic. However, existing approaches emphasize pairwise correlations and overlook the combinatorial, multilocus accessibility states that govern gene regulation in a context-specific manner. We present ocrRBBR, a computational framework that infers interpretable Boolean rules from combinations of accessible open chromatin regions (OCRs) to explain gene expression variability across diverse cell types. These rules [e.g. ] capture regulatory logic ranging from synergistic to additive (redundant) enhancer interactions, revealing how OCR combinations drive cell-type-specific gene expression. Applying ocrRBBR to paired ATAC–RNA data, we uncover thousands of Boolean rules linked to both lineage-restricted and broadly expressed genes. We show that cell-type-specific genes rely on fewer but more selective Boolean rules that integrate signals from a larger number of OCRs, including distal enhancers. In contrast, housekeeping genes depend on more numerous, simpler rules that involve fewer OCRs, reflecting a modular and additive regulatory logic. Functional enrichment analysis reveals that genes regulated by cell-type-specific rules are enriched for lineage-defining pathways. Together, ocrRBBR offers a mechanistically interpretable and biologically grounded framework for decoding combinatorial cis-regulatory logic from multimodal genomic data.

Introduction

The integration of multiome sequencing—which concurrently profiles chromatin accessibility (via ATAC-seq) and gene expression (via RNA-seq) in the same individual cells—has dramatically advanced computational strategies for deciphering gene regulation at single-cell resolution. This powerful multimodal approach enables the simultaneous mapping of two essential layers of cellular information: the chromatin landscape (i.e. whether genomic regions are accessible or closed) and the transcriptional output of genes, offering an unparalleled view of cellular states and transitions. By jointly analyzing these data, researchers can identify enhancer–gene interactions [1–5], reconstruct gene regulatory networks (GRNs) [6–9], and infer cellular differentiation trajectories [10–12], thereby advancing our understanding of cellular dynamics and plasticity. A guiding principle in these analyses is the chromatin potential hypothesis, which posits that changes in chromatin accessibility frequently precede transcriptional activation—particularly during key cellular transitions such as differentiation or response to external stimuli. According to this model, accessibility of open chromatin regions (OCRs)—including promoters, enhancers, and other cis-regulatory elements—is often predictive of downstream gene expression [13–16]. Multiome data thus provide a critical lens for uncovering regulatory mechanisms that are frequently disrupted in disease contexts [17–20]. In cancer, for example, mutations in chromatin remodelers can rewire enhancer accessibility, leading to the misexpression of oncogenes or tumor suppressors and contributing to malignant transformation.

At the most elementary level, several approaches exploit joint measurements of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq in single cells to identify pairwise correlations [13, 21, 22] between individual OCR accessibility and gene expression levels for enhancer–gene linking. For example, a recent method employs Poisson regression to test for such correlations while modeling batch or cell-specific covariates, aiming to link noncoding genetic variants within OCRs to their target genes [17]. Traditional ATAC-seq analysis approaches typically aggregate chromatin accessibility signals across promoter regions and gene bodies (with defined flanking windows), weighting each OCR by its distance to the gene. An exponentially decaying function down-weights OCR accessibility signals as distance increases [21]. The resulting gene scores serve as imputed gene expression readouts, enabling integrated analyses of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data [21]. This integration allows joint embedding of the two modalities, revealing concordant and divergent patterns in chromatin accessibility and gene expression across cells.

While reconstructed OCR–gene associations offer valuable insights into regulatory landscapes, they often fail to capture the combinatorial relationships among multiple OCRs. Gene expression is rarely driven by isolated regulatory elements; rather, it is typically regulated by cooperative interactions among OCR complexes, including enhancers, silencers, and insulators [23]. These interactions are context-dependent, varying across cell types, developmental stages, and environmental conditions. To address this limitation, we propose a computational framework that moves beyond simple pairwise OCR–gene mappings and identifies multipartite OCR complexes through Boolean rules. These complexes synergistically govern transcriptional programs via co-binding events, long-range chromatin interactions, and context-specific regulatory circuits. We introduce ocrRBBR (OCR-driven Regression-Based Boolean Rule inference), a gene-level predictive model tailored for multiome datasets. This model enables gene expression prediction based on chromatin accessibility landscapes. Unlike conventional pairwise correlation approaches—which assess OCR–gene associations in isolation—ocrRBBR captures the collective influence of regulatory elements located both within genic regions (e.g. intronic enhancers) and in distal flanking domains.

For each gene, ocrRBBR infers a Boolean rule set that describes how multiple OCRs could interact combinatorially or cooperatively to regulate gene expression across different cell types or conditions. Each Boolean rule represents a multipartite OCR complex that synergistically activates or represses transcriptional programs, potentially in a cell-type- or condition-specific manner. For example, in the case of the Spi1 gene, ocrRBBR infers the Boolean rules and , which define distinct regulatory mechanisms in B and myeloid cells, respectively. This highlights how regulatory logic can vary across cell types, with specific enhancer–promoter interactions governing gene activation in a context-dependent manner. The OCR-derived Boolean rules are further classified and linked to the specific cell types in which they regulate their target genes. We demonstrate that these cell-type-specific Boolean rules are significantly associated with known marker genes, as confirmed by functional enrichment analysis. Notably, genes that are broadly expressed across cell types tend to be governed by a larger number of shorter Boolean rules—each involving fewer OCRs on average—whereas cell-type-specific marker genes are typically regulated by fewer, but longer, rules involving more complex OCR combinations. This pattern suggests a hierarchy in regulatory architecture: ubiquitous genes may employ flexible, redundant control mechanisms to ensure robust expression across diverse cellular contexts. In contrast, specialized marker genes rely on intricate, tightly coordinated enhancer-promoter logic, requiring specific combinations of regulatory elements—such as enhancers, promoters, or insulators—that are only co-accessible in particular cell types, reflecting the higher regulatory specificity required for defining and maintaining cell identity. ocrRBBR is a reliable tool for identifying a minimal set of OCRs that combinatorially regulate their target genes and for inferring gene regulatory mechanisms through Boolean rules based on chromatin accessibility states within these multipartite OCR complexes. Using Hi-C data, we have shown that the predicted OCRs in the Boolean rules are significantly enriched for 3D chromatin interactions with the promoter of the target gene. Each Boolean rule can be associated with a specific chromatin structure in a particular cell-type or state. ocrRBBR can be applied to infer gene regulatory mechanisms through Boolean rules in both bulk and single-cell multiome datasets (ATAC-seq and RNA-seq), where peak calls and continuous chromatin accessibility levels within these peaks are available across cells.

Materials and methods

ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data for mouse immune cells

We utilized ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data [24] from 85 primary mouse immune cell types to analyze chromatin accessibility and gene expression. For ATAC-seq data processing, sorted cell populations were used, and reads were mapped to the mm10 reference genome using Bowtie2 [25]. Nonunique, mitochondrial, and duplicated reads were then filtered using Samtools and Picard. OCRs were identified using MACS2 [26] and reads were shifted (+4 bp for the plus strand, −5 bp for the minus strand) to accurately count signal intensity in each OCR using BEDTools. This process identified 512 595 OCRs, including both promoter-associated regions and distal enhancers, providing a comprehensive view of the accessible chromatin landscape in the mouse immune system. The OCR loci are consistent across all samples (cells or cell types) within each dataset used in our study. The RNA-seq data is generated on the same sorted cell populations as the ATAC-seq data, which ensures that the datasets are matched. The process starts with total RNA capture and purification using RNAClean XP beads. After library preparation, sequencing reads are mapped to the mm10 genome using hisat2 [27]. Unmapped reads, low-quality reads, and duplicates are removed. Only genes with a minimum read count of 5 were retained for analysis, resulting in 17 535 genes whose expression is quantified across the 85 different cell types. The matched ATAC-seq and RNA-seq data [24] provide a powerful resource to study the relationship between chromatin accessibility and gene expression across 85 immune cell types spanning nine lineages, including stromal cells, stem cells, dendritic cells (DC), myeloid cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILC), B, T, T, and activated T (ActT) cell populations. These cells originate from embryonic yolk sac or adult bone marrow precursors and are sampled from various tissues, offering insights into immune system development and function.

Cell-type-specific Boolean rules

To identify cell-type-specific Boolean rules underlying gene regulation, we selected, for each gene, the top three Boolean rule sets with the lowest BIC scores. For each Boolean rule set, we extracted the rules with a coefficient > 0.4 in the fitted ridge regression model, ensuring high significance and relevance of the rule in explaining cell-type-specific gene expression. For these rules, rule values were computed across cell types using the accessibility of the OCRs involved in each rule as inputs. Boolean rules with differential values between cell types or conditions were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test applied to the rule values. These cell-type-specific Boolean rules, along with their associated genes, were subsequently subjected to over-representation (enrichment) analysis to investigate their relevance to the cell types they are linked to.

Biological enrichment analysis

Enrichment analysis was performed on genes associated with cell-type-specific OCR-driven Boolean rules to assess their functional relevance to the cell types they are linked to. Enrichment was conducted using the DAVID functional annotation tool [34], focusing on Gene Ontology (GO) categories: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). Overrepresented GO terms were identified using default settings, and significance was assessed based on adjusted P-values [33] as reported by DAVID.

Processing of transcription factor-binding sequence motifs

Transcription factor (TF)-binding motifs were obtained from the JASPAR CORE 2022 [35] and HOCOMOCO V13 [36] databases, comprising 251 mouse motifs from JASPAR and 1253 mouse motifs from HOCOMOCO. To reduce redundancy, these motifs were filtered to retain only one motif per TF, prioritizing the highest-quality motif for each TF based on information content and database annotations. Motif scanning was performed using the motifmatchr [37] package in R, with a search window of flanking the summit of each OCR involved in predicted Boolean rules. The motifmatchr algorithm identifies significant motif matches by scanning OCR sequences against position weight matrices and computing statistical significance for each match.

We used Fisher’s exact test to determine whether the presence of each TF-binding motif was significantly different between two OCR groups: OCRs involved in Boolean rules for genes expressed in lineages (commonly expressed) and OCRs regulating genes expressed in only one lineage (cell-type-specific). For each motif, a contingency table was constructed, counting the number of OCRs with or without the motif in each group. The null hypothesis assumed no difference in motif frequency between the groups. Adjusted P-values were calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control for FDRs, and motifs with adjusted P-values were considered significantly enriched. Motif logos were generated using the seqLogo [38] package in R. TF-binding motifs belonging to the same subfamily were identified and analyzed based on the TFClass classification system [39].

Single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data processing and analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data from the human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) granulocyte-sorted 10k dataset (10x Genomics) were processed using Seurat (v5) [40] and ArchR (v1.0.2) [21] in R. The raw HDF5 feature-barcode matrix was imported using the Read10X_h5() function, and RNA and ATAC modalities were separated. A Seurat object was constructed using RNA count matrices, with the ATAC assay added as a secondary layer. Quality control was applied to retain high-quality cells, defined as those with >500 detected RNA molecules and <20% mitochondrial gene content for the RNA modality, and with at least 1000 fragments and TSS enrichment of at least 4 for the ATAC modality. For doublet detection, doublet scores were first estimated using the addDoubletScores() function in ArchR, and potential doublets were subsequently removed using the filterDoublets() function. The intersection of high-quality RNA and ATAC barcodes yielded 9834 cells, which were retained for downstream analysis. RNA counts were normalized using a log normalization method with a scale factor of 10 000, and the top 10 000 highly variable genes were identified Genes exhibiting sparse expression ( zero counts) were excluded, resulting in 1829 genes retained for downstream analysis. ATAC-seq counts across 108 377 peaks were normalized by dividing each cell’s peak counts by its total number of reads in TSSs (ReadsInTSS) to account for depth variation. Finally, peak–gene associations were identified by overlapping ATAC peaks within kb of annotated TSSs using GENCODE v48 annotations. Cell-type annotation was carried out using PBMC marker genes from the Azimuth reference atlas [40].

Single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq measurements are sparse and noisy, which can confound detection of true regulatory associations. To account for technical variation in single-cell multiome data, we included cell-level covariates, such as the percentage of mitochondrial reads (%mito) and total RNA molecule counts (nUMI), in our regression model. This corrects for differences in library quality and sequencing depth across cells, ensuring that the estimated effect of chromatin accessibility on gene expression more accurately reflects biological regulatory relationships rather than technical artifacts [17]. Benchmarking and performance evaluation

We calculated Pearson correlations between predicted and observed gene expression for each gene using ocrRBBR and compared them with those from Cicero [42]. In Cicero, co-accessibility scores are calculated for all pairs of OCRs within a defined genomic distance. These scores do not directly map to specific target genes at this stage; instead, highly co-accessible OCRs are grouped into modules, and the accessibility values of peaks in each module are aggregated to compute a gene activity score for each gene (promoter plus distal region accessibility). While this gene score correlates with expression, it does not identify a minimal set of regulatory OCRs for each gene or model the cell-type-specific switches of OCRs that drive gene expression. In contrast, ocrRBBR infers a minimal set of OCRs and directly links them to gene-level regulation through Boolean rules that best explain gene expression variability across cell types.

The performance of ocrRBBR was further benchmarked against a promoter-only version of itself, which uses accessibility signals restricted to promoter regions. This comparison evaluates the additional predictive power of distal regulatory elements in explaining gene expression variability.

Hi-C analysis

In the mouse immune cell dataset, we utilized publicly available HiChIP (H3K27ac) data [43] from progenitor B cells to assess the spatial proximity of predicted OCRs. H3K27ac marks active enhancers and promoters, and HiChIP combines chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) with chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) to map chromatin interactions in H3K27ac-enriched regions, enabling the identification of enhancer-promoter interactions and 3D physical loops between these regions. We first selected highly expressed genes, defined as those with expression values exceeding 0.5 on a quantile-normalized [0,1] scale across three pro-B cell types: proB.CLP.BM, proB.FrA.BM, and proB.FrBC.BM. For each gene, we then constructed the predicted OCR repertoire by aggregating OCRs associated with Boolean rules that regulate these highly expressed genes. To ensure that the selected Boolean rules reflected active regulatory programs, we retained only those rules with output values exceeding 0.5 in the aforementioned pro-B cell types. As a comparator, we constructed a background OCR repertoire consisting of all other ATAC-seq peaks within gene bodies and flanking regions that were not included in the predicted OCR repertoire. For each gene, we calculated the average HiChIP interaction scores for OCRs in both the predicted and background OCR repertoires. This approach allowed us to assess whether predicted regulatory OCRs showed increased proximity to promoters compared to nonpredicted OCRs. The 3D chromatin structure in Naive CD4+ T cells is also derived from a Hi-TrAC (High-Throughput Chromatin Accessibility Capture) dataset [44]—an assay similar to HiChIP/Hi-C that captures chromatin interactions genome-wide. We followed the pipeline described above to assess the spatial proximity of predicted OCRs to the target gene promoters for the T.4.Nve.Sp and T.4.Nve.Fem.Sp cell types, which are most relevant to the Naive CD4+ Hi-TrAC dataset.

In the human PBMC dataset, we leveraged publicly available promoter capture Hi-C (PCHi-C) data [45] from hematopoietic cells to evaluate, at a genome-wide scale, the spatial proximity of predicted OCRs to target gene promoters. PCHi-C is a chromosome conformation capture assay that identifies promoter-interacting genomic regions using a comprehensive promoter bait library. All genomic coordinates were converted from the hg19 to the hg38 reference genome using the UCSC liftover tool [46]. We used PCHi-C interactions in relevant hematopoietic cell types to independently validate the regulatory OCRs predicted by ocrRBBR for T cell subpopulations, monocytes, and B cells. For each PBMC subpopulation, we first selected highly expressed genes, defined as those with expression values exceeding 0.5 on a quantile-normalized [0,1] scale in at least 25% of constituent cells. We then constructed the predicted OCR repertoire by aggregating OCRs associated with Boolean rules that regulate these highly expressed genes. To ensure the selected rules reflected active regulatory programs, we retained only those Boolean rules with output values exceeding 0.5 in at least 25% of cells within the same subpopulation. As a comparator, we constructed a background OCR repertoire consisting of all other scATAC-seq peaks within the gene body and flanking regions that were not included in the predicted OCR repertoire. For each gene, we calculated the average PCHi-C interaction scores for OCRs in both the predicted and background OCR repertoires. This allowed us to assess whether the predicted regulatory OCRs showed increased proximity to promoters compared to nonpredicted OCRs.

Computing gene scores from OCR-driven Boolean rules

Current tools calculate gene scores by aggregating chromatin accessibility signals from OCRs located near gene TSS [21, 29, 30]. However, such tools often oversimplify the complexity of cis-regulatory logic and fail to identify the most relevant OCRs that combinatorially regulate their target genes in a cell-type-specific manner by switching OCR accessibility on or off. In contrast, ocrRBBR prioritizes the most relevant OCRs from a large pool of candidates and infers Boolean rules among them, effectively identifying the accessibility patterns—on or off—that govern gene regulation in specific cellular contexts. For example, ocrRBBR captures the cell-type-specific regulatory logic of Rag2 with the Boolean rules , corresponding to regulatory programs in T and B cells, respectively.

In this section, we demonstrate that gene scores derived from cell-type-specific, OCR-driven Boolean rules (Fig. 5A) correlate strongly with gene expression levels (Fig. 5B), highlighting the biological relevance of the identified regulatory modules. To achieve this, we classified and identified cell-type-specific Boolean rules that underlie gene regulatory programs across distinct cell types. Specifically, we performed the following steps: (i) for each gene, we selected the top three Boolean rule sets with the lowest BIC scores; (ii) for each Boolean rule, we computed rule values across cell types based on the accessibility of the corresponding OCRs; and (iii) we applied the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify rules whose output values, based on the chromatin accessibility of OCRs, differ significantly between two cell types or conditions. We employed specific thresholds for the Wilcoxon rank-sum test P-values and the log fold change []. To correct for multiple testing, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [33] to compute adjusted P-values. To assess the regulatory programs identified by ocrRBBR, Fig. 5A shows a heatmap of gene scores derived from 1194 cell-type-specific Boolean rules (rows) associated with 661 genes. These rules were selected based on a P-value < 0.05 and (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section). In this heatmap, rows represent Boolean rules associated with each gene, and columns correspond to cell types. The diverging color scale, with blue indicating low gene scores and red indicating high gene scores, highlights cell-type-specific regulatory patterns underlying gene expression variability. To further examine the relationship between Boolean rule outputs and gene expression, Fig. 5B presents a heatmap of gene expression for the same set of 661 genes. The strong concordance observed across cell types demonstrates that OCR-driven Boolean rules effectively capture gene regulatory programs. Notably, chromatin accessibility patterns within defined regulatory regions align closely with gene expression levels, underscoring the biological relevance of the identified regulatory modules.

ocrRBBR selects genes whose expression patterns can be mechanistically explained by cell-type–specific accessibility of promoters and enhancers. Consequently, genes with highly discriminative regulatory architectures—but not necessarily strong differential expression—are preferentially identified. Importantly, ATAC-seq provides biological signal that RNA alone cannot capture: many regulatory genes, including key TFs and cell-type-specific markers, often show low or moderate expression yet exhibit highly specific enhancer–promoter accessibility patterns. Such genes are typically missed by expression-based ranking but are recovered by ocrRBBR because their regulatory logic is cell-type specific even when RNA abundance is modest. Thus, ATAC-seq adds meaningful discriminatory power by revealing regulatory modules and identifying genes whose cell-type specificity arises from distinct chromatin configurations rather than expression magnitude. This enables ocrRBBR to detect biologically important regulatory or marker genes that would not be prioritized by RNA-based analyses alone.

Complexity of OCR-driven Boolean rules reflects gene expression variability

ocrRBBR offers an interpretable framework for capturing regulatory patterns in gene expression, and understanding the complexity of these patterns can provide valuable insights into regulatory specificity and redundancy. To explore how regulatory complexity relates to gene expression specificity, we examined the complexity of Boolean rules in relation to the diversity of blood cell lineages in which a gene is highly expressed (hereafter referred to as lineage breadth, defined as the number of distinct blood lineages where the gene shows high expression). We quantified the complexity of OCR-driven Boolean rules using two metrics per gene: rule number and rule length. These metrics reflect not only the combinatorial nature of the inferred regulatory OCRs but also the potential regulatory burden required to maintain expression across diverse cellular contexts. By relating rule complexity to lineage breadth, we aimed to assess whether broadly expressed genes are governed by simpler or more elaborate regulatory logic. A gene was considered highly expressed in a given blood lineage if it was expressed in at least 25% of the constituent cell types, with expression levels exceeding a threshold of 0.5 on a quantile-normalized [0,1] scale. This criterion was used to compute the lineage breadth for each gene.

To evaluate gene regulation complexity, we analyzed the average number and length of OCR-driven rules used to model the expression patterns of target genes (Fig. 7A and B). These values are plotted against the number of cell lineages in which each gene is expressed. Figure 7C shows the distribution of cell lineage diversity, where each column represents a gene group defined by its expression across specific lineages (marked in black). Figure 7D and E present violin plots of rule number and rule length across gene groups with increasing lineage breadth. These analyses suggest that genes expressed across a broader range of cell lineages require more Boolean rules to explain their expression patterns. A Pearson correlation of 0.85 () was observed between the average rule number and the number of lineages in which each gene is expressed. In contrast, the Boolean rules themselves tend to be shorter (i.e. involve fewer OCRs per rule), as reflected by a negative Pearson correlation of –0.73 () between average rule length and lineage breadth. Based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, the average rule number for genes expressed in only one lineage is significantly lower than that for genes expressed in 2, 3, or lineages, with P-values of 0.045, 0.012, and 0.007, respectively. Conversely, the average rule length for genes expressed in lineages is significantly lower than that for genes expressed in 3, 2, or 1 lineage, with P-values of 0.0381, 0.0043, and 0.0012, respectively.

This pattern reveals distinct modes of regulatory logic. Cell-type-specific genes (e.g. markers) are often governed by a smaller number of highly specific rules, each involving multiple OCRs—likely including distal enhancers [62]. These OCRs may exhibit unique accessibility profiles that tightly control expression within narrow contexts. Conversely, genes expressed broadly across lineages, such as housekeeping genes, tend to be regulated by a larger number of simpler rules, each involving fewer OCRs. This modular and potentially redundant regulatory structure is consistent with an OR-type logic [63].

These findings align with recent observations that developmental or cell-type-specific genes often rely on nonadditive regulatory interactions—either super-additive [64–67] or sub-additive [68]—in which the combined activity of multiple enhancers differs from the sum of their individual effects [69]. Such cooperative or antagonistic enhancer logic enables fine-tuned, context-dependent transcription, consistent with the specialized expression profiles of developmental genes. Supporting this, these genes show enrichment for nonredundant, combinatorial regulation at cell-type-specific loci [70]. Notably, only 14% of enhancer–promoter interactions in these genes are invariant across tissues, underscoring their reliance on tissue-specific regulatory architectures [71]. In contrast, housekeeping genes, which are expressed broadly across cell types, are predominantly regulated by enhancers acting in an additive manner, where each enhancer contributes independently to gene expression [64]. This additive logic supports robust, modular, and often redundant regulation [72], fitting for genes that must be reliably active across diverse cellular contexts. Conversely, among genes modeled with two Boolean rules (blue box), rule length decreases with lineage breadth. These patterns reinforce the idea that regulatory complexity scales with expression breadth through distinct mechanisms: additive redundancy in broadly expressed genes and combinatorial specificity in lineage-restricted genes.

In Supplementary Fig. S9, we present an example of OCR-driven Boolean rules with additive redundancy for LEF1. ocrRBBR infers that LEF1 is regulated by two OCRs, P1 and P2, located at chr4:108165117–108169957 and chr4:108170508–108173850, respectively, in the vicinity of the gene TSS. These OCRs jointly regulate LEF1 expression through the Boolean rule set , with additive redundancy (, ).

We demonstrate that gene scores derived from cell-type-specific, OCR-driven Boolean rules (Supplementary Fig. S10A) correlate strongly with gene expression levels (Supplementary Fig. S10B), highlighting the biological relevance of the identified regulatory modules. To achieve this, we classified and identified cell-type-specific Boolean rules that underlie gene regulatory programs across distinct cell types. Supplementary Fig. S10A shows a heatmap of gene scores derived from 983 cell-type-specific Boolean rules (rows) associated with 245 genes. In this heatmap, rows represent Boolean rules associated with each gene, and columns correspond to cells. The diverging color scale, with blue indicating low gene scores and red indicating high gene scores, highlights cell-type-specific regulatory patterns underlying gene expression variability. To further examine the relationship between Boolean rule outputs and gene expression, Supplementary Fig. S10B presents a heatmap of gene expression for the same set of 245 genes. The strong concordance observed across cell types demonstrates that OCR-driven Boolean rules effectively capture gene regulatory programs. Notably, chromatin accessibility patterns within defined regulatory regions align closely with gene expression levels, underscoring the biological relevance of the identified regulatory modules. Functional enrichment analyses demonstrate that genes associated with cell-type-specific OCR-driven Boolean rules are enriched for transcriptional programs that closely align with the known functional identities of each immune population (Supplementary Fig. S11). These enrichment patterns highlight lineage-defining roles, such as antigen presentation in B cells, antigen-specific activation in T cells, cytokine production in DCs, inflammation in monocytes, and cytotoxicity in NK cells.

The regulatory relationships observed in the mouse bulk (“static”) dataset—such as the number and structure of inferred Boolean rules and their associations with gene activity across blood lineages—are not expected to directly translate to single-cell datasets. Single-cell transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility data capture highly dynamic and heterogeneous cellular states, even within the same lineage. Individual cells vary substantially due to factors such as cell-cycle stage, activation status, stochastic transcriptional bursting, and transient regulatory fluctuations. Notably, the cell cycle itself comprises distinct phases (G1, S, G2, and M), each characterized by partially distinct regulatory programs that influence gene expression. In addition, cell-type boundaries in single-cell data are inherently continuous rather than discrete. Cells frequently occupy intermediate or transitional states—particularly at differentiation or activation bifurcation points—where regulators from multiple lineages are co-expressed. Such transitional populations, which are typically obscured in bulk data, give rise to mixed or “blended” regulatory signatures. As a result, Boolean rules inferred from single-cell datasets often reflect context-dependent enhancer usage that varies along developmental or activation trajectories, rather than the stable lineage-level regulatory logic inferred from bulk measurements. This behavior is exemplified by genes such as FOS, CD74, and LEF1 in our analysis (Supplementary Figs S7–S9). For instance, CD74 is governed by two rule configurations— and —both of which occur within B cells and monocytes, the two cell types exhibiting high CD74 expression. This pattern reflects cell-state-specific enhancer usage within these populations. Together, dynamic transcriptional variability, continuous cell-state transitions, and state-dependent chromatin accessibility explain why regulatory patterns inferred from bulk datasets differ from those observed at single-cell resolution. Importantly, the expanded diversity of Boolean rule architectures detected in single-cell PBMC data reflects a more faithful representation of regulatory complexity in heterogeneous cell populations, rather than inconsistency in the inferred regulatory logic.

Predicted OCRs show proximity to gene promoters in HiC data

We investigated the spatial proximity of predicted OCRs derived from Boolean rules to the promoter regions of their target genes in various cell types using HiC datasets. First, in mouse HiChIP data from progenitor B cells (proB.CLP.BM, proB.FrA.BM, and proB.FrBC.BM), predicted OCRs showed greater proximity to target gene promoters in 3D chromatin space, with significantly higher interaction strength compared to background OCRs, which were not associated with Boolean rules ( Supplementary Fig. S12A, Wilcoxon rank-sum test P-value = 2.2e-16). Additionally, mouse Hi-TrAC data from naive CD4 T cells (T.4.Nve.Sp and T.4.Nve.Fem.Sp) confirmed that predicted OCRs were in closer proximity to gene promoters, with a significantly higher interaction strength compared to background OCRs (Supplementary Fig. S12A, Wilcoxon rank-sum test P-value = 2.2e-16). In a parallel analysis, human PCHi-C data from activated CD4 T cells, monocytes, naive B cells, naive CD4, and naive CD8 T cells revealed similar findings (Supplementary Fig. S12B). For each cell type, predicted OCRs were significantly closer to their target gene promoters in 3D chromatin space, with higher interaction strength compared to background OCRs. Significant differences between predicted and background OCRs were observed across all cell types, with Wilcoxon rank-sum test results as follows: activated CD4 T cells (P-value = 0.0075), monocytes (P-value = 7.6e-08), naive B cells (P-value = 0.0032), naive CD4 T cells (P-value = 0.00072), and naive CD8 T cells (P-value = 0.007).

Benchmarking ocrRBBR against cicero and promoter-only models

Using both mouse bulk multiome and human single-cell multiome datasets, we quantified the agreement between predicted and observed gene expression by computing gene-wise Pearson correlations. Across both datasets, ocrRBBR consistently achieved higher correlations than Cicero, indicating improved predictive performance at both bulk and single-cell resolutions (Supplementary Fig. S13A). Similar limitations in the predictive performance of Cicero have been noted in prior studies [77]. Benchmarking against a promoter-only version of ocrRBBR, which restricts accessibility signals to promoter regions, demonstrated that incorporating distal OCRs into Boolean rules substantially enhances the prediction of gene expression variability (Supplementary Fig. S13B). For the mouse bulk dataset, the median gene-wise correlations for ocrRBBR, ocrRBBR promoter-only, and Cicero were 0.8844, 0.5467, and 0.3205, respectively. Compared to ocrRBBR promoter-only, ocrRBBR achieved a 33.77% improvement in prediction accuracy, which was statistically significant (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P-value = 2.2e-16). For the human single-cell dataset, the median gene-wise correlations for ocrRBBR, ocrRBBR promoter-only, and Cicero were 0.2149, 0.1922, and 0.0137, respectively. The observed 2.27% improvement in prediction accuracy when comparing ocrRBBR to the promoter-only version of ocrRBBR (P-value = 2.788) is biologically significant, despite potential technical noise, such as dropout, which can reduce correlation between chromatin accessibility and gene expression. However, this small yet significant improvement suggests that ocrRBBR effectively incorporates additional chromatin information beyond promoter regions, making it a more accurate model for predicting gene regulatory mechanisms. Notably, our predictions are strongly supported by independent validation using PCHi-C 3D chromatin interaction data, which confirms the functional relevance of the identified regulatory regions. Together, these results demonstrate that ocrRBBR more effectively leverages chromatin accessibility information to model gene regulation compared with existing approaches.

Discussion

Methods for inferring gene expression from ATAC-seq data commonly rely on the computation of gene scores, typically derived by aggregating chromatin accessibility signals from OCRs located in proximity to gene TSSs [21, 29, 30]. These methods assume that each OCR contributes independently and additively to gene regulation, summing their accessibility signals to approximate transcriptional activity. While effective for identifying general trends and regulatory potential, this additive framework may oversimplify the complexity of cis-regulatory logic. In particular, it may fail to capture the combinatorial interactions among enhancers, such as cooperative or conditional activation, that play critical roles in fine-tuning gene expression programs during development and cell-type specification.

To address these limitations, we introduce Boolean rule-driven gene scores that model nonadditive, combinatorial interactions among enhancers and provide a more interpretable, cell-type-specific view of regulatory logic. By linking enhancer activity to gene expression patterns across cell types through explicitly defined Boolean rules, our framework offers a mechanistic and biologically grounded alternative to conventional additive models. ocrRBBR is successfully applied to infer gene regulatory mechanisms through Boolean rules in both bulk and single-cell multiomics datasets (ATAC-seq and RNA-seq) from mouse and human. The core mechanisms captured by ocrRBBR—such as enhancer–promoter combinatorial logic, additive redundancy among accessible regulatory elements, and cell-type-specific OCR conjunctions—are broadly conserved across mammals. Therefore, although species-specific differences in enhancer sequences and TF usage exist, the fundamental Boolean-like structure of regulatory interactions inferred by ocrRBBR is expected to generalize across other mammalian datasets. The restriction of OCRs to those conserved among vertebrates was primarily aimed at reducing the FDR. This approach ensures that the OCRs included in the analysis are more likely to be biologically relevant. However, we acknowledge that species-specific OCRs may also play important regulatory roles and should not be considered nonfunctional by default. Users of ocrRBBR can adjust this filtering criterion based on their research needs, including the option to consider species-specific OCRs if relevant to their analysis. Selecting the top three BIC-ranked models helps capture multiple near-optimal solutions, providing a more robust representation of potential regulatory logic without introducing excessive redundancy. The coefficient threshold of > 0.4 was chosen empirically to retain rule components with significant predictive power, while filtering out weaker, likely noisy associations. Users can adjust this threshold based on dataset size or noise level. ocrRBBR is designed to identify a minimal set of OCRs whose Boolean combinations explain gene expression variability across cell types. In this study, we limited rule sets to a maximum of three OCRs to ensure interpretability and reduce model complexity. If users wish to explore higher-order Boolean rules, we recommend first stratifying rules by cell type or regulatory context and then applying Boolean rule minimization within each group [78]. This preserves biological interpretability while allowing more complex regulatory logic to be examined in a context-specific manner. Importantly, overlapping OCRs across different rules should not be assumed to act simultaneously; each rule represents an independent minimal combination that explains expression variability within a specific biological context. In ocrRBBR, samples do not need to originate from the same tissue or cell type. When samples are from the same cell type or tissue, ocrRBBR partitions them into more homogeneous groups, each associated with a distinct Boolean rule within the inferred rule set.

Our analysis reveals a striking contrast in the structure of regulatory logic between broadly expressed housekeeping genes and cell-type-specific marker genes. Boolean rules inferred for commonly expressed genes tend to be shorter, involving fewer OCRs but more numerous rules overall. This suggests that these genes are regulated by redundant (OR-like) regulatory logic, where expression is robustly maintained as long as any one of multiple key OCRs is accessible for TF binding. In contrast, cell-type-specific marker genes are governed by fewer, longer Boolean rules that involve more OCRs, indicating more complex and tightly coordinated regulatory programs. Such logic likely requires the co-accessibility of multiple enhancers and regulatory elements to drive lineage- and context-specific expression. These findings align with the idea that housekeeping genes rely on robust but simple regulatory logic, while lineage-specific genes depend on more elaborate combinatorial interactions to ensure precise spatial and temporal control of expression. Our findings also support a model in which commonly expressed genes are predominantly regulated by proximal regulatory elements, such as promoters and proximal enhancers, while cell-type-specific genes rely more on distal regulatory regions. This observation is consistent with the biological principle that proximal elements—located near the TSS—govern constitutive expression of housekeeping genes essential for basic cellular functions. In contrast, distal regulatory elements, often tens of kilobases away from the TSS, integrate complex combinatorial signals from the chromatin landscape and TF networks to achieve precise cell-type-specific regulation. Importantly, we found that the OCRs involved in the inferred Boolean rules for commonly expressed genes are enriched for ETS TF family motifs, whereas those for cell-type-specific genes show enrichment for AP-1 and MAFF family TF motifs. This suggests that ETS TFs play a central role in the robust, constitutive regulation of broadly expressed genes, while AP-1 and MAFF TFs are key components of the more specialized, context-dependent regulatory programs driving lineage-specific gene expression.

By offering a mechanistically interpretable framework, ocrRBBR provides a valuable tool for prioritizing cell-type-specific regulatory elements that may be implicated in complex diseases. This capability has important implications for functional interpretation of noncoding variants identified in genome-wide association studies. In particular, noncoding variants frequently reside in OCRs, but their downstream effects on gene regulation and cell-type-specific expression programs remain poorly understood. Our approach, by inferring explicit Boolean regulatory rules linking OCRs to gene expression, can help identify which combinations of OCRs are most critical for regulating genes of interest in specific cell types. This could enable prioritization of putative causal enhancers and reveal how these elements cooperate or compensate to modulate gene expression in disease-relevant contexts. Furthermore, by systematically linking distal OCRs to target genes in a cell-type-specific manner, ocrRBBR could also inform epigenetic editing strategies or TF perturbation experiments aimed at validating the combinatorial regulatory logic uncovered by the rules. The framework’s ability to highlight the distinct regulatory architectures of housekeeping versus cell-type-specific genes offers a foundation for engineering robust expression cassettes in gene therapy applications or designing synthetic enhancers that mimic natural regulatory logic. Collectively, these features position ocrRBBR as a powerful approach not only for basic discovery of gene regulatory logic but also for translational applications in disease genetics and functional genomics.

Using Hi-C data, we have shown that the predicted OCRs in the Boolean rules are significantly enriched for 3D chromatin interactions with the promoter of the target gene. While Hi-C enrichment supports physical proximity between predicted OCRs and promoters, we acknowledge that the current study remains correlational, and causal relationships cannot be confirmed without perturbation-based validation. To more definitively establish causality between an OCR and gene regulation, functional perturbation experiments—such as CRISPR-based gene editing—are needed [79]. CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) or CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) can be particularly useful for selectively modulating enhancer activity and assessing its impact on gene expression. The application of such techniques would provide valuable experimental validation of the inferred regulatory relationships. Furthermore, these approaches could have important clinical implications, as they offer potential for targeted manipulation of GRNs in therapeutic settings. Looking ahead, future directions include extending ocrRBBR to additional tissue types beyond blood, applying it to disease-specific datasets to pinpoint regulatory disruptions, and integrating other modalities such as Hi-C and DNA methylation data to more comprehensively decode the 3D genome architecture and epigenetic landscapes underlying complex gene regulation. These advances would enable an even more nuanced understanding of the interplay between chromatin accessibility, gene expression, and disease-relevant regulatory mechanisms. Our study underscores the importance of combinatorial regulatory logic in shaping cell-type-specific expression programs and provides a computationally tractable approach for decoding these mechanisms from multiome data.
