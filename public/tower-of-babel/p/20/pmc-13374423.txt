Association between GLP-1-based therapy and small-bowel transit time during capsule endoscopy: a prospective, multicentre, observational study
Authors: Fadi Abu Baker, Melhem Melhem, Osnat Kalnizki, Majd Khader, Michal Dardik, Mifleh Tatour, Rawi Hazzan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the association between glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) based therapy and small-bowel transit time during capsule endoscopy.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, multicentre, observational study of consecutive adults undergoing small-bowel capsule endoscopy for standard clinical indications. Patients receiving GLP-1-based therapy, defined as treatment with a GLP-1 receptor agonist or dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP-1 receptor agonist, were compared with non-exposed controls. The primary outcome was small-bowel transit time. Secondary outcomes included gastric transit time, capsule completion, bowel preparation quality, repeat-examination recommendation and endoscopic findings.ResultsAmong 129 participants, 34 were receiving GLP-1-based therapy and 95 were non-exposed controls. Exposed patients had longer unadjusted small-bowel transit time than controls (median 311.0 (IQR 252.8–433.0) vs 236.0 (188.0–282.5) min; p<0.001). Gastric transit time was not significantly different between groups (33.5 (15.2–51.8) vs 22.5 (11.0–42.5) min; p=0.263). Capsule completion was numerically lower among exposed patients, but the difference was not statistically significant (91.2% vs 98.9%; p=0.056). Repeat capsule recommendation (11.8% vs 3.2%; p=0.078) and clinically significant endoscopic findings (23.5% vs 34.7%; p=0.322) were also not significantly different. In multivariable linear regression restricted to examinations with calculable small-bowel transit time and complete covariate data, GLP-1-based therapy was independently associated with longer small-bowel transit time after adjustment for age, body mass index, diabetes mellitus and indication for capsule endoscopy (adjusted β+83 min, 95% CI 3.1 to 162.8; p=0.042).ConclusionGLP-1-based therapy was associated with longer small-bowel transit time during capsule endoscopy. These findings do not support routine discontinuation of GLP-1-based therapy before capsule endoscopy, but they support individualised assessment and larger studies powered to evaluate capsule completion and repeat examination.Keywords: GASTROINTESTINAL MOTILITY, ENDOSCOPY, GASTRIC EMPTYING

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP-1 agonists have rapidly become central therapies for type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity.1 2 As their use has expanded, their effects on gastrointestinal motility have become increasingly important in procedural gastroenterology.3 Delayed gastric emptying is the most established motility effect of this drug class; however, experimental and translational data suggest that these effects are not confined to the stomach.4 GLP-1 signalling suppresses antroduodenojejunal motility and inhibits the migrating motor complex, providing a biologically plausible basis for downstream effects on small-bowel transit.5

These considerations are particularly relevant to small-bowel capsule endoscopy, where study quality depends not only on gastric passage but also on timely small-bowel transit, adequate luminal cleansing and caecal completion within battery life.6 Any medication that slows upper-gut or mid-gut transit may therefore influence both technical performance and diagnostic yield. Despite the clinical importance of this issue, capsule-specific data remain limited.

Emerging evidence suggests that GLP-1 receptor agonists may exert broader effects on gastrointestinal motility beyond delayed gastric emptying, including alterations in small-bowel transit and increased intraluminal residue.7 A recent meta-analysis of upper gastrointestinal endoscopy outcomes reported higher rates of retained gastric contents, aborted procedures and repeated procedures among users of GLP-1 receptor agonists, further supporting the clinical relevance of GLP-1-associated motility changes in procedural gastroenterology.8 Clinical studies in patients undergoing capsule endoscopy have reported longer gastric transit times and a possible increase in incomplete examinations among GLP-1 users, while additional data in diabetic populations suggest that small-bowel transit may be modestly prolonged and that bowel preparation may be adversely affected.9 10 However, the independent contribution of GLP-1 therapy remains uncertain.

Findings from whole-gut motility assessments further support a more generalised effect on gastrointestinal transit. Wireless motility capsule studies have demonstrated frequent delays in gastric and whole-gut transit among GLP-1-treated patients, with small-bowel transit abnormalities observed in a subset, particularly with more potent agents.10 In parallel, the broader endoscopy literature has linked GLP-1 therapy to retained gastric contents and higher rates of interrupted upper endoscopy. At the same time, colonoscopy-focused studies and meta-analyses suggest a possible increase in the rate of inadequate bowel preparation.11,13 Together, these observations support a clinically meaningful impact of incretin-based therapy on procedural gastrointestinal performance. Still, the relative contributions of gastric versus small-bowel dysmotility and their implications for capsule endoscopy remain incompletely defined.

Most available data are retrospective and have evaluated exposure primarily at the class level, with limited granularity regarding individual agents, dose, treatment duration, timing of the last dose or dose-escalation phase. Official guidance from the American Gastroenterological Association and the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy does not support a universal approach for all patients before endoscopy; rather, these documents emphasise individualised assessment based on symptoms, procedural urgency, aspiration risk, glycaemic considerations and patient-specific factors.14 15 Whether capsule endoscopy requires more tailored preparation or medication-management strategies, therefore, remains unresolved.

Against this background, the present study aimed to prospectively evaluate the association of GLP-1-based therapy with small-bowel transit time during capsule endoscopy. Secondary and exploratory objectives included evaluation of gastric transit time, capsule completion, bowel preparation quality, diagnostic findings, repeat-examination recommendations and exploratory drug-related and dose-related patterns.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective, observational, non-interventional study was conducted across multiple Assuta Medical Centers in Israel. All patients underwent capsule endoscopy as part of routine clinical care. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. Reporting is in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidance (see online supplemental file 1 for the complete STROBE checklist).

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined pragmatically by prospective consecutive recruitment of eligible adults undergoing clinically indicated capsule endoscopy during the study period. Recruitment ended at database lock after completion of the planned multicentre observational enrolment period; therefore, the final sample represents all consenting eligible participants available for analysis rather than a sample expanded to reach a prespecified statistical target.

Adult patients (≥18 years) referred for small-bowel capsule endoscopy for standard clinical indications, including iron deficiency anaemia, suspected inflammatory bowel disease or gastrointestinal bleeding, were consecutively enrolled. Patients were excluded if they were pregnant, unable to swallow the capsule or if the examination could not be completed due to technical failure.

All capsule recordings were reviewed by experienced gastroenterologists blinded to GLP-1 exposure status.

Exposure

For GLP-1-based therapy, we recorded the medication type, the injected/prescribed dose, whether the dose had remained stable during the month preceding capsule endoscopy, and the number of days between the last injection and the procedure. No additional treatment-related data were systematically available.

Data collection

Clinical and procedural data were obtained from electronic medical records and capsule endoscopy reports. Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, ethnicity and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and Parkinson’s disease. Indications for capsule endoscopy were categorised as iron deficiency anaemia, suspected inflammatory bowel disease or gastrointestinal bleeding. Capsule-related variables included gastric transit time, small-bowel transit time, bowel preparation quality, completion status and endoscopic findings.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was small-bowel transit time. Small-bowel transit time was defined as the interval between the first duodenal image and the first caecal image. It was calculated only in examinations in which the capsule reached the caecum. Secondary outcomes included gastric transit time, defined as the interval between the first gastric image and the first duodenal image; capsule completion, defined as visualisation of the caecum during the recording period; bowel preparation quality; clinically significant endoscopic findings; and recommendation for repeat capsule endoscopy.

Bowel preparation quality was assessed from the endoscopist’s report and categorised as adequate or poor according to standard clinical interpretation; analyses of bowel preparation were performed in available cases. Endoscopic findings were categorised as clinically significant or non-significant and further classified as angioectasias, polyps, probable inflammatory bowel disease, non-specific findings or normal mucosa. Drug-specific and dose-specific analyses were considered exploratory due to incomplete documentation and limited subgroup sizes.

Capsule endoscopy procedure

All capsule endoscopy procedures were performed according to standard institutional protocols. The capsule was ingested with water following an overnight fast. Patients were allowed clear liquids approximately 2 hours after ingestion and a light meal approximately 4 hours later. The recording duration was approximately 11–12 hours, after which the recording device was retrieved and analysed by experienced readers.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for distribution and are presented as mean±SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The primary outcome was small-bowel transit time. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent association between GLP-1-based therapy and small-bowel transit time, adjusting a priori for age, BMI, diabetes mellitus and indication for capsule endoscopy. Regression coefficients are reported as adjusted beta (β) with 95% CIs. The model was restricted to examinations with calculable small-bowel transit time and complete data for the covariates included in the model; no imputation was performed. An additional sensitivity analysis was performed in which incomplete capsule examinations were assigned a value of 700 min, corresponding to the maximum observed recording duration in the cohort, and adjusted analyses were repeated. Because of the modest exposed sample size, the model was deliberately parsimonious, and the results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Given the strong association between GLP-1-based therapy and diabetes mellitus, a sensitivity analysis was performed restricted to patients with diabetes mellitus. Within this subgroup, GLP-1 exposure remained directionally associated with longer small-bowel transit time; however, the association did not reach statistical significance (p=0.11). This attenuation should be interpreted cautiously because diabetes is both a major predictor of GLP-1 use and a potential determinant of gastrointestinal motility, and the diabetes-restricted analysis was underpowered.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and values of p<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant selection followed the prespecified eligibility pathway: consecutive adults referred for small-bowel capsule endoscopy were assessed, exclusion criteria were applied and 129 eligible participants were included in the final analytical cohort.

The GLP-1-based therapy group also had a higher proportion of patients of Jewish ethnicity (97.1% vs 77.9%, p=0.022), while smoking rates were similar between groups. No patients in either group had Parkinson’s disease.

Indications for capsule endoscopy differed between groups: iron-deficiency anaemia was more common among users of GLP-1-based therapy (58.8% vs 35.8%, p=0.033). In contrast, suspicion of inflammatory bowel disease was more frequent in controls (64.2% vs 38.2%, p=0.015). Gastrointestinal bleeding as an indication was rare in both groups.

Among semaglutide-treated patients, an exploratory analysis by dose showed a numerically longer small-bowel transit time in the higher-dose group (1.0–2.4 mg) than in the lower-dose group (0.25–0.5 mg). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (median 324.5 vs 264.5 min, p=0.204).

Capsule completion rates were lower among GLP-1-based therapy users (91.2% vs 98.9%, p=0.056), and recommendations for repeat capsule endoscopy were numerically more frequent among GLP-1-based therapy users. Still, the difference was not statistically significant (11.8% vs 3.2%, p=0.078). Because these findings did not reach statistical significance, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and not as evidence of a definitive effect on completion or repeat examination. Multivariable analysis for these outcomes was not performed due to missing bowel-preparation data and the low number of events.

The overall rate of clinically significant findings was similar between groups (23.5% vs 34.7%, p=0.322). There were no significant differences in specific findings, including angioectasias, inflammatory bowel disease, polyps, non-specific findings or normal mucosa.

Multivariable analysis of factors associated with small-bowel transit time

In multivariable linear regression, adjusting for age, BMI, diabetes mellitus and indication for capsule endoscopy, GLP-1-based therapy remained independently associated with longer small-bowel transit time (adjusted β = +83 min; 95% CI 3.1 to 162.8; p=0.042). No other covariates were independently associated with small-bowel transit time.

In an additional sensitivity analysis in which incomplete capsule examinations were assigned a value of 700 min, corresponding to the maximum observed recording duration in the cohort, GLP-1-based therapy remained independently associated with prolonged small-bowel transit time after multivariable adjustment (adjusted β = +113.2 min, 95% CI 29.0 to 197.4; p=0.009).

Given the strong association between GLP-1-based therapy and diabetes mellitus, a sensitivity analysis was performed restricted to patients with diabetes mellitus. Multivariable analysis of capsule completion and bowel preparation outcomes was not performed due to missing bowel preparation data and the low number of events; therefore, these outcomes were considered secondary and hypothesis-generating.

Discussion

In this prospective multicentre study, GLP-1-based therapy was associated with significantly longer small-bowel transit time during capsule endoscopy. This association persisted after adjustment for key clinical confounders including age, BMI, diabetes mellitus and the indication for capsule endoscopy. In contrast, gastric transit time was only numerically longer and did not differ significantly between groups. Although capsule completion was lower and repeat capsule recommendation more frequent among GLP-1-based therapy users, these secondary outcomes did not reach statistical significance. Taken together, these findings suggest that the dominant procedural association of incretin-based therapy in this setting may extend beyond gastric emptying and involve clinically relevant slowing of small-bowel transit.

These results extend prior observations from the capsule endoscopy literature, which has largely focused on delayed gastric transit and incomplete examinations. Previous studies have demonstrated longer gastric transit times and increased rates of incomplete examinations in patients receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists. At the same time, mechanistic capsule data have suggested concomitant impairment in small-bowel transit and increased luminal residue.16 17 Our findings build on this work by demonstrating that small-bowel transit, rather than gastric transit alone, may represent the principal independently associated parameter. This interpretation is supported by emerging whole-gut motility data and by recent evidence linking GLP-1 receptor agonists to clinically relevant upper gastrointestinal retained contents and procedure interruption.8 Collectively, these data support a model in which incretin-based therapy exerts a more diffuse effect on gastrointestinal motility than is typically appreciated in routine procedural practice.

The biological plausibility of this pattern is well established. GLP-1 modulates gastrointestinal motor function through both enteric and central pathways, including suppression of antral-duodenal-jejunal motility and inhibition of migrating motor complex activity. These mechanisms provide a coherent explanation for why the procedural implications of GLP-1 therapy may extend beyond delayed gastric emptying or aspiration-related concerns. Indeed, recent work has emphasised that GLP-1-induced alterations in gastrointestinal transit may influence not only symptom burden but also luminal dynamics and the pharmacokinetics of orally administered medications.18

Our secondary findings, although not statistically significant, are clinically informative. GLP-1-based therapy users demonstrated numerically lower completion rates, a higher prevalence of poor bowel preparation and an increased likelihood of repeat capsule examination. The capsule completion difference approached statistical significance (91.2% vs 98.9%, p=0.056), and its direction was concordant with the marked prolongation in small-bowel transit time. This pattern does not establish a definitive effect on capsule completion, but it strengthens the rationale for a larger, adequately powered prospective study using completion rate as a primary endpoint. Because no formal a priori power calculation was performed and the sample size was determined pragmatically, the study was not powered to detect differences in secondary procedural outcomes such as capsule completion, repeat-examination recommendation, bowel preparation quality or diagnostic yield. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences in these outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of no clinically meaningful effect. While these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the limited number of events and incomplete documentation of bowel preparation quality, their direction is consistent with observations from other endoscopic modalities. GLP-1 therapy has been associated with retained gastric contents and higher rates of aborted or repeated upper endoscopy, and several studies suggest an increased incidence of inadequate bowel preparation for colonoscopy.19 20 Although the evidence remains heterogeneous, the convergence of these signals across modalities suggests that the effects of GLP-1 therapy on procedural performance warrant further targeted investigation.

GLP-1-based therapy is heterogeneous, and different agents, doses, injection intervals, treatment durations and dose-escalation phases may have different effects on gastrointestinal motility. The exploratory analysis of semaglutide dose should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The numerically longer small-bowel transit time observed with higher doses is biologically plausible and consistent with prior observations suggesting dose-dependent effects on gastrointestinal motility.21 However, the small sample size precludes meaningful inference, and the present study should not be interpreted as providing comparative or dose-response conclusions. In the present study, medication type, whether the dose was injected or prescribed, dose stability during the preceding month, and the interval between the last injection and the capsule endoscopy were recorded. However, the modest exposed sample size and incomplete treatment granularity limited drug-specific and dose-specific, as well as timing-specific, inference. Future studies should prospectively collect detailed treatment information, including agent, dose, duration of therapy, injection interval, recent dose escalation, timing of the last dose and gastrointestinal symptoms at the time of capsule ingestion.

A clinically relevant question arising from these findings is whether GLP-1-based therapy should be withheld before capsule endoscopy. Current American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) and American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) guidance does not support a single universal approach for all endoscopic procedures; instead, it recommends individualised assessment based on symptoms, procedural urgency, aspiration risk, glycaemic considerations and patient-specific factors.3 22 However, these recommendations are largely driven by considerations of gastric emptying and aspiration risk rather than by capsule performance. Our data do not support a universal preprocedural discontinuation strategy. Still, they suggest that selected patients—particularly those with prior incomplete capsule examinations, suspected dysmotility, high-dose therapy, recent dose escalation or constipation symptoms—may benefit from individualised preparation strategies. Although our findings do not support routine discontinuation of GLP-1-based therapy before capsule endoscopy, prolonged small-bowel transit may still be clinically relevant in borderline cases. In patients with slow baseline motility, constipation, prior incomplete capsule examination, high-dose therapy, recent dose escalation or other risk factors for delayed transit, even a moderate additional delay may reduce the likelihood of complete small-bowel visualisation and could potentially affect diagnostic yield. Potential strategies for future evaluation include enhanced preprocedure symptom assessment, consideration of medication timing in selected high-risk patients, optimised bowel preparation, real-time monitoring of gastric passage, use of prokinetic rescue when gastric retention occurs, and extended recording time when available. Future studies should specifically evaluate whether extended recording time, real-time monitoring, optimised preparation or selective prokinetic rescue strategies can reduce incomplete examinations and the need for repeat capsule endoscopy in higher-risk patients.

This study has several strengths. It represents a prospective, multicentre evaluation of GLP-1-based therapy in the context of small-bowel capsule endoscopy and, to our knowledge, is among the first to identify small-bowel transit prolongation as the principal independently associated parameter. Additional strengths include consecutive enrolment, anonymous capsule review and adjustment for clinically relevant confounders. This distinction is clinically relevant because small-bowel transit directly influences the completeness of the examination and diagnostic yield.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Despite prospective data collection, the observational design precludes causal inference and introduces the potential for residual confounding. This concern is particularly important because the exposed and control groups differed substantially in age, BMI, diabetes prevalence, ethnicity and indication for capsule endoscopy. Although multivariable adjustment and a diabetes-restricted sensitivity analysis were performed, these approaches cannot fully eliminate confounding by indication, diabetes severity, autonomic neuropathy, obesity-related dysmotility or unmeasured comorbidities. The diabetes-restricted analysis remained directionally consistent but was not statistically significant, which may reflect limited power but also underscores the need for cautious interpretation. The multivariable model was intentionally limited in scope because of the modest sample size exposed, and the findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. As noted above, the pragmatic sample size and absence of a formal power calculation further limit interpretation of non-significant secondary outcomes, including capsule completion, repeat-examination recommendation, bowel preparation quality and diagnostic yield. Missing covariate data were handled using a complete-case analysis without imputation, and multivariable modelling of completion and bowel preparation outcomes was not attempted due to missing data and few events. Small-bowel transit time was calculable only in examinations reaching the caecum; therefore, exclusion of incomplete examinations may have underestimated the true procedural impact of GLP-1-based therapy. However, the association remained robust in sensitivity analyses that incorporated incomplete examinations, using the maximum observed recording duration as a proxy transit value. Bowel preparation quality was incompletely documented and may lie on the causal pathway between GLP-1-associated dysmotility and incomplete capsule examination, limiting inference regarding cleansing-related outcomes. Finally, although data were available on medication type, injected/prescribed dose, dose stability during the preceding month, and the interval between the last injection and capsule endoscopy, additional treatment-related variables were not systematically captured. These included total treatment duration, prior dose-escalation history, adherence, temporary treatment interruptions and gastrointestinal symptoms near the time of capsule ingestion, which limited drug-specific, dose-specific and timing-specific conclusions.

In conclusion, GLP-1-based therapy was independently associated with longer small-bowel transit time during capsule endoscopy in this prospective cohort. Although secondary procedural outcomes were not statistically significant, the numerical differences suggest a possible effect on capsule performance beyond gastric emptying alone; however, the study sample size was small, and larger studies are needed to further investigate possible effects on these outcomes. Because our study was not powered for secondary endpoints, clinically relevant effects on completion rate, repeat-examination recommendation, bowel preparation quality or diagnostic yield cannot be excluded. The near-significant difference in capsule completion, together with the highly significant transit-time prolongation, supports the need for a larger prospective study powered for completion rate and the need for repeat examination as clinically meaningful endpoints.

Current guidelines do not support routine discontinuation of incretin-based therapies before all endoscopic procedures and emphasise individualised assessment. Our findings support this approach, as we did not observe statistically significant differences in completion rates or diagnostic yield. However, selected patients—particularly those with prior incomplete examinations, suspected dysmotility, constipation symptoms, high-dose therapy or recent dose escalation—may benefit from tailored periprocedural strategies. Prospective interventional studies are needed to determine whether extended recording time, real-time monitoring, optimised preparation or selective medication-timing strategies improve capsule completion and diagnostic performance.
