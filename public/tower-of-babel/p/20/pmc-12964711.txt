Disease- and gene-specific deep learning for pathogenicity prediction of rare missense variants in cancer predisposition genes
Authors: Da-Bin Lee, Hyun-Uk Kang, Kyu-Baek Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHereditary cancers frequently arise from germline pathogenic variants, yet only a small proportion of reported variants have been clinically classified, leaving most missense variants unresolved as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Although recent machine-learning approaches have explored disease-specific or gene-specific contexts to improve pathogenicity prediction, these models remain fundamentally limited by the scarcity of labeled data and the underutilization of abundant VUS.ResultsWe propose a deep-learning framework that integrates autoencoder pretraining with a deep ensemble strategy to improve variant pathogenicity prediction, effectively leverage unlabeled VUS during pretraining, and reduce uncertainty arising from limited training samples. To validate each component of our framework, we evaluated its performance under both disease-specific and gene-specific training setups. Experiments on ClinVar variants from BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2 showed that our framework achieved the best performance in the gene-specific setup for BRCA1—likely because BRCA1 contains substantially more gene-specific training data than the other genes—whereas the disease-specific setup yielded superior results for the remaining genes, which had comparatively limited gene-specific samples. Overall, our method significantly outperformed existing approaches. We also introduce an interpretability approach that provides variant-level importance profiles across pathogenicity classes, thereby enhancing transparency and clinical applicability. Moreover, by projecting feature-level importance scores into a two-dimensional space, we demonstrate that pretraining enables the model to learn distinctly different feature representations, illustrating how pretraining and ensemble learning synergistically contribute to improved predictive performance.ConclusionsOur framework preserves the specificity of disease- and gene-specific approaches, overcomes data scarcity through VUS-guided pretraining and ensembling, and offers interpretable outcomes that may be helpful for clinical decision support. Moreover, our results suggest a promising direction for pathogenicity prediction of rare missense variants and indicate that the proposed framework may be extendable to additional genes under appropriate data and modeling conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13040-026-00533-5.

Introduction

Hereditary cancer syndrome refers to a genetic predisposition to specific cancers arising from inherited pathogenic variants [1]. Representative examples include hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, associated with MLH1 and MSH2 mutations [2, 3], and hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, linked to BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations [4–6]. Among coding single nucleotide variants (SNVs), synonymous variants are usually benign, whereas protein-truncating variants—such as nonsense, start-loss, stop-loss, and splice-site variants—often result in substantial functional disruption. Missense variants, by contrast, alter only a single amino acid but represent the majority of coding variation, making their pathogenicity especially challenging to interpret [7, 8].

Clinically, variants are classified as pathogenic or benign based on their disease-causing potential; however, those that are confidently resolved into either category represent only a small minority [9], while the vast majority remain as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) [10]. Functional assays can support the clinical classification of deleterious variants, but despite their value, they remain difficult to scale because of substantial time and cost constraints [11]. Although the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines offer a practical framework for assessing the pathogenicity of variants, the interpretation of evidence still involves subjective judgment, leading to examiner-dependent variability [12, 13]. This challenge is especially acute for rare missense variants (minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.005), which are highly diverse yet individually lack definitive conservation or functional evidence. Accordingly, we focus on rare missense variants, for which allele frequency (AF) alone is insufficient and additional lines of evidence are required to assess pathogenicity [13]. To mitigate these challenges, machine learning approaches have been developed—ranging from genome-wide [14–17] to disease-specific [18, 19] and gene-specific [20, 21] models—and their relevance is reflected in the ACMG/AMP guidelines, which include machine learning based computational scores as supporting evidence [13].

The genome-wide approach leverages variants across all human genes as training data. Representative methods include CADD [22], BayesDel [23], REVEL [24], ClinPred [14], PrimateAI [25], AlphaMissense [15], MutScore [16], gMVP [26], ESM1b [27], MutFormer [28], SIGMA [29], PHACTboost [30], MisFit [31], and popEVE [17]. Among these, CADD, PrimateAI, and AlphaMissense rely on interspecies conservation–based weak supervision, whereas BayesDel, ClinPred, MutScore, gMVP, SIGMA, and PHACTboost use ClinVar-derived annotations. In contrast, REVEL and MutFormer are trained on pathogenic variants curated in the Human Gene Mutation Database, while MisFit and popEVE rely on population-based weak labels. However, genome-wide approaches have difficulty capturing the distinct mutational signatures characteristic of specific diseases or genes [18, 19, 32], and prior work has shown that their performance varies substantially across genes and often fails to reflect this specificity [33]. These findings underscore the need for analytic frameworks that explicitly account for disease- or gene-level context in variant interpretation.

To alleviate these limitations, disease-specific and gene-specific approaches have been proposed to capture context-dependent mutational features of particular diseases [18, 19, 32] or genes [20, 34–38]. Previous studies have shown that, in specific contexts, specialized models can outperform genome-wide approaches even when trained on substantially smaller datasets [18, 21]. Recently, gene-specific deep graph representation learning approaches that leverage protein sequence and structural context have been proposed [39], and genome-wide methods such as SIGMA also actively incorporate protein structural information. Although the disease- and gene-specific methods achieve strong performance, their applicability is restricted by training datasets that are narrowly defined for specific genes or diseases. Whereas ClinVar contains a substantial pool of VUS that could substantially expand the training space, these unlabeled variants cannot be directly leveraged by conventional supervised learning. By integrating VUS through an appropriate learning algorithm, this otherwise inaccessible source of information can be leveraged, offering a promising avenue to alleviate the limitations imposed by scarce labeled training data.

In this study, we propose a strategy that combines autoencoder pretraining with deep ensembles to improve deep neural network (DNN)–based pathogenicity prediction of rare missense variants associated with hereditary cancer. The proposed framework comprises two key components. The first involves autoencoder-based pretraining. Autoencoders are widely used for reconstructing input data to capture intrinsic feature structures [40]. Because autoencoders are particularly effective for leveraging large-scale unlabeled data [41–43], we apply them to disease-specific variants—especially VUS—in hereditary cancer genes. Since a substantial proportion of these variants lack definitive pathogenic labels, incorporating VUS into pretraining allows the model to learn latent representations beyond what labeled data alone can provide, thereby improving pathogenicity prediction performance.

The second involves a deep ensemble strategy. Deep ensembles mitigate variance and improve generalization, an advantage that is crucial when model capacity far exceeds the amount of labeled data. Although we incorporate VUS through autoencoder pretraining to alleviate this data limitation, the available unlabeled data remain insufficient to fully stabilize the models. Consequently, deep ensembling is expected to further address these challenges by reducing prediction uncertainty and improving robustness under limited training data.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section “Methods” describes the data acquisition and preprocessing procedures, as well as the proposed DNN architecture, autoencoder pretraining strategy, and deep ensemble framework. Section “Results” presents the experimental results and their interpretation, while Section “Discussion” discusses the implications of these findings, the limitations of the proposed approach, and directions for future research. Finally, Section “Conclusions” concludes the study.

Methods

Variant annotation and filtering

In this section, we describe our data preprocessing procedure and the overall workflow shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. We obtained a variants file in GRCh38 (file name clinvar_20220723.vcf.gz) from the NCBI ClinVar FTP site (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pub/clinvar/vcf_GRCh38/archive_2.0/2022/) on January 31, 2023, and filtered SNVs using bcftools (version 1.15.1). The filtered VCF file was annotated using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (version 94), SnpEff (version 5.1d), and SnpSift (version 5.1d). Annotation incorporated data from dbNSFP (version 4.2a), dbSNP (build 151), dbscSNV (version 1.1), gnomAD (version 3.1.2), Korean Variant Archive (KOVA) (version 2; GRCh38), and Korean Reference Genome Database (KRGDB) (phase 2). In total, 1,314,094 SNVs were retained and annotated.

We retained only variants with ClinVar clinical significance labels of Benign, Benign/Likely benign, Likely benign, Likely pathogenic, Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic, Pathogenic, or Uncertain significance. To ensure reliable curation, we further restricted this set to variants with one of the following review statuses: Practice guideline, Reviewed by expert panel, or Criteria provided (multiple submitters, no conflicts). SNVs with gnomAD all-population AF ≥ 0.005 were removed, while variants missing AF values were retained; consequently, the remaining SNVs consist exclusively of rare SNVs (AF < 0.005) or those lacking AF annotations. We selected SNVs that VEP annotated as missense_variant, including those simultaneously annotated as splice_region_variant. Transcript-level mapping was performed using VEP and dbNSFP. We selected variants located within 30 hereditary cancer predisposition genes used in [18]. We selected transcripts annotated as MANE Select and supported by both CCDS and RefSeq for each gene described above. If multiple transcripts met these criteria, the longest transcript was chosen, prioritizing protein length and then nucleotide length. The final dataset contained 19,227 missense variants, including 18,198 VUS, 477 pathogenic, and 552 benign. The Ensembl gene and transcript IDs are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Test variant sets

Based on the curated missense variants, clinical significance labels were grouped into two categories for binary classification. Missense variants labeled as Pathogenic, Likely pathogenic, or Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic were assigned to the pathogenic class (P), whereas those labeled as Benign, Likely benign, or Benign/Likely benign were assigned to the benign class (B).

Because gene-specific models draw training data solely from one gene, a dataset of only ~ 50 variants is too small to support reliable train/test splits. Thus, for fair comparison, we considered only genes with at least 50 pathogenic or benign variants and excluded those with fewer labeled variants. Missense variant counts for each gene are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Five genes satisfied this criterion: BRCA1 (P = 85, B = 141), BRCA2 (P = 36, B = 143), MLH1 (P = 61, B = 27), MSH2 (P = 57, B = 22), and TP53 (P = 87, B = 59). Because TP53 has been previously reported to achieve near-perfect predictive performance in [18] and exhibited similarly saturated performance metrics across all models in our dataset, TP53 was excluded from further evaluation.

Consequently, four genes—BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2—were used for model evaluation. Because model performance is sensitive to the class distribution of the test set [44], the distribution was set to reflect frequencies observed in clinical practice. Accordingly, the ratios of pathogenic to benign variants were matched to reported values: BRCA1 (0.19 [45], 0.20 [18], and 0.22 [46]), BRCA2 (0.03 [45], 0.07 [18], and 0.07 [46]), MLH1 (0.35 [18]), and MSH2 (0.28 [18]). The resulting test sets consisted of 86 missense variants for BRCA1 (P/B ratio: 0.19, P = 14, B = 72), 79 missense variants for BRCA2 (P/B ratio: 0.08, P = 6, B = 73), 23 missense variants for MLH1 (P/B ratio: 0.35, P = 6, B = 17), and 18 missense variants for MSH2 (P/B ratio: 0.28, P = 4, B = 14), as summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

To reduce sampling bias and ensure statistical robustness, test sets were generated by stratified random sampling according to the class ratios above and this process was repeated 30 times independently for each gene. Model performance was then evaluated using the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) for each of these independently sampled test sets.

Training variant sets

Two experimental setups were considered: disease-specific (DS) and gene-specific (GS), as summarized in Supplementary Table 3. In the DS setup, the labeled training data consisted of all disease-related missense variants labeled as pathogenic or benign, except those assigned to the test set. The resulting DS labeled sets comprised 943 variants for BRCA1 (P = 463, B = 480), 950 for BRCA2 (P = 471, B = 479), 1,006 for MLH1 (P = 535, B = 471), and 1,011 for MSH2 (P = 538, B = 473). In the GS setup, the labeled training data were restricted to variants from the same gene, excluding those assigned to the test set, yielding 140 variants for BRCA1 (P = 71, B = 69), 100 for BRCA2 (P = 30, B = 70), 65 for MLH1 (P = 55, B = 10), and 61 for MSH2 (P = 53, B = 8). The remaining 18,198 VUS were included as a shared pool and were used for pretraining in both setups.

Input features

We used six feature categories to predict the pathogenicity of variants: MAF, a missing-value indicator for MAF (naFlag), site conservation scores, predicted functional-impact scores, positional information, and other features.

MAF features consisted of 210 population-specific AF values obtained from four databases: gnomAD, KOVA, KRGDB, and UK10K (via dbNSFP). Missing values in the MAF feature were imputed as 0, and an naFlag was introduced to distinguish imputed zeros from true zeros. Nine site conservation scores were extracted from dbNSFP, and missing values were imputed using the median of all variants. To minimize type I circularity [47], we used five functional impact scores from dbNSFP as features, each of which is derived without supervised learning based on pathogenicity labels, namely SIFT [48], SIFT4G [49], MutationAssessor [50], LRT [51], and integrated_fitCons [52]. Positional information consisted of four relative indices representing the location of a variant within the exon, cDNA, coding sequence, and protein sequence. In addition, 11 other features were obtained from dbNSFP, dbscSNV, and dbSNP. Among these, five contained missing values. For the categorical variable APRIS, missing entries were encoded as “not_annotated.” For the Reliability_index feature, which ranges from 1 to 10, missing values were replaced with the most frequent value (10). Remaining numerical variables with missing values were imputed using the median.

After preprocessing, a total of 243 features were obtained. All numerical variables were standardized using the mean and standard deviation estimated from the training data. Categorical features were encoded using a one-hot representation. The same statistics were subsequently applied to normalize the test data. Detailed descriptions are provided in Additional File 1.

The input features used in this study exhibit inherent biological collinearity. For example, conservation scores and functional impact scores are often correlated, as variants occurring at highly conserved positions—reflecting strong evolutionary constraint—are more likely to disrupt protein function. To examine the extent of such collinearity, we computed pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients across all input features. AF-related variables were visualized separately in a heatmap constructed across all missense variants, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, with detailed correlation values and labels provided in Additional File 2. This analysis revealed strong positive correlations among AF-related features. After retaining gnomAD3_AF as a representative AF variable, we further examined its correlations with other features across all missense variants, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3. Notably, strong correlations were observed among site conservation scores. Although these metrics differ in terms of underlying species sets and computational algorithms, they consistently capture similar evolutionary constraints and therefore remain highly correlated, with one conservation score exhibiting an opposite directional scale relative to the others. Consistent with biological expectations, variants at conserved sites also tended to exhibit deleterious signals in functional impact predictions; depending on the score definition, this relationship may manifest as either positive or negative correlations, resulting in substantial correlations between conservation metrics and functional impact scores.

While removing collinear features is often recommended in traditional statistical modeling to improve parameter stability, we did not explicitly eliminate such variables in this study. Instead, we designed the proposed framework under the assumption that it could accommodate feature collinearity through nonlinear representation learning and regularization, while still achieving stable and reliable predictive performance.

The proposed pretraining strategy comprises two stages—pretraining and continual pretraining—the latter which adapts the pretrained model to a domain-specific distribution while preserving previously learned representations [54–56]. First, the autoencoder was pretrained on VUS from 30 cancer predisposition genes to learn generalizable representations. Second, it was further refined through continual pretraining using the labeled DS variants. To introduce sufficient diversity for the subsequent deep ensemble, we performed 50 independent continual pretraining runs starting from the same VUS-pretrained weights, each using stochastic mini-batch sampling and dropout to induce variability across runs. This procedure produced 50 distinct continually pretrained autoencoders, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

For denoising autoencoder pretraining with VUS, we trained the model for 40 epochs with a batch size of 200. Continual pretraining was then performed for 20 epochs with a batch size of 100. In both the initial pretraining and the continual pretraining phases, each hidden layer included dropout at a rate of 0.7 to mitigate overfitting. Optimization was conducted using Adam with the mean absolute error as the objective, a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, and a learning-rate decay factor of 1 × 10⁻³. Fine-tuning and ensembling of DNNs

The pretrained autoencoders described in the previous section were used to initialize the weights of the five hidden layers of the 50 DNNs for variant pathogenicity prediction. Each initialized model was then fine-tuned independently under the DS or GS setup, and their outputs were later aggregated by averaging, as shown in Fig. 3.

Although pretraining enhances model stability by leveraging a large pool of unlabeled variants, it does not fully resolve the uncertainty that arises from limited labeled data. To further mitigate this, we employed a deep ensemble strategy [57, 58]. The effectiveness of an ensemble depends critically on diversity among its constituent models, as averaging nearly identical networks yields minimal improvement [59–61]. Therefore, we introduced diversity using the procedure described in Section“Pretraining using autoencoders”.

For fine-tuning, dropout rate, learning epochs, and batch size were optimized, as described in the Supplementary Methods. Optimization was conducted using Adam with mean absolute error as the objective, a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, and a learning-rate decay factor of 1 × 10⁻³. Results

Performance evaluation of the proposed method

We systematically evaluated model performance across three axes: (i) pretraining versus no pretraining, (ii) deep ensemble versus single DNN, and (iii) the DS and GS fine-tuning setups. As described in Section “Training variant sets”, the DS and GS fine-tuning setups specify the data available for training the pathogenicity prediction models. Notably, even under the GS setup, labeled DS variants were used for continual pretraining, as described in Section “Pretraining using autoencoders”.

For the single-model comparison, we constructed 50 independently fine-tuned DNNs in each condition and compared performance using the mean across the 50 runs. The comparison results are summarized in Fig. 4. In the figure, PT denotes pretraining and NPT denotes no pretraining. An asterisk (*) marks the best-performing method and any other methods that are not significantly different from it (paired t-test, P ≥ 0.05). These comparisons evaluate the contributions of pretraining, ensembling, and the training data setup. Detailed numerical results are provided in Supplementary Tables 4–7.

Across all four genes, the effects of pretraining, ensembling, and the choice between DS and GS fine-tuning were largely consistent, although some gene-specific differences were observed. Pretraining generally improved performance in BRCA1 (in both DS and GS setups) and across all DS setups. Here, we first present the results for the single DNN. For BRCA1, it increased performance in both DS (0.853 → 0.897) and GS (0.890 → 0.908). For BRCA2, pretraining improved DS performance (0.860 → 0.901) but slightly reduced GS performance (0.794 → 0.783). For MLH1, pretraining substantially improved DS results (0.892 → 0.955) but lowered GS performance (0.938 → 0.802). A similar pattern was observed in MSH2, where DS improved (0.876 → 0.909) whereas GS performance decreased (0.855 → 0.659). Similar trends were also observed for the 50-DNN ensemble (see Supplementary Tables 4–7).

Comparison of the effects of pretraining and deep ensembling

To investigate why the effect of pretraining under GS varied across genes, we examined the characteristics of the GS setting. Compared to DS, GS relies on substantially fewer fine-tuning samples, with most genes—except BRCA1—having fewer than 100 labeled variants. In contrast, the proposed model, consisting of five hidden layers with up to 9,000 hidden units, is relatively large for such data-limited GS setup and may therefore be susceptible to overfitting. Accordingly, we performed two additional analyses using a single DNN to control for model complexity. First, we assessed GS performance by gradually decreasing model capacity (9,000, 5,000, and 1,000 hidden units) and directly comparing pretrained and no pretrained models to examine the effect of model size on predictive performance. Second, focusing on BRCA1—the only gene for which GS pretraining consistently improved performance—we subsampled the GS fine-tuning data to examine how pretraining effectiveness varies with training sample size. These analyses allowed us to disentangle the interaction between model complexity and data availability in GS setup.

Results from the model-size comparison (Supplementary Table 8) showed that for BRCA1, where GS training data were relatively abundant, the largest model (9,000 hidden units) achieved the best performance (AUPRC = 0.908). For BRCA2 (AUPRC = 0.806) and MLH1 (AUPRC = 0.862), a medium-sized model (5,000 units) significantly outperformed. In contrast, MSH2, which had the smallest and most severely limited GS training dataset, showed more stable and superior performance with a smaller model, with the 1,000-unit model achieving an AUPRC of 0.815. Notably, further reducing the model size to 500-unit for MSH2 led to an additional performance gain (AUPRC = 0.856; paired t-test, P = 0.013).

In the data-subsampling analysis for BRCA1 (Supplementary Table 9), pretraining yielded a significant performance gain when the full GS training set, consisting of 140 variants, was used (AUPRC: 0.949 → 0.974; paired t-test, P = 0.006). However, this benefit diminished at a training set size of 105 variants, with the difference between pretrained and non-pretrained models not reaching statistical significance (pretrained: 0.906 vs. non-pretrained: 0.905; paired t-test, P = 0.484). At 35 variants, pretraining resulted in a slight but non-significant performance decrease (pretrained: 0.811 vs. non-pretrained: 0.828; paired t-test, P = 0.268). These findings indicate that in GS setup, the effectiveness of pretraining critically depends on the balance between training sample size and model complexity.

Overall, these results suggest that, to maximize the benefits of pretraining, model complexity should be calibrated to the size of the labeled dataset. For a high-capacity model with 9,000 hidden units, pretraining effectiveness is strongly dependent on gene-specific data availability. In particular, pretraining appears to require more than 100 labeled gene-specific variants, with our BRCA1 case indicating that approximately 140 variants were sufficient. Nevertheless, for MLH1 and MSH2, DS fine-tuning with a high-capacity model (9,000 hidden units) achieved substantially higher performance (AUPRC = 0.955 and 0.909, respectively) than GS fine-tuning, even when the latter used optimized model capacity (MLH1: 0.862; MSH2: 0.856). Given this result, we did not further pursue model capacity optimization for GS fine-tuning in these genes.

Our pretraining strategy jointly leverages unlabeled VUS and labeled DS variants. To more clearly assess the contribution of VUS-based pretraining, we performed additional comparisons using a single DNN, with and without VUS inclusion (see Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). In DS fine-tuning (Supplementary Table 10), incorporating VUS during pretraining significantly improved performance across all four genes, with AUPRC increasing for BRCA1 (0.835 → 0.897; paired t-test, P = 1.5 × 10− 11), BRCA2 (0.863 → 0.901; paired t-test, P = 2.7 × 10− 4), MLH1 (0.911 → 0.955; paired t-test, P = 1.6 × 10− 7), and MSH2 (0.867 → 0.909; paired t-test, P = 0.001). In contrast, GS fine-tuning (Supplementary Table 11) showed different results from those above. For BRCA1 and BRCA2, VUS-inclusive pretraining led to performance gains, although the differences were not statistically significant (BRCA1: 0.902 → 0.908; paired t-test, P = 0.257; BRCA2: 0.759 → 0.783; paired t-test, P = 0.080). Notably, for MLH1 and MSH2, VUS-based pretraining resulted in substantial performance degradation (MLH1: 0.933 → 0.802; MSH2: 0.837 → 0.659), and models without any pretraining achieved the best performance in GS fine-tuning (MLH1: 0.938; MSH2: 0.855; see Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). These results indicate that under GS setup with extremely limited labeled data, both VUS-based and non-VUS pretraining can negatively affect downstream performance. Overall, pretraining consistently benefited DS across all four genes, while its effect on GS was strongly gene-dependent.

Deep ensembling provided additional performance gains in most setups compared with single DNNs (see Supplementary Tables 4–7). For BRCA1, ensembling improved performance across all configurations by 0.009–0.066, except for the DS fine-tuning no pretraining condition. For BRCA2, ensembles improved all configurations by 0.010–0.050. For MLH1, performance gains ranged from 0.009 to 0.065 across all setups. For MSH2, ensemble effects were minimal under the DS condition but were clearly beneficial under GS for both pretraining (0.659 → 0.740) and no pretraining (0.855 → 0.872).

Although the pretrained 50-DNN ensemble yielded no improvement for MSH2 compared to the pretrained single DNN (0.909 vs. 0.909; see Supplementary Table 7), the reported single-DNN performance corresponds to the mean AUPRC over 50 independently trained single DNNs, indicating that the two approaches are statistically comparable. To assess robustness, we further compared the ensemble model against the worst-performing individual model. Specifically, a DS-fine-tuned, pretrained 50-DNN ensemble achieved an AUPRC of 0.909, compared to 0.875 for the worst-performing pretrained single DNN; this difference was statistically significant (paired t-test, P = 0.002). These results indicate that the ensemble approach improves robustness by reducing vulnerability to poorly performing individual models. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that pretraining enhances representation learning, and deep ensemble plays a critical role in ensuring stable performance.

To further quantify the relationship between the performance gains from pretraining and those from ensembling, we analyzed performance improvements across folds (Supplementary Fig. 4). In the DS setup, pretraining and ensembling showed no significant association (Pearson’s r = 0.078, P = 0.395), whereas a significant negative correlation emerged when all configurations were considered (r = − 0.428, P < 0.001). In addition, cases in which ensembling has a large effect are more frequent under the GS setting than under the DS setting. Overall, the effects of pretraining and ensembling exhibit an inverse relationship, particularly under the GS setting, such that stronger pretraining gains are associated with smaller marginal benefits from ensembling. In the DS setup, pretraining tends to provide particularly strong performance improvements, a pattern consistent with the results shown in Supplementary Tables 4–7.

Performance differences between DS and GS setups were gene-dependent (see Supplementary Tables 4–7). For BRCA1, GS consistently outperformed DS (0.890–0.974 vs. 0.853–0.906), with the GS fine-tuning 50-DNN pretrained being significantly superior (paired t-test, P < 0.006). For BRCA2, DS clearly surpassed GS (≥ 0.860 vs. ≤ 0.837), with the DS fine-tuning 50-DNN pretrained achieving the best performance (paired t-test, P < 0.033). For MLH1, DS again outperformed GS (0.892–0.964 vs. 0.802–0.950), with the DS fine-tuning 50-DNN pretrained achieving the highest accuracy. For MSH2, DS also exceeded GS (0.876–0.909 vs. 0.659–0.872), with the best-performing model for this gene being a single pretrained DNN with DS fine-tuning.

In summary, pretraining consistently improved DS performance, while its impact on GS was gene-dependent. Deep ensembling generally enhanced robustness and yielded additional gains, particularly in GS setups. Specifically, BRCA1 exhibited greater performance gains under the GS setup, whereas BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2 achieved superior performance under the DS setup. Accordingly, subsequent analyses report and compare GS fine-tuned 50-DNN results for BRCA1 and DS fine-tuned 50-DNN results for the remaining genes.

Comparison with baseline and state-of-the-art methods

To evaluate the effectiveness of our method, we compared it with representative pathogenicity predictors, including ClinPred, BayesDel (addAF and noAF), REVEL, PrimateAI, AlphaMissense, gMVP, SIGMA, PHACTboost, MisFit, ESM1b, MutScore, popEVE, and MutFormer. Among these, popEVE is the most recently developed model and has demonstrated competitive performance in ClinVar evaluations compared with AlphaMissense and gMVP, while ClinPred, BayesDel, REVEL, SIGMA, PHACTboost, MisFit, ESM1b, MutScore, popEVE, and MutFormer represent additional widely used genome-wide methods. Prior studies under both DS and GS setups showed that extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and random forests (RF) consistently outperformed other algorithms [21], so we used these two models as baselines. Optimization and training details for XGBoost and RF are provided in the Supplementary Methods. For XGBoost and RF, subsequent analyses compare the better-performing DS or GS setup for each approach.

The overall results are shown in Fig. 5. An asterisk (*) indicates the best-performing method and any other methods that are not significantly different from it (paired t-test, P ≥ 0.05). Our method achieved the highest overall performance, yielding a mean AUPRC of 0.940 with a standard deviation of 0.070 across 120 test sets (30 per gene). This represents an improvement of more than 2 percentage points over the next best baseline. Pairwise paired t-tests confirmed that these differences were statistically significant (Supplementary Table 12).

Three major observations can be drawn from these results. First, ClinPred performed relatively well compared with the other baseline methods. Nevertheless, our method still achieved a significantly higher overall performance than ClinPred. Second, AlphaMissense, ESM1b, PrimateAI, popEVE, and MisFit exhibited more than 5 percentage points lower AUPRC compared with ClinPred, XGBoost, and RF, which directly utilize ClinVar annotations for training. Although PrimateAI, AlphaMissense, popEVE, and MisFit perform well in genome-wide weakly supervised setup, our results suggest that they may be less suitable for rare, disease-specific missense variants such as those we considered. Third, BayesDel showed a large performance gap depending on whether AF was included: BayesDel (addAF) outperformed BayesDel (noAF) by approximately 16 percentage points. Despite the fact that all variants evaluated here are rare missense variants, this result highlights that AF remains a highly informative predictor for pathogenicity assessment.

We further evaluated prediction performance on a per-gene basis (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 13–16). For BRCA1, our method achieved the highest mean AUPRC (0.974), although the difference compared with MutScore and ESM1b was not statistically significant. For BRCA2, our method again attained the top mean AUPRC (0.911), with no significant difference relative to XGBoost and ClinPred. In contrast, compared with our method, ClinPred showed marginally higher mean AUPRC values for MLH1 (0.965 vs. 0.964) and MSH2 (0.924 vs. 0.909); however, these differences were trivial in magnitude and not statistically significant (paired t-tests: MLH1, P = 0.444; MSH2, P = 0.097). For MLH1 only, a similar non-significant difference was observed when compared with BayesDel (addAF) (paired t-test, P = 0.380).

Performance comparison with existing methods

Overall, although our method was not statistically superior for every gene, it did not differ significantly from the best-performing method for any gene and consistently ranked among the top one or two across all four genes. While certain methods exhibited gene-specific strengths, performance rankings varied substantially across genes, with no single method dominating consistently. In particular, ClinPred showed strong performance for BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2, ranking among the top-performing methods for these genes, but performed substantially worse for BRCA1, where it ranked sixth with an AUPRC of 0.920, compared with 0.974 achieved by our method—a difference exceeding 0.05. Similarly, MutScore ranked second for BRCA1 (AUPRC = 0.974) but showed markedly lower rankings for the remaining genes, placing 14th for BRCA2, 12th for MLH1, and 13th for MSH2. These results highlight the strong gene dependency of variant pathogenicity prediction performance and underscore the robustness of our method across diverse gene contexts.

In summary, our method achieved the highest performance for BRCA1 and BRCA2. Although ClinPred showed slightly higher mean AUPRCs for MLH1 and MSH2, these differences were not statistically significant. This apparent advantage of ClinPred can be attributed, at least in part, to the substantial overlap between its training data and our test sets, as shown in Supplementary Table 17. On average, 81, 75, 21, and 15 variants overlapped for BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2, corresponding to 94%, 95%, 91%, and 83% of our test sets, respectively. While the overlap proportions for BRCA1 and BRCA2 were slightly higher, the test sets for MLH1 (23 variants) and MSH2 (18 variants) were much smaller, making the impact of overlapping variants disproportionately large for these genes. Thus, ClinPred’s higher performance for MLH1 and MSH2 likely reflects the underlying data overlap rather than a substantive difference in predictive capability between the two methods. Lastly, PrimateAI, AlphaMissense, popEVE, and MisFit consistently showed lower performance than our method, indicating that disease- or gene‐specific modeling provides a substantial advantage when predicting pathogenicity for rare, clinically relevant missense variants.

To further investigate this gene- and class-specific behavior, our method enables sample-level interpretation through LRP, allowing us to examine feature contributions separately for benign and pathogenic variants, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. For BRCA1, benign variants exhibited an unusually low MAF contribution compared with other genes, whereas pathogenic variants showed comparable MAF contributions.

Despite this BRCA1-specific difference, a common pattern was observed across all four genes: benign variants were strongly driven by MAF, which accounted for more than 50% of their total importance, whereas pathogenic variants relied less on MAF and more on positional and functional annotations. These results indicate that benign classification is primarily frequency-driven, while pathogenic prediction requires integrating additional biological information beyond allele frequency.

As noted earlier, this study focuses on rare missense variants and does not treat rarity itself as a direct indicator of pathogenicity [13]. Although we restrict our analysis to rare missense variants, MAF still carries substantial discriminative power. In our model, benign variants showed more than 60% of their total importance attributed to MAF, whereas pathogenic variants exhibited much lower MAF contributions, typically around 30%.

This asymmetry reflects how benign and pathogenic variants are defined in cancer predisposition genes. Although our dataset consists exclusively of rare missense variants—well below the general ACMG/AMP BA1 threshold of 5%, where AF would ordinarily provide little discrimination—the ClinGen ENIGMA [70] and InSiGHT [71] Variant Curation Expert Panels apply far more stringent, gene-specific filtering allele frequency (FAF) thresholds for benign evidence in BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2. These thresholds (e.g., BS1 and BA1 on the order of 0.0001–0.001) are several orders of magnitude lower than the standard criteria, such that even subtle differences in MAF within the “rare” range can meaningfully distinguish benign from pathogenic variation. Thus, despite all variants being rare, the exceptionally strict FAF thresholds used for cancer predisposition genes explain why MAF remains a highly informative signal for benign classification. In contrast, FAF supports only benignity—not pathogenicity—so pathogenic variants naturally rely less on MAF and more on positional and functional evidence. This distinction underscores that benignity is frequency-driven, whereas pathogenicity requires broader biological signals.

Location-based features such as protein position and exon position vary substantially across genes due to differences in gene length, domain architecture, and structurally critical regions; consequently, approaches that do not explicitly model gene-specific patterns may fail to account for these differences. To examine this behavior more directly, we visualized the predicted pathogenicity scores of the test variants alongside their positional distribution, with results shown in Supplementary Figs. 7–10. As illustrated, pathogenic variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, and MLH1 tend to cluster near both termini of the protein sequence. In contrast, MSH2 exhibits a markedly different pattern: pathogenic variants are distributed relatively uniformly across the entire sequence rather than being concentrated in specific terminal regions. This diffuse distribution suggests that positional features provide lower discriminative power for MSH2, offering a plausible explanation for its comparatively lower predictive performance reported earlier. Given that positional features play a central role in pathogenicity prediction, the absence of region-specific enrichment in MSH2 likely poses a significant challenge for model learning.

Building on the strong performance of our pretrained and ensemble models in Section“Performance evaluation of the proposed method”, we next examined the distribution of LRP patterns across pretrained single DNNs and the 50-DNN ensemble. In particular, we assessed whether individual models exhibit LRP configurations distinct from the ensemble, which achieved the best performance in most cases. This analysis allows us to determine whether pretraining produces meaningful representation diversity and how such diversity contributes to ensemble performance. Although pairwise measures such as differences or correlations could be used to compare LRP configurations across models, they do not provide an additional intuitive overview of model performance. Therefore, for each single model, we computed LRP-based feature importance scores and projected them into a two-dimensional space using t-SNE [72], together with the importance profile of the ensemble.

The resulting distributions (perplexity = 10) are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11. For visualization clarity, a single run was selected from the 30 repeated experiments for each gene. Each marker corresponds to a different gene, with larger markers denoting the 50-model ensemble. Marker colors distinguish pathogenic (red) and benign (blue) variants, and color intensity represents the corresponding test AUPRC. Across all genes, the ensemble model appeared at the center of the distribution, reflecting its role as the aggregate of model predictions and their average feature contributions. Notably, high-performing models were not exclusively located near the ensemble; instead, single models with varying predictive performance were spread relatively uniformly around it. These observations indicate that individual models learned heterogeneous LRP importance patterns rather than converging to a single representation. Importantly, the ensemble combining these diverse patterns achieved superior performance in most cases, suggesting that diversity in feature importance contributes to the robustness and accuracy of ensemble predictions. However, MSH2 showed an exception: the 50-DNN ensemble achieved an AUPRC of 0.770, lower than the mean of the single models (AUPRC = 0.799). Among the 50 single DNNs, AUPRC values spanned a wide range (0.735–0.872); only 16 models surpassed the single-model mean, while the majority clustered around 0.771. As a result, the ensemble was pulled downward by many low-performing models despite the presence of several strong ones. This outcome does not detract from the utility of pretraining and ensembling; rather, it highlights that high-performing single models (AUPRC: ~0.87) were relatively rare, whereas ensembling helps stabilize predictions and mitigate worst-case performance. Overall, the dispersed LRP patterns for MSH2 indicate that our pretraining and ensemble framework effectively handles model-level uncertainty.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a pathogenicity prediction model that integrates denoising autoencoder pretraining with deep ensembling, and evaluated its performance on rare missense variants from four representative hereditary cancer predisposition genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2). In this section, we examine the broader implications and significance of the results beyond their quantitative performance.

We first conducted an evaluation of each component of pretraining and deep ensembling. Both techniques generally improved performance across all test genes, although the extent of improvement varied by gene and data setups. For pretraining, BRCA1 showed consistent gains in both DS and GS setups, whereas BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2 exhibited reduced performance under the GS setups—likely due to their very small GS sample sizes (fewer than 100 variants), which may cause pretrained representations to overfit. This suggests that when GS data is scarce, using DS data is more appropriate despite its broader disease specificity. For deep ensembling, performance improvements were observed in most cases, and the ensemble effectively mitigated extreme degradation compared with the worst-performing individual model.

Next, we evaluated the statistical effectiveness of the proposed method by comparing it with established pathogenicity prediction models. As discussed in Section “Input features”, the input features exhibit substantial collinearity, which can potentially lead to unstable learning; however, the proposed training strategy, incorporating nonlinear representations, regularization, and ensembling, may help alleviate this issue and could partly account for the observed performance trends. ClinPred, a genome-wide model that relies on a feature set largely overlapping with ours, showed slightly higher performance for MLH1 and MSH2; however, these differences were not statistically significant and are likely influenced by overlap between its training data and our test sets. Importantly, across the full set of evaluations, our method demonstrated consistently competitive or superior performance, suggesting that the proposed framework more effectively captures disease- and gene-specific signals under comparable feature settings. Building on this, our approach achieved average performance gains of 12, 27, and 36% points over AlphaMissense, PrimateAI, and MutFormer, respectively, underscoring the advantages of context-specific modeling compared with genome-wide approaches. In addition, BayesDel exhibited an approximately 16-percentage-point performance difference depending on whether AF was incorporated, underscoring the critical role of population AF even in the pathogenicity assessment of rare missense variants. Consistent with this observation, the ClinGen ENIGMA and InSiGHT guidelines apply lower FAF thresholds for the four genes evaluated in this study, further supporting AF as a key informative feature for rare variant classification.

Beyond performance improvement, this study introduces an LRP-based interpretation framework that aims to improve the interpretability of the proposed model. The framework highlights gene-specific features associated with pathogenicity and provides quantitative insights that may help inform variant assessment in the context of ACMG/AMP guidelines. Furthermore, visualizing both the LRP profiles of individual models and their aggregated ensemble representations using t-SNE revealed that pretraining increases the diversity of learned feature representations across models, thereby contributing to the effectiveness of deep ensembling. This finding demonstrates that even models with comparable predictive performance can train distinct feature representations, and that such diversity is a key driver of ensemble effectiveness.

Taken together, our work presents a methodological advance that improves the accuracy of pathogenicity prediction for rare hereditary-cancer variants and provides additional interpretability relevant to clinical applications. The proposed approach offers a foundation for future gene-specific model development and holds promise for contributing to the standardization and automation of rare missense variant interpretation.

Despite the strengths of our approach, several limitations should be noted. First, our analyses did not account for isoform diversity. Because variants may affect different protein regions depending on the transcript, restricting modeling to a representative isoform may overlook isoform-specific effects. Second, variant zygosity was not considered. Although zygosity can influence clinical manifestation, this study focused on estimating the intrinsic pathogenic potential of individual variants, leaving genotype–phenotype relationships to downstream clinical interpretation. Third, both disease- and gene-specific approaches remain constrained by data availability. In our experiments, gene-specific improvements were pronounced only for BRCA1, whereas disease-specific models consistently performed better for BRCA2, MLH1, and MSH2, underscoring the critical role of sample size. Fourth, we did not perform exhaustive gene-specific optimization of model capacity. As shown in Section “Performance evaluation of the proposed method”, the optimal model size varies substantially across genes, particularly under GS setup with highly limited data. However, rather than tuning a distinct optimal architecture for each gene, this study aimed to evaluate the robustness and general effectiveness of pretraining and deep ensemble strategies under data-scarce conditions. Fine-grained, gene-specific model optimization therefore remains an important direction for future work. Fifth, the evaluation for some genes is statistically constrained by very small test sets (e.g., 23 variants for MLH1 and 18 variants for MSH2), which may compromise the reliability of AUPRC estimates despite repeated resampling. Accordingly, these gene-specific results should be interpreted with caution. Future validation using larger external datasets will be essential to further establish the robustness of the proposed framework. Sixth, while our framework was designed to reduce type I circularity by excluding functional impact predictors trained using supervised learning with pathogenicity labels, the use of ClinVar for evaluation inherently limits the complete elimination of circularity effects. Accordingly, the reported performance differences should be interpreted with caution, as they may be partially influenced by performance inflation arising from unavoidable circularity.

Finally, we outline several directions for future work. Although this study considered rare missense variants across a panel of 30 hereditary cancer predisposition genes, comprehensive model evaluation was conducted on four representative genes. Accordingly, further validation will be necessary to assess the generalizability of our findings to additional genes, disease contexts, or variant classes. Extending the proposed framework to other genetically driven conditions, such as cardiovascular disorders, or to broader gene panels may help clarify its applicability and robustness across diverse genomic settings. Moreover, the current model relies primarily on annotation-based biological features (e.g., conservation scores, positional information, and functional impact scores) and therefore does not explicitly model secondary or tertiary protein structural effects of missense variants. Since such structural perturbations may more directly capture functional consequences, incorporating protein structural representations represents an important direction for future work. In particular, integrating transformer-based sequence or structure-aware models with the proposed framework—together with predicted structures, stability changes, and molecular interaction features—may enable more comprehensive modeling and further improve both predictive performance and interpretability.

Conclusions

In this study, we presented a DNN framework for predicting the pathogenicity of rare missense variants in hereditary cancer predisposition genes by integrating denoising autoencoder–based pretraining with deep ensembling. Both components contributed meaningfully to performance gains, and the proposed method achieved statistically significant improvements over representative genome-wide predictors, demonstrating the value of tailored learning strategies for rare variant interpretation.

Beyond accuracy, we introduced an LRP-based interpretability framework that identifies the key features contributing to pathogenicity for each gene and pathogenicity class. Through t-SNE visualizations, we showed that pretraining enhances representational diversity across ensemble members, thereby directly supporting uncertainty reduction and improving the reliability of predictions. These findings suggest that our model provides competitive predictive performance alongside interpretable outputs, which may offer quantitative support for ACMG/AMP variant assessment.

Finally, we discussed current limitations and outlined directions for future research. Expanding to additional disease areas and broader gene sets, as well as integrating protein structural information within a multimodal framework, will further strengthen the generalizability and precision of variant interpretation. Collectively, this work provides an effective learning strategy and interpretive methodology for rare variant pathogenicity prediction, offering a foundation for advancing genome-based diagnostics in the era of precision medicine.
