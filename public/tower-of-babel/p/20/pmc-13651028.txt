Multivariable regression modelling of structural, intermediary and demographic determinants of health literacy: evidence from unaccompanied migrants and statelessness-prone individuals in Ghana
Authors: Ebenezer Asare Aboagye, Dina Adei, Williams Agyemang-Duah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth literacy is acknowledged as a critical determinant of equitable health outcomes. However, its social patterning among populations experiencing legal precarity, irregular mobility, and administrative exclusion remains insufficiently examined in the sub-Saharan African context, particularly in Ghana. This study addresses this gap by examining health literacy among unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness in Ghana.MethodsData were derived from a cross-sectional survey conducted in two purposively selected districts in Ghana, characterized by mobile and heterogeneous populations. Health literacy was assessed using the HLS19-Q12 instrument and analyzed in SPSS (version 27). Analyses comprised descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression modelling. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported.ResultsHealth literacy was predominantly low, with 62.6% of respondents reporting inadequate literacy, while 37.4% demonstrated sufficient literacy. In the multivariable binary logistic regression analyses, sufficient health literacy was significantly associated with formal employment (AOR = 4.05, p < 0.001, CI: 2.21–7.42), ease of healthcare access (AOR = 2.52, p < 0.01, CI: 1.47–4.34) and language proficiency particularly in Akan/Twi (AOR = 3.49, p < 0.01, CI: 1.43–8.48), Ga/Dangme (AOR = 4.76, p < 0.001, CI: 1.61–14.13), and Gonja/Dagbani (AOR = 4.28, p < 0.001, CI: 1.63–11.21). Other factors including social network, place of residence, health information sources, age, and access to basic amenities equally demonstrated significant associations but with comparatively modest effects.ConclusionThese findings suggest that health literacy among mobile and documentation-insecure populations in Ghana is structured less by demographic factors than by structural and intermediary conditions. While employment, language proficiency, and healthcare access are key determinants, other factors also contribute. Effective interventions therefore require coordinated policy responses that move beyond individual barriers and group identity to address these intersecting constraints that condition access to enabling resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01385-9.

Background

Health literacy is widely recognized as a critical determinant of health, shaping individuals’ capacity to access, comprehend, and apply health information [1–4]. Beyond this, health literacy reflects broader social and structural conditions through which education, economic resources, and living environments influence ability to obtain and utilize health information [3, 5]. Consequently, limited health literacy not only constrains individual decision-making but also amplifies disparities in health knowledge, service utilization, and preventive behavior [6]. In contexts characterised by resource constraints, and social vulnerability, these limitations further entrench cycles of marginalization and poor health outcomes [3].

Despite this, empirical research on health literacy has overlooked populations whose vulnerabilities stem from legal precarity, irregular mobility, and administrative exclusion. In Ghana, existing studies have primarily examined health literacy among relatively stable populations including university students [7, 8], street youth [9], pregnant women [10, 11], older adults [4], and faith-based organizations [12]. These studies documented limited health literacy to be consistently predicted by sociodemographic factors including age, education, gender, self-esteem, and health status. However, none examined populations whose vulnerabilities are shaped by exclusion, lack of identity documentation, or exposure to statelessness.

Unaccompanied migrants and individuals prone to statelessness represent two such populations. Unaccompanied migrants typically include children not more than 18 years who have migrated without parental or legal guardianship [13, 14]. In contrast, individuals prone to statelessness comprise people who lack recognized nationality under the laws of any country [15]. Individuals in this group thus experience ambiguous or contested nationality, which restricts access to essential services including healthcare, education, and legal protection [15, 16]. Intersecting with heightened exposure to exploitation, unstable employment, limited documentation, and social dislocation [17–20], these circumstances constrain ability to locate, interpret, and act upon health information.

In the global north, evidence suggests that migrants and displaced populations often experience limited functional and comprehensive health literacy. For instance, studies in countries such as Germany, Sweden, and Portugal demonstrated that migrants and refugees face significant barriers relating to language proficiency, documentation status, and difficulties navigating health systems [21–25]. However, the extent to which similar dynamics shape health literacy in sub-Saharan Africa remains insufficiently understood and explored.

In sub-Saharan Africa, available evidence remains fragmented and uneven. Scoping reviews highlight that health literacy research has largely focused on young people, students, and community-dwelling populations, with limited attention given to how mobility, legal invisibility, and social marginalization influence health literacy experiences [26, 27]. This gap is particularly significant in the African context, where migration is pervasive [28] and vulnerability is often reflective of intersecting socioeconomic deprivation, linguistic diversity, and infrastructural inequalities [19, 29, 30].

Furthermore, structural conditions including education, housing, income, and access to basic amenities have been documented to shape both the ability to understand health information and to effectively use health services [3]. Consequently, understanding how such factors interact among socially excluded populations is critical for advancing health promotion strategies. Against this background, this study examines how structural, intermediary and demographic factors influence health literacy among unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness in Ghana. By situating health literacy within the lived realities of legal precarity, mobility, and structural marginalization, this study contributes insight into health literacy as both a reflection and consequence of social inequality.

Theoretical framework

This study is underpinned by the Social Determinants of Health (SDH) framework. The SDH framework posits that structural and contextual conditions of daily life shape opportunities for health and wellbeing [31]. Specifically, the SDH framework illuminates how disparities in education, income, employment, housing, and social inclusion generate unequal access to resources essential for health [32]. For unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness, such inequities are compounded by the absence of legal identity, absence of parental or legal guardianship, limited social protection, and precarious livelihoods. Such conditions likely constrain access to, comprehension of, and engagement with health information, adversely impacting general health outcomes [18–20, 33].

The SDH framework was adopted for its explanatory depth in linking social context to health capacity and outcomes as evidenced in existing studies [34–39]. Informed by the SDH framework, we conceptualized health literacy as a socially conditioned construct shaped less by inherent individual attributes than by structural and intermediary factors which are beyond the direct influence of individuals. Accordingly, the independent variables were organized into structural, intermediary and demographic domains reflecting distinct but interrelated levels of influence.

Consequently, structural factors were conceptualized as defining individuals’ positions within the social hierarchy and encompassed broad socioeconomic and political conditions that determined differential exposure to health-promoting or health-constraining environments [32, 40]. Contextually, these include migration and legal status, disparities in education, income, employment status, experiences of healthcare discrimination, and civic participation, which together create systematic advantages or disadvantages among individuals and groups.

Intermediary factors on the other hand were conceptualized as factors mediating the effects of structural factors on health literacy through material, psychosocial, and health-system pathways [40]. These include living and working conditions, access to essential resources, and the organization and responsiveness of health services. Contextually, these included language proficiency, social networks, and place of residence, which influence everyday communication, information exchange, and access to services.

Furthermore, demographic characteristics including age, sex, marital status, and religion were conceptualized as inherent individual factors that further condition individuals’ exposure to information, social support and health-related opportunities. These demographic characteristics influence how individuals experience and respond to structural and intermediary influences.

Through these conceptualisations, the SDH framework provided the analytical basis for variable selection, model specification, and interpretation of findings. Variations in health literacy were therefore interpreted as manifestations of underlying social inequalities rather than solely individual-level attributes. In the Ghanaian context, where socioeconomic and spatial disparities intersect to shape access to services, the SDH framework thus offered a useful lens for understanding how structural disadvantage influences health literacy.

Methods

Study settings and sampling procedure

This study draws on data from a cross-sectional survey conducted from March 2024 to May 2024 in the Greater Kumasi Metropolitan Area (GKMA) and the Awutu Senya East Municipal Area (ASEMA). These sites were purposively selected, informed by their complex migratory dynamics and concentration of vulnerable demographics. Detailed descriptions of the study settings and their unique context for the study are reported elsewhere [41, 42].

Furthermore, a multi-stage non-probability sampling was employed. This involved purposive selection of districts followed by criterion-based recruitment of eligible participants within the selected districts. Specifically, unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness were targeted. These groups were operationally defined in accordance with Ghana’s policy frameworks and relevant international guidance. Detailed definition and operational criteria, including inclusion and exclusion parameters, are reported elsewhere [42].

Precisely, eligible participants were individuals meeting predefined residency, age, and documentation-related conditions and consented to participate in the study. A minimum sample size of 444 was estimated using Cochran’s formula with standard assumptions (95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and p = 0.5) informed by Lwanga and Lemeshow [43] and a 15% adjustment for non-response.

Survey instrument and data collection

Data were collected using the 12-item Health Literacy Population Survey 2019 (HLS19-Q12) instrument, a validated short form of the HLS19-Q47 [44]. The instrument was deliberately adopted for its conceptual depth and cross-cultural applicability in assessing general health literacy across diverse populations. Procedures for instrument pretesting, selection of pretesting site, translation, and field implementation are documented elsewhere [42]. Precisely, the questionnaire was piloted in the New Juaben South Municipality (NJSM) to assess clarity and contextual suitability. Feedback from the pretesting informed minor refinements prior to the main survey. Data were collected using structured interviewer-administered questionnaires deployed via KoBo Collect on mobile devices. Interviews were conducted by six trained field personnel. Where necessary, interpreter support was provided to ensure accurate comprehension. Interviews lasted approximately 10–15 min and yielded 491 submitted responses of which 481 were valid and retained for analysis.

Measures

This study employed a multidimensional measurement framework to capture key constructs central to the research objectives. Health literacy was operationalized as the capacity to access, understand, appraise, and apply health-related information in relation to healthcare, disease prevention, and health promotion [1, 2]. Health literacy score was computed following the HLS19 consortium’s standardized procedure [44]. In this study, the computed scores were categorised using the 0–100 scale, structured as 0–50 (inadequate), > 50–66 (problematic), > 66–84 (sufficient), and > 84–100 (excellent).

These thresholds represent the proportional equivalence of the 0–50 categorisation scheme (0–25; >25–33; >33–42; >42–50) applied in other studies [45, 46] and was adapted to align with the standard 0–100 health literacy scaling approach applied in recent studies [47]. However, for analytical modelling and interpretation, scores were further dichotomized as inadequate (0–66), and sufficient (> 66–100), which is consistent with earlier published studies [45, 47, 48].

Underpinned by the SDH framework, explanatory variables were organized into demographic, intermediary, and structural domains to reflect layered influences on health literacy. Demographic characteristics comprised age, sex, marital status, and religion. These represented baseline attributes shaping exposure to health information and outcomes. Age was initially captured across six life-course brackets but dichotomized (≤ 24 and > 24 years) to distinguish younger individuals whose engagement with formal health-system may differ from adults. This age variable was used solely as an analytical covariate and was not used to determine respondent status. Marital status was collapsed into single, married, and divorced/widowed to preserve meaningful distinctions in relational support. Religious affiliation was grouped into Christianity, Islam, and others to reflect dominant belief systems within the Ghanaian context.

Intermediary determinants included language proficiency, social networks, place of residence, healthcare access and informational environment. Language was categorized into major linguistic groups and analytically consolidated to reflect communicative accessibility within healthcare settings. Social networks were reclassified into blood relatives, non-blood relations, and none (self) to capture distinctions in kin-based, community-based, and absent support networks. Residential context was operationalized using district and locality. Locality was dichotomized into urban and peri-urban/slum settings to approximate disparities in infrastructure and service availability.

Healthcare access, initially measured as easy, somewhat difficult, or very difficult, was recoded into easy and difficult. Primary sources of health information were grouped into formal (health professionals, licensed chemical sellers), informal (family, friends), and mass media channels (radio/television, digital platforms, print materials, and “other”) to capture variation in information credibility and reach. To further characterize material conditions, three environmental indicators were included. Access to water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities, housing insecurity, and access to basic amenities were each measured dichotomously. These reflected constraints on individuals’ ability to engage with health information and maintain recommended health practices.

Structural factors included respondent status, educational attainment, income, healthcare discrimination, and civic participation. Respondent status distinguished unaccompanied migrants, persons prone to statelessness, and individuals experiencing both conditions. It was classified through the eligibility-screening criteria described in the sampling procedure and survey instrument to capture variations in legal recognition and social vulnerability. Educational attainment was recoded into none, primary–SHS, and tertiary to capture cumulative exposure to formal learning. Monthly income, originally measured across five brackets (0–485, 486–971, 972–1457, 1458–1943, ≥ 1944 Ghana cedis), was dichotomized (≤ GH¢971 and > GH¢971) to distinguish individuals at or near subsistence levels from those with relatively greater economic capacity.

Employment status was recoded into employed, student, and unemployed/retired to reflect labor force engagement. Additional structural variables included experiences of healthcare discrimination and civic participation. Healthcare discrimination was measured as a binary indicator. Civic participation was recoded into infrequent (never/less frequent) and frequent (frequent to daily) to capture levels of social inclusion and agency. Recoding decisions were guided by distributional properties and the need to ensure stable estimation in multivariable analyses. Details on initial coding of variables including sex, insurance coverage, and district are provided in the Additional File 1.

Analytic framework

Quantitative analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0, integrating both descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive statistics summarized distribution of health literacy levels, demographic, intermediary and structural factors using frequencies, percentages, and cross-tabulations. For inferential analyses, a binary logistic regression was employed to examine the predictors of health literacy among the target population. Consistent with the SDH framework, independent variables were entered sequentially to reflect the conceptual ordering of determinants. Structural factors were introduced in the first model to capture respondents’ broader socioeconomic positioning. Intermediary factors were subsequently added to assess proximate conditions through which structural inequalities may shape engagement with health information.

Finally, demographic factors were included as background factors that may condition exposure to structural and intermediary influences. This sequential specification enabled the examination of both structural and proximate contextual effects influencing health literacy among the target population. To ensure model stability, diagnostic checks for multicollinearity were performed. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance statistics (refer to Additional File 2) were examined. All variables demonstrating VIF less than 5 and tolerance greater than 0.2 were retained. Model fit was evaluated using likelihood ratio χ² tests, − 2 log likelihood, and pseudo-R² indices (Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. To assess the robustness of the estimates, all models were re-estimated using generalized binary logistic regression with a binomial distribution and logit link.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE), Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (Reference Number: CHRPE/AP/058/24). Details on the ethical principles and procedures followed are documented elsewhere [41, 42]. Briefly, participation was voluntary and based on informed consent. Consent was obtained in written or verbal form as appropriate. For minors, consent was provided by designated caregivers in accordance with the approved protocol. Furthermore, data were anonymised and handled confidentially throughout the study.

Bivariate Associations of Health Literacy with Selected Demographic and SDH-related Factors

The bivariate analysis identified significant associations between health literacy and selected factors. Among structural factors, respondent status (χ² = 8.53, p = 0.014) and employment status (χ² = 51.75, p < 0.001) were significant. Within intermediary factors, district of residence (χ² = 16.53, p < 0.001), locality (χ² = 4.06, p = 0.044), proficient language (χ² = 14.43, p = 0.025), social network (χ² = 13.06, p = 0.001), healthcare access (χ² = 31.43, p < 0.001), information source (χ² = 10.24, p = 0.006), and WASH access (χ² = 4.52, p = 0.034) showed significant associations with health literacy. For demographic factors, age (χ² = 28.80, p < 0.001) and marital status (χ² = 17.09, p < 0.001) were significant. No significant associations were observed for formal education, income, discrimination, civic participation, NHIS coverage, housing security, sex or religion (p > 0.05). The binary logistic regression models demonstrated consistent and progressive improvement in fit across specifications. Model 1, comprising structural determinants, was statistically significant (χ² [4] = 55.649, p < 0.001) and explained a modest proportion of variance (Nagelkerke R² = 0.149). Across models, employment status emerged as the most consistent and influential determinant of health literacy. Employed respondents were approximately four times more likely to exhibit sufficient health literacy compared to those who are unemployed or retired (AOR = 4.05, 95% CI: 2.21–7.42, p < 0.001). Importantly, respondent status did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with health literacy across all models.

The introduction of intermediary factors substantially improved model performance (χ² [19] = 114.092, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.288), with a substantial reduction in − 2 log likelihood. Specifically, language proficiency and healthcare access were strongly associated with sufficient health literacy across models 2 and 3. Respondents proficient in widely spoken local languages such as Akan/Twi (AOR = 3.49, 95% CI:1.43–8.48, p < 0.01), Ga/Dangme (AOR = 4.76, 95% CI: 1.61–14.13, p < 0.01), Gonja/Dagbani (AOR = 4.28, 95% CI: 1.63–11.21, p < 0.01) and Ewe (AOR = 2.84, 95% CI:1.08–7.43, p < 0.05) exhibited markedly higher odds of sufficient health literacy than those speaking Kasem and other minority languages. Similarly, individuals reporting easy access of healthcare services were more than twice as likely to exhibit sufficient health literacy (AOR = 2.52, 95% CI: 1.47–4.34, p < 0.01).

Informational source also influenced health literacy outcomes across models 2 and 3. Specifically, respondents relying on formal (AOR = 3.07, 95% CI:1.15–8.18, p < 0.05) and informal (AOR = 2.80, 95% CI: 1.87–7.31, p < 0.05) sources exhibited statistically significant association with higher health literacy compared to those relying on mass media as primary source of health information. The final model, incorporating demographic characteristics, yielded further improvement in fit (χ² [22] = 121.971, p < 0.001; Nagelkerke R² = 0.305). In this model, all significant predictors in earlier models retained their significance, Additionally, respondents with strong non-kin social networks (AOR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.01–4.00, p < 0.05) and residents of GKMA (AOR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.09–3.92, p < 0.05) were more likely to demonstrate sufficient health literacy. In contrast, respondents lacking access to basic household amenities (AOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.19–0.99, p < 0.05) and younger participants (≤ 24 years) (AOR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.16–0.75, p < 0.01) were significantly less likely to exhibit sufficient health literacy. The detailed results are presented in Table 3. Re-estimation using generalized binary logistic regression produced identical parameter estimates, confirming the robustness of the findings (see Additional File 4).

Discussion

This study, to the best of our knowledge, provides the first empirical evidence on health literacy among unaccompanied migrants and persons at risk of statelessness in Ghana. The findings revealed: (i) a markedly low health literacy, with nearly two-thirds (62.6%) reporting inadequate health literacy, (ii) structural and contextual factors including formal employment, access to healthcare services, language proficiency, social networks, and material living condition markedly shaped health literacy; (iii) conventional determinants of health literacy including education, income and health insurance coverage did not demonstrate statistically significant associations; and (iv) an absence of clear differences in health literacy by respondent status.

The high prevalence of inadequate health literacy observed aligns with evidence suggesting that socially precarious populations including migrants, refugees and undocumented groups often experience lower health literacy due to structural barriers [10, 23, 24, 49]. Within the SDH framework, these findings extend existing knowledge by illustrating how legal precarity, unstable livelihoods and disrupted social support systems jointly limit not only access to healthcare but also the capacity to engage with and act upon health information. This suggests that health literacy in such populations is less an individual attribute and more of a function of the constrained social environment they find themselves. In the Ghanaian context, this finding mirrors observations found among other vulnerable groups including older adults [4] and street youth [9]. This signals a contextually crucial public health concern and underscores the need for sustained investments and efforts in eliminating structural barriers to improve health literacy.

Among the predictors of health literacy, formal employment emerged as the most consistent structural predictor. Specifically, individuals employed were significantly more likely to report adequate health literacy. This finding rather than implying a direct causal pathway, suggests that economic participation may coincide with increased exposure to social networks, communicative environments and institutional interactions that facilitate learning, and capacity to navigate essential needs and services [50, 51]. This interpretation is consistent with evidence across diverse settings including Latin America, Asia and Europe, where stable work environments correlate with expanded social networks, information exchange, and access to services [46, 51–53].This suggests that irrespective of context, interventions aimed at improving health literacy for socially precarious populations must target their structural economic precarity.

Additionally, individuals’ proficiency in widely spoken Ghanaian languages including Akan, Ewe, Ga/Dangme, and Gonja/Dagbani demonstrated significantly higher odds of sufficient health literacy than speakers of minority languages. This finding mirrors global evidence highlighting the centrality of language concordance to health navigation [54–56] and as a vital determinant of health literacy [3]. In multilingual contexts such as Ghana, this finding highlights a critical mechanism of informational exclusion as documented in existing studies [57, 58]. This is because, even where health information is widely disseminated, linguistic mismatch can function as a barrier, effectively rendering information inaccessible. This affirms the need for multilingual communication strategies such as interpreter services and culturally responsive health information systems in Ghana’s healthcare system as has been suggested in earlier Ghanaian studies [59, 60].

Intermediary factors including social networks and healthcare access further illuminate how health literacy is socially mediated. Specifically, individuals with stronger non-kin social ties were more likely to demonstrate sufficient health literacy. This finding diverges from studies among less transient populations such as older adults who predominantly rely on familial ties as primary resources for support and essential services [61]. However, it aligns with evidence among transient and displaced groups such as refugees among whom non-kin associates often substitute for absent kin [62]. This suggests that community-based networks mediate access to resources and shape capacity to act on health information in contexts where traditional kin structure may be disrupted or absent [63, 64]. Consistent with evidence among informal caregivers [65], youth [66] and older adults [67] in the Ghanaian context, this finding underscores the need to integrate community cohesion and support networks into programmes aimed at improving health literacy among mobile or socially vulnerable populations.

Similarly, ease of healthcare access significantly increased health literacy. This observation is consistent with evidence demonstrating that individuals who can reach services without physical, financial, or bureaucratic barriers are more likely to engage with providers and receive needed health information [68–70]. In the Ghanaian context, this finding is also consistent with observations made among individuals 18 years and older [71], where healthcare access was similarly linked to improved health outcomes. This suggests that improved access enhances literacy, while low literacy undermines the ability to navigate care as observed among community dwelling adults [72]. For socially vulnerable groups in Ghana including migrants and older adults, barriers to healthcare often extend beyond geography to include poor provider attitude, financial constraint and inadequate support structures [73, 59]. This underscores the need to enhance inclusive healthcare access through mobile clinics, community outreach, incentivizing positive and punishing poor provider attitude to strengthen health literacy among this population.

Additionally, residents in GKMA were significantly more likely to demonstrate sufficient health literacy. This finding reinforces evidence that urbanized settings generally offer denser health infrastructure and services including diversified communication channels, and more consistent health messaging than non-urban settings [74]. In the Ghanaian context, similar urban advantage has been reported in the health literacy of older persons [75] and among women seeking maternal healthcare services [76]. However, the non-significant effect of locality suggests that this advantage operates primarily at the district level rather than through settlement distinctions. This likely reflects that disparities in resource distribution such as health services, and information systems operate evidently at the district-level. For unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness, access to these district-level structures may be more consequential than immediate residential conditions, particularly in contexts of rapid urban sprawl that blur locality boundaries. Overall, this points to geographic inequity driven by uneven distribution of resources across districts, rather than by settlement type alone.

Moreover, the significant association of sufficient health literacy with the use of both formal and informal information channels highlights the complementarity of institutional and interpersonal sources. This resonates with studies suggesting that individuals in uncertain or unfamiliar contexts such as refugees and migrants rely on multiple, overlapping information sources for health information [77–79]. In the Ghanaian context, this finding resonates with observations among older [80] and young [81] adults linking health information to multiple sources including healthcare providers, family members, media and friends. For unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness, who may distrust formal institutions or lack consistent access, informal networks may serve indispensable interpretive roles like observed among refugees outside the Ghanaian context [78, 79]. This suggests that health literacy enhancing efforts in Ghana must integrate and not seek to replace informal communication ecologies, leveraging trusted community actors as conduits for accurate and effective dissemination of health information.

In contrast, the absence of basic amenities such as electricity significantly reduced the odds of sufficient health literacy, aligning with studies linking infrastructural deprivation to compromised information access, and reduced exposure [82, 83]. This can be attributed to the capacity of basic amenities such as electricity to facilitate phone charging, radio, and television use with its absence capable of truncating exposure to virtually all modern forms of communications. In the Ghanaian context, this finding resonates with observations among older [80] and young [81] adults which identified electronic and print as integral source of health information. This suggests that material deprivation functions as a potent intermediary determinant shaping health information access and use. This underscores that improvement in household infrastructure is fundamental and not peripheral to equitable and effective health communication.

Age-related disparities were also observed, with younger respondents exhibiting markedly reduced health literacy. While younger populations often demonstrate higher digital and functional literacy [45, 84, 85], this pattern may not apply to socially vulnerable youth whose educational trajectories and institutional engagement may have been disrupted. Among unaccompanied migrants in particular, early entry into informal labor markets for survival and limited schooling opportunities may reduce exposure to structured health learning environments as has been identified among migrant and displaced youth [25, 26]. This age gradient emphasizes the need for age-specific interventions combining health information with psychosocial support, language strengthening, and re-integration into formal or non-formal learning environments.

Importantly, several variables commonly associated with health literacy including education, income, and health insurance coverage did not demonstrate significant associations in the final model. These findings suggest that conventional socioeconomic indicators may operate differently among populations experiencing legal and administrative exclusion. For individuals whose institutional access is constrained by documentation barriers, linguistic marginalization, or distrust of formal systems, structural conditions and intermediary environments may exert stronger influence on health literacy than formal education or insurance coverage alone. These findings reinforce the SDH perspective that health literacy is shaped by the interaction of structural positioning, intermediary living conditions, and individual characteristics.

Finally, the non-significant association between respondent status and health literacy is analytically important. Although unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness were treated as distinct vulnerability groups, the findings indicate that such categorical distinctions do not independently predict health literacy. The attenuation observed across model specifications suggests that differences in health literacy are not driven by legal or migratory status per se but are instead mediated through structural and intermediary conditions particularly employment, language proficiency, and access to healthcare. Within the SDH framework, this implies that vulnerability operates through constrained access to enabling resources rather than through group identity alone. Consequently, analytical focus and interventions must not target their classification but the conditions shaping their lived experiences.

Policy and practical implications

The findings indicate that improving health literacy among unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness in Ghana requires coordinated policy responses that address the structural and contextual determinants shaping access to information, comprehension, and healthcare service. Advancing such reforms requires engagement from institutions including the Ministry of Labour, Jobs and Employment Relations (MLJER), the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection (MGCSP), the Metropolitan, Municipal, and District Assemblies (MMDAs), the Youth Employment Agency (YEA), the Ghana Enterprises Agency (GEA), the Ghana Health Service (GHS), and relevant civil society organizations working with migrant and socially vulnerable populations.

For instance, the strong association between formal employment and sufficient health literacy suggests that economic inclusion policies are central to strengthening health information engagement. Hence, integrating livelihood support programmes such as vocational training, apprenticeships and protections for informal workers could reduce economic precarity and strengthen their capacity to engage with health information. This initiative can be spearheaded by MLJER, MGCSP, YEA, GEA, GHS, and relevant civil society organizations. Such measures would directly advance Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.8 by lowering economic barriers to essential services and support Agenda 2063’s commitment to inclusive growth and human development.

Also, the emergence of proficiency in dominant local languages as a strong determinant of adequate health literacy, reinforces communication concordance as a critical factor in enabling health information access. Consequently, institutionalizing multilingual communication across healthcare facilities and public health campaigns by GHS would reduce linguistic barriers faced by migrants and linguistically minoritized populations. Moreover, incorporating minority languages spoken by northern migrants and border populations into health messaging would enhance comprehension and promote equitable participation in health services.

Additionally, the observed spatial disparities in health literacy highlight the importance of decentralized health communication systems. Consequently, expanding community-based outreach and localized health communication platforms in peri-urban settlements and migrant-dense communities through the district health directorates and community-based health planning and services (CHPS) structures would promote equitable access to comprehensible health information. Such geographically responsive interventions strengthen progress towards equitable health service access under SDG 3.

Furthermore, the significance of non-kin social networks further highlights the crucial role of community-based actors in facilitating health information circulation among mobile and socially marginalized populations. Hence, strengthening partnerships with migrant associations, youth groups, faith-based organizations, fun/neighborhood clubs, and community leaders can enhance the dissemination of accurate health information and improve service navigation. Complementing efforts of trained community health and public health workers with such networks can provide a feasible mechanism for extending health communication beyond formal institutional settings.

Again, the strong association of improved access to healthcare services with sufficient health literacy, reaffirms that service availability and health understanding are mutually reinforcing. This suggests that simplifying registration procedures, easing documentation requirements, and expanding mobile or outreach services in migrant-dense zones would make services more reachable and deepen opportunities for literacy-enhancing provider interactions. Such reforms directly contribute to the overarching aim of SDG 3 and related health aspirations under Africa’s Agenda 2063.

Moreover, the negative association between lack of basic amenities and health literacy illustrates how infrastructural deprivation constrains exposure to health information. Reliable electricity and communication infrastructure for instance enables access and use of radio, television, and mobile technologies that are central to public health campaigns. Consequently, coordinated investment in electrification and digital connectivity particularly within underserved settlements would strengthen the reach of health promotion initiatives while supporting broader development objectives under Agenda 2063.

Finally, the absence of a significant effect of respondent status suggests that health literacy is shaped less by group classification and more by access to key resources. Accordingly, intervention to improve health literacy should shift from category-based targeting to structural interventions that address employment precarity, language barriers, and healthcare access constraints. By prioritizing and addressing these conditions, health systems can more effectively improve health literacy across vulnerable populations.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides novel evidence on health literacy among unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness, populations that remain absent from health literacy research in Ghana and the sub-Sahara African context. Also, the study’s application of the SDH framework further enables a structured examination of how structural and intermediary factors such as employment, language proficiency, social networks, and healthcare access shape literacy outcomes. Additionally, the use of culturally adapted health literacy instruments further strengthens measurement validity and contextual relevance.

Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design and geographic focus limits causal inference. Future studies could employ a longitudinal design to establish temporal ordering and causal pathways between social determinants and health literacy outcomes. Also, despite the use of trained enumerators and translated instruments, the reliance on interviewer-administered questionnaires among linguistically diverse participants may have introduced interviewer effects and language-mediated measurement bias. Furthermore, the recoding and dichotomization of variables particularly health literacy may have reduced variability and obscured potential gradients within categories.

Moreover, the reliance on self-reported documentation status and sensitive health information may have introduced misclassifications potentially due to fear of disclosure, incomplete knowledge of legal status, or unwillingness to divulge sensitive information. Finally, the clustering of health literacy scores at the upper bound suggests a possible ceiling effect, which may have inflated and heterogenized the ‘sufficient’ health literacy category. These limitations offer contextual interpretation of the study’s findings and provide a basis for targeted interventions and future inquiry.

Conclusion

This study advances health literacy research in Ghana by demonstrating that among unaccompanied migrants and persons prone to statelessness, health literacy is shaped by structural and intermediary determinants rather than individual attributes. Specifically, the findings extend the applicability of the SDH framework and highlight how economic insecurity, linguistic marginalization, infrastructural deprivation, and spatial inequalities collectively structure opportunities to access, interpret and use health information. These insights indicate that improving health literacy for mobile and documentation-insecure populations requires coordinated policy responses that address the broader social and institutional environments within which they conduct their daily activities.
