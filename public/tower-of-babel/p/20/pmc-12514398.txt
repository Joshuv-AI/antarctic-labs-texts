Investigating the frequency of unreported cases and laboratory correspondence practices for variants of uncertain significance in pediatric epilepsy
Authors: Tatum Vilaboy, Kristen Wong, Martin Tristani‐Firouzi, Amanda G Sandoval Karamian, Rachel Palmquist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic testing via multigene panels has become common practice for children with epilepsy. This results in frequent identification of variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Providers must then assess clinical suspicion of the VUS, considering clinical correlation, family variant testing, and laboratory contact for internal case data. Labs often have seen previous cases of a VUS with variant and clinical information kept in their records internally; cases that go unused in variant classification due to requirements established by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant classification guidelines. This study aims to better understand the frequency at which internal case data is suspected based on a VUS report and to evaluate provider practice around obtaining and documenting such data. We conducted a retrospective chart review of pediatric patients at the University of Utah and Intermountain Primary Children Hospital's Neurology clinic who underwent epilepsy panel testing in 2022. Review of a total of 261 reports identified 265 VUSs in an autosomal dominant or X‐linked gene across 162 patients. We found that internal cases were suspected for 122 of the 265 (46.0%) variants, with most of these variants lacking clear documentation of internal cases on the report. Chart documentation of laboratory contact existed for only 9 of 122 (7.3%) variants with suspected internal cases. VUSs on the epilepsy gene panel commonly have undocumented internal cases at the reporting laboratory. Knowledge of this unreported data is important for providers to determine when laboratory contact is appropriate and to develop guidelines for documentation.Plain Language SummaryGenetic tests, routinely done for epilepsy, often find changes in genes called variants of uncertain significance (VUS) that require follow‐up for providers to interpret. INTRODUCTION

For children with epilepsy, the benefit of genetic testing for diagnosis and clinical management is well established. 1 , 2 Multigene panels are commonly utilized in epilepsy, with whole exome sequencing (ES) recently recommended as standard of care. 1 , 3 Studies show that between 32% and 77% of patients who undergo multigene panels for a variety of indications have at least one variant of uncertain significance (VUS) on their report, 4 , 5 and a larger panel size is correlated with a higher VUS rate. 6 Interpreting VUS based on clinical information as highly suspicious or not suspicious for causing disease is a common practice among genetics providers. 7 , 8 Studies have also shown an average of 22–31 months for reclassification of VUS. 9 This creates a significant burden for providers in clinical management, requiring laboratory correspondence along with literature and database review to look for information on the VUS. 10 Providers including genetic counselors (GC) and neurologists express a desire for more information on VUS reports, including labs' internal cases of their particular variant. 11 , 12 Genetic counselors report that they often contact the lab to obtain internal case information to better understand the phenotypic spectrum and potential impact a VUS could have on their patient's health. 11 , 13 This is done with the understanding that many cases received by laboratories will not meet criteria to be used in classification or reporting.

Laboratories must operate under the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant classification guidelines to interpret variants. These guidelines provide criteria for how different types of evidence should be combined and weighted for use in classification. 14 Since the original 2015 guidelines, several updates have been issued to address inconsistencies in the application of various evidence types. Studies show that one of the most inconsistently applied areas of these guidelines is evidence level “PP4” stating that the patient's phenotype is “highly specific to disease.” 14 , 15 This is subjective and is often limited by the brief clinical data submitted to the lab by providers.

Due to this issue, many labs developed systems built off of ACMG guidelines to assign points to incorporate phenotypic information more consistently into variant classification. 15 , 16 However, there is still discordance on phenotype alignment, and variant curation systems often cannot assign points for phenotype to variants in genes that are associated with nonspecific findings like epilepsy. 15 Many genes on large epilepsy gene panels (EGP) are not associated with highly specific phenotypes and have incomplete penetrance, limiting the labs ability to apply phenotype and family segregation evidence. 4 This means many cases go unused in classification and could partially explain high VUS rates on these panels. 15 Rare variants in patients even with specific clinical findings may never have enough cases to meet reporting standards. 15 Additionally, allelic heterogeneity and genotype–phenotype correlations are often seen in epilepsy genetics, making providers curious about other cases of a VUS in a gene where the mechanism of disease is unknown. 17 , 18 Epilepsy genetics is therefore an area of interest for VUS management by providers, as the workload regarding VUS is predicted to be high.

This study aims to investigate the frequency that VUS have suspected unreported case data available at the testing laboratory and the practice around obtaining and documenting such internal data. The purpose of this study is to provide insight into the frequency with which clinicians may consider inquiry for additional information on internal lab cases and for improved documentation of such information by providers.

METHODS

This study was a retrospective review of genetic testing reports, ClinVar variant reports and chart documentation in the Electronic Medical Record (EMR). We reviewed to look for evidence of suspected internal case information on lab reports and acquisition of this data by providers. Variant and demographic data from pediatric patients at the University of Utah (UU) and Intermountain Primary Children Hospital was collected from UU Pediatric Genetics Database for patients that had an Invitae Epilepsy Gene Panel resulted between January and September 2022. Variants were sorted based on their associated inheritance pattern(s). Chart review was conducted on all patients where at least one VUS in an autosomal dominant (AD) or X‐linked (XL) gene was identified on panel testing (n = 162). Genetic test reports were reviewed for documentation of a previous case and any information about that case or other patient(s) with a pathogenic variant (PV) disrupting the same residue. For each VUS in the reviewed charts, we obtained population data including frequency and allele count in the ethnicity with the largest allele frequency from GnomAD or ExaC. ClinVar entries by Invitae for each VUS were reviewed and documented if the variant was entered in ClinVar prior to the patients' result report date, indicating a previous case of the variant at the lab. We use the term “suspected internal case data” to mean a case with a VUS that was reported, with or without any phenotypic information, or if the “first in ClinVar” date was prior to the patients initial report date. Internal case documentation on the report was considered complete if the case was clearly not referencing the current patient and any necessary phenotypic information was included.

Chart documentation was also reviewed to look for any mention of Invitae results, provider interpretation of the VUS, familial testing, GC review, and/or contact with the genetic testing lab regarding internal cases and the information obtained. Documentation of internal cases was considered complete if the case information in the chart was said to have been received from the lab and was included in a neurology note, genetic counseling note, non‐face‐to‐face note, or phone note, or if a provider documented the decision not to contact the lab. Descriptive statistics were completed.

Of the 122 variants with internal cases suspected (Figure 2A), lab contact for internal case information or decision not to contact the lab was documented by the provider for nine variants (7.4%) in nine separate patients (12%). Seven of these nine variants had internal cases and information documented in the chart. One case that was documented on the lab report was confirmed to be the provider's current patient, and in one other case, the provider documented their decision not to contact the lab. The provider documented the internal cases for one patient in a phone note and in genetic counseling notes for the other six patients. All providers referred to for these nine cases were genetic counselors. There were three additional variants where previous cases were described in a note, but the source of this case information was unclear. We also stratified data for cases where a detected PV is expected to be the cause of the child's epilepsy, which occurred for 21 patients, as this could affect provider behavior in investigating VUS further. When excluding variants with internal cases suspected where the patient's report had one of these PVs, laboratory correspondence was documented for 14.3% of patients, slightly higher than the above‐mentioned 12%. Having an explanatory PV does not mean that lab correspondence about VUS is inherently unnecessary, given the possibility of dual diagnoses.

DISCUSSION

VUS interpretation and management is a routine aspect of the workload on providers ordering multigene panels, with 62.1% of patients in this study having an AD or XL VUS on their report. Internal cases were frequently not documented in clinical reports. While internal cases were suspected for 46% of patients, they were only documented on the report for 5% of those patients. This aligns with previous studies that show providers often feel the need to contact the lab for more information about a VUS 11 , 13 and demonstrates a gap in reporting that is important for epilepsy providers to be aware of. In clinical practice, it is important to recognize the frequency of unreported cases on genetic test reports and under what circumstances it may be beneficial to request information from laboratories.

Cases not documented on reports are listed in ClinVar, a public genomic variant database used by all major laboratories. 19 , 20 , 21 A “first in ClinVar” date listed prior to the current patient's report date likely indicates that the reporting laboratory has prior cases on record. We do acknowledge, however, Invitae's semiannual upload policy and ClinVar's processing lag may be limiting the actual number of cases with previous ClinVar submissions. Therefore, the number of suspected internal cases in this study may be underestimated.

Internal case information including the number of cases seen and phenotypes is not routinely listed in ClinVar and may help providers better understand clinical suspicion of a VUS. 22 Many cases do not justify laboratory correspondence for reasons such as a lack of clinical correlation or a high population database allele count. Additionally, the collection of case data from laboratories should always be in the context of recognized limitations in the data received by laboratories from providers.

With that understanding, there are circumstances where internal case data can help epilepsy providers clarify clinical suspicion for identified VUSs. Unreported internal cases may lower suspicion in genes that are associated with complete penetrance if unreported case(s) do not display the phenotype associated with pathogenic variants in that gene. For example, for one patient in our study where internal cases were obtained, this information lowered the clinical suspicion of a VUS in the highly penetrant gene, TSC2, in conjunction with a lack of clinical presentation supporting this diagnosis. All past internal cases were identified in patients of African American descent without documented concern for Tuberous Sclerosis diagnosis. In this case, the absence of this variant in Gnomad may reflect that this ancestry is underrepresented in databases, as opposed to being a rare disease allele. Another example in our study was a patient with a VUS in SCN9A. After the provider contacted Invitae, they were told that this VUS was detected in many patients with and without epilepsy, and in one family it was identified as being the cause of their epilepsy. While this did not change variant interpretation, having this information to give to the family informed counseling and affected the patient experience. If this variant were reclassified to pathogenic, this patient may represent a milder case with phenotypic expansions for this gene. In patients with some specific findings and ultra‐rare variants, a single previously identified case with matching phenotype might raise suspicion in conjunction with other VUS follow‐up strategies, such as parental testing. For more recently identified genes that are singularly associated with a severe early onset epilepsy presentation, caution should be used in downgrading a variant's association with other epilepsy presentations. Broader spectrums of disease are often discovered in epilepsy genetics as research evolves. 17 , 18 , 23 Past cases may therefore also impact counseling without significantly shifting clinical suspicion. If the variant has been seen in multiple individuals with a more benign epilepsy course and/or unaffected relatives, counseling can inform families that if the variant were to be reclassified to pathogenic, it may be associated with a spectrum of presentations as opposed to solely with the currently described severe epilepsy presentation. This becomes increasingly important as testing becomes more commonly initiated very early on in seizure presentation and in cases where parental samples are not available for further VUS resolution.

In our study, the provider clearly documented laboratory contact for internal case information or decision not to contact the lab for only 12% of patients where an internal case was suspected. When documented, this was performed by genetic counselors. Genetic counselors were involved in 79/162 (48.8%) cases. Non‐genetics providers may have limited training on VUS interpretation and may not feel equipped to interpret them. This may reflect that non‐genetics providers were less likely to identify the need for laboratory contact or that providers appropriately more often refer patients with VUS requiring laboratory contact to genetic counseling. Anecdotally, genetic counselors at this center suspect that the lab contact rate may have been higher. Cases where internal data was collected but not impactful may not consistently be documented in the EMR. Given the frequency of suspected internal cases, there may be a benefit to encourage inclusion in the medical record by incorporating documentation of lab contact or lack thereof into a standardized template for VUS review.

A major limitation of this study is that we were unable to assess the rate at which internal case data impacts clinical suspicion. The reporting laboratory declined to provide data regarding internal case occurrences for this study due to resource limitations. Access to internal cases from the laboratory for future studies could add further perspective on the utility of this data for diagnosis and subsequent management. It is important to clearly differentiate that where unreported data may inform clinical suspicion regarding a variant's association with disease, the evidence has been determined to not be applicable to the variant's classification based on established guidelines. Significant caution should be taken in documenting changes in variant classification based on independent clinician assessment. Whenever possible, this should be done in conjunction with sharing evidence and reasoning with reporting laboratories to arrive at a joint assessment.

Other limitations to this study include patient demographics being representative of the geographic area only. We expect VUS rates and provider VUS management workload to be even higher for a more ethnically diverse sample, given higher rates of VUS in non‐western European populations. 4 , 24 , 25 We also understand that reporting standards and classification systems of a single lab are not representative of all genetic testing laboratories. Clinical VUS interpretation can also include looking at ClinVar entries from other labs, which could help a provider feel more or less suspicious of a VUS without contacting the reporting laboratory, such as when several other labs classify the VUS as benign. It is important to acknowledge, however, that another lab's classification cannot be used as evidence under ACMG guidelines. Additionally, we did not collect clinical information during chart review, such as age of epilepsy onset/epilepsy type, comorbidities, etc. We are therefore not able to draw any conclusions about specific genotype–phenotype relationships and the differing VUS rate between these. Some epilepsy phenotypes are more specific than others and may have lower VUS rates. Future studies could look closer at this information to determine the impact patient phenotype could have on the use of internal case information for clinical interpretation. Finally, this study relied on EMR documentation, without access to provider emails or phone call records, which may include additional documentation of lab correspondence.

The need for improved data sharing and communication of internal case data between laboratories and clinicians is well recognized 26 , 27 , 28 and an updated version of ACMG guidelines will likely continue to address this. We acknowledge that the transition to exome and genome sequencing (ES/GS) for epilepsy has the potential to reduce VUS burden. Lower VUS rates for ES/GS are demonstrated across a variety of presentations in comparison to multigene panels, 6 especially when run as a Trio. Parental data can allow for quicker VUS upgrade or downgrade when de novo or inherited status acts as evidence in ACMG classification criteria. ES/GS also has the potential to facilitate improved internal case data sharing, given that the generation of standardized phenotype terms from clinical notes is common practice in this setting. Further research into how clinicians investigate and interpret the clinical suspicion of VUS is important to inform continued efforts in variant interpretation, as VUS will continue to be reported regardless of the test type.
