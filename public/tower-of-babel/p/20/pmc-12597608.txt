Subdermally Placed Implanon Migration Into the Pectoralis Muscle and Its Removal in a Resource‐Limited Setup: A Case Report and Brief Literature Review
Authors: Musie Negasi Gebreslase, Birhanu Kassie Reta, Million Abraha Zeray, Awash Solomon, Tsion Haile Woldemariam, Haftom Guesh Girmay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDue to their effectiveness, convenience, and reversibility, single‐rod etonogestrel devices such as Implanon and Nexplanon are increasingly popular as long‐acting reversible contraceptive methods. Despite these benefits, rare but serious side effects such as device migration can occur, making diagnosis and surgical removal challenging. We report a case of a 40‐year‐old woman who presented with an irretrievable contraceptive implant, despite multiple unsuccessful blind surgical attempts at nearby health centers. The device, originally inserted in her left arm 6 years ago, was localized through sequential ultrasound, X‐ray, and targeted high‐resolution ultrasound imaging, which identified the implant in the left pectoralis muscle. Ultimately, ultrasound‐guided removal was successfully performed by a multidisciplinary team. This case highlights the importance of image‐guided techniques for safe and effective implant retrieval, rather than relying on blind surgical exploration.Keywords: contraceptives, migration, subdermal contraceptive implant, ultrasound

Introduction

Contraception is a crucial public health strategy that enables women to appropriately space and time births, thereby preventing unwanted or unplanned pregnancies and mitigating the harmful consequences of unsafe abortions [1]. Progestin contraceptive implants have become one of the preferred long‐acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) [2]. Despite their efficacy, convenience, and reversibility, complications can arise, including insertion‐site infection, injury to adjacent blood vessels or nerves, hematoma formation, extensive fibrosis around the implant, and, rarely, migration to distant anatomical locations [3].

Implant migration within the arm may occur due to deep insertion or external forces, such as device manipulation or contact sports [4]. There have also been reports of implant migration to the lung, chest, or shoulder, often resulting from intravascular or deep placement of the device [3, 5]. Device migration presents significant clinical challenges, especially when the implant is no longer palpable or relocates to areas where removal becomes complex [6]. This case report highlights a rare instance of pectoral muscle migration of a subdermally inserted implant, resulting in a diagnostic challenge.

Case History and Examination

A 40‐year‐old para‐3 woman, whose last normal menstrual period occurred 3 weeks prior to presentation (regular in timing and volume), had a contraceptive implant inserted in her left arm 6 years ago. For the past 3 years, she has sought removal of the implant at various medical facilities because the device has expired, and eventually she began experiencing left shoulder pain. She also reported gradual, significant, and unquantified weight loss over the last 5 years. She is a rural housewife and a physically active farmer engaged in strenuous agricultural work. She denied any unusual vaginal bleeding, lower abdominal pain, or swelling. She also has no complaints of chest pain, cough, shortness of breath, or hemoptysis. Her medical and surgical history was otherwise unremarkable.

On examination, her vital signs were within normal limits, and her BMI was 18 kg/m2. There was a clear skin marking indicating the original insertion site. On local examination of the left arm, a 4 cm vertical scar was found on the medial aspect of the upper arm; there was no palpable implant and no signs of infection. Examination of other systems was unremarkable. At a nearby hospital, a previous blind surgical exploration attempted to locate the implant but failed; this resulted in a large, disfiguring incision scar across the upper arm.

Methods (Investigation and Treatment)

Routine laboratory workup results were within normal ranges. A high‐resolution ultrasound of the entire arm and forearm was performed, but the device was not identified in the subcutaneous tissues or deeper structures. However, a 4 cm linear radiopaque structure was visible on a plain X‐ray (Figure 1). A targeted ultrasound later confirmed a linear echogenic object embedded vertically between the pectoralis major and deltoid muscles, consistent with a migrated implant device (Figure 2).

AP radiograph of the left shoulder and upper chest showing a linear, vertically oriented radiopaque foreign body (black arrow) consistent with the migrated implant. The clavicle is seen superiorly (red arrow), while the upper chest wall and pectoralis region are visualized inferiorly (star), confirming localization within the chest area.

High‐resolution ultrasound exam in long axis shows liner echogenic structure with posterior shadow seen in between the pectoralis major (arrow head) and the deltoid muscle (arrow head). Transverse axis image of the foreign body (Implant) shows a dot‐like hypoechoic lesion with posterior shadow.

Informed consent was obtained for ultrasound‐guided removal. The patient was positioned supine on the ultrasound table with the arm slightly extended and the forearm flexed to flatten the pectoralis and deltoid muscles. A high‐resolution linear ultrasound probe was used to mark the device, assess its depth, and identify underlying vessels. The chest and arm were cleaned with povidone–iodine solution and prepared under sterile conditions.

Local anesthesia was administered by infiltrating 10 mL of 2% lidocaine with adrenaline through the skin access point along the planned extraction path toward the deltoid and into the pectoralis muscle. (Figure 3A) The anesthetic was advanced into the peri‐implant area to achieve hydrodissection and minimize pain during manipulation. Additionally, 10 mL of normal saline was injected into the peri‐implant space to help release potential adhesions and create sufficient room to grasp the implant safely, while displacing it away from underlying neurovascular structures to reduce the risk of complications during extraction. (Figure 3B).

(A) High‐resolution linear ultrasound image of the anterior chest with lateral (L) and medial (M), (red arrow) demonstrating the approach of direction of the needle tract traversing the skin, subcutaneous tissue, deltoid, and pectoralis major muscle toward the migrated intramuscular contraceptive implant. The echogenic needle is visualized in plane, accurately directed toward the implant, which appears as a linear hyperechoic structure (arrowhead) embedded within the muscle fibers. Local anesthesia (2% lidocaine with adrenaline) was infiltrated prior to needle advancement. (B) Ultrasound image showing hydrodissection around the implant site following injection of 10 mL normal saline. The injected fluid creates an anechoic separation between tissue planes (arrowhead), facilitating safe mobilization and retrieval of the implant. As forceps tips may have limited capacity to capture a fibrotically adherent implant, multiple ultrasound‐guided attempts were required to disengage and remove it successfully.

A sterile cover fashioned from a surgical glove was placed over the ultrasound linear probe to maintain aseptic conditions throughout the procedure. A No. 11 surgical blade was used to create a 1 mm skin incision at the marked access point. Mosquito forceps were then introduced (Figure 4A) and bluntly advanced through the subcutaneous tissue, fascia, deltoid, and pectoralis muscles under direct ultrasound guidance. (Figure 4B) Once the jaws of the forceps were opened and engaged to grasp the implant, resistance was encountered due to possible fibrous adhesions surrounding the device. Gentle force was applied, and after three attempts, the implant was successfully removed (Figure 4C).

A clinical image shows the insertion of mosquito artery forceps through a small incision at the site marked by ultrasound. The instrument is gently advanced along the pre‐created tract toward the migrated implant, following the path of least resistance through the hydrodissected tissue (A). The mosquito artery forceps is seen advancing through soft tissue, carefully tracking toward the implant (arrow head). Tissue manipulation is minimal, and instrument positioning is guided by prior hydrodissection and tactile feedback to avoid neurovascular structures (B). The implant is successfully removed and confirms atraumatic retrieval following precise localization and dissection (C).

The extracted implant was examined for any fragmentary remnants, and a post‐procedure ultrasound was performed to assess for acute complications such as hematoma formation. Following confirmation that no complications were present, the skin incision was closed with 2/0 Vicryl cutting sutures and dressed. The procedure was conducted by a multidisciplinary team comprising a gynecologist, a general surgeon, and an experienced radiologist skilled in ultrasound‐guided interventions.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient was discharged uneventfully after 12 h of observation in the outpatient department (OPD). She was re‐evaluated 1 week later at the follow‐up appointment; the wound had healed well with no complications noted, and she was doing well.

Discussion

Migration of subdermally implanted Implanon contraceptive devices is a rare but clinically significant complication documented in the literature. According to Ismaili et al., among 100 women using Implanon, one woman (1%) experienced high‐grade migration of the device [4].

Implant migration is generally categorized into two types based on the extent of displacement. Low‐grade (clinically insignificant) migration involves movement of the implant within 2 cm of the original insertion site, either cranially or caudally. Although considered clinically insignificant, this type of migration is relatively common. For example, in one study of 100 women using implants, 39% had mild displacement of the device within a 2 cm range [4, 7]. High‐grade (clinically significant) migration refers to displacement of the device beyond 2 cm from the original insertion site. This may be localized within the arm or manifest as distant migration to areas such as the chest wall or even the lungs [8].

The precise mechanisms underlying the migration of subdermally inserted implants remain incompletely understood; however, several contributing factors have been proposed. One theory attributes migration to improper insertion technique, particularly deep placement beneath the fascia. Deep implantation increases the risk of migration into muscular or vascular structures and is a primary cause of non‐palpable implants [8]. Another mechanism is intravascular insertion, accidental placement of the device into a vein, such as the basilic vein, which can facilitate migration through the vascular system; cases of migration to the lung or chest cavity by this route have been reported [9, 10, 11].

Significant weight loss post‐insertion has also been proposed as a contributing factor, as decreased subcutaneous tissue may reduce structural support for the device, increasing susceptibility to migration along fascial planes [8]. Lastly, routine upper extremity movements may contribute to gradual micro‐displacement of the device over time, with continuous muscle contractions and mechanical pressure slowly shifting the implant from its original position [12]. When an implant becomes non‐palpable, accurate localization is crucial to avoid unnecessary or harmful surgical exploration.

High‐resolution ultrasound is the first‐line imaging modality to locate a subdermal implant [13, 14]. It is recommended to use a high‐frequency linear transducer (9–18 MHz) for precise localization [15]. To access the posteromedial aspect of the arm, where the implant typically resides, the ultrasound should be performed with the patient's arm abducted to 90°, externally rotated, and elbow flexed, replicating the insertion position [16]. The implant's appearance on ultrasound depends on the transducer orientation: in the longitudinal plane, current ENG implants cast a posterior acoustic shadow, appearing as an echogenic ~40 mm linear structure; in the transverse plane, the implant appears as a small, distinct, highly echogenic spot approximately 2 mm in diameter with a strong posterior shadow [14].

Plain radiography is also used, but it is essential to know the type of implant inserted to determine its radiopacity. Current etonogestrel (ENG) implants are radiopaque due to a barium coating and are easily identified on X‐rays and computed tomography (CT). In contrast, levonorgestrel (LNG) implants and older ENG implants without barium coating are less visible on these modalities [17]. The upper arm X‐ray, including the shoulder in anteroposterior views with the arm in neutral and with internal or external rotation, is the preferred imaging approach. Lateral views may be added if visualization is difficult. Depending on the implant type, one or two radiopaque bands may be visible. If the patient is unsure of the side of insertion, bilateral X‐rays should be obtained [14].

In our setting, when an implant is non‐palpable or deeply positioned, patients are initially evaluated by gynecologists and then referred to orthopedic and trauma surgeons for removal. This approach leverages surgeons' advanced anatomical knowledge to minimize the risk of neurovascular injury during extraction. However, such referrals often cause delays, increase costs, and burden patients, especially those who must travel long distances and face scheduling difficulties. Ideally, evaluation and management of non‐palpable implants should occur in a single, multidisciplinary setting [18].

Once localized, surgical removal traditionally requires a soft tissue incision large enough to accommodate one or two fingers, potentially resulting in cosmetically undesirable scars. Additionally, blunt dissection carries risks of neurovascular injury [19]. To mitigate these issues, minimally invasive ultrasound‐guided removal is increasingly recognized as first‐line management [20]. This outpatient procedure is performed under local anesthesia, is cost‐effective, and can be completed in a single session without the need for rescheduling.

Overall, the success rate of ultrasound‐guided removal is as high as 86%, depending largely on the implant's depth and location. Intramuscular migration presents the greatest challenge and may reduce the likelihood of successful noninvasive removal. Nevertheless, ultrasound remains invaluable for precise localization prior to surgical extraction in such cases.

Conclusion

Proper insertion of subdermal contraceptive implants by well‐trained healthcare providers is critical to preventing deep implantation and associated complications. Clients should be counseled to regularly palpate the implant site and promptly seek medical attention if the implant becomes non‐palpable. Blind surgical exploration should be avoided to prevent unnecessary and disfiguring scars. Imaging plays a pivotal role in accurately localizing migrated implants and guiding minimally invasive removal. When performed by a multidisciplinary team within a single center, ultrasound‐guided retrieval offers excellent outcomes with minimal trauma and high patient satisfaction.
