Longitudinal changes in inflammatory markers and body weight in adults with primary immunodeficiency receiving immunoglobulin replacement therapy: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yasemin Akgul Balaban, Mustafa Ilker Inan, Fikriye Kalkan, Ezgi Sonmez, Fevzi Demirel, Ali Selcuk, Sait Yesillik, Ozgur Kartal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT) is the cornerstone of treatment for adults with primary immunodeficiency [primary immunodeficiency diseases (PID)]. Its role in infection prevention is well established. However, its effects on systemic inflammation and metabolic parameters remain incompletely understood. This study evaluated one-year changes in inflammatory indices and body weight in adults with PID receiving intravenous (IVIG) or subcutaneous (SCIG) immunoglobulin therapy. This retrospective study included 32 adults with PID. The cohort consisted predominantly of patients with common variable immunodeficiency, along with selected cases of Good syndrome and CTLA-4 insufficiency. Patients received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (n = 22) or SCIG (n = 10). Inflammatory markers [neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil count] and body weight were assessed at baseline and after 12 months. Non-parametric tests were used due to sample size. Body weight increased significantly in both the IVIG (P = .01) and SCIG (P = .011) groups. In the IVIG group, CRP (P = .005), absolute neutrophil count (P = .017), and NLR (P = .034) decreased significantly. In the SCIG group, body weight increased significantly. However, changes in inflammatory markers were not significant. Platelet counts decreased (P = .012), while WBC counts increased (P = .016). IgRT was associated with increased body weight in adults with PID. This was consistent across both IVIG and SCIG groups. IVIG was also associated with reductions in inflammatory markers. These findings suggest that IgRT may have effects beyond infection prevention. Body weight may be a useful parameter during follow-up of adult PID patients.

1. Introduction

Currently referred to as inborn errors of immunity, primary immunodeficiency diseases (PID) comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by increased susceptibility to infections, autoimmune disease, autoinflammation, allergy, and malignancy.[1] Although PID has traditionally been considered a pediatric condition, increasing recognition in adulthood has been reported in recent years.[2] Despite advances in genetic diagnostics, delayed diagnosis in adult patients remains common and is associated with prolonged morbidity and increased healthcare burden.[3,4]

Immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT) represents the standard of care for many forms of PID and is generally administered lifelong. IgRT has been shown to reduce the frequency and severity of bacterial infections, including pneumonia and sepsis, as well as healthcare utilization and antibiotic use, while improving patient-reported outcomes.[5–8] Both intravenous (IVIG) and subcutaneous (SCIG) administration routes are effective, and treatment selection is typically individualized based on clinical and patient-related factors.[9–11] Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is commonly administered at regular monthly intervals, whereas subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG) is typically given on a weekly or biweekly basis, allowing for more stable serum IgG levels and improved flexibility in selected patients.[9,10]

Beyond infection prevention, increasing evidence suggests that IgRT may influence systemic immune activation in PID. Chronic inflammation in these patients may contribute to metabolic alterations and impaired nutritional status. However, data on the longitudinal effects of IgRT on inflammatory markers and anthropometric parameters in adult patients remain limited, particularly in real-world heterogeneous cohorts.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate one-year changes in body weight, body mass index, and systemic inflammatory markers in adult patients with PID receiving IgRT. We further compared outcomes between IVIG and SCIG treatment modalities in a real-world clinical setting. We also considered that differences in treatment administration routes and dosing schedules may potentially influence both metabolic and inflammatory outcomes over time.

2. Methods

This retrospective study included 32 adult patients diagnosed with PID who initiated IgRT at our clinic. The cohort consisted predominantly of patients with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) (n = 29), along with a small number of patients with Good syndrome (n = 2) and CTLA-4 insufficiency (n = 1). Patients younger than 18 years were excluded. Data obtained at treatment initiation and after one year of therapy were compared. All eligible patients with complete baseline and 12-month follow-up data were included in the analysis; no additional exclusions were applied.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ankara Gulhane Training and Research Hospital (Protocol code: 2024-45). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients received IVIG at a maintenance dose of 400 to 600 mg/kg every 3 to 4 weeks, or SCIG at an equivalent monthly dose administered in weekly divided doses, adjusted based on clinical response and serum IgG trough levels. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) trough levels were periodically monitored during follow-up, and immunoglobulin dosing was adjusted according to body weight and clinical response to maintain target IgG levels within the therapeutic range. The choice of administration route was based on clinical evaluation and shared decision-making with patient, considering disease characteristics and practical accessibility factors.

Body weight and body mass index were recorded at baseline and after 12 months of treatment. Inflammatory markers included C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, pan immune-inflammation value, and systemic inflammatory response index. These indices were calculated using standard formulas.[12,13]

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25.0. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (range), as appropriate. Paired comparisons between baseline and 12-month values were performed using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test depending on distribution. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. There were no missing data for the variables included in the analysis.

3. Results

A total of 32 patients were included in the study, of whom 9 (28.1%) were female and 23 (71.9%) were male. According to treatment modality, 22 patients (68.8%) received IVIG and 10 patients (31.2%) received SCIG. In the IVIG group, 9 patients (40.9%) were female and 13 (59.1%) were male, whereas all patients in the SCIG group were male. The age range of the cohort was 19 to 71 years.

During the one-year follow-up, weight gain was observed in 26 (81.2%) patients, while 5 patients had stable weight and 1 patient showed weight loss. The single case of weight loss occurred in a patient receiving IVIG.

3.1. IVIG group

In patients receiving IVIG (n = 22), body weight increased significantly after one year (P = .01). A significant reduction in neutrophil count was observed (P = .017).

Among inflammatory parameters, CRP decreased significantly (P = .005). NLR also showed a significant reduction (P = .034). No significant changes were observed in other hematological or inflammatory parameters (P > .05).

3.2. SCIG group

In patients treated with SCIG (n = 10), body weight also increased significantly over 12 months (P = .011). WBC counts increased significantly (P = .016). Platelet counts decreased (P = .012). No significant changes were observed in inflammatory markers, including CRP, NLR, systemic immune-inflammationindex, and systemic inflammatory response index (P > .05). Eosinophil counts were also analyzed and showed no significant change during follow-up in either group. All findings for both IVIG and SCIG groups are summarized in Table 1. No confounder-adjusted analysis was performed due to the small sample size.

4. Discussion

IgRT in PID is primarily used as a substitution treatment. It is usually administered at standard doses and adjusted according to clinical response and IgG trough levels.[14,15] Because dosing is weight-based, changes in body weight may have practical implications for treatment follow-up and dose adjustment.[16] This may indicate improvement in the underlying catabolic state, although this interpretation should be made cautiously.

Patients with PID often present with low or suboptimal body weight. This may be related to chronic inflammation, recurrent infections, or malabsorption.[17] While the effectiveness of IgRT in reducing infection frequency is well established,[5–8,18] its effects on metabolic and anthropometric parameters remain less well characterized. In our cohort, body weight increased in most patients in both IVIG and SCIG groups.

An important feature of our cohort was that all patients receiving SCIG were male. This reflects the real-world distribution of patients in our center during the study period. However, this limits the evaluation of sex-related differences. Therefore, results should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. This may reflect local practice patterns; however, no conclusions can be drawn regarding patient preference.

Another notable finding was the difference in inflammatory responses between treatment modalities. Patients receiving IVIG showed significant reductions in CRP, NLR, and neutrophil counts, which may indicate a potential effect on systemic inflammation. In contrast, although patients in the SCIG group also demonstrated weight gain, changes in inflammatory markers did not reach statistical significance. This may be related to the smaller sample size and differences in pharmacokinetic profiles between IVIG and SCIG, rather than a true absence of effect.[19–22] Therefore, direct comparisons between treatment modalities should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the imbalance in sample size and sex distribution.

In the SCIG subgroup, a significant increase in WBC and a decrease in platelet counts were observed. However, given the small sample size and retrospective design, these changes may reflect individual variability or transient clinical factors rather than a specific biological effect of SCIG therapy. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and are not sufficient to suggest a consistent treatment-related hematological effect.

The relatively small sample size and retrospective design of this study should be acknowledged as important limitations. In addition, the heterogeneous nature of PID should be considered when interpreting the results. Although most patients in our cohort had CVID, the inclusion of less common conditions such as Good syndrome and CTLA-4 insufficiency reflects real-world clinical practice. However, subgroup-specific conclusions cannot be drawn due to limited numbers.

Another consideration is that changes in body weight may be influenced by factors other than IgRT, including dietary habits or comorbid conditions. This highlights the need for cautious interpretation of anthropometric outcomes. One patient experienced weight loss during follow-up, which was attributed to a concurrent dietary intervention.

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides real-world longitudinal data on inflammatory markers and body weight in adult patients with PID receiving replacement-dose IgRT. While previous studies have mainly focused on high-dose IVIG in other inflammatory conditions,[23,24] data in adult PID populations remain limited.

In conclusion, IgRT was associated with weight gain in this cohort and, particularly in the IVIG group, with reductions in inflammatory markers. These findings suggest that body weight and inflammatory parameters may provide complementary information during the follow-up of adult PID patients. However, these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and further studies with larger, prospective cohorts are needed.
