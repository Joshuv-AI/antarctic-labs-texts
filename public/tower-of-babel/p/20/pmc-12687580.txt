Estimation of economic weights for growth, feed efficiency, health, and fertility traits in a breeding program for meat rabbits
Authors: Fabián Magaña-Valencia, César Jiménez, Raymundo Rodríguez-de Lara, Rodolfo Ramírez-Valverde, José G García-Muñiz, Rafael Núñez-Domínguez, Jorge Hidalgo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe definition of breeding objectives in meat rabbits’ breeding programs requires assigning economic weights to relevant traits. Our objective in this study was to estimate economic values for 12 key traits in meat rabbit production by calculating the first partial derivative of the profit function in a full-cycle production system. The model was parameterized to represent a semi-technified farm with 250 does managed in two triweekly groups, with natural mating occurring at 11 d postpartum. Economic data were expressed in U.S. dollars. Production costs were categorized into feed and non-feed costs associated with adult does, replacement does, bucks, replacement bucks, and growing rabbits. Revenues were derived from carcasses, pelts, and culled animals. Relative economic values (REV), expressed per doe per year, were obtained by multiplying the absolute economic value of each trait by its respective additive standard deviation. Feed costs accounted for 72.8% of the total. The REVs for litter size traits were $46.05 for the number of kits born alive, $42.83 for the total number of kits born, and $0.22 for the proportion of kits born alive. Efficiency traits’ REV were −$6.93 for feed conversion ratio, $0.08 for feed efficiency, $2.98 for daily weight gain during the fattening period, and $0.14 for carcass yield. The functional traits’ REV were $0.03 for female fertility, $0.002 for replacement doe fertility, and $0.57 for longevity. Health traits’ REV were $0.18 and $0.19 for survival during lactation and fattening, respectively. Sensitivity analyses showed that increasing feed costs by 50% and carcass rabbit sale price by 40% resulted in changes ≤ 4% for most traits in the relative proportion of economic values, compared to that of the number of kits born alive. The traits with higher changes were efficiency traits (1–16%) and mainly feed conversion ratio (15–136%). The estimated economic values provide a basis for defining breeding objectives under the given production and market conditions. The methodological framework allows adaptation of selection goals to market fluctuations and the incorporation of new traits as they become relevant. Although the economic weights were relatively stable under these variations, it is recommended that they be re-estimated periodically.Keywords: genetic improvement, profit function, selection index

Introduction

Efficiency and profitability are essential in the production of animal-derived goods to ensure both economic viability and environmental sustainability. Genetic improvement can contribute to achieving these goals by increasing, through selection, the frequency of desirable alleles for economically important traits within populations. In meat rabbit production, genetic selection has traditionally focused on a limited number of traits. Maternal lines have been selected primarily for litter size (Baselga 2004), while paternal lines have been selected for post-weaning growth or body weight at slaughter age (Piles and Blasco 2010).

Recent studies have expanded the focus to include additional productive and functional traits such as feed efficiency (Piles and Sánchez 2019), carcass yield (Larzul et al. 2005; Matics et al. 2014), female fertility (Piles et al. 2005), pre-weaning survival (Ziadi et al. 2013), stress resistance, disease resistance, and physiological adaptability (Argente et al. 2019; Beloumi et al. 2020), and longevity and resilience (García et al. 2021). This broader approach to selection enables the inclusion of multiple traits to maintain productivity, addressing concerns regarding animal welfare and antibiotic use.

Simultaneous selection for multiple traits requires the development of selection indices, which in turn depend on weighting factors. These weights are typically based on economic values (EV), which quantify the increase in profit resulting from genetic improvement in each trait, assuming all other traits remain constant (Blasco 1995; Hazel 1943). Two main approaches have been used to estimate EV: The first partial derivative of the profit function and bioeconomic models. The key difference between these approaches lies in the number of biological relationships considered (Nielsen et al. 2014). Some authors have estimated the economic importance of specific traits in meat rabbit production for various markets (Cartuche et al. 2014; Krupová et al. 2020; Prayaga and Eady 2000). However, the economic valuation of traits under specific market conditions and with updated operating costs is necessary to refine selection programs objectively. Our objective in this study was to determine the economic weights of productive and functional traits in a meat rabbit breeding program under semi-technified farm conditions in Central Mexico.

Materials and methods

Data were obtained from the Applied Research Unit in Rabbit Production at Universidad Autónoma Chapingo, Mexico, where animals are raised for commercial purposes and were not subjected to experimental manipulation. Therefore, no specific ethical approval was required for this study.

Description of the production system

A semi-technified commercial farm was used as a model, with a capacity for 250 does of the New Zealand White breed. Farm activities were assumed to be performed by a single worker. Individual cages were used for adult does, bucks, and replacements, while group cages were used for fattening, with a capacity of eight rabbits each. The does were managed in two triweekly breeding groups, with natural mating occurring at 11 d postpartum. One buck was assigned per four does. The farm produced its own replacement females and males. The first mating occurred at 125 d of age for females and at 150 d of age for males.

The pregnancy diagnosis was performed by abdominal palpation 12 d after mating. Non-pregnant females were rebred in the following group. Controlled lactation was practiced from days 3 to 11 postpartum, during which females accessed the nest box only once per day, except on day 10 when the nests remained closed to stimulate female receptivity. At 30 d of age, kits and their mothers were moved from maternity to fattening cages, and weaning occurred at 35 d. Between days 30 and 35, 1 mL/L of enrofloxacin was administered in water, followed by a second treatment with doxycycline between days 40 and 45 to prevent digestive disorders during the fattening period.

Five to seven days before kindling, females were moved to clean and disinfected maternity cages. Clean, disinfected nest boxes and barriers were installed 3 d before kindling, with wood shavings added to the nests. Females had access to the nest box 2 d before parturition and continued to have access until day 25 postpartum, when the nest was removed. Two diets were provided ad libitum: one for breeders and replacements (17.5% crude protein) and one for fattening rabbits (15.5% crude protein). Water was supplied via nipple drinkers. Fattening rabbits were slaughtered at 77 d of age, with an average body weight of 2.2 kg and a carcass yield of 55%. Revenues were generated from on-farm sales of carcasses, fresh pelts, and culled animals. Production indicators related to management are presented in Appendix 1.

Revenues and production costs

Economic data were expressed in U.S. dollars. Revenues considered in the model were derived from sales of carcasses ($6.99/kg), culled live rabbits ($6.45/animal), and fresh pelts ($0.19/pelt). Costs were classified as feed and non-feed costs and were attributed to adult does, replacement does, bucks, replacement bucks, and fattening rabbits. Total non-feed costs were proportionally allocated according to the annual number of animals of each type and were kept constant. A 5:1 ratio was used to allocate costs between fattening rabbits and the other animal categories.

Feed costs were estimated for each group of animals based on their average daily feed intake, the duration of the feeding period, and the feed price (Table 1). The cost of breeder and fattening diets was $0.51/kg and $0.55/kg, respectively. Feed consumption for adult females was calculated as the weighted average across five physiological stages: early lactation, mid-lactation, late lactation, prepartum, and early gestation or empty. Pre-weaning kit feed intake, from days 22 to 35 postpartum, was combined with the intake of lactating females during that same period. The average number of females in each stage was estimated based on a 49.5-d parturition interval. Feed costs were based on prices recorded at the Applied Research Unit in Rabbit Production (Universidad Autónoma Chapingo) during 2024, ensuring that estimates reflect current local production conditions.

In rabbit production systems, the breeding doe is generally considered the main productive unit, as farm size, reproductive rhythm, and annual output depend on the number of reproductive females. Therefore, economic and relative economic values were expressed on a per doe per year basis, allowing the results to reflect the economic impact of each trait on the profitability of a representative farm.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess how changes in key economic parameters affect the estimated economic values of the traits. This method allows evaluating the robustness of the results to potential market fluctuations (Saltelli et al. 2008). Under the baseline scenario (S0), carcass sales represented 96% of the total revenue, whereas feed cost accounted for 72% of the total cost in the simulated farm. Based on this structure, three alternative scenarios were defined using historical price variation data from the National Institute of Statistics and Geography of Mexico for the period 2019–2024 (INEGI 2024). Since specific data for rabbit production was not available, the maximum monthly variations in the prices of balanced feeds for poultry, swine, and other livestock, as well as carcass meat for cattle and swine, were used as references.

According to these data, three scenarios were evaluated. Scenario 1 (S1): 50% increase in feed cost, Scenario 2 (S2): 40% increase in the selling price of rabbit carcass meat, and Scenario 3 (S3): simultaneous 50% rise in feed cost and 40% increase in meat price. Sensitivity was computed as the difference between the economic value of a trait in the baseline scenario (S0) and its economic value in the alternate scenario (Si). This approach quantifies how much the economic value changes in response to variations in feed or product prices.

Results

Cost structure

Feed costs represented 76.2% of the total annual cost of the simulated production system. These costs were distributed across different animal groups based on their specific feed intake and the price per kilogram of feed, with growing rabbits accounting for the largest share of feed expenditure due to their population size and cumulative consumption up to 77 d of age (Fig. 1, left panel).

The distribution of non-feed costs by group of animals (Fig. 1, right panel) enabled a more accurate estimation of the production cost per kilogram of carcass meat, supporting the assessment of the economic impact of each trait. The distribution of non-feed costs was similar to that of feed costs, with growing (fattening) rabbits and adult does accounting for the majority (over 85%) of non-feed costs.

From another perspective, non-feed costs accounted for 23.8% of the total production cost, and their distribution by type of expense showed that the most relevant components were labor, slaughtering expenses, and depreciation of facilities and equipment (Fig. 2).

Considering the selling price of carcass meat, and income obtained from skins and culled animals, the benefit-cost relation of the system was 1.29, equivalent to a profitability of 29%.

Traits with the highest AEV were FCR, LB, and TB, ranging from $26.85 to $36.00, with relative proportions (p_LB) of −1.17 and 0.87, respectively. Carcass yield had a moderate AEV, equivalent to 20% of the AEV for LB. Traits such as PLB, Fd, Lon, SL, FS, and ADG showed low AEV, ranging from $1.50 to $4.20, corresponding to 5–13% of the reference value (LB). In contrast, Frd exhibited the lowest AEV, accounting for only 0.20% of the AEV of LB.

Similarly, LB and TB had the highest REV. In contrast, the REV for FCR declined substantially, from an AEV relative to LB of −117% to just −7%. All other traits had REV below $0.60, with relative proportions <1.2%. This discrepancy is explained by differences in the additive genetic variation of the traits: despite its large AEV, FCR exhibited a low additive genetic standard deviation, which limits the expected genetic response and therefore reduces its REV. These findings imply that, given the current genetic parameters and market conditions, selection pressure should prioritize traits like LB and TB, which combine high economic impact with greater expected genetic gain.

Sensitivity analysis

Table 5 shows the results of the sensitivity analysis for AEV. These values were expressed as proportions relative to the AEV of LB, and changes in these proportions were used to indicate shifts in trait ranking or relative importance. The baseline scenario (S0) served as the benchmark. For litter size traits (LB, TB, and PLB), survival traits (SL and FS), and longevity, the proportions slightly changed (≤ 4%) across all scenarios. In contrast, FE, ADG, and CY showed minimal changes in S3 (≤ 2%), small to moderate changes in S2 (≤ 6%), and the most pronounced variation in S1 (ranging from 9 to 16%). The FCR showed the greatest variation, with its AEV proportion increasing from 15% in S3 to 136% in S1. The pattern of change in the proportion of REV across all traits mirrored that observed for AEV, although with smaller magnitudes. For example, changes in REV for FCR ranged only from 1 to 9%.

Discussion

Cost structure

Economic values have been estimated for various traits across different livestock species, countries, and production systems using two main methodological approaches: bioeconomic models and the first partial derivative of the profit function. Examples include sheep in Canada (Quinton et al. 2014) and Ireland (Bohan et al. 2019), dairy cattle in the United States (De Vries 2006) and Germany (Schmidtmann et al. 2021), beef cattle in the United States (Fuerst-Waltl et al. 2016) and Spain (López-Paredes et al. 2017), dual-purpose cattle in Italy (Mancin et al. 2022) and Slovakia (Krupová et al. 2016), pigs in Australia (Hermesch et al. 2014), Hungary (Houška et al. 2010) and the Czech Republic (Krupa et al. 2017), and fish in Europe (Janssen et al. 2017), among others. For rabbits, although EV have been estimated for production systems in other countries (Armero and Blasco 1992; Cartuche et al. 2014; Krupová et al. 2020; Prayaga and Eady 2000), this is the first study to estimate EV for both productive and functional traits in rabbits under Mexican production conditions, using the first partial derivative of the profit function.

The economic value represents the change in economic return, typically per doe per year, resulting from a one-unit increase in a trait (ie, AEV) or a one additive genetic standard deviation increase (ie, REV), while keeping all other traits constant (Armero and Blasco 1992; Hazel 1943). The EV are breed-specific and sensitive to market conditions, model assumptions, and the estimation methodology. Additionally, trait definitions are not always consistent across studies. These differences, combined with variation in additive genetic standard deviations between populations, complicate direct comparisons of EV across countries or production systems (Wolfová et al. 2007).

Despite these limitations, general comparisons of the relative magnitude of EV between previous studies and the present work are possible. The cost structure in this study reflects a system with high dependency on feed inputs, similar to those reported in studies from other countries. The proportion of feed costs in our system (76%) exceeded values reported by Cartuche et al. (2014) in Spain (45%) and by Krupová et al. (2020) in the Czech Republic (65%). Although fixed costs in all three studies included labor, maintenance, administration, and depreciation, country-specific factors led to significant differences, particularly in labor cost, which accounted for 8% in our study compared to 18% in Cartuche et al. (2014), and in feed conversion ratio, which was 3.45 in our case versus 2.6 and 3.5 reported by Cartuche et al. (2014) and Krupová et al. (2020), respectively.

System profitability, defined in this study as the ratio of profit to total costs, was 29%, which falls within the range reported in the literature; however, it is higher than most previous studies. This difference could mainly be attributable to the type of product sold. In our system, over 97% of revenue came from the sale of carcasses, which generally provide higher profit margins than live animal sales. For instance, Szendrő et al. (2016) reported profitability ranging from 1.6% to 16.3% for live sales, 43% to 52% for carcass sales, and 50% to 57% for sales of parts of the carcass, with variations depending on feed costs. Among studies based on live sales, Armero and Blasco (1992) reported a profitability of 35%, Prayaga and Eady (2000) reported 17%, and Cartuche et al. (2014) published 0.81%. Even in a carcass-based system, Krupová et al. (2020) reported profitability of only 6.2%.

Economic values

Almost all the estimated EV in this study were positive, except for FCR, which showed an inverse relationship with system profitability. The highest EV were observed for FCR, LB, and TB. The magnitude of both absolute and relative EV for LB, TB, and FCR is explained by the fact that one additional kit per doe per litter, under the defined conditions, resulted in 1253 to 1350 more kits sold per year, and that an increase of one unit in FCR raised total production costs by 12%. The size of the AEV aligns with findings reported by Armero and Blasco (1992), Cartuche et al. (2014), and Krupová et al. (2020). However, the additive genetic standard deviation of LB and TB used in this study was greater than that of FCR, so the REV for FCR was lower than those of LB and TB, even though FCR had the highest AEV.

The trait RR showed one of the highest AEV, in agreement with Krupová et al. (2020); however, the AEV of RR relative to LB was considerably lower in our study (0.20 vs 0.46). Although RR can significantly influence the quantity of rabbit meat available for sale and thus the system’s revenue, its low additive genetic standard deviation translates into a low REV. A low or near-zero EV does not imply that the trait lacks importance, as noted by Cartuche et al. (2014); instead, it suggests that small changes are expected either because the trait is near its optimum or due to low additive genetic variance. In this context, optimizing RR could have a marginal impact on the overall profitability.

Pre-weaning and fattening survival showed lower AEV than litter size traits due to their smaller effect on the number of animals slaughtered annually and, consequently, on total revenue. A 1% improvement in SL or FS corresponded to an increase of 108 and 124 slaughtered kits per year, respectively. The relative AEV of these traits compared to LB (p_SL = 0.085 and p_FS = 0.117) was consistent with previous studies, where values ranged from 0.108 to 0.136 (Armero and Blasco 1992; Cartuche et al. 2014; Krupová et al. 2020; Prayaga and Eady 2000). The relative REV of both traits compared to LB was even lower (0.004 in both cases), mainly due to their low heritability.

The EV of longevity was directly evaluated in only one study (Krupová et al. 2020) and indirectly referenced in another through changes in RR (Cartuche et al. 2014). In the present study, Lng, defined as the period between the first successful mating and the culling or death of the doe, was evaluated based on a reduction in RR, assuming one additional litter per doe. This increase represented one extra reproductive cycle and resulted in a 12% reduction in annual RR. The AEV of Lng was among the lowest, accounting for only 5% of the AEV for LB. This agrees with Cartuche et al. (2014), who derived similar conclusions from changes in RR. However, when expressed in additive genetic standard deviation units (ie, as REV), Lng showed a greater impact than most traits assessed, suggesting that extending productive life could moderately enhance profitability. In contrast, Krupová et al. (2020) argued that longevity is not the most critical trait in rabbit production, yet highlighted its relevance, reporting a high p_AEV (0.987) when longevity was modeled as one additional reproductive cycle, nearly as high as for LB.

The trait with the lowest AEV was Frd. No previous studies were found that reported EV for this trait; however, the result is expected, as Frd affects only the feed cost of does during the short rearing phase, from 78 d of age to the first gestation (<2 mo). Similarly, although adult female fertility influences the total number of litters per year, its AEV was low compared to other traits. A 1% increase in Fd reduced the interval between litters by 0.33 d, translating into 0.05 more litters per doe per year and, consequently, 65.5 more kits sold annually. The relative AEV of Fd compared to LB (p_Fd = 0.05) was similar to values reported by Cartuche et al. (2014) and Krupová et al. (2020), which were 0.108 and 0.072, respectively.

The EV of average daily gain during the fattening period has been estimated in the four previous studies (Armero and Blasco 1992; Cartuche et al. 2014; Krupová et al. 2020; Prayaga and Eady 2000). Similar to the present study, ADG had a low AEV. The relative AEV of ADG (p_ADG) compared to LB in this study was 0.03, lower than previously reported values in a 0.08–0.14 range. However, the REV of ADG was 2.98, ranking above most other traits evaluated here, indicating that improving growth rate has a positive impact on profitability. Given that FCR had the highest AEV but is difficult and costly to measure routinely (Blasco et al. 2018), its genetic correlation with ADG may be used to indirectly improve FCR.

The ratio of each trait’s EV relative to LB provides an indicator of changes in their ranking or relative importance. The comparison between AEV and REV revealed changes in economic ranking among traits. For example, FCR, which had the highest AEV, dramatically loses importance in terms of REV, with its proportional value dropping from -117 to Conflict of interest8%. In contrast, TB, which had a high AEV (87% of LB), showed an increased REV, reaching 93% of LB. These shifts reflect the sensitivity of economic weighting to differences in additive genetic standard deviations (equivalently, genetic variation), a key consideration for the design of breeding programs.

Magnitude of the unit of change

It is important to note the inverse relationship between FCR and FE, two closely related traits with distinct economic implications. While an increase of one unit in FCR led to a 12% increase in total production costs (AEV = −$36.03), a 1% improvement in FE resulted in only a 1.4% reduction in those costs (AEV = $4.12). This discrepancy indicates that although both traits reflect feed utilization in relation to weight gain, their perceived economic value differs significantly.

A comparable situation arises with LBA. Increasing litter size by one kit per doe per litter represents a substantial biological change. The selection response for this trait ranges from 0.05 to 0.13 kits per generation (Baselga 2004; García and Baselga 2002), suggesting that it may take 10 to 20 generations to achieve a one–kit increase. Conversely, if the unit of change were defined as a 1% increase in average total litter size rather than one additional kit, the corresponding AEV would be considerably smaller, and the perceived relative importance of the traits would shift (AEV = $30.86 for one additional kit per litter per doe vs AEV = $2.25 for a 1% increase in litter size).

Both approaches are equivalent, but the perceived economic importance of a trait can change dramatically depending on the magnitude of the change considered, for example, one kit versus a 1% increase in litter size. Consequently, functional traits such as fertility, longevity, or survival may be perceived as more relevant (in terms of p_EV) than they are currently perceived.

In this context, the use of REV, which standardizes AEV by the additive genetic standard deviation, allows for a better comparison across traits. This approach ensures that differences in measurement scales or units of change do not distort the expected response when constructing a selection index. Nevertheless, it would be advisable to verify that using different units of change (absolute vs relative) does not alter the efficiency of the selection index.

Variation in economic values

When both feed costs and rabbit meat sale prices were simultaneously increased, according to fluctuations observed in the Mexican market over the past 5 yr, the AEV and REV risen by 33 to 58%, without altering the ranking of traits. Traits related to litter size and fertility showed high stability across the three scenarios, despite changes in feed costs and meat prices. In contrast, FCR and FE exhibited greater sensitivity, particularly under S1, corroborating that these traits are more dependent on the variations in feed cost. The most substantial changes were observed in FCR when the feed price was modified, and in LB when the sale carcass price was modified. These results are consistent with those reported in similar studies (Cartuche et al. 2014; Krupová et al. 2020).

Conclusion

The economic values estimated in this study provide a basis for defining breeding objectives for meat rabbits under the specified production and market conditions. The methodological framework used for calculating economic values allows for the adaptation of selection goals in response to market fluctuations and the integration of new traits as they become relevant. Although economic weights proved to be relatively robust to changes in input costs and product prices, periodic re-estimation is recommended after several generations of selection, as selection-induced shifts in trait means and variances may affect their economic importance.
