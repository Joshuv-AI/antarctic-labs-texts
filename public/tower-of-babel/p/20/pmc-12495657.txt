Electroacupuncture ameliorates cognitive dysfunction in T2DM rats by modulating astrocytic polarization and aberrant energy metabolism in the hippocampus via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway
Authors: Xiaolu Li, Rui Li, Jiayi Lin, Xiaoli Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment is a frequent but underrecognized complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), closely associated with hippocampal neuroinflammation, glial dysfunction, energy metabolism disruption, and tau hyperphosphorylation. Astrocyte polarization plays a pivotal role in these processes, yet its regulation and impact remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate whether electroacupuncture (EA) alleviates cognitive dysfunction in T2DM rats by modulating astrocytic polarization and hippocampal energy metabolism via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.MethodsT2DM was induced using a high-fat diet and streptozotocin (STZ) injection. Rats were divided into four groups: Control, Model, EA, and EA + DKK1 (Dickkopf-1, a Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor). EA was applied at Weiwanxiashu (EX-B3), Pishu (BL20), Zusanli (ST36), Yinlingquan (SP9), and Baihui (DU20) for 9 weeks. Behavioral, molecular, and metabolic changes were assessed using the Morris Water Maze, Western blotting, quantitative PCR (qPCR), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), immunostaining, and targeted metabolomics.ResultsEA improved spatial learning and memory, inhibited A1-type astrocyte marker C3, and enhanced A2-type marker S100A10. It also activated Wnt3a, p-GSK3β, nuclear β-catenin, and Ngn2, increased Glut1/Glut3 expression, restored ATP levels, and reduced glycolytic metabolite accumulation. Additionally, EA attenuated tau phosphorylation and neuronal injury. These effects were abolished by DKK1, confirming Wnt/β-catenin involvement.ConclusionsEA ameliorates cognitive deficits in T2DM rats by regulating astrocyte polarization and improving hippocampal energy metabolism through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings suggest a promising non-pharmacological approach to treating diabetes-related cognitive impairment and highlight the role of astrocytic immunometabolism in diabetic neurodegeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01941-z.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is a common complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which may emerge even at the early stages of the disease. Poor long-term glycemic control worsens brain structural damage and increases the risk of cognitive dysfunction in T2DM patients (up to 37%) [1, 2]. Systemic metabolic disturbances induced by T2DM can impair brain insulin signaling, directly affecting central nervous system (CNS) function, particularly damaging the hippocampus, a region crucial for learning and memory [3]. Cognitive dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of diabetes-associated neurodegeneration. Astrocytes play a central role in maintaining central nervous system homeostasis and supporting neuronal function, both structurally and metabolically [4]. Notably, their polarization state is closely associated with hippocampal neuroinflammation and disrupted energy metabolism [5]. Following CNS injury, astrocytes become reactive and polarize into either neurotoxic A1 or neuroprotective A2 phenotypes [5]. In T2DM models with cognitive impairment, hippocampal astrocytic polarization is consistently observed, with abnormal morphology, increased A1 astrocytes, and marked neuronal damage [6, 7]. The accumulation of A1-type astrocytes has been shown to promote tau hyperphosphorylation, a key pathological hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [8]. Meanwhile, dysregulated astrocyte-mediated energy metabolism—such as impaired glycogen storage and reduced cerebral glucose utilization—further compromises neuronal energy supply [9–12]. Astrocytes, as the principal glycogen storage cells in the CNS, are metabolically coupled to neuronal oxidative phosphorylation via aerobic glycolysis. One mechanism by which astrocytes support neuronal energy demands is by sensing glucose availability And regulating cerebral glucose uptake through glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) [9, 10]. Importantly, reduced brain glucose metabolism and excessive glycogen accumulation in the hippocampus are frequently observed in T2DM, potentially resulting from astrocytic polarization imbalance and consequent glycogen metabolic dysfunction [13–15].

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a critical neuroprotective cascade whose dysregulation has been closely implicated in the pathogenesis of both T2DM and AD [16–20]. It regulates neuronal development and insulin signaling and plays a pivotal role in astrocyte polarization [16–18]. Activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been shown to suppress the formation of neurotoxic A1-type astrocytes while promoting the A2 neuroprotective phenotype, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation [21]. Furthermore, modulation of key negative regulators within this pathway has been demonstrated to significantly ameliorate T2DM-related cognitive deficits [18, 20].

Electroacupuncture (EA), a non-pharmacological intervention, alleviates T2DM and its associated cognitive impairment, with potential advantages for neurodegenerative diseases [22–25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that EA can mitigate neuroinflammation by modulating the polarization of microglia and astrocytes in the hippocampus, thereby reducing tau hyperphosphorylation and amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition [23, 26]. EA activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promoting hippocampal neural stem cell proliferation and increasing brain-derived neurotrophic factor levels, key drivers of neural regeneration [27, 28]. This mechanism effectively supports the brain’s natural repair processes [27, 28]. However, it remains unclear whether EA alleviates T2DM-related cognitive impairment by modulating astrocyte polarization and brain energy metabolism through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

This study aimed to elucidate the mechanism by which EA ameliorates cognitive dysfunction in T2DM, with a particular focus on astrocyte polarization and energy metabolic remodeling mediated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. We employed behavioral analysis, histopathology, metabolomics, and molecular biology approaches to test the hypothesis that EA activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promotes astrocytic polarization toward the neuroprotective A2 phenotype. This activation is expected to restore cerebral glucose metabolic homeostasis and attenuate hippocampal neuronal injury and tau hyperphosphorylation. These findings provide experimental evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of EA in improving T2DM-related cognitive impairment.

Materials and methods

Ethics and animals

Male Wistar rats (8 weeks old, 190–210 g) were obtained from Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Rats were Maintained under SPF conditions with free access to standard chow And water. Environmental conditions were controlled at a 12 h light/dark cycle, 23 ± 2 °C, And 40± 5% humidity. All animal procedures adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. BUCM-2024052302–2127).

T2DM Modeling, animal grouping, and interventions

After one week of acclimatization, 12 rats were randomly assigned to the Control group and provided with standard chow and water ad libitum. The remaining rats were used to establish the T2DM model. They were fed a high-fat diet (67% standard chow, 20% sucrose, 10% lard, 2.5% cholesterol, And 0.5% sodium cholate) for eight weeks. After a 20-h fast, rats received an intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg; freshly prepared in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer, pH 4.2–4.5) to induce diabetes. Control rats received An equal volume of 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer (pH 4.2–4.5) as vehicle control.

T2DM model establishment was considered successful when rats exhibited random blood glucose (RBG) levels ≥ 16.7 mmol/L and/or fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L [29, 30]. These diabetic rats were then randomly allocated into three groups (n = 12 per group)—Model, Electroacupuncture (EA), and EA combined with Dickkopf-1 (EA + DKK1)—using a computer-generated randomization method. An additional 12 non-diabetic rats served as the Control group. During the subsequent evaluation phase and intervention period, all groups except the Control continued to receive the high-fat diet.

Acupuncture targeted five acupoints: bilateral Weiwanxiashu (EX-B3), Pishu (BL20), Zusanli (ST36), and Yinlingquan (SP9), as well as the Baihui (DU20) on the head. Sterile disposable acupuncture needles (0.17*7 mm) were employed. EX-B3 is located 7 mm lateral to the depression under the 8th thoracic spinous process. BL20 is located 7 mm lateral to the depression below the spinous process of the 11th thoracic vertebra. ST36 is located 5 mm inferior to the fibular head. SP9 is located in the depression between the medial border of the tibia and the medial head of the gastrocnemius muscle. DU20 is located on the rat’s skull near the middle of the spot where the two ear tips converge [31–34].

Acupuncture needle insertion depths were standardized across all treated animals. For EX-B3 and BL20, needles were inserted obliquely to a depth of 7 mm. ST36 and SP9 were needled perpendicularly to the same depth of 7 mm. DU20 was punctured horizontally to a depth of 5 mm. Electroacupuncture stimulation was administered by connecting ipsilateral EX-B3 and ST36, alternating the stimulation sides daily to ensure balanced intervention. Stimulation parameters were set to a dense-sparse wave pattern with a frequency of 2/50 Hz And An electrical current ranging from 1 to 3 mA. The needles remained in place for 20 min during each session, with slight muscle twitching observed to confirm appropriate stimulation intensity. Excessive struggling or signs of discomfort were carefully avoided. EA treatment was conducted once per day, six days per week, over a continuous period of nine weeks. In the EA + DKK1 group, Dickkopf-1 (DKK1), a specific inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, was administered intranasally at a dose of 2 mg/kg (prepared in saline; MCE, China) once daily for nine consecutive weeks. This group also received the identical EA regimen as described above. For consistency, both the Control and Model groups underwent the same handling, restraint, and fixation procedures as the EA-treated animals, and were provided with ad libitum access to water. Morris water maze test (MWMT)

Spatial learning and memory were evaluated using the Morris Water Maze Test (MWMT). The apparatus consisted of a circular tank (120 cm in diameter, 50 cm in height) filled with opaque water (non-toxic whitener added) and containing a hidden circular platform (20 cm in diameter) positioned 1–2 cm below the surface in the center of the target quadrant. The pool was divided into four virtual quadrants. One day before testing, all rats underwent habituation training.

Acquisition trials were conducted over five consecutive days. In each trial, rats were released from different quadrants, facing the Tank wall, and allowed a Maximum of 90 s to locate the hidden platform. Swim trajectories were recorded using An automated video-tracking system. Rats that reached the platform were allowed to remain there for 10 s, while those that failed were guided to the platform and given the same reinforcement.

On day 6, a probe trial was performed to assess memory retention. The platform was removed, And rats were allowed to swim freely for 90 s. Performance was quantified by target quadrant dwell time, number of platform crossings, and swim path trajectory [35]. All behavioral assessments were conducted by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Assessment of body weight, FBG, and insulin resistance index

Weight: Body weights were recorded both prior to And following the intervention. All measurements were taken following a 12-h overnight fast to ensure consistency and reduce variability due to recent food intake. Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG): FBG levels were assessed at three key time points: prior to T2DM model induction, immediately before the intervention phase, And at the end of the treatment period. Measurements were conducted using a Roche Accu-Chek glucometer with blood samples collected from the tail vein, following a 12-h fast for each assessment to ensure standardized metabolic conditions. Insulin Resistance (IR) Index: Fasting Serum Insulin (INS): Serum insulin concentrations were determined using a commercially available ELISA kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue preparation

Upon intervention completion, all rats underwent 12-h fasting with ad Libitum water access. Subsequent Anesthesia induction employed intraperitoneal 2% pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg) to achieve surgical anesthesia. Terminal blood collection (5–10 mL) via abdominal aorta preceded immediate serum separation through centrifugation. Aliquoted serum samples underwent cryopreservation at −80 °C. Post-phlebotomy euthanasia enabled rapid whole-brain extraction on ice-cooled platforms. Selected brain regions received 4% paraformaldehyde fixation for histological processing, followed by paraffin embedding And 4-μm sectioning for morphological/IHC analyses. Concurrently, dissected hippocampi underwent snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen for −80 °C storage, facilitating subsequent molecular/metabolic investigations.

H&E Staining

Rat hippocampal tissue samples preserved in paraffin were sliced into 4-micron sections, then heat-treated at 65 °C to ensure adhesion. The sections underwent xylene treatment for paraffin removal, followed by a graded ethanol series for rehydration. Histological staining was performed using hematoxylin (3-min immersion) with subsequent differentiation, a water rinse, and eosin counterstaining (1-min exposure). After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral gum and examined microscopically to assess hippocampal morphology. Investigators were blinded during histological analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Following deparaffinization, tissue sections underwent Antigen retrieval using citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was subsequently quenched via 25-min dark incubation with 3% H₂O₂ at ambient temperature. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Primary antibodies—GFAP (1:300), S100A10 (1:500), and C3 (1:500) in diluent—were applied for overnight incubation at 4 °C. After PBST (0.1% Tween-20) washing, species-matched HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were introduced under dark conditions. Tyramide-488 facilitated signal amplification (TSA), succeeded by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Sections were coverslipped with anti-fade medium post-final washing. Fluorescence microscopy imaging enabled subsequent analysis. Investigators were blinded during histological analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Post-deparaffinization via xylene-ethanol dehydration, tissue sections received triple 10-min PBS rinses. Antigen unmasking was achieved through 5-min heating in citrate-EDTA buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, 1 mM EDTA). Following ambient-temperature cooling, endogenous peroxidases were inhibited with 3% H₂O₂ (5 min) prior to PBS washing. Non-specific binding prevention involved 60-min room-temperature application of 5% BSA/PBS blocking solution. Primary Antibody incubation proceeded overnight at 4 °C in humidified chambers using rabbit anti-pTau(Thr217) (#51,625, CST; 1:500). Subsequent PBS washes preceded 1-h exposure to HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:100). Immunodetection employed DAB chromogen (ab64238, Abcam), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining (ab220365) and permanent mounting (ab64230, Abcam). Light microscopic examination facilitated analytical assessment. Investigators were blinded during histological analysis.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from rat hippocampal tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (A32961, Thermo Fisher, MO, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (23,227, Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per sample) were separated on 4–12% SDS-PAGE gels (NP0322BOX, Thermo Fisher) And transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with non-fat milk in 1× TBST at room temperature for 1 h And then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: mouse anti-GFAP (1:2000, #3670, CST); rabbit anti-C3 (1:1000, ab200999, Abcam); rabbit anti-S100A10 (1:1000, ab187201, Abcam); rabbit anti-IL-15 (1:1000, ab273625, Abcam); rabbit anti-IL-33 (1:500, ab187060, Abcam); rabbit anti-Wnt3a (1:1000, ab28472, Abcam); rabbit anti-non-phosphorylated β-catenin (np-β-catenin, non-phospho S37/T41, 1:1000, ab246504, Abcam); rabbit anti-β-catenin (1:1000, #8480, CST); rabbit anti-phosphorylated Glycogen Synthase Kinase-3 beta (p-GSK-3β, phospho-Ser9, 1:1000, ab75814, Abcam); rabbit anti-GSK3β (1:1000, #12,456, CST); rabbit Anti-Neurogenin 2 (Ngn2, 1:500, #13,144, CST); rabbit anti-Glut1 (1:1000, ab115730, Abcam); rabbit anti-Glut3 (1:1000, #40,538, CST); rabbit anti-phospho-Tau (Thr217, 1:1000, #51,625, CST); rabbit anti-Tau (1:1000, ab308439, Abcam); mouse anti-β-actin (1:5000, MA1-140, Thermo Fisher). After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated for 2 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:5000, 65–6120, Thermo Fisher) or goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:5000, A16078, Thermo Fisher). Protein bands were visualized using an ECL Plus detection kit (32,134, Thermo Fisher).

ELISA

Concentrations of INS, ADP and AMP in hippocampal samples was measured using specific rat ELISA kits (Jingmei, Co., Ltd. JiangSu, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical densities were measured at the appropriate wavelength using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated for each analyte.

Determination of ATP, glycogen and lactic acid

Lactic Acid Assay Kit (Biobase, Cat# 10521060HT) and ATP Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Cat# A095-1–1) were purchased. Hippocampal tissues were processed following the manufacturer’s instructions, and reagents were prepared. After setting the corresponding parameters on a fully automated biochemistry analyzer, samples were loaded to automatically determine lactic acid and glycogen content. Alternatively, absorbance was measured using a microplate reader to assess ATP levels.

Targeted metabolomics analysis of central carbon metabolism

Each hippocampal specimen (approximately 10 mg) underwent mechanical homogenization with grinding beads in a chilled environment (−10 °C, 50 Hz, 6 min) using a freeze grinder. The homogenization solution contained 280 μL acetonitrile, 100 μL 50% methanol, And 20 μL isotope-labeled internal standards. Following centrifugation (14,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), 100 μL of clarified supernatant was aliquoted into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. The derivatization protocol involved sequential addition of 25 μL 200 mM 3-NPH·HCl And 25 μL 120 mM EDC·HCl (supplemented with 6% pyridine), followed by thorough vortex mixing And thermal incubation at 60 °C for 40 min. Post-derivatization centrifugation preceded final transfer of processed samples to analytical vials for LC–MS/MS characterization.

Analytical separation employed an ExionLC AD system equipped with a Waters HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm) Maintained at 40 °C with 0.4 mL/min flow rate. The mobile phase comprised two components: aqueous phase containing 0.03% formic acid (A) And organic phase consisting of 0.03% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). A 22-min gradient program was implemented: initial 2 min at 1% B, Linear increase to 22% B from 2–8 min, isocratic Maintenance at 22% B until 12 min, followed by gradient elevation to 40% B between 12–13 min.

13.0–17.0 min: 40–65% B; 17.0–19.0 min: 65% B; 19.0–20.0 min: 65–100% B;

20.0–21.0 min: 100% B; 21.0–21.01 min: 100–1% B; 21.01–22.0 min: 1% B.

Throughout the analytical process, all specimens were Maintained at 4 °C.

Analyses were performed using a QTRAP® 6500 + system (Sciex, USA) equipped with An electrospray ionization source operating in dual polarity mode. Key operational settings included a source temperature of 550 °C, curtain gas pressure at 55 psi, both ion source gases set to 55 psi, collision gas at medium level, and ion spray voltage fixed at ± 4500 V.

Quality control (QC) samples were injected every 6 runs to assess system stability. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of detected metabolites in QC samples was Maintained below 15%.

Raw data were processed using Sciex OS software. Ion peaks were automatically identified and integrated using default settings, followed by manual verification. Metabolite concentrations were quantified via linear regression based on standard curves.

Statistical methods

Statistical procedures were executed utilizing SPSS statistical package version 27.0. Experimental data were expressed as mean values accompanied by the standard error of the mean (SEM). Prior to statistical testing, all datasets were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. For normally distributed data with equal variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. In cases where the data followed a normal distribution but violated the assumption of homogeneity of variance, Dunnett’s T3 post-hoc test with adjusted P-values was applied. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all reported P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

Results

EA reduces body weight, blood glucose levels, and insulin resistance index in T2DM rats

Once the model was successfully established, rats in the Model, EA, and EA + DKK1 groups showed a marked increase in body weight and FBG levels relative to the Control group, affirming the effective induction of the T2DM model. After nine weeks of EA treatment, the EA group experienced notable decreases in both body weight and FBG levels when compared to the Model and EA + DKK1 groups (Fig. 1B-C).

Figure 1D and E show INS and HOMA-IR levels across groups. The Model group showed significantly elevated INS and HOMA-IR values compared to the Control group. In contrast, EA treatment significantly lowered INS and HOMA-IR levels compared to the Model group. However, these improvements were reversed in the EA + DKK1 group, where both INS and HOMA-IR were significantly higher than those in the EA group.

EA enhances cognitive abilities and reduces hippocampal neuron injury in T2DM rats

The MWMT was used to assess spatial learning and memory after EA treatment. During the five-day acquisition phase, the escape latency of the Model group was significantly longer than Control group, indicating impaired spatial learning. EA treatment markedly shortened escape latency, suggesting improved learning ability. However, no significant improvement in the EA + DKK1 group (Fig. 1F).

On day 6, probe testing showed EA group rats spent longer in the target quadrant and crossed the platform more frequently than the Model group. Additionally, the EA group showed a trend toward faster swimming speed and significantly shorter latency to the first platform crossing (Fig. 1G-M). Representative swim path trajectories showed that rats in the Control and EA groups primarily navigated around the original platform location, while trajectories in the Model and EA + DKK1 groups appeared more random (Fig. 1L).

H&E staining of hippocampal sections further supported these findings. In the Control group, the hippocampal architecture was intact, with well-organized, tightly arranged neurons exhibiting round nuclei and abundant cytoplasm. No necrosis, degeneration, edema, or inflammation was observed. In contrast, the Model group showed significant structural abnormalities in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions, including shrunken and pyknotic neuronal nuclei and deformed neurons (indicated by black arrows). The EA group exhibited largely preserved hippocampal architecture, comparable to the Control group. However, the EA + DKK1 group again showed marked neuronal damage similar to the Model group, with visible nuclear shrinkage and neuronal atrophy (Fig. 1N).

EA decreases A1 and elevates A2 astrocytes in the hippocampus of T2DM rats

Reactive astrocyte polarization is primarily classified into two phenotypes: A1 and A2 [36]. Studies have shown that reactive astrocytes are a specific or major source of IL-15 and IL-33 in the CNS. IL-15 is considered a neurotoxic factor derived from A1-type astrocytes, whereas IL-33 is recognized as a neuroprotective cytokine associated with the A2 phenotype [21]. In this study, we used Western blotting and qPCR to assess the effects of EA on reactive astrocytes and to evaluate the expression levels of GFAP, C3, S100A10, IL-15, and IL-33.

The Model group showed a pronounced upregulation in GFAP protein expression—a standard indicator of astrocyte activation—along with elevated levels of C3 (an A1-type astrocyte marker) and IL-15. In contrast, the expression of S100A10, an A2-type marker, and its related cytokine IL-33 was notably suppressed. The same trend was observed in gene expression, where GFAP, C3, and IL-15 mRNA levels were markedly elevated in the Model group compared to Control. Conversely, S100A10 and IL-33 showed a pronounced decrease in transcriptional activity.

EA intervention significantly downregulated the expression of GFAP, C3, and IL-15, while upregulating S100A10 and IL-33 levels, compared with the Model group. These changes were also consistent at the mRNA level, with all differences reaching statistical significance. To further investigate astrocyte subtypes in the hippocampus, we performed immunofluorescence staining to identify A1-type (GFAP/C3-positive) and A2-type (GFAP/S100A10-positive) cells. The Model group exhibited a marked increase in GFAP/C3-positive astrocytes alongside a reduction in GFAP/S100A10-positive cells relative to Control. Discussion

In this research, we explored how EA can shield the brain from damage and cognitive decline in a rat model of T2DM. Our findings revealed that EA successfully stimulates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, influences astrocyte polarization, and maintains energy balance within the hippocampus. These changes ultimately lead to reduced neuronal harm and better cognitive performance. The study offers fresh perspectives on how EA might help mitigate cognitive impairments linked to T2DM.

Emerging studies indicate that cognitive impairment often manifests during the initial phases of diabetes and worsens with metabolic syndrome. Disruptions in metabolic function—particularly elevated blood sugar levels and impaired insulin response—play a key role in triggering Alzheimer’s-like neurological alterations in individuals with type 2 diabetes [1, 37, 38]. Chronic hyperglycemia induces astrocyte activation in the brain, and reactive astrocytes are known to contribute to both cognitive decline and metabolic dysfunction. Elevated expression of GFAP, a marker of astrocyte proliferation and activation, is a hallmark of neuroinflammation in neurodegenerative disorders [39, 40].

Astrocyte activation primarily results in two distinct phenotypes: A1 and A2 [5]. A1 astrocytes lose the capacity to support neuronal survival, synaptogenesis, and phagocytic activity, and actively induce neuronal and oligodendrocyte death, exerting neurotoxic effects. In contrast, A2 astrocytes secrete various neurotrophic factors, suppress neuroinflammation, and promote synapse formation, thus playing a neuroprotective role [36, 41]. C3 and S100A10 serve as specific markers for A1 and A2 phenotypes, respectively. A1 astrocytes activate the classical complement cascade via C3 expression, disrupt synaptic connectivity, and release chemokines such as CXCL10 and cytokines like IL-15, promoting immune infiltration and driving microglial polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. This cascade exacerbates neuroinflammation, triggers metabolic imbalance, and directly activates the neuronal NF-κB pathway, inducing apoptotic gene expression [21, 42, 43]. In contrast, A2 astrocytes maintain energy balance and neuroprotection by expressing S100A10 and neurotrophic factors that regulate glycolysis and support neuronal repair [36, 44]. Targeting A1/A2 polarization balance has thus been proposed as a promising strategy for neurodegenerative disease treatment [45].

In our study, T2DM rats exhibited impaired learning and memory in the MWMT, along with hippocampal histopathological damage. These rats also showed increased GFAP, C3, and IL-15 expression and decreased S100A10 and IL-33, indicating a shift toward the neurotoxic A1 phenotype and a reduction in protective A2 astrocytes. Neuronal damage was observed across hippocampal subregions. EA treatment reversed these changes: EA shortened escape latency, improved spatial navigation, reduced A1-associated markers, and increased A2-related markers. Moreover, neuronal injury was alleviated, indicating that EA enhances cognitive performance by suppressing A1 astrocyte activation, promoting A2 polarization, and reducing hippocampal inflammation. These effects were abolished by DKK1, an inhibitor of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, suggesting that EA exerts its regulatory effect on astrocyte polarization via this pathway. However, we also recognize that the current evidence linking astrocytic polarization to improvements in cognitive function remains indirect. Although the EA-induced decrease in A1 markers and increase in A2 markers were consistent with behavioral and metabolic improvements, these associations alone cannot fully establish causality. In addition, part of EA’s effects may be mediated by other glial cells or neurons. For example, activated microglia can release factors that induce neurotoxic A1 astrocytes [36]; Wnt/β-catenin signaling not only regulates astrocytes but also affects microglia and neurons [21]; and previous studies have shown that EA promotes M2-like microglial polarization and attenuates neuroinflammation [26]. Therefore, the therapeutic effects of EA are likely the result of coordinated actions among multiple glial cell types and neurons, rather than being solely dependent on astrocytes.

Under physiological conditions, astrocytes primarily express Glut1 to mediate glucose uptake and respond to neuronal energy demands, while neurons rely on Glut3 for glucose utilization [46, 47]. Hyperglycemia disrupts glucose transport and metabolism in both cell types, leading to energy imbalance. In our study, Glut1 and Glut3 expression was significantly reduced in T2DM rats, accompanied by elevated levels of mevalonic acid, itaconic acid, 2-ketoglutaric acid, gluconic acid, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, ethylmalonic acid, galactose 1-phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate, glucose 1- And 6-phosphate, fructose, glucose, lactate, glycogen, ADP, And AMP, And decreased levels of phosphoryl choline, nicotinic acid, citric acid, isocitric acid, lactose, L-carnitine, and ATP. The accumulation of phosphorylated glucose metabolites, such as fructose 6-phosphate and glucose-6-phosphate, suggests glycolytic blockage and impaired entry into the TCA cycle [48]. Gluconic acid, produced via glucose oxidation, is a marker of oxidative stress [49]. Itaconic acid, 2-ketoglutaric acid, and ADP/AMP accumulation, along with reduced ATP and TCA cycle intermediates, point toward mitochondrial dysfunction and reversed TCA flux, impairing energy production [50]. These energy deficits may hinder tau clearance and promote tau tangle accumulation, further worsening glucose metabolism [51, 52]. Accordingly, we observed hippocampal tau hyperphosphorylation in T2DM rats, closely associated with metabolic disruption.

Following EA intervention, Glut1 and Glut3 expression increased, and levels of ATP, phosphoryl choline, and L-carnitine were elevated, while glycolytic intermediates and stress markers (e.g., gluconic acid) decreased. This suggests enhanced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and improved glucose metabolism. EA also alleviated tau hyperphosphorylation. Nonetheless, suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade resulted in the absence of those effects, establishing the role of this pathway in mediating EA’s metabolic advantages. The metabolomic analysis in this study revealed that several glucose-related metabolites were restored by EA in a Wnt/β-catenin–dependent manner. These metabolic changes occurred concurrently with the shift in astrocytic polarization, raising a critical question: are these metabolite alterations a direct consequence of astrocytic repolarization, or parallel effects driven by Wnt/β-catenin activation? Given the pivotal role of astrocytes in glucose metabolism and in supporting neuronal energy demands [53], the normalization of metabolites such as gluconic acid is most likely associated with astrocytic repolarization and redistribution of metabolic substrates, rather than representing independent effects of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Nevertheless, the present study cannot definitively establish causality. Future investigations integrating cell-type–resolved metabolomics with astrocyte-specific manipulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway will be essential to further validate this mechanistic link.

Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been demonstrated to reduce tau hyperphosphorylation, activate brain insulin signaling, improve energy metabolism, and alleviate cognitive impairment [18, 54]. The Wnt signaling cascade is initiated when Wnt proteins bind to Frizzled receptors and LRP coreceptors, setting off a molecular domino effect. This interaction prompts the phosphorylation of Dishevelled (Dvl) and disrupts the β-catenin degradation machinery—comprising CK1α, GSK-3β, Axin, and APC—effectively putting the brakes on β-catenin breakdown. As a result, β-catenin accumulates and shuttles into the nucleus, where it teams up with TCF/LEF transcription factors to orchestrate the expression of target genes [55]. Wnt3a, an endogenous glycoprotein, promotes pathway activation by inhibiting GSK-3β and stabilizing β-catenin, thereby promoting A2 astrocyte polarization and suppressing A1 polarization [21]. Wnt/β-catenin activation also induces Ngn2, which inhibits astrocyte differentiation by suppressing GFAP transcription [56].

Our results showed suppressed Wnt3a and Ngn2 expression, increased β-catenin phosphorylation, and decreased GSK-3β phosphorylation in T2DM rats, indicating pathway inhibition. EA reversed these changes, activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, enhancing A2 astrocyte polarization, suppressing A1 polarization, and improving metabolic status. Moreover, EA restored Glut1 and Glut3 expression, reduced hippocampal glucose accumulation, and improved glycolytic efficiency—effects blocked by DKK1. Thus, EA may improve glucose uptake and metabolism by promoting Glut1/Glut3 expression via Wnt/β-catenin activation [57–59].

These findings were further validated by intranasal administration of DKK1 in T2DM rats. In the EA + DKK1 group, the expression of Wnt3a, Ngn2, Glut1, and Glut3, as well as the phosphorylation status of β-catenin and GSK-3β in the hippocampus, closely resembled those observed in the Model group. This was accompanied by an increased number of A1-type astrocytes and exacerbated metabolic disturbances. The findings affirm that EA’s influence on astrocyte differentiation and energy homeostasis hinges on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway’s activation. Although DKK1 is widely recognized as a classical antagonist of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, acting primarily through competitive binding to LRP5/6 receptors, its effects are not strictly confined to this pathway. Previous studies have shown that DKK1 may also influence BMP and Notch signaling in certain cellular contexts [60, 61]. In the present study, we observed that DKK1 reversed EA-induced β-catenin activation, Ngn2 expression, and astrocytic polarization, suggesting that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is an important mediator of EA’s effects. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the possibility of off-target actions of DKK1 cannot be completely excluded.

Based on our findings, EA may provide clinically relevant guidance for treating cognitive impairment in T2DM. The acupoints used in this study, such as GV20, ST36, and BL20, have well-defined human homologues and are widely applied for improving cognition and regulating metabolism. Low-frequency or mixed-frequency stimulation (2–10 Hz or 2/100 Hz), delivered for 20–30 min per session, three to five times weekly for 8–12 weeks, may serve as a reasonable starting protocol, with parameters individualized according to patient tolerance. Patients with T2DM and mild cognitive impairment, stable glycemic control, and without severe neuropathy or vascular complications may represent the most suitable candidates, while safety precautions are essential for those with peripheral neuropathy, vascular disease, or impaired wound healing. EA should be implemented as an adjunctive therapy alongside standard diabetes management, including pharmacological glucose control, lifestyle interventions, and cognitive rehabilitation, rather than as a stand-alone treatment. Future clinical trials should further optimize stimulation parameters, define patient selection criteria, and incorporate both cognitive scales and metabolic biomarkers to validate the efficacy, durability, and safety of EA in diabetic cognitive impairment.

Limitations

Although this study systematically explored the mechanisms by which EA regulates astrocytic polarization and energy metabolism in the hippocampus of T2DM rats via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, astrocyte-specific functional validation is still lacking. The polarization state of astrocytes was inferred based on GFAP/C3 and GFAP/S100A10 double labeling and related factor expression analysis, but astrocyte-specific conditional knockout models or isolated in vitro astrocyte cultures were not employed to directly verify functional alterations. While these markers are widely used in the field to identify astrocytic identity and polarization status—GFAP as a classical astrocyte marker, C3 as an indicator of neurotoxic A1 astrocytes, and S100A10 as a marker of the neuroprotective A2 phenotype—they are not entirely specific. For example, GFAP can be upregulated in certain reactive glial states, and C3 can also be expressed in microglia. Thus, without astrocyte-specific approaches such as conditional knockouts or cell-type–resolved metabolomics, contributions from other glial cells (e.g., microglia or oligodendrocytes) cannot be fully excluded.

In addition, the causal relationship of energy metabolism dysregulation remains unclear. Although our findings suggest a close association between astrocytic polarization and metabolic disturbances in T2DM rats, the precise metabolic alterations within glial cells and their direct causal link to neuronal injury were not determined using methods such as metabolic flux analysis or single-cell metabolomics.

Furthermore, the present study focused on reversible molecular changes at the early stage of diabetes-related cognitive impairment, with primary emphasis on tau phosphorylation, a process closely related to microtubule stability and neuronal plasticity. However, assessing phosphorylation alone cannot fully capture the spectrum of tau pathology. Critical downstream processes, including tau oligomer or neurofibrillary tangle aggregation, as well as neurodegenerative changes such as loss of NeuN, reduced synaptic proteins, and axonal degeneration, were not evaluated. These limitations indeed constrain a comprehensive interpretation of functional relevance. We acknowledge these shortcomings and will address them in future studies.

Conclusion

Electroacupuncture alleviates cognitive impairment in T2DM rats by modulating astrocyte polarization via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. This modulation restores hippocampal energy metabolism, reduces tau hyperphosphorylation and neuronal injury, and ultimately improves hippocampal function.
