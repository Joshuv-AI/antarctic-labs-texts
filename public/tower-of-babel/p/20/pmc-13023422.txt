Chrysoeriol-Mediated Neuroprotection in Parkinson’s Disease in Mice: Targeting Apoptosis, α-Synuclein Accumulation, and Functional Recovery
Authors: Mehak Ali, Mehwish Mehreen, Saima Batool, Shahrukh Khan, Aneeqa Noor, Sara Mumtaz, Saima Zafar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder marked by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, leading to significant motor dysfunction. Current treatments stabilize dopamine levels but fail to address underlying neuronal apoptosis, highlighting the need for novel approaches. Although chrysoeriol, a 3’-O-methoxy flavone and luteolin derivative, is well-documented for its anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, its neuroprotective potential in PD, particularly in vivo, remains largely unexplored. This study fills a critical gap by being the first to systematically assess chrysoeriol’s neuroprotective effects in a PD mouse model. We evaluated the effects of 5 mg/kg chrysoeriol administered intraperitoneally (IP) for 14 days in an acute 1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD model. Behavioral tests showed notable recovery, as chrysoeriol eliminated deficits in motor function, coordination, and balance, as assessed by the pole test, forced swim test, and tail suspension test. It also mitigated exploratory and locomotor deficits in the open field test, and the Y-maze test revealed improved spatial and learning memory. Hematoxylin and eosin staining indicated a significant reduction in neuronal damage across key brain regions. qPCR analysis showed reduced 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+)-induced toxicity, downregulation of α-synuclein, and an improved Bcl-2/Bax ratio. These findings suggest chrysoeriol may protect against MPP+-induced apoptosis in mice, potentially via the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, and reduces mitochondrial damage by downregulating α-synuclein.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common progressive neurodegenerative condition with an annual global incidence of around 18 per 100 000 people. It affects around 0.3% of the general population, 1.0% of individuals over 60 years old, and 3.0% of those over 80 years old. PD incidence is approximately 1.3-2.0 times higher in males compared to females, although regional variations exist [1]. As the global population ages, the prevalence of PD is expected to double in the next two decades. PD is characterized by the disintegration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain [2]. Pathological changes also include the formation of neuronal Lewy bodies that also affect the spinal cord and peripheral nervous system. It is clinically identified by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, resting tremor, stiffness, and postural instability, while non-motor symptoms include cognitive decline, mood disorders, and autonomic dysfunction [3]. However, distinguishing PD from other parkinsonian syndromes such as multiple system atrophy (MSA), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration (CBD), and drug-induced parkinsonism can be challenging in early stages due to overlapping clinical features [3]. Neuroimaging techniques, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET), are used in research and clinical settings to assess structural atrophy, dopaminergic terminal integrity, and dopamine transporter availability. Research indicates that PET imaging using radioligands can detect presynaptic dopaminergic deficits, while advanced MRI techniques may reveal connectivity and volumetric changes associated with disease progression. Together, these methods provide insights into the molecular and neurochemical changes associated with PD [4].

PD risk factors encompass aging, environmental exposures (eg, rural residency), traumatic head injuries, dairy consumption [1], and clinical conditions like cancer, diabetes, and postmenopausal hormone use [1]. Protective factors include alcohol consumption, smoking, and specific medications [1,3]. Genetically, mutations in SNCA, PRKN, PINK1, and DJ-1 contribute to 30% of monogenic and 3-5% of complex PD cases [3]. While PD remains incurable, pharmacologic therapies manage symptoms: carbidopa/levodopa, pramipexole, and ropinirole target motor impairments, often combined with dopamine agonists, MAO-B/COMT inhibitors, antidepressants (amitriptyline and fluoxetine), and benzodiazepines for non-motor symptoms. Surgical interventions like neural implantation and emerging approaches—genetic therapies, neurotrophic factors (eg, GM1 ganglioside), and deep brain stimulation (DBS) show therapeutic promise. DBS, though mechanistically unclear, modulates aberrant neural signaling in select patients.

Current PD therapies predominantly manage symptoms rather than targeting disease progression, necessitating novel therapeutic strategies. Flavonoids, frequently studied as a therapeutic intervention, are polyphenolic compounds with anti-inflammatory, antioxidative and anti-apoptotic properties that exhibit broad therapeutic potential for neurodegenerative disorders. Previous research has shown that various flavonoids such as Luteolin and Quercetin exhibit neuroprotective effects in different PD models by suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress and mitigating dopaminergic neuronal loss [5,6]. Chrysoeriol, a luteolin-derived 3’-O-methoxy flavone, demonstrates multifaceted pharmacological activities, including anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects [7]. Its neuroprotective action involves restoring Bcl-2/Bax balance to inhibit dopaminergic neuron apoptosis, activating the PI3K/Akt pro-survival pathway, modulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and enhancing neurogenesis through elevated nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels, as evidenced in stroke models [7,8]. Unlike dopamine replacement therapies, chrysoeriol addresses neurodegeneration at molecular and cellular levels, offering potential disease-modifying benefits. Research has shown that it acts on 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+), a neurotoxin associated with the advancement of PD, reversing mitochondrial damage and reducing α-synuclein formation induced by MPP+ [8]. Hence chrysoeriol presents an intriguing possibility in treating PD as highlighted in previous studies related to potential of flavonoids in PD treatment [9-12]. However, despite extensive research, the impact of chrysoeriol on PD mouse models remains largely unknown.

This study aims to determine if chrysoeriol can effectively mitigate MPTP-induced neurotoxicity in a mouse PD model. Specifically, it seeks to assess whether chrysoeriol can reduce brain tissue damage, improve motor and cognitive function, reverse MPP+-induced mitochondrial damage, decrease α-synuclein formation, and regulate the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, pertinent to PD.

Materials and Methods

Model Establishment: Animals, Drugs, and Chemicals

The behavioral test, drug administration, and euthanasia in this study were approved by the NUST-IRB committee (IRB No: 2024-IRB-A-01/01). Adult male BALB/c mice (n = 18) weighing between 25-30 g were provided by the National Institutes of Health, Islamabad, the mice were housed in the ASAB animal facility at a constant temperature (25 ± 2 °C), natural light, and dark cycles ranging between 14 h light and 10 h dark, with free access to specific pathogen-free-grade water and food. The mice were categorized into three groups, with six mice (n = 6) in each group: the control group, the diseased group administered with MPTP, and the treatment group administered with 1% DMSO dissolved 5 mg/kg chrysoeriol. MPTP (Sigma Macklin, China, CAS: 23007-85-4) was used to induce Parkinson’s-like symptoms, while chrysoeriol (Shaanxi Dideu Medichem Co., CAS: 491-71-4, 98% purity) was administered intraperitoneally (IP) as a therapeutic agent.

Induction of the PD model

MPTP was administered to the mice IP after a one-week acclimatization period. One day prior to administration, the mice were weighed, numbered, and grouped to ensure effective and efficient administration of MPTP. The control group received intraperitoneal injections of saline (0.9% NaCl) equivalent in volume to MPTP (10 μl/g body weight), while the disease and treatment groups received MPTP. The injection dosage was established at approximately 10 μl per gram of body weight (equivalent to 0.01 ml for 1 gram) and administered accordingly. Both the disease and treatment groups received intraperitoneal injections of MPTP (20 mg/kg) at 2-hour intervals for a total of four times [13, 14]. To ensure that MPTP-induced damage persisted throughout the study, we optimized the disease induction protocol. Following MPTP administration, behavioral tests were conducted to confirm the retention of acute injuries, and we conducted behavioral tests at different time points throughout the study to monitor the ongoing effects of MPTP.

Chrysoeriol Treatment Protocol

Chrysoeriol (5 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) was administered to MPTP-treated mice for 14 days starting with 7 days post-MPTP, with doses adjusted to individual weights. Transient adverse effects in the treatment group prompted real-time protocol refinements to reduce toxicity without compromising efficacy. Control groups received no pharmacological interventions to isolate MPTP-specific neurotoxicity and exclude confounding factors. Therapeutic efficacy was validated behaviorally and histologically [15,16].

In silico Analysis

In silico analysis preceded experiments to predict chrysoeriol interactions. BAX, BCL-2, and α-Synuclein structures (RCSB PDB) and chrysoeriol (PubChem) were cleaned (Discovery Studio 3.0). Docking (PyRx 0.8) evaluated chrysoeriol’s binding affinity and selectivity to these targets. PyMOL 2.5.8 and Discovery Studio 3.0 visualized key molecular interactions

Behavioral Assessments

Behavioral assessments were conducted at three timepoints: pre-treatment (7 days post-MPTP), interim (7 days into chrysoeriol treatment for dose optimization), and post-treatment (24 hours after final dose, day 14). To minimize stress, tests (Open Field Test (motor activity), Pole Test (coordination), Y-Maze (spatial memory), Forced Swim Test, and Tail Suspension Test (depressive behaviors)) were administered sequentially with 24-hour intervals, progressing from least to more invasive. Six mice per group were used under randomized, blinded protocols, with 2-3 independent observers scoring outcomes to reduce bias. This structured approach enabled systematic evaluation of MPTP-induced Parkinsonian deficits and chrysoeriol’s therapeutic effects, adhering to established methodologies [17-22].

Dissection and Histological Sample Preparation

Following the behavioral tests (conducted 24 hours after the final chrysoeriol injection for tissue harvest), mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg in sterile saline; 100 mg/ml solution) [23,24] and euthanized. Tissue fixation began with transcardial perfusion using 100 ml saline (0.9% NaCl) followed by 80 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to flush residual blood and ensure uniform fixation. The brain was carefully extracted after decapitation and bisection of the skull, rinsed in PBS (A9162.0100, Avantor, USA) to remove residual fixative and blood, and post-fixed in 4% PFA (E-IR-R114, Elabscience, USA) for 24 hours.

Histopathological Assessment

Brain sections (5 μm thick) were sagittally sliced, H&E-stained following standard protocols [25], and analyzed across PD-relevant regions: midbrain (substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons), cortex (pyramidal neurons), hippocampus (dentate gyrus, CA1/CA3), and cerebellum (Purkinje cells). Three sections per brain (200 µm apart; bregma: −1.34 to −2.30 mm) were imaged using an OPTIKA B-150 microscope (10x/40x) with Optika Vision Lite 2.1 software. ImageJ (v1.53) quantified cellular morphology, tissue architecture, and neuronal integrity, with blinded group allocation during analysis. This standardized, region-specific approach enabled systematic evaluation of chrysoeriol’s neuroprotective effects in PD [26].

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Mice were deeply anesthetized with ketamine IP and decapitated. Brains were excised via midline skull incision (fine scissors/scalpel), gently extracted with forceps, snap-frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80°C. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Fine Biotech Life Sciences, FTR-100), with quality verified by A260/A280 ratios (1.8-2.0) via Colibri NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Titertek Berthold). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA using RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher, EP0441). Primers for BAX, α-synuclein, and BCL-2,selected for their PD relevance, were designed using literature [27] and validated via NCBI Primer-BLAST, with annealing temperatures of 61.5°C (BAX), 64.8°C (α-synuclein), and 64.8°C (BCL-2). PCR products were analyzed by gel electrophoresis.

Quantitative PCR

Real-time PCR was performed using the CFX96 Touch™ System (BIO-RAD) with WizPure™ qPCR Master Mix (Wizbio Solutions, W1711) in 20 µL reactions containing 4 µL master mix, 1 µL each of gene-specific primers, and 1 µL cDNA. Thermal cycling included initial denaturation at 95°C (3 min), 35 cycles of 95°C (30 s), annealing at 61.5°C (BAX) or 64.8°C (BCL-2/α-synuclein; 30 s), and extension at 72°C (30 s), followed by a final extension at 72°C (7 min). Gene expression was normalized to β-actin (ΔCt method) using the following primers: β-actin (F: CATCCCCCAAAGATTCTAC, R: CAAAGCCTTCATACATC; reference), BAX (113 bp; F: TGCTAGCAAACTGGTGCTCA, R: CTTGGATCCAGACAAGCAGC), BCL-2 (206 bp; F: TTCTTTGAGTTCGGTGGGGTC, R: TCAGAGACAGCCAGGAGAAATC), and α-synuclein (104 bp; F: CACTGGCTTTGTCAAGAAGGACC, R: CATAAGCCTCACTGCCAGGATC).

Statistical Analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, normality assessment was conducted on all data sets. Comparative analyses among the control, MPTP-treated, and chrysoeriol-treated groups were performed using statistical tests including the T-test and one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s test for post hoc comparisons. Graphs were constructed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. The data and results were expressed with the standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Enhanced Motor Function, Coordination, and Balance Post-Chrysoeriol Administration

Chrysoeriol administration improved motor function, coordination, and depressive-like behavior in PD mice, as assessed via tail suspension, forced swim, and pole tests. In forced swim and tail suspension tests (Figure 1A-B), the control group showed shorter floating and immobility times (normal motor function), while the diseased group exhibited prolonged durations (motor impairment and depressive-like behavior (p<0.0001 in control vs disease assessment for forced swim test; p<0.01 in control vs disease assessment for tail suspension test). Chrysoeriol-treated mice displayed intermediate values closer to controls, indicating functional recovery (p<0.05 in control vs treatment assessment and p<0.001 in disease vs treated evaluation for forced swim test; p values nonsignificant in control vs treated and p<0.01 in disease vs treated for tail suspension test). The pole test (Figure 1C) revealed shorter turn times in controls (optimal balance and coordination) versus prolonged times in diseased mice (motor deficits) (p<0.0001 in control vs disease and disease vs treatment evaluations); treated mice approached control-level performance (p<0.01 in control vs treated group). These results suggest chrysoeriol mitigates PD-associated motor, balance, and coordination impairments (Statistical data is provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1).

Elevated Exploratory and Locomotor Behaviors, Decreased Thigmotaxis

The control group showed a higher average number of entries into the central region (Figure 1E) compared to the diseased (p<0.05) and treatment groups (non-significant). In contrast, it has a lower average number of entries into the periphery (p<0.0001 in control compared to disease group and p<0.05 in control compared to treated group) (Figure 1D), suggesting a more focused and centralized exploratory pattern. Conversely, the diseased group exhibited reduced central exploration and heightened thigmotaxis, indicating potentially diminished exploratory behavior following PD. The chrysoeriol-treated group’s exploratory pattern mirrored that of the control group, highlighting the potential of chrysoeriol to mitigate the exploratory and locomotor activity deficits associated with PD (p<0.05 for central exploration and p<0.01 for peripheral exploration in disease vs treated groups) (Statistical data is provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1).

Elevated Spatial and Learning Memory and Exploratory Behaviors

The control group showed a higher percentage of alterations than the diseased group (p<0.01) (Figure 1F), indicating stronger spontaneous alterations and robust spatial working memory. The chrysoeriol-treated group demonstrated a significant increase in alterations compared to the diseased group (p<0.01), with levels like those of the control group (non-significant), suggesting chrysoeriol potential role in enhancing the spatial working memory by improving the ability to switch between maze arms (Statistical data is provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1).

Neuroprotection by Chrysoeriol Against MPTP-Induced Neurodegeneration

Control cortex displays viable neurons with round cells and visible nuclei (Figure 2A), contrasting with the diseased group’s marked neuronal shrinkage and degenerated clusters (p<0.001) (Figure 2B). Chrysoeriol treatment partially mitigates cortical degeneration, showing reduced shrinkage compared to diseased mice (p<0.05) (Figure 2C). Midbrain analysis (substantia nigra region) reveals healthy pyramidal cells in controls (Figure 2D), versus deformed neurons and tissue voids in the diseased group (p<0.05) (Figure 2E), while treatment restores neuronal integrity (non-significant) (Figure 2F). Hippocampal DG areas in diseased mice exhibit neuronal atrophy (Figure 2H) absent in controls (p<0.001) (Figure 2G), with chrysoeriol normalizing cellular morphology toward control-like states (p<0.01 in disease vs treatment and p<0.04 in control vs treatment group) (Figure 2I). Cerebellar Purkinje layers uniform in controls (Figure 2J) display disorganization and cell loss in disease (p<0.05) (Figure 2K), whereas treatment restores neuronal alignment and reduces degeneration (p value was non-significant in control vs treated and disease vs treated group) (Figure 2L) (Statistical data is provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1). These findings demonstrate chrysoeriol’s neuroprotective efficacy across PD-relevant brain regions, corroborating its therapeutic potential against MPTP-induced histopathology [28-31].

Binding Affinities and Potential Binding Sites of Chrysoeriol with BAX, BCL-2, and α-Synuclein

Molecular docking was performed to assess the potential targets of chrysoeriol (Figure 3A). In addition, BAX (Figure 3B), BCL-2 (Figure 3C), and α-Synuclein (Figure 3D) were selected based on their relevance to PD pathology. In the context of molecular docking studies, the binding affinity scores serve as numerical indicators of the strength of the interaction between the ligand (chrysoeriol) and the target proteins BAX, BCL-2, and α-Synuclein. The most negative binding indicates a strong, stable, and energetically favorable interaction between ligand and macromolecules. As depicted in the graph (Figure 3E), chrysoeriol has the most favorable interaction with BCL-2, given that it has an average binding energy of -6.5, followed by BAX, which shows a binding energy of -6.3. α-Synuclein, however, exhibits a lower binding energy of -4.5 compared to others; thus, we can assume that a slightly less energetically stable ligand-receptor complex will form for α-Synuclein. The assessment of chrysoeriol as a ligand for BAX, α-Synuclein, and BCL-2 aims to unravel the stability of the ligand-protein complexes and their potential implications for neuroprotection.

The interaction patterns of chrysoeriol with targeted proteins BAX, BCL-2, and α-Synuclein were visualized using Discovery Studio Visualizer 3.0, emphasizing key molecular interactions. The α-Synuclein’s Val 74 forms alkyl interactions with chrysoeriol, and residues ALAA 78, THRA 75, VALA 70, and GLYA 73 engage in Van der Waals interactions. In BAX, residues ALA 96, LEU 26, LEU 113, VAL 110, PHE 143, and LEU 144 contribute to alkyl and pi-alkyl interactions, with LEUA 214 forming a Pi- Sigma, THR 140 forming pi-sigma interactions and TRP 139 and TRP 158 contributing carbon-hydrogen and pi-hydrogen interactions. BCL-2 interactions include residues ALA 96, LEU 182, LEU 134, ALA 146, and VAL 131 with alkyl and pi-alkyl interactions; MET 112 with Pi-Sulfur; and TRP 211 with Pi-Pi T-shaped interactions. These interactions showcase the diverse forces guiding chrysoeriol’s binding to these proteins. This in silico analysis provided valuable insights into potential molecular interactions, helping to guide experimental design and hypothesis formulation before the physical experimentation.

Optimized Annealing Temperatures for BAX, BCL-2, and α-Synuclein

The gel electrophoresis results showed amplification of BAX, BCL-2, and α-Synuclein mRNA using gene-specific primers. Distinct bands were observed at 113 base pairs for BAX, 205 base pairs for BCL-2, and 104 base pairs for α-Synuclein, indicating widespread expression in brain tissues. Bands were analyzed across a temperature gradient from 60.0°C to 68°C for BCL-2 and α-Synuclein, and 54°C to 63.5°C for BAX, with 2°C increments. The optimal annealing temperatures were 61.5°C for BAX and 64.8°C for BCL-2 and α-Synuclein, ensuring well-defined bands. This optimization confirms the PCR specificity and provides valuable insights into gene expression patterns in the brain (Appendix A: Supplementary Data).

Chrysoeriol Mitigates MPTP-Induced Apoptosis by Upregulating Bcl-2/Bax Ratio via PI3K/Akt Pathway

Gene expression analysis (β-actin-normalized) showed MPTP-induced elevation of proapoptotic BAX mRNA (Figure 4A), correlating with PD-related neuronal apoptosis, while chrysoeriol treatment attenuated BAX upregulation (p<0.01 in disease vs treated). Concurrently, BCL-2 mRNA increased significantly in chrysoeriol-treated mice (Figure 4B), demonstrating enhanced anti-apoptotic transcription (p<0.05 in disease vs treated). The disease group exhibited a reduced BCL-2/BAX ratio, which chrysoeriol reversed (p value non-significant in disease vs treated mice) (Figure 4C), confirming its anti-apoptotic effect via dual regulation of these apoptotic markers (Statistical data is provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1). These results align with mechanisms linking BCL-2/BAX balance to Akt activation, implicating PI3K/Akt pathway modulation in chrysoeriol’s neuroprotection [20,32,33].

Chrysoeriol Downregulates mRNA Expression of α-Synuclein in PD Mouse Model

There was an up-regulation in the expression of α-Synuclein mRNA in the mice subjected to MPTP compared to the control group (p<0.01 in control vs disease), suggesting an increased transcription of this gene under PD conditions, which may potentially lead to neuronal loss and α-Synuclein aggregate formation (Figure 4D). In contrast, treatment with chrysoeriol resulted in a downregulation of α-Synuclein, implying a potential ameliorative effect of chrysoeriol on the up-regulated α-Synuclein expression associated with the neuropathology of PD (p<0.05 in disease vs treated mice) (Statistical data is provided in Appendix A: Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion and Conclusion

Currently, PD treatment is primarily symptomatic, focusing on strategies such as dopamine replacement and the suppression of abnormal neuronal oscillations through deep brain stimulation. Unfortunately, these treatments only temporarily reduce early symptoms and do not halt the disease’s progression. As a result, therapeutic approaches for PD remain challenging due to the variability and lack of specificity in treatment procedures [34]. Investigating compounds capable of mitigating apoptotic neuron loss offers a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. In this investigation, the potential antioxidant and neuroprotective effects of chrysoeriol, administered IP were evaluated in an in vivo acute PD model, established via MPTP administration. While the neuroprotective effects of various flavonoids including chrysin and protocatechuic acid have been studied in different experimental models, their effects cannot be directly extrapolated to chrysoeriol [9,12,35]. Research has highlighted its benefits in other contexts, such as anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory properties [7]; however, our study is focused more on systematically assessing its effects on PD mouse model, while specifically analyzing multiple neuroprotective mechanisms and functional recovery. In this study chrysoeriol’s therapeutic effects are assessed through computational modeling, behavioral evaluations, histological analysis, and molecular studies which highlights the novelty of exploring its role in neurodegenerative diseases.

BALB/c mice were selected for their established utility in PD research under optimized conditions [13,14], with MPTP chosen for its selective dopaminergic neurotoxicity in the substantia nigra, recapitulating PD’s motor and neurodegenerative hallmarks [14]. Acute MPTP administration (20 mg/kg) induced neurodegeneration validated by behavioral deficits (reduced open field activity, increased forced swim immobility) mirroring PD-like motor dysfunction and depressive phenotypes [17]. MPTP’s neurotoxicity stems from glial MAO-B-mediated conversion to MPP+, causing mitochondrial dysfunction. Protocol optimization ensured consistent injury throughout the 14-day chrysoeriol treatment window, confirmed via pre-/mid-treatment behavioral assessments aligning with acute MPTP model standards [33].

Chrysoeriol treatment reduced immobility (indicative of improved depressive-like behavior) and enhanced motor coordination (assessed via pole test), spatial working memory (evaluated using the Y-Maze), and exploratory behavior (measured in the open field test), consistent with neuroprotective flavonoid-related effects observed in PD models [20,33,36,37]. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of cortical, midbrain, cerebellar, and hippocampal tissues demonstrated preserved neuronal architecture in treated mice, including intact pyramidal cells and nuclei, with ImageJ-confirmed increases in cell counts (Appendix A: Supplementary Material). PCR analysis revealed upregulated BCL-2 (an anti-apoptotic marker), downregulated Bax (a proapoptotic protein), and reduced α-synuclein levels, supporting chrysoeriol’s potential neuroprotective effects against MPTP-induced dopaminergic neuron loss and neurodegeneration [7,38]. The higher number of normal and newly generated neurons also aligns with studies showing that chrysoeriol may promote neuroprotection through significant expression of growth factors such as NGF and BDNF [7]. These findings underscore its potential to preserve neuronal integrity and counteract PD progression [32].

Before beginning physical experimentation, we conducted an in silico analysis using various computational methods to explore chrysoeriol’s interactions. Molecular docking simulations indicated that chrysoeriol binds to Bax, α-Synuclein, and BCL-2 with notable affinities, suggesting key molecular pathways for its neuroprotective properties. The loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, characterized by mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis and variations in apoptotic markers such as Bcl-2 and Bax, is a key feature of PD [39].

Our study found that MPTP treatment induced apoptotic features in mice, indicating mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal death. The PI3K/Akt pathway, vital for cell survival, is downregulated in PD and disrupted by MPP+, reducing phosphorylation of PI3K, Akt, and mTOR [40,41]. Chrysoeriol treatment was associated with increased Bcl-2 mRNA expression and decreased Bax expression, resulting in an improved Bcl-2/Bax ratio. This aligns with studies linking Bcl-2 and Bax expression with Akt activation and underscores chrysoeriol’s potential to protect neurons via the PI3K/Akt pathway [42,43]. By reversing MPP+-induced toxicity, chrysoeriol may lead to increased phosphorylated PI3K, Akt, and P85 levels (Figure 5). As reported in literature, phosphorylation of Akt initiates a signaling cascade that activates NF-kB and mTOR while deactivating pro-apoptotic factors like BAD and Bax, promoting cell survival through the PI3K/Akt pathway. This underscores chrysoeriol’s neuroprotective potential against MPP+-induced neurotoxicity [8,44-46]. Further investigation may enhance understanding of chrysoeriol’s role in PD, suggesting it may be a promising therapeutic agent.

α-Synuclein, a major protein in PD pathology, interacts with mitochondrial membranes and impairs complex I function, leading to oxidative stress [3,47]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, marked by deficits in mitochondrial complex I, is strongly associated with PD and involves increased oxidative stress and apoptotic cell death. Toxins like rotenone and MPP+ target mitochondria, highlighting the significance of mitochondrial health in PD. In our study, MPP+ treatment increased α-Synuclein levels, contributing to mitochondrial damage [46]. Chrysoeriol treatment may have reduced α-Synuclein expression, potentially protecting mitochondria by enhancing the PI3K/Akt pathway, which regulates α-Synuclein stability, reduces oxidative stress, and maintains mitochondrial function [42]. Thus, chrysoeriol may help mitigate MPP+-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis, suggesting it could have potential therapeutic benefits for PD and other neurodegenerative disorders.

This study investigates chrysoeriol’s neuroprotective mechanisms in PD, contrasting with current therapies (carbidopa/levodopa, dopamine agonists, MAO-B/COMT inhibitors) that alleviate symptoms but fail to halt neurodegeneration [48]. In PD models, chrysoeriol improved motor and cognitive deficits, preserved neuronal integrity, and modulated apoptotic pathways (BAX/BCL-2), consistent with its antioxidant/anti-inflammatory properties [42].

Limitations and Future Directions

Limitations of this study, including limited sample size and the absence of DMSO-only or chrysoeriol-only control groups must be acknowledged while considering the outcome. Furthermore, neurogenesis and non-motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease, such as sleep or autonomic changes, were not directly assessed. In light of the recent findings in PD research, future studies could also include microbiome analysis to further strengthen translational relevance. Studies should explore additional biomarkers, such as NF-κB, BDNF, caspases, p53, and tau, and use proteomics to better understand how chrysoeriol protects neurons. Validating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway with Western blot or immunohistochemistry, testing different doses, and comparing its effects with standard PD treatments like levodopa or MAO-B inhibitors will help clarify its therapeutic potential. Long-term studies in chronic PD models, along with pharmacokinetic and blood-brain barrier analyses, are needed to support translation to humans. Human studies will require careful planning, including Phase I safety trials, pharmacokinetic and dose-response analyses, and evaluation of tolerability and contraindications. As chrysoeriol naturally occurs in some foods and medicinal herbs, future work could also explore safe dietary supplementation for early-stage PD, with careful evaluation before any clinical trials.
