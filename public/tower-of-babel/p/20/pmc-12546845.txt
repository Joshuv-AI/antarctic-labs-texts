Assessment of microbiological quality of some selected street vended foods, vendor’s safety practices, knowledge, and attitudes in Dessie Town, Ethiopia
Authors: Huriya Ebrahim, Moges Kibret Wondimagegn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Street-vended foods are preferred for their convenience and affordability, yet unsanitary handling practices can raise the risk of microbial contamination. This study aimed to assess the microbiological quality of some selected street foods, vendor’s safety practices, knowledge, and attitudes in Dessie Town, Ethiopia. A cross-sectional study involving 170 street food vendors that employed a structured questionnaire and observational checklist as data collection tools. A total of 75 food samples were collected from various locations in the town using sterile stomacher bags. The samples comprised 15 portions each of “Chips”, “Sambusa”, “Bonbolino”, “Ambasha”, and “Sweet potatoes”. Every food sample was subjected to homogenization, serial dilution, and spread plating to detect microorganisms using standard microbiological techniques. The data was analyzed using SPSS software, version 20.00. The study revealed a predominance of female vendors (88.8%), and vendors lack awareness about spoiled food characteristics (94.1%). Surprisingly, a significant portion of vendors did not use gowns (68.8%) or gloves (82.4%) while vending. Most vendors 60.0% did not cover cooked food, and 58.8% of vending sites had dust or filth nearby. Regarding microbial quality, “Sambusa” had the highest mean count (1.23 × 10− 3 CFU/g) of aerobic mesophilic bacteria. “Bonbolino” and “Sweet Potato” showed Staphylococci counts of 2.61 × 10− 2 CFU/g and 2.64 × 10− 2 CFU/g, respectively. “Sweet potatoes” had the highest coliform count (2.4 × 10− 2 CFU/g), while “Ambasha” exhibited the highest Salmonella count (3 × 10− 2 CFU/g). Vis-a-vis the study points, the finding indicated that the bus station had the highest aerobic mesophilic bacteria count (1.12 × 10− 3 CFU/g), while Kelem meda, Piasa, and Segno gebeya recorded the highest Staphylococcus counts. Regarding the antibacterial susceptibility profiles, Salmonella has shown susceptibility to most antibiotics tested, such as Amoxicillin and Cefradine, but has been found resistant to Oxacillin. Conversely, E. coli has exhibited resistance to all antibiotics except for Gentamicin. Given the concerns observed in microbiological quality and safety issues of street vended foods in Dessie Town, it is crucial to introduce inclusive training programs for vendors.

Introduction

As per the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), street food encompasses “ready-to-eat foods and beverages vended and prepared by vendors or hawkers in streets or other public spaces”1. Street food vendors commonly set up shops in bus terminals, industrial zones, schools, markets, and streets, aiming to offer their products directly to consumers. In many developing countries, street food is favored for its convenience and serving as a popular dietary choice2.

An estimated 2.5 billion people partake in street food daily, a practice that sustains the livelihoods of millions of low-income individuals and contributes significantly to the economy3. According to the FAO report, street food vendors are ubiquitous across both industrialized and developing nations, with a noticeable surge in the latter. In low-income countries, the consumption of foods sold by street vendors and eaten without additional processing is widespread4. The World Health Organization5 highlighted that the consumption of street food is prevalent in developing nations marked by high unemployment rates, limited income sources, few employment opportunities, and rapid urbanization.

However, a significant and persistent issue linked to street-vended food is the upsurge of foodborne illness outbreaks in developing countries. Microbiological contamination stands as the primary health hazard associated with street food consumption. Various street-vended food products in different countries have undergone microbiological examination, unveiling a substantial prevalence of foodborne pathogens and elevated bacterial counts1,6. Despite global efforts by governments to enhance the safety of the food supply, foodborne illnesses remain a critical health issue in both developed and developing nations. Foodborne illnesses can arise from consuming food contaminated with microorganisms or their toxins, from cross-contamination on surfaces that come into contact with food, from inadequate preservation methods, from unhygienic handling practices, or from individuals carrying the microorganisms on their skin and in their nasal passages. Unhygienic practices in food handling, preparation, and storage create a conducive environment for the proliferation and transmission of pathogenic microorganisms7.

Studies on the microbiological quality of street vended foods across various Sub-Saharan African countries such as Ghana, Kenya, and Ethiopia has demonstrated that most of the popular types of ready-to-eat foods, sold on the streets are contaminated, and do not meet the required quality and safety levels8–10. Reports have also highlighted significant variations in microbial quality among different food types, vending sites, and countries. For example, investigations in Nigeria, have consistently reported high bacterial loads in items such as meat products, salads, rice dishes, and fruit juices11,12. Therefore, improving the microbial safety of street foods requires an integrated approach that includes education of vendors, enforcement of food safety regulations, provision of clean water supply and facilities, and community-level awareness campaigns11,12. Addressing these challenges is critical to safeguarding public health while supporting the livelihoods of those who depend on the street food economy.

Similar to other Sub-Saharan African countries, foodborne illnesses pose a significant concern in Ethiopia. For instance, data have been documented about the detection of Shigella and Salmonella in street-sold meals in Ethiopia, particularly in Addis Ababa. Diarrheal diseases rank as the second leading cause of death in Ethiopia13. The health hazards linked to consuming contaminated food have elevated street food vendors to a realm of significant public health apprehension, considering the widespread occurrence of foodborne illnesses. Furthermore, the public health landscape regarding food safety is further complicated by the emergence and spread of multi-drug-resistant foodborne bacteria14, heightening the challenges in safeguarding public health through safe food practices. Broadly, foodborne illnesses and fatal infections pose a significant threat to public health, substantially hindering the nation’s socioeconomic progress. This underscores the critical need to assess the microbial quality and safety of ready-to-eat foods in regions with diverse street food markets.

In this regard, Dessie town serves as a major commercial hub in the northeastern highlands, with vibrant street food markets that attract a diverse consumer base. These specific vending environments and street-sold foods, often prepared and sold in open-air settings with no hygienic control, are widely consumed due to their affordability and convenience. However, the risk of microbial contamination and the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria remains a public health concern, highlighting the necessity for assessements like the one carried out in this study. Therefore, the objective of this study was to examine the microbial quality and safety of street-vended food in Dessie Town, seeking to enhance understanding of the risks linked to the consumption of such food items.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in Dessie town, which is positioned in the Amhara Region of Ethiopia, about 401 km away from Addis Ababa. Situated at an elevation of about 2470 m (8,104 feet) above sea level, the town is surrounded by beautiful mountains. The town’s geographical coordinates are approximately 11.13° N latitude and 39.63° E longitude. The base administrative boundaries for the study area map were sourced from the Ethiopian Mapping Agency. The data were originally referenced to the Adindan datum. All geospatial processing, analysis, and cartographic output were performed using a Geographic Coordinate System (GCS) based on the Adindan datum within ArcGIS version 10.8 (Esri) (Fig. 1). According to the central statistical agency national census and projection data provided by the city administration, Dessie has a sizable population of 320,220 residents15.

Study design and period

A comprehensive cross-sectional study was undertaken in Dessie town from December 2023 to July 2024. The study encompassed a blend of experimental and descriptive survey methodologies to assess the bacteriological quality and food handling practices associated with popular street food items. These items, including “Sambusa” (a flavorful pastry, deep-fried or baked pastry filled with spiced lentils, minced meat, or vegetables, triangular in shape and crispy exterior), “Bonbolino” (resembles a doughnut, made from a yeasty-based dough usually soft on the inside, deep-fried until golden brown on the outside), “Chips” (thinly sliced and deep-fried potatoes), “Ambasha” (a traditional leavened flat bread prepared from refined wheat flour at home, slightly sweet and spiced), and “Sweet Potato” (commonly prepared by boiling or deep-frying, soft and served warm); are frequently found at diverse locations across the town, such as the Kelem Meda (vicinity of Wollo University), Menafesha, Bus Station, Piassa, and Segnogebeya (Fig. 1).

Questionnaire and observational survey

The survey was conducted using questionnaires and observational checklist. Semi structured questionnaires were prepared and filled in by food vendors to evaluate street food vendors’ knowledge, attitudes and food safety practices. The questionnaire was initially drafted in English, translated into Amharic (the local language) by a language expert, and then back-translated into English to ensure consistency and clarity. The translated version was then pre-tested on 17(10%) study subjects not included in the main study to assess its clarity, comprehensibility, cultural appropriateness, and to ensure all the questions were understandable and appropriately formatted. Based on the pre-test feedback, minor modifications were made to the wording and sequencing of questions. During data collection a total of 170 respondents were provided with the questionnaires to collect their responses. The questionnaire was divided into six main sections: demographic information (6 questions), food safety attitudes (6 questions), knowledge (10 questions), food handling practices (10 questions), food preparation and storage (6 questions); and environmental hygiene (4 questions) were included in the questionnaires. Level of education and exposure to training were included in the questionnaires. The researchers assisted the individuals who lacked formal education to complete the questionnaire by their own.

Food samples collection and preparation

About 75 food samples representing the five most prevalent varieties of street food items such as “Chips”, “Sambusa”, “Bonbolino”, “Ambasha” and “Sweet Potatoes” were carefully collected for analysis. Upon procurement, each sample was picked using a sterile spoon and transferred into sterile stomacher bags. Subsequently, these bags were labeled with details regarding the specific location and date of collection sites, sealed and transported promptly to Microbiology Laboratory, Wollo University16. Samples were processed promptly within a few hours of collection without extended storage. The remaining samples were initially frozen and then stored in a refrigerator until the time of analysis. These refrigerated samples underwent a controlled thawing process by being transferred from the freezer to a refrigerated environment maintained at 4 °C, where they remained for a full 24 h to ensure gradual thawing before microbiological analysis was conducted17. Following thawing, using a sterile spoon 50 g of the sample from each item was transferred and deposited into fresh sterile stomacher bags. To initiate the primary decimal dilution step, the required quantity of 450 ml of Buffered Peptone Water (BPW) was carefully introduced. Subsequently, a stomacher machine was employed to homogenize the diluted samples for a duration of ten minutes. Upon achieving uniformity in the initial dilutions, serial decimal dilutions were carefully prepared in tubes, with each containing 9 milliliters of sterile BPW, in accordance with the Bacteriological Analytical Manual.

Microbial detection and enumeration

During the microbial enumeration, the total aerobic mesophilic count (TAMC) was determined through a standardized plate count procedure18. Initially, 50 g of each food sample was blended with 450 mL of 1% peptone water in a sterile stomacher bag and shacked using stomacher machine (serial number of 2019121907) for ten minutes, followed by serial dilution up to 10− 4 in sterile test tubes containing 9 mL of sterile BPW. A 0.1 mL aliquot from the suitable dilution was then pipetted and spread plated on standard plate count agar media (OXOID, Hamshire, England) in triplicate, with subsequent incubation at 32 °C for 48 h. Post-incubation, plates exhibiting colony counts ranging between 30 and 300 were enumerated using a colony counter (Model-J-2 colony counter)18. Enumeration of Staphylococcus species was performed via the spread plate method on Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) (Alpha Chemicas, Mumbai, India). A 0.1 mL aliquot from the appropriate dilution was inoculated onto pre-dried MSA plates and incubated at 35 °C for 48 h. Yellow colonies were then counted and recorded as Staphylococcus counts using the colony counter19. Escherichia coli detection was accomplished using Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar (Accumix, Malaga, Spain), where 0.1 mL of the sample was spread onto EMB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The colonies were confirmed through Gram’s staining and biochemical tests20. Total Coliform assessment involved pipetting of a 0.1 mL aliquot from the appropriate dilution onto Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBA) plates (Accumedia, Michigan, USA), which were then incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h21. Additionally, yeasts and molds were counted via a direct plate count using Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA), where 0.1 mL from the suitable dilution was spread plated on PDA (Sisco Research laboratory, Mumbai, India). Total yeasts and molds were subsequently enumerated after incubation at 30 °C for 3 days22. For the assessment of Salmonella spp., 50 g of each food sample were transferred to 450 mL of BPW and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A 0.1 mL aliquot from the pre-enrichment was then spread plated onto Salmonella-Shigela agar (SSA) plates (HIMEDIA, Mumbai, India) and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. The presence of colonies displaying black centers or gray colors on the SSA indicated presumptive Salmonella spp21,23.

Characterization and identification of dominant bacteria

The bacterial isolates were characterized by evaluating their cultural, morphological, biochemical traits and searching for characteristic colonies of the target pathogens on plates containing the corresponding selective medium. Subsequently, plates with characteristic colonies were stored at a temperature of 4 °C until verification was conducted. To ensure accurate confirmation of the identified bacteria from various food samples, further confirmation of the bacterial isolates was achieved through biochemical tests including Oxidase, Catalase, Methyl Red (MR), Voges-Proskauer (VP), Urease, Triple Sugar Iron (TSI), Indole, and Citrate utilization tests. During characterization the biochemical analysis procedures were carefully followed as outlined in practical food microbiology24. The collected data was cross-referenced or compared with Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology to identify the isolates tentatively. Besides, Gram staining was conducted by preparing an air-dried and heat-fixed smear of pure isolates on a sterile slides following the established procedure25.

Antibacterial sensitivity tests

The pure pathogenic bacteria cultures were examined for their antibacterial sensitivity profiles using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method, as described by Bauer and colleagues and detailed by the National Committee for Clinical Laboratory Standards26. Standardized antibiotic disks with known concentrations were placed on Mueller–Hinton agar plates that were previously seeded with each specific bacterial strain. The selection of standard antibiotics was based on their spectrum of activities and access in the laboratory. After an incubation period of 24 h at 37 °C, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters (mm). The results were then recorded and interpreted according to established criteria as either sensitive (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R)27.

Data analyses

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. Mean comparisons were executed utilizing one-way ANOVA and LSD tests within the SPSS software. Descriptive statistics, encompassing frequency distribution, mean, median, and standard deviation, were calculated and showcased as numerical values and percentages. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered as indicative of a statistically significant difference.

Socio-demographic characteristics of the street food vendors revealed a predominance of females, with 151 individuals (88.8%) falling into this category. In terms of age distribution, majority 123 (72.4%) of vendors were between 21 and 30 years old. Regarding educational background, most of the vendors, 104 (61.2%) had finished secondary school. Additionally, the majority of vendors, 160 individuals (94.1%), have had less than five years of experience in the sector (Table 1).

Estimates of microbial load of the street food samples

In the current study, “Sambusa” exhibited the highest mean count of aerobic mesophilic bacteria (AMB) at 1.23 × 103 CFU/g, followed closely by “Sweet potatoes” at 1.2 × 103 CFU/g. Similarly, “Sweet Potato” led with a mean count of Staphylococci at 2.3 × 102 CFU/g, trailed by “Bonbolino” at 2.61 × 102 CFU/g. Additionally, the highest mean counts of mold (2.9 × 102 CFU/g) and coliform (2.4 × 102 CFU/g) were observed in “Sambusa” and “Sweet potatoes,” respectively. Notably, “Sambusa” surprisingly exhibited a relatively low mean count of coliform bacteria at 1.5 × 102 CFU/g. Furthermore, “Ambasha” showcased the highest average Salmonella count at 3 × 102 CFU/g. Interestingly, the maximum count of E.coli, 1.4 × 102 CFU/g, was documented in “Chips” (Table 6).

Discussion

Based on the socio-demographic characteristics analyzed in this study, females dominate the street food vending sector in Dessie town. Similarly, a study from Accra, Ghana, reported that all vendors were women28. Furthermore, majority of the food vendors in Gondar were reported to be female, making up the bulk of the market29. This can be attributed to the fact that women are expected to process and prepare meals according to societal norms in the study area. In terms of education, majority of vendors in Dessie town completed secondary school. This vendors profile generally followed the same pattern as several other studies conducted in Kenya, Burkina Faso, Nigeria and Uganda30–34. Low levels of education are probably associated with poor handling and food storage procedures, which raises the possibility of contaminating street food35. However, another study found that the majority of food vendors in India had only completed their primary or high school education14.

According to the current study’s assessment of street vendors’ attitudes and awareness regarding food safety, 32.4% of them were ignorant of the risks associated with both waterborne and foodborne illnesses. Among the vendors surveyed, 28.2% were unaware that wearing gloves while handling food could reduce the risk of food contamination, while considerable number of them were unaware that using watches, earrings, and rings could increase the risk of food contamination. These results align with the research findings presented by Nemo et al.36. One plausible explanation for this trend could stem from inadequate training in food handling and customer service. This highlights a key point in which the primary route for germs from fecal matter, nasal secretions, and skin to contaminate food is through the hands of food handlers37,38. The study unveiled that a significant majority of street food vendors directly handled food items with their bare hands, and all of them served customers without the use of any protective coverings. This underscores a crucial reality where food items are at risk of contamination at various stages, including preparation, handling, transportation, and sale by these vendors.

A majority of the vendors at the study site displayed poor personal hygiene practices. While handling and serving food, over half of the vendors neglected to wash their hands after handling money. However, as per the FAO39, money is known to harbor contaminants that can transfer to food. Additionally, a considerable proportion of these vendors operated without wearing protective gowns. These results were consistent with studies conducted in developing countries40, Uganda34, China41 and Nigeria42. It implies that food-borne illness outbreaks may arise as a result of hand hygiene practices being neglected during food preparation, serving, and handling43. This danger increases significantly when food is handled with bare hands, as per the FAO39, the hands play a critical role in the contamination and transmission of fecal-oral transmitted bacteria. The FAO guidelines for handling street foods, recommends using clean tongs, forks, spoons, or disposable gloves whenever handling, serving, or selling food44.

Moreover, the study revealed that some of the vendors wore jewelry without covering it, and many others did not cover their hair. Additionally, many operators were observed cleaning their booths using soiled cloths. In contrast to these findings, a study in Bahir Dar Town, Ethiopia45, highlighted that food handlers exhibited better food safety practices, with majority demonstrating safe food storage practices and an average compliance rate of 68.5%. In terms of street food storage practices in Dessie Town, the majority of vendors (60%) openly displayed food items in their stalls. It is crucial to note that to inhibit the proliferation of harmful microorganisms on street food, items not immediately served should be kept either hot or rapidly cooled down and then thoroughly reheated to a minimum core temperature of 70 °C before being served30.

Regarding the hygiene of the areas surrounding the vending sites, it was notable that all of them lacked protection from the sun, wind, and dust. Given that dust can harbor germs, it has the potential to serve as a carrier for disease-causing organisms, facilitating the contamination of prepared foods. Studies on street cuisine in Jimma, Ethiopia36, and Nairobi, Kenya30, revealed similar findings. At the stalls, there was evidence of the presence of flies, bugs, and/or animals. Similar circumstances have been documented in Uganda34, India14 and Kenya30. On the other hand, some of the vending booths lacked waste water and leftover food disposal facilities, and there was no direct access to potable water at the location. There were also insufficient hand washing stations. This study confirms the findings of studies conducted in Uganda34, Kenya30, and Jimma36 that tape water was not easily accessible at street selling locations. However, according to a survey in Nigerian46, only 9.5% of the vendors reported experiencing water shortages. As safe water is a crucial component of good health; hygiene and sanitary practices cannot be properly implemented without adequate water13. Additionally, access to sufficient potable water is a prerequisite for maintaining personal cleanliness31.

Of the stalls under investigation, 58.8% had unacceptable hygiene conditions surrounding them. Comparably, research conducted in Kenya and Mauritius found that 93% and 78% of the stalls, respectively, lacked trash cans; as a result, people disposed of their trash very close to the stalls30,47. In this regard, FAO, states that the area used for food preparation must be kept clean at all times and kept away from any potential sources of contamination, including dust, animals, waste water, and trash48. Furthermore, the merchants dumped their leftover food and water near to their booths. Consequently, this unclean surrounding brought in flies, who are not only a sign of unhygienic environments and a lack of hygiene, but they are also carriers of fecal pathogens. Likewise a study conducted in Nairobi, Kenya, documented that proper rubbish collection and disposal facilities were missing30. Adequate drainage and suitable waste disposal facilities should be established in the street food stalls to reduce the possibility of contamination of prepared food and potable water49.

Regarding the hygienic status of utensils used by street food vendors in Dessie town, half (50%) of the stalls that were examined had uncovered utensils. As they can readily become contaminated, utensils used to display food for sale need to be kept clean, covered, and protected50. In order to avoid contamination from dust and flies, it has been suggested that all types of foods be kept appropriately covered51. The majority of the vendors rinsed their utensils several times during the day using the same water. This implies that using the same water repeatedly could cause cross-contamination from the water to cooked food via rinsed utensils31.

Regarding the microbiological quality of the street food items, variations were observed in aerobic mesophilic bacteria counts among the different items analyzed. The variations could be due to difference in the intrinsic and extrinsic properties of the food items like pH, water activity etc. or the different levels of awareness and hygienic practices of the vendors. However, the average number of aerobic mesophilic bacteria per gram in the current investigation was 1.11 × 103 CFU/g. Likewise, similar study found that the mean counts of foods sold on the streets in Woldia town was (5.2 × 102 CFU/g)52. This may be the result of street sellers’ poor personal hygiene practices, which include handling food with their bare hands, cleaning utensils with spring water, using the same water to clean utensils several times and not receiving the necessary training, which raises the risk of cross-contamination and a higher degree of microbiological load. The presence of high number of aerobic mesophilic bacteria indicated that inadequate hygienic practices could be the cause of street foods contamination36.

In the current investigation, the average Staphylococci count per gram was 2.82 × 102 CFU/g. This aligns with the microbiological investigations conducted on food items sold on the streets of Jimma town36. This figure, however, is less than the average Staphylococci count (3.0 × 103 CFU/g) on items sold on the street in Gondar53. The increased number of S. aureus was a sign of contamination via food handlers’ hands, mouths, or noses through coughing and sneezing, as well as ignorance of hygiene precautions and food product safety54. Previous study reported that, the use of a fork or spoon to serve food reduced the level of contamination, while the use of bare hands resulted in an increase of contamination28. This is because S. aureus can be found in the nose (50%), on hands (5–30%), in the hair, eyes, and throat of healthy persons55. On the other hand, the mean Salmonella count (2.52 × 102 CFU/g) found lower than the previous report by, Mosupye and his colleague, where counts ranged from 1.2 to 2.0 log CFU/g56. Likewise higher counts of Salmonella (4.0 log CFU/g) was reported, from Jimma, Ethiopia36. A high concentration of dangerous bacteria, including Staphylococci and Salmonella species, can result in foodborne illnesses such as food poisoning, typhoid fever, and diarrhea.

In this study, the mean count of coliforms recorded was 1.93 × 102 CFU/g. This finding found less than that of Nemo and his colleagues, who reported the mean count of coliforms from Jimma town was between (1.7 × 106 − 6.3 × 105 CFU/g)36. This can be the result of post-preparation fecal contamination of food or water. It can also be attributed to the fact that food may eventually become tainted, though, either the water used to wash utensils or the sellers’ hands directly contaminated the food57. This implies that the incidence of coliforms and other indicators of fecal contamination often reflects inadequate food handling practices and poor environmental sanitation. On the other hand, the average counts of yeasts and molds were 2.31 × 102 CFU/g in the current study. In this regard, litters in the surroundings may have contributed to the high yeast and mold count in the current investigation. This substantiate for the need for keeping food processing and storage areas clean, to reduce the amount of mold that grows on food. This is because mold spores can easily transported by the wind and contaminate food samples58.

In the present study, the high presence of significant pathogenic bacteria is likely linked to the prevalent dust, litter, and unhygienic conditions at vending sites, the common practice of handling food with bare hands, cross-contamination, inadequate cooking, and prolonged use of utensils by vendors before replacement, among other factors. These findings consistent with similar findings reported in prior studies59–61. Overall, when compared to the standards set by regulatory bodies, the microbiological quality of street-vended foods in Dessie Town was deemed unacceptable62,63.

Regarding the antimicrobial sensitivity profiles of the pathogens, the study showed that a significant portion of Salmonella strains demonstrated higher susceptibility to Ampicillin, Amoxcillin, Oxacillin, Clindamycin, and Cefradin, while showing resistance to Gentamycin. This aligns with the findings from Jimma36. Similarly, in a study carried out in Sarab, Iran64, it was reported that Salmonella spp. exhibited high susceptibility rates to Chloramphenicol, Ciprofloxacin, and Gentamicin. However, there has been a recent emergence of Salmonella strains that display resistance to these frontline medications on a global scale, posing significant concerns. Consequently, the threat to public health has escalated, particularly in low-resource countries where bacterial infections persist as a leading cause of mortality65. Essentially, the isolates examined in the present study displayed notable patterns of antibiotic resistance, particularly a significant prevalence of E. coli resistance. Resistance profiles were also observed in Staphylococci against antibiotics like Amoxicillin, Gentamicin and Clindamycin. Conversely, a lower incidence of S. aureus displaying resistance to Chloramphenicol was also noted66.

Conclusion

Considering the overall survey, it can be concluded that street food has been sold in Dessie Town under typically unsafe (unhygienic) conditions, including unclean outdoor settings where the food is combined with improper food handling procedures and frequently insufficient storage conditions. In comparison to the standards established by other regulatory agencies, the microbiological quality of the foods sold on Dessie Street found to be unacceptable. As a result, the existence of microbiological indicators confirm the likelihood of the presence of other pathogens in street vended foods. The associated risk factors for poor microbiological quality and contamination of items sold on the street were inadequate personal hygiene, inappropriate food handling and storage practices, and inadequate vendor knowledge about food-borne illnesses. Based on the current findings, it is crucial to ensure that vendors have access to an ample supply of safe drinking water for hydration, cooking diverse meals, and carrying out necessary cleaning operations. Additionally, conducting regular and thorough assessments of the microbiological quality and safety practices of street vended foods is essential.

Limitation of the study

The study has its own limitations. For instance, it was cross-sectional; thus, it may not truly reflect the dynamic nature of socio-behavioral factors of the respondents. Moreover, due to resource, financial and time constraints, we did not perform molecular techniques to identify the pathogens. Only microscopy, cultural, physiological and biochemical technique was used to determine the identity of the isolates in this study.
