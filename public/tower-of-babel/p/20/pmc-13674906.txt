Inverse chop/Prop technique for phacoemulsification of soft cataracts and posterior polar cataracts
Authors: Vidhya Nagasubramanian, Muralidhar Rajamani, Ramamurthy Dandapani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soft cataracts pose significant challenges during phacoemulsification due to the pliable nature of the nucleus, which often leads to difficulty in cracking, cheese-wiring of instruments, and frequent occlusion breaks. Several fragmentation and non-fragmentation techniques have been described to manage such cases safely. We describe an inverse chop/prop technique that utilizes a blunt Sinskey hook to mobilize and crack the nucleus, facilitating safe and controlled phacoemulsification. This approach is also applicable for in situ phacoemulsification in posterior polar cataracts (PPCs), eliminating the need for nucleus rotation. We used this technique in patients with lens opacities classification system (LOCS) III grade 1–2 soft cataracts and PPCs. The technique involves introducing the Sinskey hook beneath an uncracked, partially cracked, or cracked nucleus to further crack or prop it, enabling subsequent emulsification. In PPCs, the hook is inserted via either side port to mobilize and crack the nucleus without rotation, permitting direct in situ emulsification. Cortex aspiration and intraocular lens (IOL) implantation are completed as per standard protocol. The technique was successfully performed in 188 patients without any procedure-related complications, including four cases with PPCs. In 50 cases, it proved particularly advantageous when conventional techniques encountered difficulty during emulsification. The inverse chop technique represents a valuable addition to the armamentarium for phacoemulsification of soft cataracts and PPCs, enhancing safety, control, and intraoperative efficiency.

Soft cataracts present many challenges in phacoemulsification. The traditional techniques, like divide and conquer and stop and chop, do not work well with soft cataracts due to the pliability of the nucleus. These techniques involve occlusion to stabilize and ultrasound to emulsify the nucleus. Occlusion breaks are common with soft cataracts, and the surgeon may end up with an unfractured and damaged nucleus. The instruments tend to cheese wire through a soft nucleus, making it difficult to achieve a complete crack.[123]

A number of fragmentation and non-fragmentation techniques have been described for safe emulsification of soft cataracts.[12345] The Y sign has been reported as a useful sign to achieve a good trench depth for successful cracking of soft nuclei.[3] We describe a technique that can be used for phacoemulsification of soft cataracts as an alternative to (or in addition should the surgeon experience difficulty with) the above techniques.

A number of approaches have been described for safe phacoemulsification of posterior polar cataracts (PPCs).[678910] We believe that our technique of inverse chop would be a useful addition.

Surgical Technique

The study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital. All surgeries were done under topical/subtenon’s anesthesia. Patients with LOCS III cataract grade 1-2 and PPCs without pre-existing posterior capsular dehiscence were included. We included patients in whom inverse prop/chop was used as the initial procedure and when it was used after difficulties were encountered with other techniques. Under surgical asepsis, a temporal clear corneal incision was made with a 2.4 mm Keratome. A side port entry was made with the microvitreoretinal blade (MVR) blade two clock hours to the left. In patients with PPC, we made an additional side port entry 3 clock hours to the right of the main entry wound. About 0.2 cc of preservative free 1% lignocaine was injected into the anterior chamber. Viscoelastic was injected, and capsulorhexis, hydrodissection, and hydrodelineation were done. In patients with PPC, a gentle hydrodelineation was attempted by burying the tip of the hydrodissection cannula into the nucleus to the estimated thickness of the posterior capsular plaque.[6] Hydrodelineation ensures an epinuclear cushion to protect the posterior capsule during maneuvers with the Sinskey hook. A trench was fashioned with the phaco tip using low vacuum and power. The trench was deepened with the phaco tip until the striations appeared parallel to the direction of the trench.[3] An attempt was made to crack the nucleus.

Postoperatively, the patients were placed on a tapering regimen of topical moxifloxacin qid for a week, topical loteprednol tapered over 8 weeks, and topical bromfenac drops bd for 6 weeks. Patients were reviewed on day 1, 2 weeks, and 6 weeks. The machine was switched to continuous irrigation mode whenever the Sinskey hook was passed under the nucleus.

For posterior polar cataracts (without pre-existing posterior capsular dehiscence)

The vacuum was kept at 200 mm Hg, the bottle height at 60 cm, and the aspiration rate at 20 cc/min. When an incomplete crack was obtained, the phaco tip was withdrawn (after injecting viscoelastic through the side port), and viscoelastic was injected into the anterior chamber and under the nucleus through the crack. A Sinskey hook was inserted with the tip pointing sideways into the crack through the main entry wound, and the nucleus was lifted to complete the separation. Additional viscoelastic was injected when needed, and care was taken to avoid sudden shallowing of the anterior chamber. Once nuclear division was achieved, the Sinskey hook was inserted through one side port under the opposite nuclear fragment, and the fragment lifted. Further management of this nuclear piece was according to scenarios 1-3 as mentioned above. Results

A total of 188 patients aged 45–70 years were operated by this technique. In 50 patients, the inverse chop/prop technique was used after difficulty was encountered in nucleus emulsification with the conventional techniques. No patient had posterior capsular dehiscence or zonular damage during nucleus manipulation. One patient with a shallow anterior chamber had extension of the capsulorrhexis. Hydrodissection was avoided and a gentle hydrodelineation done. Surgery was completed uneventfully with lowered parameters. In 14 patients, nuclear fragments prolapsed into the anterior chamber. The pieces were repositioned in the bag by injecting viscoelastic over them and gently tapping them back. Small fragments that popped out of the bag were emulsified by grasping their lower end. Two patients had mild striate keratopathy, and one patient had 4+ cells on the first postoperative day. These subsided with routine postoperative medications. This technique was also tried without any complication in four patients with PPC.

Discussion

Soft cataracts are difficult to crack. We found the technique of Kurian et al.[3] to be of great value in fashioning a trench of adequate depth. Further mobilization can be accomplished by the inverse chop/prop technique that in addition to cracking the nuclear fragments also serves to break the adhesions around the nucleus.

The non-fragmentation techniques described for soft cataracts attempt to carousel the nucleus to the center for phacoemulsification.[15] These may cause zonular damage and are difficult for the novice surgeon. The fragmentation techniques for soft cataracts are difficult for surgeons not experienced with pre-chopping. A technique similar to ours was described by Braga-Mele et al.[4] to crack the nuclear fragments by using a phaco tip to buttress the nuclear fragment over a Koch chopper. We used a Sinskey hook facing sideward and did not always find the need to use the phaco tip, though the same can be used to buttress the nuclear piece should there be cheese wiring. Sometimes, the Sinskey hook pushed up a pole of the nuclear fragment (instead of achieving a crack) that also permitted easy phacoemulsification. A small crack anywhere in the nucleus (or even the edge of the nucleus) should be enough to slide in the Sinskey hook and mobilize the nucleus for phacoemulsification in the bag. There is a small chance of the nuclear fragments prolapsing into the anterior chamber during these maneuvers. This should get less as the surgeon gains more experience. Surgeons performing manual small incision cataract surgery frequently use the Sinskey hook to mobilize the nucleus. This technique may shorten their learning curve when transitioning to phacoemulsification. As the chamber was always kept deep by turning on continuous irrigation and the blunt tipped Sinskey hook inserted sideways under direct visualization for a short distance only [Video 3], there is minimal chance of damaging the posterior capsule. Soft cataracts usually have an adequate epinuclear cushion to protect the posterior capsule during such maneuvers. But if the epinuclear cushion is deemed insufficient or in novice hands, we recommend injecting dispersive ophthalmic viscosurgical device (OVD) under the nucleus before attempting inverse chop [Video 4]. It may be noted that the Sinskey hook has been used for mechanically delaminating the soft nucleus in PPC.[11] Gupta et al.[12] used the Sinskey hook in the “hook and flip” technique by inserting the Sinskey hook under partially chopped nuclei to lift and complete the crack, and also to flip the nucleus when it could not be rotated, as in PPC. Both techniques testify to the safety of using the Sinskey hook. We have used the inverse chop maneuver to complete the crack in harder nuclei when the division was incomplete. The results were not reported in the present study as the numbers were too small to report.

We found that inverse chop was very useful for “in situ” phacoemulsification of PPCs. The general principles in the management of these cataracts include a 5 mm capsulorrhexis, avoidance of hydrodissection, and rotation of the endonucleus. After nuclear emulsification, the epinucleus is gently detached from the fornices and the central part is removed last. The cortex is pulled tangentially, and a blunt chopper may be used to gently sever any attachments to the posterior plaque.[678910] A layer-by-layer removal has been recommended when there is a pre-existing posterior capsular rent.[8] Our technique has the advantage of allowing “in the bag” phacoemulsification, mobilizing the endonucleus and separating it from the plaque.

Conclusion

The phaco chop/prop technique is a useful addition to the current methods for phacoemulsification of soft cataracts and possibly in patients with PPC without pre-existing posterior capsular dehiscence. We believe that this can be used by surgeons with various levels of experience as it is simple and needs no special instruments. It can also be used when difficulty is encountered with other techniques.
