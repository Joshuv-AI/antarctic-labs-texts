High-throughput sequencing and analysis of target genes uncover microRNA-mediated responses to low-temperature stress in Medicago falcata
Authors: Mei Yang, Lu Chai, Yuxuan Liu, Jiuding Sun, Han Li, Kailin Qian, Lulu He, Bing Li, Pan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow temperature severely restricts plant growth and development. Medicago falcata is an important germplasm resource for alfalfa breeding because of its strong adaptability and tolerance to abiotic stresses, particularly low-temperature stress. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are key regulators of stress-responsive genes and modulate plant adaptation to stress by directing transcript cleavage or translational repression. However, the roles of miRNAs in low-temperature response mechanisms in M. falcata remain largely unclear.ResultsHere, we constructed miRNA libraries from M. falcata roots grown under normal or low-temperature conditions. High-throughput sequencing identified 442 miRNAs, including 245 known and 197 novel miRNAs. Among these, 74 were putatively upregulated and 37 were putatively downregulated under chilling and freezing treatments. Based on degradome sequencing, 193 target genes involved in diverse biological processes were predicted. The M. falcata MfbZIP transcription factor family, consisting of 41 members across six subfamilies, responded to the cold treatments. Of these, MfbZIP10, a nuclear-localized protein homologous to a bZIP transcription factor in M. truncatula, showed the most significant response to cold stress in stems and roots. miR408-5p was responsive to chilling and freezing, and could cleave MfbZIP10 transcripts in a transient assay, as determined by RLM-RACE analysis. Physiological analyses and gene expression profiling of transgenic lines further demonstrated that the miR408-5p–MfbZIP10 module plays a vital role in low-temperature stress responses, with MfbZIP10 functioning as a key regulator by modulating physiological traits and the expression of genes in multiple signaling pathways.ConclusionsOur results identified candidate cold-responsive miRNAs in M. falcata and predicted their target genes involved in diverse biological processes. miR408-5p was found to target MfbZIP transcription factors in response to cold stress. Physiological characterization and quantitative expression analyses of MfbZIP10 transgenic lines showed that the miR408-5p–MfbZIP10 module plays a crucial regulatory role in low-temperature adaptation. miRNA sequencing and target gene identification provide new insights into miRNA-mediated regulatory mechanisms in M. falcata. These findings may facilitate the development of cold-tolerant forage legume crops through genetic improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13122-x.

Introduction

Low temperature, including both chilling (> 0 °C) and freezing (< 0 °C) conditions, is a major abiotic factor that reduces crop yield and quality [1, 2]. Prolonged exposure to low temperatures disrupts diverse biological processes, such as respiration, disturbs cellular osmotic homeostasis, and decreases flower bud quality and seed set [3, 4]. Under low-temperature conditions, physiological and biochemical adjustments, including changes in fatty acid composition that enhance membrane stability, maintenance of cellular redox balance, sustained photosynthetic activity, and accumulation of compatible osmolytes, are thought to facilitate plant adaptation to low-temperature stress [5]. These cold acclimation responses are largely governed by cold-responsive genes [6].

C-repeat-binding factor (CBF)-dependent and CBF-independent signaling pathways represent two major mechanisms underlying plant responses to low temperature [5]. In the CBF-dependent pathway, INDUCER OF CBF EXPRESSION (ICE) transcription factors can activate CBF transcription factors, which in turn regulate the expression of cold-regulated (COR) genes. This ICE–CBF–COR signaling cascade has been implicated in maintaining plant growth and development under cold conditions [7]. Other transcription factors, such as HEAT SHOCK TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR C1 (HsfC1), can modulate this pathway; for example, overexpression of HsfC1 in Arabidopsis has been reported to enhance COR expression and improve cold tolerance [8]. In contrast, the CBF-independent pathway appears to control the majority of cold-induced genes [2]. Additional components, including aquaporin (AQP) proteins and basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors, also contribute to cold adaptation in plants. AQPs facilitate water transport across biological membranes and thereby help preserve cellular water homeostasis under cold stress. In the cold-tolerant species Saussurea involucrata, tonoplast intrinsic proteins (TIPs) and plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs), which belong to the AQP family, are differentially regulated in response to low temperature [9]. bZIP transcription factors are important regulators of plant growth, development, and environmental signaling [10, 11]. In particular, several bZIP genes have been shown to modulate plant responses to abiotic stresses, including cold stress, partly by influencing abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis, a key hormone in activating stress-responsive pathways and enhancing stress resilience [12, 13].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are pivotal regulators of stress-responsive genes [14] and can modulate plant stress adaptation by controlling gene expression through transcript cleavage or translational repression [13, 15, 16]. Numerous studies have examined the roles of miRNAs in regulating stress responses across diverse plant species. For instances, the downregulated miR408-5p was selected as a key hub in Agropyron mongolicum under drought conditions, thereby modulating the function of target genes [17]; miR319 has been implicated in cold stress responses in several species, such as tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [18] and wheat (Triticum aestivum) [19].;In Arabidopsis thaliana, miR169 has been reported to modulate the expression of IAA14 (an auxin-responsive gene involved in maintaining cellular signaling) when plants are exposed to low temperature [20]; In Medicago truncatula, miR396 acts together with its target gene MtGRF4 to regulate cold stress tolerance [21].

Medicago falcata is an important germplasm resource for alfalfa breeding due to its strong adaptability and tolerance to abiotic stresses, particularly low-temperature stress. Several studies have identified genes associated with low-temperature tolerance in M. falcata. For instance, a number of transcription factors, such as NAC proteins [22, 23], AUXIN INDUCED IN ROOT CULTURE12 (MfAIR) [24], and the ethylene-responsive factor MfERF1 [25], have been found to participate in the regulation of low-temperature stress tolerance in M. falcata. However, relatively few studies have examined miRNA-mediated responses to low-temperature stress in M. falcata.

In this study, we systematically screened low-temperature-responsive miRNAs in M. falcata and predicted their target genes using a combination of degradome analysis and online prediction tools. Our objective was to explore the miRNA–target gene regulatory network in M. falcata under low-temperature conditions. We specifically focused on bZIP transcription factors (MfbZIPs) in M. falcata as miR408-5p targets, because our previous work indicated that several MfbZIPs are strongly induced in low-temperature-treated M. falcata plants [26]. This study is expected to improve our understanding of miRNA-mediated regulation of low-temperature adaptation in M. falcata and may provide a basis for the molecular breeding of cold-tolerant Medicago cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment conditions

Seeds of Medicago falcata cv. Hulunbuir were surface-sterilized with 5% (v/v) NaClO for 5 min, rinsed several times with distilled water, and placed on moist filter paper in Petri dishes for germination. After 5 d, uniformly germinated seedlings were transferred to plastic pots containing a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture of vermiculite, perlite, and peat. Plants were grown in a greenhouse at 25 °C /20°C (day/night) and relative humidity of 75%/55% (day/night) [27]. Seedlings were irrigated with half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution every 2 d.

After 90 d of growth, plants with uniform vigor were transferred to a cold incubator and subjected to low-temperature treatments of − 15, −10, − 5, 0, and 4 °C for 2 h (16 h light/8 h dark), while control plants were maintained at 25 °C in an incubator with similar light and humidity conditions. Before exposure to sub-zero temperatures, plants were acclimated at 4 °C for 2 d, and the temperature was then decreased from 4 °C to each target temperature at a rate of 1 °C h− 1 [28, 29]. Root tissues with three independent biological replicates per treatment were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for high-throughput small RNA sequencing, degradome sequencing, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis of key miRNAs and their corresponding target genes. The detailed experimental design is summarized in Table S1.

To analyze the expression patterns of target genes under different abiotic stresses, 5-week-old M. falcata plants with uniform growth were exposed to the following treatments: low temperature (4 °C), osmotic stress (20% PEG-6000), salt stress (150 mmol·L− 1 NaCl), alkaline stress (150 mmol·L− 1 NaHCO₃), and exogenous ABA (100 mmol·L− 1 ABA). Root, stem, and leaf samples were collected at 0 (control), 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h after low temperature treatment; at 0 (control), 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after ABA treatment; and at 0 (control), 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after the other treatments. All samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction for subsequent gene expression analyses (Table S1).

Preparation and sequencing of miRNA libraries

Total RNA was extracted from roots of control and low-temperature-treated plants using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and residual genomic DNA was removed by DNase I digestion (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan). RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and genomic DNA contamination was further checked by agarose gel electrophoresis.

For each sample, high-quality total RNA was adjusted and used for small RNA library construction with the Small RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). T4 RNA ligase 1 and truncated T4 RNA ligase 2 were used to ligate the 3′ and 5′ adapters to small RNAs, respectively. The adapter-ligated RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA and amplified by PCR. Target fragments corresponding to small RNAs were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and purified to obtain the small RNA libraries. Library concentrations were measured with a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, and diluted libraries (1 ng·µL− 1) were analyzed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to verify fragment size distribution and quality. Six small RNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, USA) at Biomarker Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Full-length transcript of a control sample was sequenced using PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) technology serving as the reference. Each temperature treatment included three biological replicates.

Identification of known and novel miRNAs

To identify miRNAs, total RNA from roots subjected to the six temperature treatments (control and five low-temperature treatments) was used to construct six small RNA libraries. For each temperature, equal amounts of RNA from the three biological replicates were pooled to generate one library. Raw sequence reads were obtained from the Illumina HiSeq 2500 output by base calling. Low-quality reads, including reads containing ≥ 10% unknown nucleotides (N), reads consisting only of 3′ adapter sequences, and reads outside the length range of 18–30 nt, were removed to obtain clean reads.

Clean reads were aligned to the Silva, GtRNAdb, Rfam, and Repbase databases using Bowtie 1.3.1 (http://bowtie.cbcb.umd.edu) [30] to identify and remove rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, snoRNA, and other repetitive or non-coding RNA (ncRNA) sequences. The remaining miRNA and unannotated reads were extracted; unannotated reads were mapped to the reference genome using Bowtie 1.3.1 to obtain genome-matched reads. The mapped reads were then aligned to known mature miRNA sequences in miRBase v21 (https://www.mirbase.org/) to identify conserved miRNAs. Novel miRNAs were predicted with miRDeep 2.0.0.5 based on the characteristic features of miRNA precursor sequences after genome alignment [31, 32]. Bayesian analysis was used to score candidate novel miRNAs according to the distribution and energy characteristics of their predicted precursors. miRNA expression levels in each library were calculated and normalized as transcripts per million (TPM) [33].

Analysis of candidate cold-responsive miRNAs in low-temperature and control libraries

Candidate cold-responsive miRNAs in low-temperature and control libraries were identified using DESeq and IDEG6 [34]. To reduce false positives in the miRNA expression analysis, raw P-values from the initial hypothesis tests were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The false discovery rate (FDR) was used as the principal significance criterion, and miRNAs with |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and FDR ≤ 0.01 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Preparation and sequencing of degradome libraries and data analysis

For degradome library construction, mRNA fragments from total RNA of each treatment were captured on magnetic beads and ligated to a 5′ RNA adapter. Biotinylated random primers were then used to reverse-transcribe the ligated mRNA, and the resulting cDNA was amplified by PCR to construct the degradome libraries. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using single-end 1 × 50 bp reads (Illumina, USA) at Beijing Biomarker Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Adapter sequences were removed from the raw reads, and low-quality tags were filtered to obtain clean tags and cluster tags. Cluster tags were aligned to the reference genome to determine their distribution. After cluster tags were compared with the Rfam database and ncRNAs were annotated, unannotated sequences were retained for degradome-based target analysis. CleaveLand software was used to identify miRNA-guided cleavage sites based on the sequence information of known and predicted miRNAs and their corresponding gene transcripts.

Prediction of miRNA targets and functional annotation of degradome-validated targets

Putative miRNA target genes were predicted using TargetFinder. BLASTX searches were performed to annotate predicted targets against the non-redundant (NR), Swiss-Prot, Gene Ontology (GO), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Eukaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG), and Pfam databases [35]. GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the GOseq package in R 4.3.1, and KEGG pathway enrichment was evaluated with KOBAS 3.0. miRNA target genes supported by degradome evidence were subjected to GO annotation and functional categorization using the NCBI database.

Determination of miRNA and target gene expression levels by qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of plants subjected to different treatments using VeZol Reagent R411 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and the Ultrapure RNA Kit (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. RNA integrity was examined by agarose gel electrophoresis, and RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoPhotometer (Implen, Munich, Germany).

For miRNA quantification, reverse transcription was performed using the TUREscript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit and specific stem-loop RT primers (Aidlab, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Relative expression levels of seven miRNAs showing significant differential expression were determined using 2× miRNA Unimodal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with U6 as the internal reference.

To quantify target gene expression, total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The expression levels of seven target genes were measured by qRT-PCR with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), using MfActin as the reference gene. MfbZIP transcript levels were quantified by qRT-PCR using gene-specific primers listed in Table S2A, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Analysis and characterization of the MfbZIP gene family

Protein sequences of bZIP transcription factors in Arabidopsis were retrieved from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Using Arabidopsis bZIP proteins as queries, the transcriptome databases of M. falcata were searched with TBtools v2.096 [36]. Conserved domains and motifs of the 41 bZIP members identified in M. falcata were analyzed using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (NCBI-CDD) and MEME [37].

The ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to calculate the molecular weight, amino acid length, and theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of MfbZIP proteins. Subcellular localization was predicted using CELLO v2.5 (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/). A phylogenetic tree of bZIP transcription factors from Arabidopsis and Medicago falcata was constructed in MEGA 11.0 using the neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates [38]. All results were visualized with TBtools, as in previous studies [39].

Gene cloning, sequence analysis, and subcellular localization of MfbZIP10

The coding sequence (CDS) of MfbZIP10 was amplified by PCR using degenerate primers (MfbZIP10-A/S) designed based on bZIP sequences identified in the M. falcata transcriptome (Table S2B). PCR amplification was performed using 2× Phanta Max Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The PCR product was ligated into a T-vector (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and sequenced.

Multiple sequence alignment was carried out with DNAMAN 6.0. A phylogenetic tree showing the relationships among MfbZIP10 and bZIP proteins from other legume species was constructed using MEGA 11 with the neighbor-joining method. The secondary structure of MfbZIP10 was predicted with SOPMA (http://pbil.ibcp.fr), and its physicochemical properties were analyzed with ProtParam. Subcellular localization was predicted using CELLO v2.5, and the three-dimensional structure of MfbZIP10 was modeled using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/).

For experimental determination of subcellular localization, the MfbZIP10 CDS without the stop codon was amplified with gene-specific primers MfbZIP10-TE-A/S (Table S2B) and cloned in-frame into the pCAMBIA1300 vector to generate a MfbZIP10–GFP fusion construct. The pCAMBIA1300-MfbZIP10-GFP construct was introduced into Escherichia coli strain DH5α for propagation and then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105. Agrobacterium cultures harboring the construct were infiltrated into the abaxial side of Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After 48 h of dark incubation, green fluorescent protein (GFP) signals were observed using a Leica TCS SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Plant transformation and generation of transgenic Arabidopsis lines

Full-length MfbZIP10 was amplified by PCR with primers MfbZIP10-OE-F/R (Table S2B), and the resulting fragment was inserted into the pMDC123 vector using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China) to generate the pMDC123-MfbZIP10 construct. The construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and used to transform wild-type (WT, Col-0) Arabidopsis and an atbzip17 mutant line via the floral dip method, thereby producing MfbZIP10-overexpressing (MfbZIP10-OE) lines and complemented lines.

Based on the conserved domain structure, sequence alignment, and phylogenetic analysis, AtbZIP17 was identified as the putative Arabidopsis homolog of MfbZIP10. Seeds of the atbzip17 mutant (SALK_152843C) were obtained from AraShare (https://www.arashare.cn/index/Product/index.html). T₁ transformants were selected on 1/2 MS medium containing 10 mg·L− 1 glufosinate ammonium. Herbicide-resistant plants were verified by PCR using Bar-F/R primers (Table S2A) and Taq Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a Quantagene q225 instrument (Jingxin, Shanghai, China). MfbZIP10 expression in transgenic lines was confirmed by qRT-PCR using AtActin as the reference gene and ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the qRT-PCR protocol described above. Homozygous lines were used for subsequent analyses of MfbZIP10 function.

Physiological measurements and gene expression analysis in Arabidopsis homozygous lines under low-temperature treatment

Arabidopsis WT, MfbZIP10-OE lines, atbzip17 mutants, and complemented lines were grown in soil for 20 d after germination and then transferred to 4 °C to evaluate their low-temperature stress responses. Physiological parameters were measured after 6 h of low-temperature treatment, and plant phenotypes were recorded after 7 d (Table S1). Rosette tissues were harvested, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for physiological assays and gene expression analysis.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using a modified thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method [40]. Superoxide anion (O₂·⁻) production was measured as described by M Jiang and J Zhang [41]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed by the nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction method [42]. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured according to Maehly’s procedure, and peroxidase (POD) activity was determined using the guaiacol method [43]. Free proline content was quantified by the ninhydrin method [44]. Soluble sugar and soluble protein contents were measured following the methods of R Dreywood [45] and MM Bradford [46], respectively. Reduced glutathione (GSH) content was measured fluorometrically [47].

For gene expression analysis, primers for Arabidopsis genes related to ABA and cold signaling (AREB3, CBF1, CBF2, SOD1, CAT1, TIP2, COR47, COR15, ICE1, CAS1, and PIP1) were designed using Primer5 (Table S2A). Transcript levels were quantified by qRT-PCR with AtActin as the reference gene on a Quantagene q225 system (Jingxin, Shanghai, China). miR408-5p expression in transgenic lines was determined by stem-loop qRT-PCR using miRNA Unimodal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) after designing the stem-loop primers for reverse transcription (Table S2A).

RLM-RACE (RNA ligase-mediated rapid amplification of cDNA ends) of miR408-5p and MfbZIP10

The recombinant vectors, pGreenII-62SK-pre-miR408-5p and pGreenII-62SK-MfbZIP10, were constructed (Table S2B) and subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium. For the control group, tobacco cells were co-transformed with Agrobacterium harboring pGreenII-62SK and Agrobacterium harboring pGreenII-62SK-MfbZIP10. In contrast, the test group involved co-transformation of tobacco cells with Agrobacterium carrying pGreenII-62SK-pre-miR408-5p and Agrobacterium carrying pGreenII-62SK-MfbZIP10. RNA from both groups was extracted from the co-transformed tobacco cells 72 h post-transformation.

The cleavage of MfbZIP10 by miR408-5p results in a cleavage product with a 5′ end bearing a single phosphate group. This characteristic allows for recognition by T4-RNA ligase, facilitating the ligation of an RNA adaptor to the 5′ end of the cleavage product. Reverse transcription was performed using GSP1 as the primer to synthesize the first-strand cDNA. Subsequently, PCR amplification and sequencing were conducted using the AP and GSP2 primers (Table S2A). The starting position at the 5′ end of the successfully obtained sequence indicates the cleavage site. These were conducted by Wuhan Biorun Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three biological replicates unless otherwise stated, and data are presented as means ± standard errors (SE). Data were processed using Excel 2019, and statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 22.0. Differences between two groups were evaluated by Student′s t-test, and multiple comparisons were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan′s test. Figures were prepared using Excel 2019, PowerPoint 2019, MEGA 11.0, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and TBtools (v2.096).

Results

miRNA sequencing data overview

To identify M. falcata miRNAs involved in the responses to low temperatures, 6 miRNA libraries were constructed using M. falcata roots subjected to both normal (CK) and low temperature conditions (4, 0, − 5, −10, and − 15 °C). The sequencing data revealed an average of approximately 17 million raw reads per library. After filtering, a total of 77.79 million clean reads were obtained, the average number of these is shown in Table S2. Most clean reads were 21 to 24 nucleotides long, aligning with plant miRNA characteristics (Fig. S1A). After filtering repetitive sequences and ncRNAs, we obtained unannotated reads containing miRNAs and aligned them with the “zhongmu NO.1” alfalfa genome. The majority of the acquired mapped reads were identified as “+”Mapped sRNA (Table S3). The mapped sequences were categorized (Table S4).

Identification of known and novel miRNAs

To validate the identified miRNAs, all mapped read sequences blasted against the miRNA database (miRBase v21). Additionally, miRDeep2 software was employed to predict novel miRNAs from sequences that did not match known miRNA sequences. Our investigation identified 197 potential novel miRNA precursors, with lengths ranging from 102 to 249 nt (Table S5). Overall, in the 6 samples, 442 miRNA sequences were expressed, comprising 245 known and 197 novel miRNAs from 91 families. The miR2593 family, comprising 19 members, was the largest among families, followed by miR1122, miR818, miR1507, miR166, miR5208, miR2600, miR2629, and miR156 (Fig. S1B). Based on base bias analysis, all reads were distributed across four bases at each position from the 1st to the 24th nt. The majority of reads started with a 5′ U, including those at the 18th, 19th, 21st, 22nd, and 25th nts in length (Fig. S2). Known miRNAs were predominantly 21nt in length, while novel predicted miRNAs were measured 21 and 24 nt, predominantly (Fig. S3).

Changes in candidate cold-responsive miRNAs expression in M. falcata roots under varied low-temperature conditions

Pearson correlation analysis of the six treatments showed correlation coefficients ranging from 0.937 to 0.997 (Fig. S4), indicating a positive and strong correlation among all treatments. The expression levels of miRNAs were normalized via the TPM algorithm, and the overall distribution map revealed similar patterns across the 6 libraries (Fig. S5). In total, 187 putatively differentially expressed miRNAs were identified, including 77 known and 110 novel miRNAs (Table S6, Fig. S6). Cluster analysis further demonstrated distinct expression patterns of miRNAs in M. falcata roots across the treatments (Fig. 1).

In the chilling group (4 °C and 0 °C), 93 miRNAs were putatively upregulated and 56 miRNAs were putatively downregulated. In the freezing group (− 5 °C, − 10 °C, and − 15 °C), the number were 91 and 48, respectively. Among these, 19 upregulated and 19 downregulated miRNAs were chilling-specific, while 17 upregulated and 11 downregulated miRNAs were freezing-specific. Additionally, 74 upregulated and 37 downregulated miRNAs showed common responses to both chilling and freezing treatments. Among these commonly responsive miRNAs, the upregulated set comprised 31 known and 43 novel miRNAs, while the downregulated set comprised 15 known and 22 novel miRNAs (Fig. 1). Notably, the miR319, miR5558, miR5037, miR2600, and miR396 families were upregulated in the roots, while the miR166, miR168, miR1314, and miR1507 families showed downregulation (Fig. 1C).

Targets of low-temperature-stress-related miRNA

A total of 32,834 target genes were predicted, comprising 19,909 genes identified by known miRNAs and 17,048 by novel miRNAs. Comparison of the predicted sequences with various databases resulted in the annotation of 31,899 target genes, with a majority of the target genes exhibiting enrichment in M. truncatula (Table S7, S8). The target genes primarily played roles in transcription, replication, recombination, repair, and signal transduction. Functional annotation revealed key molecular functions, such as binding and catalytic activity, as well as key biological processes, including metabolic and cellular processes (Fig. S7).

miRNA targets were identified according to the combination of target prediction and degradome. The six degradome libraries comprised raw reads totaling 18,752,248; 19,434,334; 16,288,233; 20,393,314; 16,972,812; and 15,551,775, respectively (Table S9). A total of 193 target genes were identified with 96, 118, 109, 123, 116, and 97 targets in each group (Table S10), of which177 genes were enriched in M. truncatula, consistent with the predicted outcomes (Fig. 2D). Functional classification analysis utilizing COG and KOG revealed various biological processes, such as recombination, transcription, replication, and repair, along with additional functions associated with RNA processing and modification, signal transduction mechanisms, cell division, cell cycle control, energy production and conversion. The annotation of most target genes in GO highlighted their involvement in biological regulation, the oxygen monomer process (single-organism process), and a number of other biological processes. The remainder was involved in various biological processes such as cellular process, multicellular organismal process, and response to stimulus. Target genes were enriched in cellular components, including cells, organelles, and compartments. The combination between nucleic acids, transcription factors, and binding sites enhances the enrichment of target genes across various GO categories (Fig. 2C). Seven miRNAs and their corresponding target genes were randomly selected for quantitative confirmation, and the results demonstrated the accuracy and reliability of the sequencing data (Fig. S8).

Prediction of miR408-5p targeting bZIP gene

miR408-5p was further selected for analysis. Although its expression level was relatively low at − 15 °C, it was significantly upregulated under other low-temperature conditions (Fig. S9A). A target site between miR408-5p and F01.PB27424 (bZIP) was predicted using psRNATarget (Fig. S9B). In view of our previous findings that bZIP genes were differentially expressed in response to temperature changes [26], we further screened the bZIP gene family.

Identification and characterization of MfbZIP family in M. falcata

A total of 41 MfbZIP genes were identified from the previously M. falcata transcriptome dataset and named MfbZIP1–41 based on the sequence ID (Table S11). The physicochemical properties of MfbZIP genes showed considerable variation in sequence length (109–591 amino acids), isoelectric point (pI; 4.93–9.89), and molecular weight (12.61–65.21 kDa), indicating that these genes are complete and of high confidence. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that the majority of MfbZIPs are primarily situated in the nucleus, with some also found in the mitochondria, extracellular space, and chloroplast (Table S12).

The phylogenetic tree showed the MfbZIPs are distributed across six subfamilies, with the V subfamily only containing the AtbZIP31 gene (Fig. 3). The maximum and minimum number of the MfbZIP genes were observed in the IV (12), and VII (1) subfamilies. Conserved motifs analysis showed the members of the MfbZIP in the IV subfamily contain a higher number of motifs, whereas those in the III and VII subfamilies have fewer motifs (Fig. S10 and Table S13). NCBI-CDD analysis revealed 11 distinct domains among the MfbZIPs, with all of them harboring the bZIP domain (Fig. S11).

Expression patterns of MfbZIPs under low-temperature stress

The expression levels of eight MfbZIPs were measured following low-temperature stress. MfbZIPs were expressed in the leaves, stems, and roots of M. falcata, showing varied expression patterns under cold stress (Fig. 4A–C). Most of the MfbZIPs were downregulated in the leaves but significantly upregulated in the roots and stems following low temperature treatments. The highest variations in MfbZIP expression levels were observed in stems, while leaves exhibited the lowest variations. All MfbZIP genes were upregulated in the roots, except for MfbZIP1 and MfbZIP6. However, only MfbZIP10 and MfbZIP21 were found to be significantly upregulated in stem and leaf, respectively, across most time points following low temperature stress. The expression of MfbZIPs in drought, salt, alkali stress, and ABA treatments showed similar trends across different treatments (Fig. S12). Notably, MfbZIP10 was significantly upregulated in stems across all treatments and could respond to low temperature.

Characteristics and subcellular localization of MfbZIP10

The MfbZIP10 coding sequence (GenBank accession: PQ497338) encodes a 274 amino acid protein with a molecular formula of C1286H2107N383O402S13, a molecular weight of 29.78 kDa and a pI of 9.34. MfbZIP10 exhibits an average hydrophilic coefficient of − 0.386, and an instability coefficient of 46.80, indicating its hydrophilic and unstable nature. The phylogenetic tree showed MfbZIP10 and MtbZIP cluster within the same branch, indicating a homologous relationship (Fig. S13A-B). Structural prediction showed MfbZIP10 consists of 14.96% α-helices, 3.28% β-turns, 17.52% extended strands, and 64.23% random coils (Fig. S13C).

Laser scanning confocal microscopy showed that MfbZIP10-GFP was distributed within the nucleus (Fig. 5A). The predicted subcellular localization of MfbZIP10 (Table S12) also confirms its primary localization in the nucleus.

Overexpression of MfbZIP10 reduces the low-temperature tolerance of transgenic plants

We obtained 3 MfbZIP10-OE lines and 5 bzip-OE lines. The lines with the highest expression levels from each group were selected for subsequent experiments. T3 transgenic Arabidopsis plants were generated, and the expression level of MfbZIP10 were analyzed (Fig. 5B). The MfbZIP10 transcript levels in the MfbZIP10-OE and the bzip-OE complemented lines were 158.6 and 13,860.3 times greater than those in WT, respectively. MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE plants were relatively smaller under normal conditions. Following exposure to cold stress for one week, the leaves of both MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE plants displayed severe defects, including purple discoloration (Fig. 5C). In contrast, WT and mutant plants showed milder purple discoloration and the leaves were distinctly bright green.

MfbZIP10 mRNAs can be cleaved by miR408-5p

We successfully engineered the pGreenII-62SK-pre-miR408-5p and pGreenII-62SK-MfbZIP10 vectors. Utilizing tobacco cells co-transfection and RLM-RACE, we identified miR408-5p can cleave MfbZIP10 transcripts in a transient assay. Two cleavage sites were identified at positions 14/13 and 10/9 of miR408-5p, corresponding to positions 71/72 and 75/76 of MfbZIP10, respectively (Fig. 6C). Additionally, we quantified the expression levels of miR408-5p in MfbZIP10-overexpressing lines. The findings revealed a significant reduction in miR408-5p expression in MfbZIP10-OE compared to WT under low-temperature stress (Fig. 6D).

Physiological measurements of transgenic plants under low-temperature treatment

To further understand the role of MfbZIP10 in low-temperature tolerance, physiological responses in MfbZIP10 transgenic plants subjected to low-temperature stress were studied (Fig. 7). MfbZIP10-OE and complemented lines exhibited increased proline concentrations under both normal and low-temperature condition due to MfbZIP10 overexpression. In contrast, soluble sugar (SS) content exhibited a significant decrease following low-temperature treatment in the MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE lines, while soluble protein (SP) and MDA content remained largely unchanged. Following low-temperature stress exposure, the MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE lines exhibited a significant increase in CAT, and POD activity, along with elevated O2·− and glutathione (GSH) content. The content of carotenoids, chlorophyll A, and B in the MfbZIP10-OE lines significantly increased following low-temperature treatment. Similarly, the carotenoid and chlorophyll A content in the bzip-OE line were higher than that of bzip mutant line under the same low temperature stress condition.

Quantitative expression analyses of associated genes under low-temperature treatment

According to the results of the physiological indicators, quantitative analyze of cold-related associated genes in MfbZIP10-OE strain were explored. The expression modifications of 11 associated genes showed distinct responses under low-temperature condition. The expression level of CAT1 was notably promoted in the MfbZIP10-OE line which was consistent with the results of the above physiological indicators, but SOD1 showed no change trend. It is worth noting that the expression levels of CBF1, CBF2, ICE1, and COR47 in MfbZIP10-OE line were extremely significantly elevated compared to the WT after low-temperature treatment. Whereas COR15 and AREB3 expression were not notably variable under low-temperature treatment, although significant differences were observed under normal conditions. The expression levels of TIP2, PIP1 and CAS1 in MfbZIP10-OE line were also significantly higher than those in WT under low-temperature condition.

Discussion

Conserved and species-specific features of miRNA responses to chilling and freezing stress

Plant miRNAs are a class of non-coding small RNAs of approximately 20–24 nt that typically mediate post-transcriptional regulation by directing target mRNA cleavage or translational repression, thereby contributing to plant adaptation to adverse environmental conditions [48]. Numerous studies have identified cold-responsive miRNAs in diverse plant species, including tomato [49], tea (Camellia sinensis) [50], wheat [51], rice (Oryza sativa) [52] and Arabidopsis [53], indicating that miRNA-mediated regulation is a common feature of low-temperature responses.

miRNA regulation of low temperature responses in M. falcata has remained largely unexplored. In this study, we identified 442 miRNAs, including 74 putative up-regulated and 37 putative down-regulated miRNAs responsive to all chilling and freezing treatments in M. falcata roots (Fig. 1). Chilling and freezing conditions are biologically distinct. Freezing damage causes the plant tissues to freeze and leads to the rupture of the cell walls. While chilling damage does not cause the plant tissues to freeze, and mainly causes growth and development disorders through physiological responses. Although there are biological differences between chilling and freezing, this study combined chilling and freezing damage and identified miRNAs that can respond simultaneously to chilling and freezing.

In this study, miRNA-seq analysis did not include biological replicates because three biological replicates were pooled into a single sequencing library. This may reduce the statistical robustness of differential expression analysis and compromise the reliability of FDR control. Therefore, we conducted additional independent validation to verify the qPCR analysis of miRNAs and their corresponding target genes (Fig. S8). Despite the limitations, we endeavored to demonstrate the accuracy and reliability of the data as much as possible. For comparison, 93 cold-responsive miRNAs have been reported in Arabidopsis [54], the relatively large number of cold-responsive miRNAs observed in M. falcata of this study may reflect a complex miRNA regulatory network that could contribute to its adaptation to cold environments.

miRNA modulates the stress resistance of M. falcata through target gene regulation

In our dataset, the expression level of miR408-5p continuously increased at 4 °C, 0 °C, -5 °C, and − 10 °C (Fig. S9). However, the expression of miR408-5p significantly decreased at -15 °C, likely due to severe freezing damage caused by this temperature to roots. miR408 has been regarded as a promising candidate for crop improvement because its overexpression can enhance plant growth, photosynthesis, and grain yield by downregulating the plant cyanine family gene UCLACYANIN 8 (UCL8) [54, 55]. Previous studies have indicated that miR408-5p acts as an important negative regulator in the auxin signaling pathway. In rice, it typically suppresses the translation of auxin/indoleacetic acid 30 (IAA30); however, when the target mRNA accumulates at high levels, miR408-5p can also mediate mRNA cleavage [56]. Moreover, miR393 and miR156 have been reported to act with miR408-5p in the rice auxin pathway to regulate leaf inclination. In addition, previous studies have indicated that miR408 is widely implicated in plant growth and development, as well as in responses to abiotic stresses [57].

RLM-RACE suggests that miR408-5p in M. falcata can target transcripts encoding the MfbZIP10 in a transient assay (Fig. 6C). bZIP transcription factors are known to play central roles in regulating plant growth, hormone signaling, and abiotic stress responses, including cold stress [58, 59]. The miR408-5p-bZIP regulatory module identified in M. falcata is a non-canonical pathway, which may be due to the species-specific regulation in M. falcata.

Based on the expression profiles observed in this study, bZIP transcription factors appear to be important components of the low-temperature response network in M. falcata. Therefore, the miR408-5p–MfbZIP10 regulatory module may represent a potential link between miRNA-mediated regulation and transcriptional reprogramming under low-temperature stress in M. falcata.

Stress responsiveness of MfbZIP genes in M. falcata

Building on the identification of 78 bZIP genes in Arabidopsis [11], we identified 41 bZIP transcription factor genes (MfbZIPs) in M. falcata and classified them into subfamilies analogous to those in Arabidopsis (Fig. 3). There are no MfbZIP members in the V subfamily, which might be the result of the evolutionary process of this species. Members within each MfbZIP subfamily shared conserved motif compositions and gene structures (Fig. S11), similar to bZIP family features reported in soybean [60]. These observations indicate that the MfbZIP family is evolutionarily conserved while still allowing potential functional diversification.

Expression profiling revealed that eight representative MfbZIP genes exhibited significant transcriptional changes under cold stress (Fig. 4). Consistent with the broad functional diversity of bZIP TFs in plants [61], the selected MfbZIPs displayed tissue-specific and stress-dependent expression patterns. Among them, MfbZIP10 showed particularly strong induction under low-temperature stress, especially in stems and roots. Subcellular localization analysis demonstrated that MfbZIP10 is a nuclear protein (Fig. 5A), in agreement with previous reports of nuclear localization for a closely related bZIP protein in M. truncatula [62]. This similarity is consistent with the close homology between MfbZIP10 and the M. truncatula bZIP protein (Fig. S13). Together, these findings support the notion that MfbZIP10 is a cold-responsive transcription factor that may participate in the regulatory network underlying M. falcata responses to low temperature.

Physiological responses of Arabidopsis overexpressing MfbZIP10 under cold stress

To gain functional insight into MfbZIP10, T₃ generation transgenic Arabidopsis lines were analyzed. Although there are limitations and species differences when expressing MfbZIP10 in Arabidopsis, heterologous expression in Arabidopsis can serve as a useful tool for addressing scientific questions in the absence of a mature genetic transformation system for M. falcata. Exposure to low temperatures is known to alter plant morphology, physiology and metabolism [5]. In our experiments, both MfbZIP10-OE and complemented bzip-OE lines displayed more pronounced growth inhibition and leaf discoloration than WT and the atbzip17 mutant under cold treatment (Fig. 5C). These phenotypes suggest that elevated MfbZIP10 expression is associated with increased sensitivity to prolonged cold stress [63].

ROS function as important signaling molecules in abiotic stress responses but can cause oxidative damage when excessively accumulated [64]. O₂·⁻ is a primary ROS that can trigger metabolic disturbances, while enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems normally keep ROS at manageable levels [65]. Under cold stress, MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE plants exhibited higher O₂·⁻ levels together with increased activities of the antioxidant enzymes CAT and POD (Fig. 7). These patterns are consistent with the visible damage in transgenic lines and may reflect stronger oxidative stress accompanied by compensatory activation of antioxidant defenses. SOD activity was significantly higher in bzip-OE than in the bzip mutant under cold stress, but this increase was not evident in the MfbZIP10-OE line, which may relate to differences in MfbZIP10 expression levels or genetic background.

The AsA–GSH cycle is a key redox pathway in ROS detoxification [66]. In our study, GSH content significantly increased after low-temperature treatment in both MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE lines, suggesting that high MfbZIP10 expression may influence cold responses, at least in part, by modulating the cellular redox state via the AsA–GSH pathway.

Cell membranes are primary targets of low-temperature and ROS-induced injury [67]. MDA, a product of membrane lipid peroxidation, is widely used as an indicator of membrane damage. In our experiment, MDA content did not show marked changes among genotypes, implying that MfbZIP10 overexpression did not markedly aggravate membrane lipid peroxidation under the tested conditions, even though visible damage was more severe in the overexpressors.

Cold stress also affects root activity and osmotic balance, leading to increased osmotic potential of extracellular fluids. Proline acts as a compatible osmolyte and can mitigate membrane lipid peroxidation by stabilizing proteins and membranes. After cold treatment, proline content significantly increased, whereas SS content significantly decreased in both MfbZIP10-OE and bzip-OE lines (Fig. 7). In addition, these lines showed notably higher levels of carotenoids and chlorophyll a and b than WT and the bzip mutant under cold conditions. These findings suggest that MfbZIP10 overexpression alters osmotic adjustment, antioxidant capacity and pigment accumulation under cold stress, and may thereby influence photosynthetic performance and overall cold stress responses. Nevertheless, direct measurements of photosynthetic efficiency would be required to substantiate this interpretation.

Examination of stress-responsive genes in Arabidopsis overexpression MfbZIP10 under low temperature

The ICE–CBF–COR pathway is a central regulatory module controlling plant cold tolerance [68]. The MYC-like bHLH transcription factor ICE1 activates expression of CBF1/DREB1, an ERF family TF, and in turn induces downstream COR genes to promote cold acclimation [69]. During the early phase of low-temperature exposure, CBF transcription factors are rapidly up-regulated, and subsequently activate COR genes such as COR15 and COR47 in Arabidopsis [70]. CBF transcription factors also contribute to the regulation of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism under cold stress [71]. Positioned downstream of the ICE–CBF cascade, COR proteins support cold tolerance by mediating a range of physiological and biochemical adjustments [2]. For example, CBF3 overexpression enhances COR15a expression, promotes SS accumulation and increases proline content via up-regulation of P5CS, thereby improving cold tolerance [72].

In MfbZIP10-OE plants, transcript levels of CBF1, CBF2 and ICE1 were elevated under cold stress, concomitant with increased proline content and significantly reduced SS content (Figs. 7 and 8). Although COR15 expression did not change markedly, COR47 expression was significantly higher in MfbZIP10-OE lines. This is undoubtedly surprising because higher CBF expression is usually associated with enhanced low temperature tolerance. These patterns suggest that MfbZIP10 overexpression affects components of the ICE–CBF–COR pathway and may enhance stress perception and signaling rather than simply improving protective regulation. The apparent discrepancy likely caused by ROS accumulation because the activity of antioxidant enzymes and related genes were all significantly upregulated (Figs. 7 and 8). This may be a kind of negative feedback regulation, and needs further in-depth exploration and research.

Aquaporins also form an important part of cold stress responses. Tonoplast intrinsic proteins (TIPs) facilitate water transport across the tonoplast and show development- and stress-dependent expression patterns in many species [73]. For instance, the cotton GhTIP1;1-like protein positively regulates cold tolerance when expressed in Arabidopsis [74]. In our study, overexpression of MfbZIP10 modified TIP2 expression under low temperature, suggesting that MfbZIP10 may influence cold responses partly by regulating membrane water transport. Plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs), a group of highly conserved aquaporins, are critical for mesophyll and stomatal CO₂ transport and thus for maintaining high photosynthetic rates [75]. MfbZIP10 overexpression in transgenic plants markedly enhanced PIP1 expression under cold stress (Fig. 8), suggesting that MfbZIP10 may also help adjust mesophyll and/or stomatal properties to sustain photosynthetic capacity under low temperature [76]. This hypothesis is consistent with the observed increases in carotenoid and chlorophyll contents (Fig. 7), although direct anatomical or gas-exchange measurements would be needed to confirm structural or functional changes.

In eukaryotes, sterols play multiple structural and signaling roles. In plants, phytosterol biosynthesis begins with the conversion of 2,3-oxidosqualene to cycloartenol, a reaction catalyzed by cycloartenol synthase 1 (CAS1). CAS1 is essential for sterol biosynthesis in Arabidopsis [77]. Under cold stress, MfbZIP10 overexpression significantly altered CAS1 expression, suggesting that MfbZIP10 may also influence cold responses by modulating sterol biosynthesis and thereby affecting membrane properties.

AREB/ABFs and DREB2A operate in ABA-dependent and ABA-independent signaling pathways, respectively [78]. In the ABA signaling pathway, AREB/ABFs act as key transcription factors downstream of the SRK2D/E/I kinases. bZIP68 has been reported to negatively regulate cold-induced expression of DREB1 in maize (Zea mays) [3], highlighting the link between bZIP TFs and ABA-related cold signaling. In our study, AREB3 expression did not show notable changes after low-temperature treatment, suggesting that MfbZIP10 may not primarily modulate cold responses through the classical ABA–AREB3 pathway. After cold exposure, SOD1 transcript levels remained relatively constant between WT and MfbZIP10-OE lines, whereas CAT1 expression varied in a manner consistent with changes in CAT activity. These results imply that CAT and POD, rather than SOD, are the major ROS-scavenging enzymes contributing to antioxidant defense in MfbZIP10-overexpressing lines under cold conditions in this study.

Finally, quantitative analysis revealed that MfbZIP10 overexpression was accompanied by reduced miR408-5p accumulation under cold stress (Fig. 6D), which may be an indirect effect, or it may be associated with the transgenic background or the severity of low-temperature injury. Together with the RLM-RACE results, these findings demonstrate that the miR408-5p–MfbZIP10 regulatory module is involved in the low-temperature response by coordinating transcriptional regulation, maintaining redox balance, and modulating physiological adjustment (Fig. 9).

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified 74 upregulated and 37 downregulated candidate miRNAs associated with the chilling and freezing response in M. falcata. RLM-RACE analyses revealed that miR408-5p can cleave MfbZIP10 transcripts in a transient assay. Physiological analyses, together with quantitative expression of miR408-5p, demonstrated that the miR408-5p–MfbZIP10 module plays a critical regulatory role in low-temperature adaptation. MfbZIP10 functions as a negative regulator and modulates the expression of genes involved in multiple signaling pathways under low-temperature stress. These findings underscore the regulatory importance of the miR408-5p–MfbZIP10 module in cold adaptation and provide potential targets for the genetic improvement of low-temperature tolerance in alfalfa.
