Quality in Australian Health Care Study 2: a national imperative for safer care
Authors: Peter D Hibbert, Johanna I Westbrook, Jeffrey Braithwaite
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe argue for and outline the design of a second Quality in Australian Health Care Study (QAHCS2), 30 years after the original landmark study. A narrative review of historical developments in Australian patient safety since the publication of QAHCS in 1995, supported by international comparisons and contemporary evidence, was undertaken. QAHCS demonstrated that 16.6% of hospital admissions were associated with patient harm (conceptualised as adverse events), establishing patient safety as a national priority. In the three decades since, extensive reforms and new techniques and technologies have reshaped care, governance and standards. However, we lack benchmarked, large‐scale, epidemiologically rigorous data on patient harm in contemporary Australian healthcare. QAHCS2, incorporating advances in digital records, automated adverse event detection and inclusion of primary care, would provide unique, actionable insights for Australia. QAHCS2 aims to determine whether care is safer now than in 1995, to evaluate national safety metrics and to benchmark Australia internationally. Without such evidence, healthcare improvement risks continuing without a reliable compass.Keywords: adverse events, medical record review, patient safety

Background: the Quality in Australian Health Care Study and its legacy

The original Quality in Australian Health Care Study (QAHCS), published in the Medical Journal of Australia in 1995, remains the most cited article in that journal's history. 1 It found that 16.6% of hospital admissions were associated with patient harm (adverse events), with about half felt to be preventable, placing patient safety firmly on the national agenda. 1 The Minister for Human Services and Health deemed the findings of such concern that she released the results of the QAHCS earlier than the project planned in Federal Parliament. 2 The results shocked clinicians, managers, policymakers, the media and the public, catalysing the establishment of new governance structures and activities that aimed to improve patient safety. 2

The QAHCS was established by the Federal Government as part of a review of medical professional indemnity. 3 The study was undertaken to identify the nature and incidence of adverse events, their causes and contributing factors, the levels of disability arising from them, and how preventable they were. 3 In echoes of our world today, the rationale in the professional indemnity review for the study was titled ‘myths, misinformation and getting to the truth’. A consortium involving the Universities of Newcastle and Adelaide was selected by tender to carry out the study. The QAHCS methodology, modelled on the Harvard Medical Practice Study (HMPS) in the United States, 4 , 5 involved structured two‐stage reviews of over 14 000 hospital medical records to assess whether patients suffered adverse events. 1

The Taskforce on Quality in Australian Health Care (1996) was convened in response to the release of the QAHCS results. It recommended 51 actions, including regular and consistent national monitoring of key aspects of the safety and quality of healthcare. 6 Subsequent milestones included establishing a National Expert Advisory Group (1998), the Australian Council for Safety and Quality in Health Care (2000), and, later, as the quality of care and patient safety field matured, the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC) in 2006, legislated in 2011. 7 The introduction of the National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards in 2013 and the creation of state‐based safety bodies such as the Clinical Excellence Commission (NSW) and Safer Care Victoria illustrate the enduring influence of QAHCS (Fig. 1).

What has changed in 30 years

Since 1995, healthcare delivery has been transformed. Patients in Australia's ageing population more commonly present with multimorbidity than single conditions. 8 Real expenditure on healthcare has increased to attempt to improve the quality of care and provide more effective treatments. 9 Care has increasingly shifted from hospitals to community and home settings. 10 Digitalisation and technology have reshaped diagnostics, prescribing and medical records. 11 Genomics and personalised medicine are revolutionising the prediction and prevention of diseases. 12 Safety initiatives such as rapid response systems 13 and surgical safety checklists 14 are now routine. Significant expenditures and organisational investments have been made in quality assurance with accreditation against national standards and electronic incident reporting and management. Governance structures balance corporate, financial and clinical metrics, with quality indicators publicly reported. 15

Despite these advances, Australia lacks reliable evidence on whether healthcare is safer today than it was in 1995. Patient safety data remain fragmented and often limited to narrow metrics, such as hospital‐acquired complications (HACs). Incident reporting systems, the most common monitoring tool, capture only a small fraction of harmful episodes; one review found that just 7% of adverse events detected by medical record review appeared in incident reports. 16 Since QAHCS, only three modest, cross‐sectional record review studies of adverse events have been undertaken in Australia – in paediatrics, 17 general practice 18 and paediatric intensive care. 19 By contrast, more than 60 such studies have been completed in Europe and North America. 20

Why QAHCS2 is needed

A second national study is overdue. Repeated calls over the past 30 years have sought to measure safety in Australian healthcare with methodological rigour. 6 , 21 , 22

Proposed methodology

QAHCS2 would involve large‐scale medical record reviews across a representative sample of hospitals and primary care facilities in multiple jurisdictions. The HPMS methodology, 25 together with adherence to newly published standards for adverse events studies, is still the most appropriate method to undertake large‐scale contemporary adverse events research. 25 , 26 Building on QAHCS (28 hospitals in two states), 1 QAHCS2 would:

Sample more broadly to reflect contemporary healthcare delivery – including metropolitan, regional and rural hospitals and community settings.

Combine manual and digital approaches – trained reviewers supported by electronic algorithms and trigger tools to reflect the availability and use of digital medical records.

Feasibility and research capacity

Australian researchers have undertaken large‐scale record review studies examining evidence‐based care in adults, 27 children 28 and aged care 29 cohorts at the scale required for QAHCS2, comprising more than 10 000 records across 388 facilities (the CareTrack studies). We now need a coalition of committed stakeholders to drive this important initiative forward with the researchers. This should include convening nationally representative advisory groups of policy makers such as the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care, the Australian Department of Health, Disability and Ageing, the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, State health departments, Primary Health Networks, as well as colleges, consumer groups such as the Consumers Health Forum, and state‐based groups, clinicians and clinical governance experts for a multi‐state, multi‐centre, multidisciplinary study.

Implications for policy and practice

QAHCS2 would provide policymakers, clinicians and patients with the most comprehensive assessment of safety in Australian healthcare since 1995. Its findings could guide resource allocation and safety initiatives based on epidemiologically robust data, validate or recalibrate HACs and other safety metrics, provide assurance (or warning) about progress in patient safety and reinforce Australia's role as a global leader in safety and quality research.

Conclusion

Thirty years after QAHCS, we still lack the evidence to say with confidence whether patient care in Australia is safer. QAHCS2 would fill this gap, provide essential benchmarking and guide the next generation of reforms. After 31 years, the time to act is now.
