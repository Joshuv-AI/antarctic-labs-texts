Navigating Privacy in Facial Transplantation: Ethical Considerations and Institutional Strategies
Authors: Hailey P. Wyatt, Alexis K. Gursky, Sachin R. Chinta, Alay R. Shah, Bruce E. Gelb, Eduardo D. Rodriguez, Laura L. Kimberly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:During the last 20 years, the novel and highly visible nature of facial transplantation (FT) has posed a dual and potentially conflicting obligation for FT programs: to disseminate findings for scientific advancement while respecting recipients’ preferences for privacy, given the significant public and media attention. The individually identifying nature of FT is not present in other forms of vascularized composite allotransplantation, making it unique. Despite extensive ethical discourse on FT, implications for privacy are largely absent from ethical analyses.Methods:This conceptual analysis assesses the complex ethical implications associated with privacy in FT and offers guidance to FT programs for navigating concerns related to donors, recipients, and their families. A literature review was performed and supplemented by our institutional experience.Results:Ethical approaches to privacy require balancing the conflicting ethical principles at the center of this dual obligation. Patient-centered strategies should include comprehensive psychological screening to understand patients’ goals for care, informed consent that clearly addresses the dissemination of information and media involvement, and control of media coverage until the recipient is ready and willing. Public relations support for recipients has been helpful in our institution’s experience. Additional community-based strategies include educational outreach interventions to satisfy public curiosity.Conclusions:Findings from published reports alongside our institution’s experience suggest that a nuanced, multifaceted approach is essential to navigate the ethical complexities of privacy in FT. Aligning key stakeholders to support FT recipients’ well-being while maintaining scientific integrity reflects the ethical tension between duties to patients and obligations to advance transplant science.

Takeaways

Question: How should facial transplantation programs navigate the ethical tension between advancing science through dissemination and protecting recipients’ privacy in the face of heightened public and media attention?

Findings: A conceptual analysis and institutional experience highlight strategies including thorough psychological screening, explicit informed consent on media involvement, delaying public disclosure until the recipient is ready, providing public relations support, and community education to manage curiosity.

Meaning: A nuanced, multifaceted approach is essential to protect facial transplantation recipients’ privacy while maintaining scientific integrity, balancing patient-centered care with obligations to advance transplant science.

INTRODUCTION

The field of facial reconstructive surgery has been profoundly transformed by the advent of facial transplantation (FT), first performed in 2005; approximately 50 face transplants have now been completed worldwide.1,2 Unlike any other organ or limb, the face is uniquely tied to personal identity, recognition, social interaction, and communication, serving not only expressive functions such as speaking and emoting, but also essential physiological functions such as breathing, eating, and blinking. For patients with severe facial disfigurement, restoration of both appearance and function leads to improved psychosocial health and enhanced quality of life.3–8

Despite these remarkable strides, the field still faces significant challenges, particularly concerning limited long-term patient-reported outcomes, aesthetic and functional trajectories, and the evolution of immunosuppression strategies.8 Aggregating data from robust clinical research is also difficult due to the low volume of cases performed and is further complicated by the individualized nature of each procedure. These gaps emphasize the scientific and ethical imperative to continue research and to transparently disseminate findings to continue advancing the field.

At the same time, the protection of patient privacy in FT remains a central ethical concern, grounded in the principles of autonomy and nonmaleficence.9,10 The impetus for continued protection of patient privacy involves understanding and respecting patient preferences, ensuring that potential risks are minimized, while also promoting the fundamental goals of social reintegration and improved quality of life.

Accordingly, the field of FT faces a dual and often conflicting obligation: to advance scientific knowledge through scholarly publication and public discourse, while also protecting the privacy of recipients who, by virtue of the procedure’s visibility and novelty, are especially vulnerable to public attention (Fig. 1). This analysis explores the interactions of this ethical tension in FT and draws on our institution’s experience to propose strategies to effectively navigate this dilemma.

SCIENTIFIC AND ETHICAL IMPERATIVE TO DISSEMINATE INFORMATION

The Duty to Share Knowledge

Disseminating research findings is both a professional responsibility and an ethical imperative grounded in the principles of human subjects research. Both the Belmont Report and the Declaration of Helsinki emphasize beneficence as a core ethical principle, obligating researchers to maximize benefits and minimize harm.9,10 This duty includes ensuring that knowledge generated through research contributes meaningfully to society. The Declaration of Helsinki explicitly states that results from research studies involving human participants must be made publicly available, reinforcing the ethical necessity of sharing findings, which is especially important in innovative and ethically complex fields such as FT. Dissemination of research findings, in this context, is not optional; it is central to upholding the trust and welfare of both participants and the broader public.

Dissemination of scientific discovery is essential to scientific advancement. Moreover, dissemination can enhance the impact of scientific research, which depends heavily on how effectively it is communicated. Using a range of dissemination modes, including peer-reviewed publication, media releases, collaborative conferences, and visual media, broadens the reach and relevance of findings.11 These dissemination strategies not only have scientific implications but can also help to shape public perception, influence health equity, and garner necessary funding to support the research enterprise.12–15 This is especially relevant in the context of FT, where scientific advancement, public understanding, ethical discourse, and continued funding are essential.

The principles at the core of the Hippocratic Oath that all physicians take, beneficence and nonmaleficence, are considered “prima facie” obligations or duties that must be fulfilled unless they conflict with another principle within a specific context.16,17 For FT, the act of sharing scientifically complete findings potentially conflicts with the principle of autonomy and could inadvertently cause harm if a patient values privacy and seeks to maintain anonymity. Withholding clinically significant insights to protect privacy, especially in domains such as FT, may constitute a failure to uphold beneficence toward the public, current transplant recipients, and future recipients.

Scarcity, Individualization, and Innovation

FT represents a unique subarea within the field of transplantation. In solid organ transplantation (SOT), which has been the standard of care for end-stage organ dysfunction, there is exponentially greater demand and higher surgical volume for procedures such as kidney transplantation. In 2024 alone, more than 27,000 kidney transplants were performed in the United States, contributing to a vast, continually expanding repository of de-identified clinical data.18 By contrast, only 50 FT procedures have been performed worldwide during the past 2 decades.1,14 As of September 2023, FT data became available in the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network database, a major milestone in systematically reporting and capturing aggregate data for future use. However, due to limited demand, complex candidacy criteria, and the small number of FT programs, FT will likely never achieve the volume seen in SOT, and therefore, it will not be possible to publish large-scale de-identified cohort data.13

Furthermore, the technical aspects of each FT procedure are highly individualized, necessitating publication of case reports and case series that include recipient-specific details with increased granularity.19–21 For example, in 2023, our institution performed a FT that included the world’s first-in-human whole-eye transplantation, resulting in the need for a patient-specific report sharing surgical, immunologic, and clinical details.19

One of the most crucial sources of outcome data for these procedures is medical photography of the allograft. Photographs provide the most accurate representation of the allograft’s aesthetic evolution and functional condition, conveying essential data that written descriptions or measurements alone cannot capture. Standard masking techniques are insufficient to protect recipient privacy; effectively deidentifying facial photographs requires leaving out significant data and compromising transparent reporting.22 The necessity of photographic documentation must therefore be carefully balanced with the ethical obligation to protect patients through comprehensive informed consent.23 Patients must be fully aware of the lifelong implications of publishing identifiable images and the tension between advancing science and preserving personal privacy.24

The dissemination of FT outcomes is not merely an academic exercise; it is essential to progress in a field that can vastly improve the quality of life for patients. Prior reports have played a pivotal role in refining clinical protocols and improving patient outcomes.8,25,26 Transparency and consistent reporting across FT centers have driven advances in surgical technique, immunosuppressive strategies, and functional as well as psychosocial evaluation and reporting.21 Each new case adds a data point to a field still in its formative stages, and case reports can be foundational for developing and revising standards of care.2,19–21

The Role of the Media in Scientific Innovation

In the current climate, the media plays a powerful and multifaceted role in the advancement of scientific innovation. In the case of FT, media coverage has been instrumental in raising public awareness, humanizing the procedure, and generating broader support for its development. The media has been argued to be a central component of medical innovation.5,27,28 By showcasing transformative patient stories, the media can inspire societal interest, stimulate funding, and influence public policy.29–31

However, this visibility also introduces ethical tensions. Media portrayals often emphasize dramatic physical outcomes, sometimes at the expense of nuanced discussions about long-term medical risks, psychosocial challenges, and ethical considerations. These oversimplified narratives may contribute to misinformation or unrealistic expectations among patients and the public.32,33

Despite these challenges, media coverage can serve as a valuable complement to peer-reviewed literature when approached responsibly. Media messaging can broaden the reach of innovation, spark interdisciplinary discourse, and place pressure on regulatory bodies to adapt to emerging technologies.34,35 For a complex and evolving field such as FT, a responsible partnership between scientific reporting and public communication is essential to maintaining momentum and trust.

The Cost of Inadequate Dissemination

A lack of adequate and complete sharing of information in FT poses significant risks, including stagnation of scientific progress, poor generalizability of outcomes, and the formation of institutional silos.24 Although the field of FT has had considerable growth, critical clinical challenges such as acute and chronic rejection, allograft surveillance, and emerging complications such as malignancy or lymphoproliferative disorders persist.14 Additionally, the body of peer-reviewed publications regarding FT is relatively small compared with SOT and often experiences lag in dissemination. In contrast, there are a larger number of media reports surrounding each FT recipient case. As Sosin and Rodriguez14 suggested, media coverage often outpaces academic reporting partly due to efforts to preserve patient anonymity and privacy. Ultimately, significant gaps in the literature regarding long-term patient follow-up and outcomes, as well as patient-reported outcomes, exist.13,36 This further emphasizes that dissemination of more peer-reviewed research is essential and should occur in tandem with exploring ways to honor and preserve patient privacy. Without consistent sharing of outcomes, both successes and setbacks, the field risks repeating mistakes, missing opportunities for innovation, and failing to provide current and future FT recipients with accurate counseling and care guidance.

THE ETHICAL OBLIGATION TO PROTECT PRIVACY

Respect for Persons and Confidentiality

Respect for persons is a core ethical principle rooted in autonomy and the rights of individuals to make choices about their care. The Belmont Report emphasizes this through the responsibilities of honoring individuals’ decision-making capacity to engage in informed consent. In FT, where patients face a highly experimental, irreversible, and visible procedure, this principle necessitates an especially rigorous approach to addressing confidentiality in the informed consent process. The Declaration of Helsinki reinforces this obligation, requiring that patients be fully informed about the risks, benefits, and uncertainties involved in any medical research. Given the evolving nature of FT, respect for persons extends beyond initial consent; it involves a sustained ethical commitment to transparency, privacy, and the patient’s right to make ongoing, informed decisions about their care.9,10

Informed consent is a process of information sharing, discussion, opportunity for questions and answers between the patient and the team, reflection, and ensuring comprehension before consent is formally given to move forward with a procedure. Communicating the complexities of surgical innovation can be challenging.37 Zahra et al37 noted that surgeons feel patients often perceive “new” procedures as inherently superior, and aligning expectations can be difficult, especially when those expectations reflect the surgeons’ own enthusiasm for advancement. This positivity bias makes truly informed consent both ethically critical and logistically complex. In FT, consent is multifaceted in that patients must provide consent to participate in medical and surgical innovation, which includes publication and media dissemination of identifying information and photographs. Patients may strongly oppose sharing personal information and may wish to maintain complete anonymity. In such cases, dissemination of information could either be significantly hindered, or patients may choose not to participate in the study altogether. These factors place immense ethical weight on the clinicians to ensure patients understand not only the medical aspects of the procedure but also the broader implications of public exposure and lifelong monitoring.

Visibility Paradox

Candidates for FT have severe facial disfigurement, which is inherently visible and often subjects individuals to heightened public attention and a profound loss of privacy due to their outward differences. As a result, many individuals with severe facial injuries withdraw from public life, avoiding social spaces and even close relationships to escape unwanted stares, questions, or attention.38,39 FT can help reverse this isolation, restoring both form and function to facilitate social reintegration and improve quality of life.4 However, because FT is rare, innovative, and symbolically powerful, it attracts significant attention from both the scientific community and the lay public. This widespread interest, although reflective of the procedure’s importance and relative novelty, can paradoxically increase the patient’s social visibility after transplantation, whereas a patient’s goal might be to blend in socially. In this way, individuals may progress from social isolation due to social stigma to being highly visible as representatives of a pioneering medical achievement. This raises important ethical questions about identity, autonomy, and the challenges of reintegration.

Consequences of Overexposure

Although visibility can promote awareness and advance scientific understanding, overexposure carries significant risks for FT recipients. Media attention, especially when uninvited, can perpetuate stigma, contribute to psychological distress, and strip patients of control over how their stories are told.33 Changes in facial appearance and identity following transplantation may already challenge a patient’s self-perception and social reintegration; when compounded by public scrutiny, these shifts can increase emotional vulnerability.40 For some, the exposure may reopen feelings of trauma, invite intrusive commentary, or reduce their identity to a medical case rather than a whole person.

Navigating Fame: Benefit or Burden

The public visibility that often follows FT can be a double-edged sword. Some patients choose to embrace a public role, using their experiences to advocate for others, raise awareness, or advance research.41 For them, sharing their journey can be empowering and purposeful. However, others may feel overwhelmed by the attention or misrepresented by media narratives that emphasize spectacle over substance.33 This divergence highlights the need for personalized support and a patient-centered approach to communication, ensuring that individuals retain agency over how and whether their stories are shared.

PARTIES AND THEIR RESPECTIVE ROLES: INSTITUTIONS, SURGEONS, AND PATIENTS

Successfully navigating these ethical complexities in FT requires coordinated efforts among institutions, surgical teams, and patients. Institutions housing vascularized composite allotransplantation programs are responsible for establishing and maintaining robust, institutional review board–approved protocols that address both the clinical and ethical dimensions of care.42 These include defining appropriate publication pathways that balance transparency with patient privacy, as well as developing dissemination strategies that ensure meaningful impact without compromising individual rights.34 Institutions may also consider implementing internal privacy protections. One such example is through password protection for sensitive patient charts. This approach is commonly used in electronic health record systems across the United States for patients with sensitive conditions such as behavioral health or substance abuse diagnoses, and for public figures or hospital employees, to reduce unauthorized access to patient records and to minimize the risk of inappropriate chart reviews, particularly given heightened curiosity.

Surgeons play a critical role in upholding these ethical standards. They are responsible for ensuring a rigorous and patient-centered informed consent process, one that provides adequate time and support for individuals to fully understand the magnitude and risks of the procedure and potential for public exposure. Surgeons must also carefully balance their professional drive for innovation and scientific contribution for the greater good with a deep respect for the autonomy and well-being of their patients. Additionally, as public interest often gravitates toward surgical pioneers and their patients, surgeons may find themselves in the position of managing media attention. In such cases, they must help shield patients from undue pressure or exposure, and when appropriate, redirect public focus toward broader institutional or scientific achievements.

At the heart of this approach are FT patients and families, whose voices and preferences must guide all aspects of care and disclosure. They should be active participants in discussions about media engagement, privacy protections, and the use of their data in research or publication. Their individual boundaries and comfort levels must be respected throughout the continuum of care, from preoperative counseling to postoperative life in the public eye. Ensuring that patients remain at the center of decision-making is essential to honoring the ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, and respect for persons.

INSTITUTIONAL STRATEGIES

As illustrated in Figure 2, our institutional strategies can be conceptualized as an interdependent system. At the heart of this approach is the commitment to ethical stewardship, supported by 6 key components: psychosocial evaluation, patient values and goals of care, informed consent, controlled media coverage, educational outreach, and public relations support.

Conceptual framework for visualizing our institution’s approach to balancing essential elements of privacy and dissemination to reduce ethical tensions in FT.

Psychosocial evaluation plays a key role in this process, assessing the patient’s viewpoints and preferences regarding public exposure, including publication and media involvement. Patients are advised to have open discussions with their families to clarify these preferences, and they retain the right to permit or restrict the release of identifying images at any time, both pre- and postoperatively. Participation in the vascularized composite allotransplantation program is never contingent upon consent for publication or publicity, and patients are informed when initial publications or press releases involving images or identifying information are being prepared.

The informed consent process includes discussion with the surgical team to clearly delineate how the patient’s information will be shared through scientific publications and institutional media. Separately, a senior director of clinical media relations obtains separate informed consent from the patient and family. As part of this process, patient photography is trademarked by the institution to safeguard against unintentional spread. As public interest in FT grows, controlled media coverage and public relations support become vital tools for safeguarding patient identity and managing external communications. These efforts must be proactive, coordinated, and transparent.

As public interest in FT continues to grow, often marked by measurable increases in search engine traffic following high-profile cases, controlled media coverage and coordinated public relations efforts become essential. Our institutional strategies involve selectively inviting trusted media outlets that collaborate with public relations teams to maintain high ethical standards. Patients are encouraged to use institutional media relations teams to help navigate publicity and media requests, ensuring their comfort and preferences remain central. Despite these efforts to communicate accurate and objective information to the media, not all media reporting will be balanced, which is beyond the control of both the surgeons and institutions performing these procedures.

Educational outreach plays a dual role, not only fostering public and professional understanding of FT but also helping to redirect attention away from individual recipients. Producing and sharing accurate, accessible educational materials such as publicly available surgical animations (as developed by New York University) can satisfy public interest while protecting patient anonymity. Such educational content can reduce inappropriate public curiosity or attempts to identify recipients by providing a reliable and informative resource for those seeking to learn more about the procedure. Additionally, given the rise of artificial intelligence, there may be a role for artificial intelligence in creating these animations, as well as in providing an additional layer of privacy or anonymity.

Taken together, these institutional strategies create an ethical infrastructure that supports both the advancement of FT and the protection of the individuals at its center. A nuanced, multifaceted approach is essential to navigate the ethical complexities of privacy in FT, with the goals of aligning all stakeholders to support patient well-being and scientific integrity.

CONCLUSIONS

FT presents a dual obligation: to advance science through innovation and data sharing while simultaneously safeguarding patient autonomy, dignity, and privacy. Prioritizing one obligation at the expense of the other can lead to unintended harm, whether through stalled scientific progress or violations of individual privacy. Therefore, a model of shared responsibility among institutions, clinicians, and patients is essential to navigating the ethical complexities of this evolving field.

Institutional approaches must support transparency, protect confidentiality, and facilitate patient-centered communication at every stage. As FT transitions toward standard care, continued dialogue across ethical, surgical, and patient communities will be critical. Only through this ongoing engagement can the field responsibly realize FT’s full potential scientifically, socially, and ethically.
