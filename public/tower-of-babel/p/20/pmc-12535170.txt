Prevalence, radio-clinical patterns and factors associated with pulmonary tuberculosis among children with severe acute malnutrition: a cross-sectional study in Uganda
Authors: Oliver Asiimwe, Grace Ndezi, Joshua Muhumuza, Faith Namukasa, Charles Mwendwa Mutisya, Salad Hassan Abdirahman, Ali Muhuyadin haji Abdifatah, Omar Mohamed Abdirizak, Said Ibrahim Amatarahman, Martin Nduwimana

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a contagious, infectious disease, due to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MT), that results in the formation of tubercles in different parts of the body [1]. Both TB and severe acute malnutrition (SAM), particularly in settings with low resources and for children under the age of five, are significant causes of mortality [2]. Literature from other countries has shown that the occurrence of malnutrition is significantly associated with tuberculosis [2]. Undernutrition increases the risk of TB, and TB can cause or worsen under nutrition. One study estimated that 26% of all TB cases in 22 high-burden countries were attributed to under nutrition [3].

Due to nonspecific symptoms, signs, and radiological abnormalities as well as the difficulties in achieving a conclusive microbiologic diagnosis, pulmonary tuberculosis in children can be difficult to diagnose [4]. Even worse, lethargy and the blunting of symptoms in SAM-affected children make diagnosis more challenging [2]. Further research is required to: better understand the epidemiologic links between child pulmonary tuberculosis and SAM; improve case finding of tuberculosis in children with SAM and assess special treatment considerations for tuberculosis when children also have SAM in order to achieve the World Health Organization’s goal of eliminating deaths from childhood tuberculosis and improve outcomes of children with SAM [2].

Globally, pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) is a significant public health concern [5]. It comes up number four among the deadliest infectious diseases [6]. In the 2023, 10,800,000 suffered from tuberculosis worldwide, 1,300,000 of whom were children [7]. In the same year, 1,250,000 died from tuberculosis [7]. According to the 2023 TB report, 80% of the cases and mortalities arising from tuberculosis occurred in low- and middle-income countries.

According to estimates, under-nutrition accounts for 45% of fatalities among children below the age of five in the world [8]. Malnutrition is still one of the top causes of death in children under the age of five, and 26.9% of those children are from Sub-Saharan Africa [8]. Between 4% and 10% of Ugandan children aged 6 to 59 months are acutely malnourished [8].

With recent requests to concentrate on malnourished children for focused TB case detection efforts, there is rising acknowledgement of malnourished children as a significant high-risk category for TB [2]. In its 2018 Roadmap towards eliminating TB in children and adolescents, the World Health Organization (WHO) stressed the link between malnutrition and child TB [9]. Despite wide variation within and between nations, Africa and Asia have the highest rates of SAM and TB in children [2].

Results from studies among children with SAM show a large variation in the prevalence of TB disease, ranging from 0.4% with microbiologic diagnosis at a tertiary hospital in Zambia [2], to 44% starting TB treatment at a tertiary hospital in Malawi [10]. Less than half of the TB patients discovered on autopsy were diagnosed prior to death, and 80% of autopsy-confirmed TB cases had SAM, according to a post-mortem autopsy study of children under 15 years of age with in-hospital mortality at Zambia’s national referral facility [11].

Multiple studies have reported increased risk of mortality among children with SAM that were diagnosed with TB [2, 10]. Literature from other countries has shown that the occurrence of malnutrition is significantly associated with tuberculosis, but there is a paucity of data about this subject in Uganda [2], yet throughout the past 15 years, Uganda’s rate of malnutrition reduction has been quite very slow [8].

According to Kizito et al. [5], tuberculosis among children is underdiagnosed and undertreated in Uganda, even much more in children with severe acute malnutrition where the diagnosis is masked by the reduced immunity. Though many studies have been published elsewhere about tuberculosis (TB) in children with severe acute malunion (SAM), the methods used to diagnose tuberculosis have varied widely and hence the prevalences reported have also varied very widely. In the presence of such wide variations, it is necessary to have studies done in our setting to improve care and outcomes of the children that present with malnutrition; the reason this study was done. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, describe the radio-clinical patterns and factors associated with pulmonary tuberculosis among children aged 6 to 59 months admitted with severe acute malnutrition at Jinja Regional Referral Hospital (JRRH).

Methods

Study design and period

This was a cross-sectional study conducted for a period of three months (22nd Novemebr 2023-22nd February 2024) at Jinja Regional Referral Hospital (JRRH).

Study setting

The study was carried out at JRRH one of the regional referral health facilities in Uganda. JRRH serves as a public referral hospital for the Eastern region Uganda but also receiving patients from the neighboring Kenya border districts. The hospital has functional radiology and laboratory departments with capacity to diagnose pulmonary tuberculosis. The Nutritional unit is located in Nalufenya about 1 km from the main hospital with a bed capacity of 32 and it’s run by 2 pediatricians, senior housing officers, 1 nutritionist and 3 nurses. The malnutrition unit on average receives a total of 50 children with SAM every month.

Study population

All children aged 6 to 59 months with severe acute malnutrition in eastern Uganda were targeted, though only those admitted at JRRH during the study period were accessible. Children aged 6 to 59 months admitted with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) whose parents/next of kin consented for participation were included. Children in whom nasogastric tube insertion was contraindicated such as those with nasopharyngeal or esophageal obstruction were excluded because gastric aspirates were required to examine for mycobacteria.

Sample size and sampling

The sample size was determined using the Epi-info online sample size calculator [12]. Considering a history of contact with a TB patient increasing the odds of TB by 3.58 among patients with SAM in Eastern, Ethiopia [13], with a power of 80% and 95% level of confidence, considering the percent of un-exposed with outcome to be 15 according to [13], the sample size required was 124. Adding 10% to cater for non-respondents, the sample size required was 137 study participants. Participants were consecutively enrolled until the desired sample size was achieved.

Recruitment and data collection procedure

Children admitted to JRRH were assessed for severe acute malnutrition by taking the weight, height or length, MUAC and checking the corresponding z score. This was done at the time of admission. Those children with z scores less than − 3SD, MUAC < 11.5 cm or edematous were considered to have severe acute malnutrition (SAM). Those found to have SAM were considered eligible for the study. Detailed clinical history was obtained from the caregivers and recorded.

The children had full physical examination (general and systemic) done, comorbidities identified, and clinical features suggestive of TB identified. The specific investigations done included; complete blood count, GeneXpert, urine Lipoarabinomannan Antigen Assay for TB (TB-LAM) and chest radiography. HIV serology was done on participants above 18 months of age and on the mothers for the participants below 18 months. Chest X-ray was done for all children who presented with cough and difficulty in breathing. A participant was considered to have Tuberculosis if GeneXpert or urine Lipoarabinomannan Antigen Assay for Tuberculosis (TB-LAM) was positive, or if they had clinically diagnosed Tuberculosis.

Clinical tuberculosis diagnosis in HIV-negative children required the presence of at least two of the following: physical symptoms and signs suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis (persistent cough (>2 weeks), persistent fever (>1 week), persistent unexplained lethargy or reduced playfulness and weight loss or failure to thrive), a chest X-ray compatible with pulmonary tuberculosis (such as cavitation, lymphadenopathy, infiltrates, pleural effusion or a miliary pattern), and a history of patient exposure to tuberculosis. At least one of the following had to be present in infants with HIV before a diagnosis of tuberculosis could be made: clinical symptoms or a CXR suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis or contact with a smear- or GeneXpert positive Tuberculosis patient [6].

Specimen collection

The parent or guardian was informed that the child would have a sample for testing taken early in the morning before the child took the early morning feed. A nasal gastric tube was passed and 5–10 mls of gastric content aspirated. The aspirated gastric content was placed in a sterile sputum container. The sterile sputum container was labelled with the participant code number. The primary sputum container was wrapped with absorbent materials (cotton), placed in a secondary plastic bag and a third plastic zip bag (triple package). Collected samples were transported within one hour in a biohazard bag labelled infectious to the Microbiology laboratory at JRRH [14].

GeneXpert (PCR)

After receiving the sample, the volume of the sample was checked by the lab technician. Double dilution with the diluent was done using a calibrated container. The sample was shaken to mix. Incubation was done at room temperature for 10 min and shaken again and incubated again for 5 munities. A check was made to see if the solution had liquefied. The sample was loaded in the Gene Xpert cartridge and the cartridge loaded into the 2021 Cepheid molecular analyzer gene expert XVI. The samples ran for 25 min till the machine notified availability of the results.

TB Lam

A urine sample was also taken for TB LAM in children who had CD4 below 200. The strip was opened and labeled. Using a Pasteur pipette, 2 drops of urine were placed on the pad. Results were read after 15–20 min. Two bars (one on patient side and one on control line) represented a positive result and one bar (only on the control line) represented a negative result.

X-Ray

The chest X-ray was done for all the children that presented with cough and difficulty in breathing. Chest x-ray was done in the anteroposterior direction. The principal investigator underwent refresher training on identification of radiological findings suggestive of TB before the start of data collection. The principal investigator read the Xray films with the help of the Jinja Regional Referral Hospital radiologist to minimize the chances of misinterpreting the Xray findings. The radiological findings that were suggestive of TB included: cavitation, lymphadenopathy, infiltrates, pleural effusion or a miliary pattern.

Complete blood count

Automated coulter counter (BC 3000 Plus Mindray Auto Haematology Analyser, Shenzhen, China) determined the complete blood count. The researcher obtained 2mls of venous blood sample from each child after cleaning the area with an alcohol swab. The blood was transferred to a vacutainer containing EDTA with a dilution effect of 1–2% to prevent clotting before using the automated coulter counter. Anaemia status was defined as recommended by WHO; Hb < 11.0 g/dl for age 6 to 59months. Thromocytopenia was defined as platelet count < 150,000/microliter, thrombocytosis as > 450,000/microliter. Leukocytosis was defined as > 19,000 cells/mm3 for children below 2 years and > 11,000 cells/mm3 for children above 2 years while leucocytopenia was defined as < 5,000 cels/mm3.

Study variables

In this study, the dependent variable was the presence of tuberculosis. The independent variables included: Child’s age and sex, parents/guardians’ address, age, occupation and education level and as well as the child’s immunization status, vital signs, HIV status, type of SAM and laboratory findings.

Quality control

To guarantee proper use of data collection instruments and adherence to ethical principles, the principal investigator appropriately trained and routinely monitored the research assistants. Throughout data collection, the lead investigator regularly crosschecked the questionnaires and fixed mistakes when found. The machines for laboratory tests were calibrated routinely according to the manufacturer’s manual. The weight scale was standardized daily by taking the weight of a known object. The weight and heights were measured two times and the average taken.

Data analysis

Microsoft Office Excel was used to create an excel sheet with a summary of the data collected and then exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 26. The percentage of children diagnosed with tuberculosis was computed as a fraction of all the children enrolled in the study. Descriptive statistics were used in which frequency and percentage of children with the different radio-clinical patterns were computed. Bivariate and multivariate analysis was done using binary logistic regression reporting both odds ratios and P values. Variables which were thought to be biologically plausible in line with previously published literature were included in the bivariate analysis [15]. Variables with P value < 0.2 at bivariate were re-analyzed at multivariate. Variables with P < 0.05 were considered significant factors associated with tuberculosis among children with SAM. The adjusted odds ratios and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals were documented as well.

Ethical considerations and consent

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was granted by the Research and Ethics Committee of Mulago Hospital (Ref No: MHREC 2635). All participants gave written informed consent as evidenced by the guardian’s signature.

Results

One hundred and sixty-six children were screened, of which 140 were eligible. Of the 140 eligible participants, three parents declined to consent. The remaining 137 were enrolled. In this study that enrolled 137 children with severe acute malnutrition, majority were in the age categories of 6–11 (35.5%) and 12–23 (46.0%) months and majority were males 81(59.1%). Only 77(56.2%) had up-to-date immunization. Majority presented with a fever 112(81.8%) or cough 93(67.9%). The commonest type of malnutrition was non edematous 73(53.3%). Of the 137 participants, 32 were diagnosed with TB representing a prevalence of 23.4%. Of the 32 diagnosed with TB, only 5 had a positive gene expert test, 2 had a positive urine TB LAM and the remaining 25 were diagnosed using clinical criteria. Of those diagnosed clinically, only 2/25(8.0%) had the combination of cough, fever, weight loss and a positive chest Xray (Table 1).

Discussion

Regarding the prevalence of Tuberculosis among children aged 6 months to 59 months admitted with Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) at Jinja Regional Referral Hospital and we found the prevalence to be 23.4%. This prevalence was high. The high prevalence can be attributed to the big proportion of participants whose immunization status was not up to date, since the BCG vaccine offers protection against TB [6, 16]. Also, the fact that majority of the participants had a clinical diagnosis (25/32), makes it possible that some of the participants considered to have TB could have been falsely classified. This is because we did not do any investigations to confirm bacterial pneumonia and therefore some of the patients clinically diagnosed with TB could have had bacterial pneumonia.

Our prevalence was higher than that reported in India (13%) [17], in Nepal (4.67%) [18], in Ethiopia (10.39%) [13] and in Congo (8.4%) [19], but slightly lower than that reported in South Africa (25.6%) [20]. The differences in the prevalence of tuberculosis can be attributed to the study population differences, the burden of tuberculosis varies geographically, and the criteria used for diagnosis of tuberculosis in these studies. For example, in our study, the clinical criteria, TB-LAM and Gene x-pert were used to diagnose TB which could have resulted in a higher prevalence. The study in South Africa that reported a prevalence of 25.6% (almost similar to ours but slightly higher) also used the clinical criteria for diagnosis.

The radiological manifestations of pulmonary tuberculosis in children exhibit variations across different populations and studies. Common findings include consolidation, hilar/mediastinal adenopathy, and pleural effusion. However, there are notable differences in the distribution of these abnormalities, as highlighted by the findings from this study compared to others [21–23].

The distribution of consolidation and hilar/mediastinal adenopathy aligns with the typical presentation of pulmonary tuberculosis in children [21, 23]. These findings serve as important diagnostic markers, aiding in the identification and management of the disease. However, the absence of cavitary lesions in the current study suggests a potentially less severe disease course compared to findings from other studies where cavitary lesions were more prevalent [24].

The predominance of right unilateral lesions in the abnormal radiographs observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports, indicating a propensity for TB to affect the right lung more [23]. However, the distribution of lesions may vary among populations, as evidenced by differences in lesion laterality across studies.

The distribution of radiological findings observed in this study contrasts with those reported by Hassanzad et al. [24] and findings from Brieflands [25]. While our study found no cavitary lesions (0%), both Hassanzad et al. and Brieflands reported the presence of cavitary lesions in a subset of children with pulmonary tuberculosis.

According to Brieflands, cavitary pulmonary tuberculosis typically seen in adults, is rare in children, which aligns with our findings of no cavitary lesions observed in the studied population. However, the presence of cavitary lesions in other studies indicates a higher mycobacterium load, serving as a potential source of infection [25]. This discrepancy underscores the variability in disease presentation across different populations and highlights the importance of considering regional and demographic factors when interpreting radiological findings in pediatric tuberculosis.

Understanding the variations in radiological findings of pulmonary tuberculosis among children has significant implications for clinical practice. Recognizing common patterns such as consolidation and adenopathy facilitates accurate diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment. The absence of cavitary lesions in the current study may suggest a less advanced disease stage, influencing treatment decisions and prognostic assessments.

Regarding the presenting signs and symptoms, the common symptoms seen among patients with TB were fever seen in 75% of the TB patients and cough in 78.1%. Our findings were in agreement with a cross sectional hospital based study done in Kassala Teaching Hospital and Kwaiti Paediatric Hospital in Sudan which reported that the commonest symptoms among children with TB and SAM were fever in 95.5% and cough in 79.8% [26]. The presence of fever and cough as the commonest symptoms is likely because pulmonary tuberculosis affects the lungs causing the irritation of the airways that results in Cough. In addition, the inflammatory reaction to the infection results in the release of markers of inflammation in the blood resulting in fever.

Regarding the factors associated with Tuberculosis among children aged 6 months to 59 months admitted with SAM at JRRH. We observed that a child coming from a rural area had the odds of having TB increased by 1.205 times compared to the one from the urban areas. The social-economic status has been noted to differ between the rural and the urban areas. The poor social-economic status in the rural areas could be responsible for the increased odds of TB in the children from rural areas. A case in point is a study which included 9 rural eastern Uganda communities that reported a high prevalence of tuberculosis among the children [27]. In addition to the above, the use of traditional treatments in rural communities may also be contributing to the high burden of TB in the rural areas. These treatments could worsen the clinical presentation of the disease and even delay the parents from seeking medical attention on time and thereby increasing the number of TB contacts.

Though some studies reported the history of TB contact to be significantly associated with TB, this was not the case in this study. This is possibly because only 5 participants reported a history of TB contact. Therefore, the number of participants was small, making it not possible to make statistically relevant comparisons regarding this variable. Having a small number of participants reporting TB contact yet we had a big number diagnosed with TB could be because: we did not do contact tracing nor did we screen the household members of these children.

A child who was HIV positive was 1.619 times more likely to have TB compared to one who was HIV negative. HIV infection attacks the white blood cells resulting in the impairment of the child’s immune system [28]. This impairment in the immune system is even made worse by the presence of malnutrition in these children [13]. This makes it difficult for the body to contain the mycobacterium tuberculosis organisms resulting in clinical tuberculosis. This was in agreement with two studies in Ethiopia where HIV infection increased odds of having TB [13, 28].

The altered platelet count was also found to be associated with tuberculosis, with thrombocytopenia increasing the odds of having TB by 1.407 times and thrombocytosis increasing the odds by 1.202 times. Disseminated tuberculosis, which is more common in immune compromised children such as these with severe acute malnutrition results in platelet activation which may result in thrombocytosis [29]. However, a number of studies have also reported the occurrence of immune thrombocytopenia in patients diagnosed with tuberculosis [30]. This could explain why these derangements in platelet count were found to be significantly associated with the occurrence of tuberculosis.

Study limitations

Not all patients had chest X-Ray done, yet even those who did not have respiratory distress could have had radiological findings consistent with TB. This could have introduced bias in the study. We did not do any investigation to rule out bacterial pneumonia, which could have resulted in some bacterial pneumonia being clinically diagnosed as TB. The BCG vaccination confirmation was not done in this study; therefore, it was not able to ascertain its association with PTB in SAM. This study was cross-sectional, so the associations found do not necessarily infer a causal relationship. Therefore, future studies on the subject matter should consider the use of other study designs like case control. The study took place during a 3-month period at a single location in a semi-urban setting, which limits the generalizability of our findings; however, these findings can be used as a basis to design multicentre studies over a longer period of time.

Conclusion

The prevalence of tuberculosis in SAM was high. The most common radiological findings observed, in order of frequency, were hilar/mediastinal adenopathy, consolidation, and pleural effusion. The commonest symptoms seen among patients with TB were fever and cough. Coming from Rural areas than Urban, being HIV positive, thrombocytopenia and thrombocytosis were independently associated with presence of Tuberculosis.

Children with SAM should be routinely screened for TB since almost 1: 4 have PTB. If the resources are limited, these investigations should be focused on those coming from rural areas, the HIV positive, those with thrombocytopenia and those with thrombocytosis. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms driving the observed variations in radiological findings of pulmonary tuberculosis among children. Longitudinal studies incorporating advanced imaging modalities and molecular diagnostics may provide deeper insights into disease pathogenesis and progression.
