Understanding the rural injury providers’ experiences with trauma clinical guidance: a qualitative case series
Authors: Andrew Steiner, Michael A Person, Darren D Bowe, Alyssa Johnson, Gabriela Zavala Wong, Kirsten Senturia, Ashley N Moreno, Lacey N. LaGrone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTrauma is the leading cause of death among individuals aged 1–44 years, and it is estimated that many of these deaths could be prevented. Clinical guidance is an essential step toward the optimization of trauma care, especially within rural environments. This qualitative case series seeks to better understand how trauma clinical guidance (TCG) plays a role in rural trauma providers’ patient management.MethodsAn initial exploratory qualitative case series consisting of five semi-structured interviews with rural providers recruited using snowball sampling from existing professional networks were conducted between February and April 2024. Providers were asked to provide details on how they approach clinical uncertainty and if clinical guidance plays a role in their decision making. Then, providers performed real-time reviews of clinical guidance documents, identifying areas for clinical guidance improvement. Interviews were recorded, transcribed, and data analyzed using narrative and thematic approaches, with key themes identified through peer debriefing with relevant quotes selected.ResultsOf the five providers interviewed, three provide care at a critical access hospital, one provides care at a level II trauma center, and one at a level III trauma center. Two interviewees mentioned that they do not use clinical guidance often in direct patient care, and three highlighted the use of advanced trauma life support as the foundation of their practice on which they expand their tools and training. Common requests of TCG from rural providers included: (1) visual components to guide workflow, (2) easy discoverability in a central place, (3) relevant across various resource settings, (4) a centralized ‘stamp of approval,’ for guidelines that have been mutually agreed on via extensive collaboration, and (5) transfer guidance.ConclusionThe needs of rural trauma providers should be a focal point when working to improve the creation and dissemination of TCG. Collaboration when creating new TCG is essential. By intentionally designing for the rural population, we will increase the reach and impact of the guidance developed, as well as improve its accessibility and usability for all providers, regardless of resource setting. Through these efforts, we will decrease the disparate burden of trauma and unintentional injury on rural patients and their healthcare providers.Level of evidenceLevel V.

Introduction

As of 2020, the leading cause of death among individuals aged 1–44 years in the USA was unintentional injury, resulting in 3.4 million years of potential life lost.1 It is estimated that a significant portion of these deaths could be prevented if all hospitals achieved outcomes comparable to those of the highest-performing hospitals.2 3 Trauma clinical guidance (TCG) relevance and adherence is an essential step toward optimizing trauma care.47 With over 60 million Americans living in rural areas,8 and almost 30 million over an hour from a verified trauma center,9 the authors of TCG must do better to serve the needs of rural providers.

The limited literature on rural trauma providers’ perspectives identifies resource challenges ranging from workforce to physical resources.10 11 It is unclear how the rural trauma community views and uses TCG. Musgrove et al found rural surgeons are interested in leveraging accessible and innovative tools like online training,12 suggesting an openness to new, useful tools including improved guidelines.

To better identify clinicians’ needs, our team adopted a multipronged research approach partnering with the Design for Implementation (DFI) conference series. The DFI conference exists to improve clinical guidance, recognizing that community-engaged co-design has been shown to result in products which more closely meet end-user needs.13 As a starting point toward assessing needs and developing interventions, we developed a targeted case series, interviewing rural, emergency, and global trauma providers, starting with the rural community which has been traditionally underassessed. We will follow our qualitative research with quantitative surveys complementing the brokered dialogue occurring at the DFI conferences. We began with open-ended qualitative interviews because we wanted to create an interview space in which the rural providers would raise topics and ideas that were most relevant to them rather than us setting all areas of inquiry in advance.

This exploratory qualitative case series seeks to: (1) understand how TCG, including Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs), play a role in rural trauma providers’ patient management and (2) ascertain the needs of various rural acute care and trauma professionals to adapt guidance to better meet their needs. The insight gained will inform further quantitative studies aimed at developing more equitable and effective TCG.

Methods

Study design and research personnel

We conducted an initial exploratory qualitative assessment using 1-hour in-person or online interviews with five rural acute care and trauma providers from a variety of settings. This is part of a larger project focusing on first defining then implementing the ideal future state for TCG.

The research team included: medical student researcher (AS), physicians (LNL, DDB, MAP, GZW), trauma nurse (AJ), medical anthropologist (KS), and research supervisor (ANM). Data were collected in 2024 by study personnel (AS, LNL) trained in qualitative methods, in partnership with research personnel with contextual experience (DDB, MAP, AJ).

Data collection tools

Interview guides were developed according to study aims, using best practices,14 and adjusted as necessary during interviews per standard qualitative methodology.15 The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research constructs16 were considered to ensure providers’ challenges were examined from multiple domains.

The interview guides were iteratively developed in conjunction with relevant advisers and study team members who are experts in trauma and acute care, rural care, and qualitative research methods. The interview guide was then piloted with a rural trauma surgeon.

Interview questions included three content areas: (1) professional background, primary facility characteristics, approach to clinical uncertainty, and TCG influence on decision making; (2) description of the interviewee’s recent case representing one of five injuries with multiple sources of TCG; and (3) a real-time review of TCG relevant to the chosen case. See online supplemental item 1 for the guide.

Sampling frame and recruitment

We identified volunteer participants through existing networks and contacts through purposive snowball sampling using a recruitment matrix (table 1) to ensure representation across geography, gender, provider role, specialty discipline, and care-center level, anticipating these criteria could impact providers’ use, utility, and experience with TCG. All communication was conducted via email. We successfully communicated with nine potential participants, five of whom agreed to participate in the study.

We expected that interviewing three to five providers from the recruitment matrix would adequately reveal the salient issues that would warrant deeper exploration during subsequent quantitative activities. Our goal was exposing further areas for study and common themes rather than achieving certainty. Because we adhered to qualitative standards of having a very focused research topic and very focused questions, our five-person sample was adequate to achieve data sufficiency.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio recorded, digitally transcribed verbatim, and quality checked by a study member to ensure data integrity. Data were analyzed by study personnel (AS, LNL) using both narrative and thematic approaches. Similarities and differences were noted, and discussion of relevance was resolved to 100% through peer debriefing between staff with relevant quotes identified. The exploratory nature of this analysis did not warrant the creation of a codebook, instead allowing themes to emerge naturally. The interview write-ups represent a systematic summary of each interview highlighting the participants’ key interview themes.

Interview 1: trauma and acute care surgeon; Mountain West, man

This participant works at a level II trauma center that is a regional referral center for a large surrounding area.

Clinical uncertainty

This provider preferred to ask people in his immediate network (eg, a back-up provider), then in his broader network (eg, from training), stating he does not typically use TCG when facing uncertainty at the bedside. When asked what he would do in a time-sensitive scenario with no colleagues available for counsel, he would first look on UpToDate and then look at published guidance from professional trauma societies. He primarily relies on the guidance he is most familiar with, such as that of the Western Trauma Association, and occasionally consults the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma. His objective is to ensure that the care he provides is not divergent from the current recommendations.

Case discussion

This surgeon reported he did not use TCG during the discussed clinical case, primarily because he has frequent experience with the injury and did not feel it was necessary. In the past, he would consult the TCG to support requests made to consultants. For non-urgent/non-procedural reasons, such as preparing a case for the Trauma Morbidity and Mortality (M&M) conference, he often reviews and compares recommendations from multiple professional societies.

Guidance review

This provider emphasized he preferred guidance that contained a visual algorithm or summary table, stating:

If I need to rely on a guideline in the moment, a picture is much easier to decipher.

Ease of access was also critically important:

…sometimes you don’t have 4 or 5 minutes to search and look something up on a computer.

One way of addressing the time barrier is having existing familiarity with a society that publishes guidance, stating:

I already knew what’s there and where to look; I don’t have to discover the guideline for the first time.

Another essential aspect was having bedside-ready resources, or ‘app-based,’ something that was quick, visual, and accessible without in-depth searches or cumbersome logins.

Guidance for care coordination

This provider emphasized two scenarios where additional published guidance could be particularly useful: venous sinus thrombus and neurosurgery recommendations are ‘sticky topic[s]’ saying that clinical practice can vary depending on what provider is on call (eg, deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis). He also mentioned having hospitals still pursue American College of Surgeons (ACS) verification and accreditation is helpful and ‘holds people accountable’ to practice guidance-concordant care.

Interview 2: trauma and acute care surgeon; West Coast, man

The next interviewee works at a level III trauma center roughly 1 hour from a level I trauma center. He estimates trauma represents 20% of his practice; general surgery makes up the remainder.

Clinical uncertainty

Like the first participant, this surgeon does not typically use TCG when facing uncertainty at the bedside. It is typically only for cases where a patient has multiple injuries and multiple physicians involved, and the team needs some clarity on how best to proceed. However, it can be difficult to find appropriate guidance in these scenarios. He expressed concern that no centralized body or organization endorses TGC; for example, he posits the ACS could be ‘the stamp of approval.’

For TCG, he relies on:

…the ones I know about … I don’t think there’s any rural surgeon that knows all the guidelines, there’s a lot of them.

He expressed frustration not knowing when new guidance had been published, indicating he occasionally receives updates at annual state-level trauma conferences. His idea for a solution to the knowledge problem was:

…an app would be helpful, something like ‘trauma.com’ where you could see all the guidance for a particular type of injury and not have to scour the all the associations and journals for the latest guideline.

Like the first interviewee, he mentioned guidance can be useful during retrospective analyses at one of their trauma process improvement conferences.

Case discussion and ideas to improve trauma care standardization

He proposed several ways to improve standardization of trauma care, borrowing examples from other types of care: (1) colonoscopies have protocols built into software with notes where you must capture relevant details related to quality and (2) the standardization of notes for National Comprehensive Cancer Network, indicating these standardized notes drive standardization in practice serving to provide direction as to ‘how’ something is to be implemented, observing:

But for trauma, there’s no real standardization, but in these other spaces, it’s baked-in […] you can’t NOT do it.

He envisioned the ACS having a ‘trauma toolkit’ as a starting point to standardize things at a logical point in the workflow, like note writing. But he cautioned that:

It shouldn’t dictate ‘thou shalt’ but instead provide helpful tools or suggestions organizations can use to accelerate them on their path to change.

Guidance review

On briefly reviewing two existing guidance examples, he likely would not use either at the bedside. He found the wordier example ‘complicated’ and would more likely glance at the visual algorithm if it was available on his phone.

Rural community strengths

A noteworthy theme from this interview was the unique intimacy of the rural community that encompasses the management of rural trauma patients. He noted that despite the Verification, Review, and Consultation program requiring injured patients who meet criteria to be on the trauma service, the patient’s primary doctor may also be involved. While this can be great for continuity, it can make managing their care challenging. Giving the example of an older patient who

… fell off a ladder and the primary care doc[tor]s come to the hospital and round on their patients; I don’t even have to call; they just show up. Then you get into a ‘who’s managing the pain for grandma?’ scenario.

Lastly, he closed with:

In a rural community, I see the kids. I can’t go to the store without seeing a patient. I don’t need extra incentive to provide excellent care, to do the right thing for the patient; I just need to know what the right thing is.

Interview 3: certified registered nurse anesthetist; Midwest, woman

The third participant works primarily at designated critical access hospitals and is called for every full trauma activation.

Clinical uncertainty

She reports TCG does not routinely play a role in her clinical decision making, but when faced with clinical uncertainty, she will use UpToDate, advanced trauma life support (ATLS), or the Stanford Manual for Anesthesia. She is ATLS trained, stating:

I’ve taken it upon myself because I respond to traumas as part of the team and I want to be using the appropriate trauma systems-thinking, so generally I rely on ATLS guidance and guidelines.

She is interested in using the MyATLS app to get easier access to protocols, but reported it does not work on Android phones:

…it would be really nice to have something like that to help guide decision-making!

When asked how she responds to clinical uncertainty, she reports collaboration with the emergency room provider and phone support from potential receiving trauma centers to discuss care or transfer.

Guidance review

She was immediately drawn to the TCG with a visual element, stating:

I like a visual graphic like that, it really helps to support my decision making and it will help us know where we are going to go. It looks concise, easy to use and helpful.

and

Guidelines that are a lot of words are harder to read, especially when I’m trying to figure out what to do with this patient in front of me, it’s not as quick to find what I need.

She said guidance can serve as justification for doing the right thing when it is not as obvious, stating:

When you are doing something that you don’t do all the time, but especially when the stakes are so high, having something you can quickly look at to see the information and help you make decisions that benefit the patient is invaluable […] and help me consider things I may be missing.

She was surprised this guidance was not behind paywalls, saying:

I don’t think anyone at our facility knows they exist.

She preferred a tool readily accessible by phone or website, portable and quick to use. It should provide:

… decision support, giving visual cues to help guide my workflow, even better if they are color coded or leveled.

Resource-relevant guidance

This provider emphasized that her team did not encounter trauma patients every day, making it challenging to perform procedures required to stabilize them. She felt it was important to stratify TCG based on the types of surgical services available.

She closed by saying the most rewarding part of caring for trauma in a rural community is that:

…people are very eager to come and help when help is needed, even when not on call; people show up.

Interview 4: emergency physician; Mountain West, man

The fourth participant is an ATLS instructor working at a critical access hospital serving his whole county. The nearest receiving facility is a level III trauma center an hour away. Since his facility does not have an intensive care unit/intermediate care, the most medically complex patients are managed in the emergency department. Regarding decisions to transfer, protocols dictate that some stable patients can stay, while others are sent to level III or level I trauma centers.

Clinical uncertainty

TCG did not play a major role for him when facing uncertainty. Instead, especially when transferring a patient to definitive care, he called the receiving provider to determine how they want to proceed, ultimately discussing the plan with the patient. He observed:

I don’t know if looking up guidelines would really be in the best interest of time.

He identified three main drawbacks to using TCG: not offering procedural guidance (instead referencing Roberts and Hedges’ textbook to quickly refresh on a procedure), delays in finding needed information, and competing guidance or provider preference where there are gray areas. He says, as an emergency medicine provider,

Rather than get drug over the coals by the receiving provider, I would rather just have a conversation with the expert and do what they want me to do instead of leaving ourselves open to criticism and liability […] I would rather share the responsibility.

One challenge with using guidance is the lack of someone to share in the decision-making process—someone who understands the nuances of the patient’s condition, the available resources, and the responsibility of stewarding those scarce resources.

Guidance review

This provider observed that the damage control resuscitation guidance was helpful regarding frequently overlooked elements of management and the flow chart to guide decision making was helpful, stating:

…don’t oversimplify, it’s preferred to be comprehensive.

He reported that TCG can have significant utility to absolve liability for the rural emergency physician, stating:

If I do what the guideline says, then my position is much more defensible. Guidelines can be really helpful if they’re supporting what you’re doing; I think going against guidelines is a lot riskier.

Guidance stratification and transfers

He noted that TCG should be evidence-based and vary by resource availability but not by provider, cautioning:

When looking at resource stratification (or tiered resources), make sure you are not doing things that will delay transport to definitive care. Getting a patient to definitive care is the number one priority.

Regarding transport, he reported that providers must weigh the risks and benefits of going against guidance (eg, because of resource reasons or patient preference). To manage this, he communicates with receiving providers and discusses plans with patients to confirm agreement. Lastly, he specifically requested recommendations from TCG regarding rural versus urban setting transport considerations and patient counseling. He shared an example of a decision between flying a patient with a small subdural to a level 1 trauma center versus keeping them at his facility; it would be helpful to have guidance for how to counsel patients who do not want to transfer from their own community. He called these

Guidelines for the ‘transfer just in case’ scenario…

Interview 5: family/emergency physician; Mountain West, woman

The fifth participant was an emergency medicine supervising physician, and trauma medical director at a trauma receiving facility (25-bed critical access hospital) in the remote Mountain West. Her facility had an established transfer relationship with a level II trauma center, ~55 miles away with variable transport times largely dependent on weather. The on-call acute provider decides whether to keep or transfer; she states,

…we don’t keep any significant trauma.

She specifically mentioned challenges recruiting providers comfortable with both emergency trauma patient care and managing clinic patients.

All her providers are ATLS trained. She states,

Given that trauma happens infrequently, we follow ATLS guidance as a framework to systematically approach managing and stabilizing each trauma patient.

When traumas arrive, they are often

…high-risk, low volume. We want to get everything right, and don’t have a lot of time to look things up.

Clinical uncertainty

This provider’s first response was to call an emergency provider at the level II trauma receiving facility for guidance. All trauma patients requiring intervention were transferred to a higher level of care. Their main goal is to ‘stabilize enough for transport.’

The most helpful guidance reduced cognitive load for providers at her facility by providing clear direction on whom to transfer or retain. She reports they have ‘standing orders and protocols’ to expedite care of trauma patients on arrival. This serves to increase each team member’s autonomy and reduce cognitive load.

Like the emergency physician, she emphasized the importance of respecting the preferences of patients who do not want to transfer to a higher level of care:

…determining capacity and patient preference is just as important as medical necessity for the transport.

Guidance review

On reviewing traumatic brain injury (TBI) guidance, she found the resource-stratified guidance with a visual flow ‘very helpful’ (representing the resources available at her facility). She said the algorithm mirrored how they manage their patients with TBI and that they have a similar resource in their protocol book. When looking at a non-resource stratified TBI guideline, she stated it, “goes way further than we’re ever going to go,” reiterating that their job is to stabilize then discharge or transfer.

She closed by stating she likes using ATLS training as a starting point since it provides a stepwise framework for her providers to methodically approach and stabilize their patients, while also thinking critically about the patient in front of them.

Common themes across all interviews

Common requests of TCG from rural providers included: (1) visual components, (2) easy discoverability, (3) resource relevance, (4) a centralized ‘stamp of approval,’ and (5) transfer guidance (table 3).

Discussion

All participants preferred guidance that contained visual elements to guide workflow. The two trauma surgeons shared similar perspectives: they seldom use TCG when facing clinical uncertainty at the bedside, and when they do use guidelines, they reach for familiar resources, to coordinate care with other specialists or for process improvements in trauma care. They requested an ‘app’ to improve the discoverability of all guidance and suggested that the ACS could provide a ‘stamp of approval’ to the included guidance. The three non-surgeon providers said ATLS was the foundation for their care, and ATLS certification was important to practice and trauma patient management. Two wanted guidance to address transfer and retention issues and best ways to counsel patients with divergent preferences. Future quantitative work should explore specifics about how ATLS can be improved for rural providers.

A common theme was guidance must be mutually agreed on across specialties involved. Otherwise, providers can ultimately rely on the recommendations of receiving providers, decreasing the likelihood of practicing the most evidence-based medicine. Shared TCG can empower rural and non-trauma surgeons to elevate patient care via updated, evidence-based practices, then collaboratively modifying care as needed following communication with receiving doctors and shared decision making with the patient.

Another critical point from participants was that TCG needs to be relevant across resource settings. This enables providers at facilities with diverse levels of care to provide the most evidence-based medicine for their patients. When guidance is not tailored to available resources—such as recommendations that fail to account for key constraints (eg, ‘Is neurosurgical care available within 30 min?’) with corresponding pathways for ‘yes’ and ‘no’—TCG can exacerbate provider moral injury by promoting ‘best practices’ without offering feasible or accessible alternatives. The Joint Economic Commission reports that over nine out of 10 rural areas have healthcare shortages.17 By providing resource-relevant recommendations, the rural provider will be empowered to provide evidence-based care in their context, potentially limiting moral injury and thus burnout/turnover.

Limited existing research precludes significant comparison. One systematic review of pediatric multisystem trauma CPGs supported our findings indicating the need for allied health collaboration and addressing inequities such as remote living.18 They also requested implementation support.18

Limitations

Sample: interviewees were rural providers and did not include rural healthcare consumers or administrators. While our qualitative sample is not intended to be generalizable—that is the role of the quantitative survey—we acknowledge it does not reflect all rural providers. We feel confident that this in-depth analysis of a small cohort is a meaningful addition to the literature given the paucity of rural experience represented generally.

Exploratory research: this work is exploratory and aimed at identifying future research areas rather than drawing definitive conclusions. It enables the reader to immerse themselves in the experience of the rural providers, who are consistently under-represented in peer-reviewed literature.

Biases: given the small sample, we recognize that selection bias was possible but minimally problematic. Recognizing potential researcher bias, we maintained openness and flexibility, following participants’ insights during data collection and relying on our qualitative research expert advisor to review our work at each step of the research.

Conclusion

The current approach to TCG development does not result in high utility for rural trauma providers who want a centralized location to easily find collaboratively developed guidance, containing visual elements, and adequately resource-stratified for all providers to provide evidence-based patient care.

Collaboration across professional trauma societies and specialties when creating new TCG is essential, and the rural trauma community should be a focal point of collaboration. By intentionally designing for this population, we will increase the reach and impact of the guidance developed, as well as improve its accessibility and usability for all providers, regardless of resource setting. Additionally, we need sustainably built resources for trauma providers to locate TCG, partnering with professional societies to ensure there is visibility and transparency across all entities involved. Through these efforts, we will decrease the disparate burden of trauma and unintentional injury on rural patients and their healthcare providers.
