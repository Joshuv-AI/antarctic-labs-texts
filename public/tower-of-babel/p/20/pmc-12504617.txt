Heavy mental task has no effect on sex differences in muscle fatigability during moderate-intensity isometric contraction
Authors: Patrik Vymyslický, David Marko, Dagmar Pavlů, David Pánek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn some physical activities with simultaneous mental task, we can observe greater muscle fatigability in women than in men. These differences are evident in various muscle groups, especially during low-intensity isometric or intermittent isometric contractions with the simultaneous heavy mental task. The aim of this study was to monitor the effect of heavy mental task on sex differences in muscle fatigability during isometric contraction of moderate intensity in the biceps brachii muscle. Seventeen men (27.1 ± 2.5 years) and seventeen women (26.2 ± 3.5 years) attended two sessions (control and stressor) where they performed isometric contraction of the biceps brachii muscle to failure at an intensity of 40% maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). In control session, the participants performed the isometric contraction to failure alone. In stressor session, a simultaneous heavy mental task was included (subtracting the number 13 from any four-digit number). The time to task failure (TTF) and percentage decrease in the median frequency (MDF) were monitored. We did not observe a sex: session interaction effect for the TTF nor the MDF percentage decrease and therefore we can assume that simultaneous heavy mental task did not affect women more than men. We also did not observe a session effect, so we also can state that the simultaneous heavy mental task did not affect the TTF or the MDF percentage decrease. A sex effect was not observed for the percentage decrease in MDF, but it was statistically significant for TTF, where women lasted 47.9% longer in the control session and 56.4% longer in the stressor session. The simultaneous heavy mental task during isometric contraction to failure of the biceps brachii muscle of moderate intensity does not have a greater effect on women than on men.Keywords: Mental task, Sex differences, Muscle fatigueSubject terms: Physiology, Health care

Introduction

Fatigue is an umbrella term for psychophysiological processes that necessarily accompany motor or cognitive activity and, beyond a certain intensity or duration, become its limiting factor1. It can also be defined as a “self-reported symptom”, which is the result of the relationship between two “interdependent attributes”: performance fatigability and perceived fatigability2. Performance fatigability is traditionally measured objectively via a reduction in muscle strength or time to task failure (TTF) during certain tasks, whereas perceived fatigability is measured via questionnaires, e.g., the rate of perceived fatigue (RPF)2–4.

In studies investigating the effect of mental task on sex differences in muscle fatigability, the mental task can be categorized as light mental task and heavy mental task5. Light mental task acts only as a distraction and does not increase levels of stress (does not activate sympathetic nervous system) and therefore it should not increase fatigability, whereas heavy mental task increases levels of stress, which might cause a reduction in performance and increase fatigability6,7. Mathematical operations were used as a mental task in previous studies – for light mental task it was subtracting the number 1 from the number 505,7–9 and for heavy mental task subtracting the number 13 from any four-digit number5,7–9.

In our previous study, we reported an increase in muscle fatigability with the simultaneous heavy mental task, especially in isometric and intermittent isometric contractions10. There are observable differences in fatigability between men and women, which is probably caused because of the anatomical and physiological differences between the sexes11 – such as different proportions of type I and II muscle fibres12, differences in muscle strength and amount of muscle mass13, or differences in the vascular supply of the muscle (Porter et al., 2002). Women are usually less fatigable than men during isometric, intermittent isometric contractions and slow dynamic contractions in various muscle groups14. This type of physical activity is the basis of many work-related tasks and daily activities (ADLs), but are rarely performed in isolation, as they often involve an increased level of stress or require a certain level of cognitive activity6. The simultaneous mental task during a isometric contraction of low or moderate intensity can thus deepen sex differences in muscle fatigability, which was also shown by recent research5,7,8.

The purpose of this study was to determine whether there is a significant increase in muscle fatigability in women more than in men during simultaneous heavy mental task. Because less fatigability was observed in women during low-intensity isometric contractions, for moderate intensities the research is not sufficient14. Also, a more significant physiological response (higher levels of stress) was observed in women with simultaneous heavy mental task15 – which can have a negative effect on the contractility of type I muscle fibres16 – we hypothesized that (1) a longer time to task failure would be observed in women than in men and that (2) a simultaneous mental task would shorten the time to task failure more significantly in women than in men. Tracking sex differences in fatigue is a current topic, as historically possible differences were overlooked, and mainly men were investigated or sex was not taken into account at all14. Recent research highlights the need for new studies investigating the effect of mental tasks on muscle fatigability and the susceptibility of women11, especially in the context of work-related tasks that combine both physical activity of various intensities and simultaneous cognitive load6.

Methods

Participants

Seventeen men and seventeen women were included in this study (see Table 1 for the characteristics of the research group) – we proceeded according to the analysis of the G*Power 3.1 program, which recommended 34 participants (17 women and 17 men) for this type of study. There were no statistically significant differences between men and women in these variables: age, BMI, laterality, walking/day (km per day), exercise/week (hours per week). None of the participants currently or in the past had neurological, cardiovascular or psychiatric diseases or experienced an injury to the tested upper limb. None of the participants were taking medications that could affect their cognitive or neuromuscular abilities. This study was approved by the Faculty of Physical Education and Sport, Charles University ethics committee, and each participant was informed of the purpose and course of the study and signed an informed consent.

Procedure

Each participant visited the laboratory twice. The participants were asked to abstain from alcohol and caffeine consumption for 48 h, to refrain from activities potentially leading to excessive loading on the tested upper extremity for at least 48 h, and to sleep for at least 8 h before each measurement. At the first session, baseline data were collected (age, body height, body weight, laterality, walking/day, exercise/week and women were asked about the date of the start of their last menstruation cycle), and the first measurement was performed. The second session took place at least 7 days after the first, the women were again asked about the day of the start of their last menstruation cycle, and a second measurement was performed. In our study, we used the assumption of a 28-day menstrual cycle and divided women into two groups – pre-menstrual (day 15–28 from the start of the last menstruation) and post-menstrual (day 1–14 from the start of the last menstruation), which was one of the classification models used in the past17.

Measurement process

The participants were exposed to the same physical task in both sessions, but the second one included a simultaneous mental task. The measurement process of both sessions is shown in Fig. 1. First, the value of the rate of perceived fatigue (RPF) was taken, and then the maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) was measured via electromyography (EMG). Within- and between-day reliability and repeatability of surface EMG measurement is a thoroughly studied topic with very good outcomes (Rainoldi et al., 1999; Dutra et al., 2023) and the same procedure as ours was already used in similar previous studies (e.g.5,7. We used single MVC to avoid excessive fatigue of the participants, based on methodological considerations proposed by Froyd et al.18: Subjects knew beforehand that the experiment consisted of three trials and might therefore terminated the first or second trial in a submaximal state of fatigue, despite strong encouragement.” We, therefore, used only single MVC trial with very strong verbal encouragement to the highest possible performance for at least 5 s. Immediately after the end of the MVC measurement, the RPF value was taken again. This was followed by a four-minute of quiet sitting without mental task (if it was participant’s first measurement – control session) or by a four-minute sitting with simultaneous mental task (if it was participant’s second measurement – stressor session), then the RPF value was again taken. The participant was subsequently asked to contract the biceps brachii muscle at an intensity of 40% MVC for the longest possible time (until failure). If the muscle activity fell below 40% MVC and could no longer exceed this value within the next 5 s, the time of failure was considered to be the time when the participant last maintained activity above 40% MVC (inspired by19. The initial time (start of the task) was determined as the moment when the participant first reached 40% MVC during the measurement. The exact start and end of isometric contraction was determined via offline analysis of the EMG data. During the isometric contraction, each participant was strongly verbally encouraged to achieve the longest possible time to task failure. The muscle activity rate of 40% MVC was controlled via biofeedback on a laptop monitor that was placed 1 m in front of the participant.

Experimental protocol. Before the maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) measurement, the rate of perceived fatigue (RPF) value was obtained (T1), and then the MVC was measured; afterwards, the RPF value was measured again (T2). Then, the participant just quietly sat for 4 min (if it was participant’s control session) or sat with simultaneous mental task for 4 min (if it was participant’s stressor session – SS). Halfway through (T3), and after sitting (T4), the RPF value was taken again. Afterwards, the isometric contraction was performed until task failure.

The position of the participant

The isometric contraction was performed in a special metal structure, which was used elsewhere20, in a sitting position with a straight back (ear, shoulder, hip in the axis – accuracy verified with a plumb line), feet on the ground at the width of the participant’s shoulders, shoulder joint and elbow joint of the tested upper limb in 90° flexion21, elbow resting on the lower bar and the forearm in supination with the wrist pushing against the upper bar (See Fig. 2). The other arm was placed on the table facing palm down. The metal structure was fixed to the table and bars were manually set to match desired shoulder and elbow joints angles for each participant. Verification of the accuracy of the set angles in the joints was ensured via a standard metal goniometer.

Schematic picture of participant’s position. The arrow shows the placement of participant’s wrist and also the direction of the participant’s pushing.

Mental task

A difficult math task is an established stress-inducing technique15. Subtracting the number 13 from any four-digit number, where the participant must report the digit every three seconds22, has been used several times in previous studies and was referred to as a heavy mental task5,7,8,22. In case of an incorrect answer or failure to meet the time limit, the participant was asked to correct it and subtract again from the originally selected number. In the case of three incorrect answers, the participant was prompted to choose a new four-digit number and start subtracting again. Each participant was exposed to heavy mental task only during the second visit (stressor session). Mental task started after the MVC measurement and was present during 4 min of sitting and the whole isometric contraction until task failure (See Fig. 1).

Electromyographic recordings

Measurements were performed on the nondominant upper limb to minimize variability between participants that could occur due to different activities performed by the dominant upper limb7. Noraxon Telemyo 2400 G2 was used for signal acquisition. Disposable self-adhesive Ag/ACl electrodes with a conductive gel were used for the measurements. The diameter of the adhesive surface of the electrode was 3.8 cm, and the diameter of the conductive surface was 1 cm. A pair of electrodes was placed on clean and degreased skin. They were placed 1 cm apart across the longitudinal axis of the muscle fibres of the biceps brachii muscle – parallel to the assumed direction of muscle fibre tension (according to SENIAM standards – surface electromyography for the noninvasive assessment of muscles). The reference electrode was placed according to the recommendation of Konrad21 on the olecranon of the tested upper limb. The sampling frequency for the measurements was 1500 Hz, the bandwidth was 5–500 Hz and time window was 1 s.

Subjective scale measurement

Micklewright et al.4 described the rate of perceived fatigue (RPF), a validated questionnaire created by modifying Borg’s CR-10 scale. This scale ranges ​​from 0 (no fatigue) to 10 (absolute fatigue and exhaustion – inability to continue). The data collection took place in the form of a question about the currently perceived overall fatigue (value 0–10) before the MVC measurement, after the MVC measurement, in the middle and after the 4-min sitting. When asked about the RPF value during the stressor session, the last reported number was always given to the participant after he reported the RPF value.

Data analysis

Data recorded from surface EMG were subjected to frequency analysis (which also included automatic pre-processing and filtering of the signal) via the MR 3.8.30 software of Noraxon, Inc., USA. Monitored frequency characteristic of the electromyographic signal was the median frequency (MDF), which is a frequently used indicator of the degree of muscle fatigue23 and is less burdened by unwanted noise than the mean value24. For this study were used the values of MDF at the onset of the isometric contraction at an intensity of 40% MVC (as the average of MDF values of the first five seconds after participant reached 40% MVC) and at the time of failure (as the average of MDF values 5 s before failure). To determine muscle fatigue itself, the percentage decrease in the median frequency was calculated (from the start of contraction until task failure).

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel and Jamovi 2.3.28 software was used for the statistical tests. Unless otherwise stated, the data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. All the data were at first tested for normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test and possibly for analysis of variance via Levene’s test. Parametric or nonparametric variants of statistical tests were used according to the results. Because of technical EMG issues, data from one participant (male) were not included in TTF or MDF statistical analysis. For the evaluated time to task failure (TTF) parameter, the data were normally distributed, so repeated-measures ANOVA with sex as a between-subject factor and session as a within-subject factor was used to monitor the interaction effect (sex: session). A post-hoc analysis was used for the session effect for individual sexes and also for the sex effect for individual sessions. For the overall percentage decrease in the median frequency from the beginning to the end of the isometric contraction, the data were normally distributed, so we used ANOVA with sex as a between-subject factor and session as a within-subject factor to observe the interaction effect (sex: session). A post-hoc analysis was used for the session effect for individual sexes and also for the sex effect for individual sessions. For the rate of perceived fatigue (RPF) parameter, the data were normally distributed, so repeated-measures ANOVA with sex as between-subject factor and time as within-subject factor was used, a post-hoc analysis was then performed to analyze desired relationships. To examine the influence of the menstrual cycle, the results of women in pre-menstrual and post-menstrual phase were analyzed using independent and paired t-tests.

Independent t-tests were used to compare the physical characteristics (age, height, body mass, body mass index and laterality) and physical activity levels (walk distance per day and exercise time per week) of men and women. A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was used to identify statistical significance.

Results

There were no statistically significant differences between men and women in terms of age, body mass index (BMI), laterality, walk distance per day or exercise time per week (p > 0.05), but men and women differed in height and weight (p < 0.05; see Table 1).

Time to task failure

Men in the stressor session lasted 8.3 s (4.9%) longer than in the control session. Women in the stressor session lasted 27.2 s (10.3%) longer than in the control session (see Table 2). Women in the control session had a time to task failure that was 81.2 s (47.9%) longer than that of the men and that in the stressor session was 100.1 s (56.4%) longer than that of the men (see Table 3). There was no statistically significant interaction effect of session × sex (p = 0.478). There was no effect of the menstrual cycle phase on the time to task failure in the control session (p = 0.564) or stressor session (p = 0.340).

Neuromuscular activity

Muscle fatigue from an electromyographic point of view was analysed by the percentage drop in MDF (measured from the start of the contraction until task failure) and was similar in both sexes in both sessions (See Table 4). For men the percentage drop was −34.5% in the control session and -29.8% in the stressor session. For women the percentage drop was -33.6% in the control session and -32.8% in the stressor session. There was no significant difference in the percentage drop between sessions (session effect) in men (p = 0.236) or in women (p = 0.634). There was no significant difference in the total percentage decrease between the sexes (sex effect) in the control (p = 0.556) or stressor sessions (p = 0.792). There was no statistically significant interaction effect of session × sex (p = 0.448). There was no effect of the menstrual cycle phase on the percentage drop in MDF in the control session (p = 0.765) or stressor session (p = 0.780).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are that during an isometric contraction of the biceps brachii muscle at an intensity of 40% MVC: 1) the simultaneous heavy mental task has no greater effect on time to task failure or median frequency percentage drop in women than in men; 2) a simultaneous heavy mental task does not decrease time to task failure or increase median frequency percentage drop in men or women at all; 3) women present significantly longer time to task failure than men regardless of the simultaneous heavy mental task.

A total of three studies5,7,8 used a methodology similar to ours – isometric contraction to failure of the biceps brachii muscle of the nondominant upper limb in 90° elbow flexion and 90° shoulder flexion, mental task in the form of subtracting the number 13 from any four-digit number numbers (described as heavy mental task – increasing levels of stress). All three studies used an isometric contraction at an intensity of 20% MVC, and in all the studies, a greater effect of heavy mental task was observed on women than on men,the evaluation criterion was time to task failure (TTF). In our study, an intensity of 40% MVC was used to investigate the effect of heavy mental task on sex differences in muscle fatigue during moderate-intensity isometric contraction. Our hypothesis was based on the findings of previous studies, where during isometric contraction under simultaneous heavy mental task, a significantly greater increase in muscle fatigue was observed in women than in men5,7,8. This study did not confirm our hypothesis, as a statistically significant interaction effect of sex and session was not observed – both sexes responded similarly to the heavy mental task during the isometric contraction of 40% MVC. No session effect was observed either, as the heavy mental task did not cause any statistically significant changes in TTF or percentage drop of MDF within the individual sexes. On the other hand, we observed a sex effect, as women presented significantly longer time to task failure than men did in both the control and stressor sessions.

A possible reason for the greater influence of heavy mental task on sex differences in muscle fatigability in women observed in studies with an intensity of 20% MVC is reported by Keller-Ross et al.5, who discussed differences in the strength of individual participants (i.e., a more significant effect of heavy mental task on muscle fatigue was observed in weaker individuals than in stronger individuals, regardless of sex)5,7. In our study, we did not focus on the selection of strength-matched participants, as other authors did in some studies on this topic25–27, and we assume that our two groups were not strength-matched and therefore that men were naturally stronger, as they usually are14. The possible link that a weaker individual with simultaneous heavy mental task presents a more significant reduction in time to task failure observed in previous studies can be explained by increased sympathetic activation, which can be achieved precisely by a heavy mental task5,7. Differences in the effect of heavy mental task on muscle fatigability may also be muscle group dependent, as there were no sex differences with simultaneous heavy mental task during isometric contraction to failure in the ankle dorsiflexors at 30% MVC, but there was a difference in elbow flexors (Vanden-Noven et al., 2017). This may be explained by small intersex differences in the proportions of type I and type II muscle fibres in the ankle dorsiflexors28. Mental task can also have an effect on muscle fatigue only within a specific contraction type – muscle fatigability was increased during low-intensity isometric contractions of the biceps brachii muscle5,7, but during intermittent contractions of the shoulder abductors, fatigability was most increased during moderate-intensity contractions, less so during low-intensity contractions, and not at all during high-intensity contractions29.

Increased sympathetic activation affects the activity of type I and II muscle fibres – it can increase the contractile force in type II fibres but decrease it in type I fibres (along with the rate of relaxation), causing muscles with a proportionally larger cross-section area of type I fibres to be more fatigable than muscles with a proportionally larger cross-section area of type II fibres5,16. The biceps brachii muscle in men has a proportionally larger cross-sectional area of ​​type II muscle fibres than that in women13 and that may predispose women to greater changes in muscle fatigue with simultaneous sympathetic activation – for example with simultaneous heavy mental task7.

Increased muscle fatigability with simultaneous heavy mental task observed in previous studies may also be due to differences in blood flow to muscles in strong and weak individuals – women, who are usually weaker than men, present higher blood flow during submaximal contractions and a slower increase in mean arterial pressure (MAP), which can result in slower accumulation of metabolites and, under control conditions, reduce muscle fatigability12,27,30. Mechanical compression occurs during isometric contraction, the muscle becomes more ischemic, which results in vasodilation on the basis of the effects of accumulated metabolites on smooth muscle and the stimulation of ß-2 adrenergic receptors31. Furthermore, we often observe a reduced vasoconstrictor response to α-adrenergic stimulation in the brachial artery in women32, and during isometric contractions, women show lower muscle sympathetic activity (MSNA)33. Therefore, a heavy mental task can disturb the balance between vasoconstriction and vasodilatation in the sense of increasing vasoconstriction in weaker muscles, thereby contributing to greater fatigability34. Among other things, a heavy mental task in the context of increasing stress can cause an increase in blood pressure and heart rate9 and therefore might represent a greater cardiac load5, which was already observed in women more than in men7.

Voluntary activation represents the ability of the motor cortex to excite the muscle to produce maximal force5,12. Voluntary activation does not differ between men and women14, so we cannot attribute sex differences in muscle fatigability to it, as both sexes are able to activate a similar amount of muscle during contraction5. However, a heavy mental task can accelerate the loss of neural drive (regardless of sex) relative to the control conditions, making it impossible to activate muscles to the required capacity and thus increasing muscle fatigability5,35. Despite previous studies that reported a reduction in time to task failure during isometric contractions with simultaneous heavy mental load regardless of sex5,7, in our study, there was no statistically significant reduction in time to task failure (session effect) in men or women during the stressor session.

However, we observed a significant difference between the sexes within the individual sessions (sex effect), where the time to task failure in women was 47.9% longer than that in men during the control measurement and 56.4% longer than that in men during sessions with a heavy mental task. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that reported longer time to task failure in women than in men during isometric and intermittent contractions at the same relative intensities in different muscle groups, including the elbow flexors11. The sex differences in muscle fatigability might depend on specific muscle group, since in elbow extensors and ankle dorsiflexors at the same relative intensities there were no differences in time to task failure observed36,37. Sex differences also depend on the intensity of the contraction – with increasing contraction intensity the sex differences usually decrease12. A possible reason for sex differences in the fatigability of individual muscle groups may be the different proportions of type I and II muscle fibres or differences in blood supply12. The individual’s strength can also play a role (as it was noted in tasks with simultaneous heavy mental task), because for strength-matched men and women, no differences in isometric contraction time to failure were recorded38. In intermittent isometric contraction, however, strength-matched women lasted nearly three times longer39. Further research is needed to fully understand this phenomenon12.

Measurement of the changes in the frequency of the EMG signal is often used to assess muscle fatigue24. As muscle fatigue increases, an observable decrease in the frequency spectrum of the EMG signal can be observed40–42. In our study, we observed a statistically significant effect of sex on the time to failure in both the control and stressor sessions, but sex had no effect on the percentage drop of MDF – differences in muscle fatigue may be therefore caused by peripheral mechanisms rather than central mechanisms (e.g., neural drive).

Our results regarding the subjective scale (RPF in our case) agree with the study of Yoon et al.7, where both men and women experienced greater fatigue after performing a heavy mental task during rest period (4-min time window after MVC measurement and before the start of isometric contraction). Women also show greater physiological changes following the stressor itself (without physical activity) than men do7,43,44. Considering the results of the present study, when the simultaneous heavy mental task did not increase muscle fatigability in men or women, we can assume that in our research sample, for most participants it did not represent a stressor but rather a distraction and therefore did not have a negative effect on fatigue.

Nowadays, the use of the meanings of “sex” and “gender” is often debated and the interest in inclusion, diversity and representativeness is on the rise45. Sex is a set of biological characteristics, where each human, animal, tissue or cell has a sex defined by the presence of XX (female) or XY (male) chromosomes (Miller et al., 2012,Short et al., 2013). Gender is a multidimensional construct that includes gender identity and self-expression, social and cultural circumstances, etc. Gender is determined by each individual, can change during life and at the same time may or may not correspond to the expectations of the relevant society, which are based on biological sex characteristics (Miller et al., 2012, Short et al., 2013; Raele et al., 2023). In our study, the terminology “woman” was meaning female – XX chromosomes and “man” was meaning male – XY chromosomes. We believe that future studies should clearly define the sex and gender of the research groups, or use tools designed for this purpose – e.g. SAGER guidelines proposed by Heidari et al.46.

Although we obtained interesting results, this study has several limitations. First, we did not measure the muscle strength of the participants, because we assume that men are generally stronger than women14 as it was in previous studies in various muscle groups7,47–49. Men’s and women’s strength was similar only after specific selection of strength-matched participants, as in the framework of previous studies39,50. Second, the heavy mental task used in our study increased the levels of stress in previous similar studies5,7 and therefore it negatively affected muscle fatigability5,7,11. We, however, did not measure the levels of stress (via monitoring the activity of sympathetic nervous system) and therefore it is difficult to state if the heavy mental task truly increased levels of stress in each participant. Based on possible individual differences across participants, mental task used by us might not be stressful enough to increase levels of stress and, on the contrary, might act only as a distraction. Distraction does not have a negative effect on muscle fatigability (since it does not increase levels of stress)7 and therefore does not affect muscle fatigue in men or women5,7, as it was observed in our study. Another limitation is the absence of precise indicator of hormonal fluctuation related to the menstrual cycle. To establish in which phase women in our study were, we used the traditional 28-day menstrual cycle model and divided women into two groups – pre-menstrual (day 15–28 from the start of the last menstruation) and post-menstrual (day 1–14 from the start of the last menstruation), which was used before17. Nowadays, there are authors that recommend the use of a three-step method consisting of serum concentration assessment, cycle mapping, and urinary ovulation prediction to correctly classify a given woman into the appropriate phase51,52. The classification of menstrual cycle phases is also a common limitation of previous studies on this topic17.

Conclusion

Simultaneous heavy mental task during moderate-intensity isometric contraction of the biceps brachii muscle does not cause a greater increase in muscle fatigability (measured with time to task failure and percentage drop of median frequency) in women than in men as was hypothesized – simultaneous heavy mental task did not cause an increase in muscle fatigability at all. However, we observed significantly longer time to task failure in women than in men in both the control and the stressor sessions, which is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies on this topic.
