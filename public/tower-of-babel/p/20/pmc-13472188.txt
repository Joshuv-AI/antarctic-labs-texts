Size-Dependent Reaction Pathways in Multiphase SO2 Oxidation Chemistry
Authors: Kedong Gong, Adriane Tam, V. Faye McNeill, Vicki H. Grassian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The paradigm for atmospheric multiphase SO2 chemistry, traditionally based on bulk aqueous-phase studies, involves a multistep process of SO2 uptake followed by oxidation. Here, we decouple these steps at the single-droplet level by comparing SO2 uptake and reported dissolved S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation in aqueous microdroplets of varying sizes. Our results reveal that SO2 uptake into microdroplets to form S­(IV)­(aq) is suppressed as the droplet radius (r) decreases, in contrast to our previous finding that S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation is enhanced at smaller radii. Fitting these opposing size-dependent processes shows that SO2 uptake is 1–2 orders of magnitude slower than S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation for typical atmospheric aerosols (r ≲ 5 μm). Thus, larger cloud and fog microdroplets follow the conventional bulk-phase pathway while smaller aqueous aerosols become uptake-limited and favor direct oxidation at the surface. Mn­(II)-catalyzed SO2 oxidation in Na2SO3 microdroplets with contrasting radii (r = 5.9 and 94.0 μm) further supports this size-dependent mechanistic transition. Overall, by distinguishing SO2 uptake from subsequent S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation, we show that size can modulate the rate-limiting step, leading to different multiphase SO2 oxidation mechanisms. This step-specific perspective highlights the critical and multiple roles of the air–water interface and emphasizes how different size dependencies in individual steps within a multistep mechanism can further our understanding of atmospheric multiphase chemistry.

Introduction

Multiphase sulfur oxidation plays an important role in particulate sulfate formation. − The current paradigm for multiphase SO2 oxidation involves bulk aqueous-phase chemistry that begins with the partitioning of gas-phase SO2(g) into the aqueous phase of aerosols to form dissolved S­(IV)­(aq) species (SO2·H2O, HSO3 –, and SO3 2–), followed by oxidation to S­(VI)­(aq), with many past studies largely neglecting possible interfacial processes. However, more recently, accelerated SO2 oxidation [SO2(g) → S­(VI)­(aq)] has been observed in aqueous aerosols by measuring S­(VI)­(aq) formation, − yet it remains unclear how microdroplet size and the air–water interface influence individual steps in the reaction. Each step in multiphase chemistrySO2 uptake [SO2(g) → SO2(aq)], hydration [SO2(aq) → S­(IV)­(aq)], and S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation [S­(IV)­(aq) → S­(VI)­(aq)]contributes to the overall reaction mechanism and reaction rate.

Recent studies have shown that soluble S­(IV) oxidation proceeds faster in smaller microdroplets. Both Li et al. and our recent study demonstrated that the size-dependent S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation scales with the surface-to-volume ratio of aqueous aerosols. However, it remains unclear how SO2(g) is converted to S­(IV)­(aq) in aqueous aerosols of varying size. SO2 uptake is essential for multiphase reactions and has been extensively studied in bulk solutions. In the past, the observed rapid SO2 uptake was attributed to fast interfacial reactions, − and a H2O···SO2 surface complex has been proposed to facilitate its solvation. , In fact, recent simulations suggest that SO2·H2O complexes preferentially reside at the air–water interface relative to bulk water. This interfacial preference may facilitate SO2 repartitioning to the gas phase or limit SO2 transport into the droplet bulk, suppressing dissolved S­(IV)­(aq) formation. Therefore, distinguishing SO2 uptake from subsequent S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation is essential for understanding the kinetics and pathways of multiphase SO2 chemistry.

Here, we utilize a single-microdroplet approach to examine whether microdroplet size leads to intrinsic differences in the reaction mechanism through size-dependent rates of individual steps in the overall multiphase SO2 oxidation. To evaluate the role of the microdroplet air–water interface, we study size effects from ∼4 to ∼130 μm (r). These experiments used relatively high pH and gas-phase SO2 concentrations to facilitate data interpretation. Although these conditions exceed those typically found in the atmosphere, they allow us to distinguish SO2 uptake from subsequent S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation. We then compare the size dependence of SO2 uptake with reports of sulfite oxidation in aqueous microdroplets. The observed inverse size dependence provides insight into reconciling traditional bulk-phase paradigms with emerging microdroplet observations in multiphase sulfur oxidation chemistry.

Materials and Methods

All reactions in microdroplets of different sizes have been monitored utilizing in situ micro-Raman spectroscopy with an environmental cell (Figure S1), as described previously. All experiments were conducted at 90 ± 3% relative humidity (RH) and 296 K. Microdroplets are supported on a hydrophobic substrate with a contact angle of ∼103.6° (Figure S2). Results and Discussion

Size-Dependent Uptake and Oxidation of S­(IV)­(aq)

Figure a shows temporal changes in the sulfur concentration (m, mol/kg) for three microdroplets with radii (r) of 55.0, 8.0, and 4.5 μm, generated from a pH 9.5 Na2SO3 bulk solution and exposed to three gas conditions: pure N2, SO2 in N2, and SO2 in O2. Under N2, all microdroplets showed stable sulfur concentrations, as determined from the Raman spectra (Figure S9), with sulfite as the major species, consistent with the expected S­(IV)­(aq) speciation at high pH (Figure S3). When SO2 is introduced, the larger microdroplets (radii of 55.0 and 8.0 μm) show sulfur speciation changes indicative of SO2 uptake: the sulfite concentration ([SO3 2–]) decreases, while the bisulfite concentration ([HSO3 –]) increases, with a concomitant decrease in pH (Text S3 and Figure S10 showing the pH evolution). In contrast, the smallest microdroplets (4.5 μm) show limited HSO3 – formation (Figure a). Upon the introduction of O2, the S­(IV) speciation profiles diverge further: the 55.0-μm microdroplet showed substantial sulfite depletion along with a slow increase in [HSO3 –]; the 8.0-μm microdroplet showed faster depletion of both sulfite and bisulfite, whereas the 4.5-μm microdroplet displayed the fastest sulfite depletion. Sulfate concentrations increased in all microdroplets but with different rates. Additionally, during SO2 introduction, the total sulfur increased from 4.5 to 4.9 mol/kg in the 55.0-μm microdroplet, whereas the smaller droplets showed only fluctuating quasi-steady plateaus (∼4.9 mol/kg), likely reflecting Raman detection limits (Text S2.3).

(b) Formation rates of HSO3 – and SO4 2– in these microdroplets, determined from linear fits of [HSO3 –] and [SO4 2–] versus time under SO2 + N2 and SO2 + O2 conditions, respectively, prior to reaching steady state. As shown in Figure b, the comparison of HSO3 – and SO4 2– formation rates within each individual droplet suggests that the balance between S­(IV) uptake and S­(IV) oxidation shifts with the microdroplet size. For 55.0-μm microdroplets, SO4 2– formation proceeds slightly slower than SO2 uptake (half of the HSO3 – formation rate based on –), resulting in a continued slow increase in [HSO3 –]. When the radius decreases further to 8.0 μm and smaller down to 4.5 μm, the SO4 2– formation rate becomes significantly higher than that of SO2 uptake, indicating faster S­(IV) consumption relative to any replenishment by SO2 partitioning into the microdroplet, which results in the sharp depletion of S­(IV)­(aq) species. Therefore, sulfate formation is mainly from the pre-existing SO3 2– in 8.0-μm and 4.5-μm microdroplets but not in the 55.0-μm case.

Size-Dependent SO2 Evaporation

Given the limited observation of SO2 uptake in smaller microdroplets, we investigated the size dependence of SO2 evaporation, the reverse of reactions –, under N2. Microdroplets were generated from Na2SO3 solutions adjusted with H2SO4 to a pH of 7.2, near the pK a 2 of H2SO3. As shown in Figure a, Raman spectra of bulk solutions remained unchanged over time at this pH. However, for microdroplets, bisulfite peaks (1024 and 1054 cm–1) rapidly decreased and the sulfite peaks (940 and 967 cm–1) slightly increased. These changes correspond to speciation shifts and an overall pH increase (Figure b,c). According to the reverse reactions for –, protonation of HSO3 – yields SO2·H2O­(aq), followed by SO2(g) release, resulting in the depletion of HSO3 – and an increase in pH. The pseudo-first-order rate constant for this SO2 evaporation, k evap, was obtained by fitting ln­([SO3 2–] + [HSO3 –]) versus time. Figure d shows a size-dependent SO2 evaporation, where k evap increased from (3.8 ± 0.7) × 10–3 to (1.7 ± 0.5) × 10–2 min–1 as the microdroplet radius decreased from 115.5 to 7.5 μm (Figure S12), scaling with 1/r. Notably, the Kelvin effect is negligible over this size range. These results suggest that HSO3 – protonation might be enhanced at the air–water interface of smaller microdroplets, leading to the partitioning of SO2 into the gas phase.

To investigate the effect of the air–water interface on the proton activity, the peak area ratio (R) of the conjugate acid/base pair HSO4 –/SO4 2– was used as an acidity probe in NaHSO4 microdroplets (bulk pH ≈ 1.0) under N2, where volatile sulfur species are absent. As the droplet radius decreases from 51.4 to 3.0 μm, R edge/R center increased (Figures e and S13), indicating stronger apparent interfacial proton activity in smaller droplets. This trend is consistent with previous reports of increased aerosol acidity with decreasing size, − as well as enhanced interfacial proton activity. ,, For S­(IV) speciation, our results, along with reported simulations, suggest that HSO3 – preferentially populates the subsurface, while SO2·H2O­(aq) is surface-active (Figure f). Therefore, a shift in interfacial speciation toward more protonated forms (e.g., SO3 2– → HSO3 – → SO2·H2O) could thermodynamically favor SO2 release into the gas phase. The observed size-dependent SO2 evaporation suggests that the protonation of HSO3 – to molecular SO2·H2O/SO2(aq) may be kinetically accelerated.

The observed 1/r scaling is consistent with surface-to-volume considerations, suggesting that the size-dependent trend is unlikely to arise solely from the substrate interactions. Moreover, our recent studies of sulfite oxidation and SO2 evaporation in suspended microdroplets, which eliminate substrate effects, showed results similar to those observed in deposited microdroplets, suggesting that substrates have a limited influence on the observed behavior. ,

Microdroplet Size as a Mechanistic Switch

Figure a shows the effect of the microdroplet size on the rate constant for SO2 uptake, k uptake, determined by fitting ln­([SO3 2–]) versus time under 5 ppm of SO2 in N2 (Figure S14 and Texts S2 and S4). As the microdroplet size decreases, k uptake remains nearly constant around (1.7–1.8) × 10–3 min–1 for larger microdroplets but then decreases sharply to ∼6.8 × 10–4 min–1 for a 4.5-μm microdroplet. However, previous studies have demonstrated an opposite size dependence for both uncatalyzed and Mn­(II)-catalyzed S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation under O2, , where the oxidation rate constant, k ox, is positively correlated with 1/r. As two key steps that govern SO2 multiphase chemistry, the source of S­(IV)­(aq) via uptake and its consumption via oxidation exhibit different size dependencies, suggesting that the droplet size can act as a mechanistic switch controlling the overall SO2 multiphase transformation.

To quantitatively evaluate the size effect on each individual step, k uptake was fit to an empirical function: k uptake(r) = C(1 – e–ar ), , across all droplet sizes, yielding C = (1.632 ± 0.079) × 10–3 and a = 0.118 ± 0.019 μm–1, with χw 2 = 1.54. In addition, oxidation rate constants for both uncatalyzed (k ox,uncata) and Mn­(II)-catalyzed (k ox,Mn(II)) S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation were replotted based on the reported relationship (Table S1). , Based on the size-dependent fits of k uptake(r) and k ox(r) (Figure a), the calculated k ox,Mn(II)/k uptake ratios are ∼0.8 at r = 94.0 μm and ∼26.0 at r = 5.9 μm, indicating distinct size-dependent rate-determining steps: under O2, SO2 uptake becomes the rate-limiting step for small aqueous aerosols without detectable accumulation of S­(IV)­(aq), while S­(IV) oxidation becomes rate-limiting for larger cloud and fog microdroplets resulting in net S­(IV)­(aq) accumulation.

This size-dependent mechanistic shift is further supported by Mn­(II)-catalyzed oxidation (Figure b). Even with the TMI-catalyzed oxidation pathway available, the 94.0-μm droplet displays slower S­(IV) oxidation but faster SO2 uptake, supported by growth of the SO4 2– peak and the accumulation of HSO3 –. In contrast, even under this high SO2 concentration (10 ppm), only S­(VI) formation was observed in the 5.9-μm droplet. Specifically, SO4 2– first appears and increases but then eventually decreases, forming HSO4 –, as the pH rapidly decreases due to SO2 uptake and oxidation but without any detectable HSO3 –, consistent with our predicted surface-dominated oxidation pathway in microdroplets, as shown in Figure a.

Atmospheric Implications

As illustrated in Scheme , based on our results, in larger microdroplets, SO2 uptake exceeds S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation, allowing S­(IV)­(aq) to accumulate and be oxidized first near the interface, where oxygen is most readily supplied from the gas phase and then in the bulk. In contrast, in smaller aqueous aerosols, our size-dependent fits of k uptake(r) and k ox,uncata(r) suggest that k ox,uncata/k uptake increases from ∼8.8 at r = 5 μm to 175.4 at r = 1.0 μm, indicating that SO2 uptake becomes the rate-limiting step. Thus, once SO2 reaches the air–water interface, it is rapidly oxidized. This interpretation is further supported by reported Mn­(II)-catalyzed sulfate formation rates, where oxidation of dissolved S­(IV)­(aq) increasingly exceeds that of SO2(g) as the droplet size decreases (Figure S15 and Table S2). Consequently, the dominant pathway shifts from the conventional mechanism to direct interfacial oxidation of SO2 to S­(VI)­(aq), with size acting as a mechanistic switch.

Schematic of the Inverse Size Dependence for SO2 Uptake and S­(IV) Oxidation during a Multiphase SO2 Oxidation Process, Leading to Distinct Pathways for Aqueous Aerosols and Cloud/Fog Microdroplets

Recent studies by Li et al. and our group revealed distinct size-dependent regimes in S­(VI)­(aq) oxidation. Building on this, we developed CHAI (CHemistry of Aerosol Interfaces), a physicochemical modeling framework that connects interfacial, bulk, and transport processes to represent the size-dependent oxidation kinetics across aqueous aerosol and aqueous microdroplet sizes. The experimental results we present here reveal the opposing size dependencies of SO2 uptake and S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation, providing a more complete understanding that further highlights the inadequacy of traditional bulk-phase paradigms to describe sulfate production in aerosols and microdroplets. For larger marine clouds and fog droplets (∼5–50 μm), , more efficient SO2 uptake supports SO2 oxidation mainly occurring at both the surface and in the bulk, where the overall process is limited by relatively slow bulk-phase diffusion and reactions. However, for the smaller aqueous aerosol and microdroplet paradigm, such as submicron urban aerosols (∼0.1–1.0 μm), , oxidation to sulfate is faster than SO2 uptake and is largely confined to the air–water interface, which bypasses those slower steps in the traditional uptake–hydration–bulk oxidation pathway. Besides, the unique physicochemical properties of the microdroplet surfaceincluding a “water-poor” environment, disrupted hydrogen-bonding networks, alignment of reagents, and presence of protonscan lower reaction energy barriers and enhance reactivity. − This therefore provides the start of a mechanistic framework for size-dependent processes and the enhanced SO2 oxidation reported in microdroplets. −

Overall, our study highlights the critical and multiple roles of the air–water interface, emphasizing how different size dependencies in multistep processes can further our understanding of multiphase atmospheric chemistry. When interfacial pathways dominate, photochemical processes may be enhanced by direct solar exposure at the air–water interface. Surface-active SO2, photosensitizers, and dissolved NO2 may be directly photoactivated at the interface to generate 3SO2*, excited triplet states (T*), HONO, OH•, and other reactive radicals, ,,,− thereby promoting SO2 oxidation. Under interface-dominated conditions, oxidant solubility may shift their relative contributions, with less soluble species, e.g. O3, contributing more at the air–water interface than soluble species, e.g. H2O2, potentially producing distinct oxygen isotope signatures (e.g., Δ17O and δ18O). More broadly, the observed inverse size dependence of SO2 uptake and S­(IV)­(aq) oxidation provides new constraints for kinetic models (e.g., KM-SUB, KM-GAP, and CHAI). ,, The transition from uptake-limited to oxidation-dominated regimes with decreasing size suggests that a single size-independent parametrization may not adequately capture multiphase SO2 chemistry across particle sizes, and a size-dependent parametrization such as provided by CHAI for interfacial aerosol reactions is likely necessary to accurately represent atmospheric sulfur oxidation. Future studies should further address these issues discussed here for size-dependent processes in experiments and in models.
