Impending crisis in oral and maxillofacial surgery in Korea related to the government’s structural transformation project for tertiary general hospitals
Authors: Tae-Geon Kwon
Published: 2026

I. The Government Project for Structural Transformation of Tertiary General Hospitals

The Korean healthcare system is undergoing one of the most extensive reforms in its history. To address long-standing inefficiencies in the healthcare delivery system, the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) has implemented the “Support Project for the Structural Transformation of Tertiary General Hospitals” (hereafter referred to as the Structural Transformation Project). The primary objective of this initiative is to restore the original role of tertiary hospitals by encouraging them to focus on severe, emergency, and rare diseases while reducing the treatment of relatively mild conditions that can be managed at primary or secondary healthcare institutions.

To achieve this goal, the Korean government committed approximately 3 trillion won over a three-year period beginning in 20241. Recently, an additional 800 billion won was allocated for the second year of the project2. The government expects tertiary hospitals to increase the proportion of patients with severe diseases and strengthen their capacity to manage highly complex cases3. It also aims to establish a more efficient healthcare delivery structure by promoting a clearer division of roles among healthcare providers.

However, despite these positive intentions, concerns are emerging regarding the evaluation framework used to determine whether hospitals have successfully achieved the goals of the Structural Transformation Project. In particular, the project requires the proportion of “severe” inpatients to exceed 70% or to increase by more than 3% compared with the previous year4. Subsidies may be suspended if these standards are not met. Tertiary hospitals that fail to adapt to this reclassification may experience financial losses additional charges. As a result, hospitals may increasingly restrict the admission and surgery of patients classified as “mild” under current indicators. These concerns are particularly relevant to oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMS) and several other surgical specialties5.

II. Limitations of the Korean Diagnosis-Related Groups (KDRG) System and Structural Discrepancies

The success of the Structural Transformation Project is largely determined by indicators based on the KDRG system. Hospitals are encouraged to increase the proportion of patients classified in the highest severity categories, and financial incentives are closely linked to these performance indicators.

The fundamental problem is that the current KDRG severity classification does not always reflect the actual clinical complexity of many surgical procedures. Major cancer surgery, organ transplantation, complex cardiovascular surgery, and neurosurgical procedures are generally classified as highly severe conditions, or Specialized Disease Group A (DRG-A). In contrast, many highly demanding oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures are categorized as general (DRG-B) or even simple diseases (DRG-C), despite requiring substantial surgical expertise and healthcare resources6.

Examples include maxillomandibular resection and reconstruction, management of multiple facial fractures, surgery for malignant soft-tissue tumors, complex revision procedures, and reconstructive surgery after severe facial trauma. These procedures frequently involve prolonged operating times, extensive blood loss, difficult airway management, multidisciplinary consultation, postoperative intensive monitoring, and substantial perioperative risk. Nevertheless, many of these cases are not classified as DRG-A within the current severity assessment framework.

This discrepancy illustrates a fundamental limitation of the current KDRG system. Disease severity cannot be fully captured by administrative classifications alone. Surgical complexity, anatomical risk, resource utilization, and perioperative management requirements must also be considered. Similar concerns have been raised by specialists in orthopedics, otolaryngology, ophthalmology, and rehabilitation medicine, suggesting that this issue extends beyond OMS alone7,8.

III. A Paradoxical Threat to Essential Surgical Care

Ironically, a policy designed to strengthen essential healthcare services may inadvertently weaken some specialties that provide indispensable care9.

The Korean population is aging rapidly, and the number of elderly patients requiring oral and maxillofacial surgical treatment continues to increase. Many of these patients have multiple chronic systemic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, respiratory disorders, and cognitive impairment. Their treatment frequently requires multidisciplinary management, sophisticated anesthesia support, and close postoperative monitoring.

These are precisely the patients who should receive treatment at tertiary hospitals. However, if hospitals focus excessively on patient severity indices, they may become reluctant to admit patients whose diagnoses are categorized as non-severe, despite the substantial clinical complexity involved in their care.

This situation creates a paradox. Patients who require tertiary-level expertise may face increasing barriers to treatment simply because existing classification systems fail to recognize the complexity of their conditions. Over time, departments that negatively affect institutional performance indicators may face reduced access to operating rooms and inpatient beds. The consequences could be profound. If clinical activity becomes financially disadvantageous under the current evaluation system, hospitals may gradually reduce investment in these departments. Recruitment and retention of highly qualified surgeons may become increasingly difficult, further accelerating workforce shortages. Ultimately, the sustainability of academic OMS programs and tertiary-level surgical services may be jeopardized.

IV. Unique Challenges Within the Dental Healthcare Delivery System

The potential impact of the Structural Transformation Project may be even greater in dentistry because the dental healthcare delivery system differs fundamentally from the medical healthcare delivery system.

In medicine, patients discharged from tertiary hospitals can often be transferred to secondary general hospitals that have operating rooms, inpatient wards, intensive care units, blood banks, and multidisciplinary consultation systems. Consequently, the government’s strategy of strengthening referral pathways to secondary hospitals can function relatively effectively. In contrast, the dental healthcare system lacks a comparable secondary referral network. Most university dental hospitals and tertiary-level OMS departments provide services that cannot be readily replicated in local dental clinics or small dental hospitals. Facilities capable of managing prolonged general anesthesia, medically compromised patients, severe facial trauma, extensive oncologic surgery, or postoperative airway complications remain limited.

Therefore, transferring these patients outside tertiary hospitals is often impractical. Unlike many medical specialties, OMS cannot simply rely on a well-developed secondary care infrastructure to absorb patients excluded from tertiary institutions. This structural difference must be recognized when healthcare policies are developed and implemented.

V. Conclusion: Toward a More Balanced and Sustainable Policy

Recognizing these concerns, the MOHW recently acknowledged the possibility that patients requiring high-complexity dental treatment under general anesthesia could experience reduced access to care under the current structural transformation framework. The ministry has indicated its intention to develop a more appropriate severity classification system that reflects the unique characteristics of dental hospitals and OMS10,11.

The core issue is that the current evaluation system fails to accurately measure the severity and complexity of OMS care. Future revisions of the KDRG system should incorporate factors beyond disease diagnosis alone. Patient-related variables such as advanced age, multiple systemic diseases, disabilities, anesthesia-related risks, multidisciplinary treatment requirements, and expected resource utilization should be integrated into severity assessment.

A more comprehensive evaluation system would better reflect real-world clinical practice and reduce unintended incentives that discourage hospitals from treating medically complex OMS patients. Such modifications would also help preserve access to tertiary-level surgical care while maintaining the government’s broader goal of improving the efficiency of the healthcare delivery system.
