Beyond the narrow lens: the power of an interdisciplinary approach to climate and health research and training
Authors: Erin Coughlan de Perez, Ahmed Ishtiaque Amin Chowdhury, Glecy Atienza, Sonia Binte Murshed, Ramon Christopher A Caballero, Dennis De La Torre, Aira Joy Delos Angeles, Desderius Haufiku, Alexa Samantha R Hernandez, Md Monirul Islam, Kenneth Lekatsa, Selma Lendelvo, Tandi Litwayi, Makoala V Marake, Joalane Marunye, Mashfiqus Salehin, Kebitsamang Mothibe, Elena Naumova, Mothusi Nyofane, Garimoi Christopher Orach, Mecthilde Pinto, Leah Poole-Selters, Md Hasanur Rahman, Relebohile Agnes Mojaki, Mamakase Grace Sello, Shampa, Akriti Sharma, Nazifa Tabassum, Carolyn Van Sant
Published: 2025

Over the past 15 years, there has been an increasing commitment to educate and develop the health workforce on the impacts of climate change, integrating key competencies related to climate into medical education and training.1–3 Modifications in medical training include the introduction of new core competencies such as climate change into global health curriculum.4

However, to effectively tackle the health impacts of climate change, we argue that integrating climate into medical and public health education is insufficient; rather, we call for an integration of climate and health into a wider variety of disciplines. We are a consortium of researchers working on climate and health from Bangladesh, Lesotho, Mozambique, Namibia, the Philippines, Uganda, and the USA, and we come from a wide range of disciplinary backgrounds (table). We represent educators, researchers, and students. In this Comment, we argue that climate and health curriculum and competencies that are being proposed for the medical community should also be integrated into the curricula of many additional disciplines.

For example, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report chapter on health, wellbeing and the changing structure of communities lists a wide range of adaptation strategies to promote health in a changing climate.5 The table lists a selection of these adaptation strategies, and we identify one non-medical discipline that would support this adaptation in an interdisciplinary manner.

Our vision is that students studying earth sciences, social sciences, and arts are exposed to curricular content on climate and health, with the aim of developing a truly interdisciplinary workforce that can meaningfully reduce the health impacts of climate change. Students with this interdisciplinary education would be equipped to tackle climate and health solutions, such as tailoring disaster risk management plans to community contexts, designing climate-resilient infrastructure that incorporates both hydrology and health information, and applying systems thinking or modelling to evaluate which interventions are working in a given context.

For example, hydrology students would benefit from education in climate and health. Any flood protection embankment, along with its associated structures, constitutes a component of a floodplain system, and hydrologist designers need to fully comprehend the floodplain. Students who go on to design flood protection embankments that are designed for both the hydro-morphological characteristics and the social dimensions of the floodplain will ensure sustained and optimal performance of the embankment, providing long-term health benefits to the population. If the social and environmental dimensions of the adjacent river are not thoroughly understood, the embankment will fail, regardless of its design and construction quality. Similarly, post-disaster reconstruction teams require education in climate and health to ensure that rebuilt infrastructure is protective of human health in a changing climate. In decision-making contexts, stakeholders’ uncertainty and risk perceptions, along with the significance of social tipping points, should be considered for the co-production of climate-resilient infrastructure.

The next generation of students should be equipped to design new creative solutions that make use of the strengths of different disciplines. This approach could include nature-based solutions that work for both climate change mitigation and adaptation, the use of arts and communication techniques to turn behaviour change education into habits, and inventions for novel food preservation techniques (eg, drying and high pressure processing) to address post-harvest loss and food wastage.

Ultimately, we envision that greater integration of climate and health across disciplines would enable graduates to design and implement better policies. Health and health-adjacent policies can reduce inequality, encourage inclusivity, and function in an adaptable way in a changing environment. Food and environmental policies can improve food systems and promote environmentally friendly and sustainable healthy diets, while conserving ecosystems and minimising climate impacts on health.

Suggesting that everyone should learn everything would be pointless; the world needs both generalists and specialists. When it comes to generalists, the unprecedented challenges of climate change require educating students who think critically, adapt to diverse situations, and engage with the complexities of their dynamic world. The student of the future should be a master of multi-disciplinary strategies, to approach problems from multiple perspectives and with critical thinking. The job market is evolving rapidly, with many careers now requiring a blend of skills from different fields. Thus, a generalist education can equip students with the flexibility to adapt to new roles, industries, and challenges, while highly specialist qualifications could limit future opportunities.

In addition, generalist cross-disciplinary knowledge often sparks innovation, enabling students to explore their curiosity, become lifelong learners, and create novel solutions to complex problems. We can recall such experiences in our own careers; for example, one of the author team applied philosophy concepts to a soil classification exam, interrogating the soil classification categories constructed through paradigms of our training and experience. The skill to work across disciplines also teaches communication and collaboration, which is crucial for enabling diverse teams. Cross-disciplinary work requires shared language, which is often a barrier to communication between disciplines.

We accept that advancement in knowledge will always require specialisations, but we argue that future professions will require the ability to hybridise disciplinary knowledge. Thus, for many students, the appropriate path will consist of a specialist education with a secondary specialisation in another discipline. As has been discussed in previous literature, cross-disciplinary expertise can enable public health experts to collaborate more effectively with professionals from other fields, such as soil science, disaster management, urban planning, and environmental science, enabling a holistic approach to addressing the public health challenges caused by a changing climate.6–8 As an example of good practice, the University of California, San Diego, CA, recently introduced a requirement for all students to complete at least one climate course, ensuring exposure to climate across disciplines.9

A secondary specialisation in climate and health should be offered in other disciplines as well. For example, in the field of arts, a secondary specialisation in climate and health can educate a new generation of communicators who appreciate the nuance of geographically rooted cultural behaviour. For example, such specialists can design for the nuances of colour and word choice in emergency communication materials. Arts educators in the Philippines have created handwashing songs, social media materials on climate change, and a children’s television show called Moski Toks, in which mosquito puppet characters provide information about dengue fever. The mosquitoes’ struggle for survival is conveyed in relation to village-level management of the dengue epidemic.

Experiential learning is key for building the needed skills. At the graduate level, this learning might include incorporating semester-long projects with real data sets as part of curricula to require formulating a research question, translating the question into a hypothesis, and creating an analytical roadmap.

Seventeen of the authors who contributed to this Comment are university professors or lecturers, all of whom said that they would aspire to add additional health or climate content to at least one course that they were teaching. We are designing guest lectures, case studies, experiential learning opportunities, and applied data analysis exercises in climate and health for each of our disciplines. Given the efforts to design curriculum materials and core competencies for integrating climate into medical education, we advocate for this curriculum work to go beyond medical education to provide curriculum materials for those studying earth system science, economics, social science, agriculture, and the arts, in addition to many other disciplines that are necessary to fully tackle the health impacts of climate change.
