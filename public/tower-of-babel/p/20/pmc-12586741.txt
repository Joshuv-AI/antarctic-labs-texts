Reliability of different composite materials in aligner treatments: a comprehensıve in vitro study
Authors: Hilal Yildiz, Mustafa Dedeoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear aligner therapy has become increasingly prevalent in orthodontics, with composite attachments serving as critical components for achieving predictable tooth movements. Despite their importance, composite material selection remains largely empirical. This double-blind in vitro study assessed four composite materials' performance as aligner attachments under simulated clinical conditions.MethodsNinety-six extracted human teeth were arranged in upper and lower arch configurations. Four composite systems were evaluated: Omnichroma (79% filler), GC Aligner Connect (orthodontic-specific), G-Aenial Universal Injectable (69% filler, flowable), and Tetric Prime/Evoflow combination (dual-viscosity system). Each underwent aging simulation including 2308 thermal cycles (5–55 °C) and 840 insertion-removal procedures per 12-week period. Digital analysis assessed placement accuracy and volumetric changes at 12, 24, 48, and 96 weeks. Parameters evaluated included operation time, shear bond strength, placement accuracy, and wear resistance. All procedures were performed by a single calibrated operator following double-blind protocols. Polymerization utilized LED curing (1200 mW/cm2, 430–480 nm), verified with radiometry.ResultsThe Tetric combination required significantly longer placement time (31.08 ± 5.14 versus 5.22 ± 0.74 s for GC Aligner Connect, P < 0.001). Three-dimensional analysis revealed the Tetric combination demonstrated 81.24 ± 19.71% overflow versus 44.62 ± 19.91% for GC Aligner Connect (P < 0.001). After 96 weeks, G-aenial Universal Injectable showed significantly greater volume loss (27.40 ± 18.44%) compared to Omnichroma (18.29 ± 6.78%), GC Aligner Connect (17.56 ± 2.18%), and Tetric combination (14.22 ± 3.70%, all P < 0.001). Bond strength testing revealed no significant differences between groups (18.88–23.23 MPa, P = 0.121), with all materials exceeding clinical requirements.ConclusionsMaterial viscosity and filler content substantially influence clinical performance during extended treatments. GC Aligner Connect demonstrated optimal balance of placement efficiency, dimensional accuracy, and durability for routine applications. High-viscosity composites showed superior long-term stability despite longer placement times. Injectable composites offered excellent efficiency but demonstrated accelerated wear after 48 weeks. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for material selection in clear aligner therapy, suggesting material choice should align with anticipated treatment duration and clinical requirements.

Background

The evolution of orthodontic treatment has responded to growing aesthetic demands from patients seeking discrete alternatives to traditional metal appliances. Clear aligner treatment represents more than simply an aesthetic alternative to conventional braces, offering patients the ability to maintain normal oral hygiene, dietary freedom, and social confidence throughout treatment [1, 2].

Composite attachments have become indispensable elements in contemporary aligner therapy, serving as mechanical interfaces between aligners and tooth surfaces. These small resin buttons enable complex tooth movements including rotations, intrusions, and root torquing that would be impossible with aligners alone [3, 4]. Recent advances in composite materials for aligner attachments have been extensively documented. Gazzani and colleagues demonstrated that light-curing processes significantly affect attachment reproduction accuracy when comparing different nanocomposites cured by the auxiliary of a new tool [5]. The precision of aligner fitting on anchorage attachments has been systematically analyzed through scanning electron microscopy by Mantovani et al., revealing critical insights into material-surface interactions [6].

The influence of attachment bonding protocols on placement precision has been comprehensively evaluated by Weckmann et al., who highlighted the importance of standardized procedures for achieving consistent clinical outcomes [7]. Furthermore, Kravitz and colleagues demonstrated that attachment configuration directly influences the accuracy of canine rotation movements during clear aligner treatment, emphasizing the critical role of both material selection and geometric design [8].

Despite their critical importance, composite material selection for attachment fabrication remains largely empirical. Practitioners often rely on manufacturer recommendations rather than evidence-based selection, reflecting a significant literature gap [9]. While extensive research exists on traditional orthodontic bonding materials, limited data addresses the specific requirements of aligner attachments [10]. Recent comparative analyses by Gazzani et al. have begun to address this gap through systematic evaluation of different composite resins used for clear aligner attachments, providing valuable insights into material-specific performance characteristics [11, 12].

The unique demands on aligner attachments differ substantially from conventional orthodontic applications. These structures must withstand repeated insertion-removal forces while maintaining dimensional integrity throughout treatments extending beyond two years [13]. Current clinical practice employs various composite types for attachment fabrication, from high-viscosity packable composites to low-viscosity flowable materials [14].

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of material properties in attachment performance. Barreda et al. documented surface wear patterns of resin composites used for clear aligner attachments [15], while Möhlhenrich et al. evaluated the forces delivered by different attachment configurations [16]. Additionally, the impact of attachment loss on treatment efficiency has been investigated by Yaosen et al., demonstrating that material selection directly affects clinical outcomes [17].

This investigation evaluated the clinical performance of four composite material systems used for clear aligner attachment fabrication, assessing handling characteristics, placement accuracy, long-term durability, and bond strength under controlled laboratory conditions simulating extended clinical use.

Materials and methods

Study design

This comprehensive double-blind in vitro study evaluated four distinct composite material systems representing products commonly used in clinical practice for aligner attachment fabrication. The experimental design incorporated multiple assessment parameters to provide a comprehensive evaluation of material performance under simulated clinical conditions. Primary outcome measures included operation time, placement accuracy, volumetric changes over time, and shear bond strength. Secondary outcomes comprised failure mode analysis and adhesive remnant evaluation. Both the operator performing procedures and the evaluator analyzing results were blinded to material identity through coded specimen labeling.

Material selection

Four composite systems were selected based on clinical relevance and distinct material properties, representing different categories of viscosity and filler content commonly employed in orthodontic practice. Omnichroma (Tokuyama Dental Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) represents a high-viscosity, packable composite with 79% filler content by weight, featuring spherical silica-zirconia fillers. GC Aligner Connect (GC Europe N.V., Leuven, Belgium) is a specialized orthodontic composite specifically formulated for aligner attachments, featuring reduced viscosity for improved handling while maintaining appropriate mechanical properties. This material was developed specifically for aligner applications as a new product with limited scientific evaluation to date.

G-aenial Universal Injectable (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) exemplifies nano-hybrid flowable composites with 69% filler content and 150 nm particle size, offering excellent flow characteristics for precise template filling. The fourth system combined Tetric Prime (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein), a high-viscosity restorative composite with 79–80% filler content, with Tetric Evoflow, a low-viscosity flowable material with 35–40% filler content, representing a dual-material approach recommended for enhanced mechanical properties.

Specimen preparation

Ninety-six extracted human teeth were carefully selected following institutional ethical approval. Teeth were collected for orthodontic, prosthetic, or periodontal reasons and stored in distilled water at room temperature after debris removal. Specimens with caries, restorations, or structural defects were excluded. Sample size calculation was performed with Type I error of 0.05 and power of 0.80, determining 18 specimens per group, expanded to 24 per group to account for potential data loss.

Teeth were arranged in anatomically correct upper and lower arch configurations using acrylic bases to simulate clinical conditions. Each arch was digitally scanned using an intraoral scanner (iTero Element 2, Align Technology, San Jose, CA, USA) to generate stereolithography files for attachment planning. Attachment designs followed standardized specifications with dimensions of 3.0 × 1.2 × 0.75 mm. Customized placement templates were fabricated for each arch to ensure consistent attachment positioning. Bonding protocol

A standardized bonding protocol was implemented to minimize variables related to adhesive procedures. Tooth surfaces were cleaned, etched with 37% phosphoric acid (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) for 30 s, thoroughly rinsed, and air-dried. G-aenial Bond (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was applied as a thin layer, air-thinned for 5 s, and light-activated for 20 s. Composite placement followed manufacturer specifications with template positioning verified before polymerization.

All attachments were light-cured for 40 s using a standardized LED curing unit (3 M Elipar S10, 3 M Unitek, Monrovia, CA, USA) with an irradiance of 1200 mW/cm2 and wavelength range of 430–480 nm. Light intensity was verified with a radiometer before each use to ensure complete polymerization. All procedures were performed by a single calibrated operator following strict double-blind protocols to minimize bias.

Aging simulation protocol

The aging protocol was designed to simulate clinical conditions over extended treatment periods [18]. Four time intervals were evaluated: 12, 24, 48, and 96 weeks, representing short, medium, and long-term treatment scenarios.

Thermal cycling was performed in a calibrated thermal cycling unit between 5 and 55 °C with 30-s dwell times and 5-s transfer periods, totaling 2308 cycles per 12-week period. This protocol was based on ISO/TS 11405:2015 standards for testing adhesion to tooth structure [19]. Mechanical aging involved standardized insertion and removal procedures performed 840 times per 12-week period, simulating average patient compliance with 10 daily aligner changes [20], using the custom apparatus shown in Fig. 1. A custom-designed apparatus ensured consistent force application during insertion-removal cycles. Assessment parameters

Operation Time Measurement: Placement time was recorded from initial composite dispensing through completion of placement procedures, excluding standardized preparation and curing times. Timing was performed using a digital chronometer with 0.01-s precision.

Placement Accuracy Analysis: Digital scanning technology (iTero Element 2) enabled precise three-dimensional analysis of attachment reproduction accuracy. Post-placement scans were superimposed on original digital designs using specialized software (Geomagic Control 2024.2, 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA) to quantify dimensional deviations and overflow characteristics using best-fit alignment algorithms.

Volumetric Change Assessment: Serial digital scans at each time interval allowed calculation of progressive volume loss through sophisticated three-dimensional analysis. Individual attachment files were processed using Netfabb Premium 2024 (Autodesk, San Rafael, CA, USA) to enable precise volumetric measurements. Volume changes were calculated as: Volume Loss (%) = [(Initial Volume—Final Volume)/Initial Volume] × 100.

Mechanical Testing: Shear bond strength testing employed a universal testing machine with standardized parameters. A knife-edge blade was positioned parallel to the tooth surface at the attachment-enamel interface. Testing was performed at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/minute following ISO 29022:2013 standards. Shear bond strength was calculated using the formula: SBS (MPa) = Force (N) / Bonding Area (mm2).

Failure Mode Analysis: Stereomicroscopic examination at 40 × magnification enabled classification of failure modes (adhesive, cohesive, or mixed). Adhesive remnant patterns were evaluated according to the modified Adhesive Remnant Index used in recent orthodontic studies [21]. Classification was performed by a single calibrated examiner blinded to material group to ensure consistency and minimize bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluation employed IBM SPSS 22 software package with appropriate parametric and non-parametric tests based on data distribution characteristics assessed by Shapiro–Wilk test. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) assessed between-group differences, while paired t-tests evaluated within-group changes over time. The significance level was established at P < 0.05, with Tukey HSD post-hoc testing used for multiple comparisons when indicated. All analyses were performed with evaluators blinded to group assignments.

Results

Clinical handling and operation time

The evaluation of placement efficiency revealed substantial differences between material systems (F = 229.2, P < 0.001), as summarized in Table 1 alongside material properties. The Tetric combination required the longest placement time, averaging 31.08 ± 5.14 s per attachment, reflecting the complexity of managing two materials with different handling characteristics and the need for precise proportioning between components.

Omnichroma demonstrated intermediate placement times of 27.10 ± 6.81 s, consistent with expectations for high-viscosity composites requiring manual adaptation and contouring. In contrast, the injectable systems showed markedly superior efficiency, with GC Aligner Connect requiring only 5.22 ± 0.74 s and G-aenial Universal Injectable averaging 7.32 ± 0.86 s per attachment, differences that are clinically significant for practice efficiency.

Bond strength evaluation

Mechanical testing revealed all composite systems achieved bond strengths well above clinically acceptable thresholds, with mean values ranging from 18.88 ± 6.89 MPa (Tetric combination) to 23.23 ± 8.01 MPa (Omnichroma). Failure mode analysis

Failure mode analysis revealed predominantly mixed failure patterns across all groups, with similar adhesive remnant distribution patterns. Adhesive failures ranged from 37.5% (Omnichroma) to 45.83% (GC Aligner Connect), while cohesive failures were minimal across all groups (4.16–8.33%). The predominance of mixed failures (50–58.33%) suggests optimal bond strength levels that balance clinical effectiveness with safe debonding characteristics (Fig. 5).

Failure mode analysis

Failure mode analysis revealed predominantly mixed failure patterns across all groups, with similar adhesive remnant distribution patterns. Adhesive failures ranged from 37.5% (Omnichroma) to 45.83% (GC Aligner Connect), while cohesive failures were minimal across all groups (4.16–8.33%). The predominance of mixed failures (50–58.33%) suggests optimal bond strength levels that balance clinical effectiveness with safe debonding characteristics (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This comprehensive evaluation provides new insights into composite material performance for aligner attachment applications and offers evidence-based guidance for clinical material selection. The findings demonstrate that material properties significantly influence both immediate procedural outcomes and long-term treatment success, aligning with recent investigations by Gazzani et al. who emphasized the importance of material selection in attachment reliability through their comparative analysis of different composite resins [11, 12].

Aging protocol validation

Our 96-week aging protocol substantially extends beyond the 8-month clinical observation period reported by Li and Yang [23], who documented progressive attachment volume loss in clear aligner patients. While their clinical study provides valuable real-world wear patterns, our laboratory simulation enables controlled evaluation over extended periods following ISO/TS 11405:2015 standards [19]. This extended timeframe reveals non-linear degradation patterns not observable in shorter clinical studies, particularly the exponential wear acceleration in flowable composites that becomes apparent only after 48 weeks of simulated use, as clearly demonstrated in both Table 2 and Fig. 6.

The additional insights provided by extended simulation are particularly valuable for understanding the non-linear nature of material degradation. Our results demonstrate that while short-term performance (12–24 weeks) showed minimal differences between materials, pronounced performance distinctions emerged at 48 weeks and became highly significant at 96 weeks. This finding corroborates the light-curing process observations by Gazzani et al., who noted that material-specific polymerization characteristics significantly influence long-term stability [5].

Clinical failure mechanisms: bond strength versus geometric degradation

Our findings reveal an important paradigm in attachment failure mechanisms. While all tested materials achieved bond strengths (18.88–23.23 MPa) substantially exceeding clinical requirements (6–8 MPa), the progressive volume loss data suggests geometric degradation represents the primary limiting factor for attachment longevity. The 27.40% volume loss observed in G-aenial Universal Injectable at 96 weeks constitutes sufficient geometric alteration to compromise aligner fit and force delivery, independent of adhesive integrity, as shown in the three-dimensional analysis (Fig. 3).

This observation aligns with the scanning electron microscopy analysis by Mantovani et al., who demonstrated that surface wear patterns directly affect aligner seating and force transmission [6]. Surface characteristics observed in our SEM analysis (Fig. 7) corroborate these findings, showing distinct wear patterns correlating with material viscosity. The findings indicate that clinicians should implement systematic attachment monitoring based on treatment duration rather than waiting for visible bond failures, particularly when using flowable composite systems in treatments exceeding 18 months.

Material-specific performance characteristics

The superior performance of GC Aligner Connect in multiple parameters deserves particular attention. Despite being marketed as an orthodontic composite with reduced viscosity, this material demonstrated placement times comparable to flowable composites while maintaining wear resistance approaching that of high-viscosity materials, as evident in both Table 2 and Fig. 6. This unique combination of properties may be attributed to its proprietary formulation specifically developed for aligner applications (Table 1), incorporating thixotropic behavior that facilitates placement while maintaining structural integrity. The influence of material selection on treatment outcomes has been further validated by Kravitz et al., who showed that attachment precision directly affects the accuracy of planned tooth movements, particularly for complex rotations [8]. Our results suggest that material selection should be considered alongside attachment geometry when planning challenging movements.

Recent investigations have provided additional context for our findings. The systematic review by Robertson et al. highlighted the importance of attachment material properties in treatment efficiency [24], while Dalaie et al. demonstrated that composite type significantly affects force delivery in aligner systems [25]. These studies reinforce our observation that material selection has direct clinical implications beyond simple mechanical retention.

Evidence-based clinical decision framework

These findings support the development of an evidence-based decision framework where material selection aligns with treatment characteristics and practice requirements. For routine cases with anticipated treatment durations under one year and moderate complexity, injectable systems like GC Aligner Connect offer optimal efficiency without compromising outcomes. This recommendation is consistent with the comparative analyses by Gazzani et al., who found similar performance patterns when evaluating different composite systems in their recent studies [11, 12].

For complex cases requiring extended treatment periods, high-viscosity composites provide superior durability despite procedural considerations. The additional chair time required for placement may be justified by reduced replacement frequency and improved treatment predictability. This approach aligns with the precision-focused methodology advocated by both Kravitz et al. and Weckmann et al. for achieving accurate tooth movements [7, 8].

The clinical implications extend to treatment planning strategies. As demonstrated by Papadimitriou et al., attachment design and material selection should be integrated into the initial treatment planning phase [26]. Our findings suggest that anticipated treatment duration should be a primary consideration when selecting composite materials, with longer treatments benefiting from high-viscosity options despite increased placement time. Limitations and future directions

While this investigation provides valuable insights, several limitations merit consideration. The in vitro nature of the study cannot fully replicate the complex oral environment, including variations in salivary composition, pH fluctuations, and individual patient factors such as parafunctional habits. Recent work by Bilal has emphasized the importance of patient-specific factors in attachment retention [27], suggesting that our standardized testing may not capture all clinical variables.

Future research should incorporate patient-specific factors including occlusal forces, dietary habits, and compliance patterns to refine material selection algorithms. The development of artificial intelligence algorithms for predicting attachment performance, as explored by Liu et al. [28], may offer personalized material selection based on individual patient characteristics. Additionally, investigation of novel composite formulations specifically designed for aligner attachments may yield materials that optimize both handling characteristics and long-term durability.

Clinical translation

These findings establish evidence-based guidelines for attachment material selection based on anticipated treatment duration. For treatments under 12 months, injectable systems like GC Aligner Connect provide optimal efficiency without compromising durability. Extended treatments exceeding 18 months benefit from high-viscosity composites despite longer placement times, or alternatively, planned attachment replacement protocols when using flowable systems.

The practical implementation of these findings should consider practice workflow and patient factors. As suggested by Kuo et al., standardized protocols for material selection can improve treatment predictability [29]. Clinicians should also consider the recent advances in digital treatment planning, as reviewed by Bichu et al., which allow for more precise attachment design and placement [30].

Conclusions

This comprehensive evaluation of composite materials for aligner attachment applications provides evidence-based guidance for clinical material selection. The findings demonstrate that material properties significantly influence both immediate procedural outcomes and long-term treatment success, supporting the need for thoughtful material selection based on treatment requirements.

GC Aligner Connect emerged as the most well-rounded material for routine applications, offering an optimal combination of placement efficiency, dimensional accuracy, adequate durability, and reliable bond strength. High-viscosity composites demonstrated superior long-term durability that makes them particularly suitable for extended treatment applications despite requiring longer placement procedures.

All tested materials demonstrated clinically acceptable bond strengths well above established thresholds, confirming that proper adhesive protocols can achieve reliable retention regardless of composite selection. However, the significant differences in wear resistance and volumetric stability highlight the importance of matching material properties to treatment duration and complexity.

These findings support the development of clinical protocols that match material selection to treatment requirements, moving beyond manufacturer recommendations toward evidence-based material selection for optimizing clear aligner treatment outcomes. As clear aligner therapy continues to evolve, the selection of appropriate attachment materials will remain critical for achieving predictable and efficient orthodontic treatment.
