Alternative splicing serves as a molecular strategy for saliva evolution and diversification in an endoparasitoid, Pteromalus puparum
Authors: Jiamin Shi, Chun He, Fang Wang, Gongyin Ye, Yi Yang, Qisheng Song, Xinhai Ye, Qi Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnimal saliva represents a powerful model for investigating adaptive evolution. In parasitoid wasps, salivary proteins are known to modulate host cellular and humoral immunity. However, the functional significance of widespread alternative mRNA isoforms derived from salivary genes remains largely unexplored. To fill this gap, we applied an integrative full-length isoform sequencing and profiling pipeline in the endoparasitoid wasp Pteromalus puparum, enabling the reconstruction of a high-resolution transcriptomic landscape of salivary genes.ResultsA total of 133 high-confidence salivary genes were identified, more than 75% of which produce multiple transcript isoforms. Mass spectrometry analysis suggested that alternative splicing contributes to salivary proteome diversity, with eight genes encoding distinct protein isoforms. Notably, 12 salivary genes displayed differential isoform usage with elevated expression in salivary glands relative to the carcass. A striking example is P. puparum serpin3 (PpSerpin3), whose short isoform is specifically expressed in both salivary and venom glands. Functional assays revealed that this short isoform actively suppresses host humoral melanization. Given that active components in injected venom may gradually lose their efficacy, we propose that salivary secretions function to sustain host manipulation throughout parasitization. Comparative multi-omics analyses further showed that although salivary and venom systems share a conserved core genetic toolkit, they achieve functional specialization via tissue-specific gene family co-option and extensive isoform switching.ConclusionThis study presents an isoform-resolved transcriptomic framework of the parasitoid salivary system. The findings indicate that alternative splicing contributes to salivary protein diversity by encoding distinct protein products and, further, facilitates salivary gene evolution through gland-specific isoform switching. Background

Animal saliva embodies a functionally complex and evolutionarily dynamic molecular system, providing a valuable model to investigate adaptive evolution [1–3]. The diversity and specificity of salivary proteins have been shaped by persistent host-predator (or host-parasite) arms races and ongoing environmental adaptation [4–6]. The genomic architecture underlying salivary adaptation is shaped by a suite of non-mutually exclusive molecular mechanisms, including gene duplication followed by neofunctionalization, gene co-option, alternative splicing (AS), horizontal gene transfer, and de novo origination [7–13]. However, current understanding of salivary transcriptomes relies predominantly on short-read RNA-seq data, which lack the resolution to accurately identify full-length transcript isoforms or distinguish closely related paralogs [14–16]. Full-length transcriptome sequencing provides an unambiguous reference for precise annotation of splice variants and novel genes, offering a clearer view of the evolutionary innovations shaping these secretory systems [17].

Parasitic wasp saliva, a crucial bioactive secretion produced during the larval stage, serves as an important biochemical agent for manipulating host physiology and ensuring successful offspring development [18]. Research indicates that these secretions comprise a complex cocktail of bioactive molecules designed to precisely dismantle host defense systems [19–21]. These findings collectively highlight the specialized functions of parasitoid saliva shaped by co-evolutionary arms races, making it a suitable model for studying salivary adaptation. However, current research relies mainly on crude salivary extracts, which fail to provide an unbiased proteomic profile and often misses low‑abundance or novel effector proteins [22], and the consequent lack of a high-quality molecular blueprint hinders a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms by which hosts are manipulated.

Here, we present a high-resolution, isoform-resolved transcriptomic atlas of the salivary glands from the globally distributed parasitoid wasp, Pteromalus puparum. By integrating this isoform landscape with quantitative expression and proteomic data, we examine the contribution of AS to salivary protein diversification and trace the evolutionary origins of these isoforms. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying salivary gene innovation and functional specialization.

Material and methods

Insect rearing

Laboratory strains of P. puparum were reared on pupae of the host, Pieris rapae, which were fed on insecticide-free cabbage leaves in a greenhouse at Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China. Both wasps and hosts were maintained under standard rearing conditions: a 14L:10D photoperiod at 25 ± 1 °C [23]. Mated, 2-day-old female wasps were provided with host pupae for a 12-h parasitization period. Following emergence, adult wasps were kept in plastic containers on a 20% (v/v) honey solution diet.

RNA sequencing

To comprehensively characterize the transcriptome, we employed two complementary strategies: PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing for isoform resolution and strand-specific Illumina RNA-Seq for quantification. For PacBio sequencing, 100 P. puparum larvae were first iced at −20 °C for 5 min to reduce motility prior to dissection. Salivary gland (SG; including both the salivary gland duct, SGD, and salivary gland reservoir, SGR) and carcass (CA, i.e., female larval tissues excluding SG) tissues were dissected from the same 100 individuals, with SG and CA pooled separately. Total RNA was extracted immediately after dissection using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was then stored at −80 °C until all samples were collected. RNA quality was rigorously controlled: integrity was confirmed via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and agarose gel electrophoresis, while purity and concentration were measured using Nanodrop and Qubit systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Only RNA samples meeting stringent criteria (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, and RIN ≥ 8) were processed for library construction. For PacBio HiFi sequencing, approximately 300 ng of high-quality RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the NEBNext Single Cell/Low Input cDNA Synthesis & Amplification Module and Iso‑Seq Express Oligo Kit. The resulting cDNA was purified with ProNex Beads and converted into SMRTbell libraries using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences), following the manufacturer’s protocol for end repair, A‑tailing, and adapter ligation. Final library templates were annealed with sequencing primers, complexed with polymerase, and sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform with the Sequel II Binding Kit 2.0. PacBio replicates were omitted due to sample limitations and the exploratory goal of isoform discovery.

For Illumina short-read sequencing, three tissue types of P. puparum larvae were collected: SGD, SGR and corresponding CA. For each tissue type, three biological replicates were prepared. Each replicate of the SGD and SGR consisted of a pool of approximately 100 individual tissues. Total RNA was subsequently isolated from every sample using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Ribosomal RNA was depleted from total RNA using the Ribo‑clean rRNA Depletion Kit Mega (Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were then constructed with a target insert size of 300 bp for paired-end sequencing. Finally, all libraries were sequenced individually on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. Library preparation and sequencing for both PacBio and Illumina platforms were performed by Grandomics Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Processing and annotation of PacBio Iso-Seq data

Full-length isoforms from PacBio data were identified using the IsoSeq v3 pipeline [24] and SQANTI3 framework [25]. Specifically, we generated circular consensus sequences (CCS) with ccs, setting the minimum predicted accuracy to 0.9 (–min-rq 0.9). We removed primers and performed demultiplexing with lima using the –isoseq and –peek-guess flags. The resulting full-length, non-chimeric (FLNC) reads were refined with Isoseq3 refine requiring a polyA tail (–require-polya). To improve transcript quality, FLNC reads were clustered using Isoseq3 cluster with the –use-qvs option to generate high-quality consensus isoforms. These polished isoforms were aligned to the reference genome of P. puparum (GenBank accession: GCA_012977825.3) using minimap2 [26]. We used cDNA_Cupcake v28.0.0 [27] to collapse redundant transcripts, where the –dun-merge-5-shorter option and filter_away_subset.py were sequentially applied to merge and remove potential 5′ degradation products. Structural quality control and annotation improvement were carried out with SQANTI3 [25]. Specifically, the sqanti3_filter.py script was employed to exclude low-confidence isoforms, including those flagged as RT-switching artifacts, intra-priming events, or non-canonical transcripts with default parameters, ensuring a high-quality transcript set for downstream analysis. We then merged these filtered isoforms with the existing genome annotation of P. puparum (OGS2.1) [28] to generate an integrated transcriptome. Coding potential was assessed with TransDecoder v5.5.0 [29], which integrates homology evidence from BLASTP searches against UniProt (released in February 2021) [30] and domain predictions from Pfam v35.0 [31].

Transcript quantification

Raw Illumina reads were quality-filtered using fastp v0.22.0 [32]. Gene and isoform expression levels in SGD, SGR, and CA were quantified from the nine Illumina RNA-seq datasets using Salmon v1.9.0 [33]. The expression levels were reported as transcripts per million (TPM) and read counts, respectively. Differential expression analysis between tissue pairs was subsequently performed using DESeq2 v1.30.1 (padj < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1) [34], with three biological replicates per tissue.

Alternative splicing analysis

AS events were profiled using SUPPA2 v2.3 [35], which identified seven major types: skipped exon, retained intron (RI), mutually exclusive exons, alternative last exon (AL), alternative first exon (AF), alternative 5′ splice site, and alternative 3′ splice site. Differential splicing analysis between tissue pairs (SGD, SGR, and CA) was subsequently performed with the same tool, utilizing three biological replicates per tissue, each from an independent pool of ~ 100 larvae. Events with a P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially spliced.

Gene ontology annotation and enrichment analysis

We annotated protein sequences from all species using eggNOG-mapper v2 [36] against the eggNOG5 database. Based on the resulting Gene Ontology (GO) annotations, GO enrichment analysis of the candidate gene set was performed with GOATOOLS [37]. P-values for each GO term were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05.

Saliva proteome

Prior to saliva collection, approximately 200 cleaned larvae were subjected to a 6-h starvation period on sterile water-moistened filter paper, followed by three sterile water rinses. Saliva was then induced using an SDZ-II nerve and muscle stimulator (Huatuo, China) set to continuous-wave mode at 50 Hz and 2.5 mA [18]. Following 15 s of stimulation, which induced a distinct curling response, emerging saliva droplets around the mouthparts were carefully collected with a 1 μL pipette tip into 0.4 μL PBS. Saliva protein concentration was quantified by the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Collected salivary proteins (200 μg) were reduced with 100 mM DTT (5 min, 100 °C) and alkylated with 100 mM IAA in UA buffer after buffer exchange through a 30 kDa filter. Due to limited sample availability, no biological replicates were included in this proteomic analysis. After successive washes with UA buffer and 25 mM NH₄HCO₃, proteins were digested with 4 μg trypsin in 100 mM NH₄HCO₃ (37 °C, 16–18 h). The resulting peptides were collected, desalted using a C18 cartridge (Sigma), lyophilized, and reconstituted in 40 μL of 0.1% formic acid. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker) coupled to nanoElute (Bruker Daltonics). The peptides were loaded onto a homemade C18-reversed phase analytical column (25 cm long, 75 μm inner diameter, 1.9 μm, C18) in buffer A (0.1% formic acid) and separated using a linear gradient of buffer B (99.9% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mass spectrometer operated in positive ion mode, collecting ion mobility MS spectra within a mass range of m/z 100–1700 and 1/k0 of 0.75–1.35. It then performed 10 cycles of PASEF MS/MS with a target intensity of 1.5 k and a threshold of 2500. Active exclusion was enabled with a release time of 0.4 min. The raw data were processed using MaxQuant v2.0.3.1 [38] and searched against a custom protein database consisting of the annotated protein sequences of P. puparum, which were obtained from the transcriptome assembly generated by the PacBio Iso-Seq processing and annotation pipeline described above.

Identification of salivary genes

Salivary genes were identified using an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic strategy [28]. Candidate selection was based on gland-specific enrichment, defined as TPM value above N90 in either the SGD or SGR. Final validation required definitive proteomic evidence, specifically the detection of at least two unique peptides with perfect matches to the predicted protein sequences by mass spectrometry. Salivary genes were manually classified into functional categories based on their gene names and known functions as described in previous studies [39, 40].

Isoform analysis of salivary genes

To characterize salivary gene isoforms, transcript annotations were extracted from the integrated transcriptome annotation (GTF file) generated by the full-length Iso-Seq processing and annotation pipeline (see “Processing and annotation of PacBio Iso-Seq data”). Differential isoform usage was analyzed with IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR v1.21.0 [41]. Transcript-level abundances from Salmon were imported and filtered using preFilter with default settings: genes with an overall mean expression < 1 were removed, isoforms with an overall mean expression < 0 or an isoform fraction (IF) < 0.01 were discarded, and single-isoform genes were excluded. Isoform switches were tested using isoformSwitchTestDEXSeq. Resulting P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. To identify isoforms encoding distinct proteins, mass spectrometry data were manually curated. A protein was validated as a salivary component if it was supported by at least one perfectly matched peptide, as alternatively spliced isoforms typically differ in limited regions (e.g., a single exon or a unique junction) and requiring two or more unique peptides would systematically miss many genuine isoform-level events. The genomic coordinates of validated peptides were then converted to BED format for visualization in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).

Identification of venom and saliva gene families

We annotated the protein domains of each venom and salivary gene using HMMER version 3.4 [42] against the Pfam database [31], applying an e-value threshold of 1e-5. Genes sharing the same Pfam domain were classified into a single family.

Analysis of serpin genes in additional Pteromalid wasps

To investigate the conservation of serpin3 isoforms across Pteromalidae, we analyzed publicly available genome assemblies and salivary gland transcriptomes from three additional species: Nasonia vitripennis, Pteromalus qinghaiensis and Pteromalus venustus. Genome assemblies and raw RNA-seq reads have been deposited in public databases under the accessions provided in Table S1. Raw reads were quality-filtered with fastp v0.22.0 [32] and aligned to their respective reference genomes using Hisat2 v2.2.1 [43]. Transcriptomes were assembled with StringTie v2.1.7 [44], and protein-coding sequences were predicted using TransDecoder v5.5.0 [29]. Putative orthologs of Ppserpin3 were identified through BLASTP searches of the predicted proteins against the Ppserpin3 protein query combined with TBLASTN searches against the genome assemblies. The expression levels of isoforms of the identified serpin3 genes were quantified using Salmon v1.9.0 [33].

Recombinant protein expression, purification and antibody preparation

The short isoform (PB.2036.2) of P. puparum serpin3 (PpSerpin3) was amplified and cloned into the pCold I vector (primers are listed in Table S2). After sequence confirmation, the recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for protein expression. Expression was induced with 0.1 mM IPTG when the culture reached an OD600 of approximately 0.6, followed by incubation at 10 °C for 20 h. The recombinant protein was extracted and purified by His-tag affinity chromatography, and then used to generate a rabbit polyclonal antibody for downstream applications.

Immunofluorescence of PpSerpin3

For immunofluorescence, SGs from P. puparum larvae and venom glands (VGs) from P. puparum adults were dissected, washed, and processed as described previously [45]. The dissected tissues were incubated with rabbit anti-PpSerpin3 (diluted 1:500), followed by Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (diluted 1:500). F-actin and nuclei were counterstained with Phalloidin-FITC (1 μg/mL) and DAPI (1 μg/mL), respectively. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope and processed with ZEN 3.2 software (Zeiss).

Effects of PpSerpin3 on host hemolymph melanization

Prophenoloxidase (PPO) activation and phenoloxidase (PO) activity assays were performed to assess the effect of PpSerpin3. For the PPO activation assay, diluted P. rapae hemolymph was incubated with PpSerpin3 (at concentrations ranging from 62.5 to 1000 μg/mL) in a 384-well plate. Micrococcus luteus activator and L-DOPA substrate were then simultaneously mixed in by brief centrifugation. For the PO activity assay, hemolymph was pre-activated with M. luteus and then incubated with 500 μg/mL PpSerpin3, after which PO activity was measured. Absorbance at 470 nm (A470) was monitored kinetically over 120 min. Controls included PBS (a blank control), N-phenylthiourea (PTU, a positive control), and recombinant catalase (CAT, a negative control). All assays were performed five times. One unit of PO activity was defined as 0.001 ΔA470/min. Statistical significance was determined using Student′s t-test for pairwise comparisons and ANOVA followed by Tukey′s test for multiple-group comparisons.

Results

A high-resolution isoform atlas from salivary gland

PacBio Iso-Seq analysis of P. puparum SGD, SGR, and CA generated 1,452,526 CCS reads, yielding 1,447,882 high-quality FLNC reads. These reads were clustered to produce 62,680 polished consensus transcripts. Detailed statistics for the sequencing libraries and Iso-Seq workflow are provided in Table S3. After deduplication and filtering, 27,160 non-redundant isoforms representing 8,121 gene loci were retained. By integrating these results with a previously reported gene set [28], we established an updated P. puparum annotation, named Ppup_OGSv2.2, which comprises 18,426 genes and 40,026 isoforms (Fig. 1B). Of these, 97.5% of genes (17,979) and 96.4% of isoforms (38,584) were predicted to be protein-coding (Fig. 1C). All subsequent analyses in this study were based on Ppup_OGSv2.2.

The Iso-Seq processing, structural quality filtering, and integration with the reference annotation produced a high-resolution isoform atlas for larval P. puparum. For downstream tissue-specific analyses, we applied an expression threshold of TPM > 1; under this criterion, 5,889 genes expressed multiple isoforms, with 2,848 multi-isoform genes in SGD, 2,985 in SGR, and 3,740 in CA (Fig. 1B). Notably, 428 genes produced more than 10 isoforms each, highlighting substantial transcript diversity (Table S4). Collectively, these results establish a precise, isoform-resolved transcriptomic atlas for this species, providing a foundational resource for investigating tissue-specific AS dynamics.

Prevalence and complexity of alternative splicing

Based on the high-quality isoform annotation, AS was comprehensively profiled. Seven major AS event types were identified (Fig. 1D; Table S5). In total, 27,971 AS events corresponding to 3,894 genes were detected, with canonical GT-AG splice sites predominating (Fig. 1E). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were significantly associated with biological regulation processes (FDR < 0.05). Furthermore, 1,881 genes exhibited co-occurrence of multiple AS event types, highlighting the complexity of splicing regulation. Comparison between SG tissues revealed that SGD and SGR shared highly similar AS profiles, with both tissues exhibiting the same dominant event types (AF and AL) and a conserved distribution pattern of RI events, despite a slightly higher total event count in SGD. This suggests the presence of a core splicing program common to the SG. In contrast, when compared to the CA, the SG (including both SGD and SGR) displayed a substantially higher number of both AS-associated genes (3,439 vs. 2,272) and total AS events (11,544 vs. 10,502). Collectively, these results show a greater number of AS events and AS-associated genes in the SG than in the CA, suggesting a potentially more prominent role for AS in transcriptomic diversification and functional regulation in SG.

Characterization and isoform diversity of salivary genes

By requiring high expression in the salivary gland (TPM > N90 in either SGD or SGR) and proteomic confirmation by at least two unique peptides (see Table S6 for salivary proteome results), we identified 133 salivary genes in P. puparum, 97 of which met the expression criteria in both the SGD and the SGR (Fig. 2A; Table S7). Functional classification revealed that enzymes (42.9%) constitute the majority of salivary proteins (Fig. 2B). We next investigated the prevalence of multiple isoforms among these salivary genes. Of the 133 genes, 102 (77.4%) were found to generate multiple transcript isoforms. Using a TPM threshold of 1, we confirmed that 98 genes expressed between 2 and 97 isoforms in either SGD or SGR, while the remaining 4 genes expressed only a single isoform (Fig. 2C; Table S8). Further examination revealed that 77 of these multi-isoform genes encode distinct protein products (Fig. 2C). Corroborated by mass spectrometry analysis, eight salivary genes were found to produce multiple distinct protein isoforms, each supported by at least one unique peptide (Fig. 2C). These genes encode proteins with diverse functions, including proteases (e.g., kallikrein-8, trypsin-3), hydrolases, and other categories. Collectively, this integrated multi-omic evidence suggests that AS contributes to salivary protein diversity in parasitoid wasps.

Alternative splicing may drive salivary evolution

To investigate the role of AS in salivary evolution, we assessed tissue-specific isoform usage by comparing SG with CA. Significant differential isoform usage (ΔIF > 0.4, q < 0.05) was observed for 267 isoforms from 172 genes between SGR and CA, with 131 isoforms (from 131 genes) preferentially used in the SGR (Table S9). Between the SGD and CA, 151 isoforms from 109 genes showed differential usage, of which 76 isoforms were upregulated in the SGD. Notably, 36 isoforms exhibited significantly elevated usage in SG tissues (SGD and SGR) compared to CA, revealing a conserved pattern of tissue-regulated isoform switching specific to the salivary system. Direct comparison between the two salivary tissues further revealed considerable divergence: 142 isoforms from 105 genes showed significant differences in usage between SGD and SGR. This divergence may reflect their distinct functional roles in saliva transport versus storage and secretion, suggesting that AS fine-tunes gene expression to support these specialized tasks. In total, 233 isoforms from 182 genes exhibited elevated usage specifically in salivary tissues. Interestingly, only 12 of these genes were previously annotated as salivary genes, indicating that isoform-level regulation represents a widespread but previously underappreciated mechanism underlying SG functional specialization. Together, these results demonstrate that tissue-specific isoform regulation has played an important role in SG evolution, potentially enabling the functional recruitment of non-salivary genes through AS.

Differential isoform usage of Ppserpin3 is associated with saliva recruitment

A striking example of tissue-specific isoform switching was observed for PpSerpin3 (Ppup_OGSv2.2 ID: LOC.12955), a gene encoding proteins present in both venom and saliva. According to our updated annotation, PpSerpin3 produces four major isoforms (Fig. 3A). In CA, expression was dominated by the long isoform (PB.2036.1), which encodes a tandem two-domain serpin protein and accounted for 82.5% of total PpSerpin3 isoform usage (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the short isoform (PB.2036.2), which retains only the C-terminal serpin domain, was almost exclusively utilized in the SG, representing 96.4% and 96.2% of usage in the SGD and SGR, respectively (Fig. 3C; FDR-corrected P ≤ 2.28e–77). This salivary-specific pattern mirrored the preferential usage of the same short isoform in the VG and was further corroborated by significant differential expression between SG and CA (Fig. 3B; FDR-corrected P ≤ 2.49e–10). Critically, mass spectrometry analysis detected peptides uniquely mapping to the C-terminal domain of the short isoform, confirming its secretion as a salivary protein (Fig. 3B). Collectively, the highly specific recruitment of the short PpSerpin3 isoform illustrates a potential role for AS in contributing to salivary evolution.

Given the conserved presence of the long, two-domain PpSerpin3 isoform across Hymenoptera, we investigated whether the specific upregulation of its short, single-domain isoform in the SG represents a shared evolutionary feature. To this end, we analyzed high-quality genome assemblies and SG transcriptomes from three additional Pteromalid wasps. Genome-guided assembly confirmed the presence of the short isoform in each species. Isoform quantification revealed that, consistent with the pattern in P. puparum, this short isoform was highly expressed (TPM > 120) and dominant (~ 90% of total isoform usage) in the SGs of three species (Table S10). This conserved expression pattern is consistent with a possible role for preferential utilization of the short PpSerpin3 isoform in salivary gland functional specialization within the Pteromalidae.

Multi-level molecular parallels between venom and salivary protein repertoires

To explore whether saliva sustains the initial effects of venom or has evolved independent functions, we performed a multi-level comparative analysis between adult VG and larval SG. At the gene level, 23 genes were directly shared between the two systems (Fig. 4A). GO enrichment analysis showed that salivary-specific genes were associated with terms related to hemostasis and chitin metabolism, whereas venom-specific genes were enriched for terms including protein synthesis and energy metabolism. At the gene family level, 28 of 135 identified families were shared by the two systems (Fig. 4B; see Table S11 for the complete list). The trypsin-like serine protease family was the most abundant in both systems. System-specific recruitment was also observed: SGs uniquely recruited families including Glyco_hydro_18&CBM_14, Metallophos&SapB_1, EF-hand, and BTB, while VGs specifically recruited families including eIF3, HSP90, Thioredoxin, and AhpC-TSA (Fig. 4C).

Comparative isoform analysis further revealed extensive transcript-level divergence between venom and salivary systems. Significant differences in isoform usage were detected for 715 isoforms between VG and SGD, and for 803 isoforms between VG and SGR, with 481 isoforms consistently altered across both salivary tissues (Table S12). Among the 23 shared genes between the two systems, four exhibited clear isoform switching. For example, kynurenine aminotransferase uses AF to produce system-specific isoforms (Fig. 4D), while a beta-1,3-glucan binding protein employs AS to generate distinct protein variants (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these findings, along with the observed patterns of shared gene families and system-specific recruitment, indicate that the salivary system retains a functional link to the venom system but has diverged substantially through precise transcript-level regulation to fulfill its distinct role in sustained host interaction.

Tissue distribution of PpSerpin3

Recombinant PpSerpin3 was successfully expressed and purified, and a corresponding polyclonal antibody was generated (Fig. S1). Using this antibody, we assessed the tissue distribution of the PpSerpin3 protein within the wasp by immunofluorescence. Confocal imaging confirmed abundant expression of PpSerpin3 in both the larval SG (Fig. 5A) and the adult VG (Fig. 5B). In these images, PpSerpin3 is shown in red, the cytoskeleton in green (phalloidin-stained F-actin), and nuclei in blue (DAPI). Notably, higher-resolution imaging revealed differential subcellular localization: the protein was primarily localized to the cytoplasm in SG cells (Fig. 5A') but predominantly at the cell membrane in VG cells (Fig. 5B′).

PpSerpin3 suppresses host hemolymph melanization

To determine whether PpSerpin3 modulates host hemolymph melanization, we assayed PPO activation and PO activity. The PPO activation assay revealed that PpSerpin3 inhibited host hemolymph PPO activation (Fig. 5C; Student′s t-test: t = 8.97, df = 8, p < 0.001). At 120 min, the A470 value following treatment with 500 μg/mL PpSerpin3 was 1.14, significantly lower than that of the blank control (about 1.45) and the negative control (about 1.57). Dose-dependent suppression of PPO activation by PpSerpin3 was evaluated using six concentrations. The resulting relative PO activity (derived from A470 at 120 min) showed that concentrations above 125 μg/mL significantly suppressed PPO activation compared to the blank control (Fig. 5E). In contrast, the PO activity assay showed that 500 μg/mL PpSerpin3 did not affect PO activity (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

AS has been suggested to contribute to innovations in gene function or protein neofunctionalization over extensive evolutionary timescales, and may also play a role in facilitating rapid evolutionary innovations [46–49]. While gene duplication followed by neofunctionalization and horizontal gene transfer have been widely implicated in the origin of insect salivary proteins, particularly in hematophagous and hemipteran species, the contribution of alternative isoforms to salivary system evolution remains largely unexplored [7, 11, 50–54]. Our high-resolution isoform atlas of the P. puparum SG suggests that AS is pervasively involved in functional specialization. Although SGD and SGR share a conserved core splicing program and similar gene repertoires, detailed analysis uncovers pronounced tissue-specific regulatory divergence. A substantial subset of isoforms exhibits differential usage between these tissues, indicating that AS may contribute to post-transcriptional fine-tuning, which adapts gene output to meet their distinct physiological demands [46, 55]. This widespread isoform diversity expands the salivary proteome and suggests that functional differentiation may be achieved through precise isoform-level regulation rather than through distinct gene sets [56]. In the host-parasitoid arms race, AS-mediated isoform co-option may provide an efficient evolutionary route for generating new effector functions [57, 58].

The Ppserpin3 locus exemplifies isoform-specific recruitment in salivary evolution. A short, single-domain isoform is highly expressed in both the SG and VG, whereas the long, two-domain isoform predominates in the corresponding CAs. Its confirmed secretion and conserved expression across related wasp species indicate that this may represent an evolutionarily stable strategy. Functional assays demonstrate that the short isoform suppresses host melanization, a key immune defense, indicating that this isoform switch could contribute to immune regulation [59, 60]. Together, these results suggest that AS can generate functional salivary effectors, consistent with the view that isoform-specific recruitment facilitates the evolution of host-manipulating traits. However, it should be noted that functional analysis focused primarily on PpSerpin3, and whether other isoform switches have similar effects remains unknown. Besides, the evolutionary analysis was based on a small number of parasitoid species, and broader taxonomic sampling will be required to generalize the conclusions.

Given that active components in injected venom may gradually lose their efficacy [61, 62], we sought to explore whether saliva sustains the initial effects of venom or has evolved independent functions. Comparative analysis reveals that the salivary system of P. puparum retains core components of the venom system but has undergone functional specialization. Although shared genes point to a common evolutionary ancestry, divergence in gene family recruitment likely reflects their distinct physiological roles. However, because this comparison involves adult VG and larval SG, it is important to note that some observed differences may also reflect developmental stage effects in addition to gland‑specific specialization. The trypsin-like serine protease family, the most abundant in both systems, has been suggested to play a role in the prolonged regulation of host immunity [63]. System-specific recruitment may further indicate functional divergence: SGs uniquely recruit families involved in cuticle or membrane remodeling (e.g., Glyco_hydro_18&CBM_14, Metallophos&SapB_1) and potential intracellular modulation (e.g., EF-hand, BTB) [64–67], whereas VGs specifically recruit families involved in protein synthesis and folding (e.g., eIF3, HSP90), which could reflect an adaptation for high‑efficiency production and precise regulation of venom components [68, 69]. The specific recruitment of venom genes containing Thioredoxin and AhpC-TSA domains may also suggest a specialized adaptation for coping with oxidative stress [70, 71]. Extensive isoform switching among shared genes further suggests that AS is a potential mechanism contributing to this functional divergence [72]. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the possibility that the salivary system may have evolved partly as an extension of venom‑based mechanisms, potentially co‑opting and building upon this molecular foundation to support prolonged host manipulation during parasitism.

Taken together, our findings suggest that AS and isoform-specific regulation are contributing molecular mechanisms driving the evolution and functional specialization of parasitoid SGs. This mechanism contributes to salivary protein diversity and may enable functional innovation by generating new effector capabilities from conserved gene loci. These findings provide a perspective on secretory system evolution, highlighting transcriptional and post-transcriptional plasticity as an important, yet previously underappreciated, factor in shaping host-manipulative traits. Future work aimed at deciphering the cis-regulatory elements and transcription factors that control salivary isoform switches and tracing their evolutionary history across parasitoid lineages will further clarify the molecular and evolutionary basis of this regulatory strategy.

Conclusion

This study presents a high-resolution, isoform-resolved transcriptomic atlas of the parasitoid wasp salivary gland, providing a foundational resource for understanding the molecular complexity of secretory systems in parasitoids. Our integrative analysis suggests that AS contributes to the diversification of salivary proteins and may enable tissue-specific functional specialization through precise isoform switching. The case of PpSerpin3 exemplifies how isoform-specific recruitment may generate potentially relevant effector functions, such as suppression of host melanization, from evolutionarily conserved gene loci. Collectively, these findings highlight AS as a potentially important contributor to salivary system evolution, offering insights into the post-transcriptional mechanisms that may shape host-parasitoid interactions and providing a valuable framework for comparative studies of secretory tissue specialization across insects.
