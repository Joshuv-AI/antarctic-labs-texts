Phaco-endocyclophotocoagulation versus phaco-trabeculectomy in advanced glaucoma: A pilot study
Authors: Vanita Pathak-Ray, Kushal Goud Taduri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To investigate the safety and efficacy of combining minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) endocyclophotocoagulation (ECP) in advanced primary glaucomas and compare them with those of the current gold standard of phaco-trabeculectomy (PT).Methods:This is a retrospective study of subjects with advanced primary glaucomas (primary open angle glaucoma and primary angle closure glaucoma), more than 30 years, and who underwent ECP or trabeculectomy with Mitomycin C along with phaco-surgery. The primary outcome measure was IOP. The secondary outcomes were the number of anti-glaucoma medications (AGMs), best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), total success (defined as complete and qualified), complications, and interventions for these.Results:Twenty-eight eyes with phaco-ECP and a mean follow-up of 18 months were compared to 24 eyes that underwent PT (mean follow-up of 16 months). IOP reduced from 24.9 ± 12.9 to 14.0 ± 2.3 mmHg in the former group, and from 26.7 ± 8.7 to 13.7 ± 3.0 mmHg in the latter (p=.667); AGM was reduced from 3.6 ± 1.0 to 0.9 ± 1.0 and 3.5 ± 1.1 to 1.4 ± 1.5 in each group, respectively (p=.624). No sight-threatening complications occurred in either group; however, BCVA improved significantly in the phaco-ECP group (p=.04). Though complications were more in the PT group, and so were the interventions (excluding suturelysis) they were marginally short of reaching significance (p=.05). No loss of vision occurred in either group. There were two failures in the PT group.Conclusion:Combining ECP with phaco has the potential to be as effective as the current gold standard in advanced primary glaucomas. It is safer with significantly improved BCVA and a much-reduced need for any interventions. Larger studies and longer follow-ups are recommended.

Glaucoma is the second most common cause of blindness worldwide, with primary open angle glaucoma (POAG) and primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) being the most common diseases.[1] PACG is the scourge of our country as the morbidity caused by this disease is almost equivalent to that of POAG, even though the incidence of PACG is much lower, globally[1] as well as in India.[2]

The backbone of the management of glaucoma is in the form of anti-glaucoma medications. However, when intraocular pressure (IOP) is uncontrolled or when glaucoma progresses in either POAG or PACG, then a trabeculectomy is performed. In the presence of visually significant cataracts, phaco-trabeculectomy is the gold-standard surgery.

However, angle closure disease exists in a spectrum and the management of the disease depends upon the presentation – in terms of IOP, presence or absence of peripheral anterior synaechiae (PAS) on gonioscopy, disc damage, and visual field changes. Moreover, the crystalline lens and the presence of a plateau iris may coexist and contribute to the closure with mechanisms other than relative pupillary block. The factors related to the natural crystalline lens – its anterior position, thickness, increased lens rise, and opacity – all can have an impact on angle closure, so early removal of the lens is quite often recommended, with an ever-growing body of information in favor of it. Therefore, of late, there has been a gradual change in the thought process and early extraction of the lens has been proposed as a management option in PACG, even when it is clear,[3] though this may be a somewhat contentious issue. Nonetheless, when PAS in the angle is extensive, then a lens extraction alone may not be sufficient in controlling IOP in PACG. Under these circumstances, the current gold standard surgical management is a phaco-trabeculectomy.[45] However, a trabeculectomy presents some unique sight-threatening complications – inclusive of, but not limited to, flat anterior chamber, aqueous misdirection, hyphema, hypotony, choroidal detachment, bleb leak, blebitis, bleb-associated endophthalmitis, and failure of filtration.[6] Thus, it becomes imperative to find newer, better, and safer solutions for PACG (and other glaucomas). Minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGSs) have gained inroads in the last decade or two and offer safer alternatives, mostly in POAG. One such modality is combining endocyclophotocoagulation (ECP) with phaco to control IOP while visually rehabilitating the patient. It affords extremely precise laser ablation of the ciliary body, with reduced postoperative inflammation. This approach has been successful in POAG[7] pseudophakic and aphakic glaucoma,[8] pediatric glaucomas,[9] and even refractory glaucomas.[10] Most notably, very favorable outcomes have been reported in PACG recently in the Indian population.[1112]

ECP has been reported to be very safe; the ECP Collaborative Study Group reviewed 5824 patients following ECP and found that the incidence of serious complications of hypotony and choroidal exudation is extremely low.[13]

In this pilot study, we investigate the safety and efficacy of combining the specific MIGS procedure, ECP, with phaco in eyes with visually significant cataracts, in advanced primary glaucomas and compare them with those of the current gold standard of phaco-trabeculectomy (PT).

Methods

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the surgical procedure, and the study was approved by the ethics committee and review board (IRB) vide AHJ-C-S-011/07-24. It was conducted following the tenets laid down by the Declaration of Helsinki.

A review of all subjects with advanced primary glaucomas, who had coexisting visually significant cataracts, and had undergone either ECP or Trab MMC along with phaco surgery, at a tertiary level practice, was done. The study period was September 2021 to August 2023. Only those subjects who had a documented postsurgical follow-up of at least 3 months were included, and only those eyes with a mean deviation (MD) > -12 dB on Humphrey Visual Field (HVF) 24-2 program were included in either group. In cases where HVF 24-2 with stimulus size III was not possible, then 10-2 or the macular program was done with stimulus size V. Furthermore, in cases where poor vision precluded an HVF altogether, the disc assessment determined the advanced stage of the disease.

In both groups, subjects with any previous intraocular surgery or developmental glaucomas or those who had phaco-ECP or PT in complex refractory glaucomas (previous filtering surgery, corneal graft, retinal surgery, etc.) were excluded.

Relevant history for all subjects including the use of AGM was recorded, and each subject underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination. This included best-corrected Snellen’s visual acuity (BCVA) assessment, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, IOP assessment with Goldmann applanation tonometry (Zeiss SL 130 slit lamp with Goldmann style applanation tonometer AT 030), gonioscopy with Sussman 4-mirror under standard dark-room conditions, and dilated stereo fundoscopy with +90D Volk lens (Volk Instruments, OH, USA). Visual fields were also documented by the Humphrey field analyzer (HFA) – to determine the staging of the disease.

Inj. Mannitol 20% (1–3 mg/Kg, Claris Otsuka, In) was administered preoperatively, when indicated.

All surgeries were done by a single fellowship-trained senior surgeon and were performed in the operating room under sterile conditions and sub-tenon anesthesia. Only up to 5 ml of anesthetic agent lignocaine 2% and bupivacaine 0.5% (in a ratio of 3:2) was used.

Procedure of Phaco-ECP: A clear corneal phaco through a 2.2 mm wound was completed, and a foldable intra-ocular lens implant (IOL) was inserted in the bag. Following this, cohesive viscoelastic was used to create space in the sulcus and then a 19-gauge curved endoscope (BVI Endo Optiks®, Beaver International) was introduced through the phaco wound. The ciliary processes (CPs) were visualized, and the laser was delivered under direct vision. The endpoint was whitening and shrinkage of CP, and this determined the power of the laser delivered. The laser energy varied between 250 and 500 mw (or 0.25-0.5 W), with the duration being constant at 2000 ms (or 2 seconds); ‘pops’ were strictly avoided, and whenever they occurred, they warranted an immediate reduction in power. Only approximately 180–210 degrees were treated through the single phaco wound.

At the end of the procedure, the viscoelastic was washed, the wound was checked for watertightness, and a sub-tenons steroid was administered (Dexamethasone 4 mg).

Postoperatively, all the eyes were administered topical steroids (difluoro prednisolone butyrate acetate, or DFBA 0.05%, Entod pharmaceuticals, India) for 1 week, followed by loteprednol in tapering dose over 4 weeks and Moxifloxacin Hydrochloride 0.5% (Vigamox, Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA) four times daily for 1 week, and, if required, cycloplegic eye drops (Homatropine Hydrochloride 2%, Homide, Warren Excel, In) thrice daily was also used for 3–7 days. All the anti-glaucoma medications were discontinued and were reintroduced as per indication.

The procedure of phaco-trabeculectomy was by and large the same as has been described in detail elsewhere,[14] with one notable exception—Mitomycin C (MMC) was used in an injected form also. All the patients received 20 micrograms of injected MMC 0.05 ml of a 0.4 mg/ml concentration of freshly reconstituted MMC via an insulin syringe and injected 8–10 mm away from the limbus superiorly. Before the injection, the eye was prepared with three applications of povidone-iodine 5% (Aurodone, Aurolabs, India), topical anesthesia, and topical dilating eyedrops. Following this, either the patient was given sub-tenons anesthesia 5 ml as described above or surgery was done under topical anesthesia with supplementation of nonpreserved lignocaine 2% under the conjunctiva with a blunt cannula after conjunctival opening. Intracameral nonpreserved lignocaine 2% was also used. A circumferential superior conjunctival incision was made 1–1.5 mm posterior to the limbus for about 3–4 mm (approximately 1 clock hour), leaving a conjunctival frill, supero-nasally in the right eye (RE) and supero-temporally in the left eye (LE). A deep conjunctival pocket was dissected, and a partial thickness 3 × 2 mm rectangular scleral flap was reflected. One sponge, fashioned out of a weck-cel spear, was soaked with MMC and placed under the scleral flap for 1 minute. The conjunctival wound edge was held with atraumatic forceps and kept dry with a weck-cel sponge. After 1 minute of exposure, copious irrigation of MMC was done. Using a 2.2 mm keratome, the anterior chamber (AC) was entered through a clear-corneal tunnel, also superiorly at an adjacent site (supero-temporal RE and supero-nasal LE), and phacoemulsification was performed by a vertical direct-chop technique. After in-the-bag placement of a foldable acrylic IOL, the phaco incision was closed with an interrupted 10-0 nylon suture. Following this, corneal block excision was done using a Descemet punch underneath the scleral flap; a peripheral iridectomy was performed in all cases. The scleral flap was closed with two 10-0 nylon sutures. Using 10-0 vicryl on a vascular tapering needle, conjunctiva-to-conjunctiva closure was done with running mattress suturing. At the end of the surgery, the wound was tested for leakage along with bleb formation by injecting a balanced salt solution through the paracentesis.

Postoperatively, topical steroid drops (difluoro prednisolone butyrate acetate, or DFBA 0.05%, Entod Pharmaceuticals, India) were used hourly for 1–2 days, 2-hourly for 2 weeks, and thereby tapered slowly over 12 weeks. Topical antibiotics Moxifloxacin (Vigamox, Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA) was used four times daily for 1–3 weeks, and topical cycloplegic eye drops (Homide, Homatropine 2%, Indoco Remedies, India) was used for 1–2 weeks or as per requirement.

Data points in both the groups for IOP, AGM, BCVA, and complications were day 1, week 1, week 6, week 12 (month 3), week 26 (month 6), week 52 (year 1), and at last follow-up postoperatively. Eyes may have had more frequent follow-ups as necessary.

The primary outcome measure was IOP with complete success being defined as an IOP >5 and ≤18 mmHg without medication; qualified success was defined as meeting these criteria with medication. Failure to meet these criteria and/or requirements for reoperation (trabeculectomy or glaucoma drainage device, GDD or transcleral-cyclophotocoagulation, TSCPC) was defined as failure of the phaco-ECP procedure. A re-do trab or GDD or ECP/TSCPC were the procedures considered following the failure of the index trab.

Secondary outcome measures included LogMAR BCVA, number of AGM, complications, and interventions. Complications were documented as early, <3 months postsurgery and late, >3 months postsurgery. Early complications that were specifically identified were wound leak, hyphema, shallow AC, fibrin/hypopyon, cystoid macular edema, hypotony, choroidal detachment, aqueous misdirection, and others.

The following late complications were documented: hypotony, cystoid macular edema, late bleb leak, blebitis, endophthalmitis, failure of procedure, and others.

Statistical methods: Descriptive statistics data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine parametric data. Univariate comparisons were performed using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon Signed Rank test within the group and the Independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for between-group comparisons. Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Snellen visual acuity was converted to a logarithm of minimal angle of resolution (logMAR) for analysis. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p <0.05.

Results

During the study period of September 2021 to August 2023, 52 eyes with advanced glaucoma and a postsurgical follow-up of at least 3 months were identified. All subjects had an advanced primary glaucoma – either PACG or POAG. Twenty-eight eyes that underwent phaco-ECP and a mean follow-up of 18 months were compared to 24 eyes with PT (mean follow-up of 16 months). Though age and MD on the HVF 24-2 plot differed between groups, the preoperative IOP, number of AGM, and BCVA did not. The Mean MD in PE group was -19.6 ± 5.4, 95% CI [-21.7, -17.5], and that in the PT group was -24.4 ± 6.8, 95% CI [-27.3, -21.5]. Three eyes in the phaco-ECP group and seven eyes in the phaco-trab group could not perform the HVF test either due to poor vision or because it could not be performed with stimulus size III.

Success: Complete success in the phaco-ECP group was 32%, and total success (inclusive of qualified success) was 96.8%; there was one failure in this group, as per the predefined characteristics. On the other hand, complete success was 37.5% in the PT group, and total success was 91.7% with 2 failures (p not significant). The two eyes that failed in the phaco-ECP group both were PACG. One eye had bleb failure and underwent Ahmed Clear Path 250; the other PACG eye failed on IOP criterion (had IOP > 18 on 3 AGM).

There was one failure in the PE group (also PACG) – IOP became uncontrolled after approximately 1.5 years on 3 AGM in a known Brimonidine allergy. This one-eyed patient underwent Ahmed Glaucoma Valve.

Discussion

This study compared the surgical outcomes of phaco-ECP with those of the current gold standard phaco-trab in advanced primary glaucomas and found that the reduction in the IOP and the number of AGM was similar in both the groups, but the BCVA was better, and the complications and interventions were lower in the phaco-ECP group.

ECP is the original MIGS as described by Uram in the early 1990s[15] – a diode laser is incorporated in an endoscopic probe with a 670-nm laser to provide the aiming beam and a 175 W xenon light source. A 19-G probe requires a wound not larger than 2.2 mm, and the procedure is entirely ab-interno through the phaco-wound. In a review article on the use of ECP in moderate to advanced glaucomas, Kaplowitz et al.[16] reported a reduction in the IOP in the range of 3.9 to 10.9 mmHg by various authors. However, this was only in POAG, whereas we obtained an IOP reduction of 10.9 mmHg in a cohort comprising advanced glaucoma in both PACG and POAG, representing a 43.7% reduction. In the same review article, studies in POAG reported a corresponding decrease in the number of AGMs ranging from 0.06 to 1.3. Our study demonstrated a greater reduction in the number of AGMs (2.7, representing a 75% decrease). This reduction in the IOP and AGM is consistent with previously published reports in Indian eyes in PACG.[1112]

Furthermore, in contrast to most other MIGS, it is not angle-dependent at all. But like the other MIGS, it is very safe, especially with our protocol that targets only approximately half of the ciliary body through the anterior route. Therefore, the risk of hypotony is extremely low. In the Collaborative ECP study, very few eyes developed serious complications such as choroidal hemorrhage (0.09%), hypotony/phthisis (0.12%), or loss of vision (0.12%) in a cohort of 5824 eyes that received ECP.[13] Clement et al.[17] in their study found fibrin, hyphema, and CME to occur relatively more commonly – 22%, 11%, and 10%; Lima et al.[18] have also reported a 22% rate of occurrence of fibrin on the first postoperative day despite subconjunctival dexamethasone injection. We did not encounter fibrin at all since our practice changed from subconjunctival[12] to sub-tenons injection of dexamethasone. Hyphema too was not seen in our study, but CME occurred in two eyes.

Most notably, none of the eyes developed hypotony or lost vision >2 Snellen lines. On the contrary, when compared to PT, the phaco-ECP group demonstrated significant improvement in vision.

On the other hand, complications and interventions in trab and phaco-trab have been well described in the literature.[619] Perioperative complications of trabeculectomy as obtained in our study (41.6%) are somewhat less when compared to that reported in the Collaborative Initial Glaucoma Treatment Study (CIGTS) – a 50% rate.[6] Kirwan and colleagues[19] in their survey of over 400 eyes declared that good trabeculectomy outcomes with low rates of surgical complications can be achieved, but intensive proactive postoperative care is required. This is reflected in our study too as intense proactive care of blebs was undertaken to avoid failure. Kirwan reported a 43% suture manipulation rate, 17% for bleb needling, and worsening of vision of > 2 Snellen lines occurred in 5.6%; the corresponding values in our study are 58.3%, 16.6%, and 4.2%. Previous studies comparing Phaco-ECP and Phaco-Trab have also reported significantly more complications and interventions in the latter group.[1220] Bakr and colleague[20] in their study with a 4-year follow-up recommend phaco-ECP as primary intervention when cataract and glaucoma coexist.

All bleb-related complications are avoidable if gentle cyclo ablation is done. Furthermore, it can be repeated if required and if in the off chance that it is still not effective on repetition, it does not preclude, or compromise, future gold standard surgery, which may be undertaken, in case IOP does not get controlled by laser alone.

Overall, phaco-ECP is a very safe and effective procedure, and as opposed to the gold standard surgery, it takes much less time (mean 17.8 minutes) when compared to phaco-trab (42.5 min), as already reported before.[12] The average number of visits postoperatively before updated spectacles were prescribed also differs significantly (phaco-ECPL 3.45 visits vs. 5.2 visits in PT).[12] Both these aspects make phaco-ECP a very patient-centric procedure, with a positive impact on their quality of life. Added to this, the absence of a bleb negating the chance of bleb dysaesthesia and a lifelong risk of blebitis and bleb-related endophthalmitis makes phaco-ECP a very desirable procedure from the patient’s (as well as the provider’s) perspective.

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, there are inherent limitations – the most important of which is a selection bias. It is possible that the relatively more advanced glaucoma patients with poorer vision received PT rather than phaco-ECP. However, the pre-op characteristics of IOP, AGM, and BCVA were well matched. Other perceived limitations are the small sample size and relatively short follow-up; however, this study was undertaken to check the feasibility of phaco-ECP, a MIGS procedure, in advanced glaucoma.

In any case, the selection of consecutive patients of a rather homogeneous cohort (advanced primary glaucomas), without the inclusion of secondary glaucomas and standardised surgeries by a single senior surgeon, ensured that at least these confounders were eliminated.

To summarize, combining ECP with phaco has the potential to be as effective as the current gold standard in advanced primary glaucomas, with a reduction of AGM and improvement of BCVA, and a much-reduced need for any interventions. The gold standard procedure needs more surgical time, is prone to several intraoperative and postoperative complications, and frequently requires postoperative manipulations, outpatients as well as operating room-based, to control the IOP. All of this requires skill and time for training. Furthermore, the bleb, as created by the surgery, carries a lifelong risk of infection leading to loss of sight, independent of the disease process. Failure of PT can and does compromise the results of subsequent surgery too.

Conclusion

Phaco-ECP is as efficacious as phaco-trab, but it is a safer and faster surgery, significantly improving vision, with early visual rehabilitation. It is repeatable too and can delay the need for future filtration surgery, if required at all. Larger studies and longer follow-ups are recommended to validate these results.
