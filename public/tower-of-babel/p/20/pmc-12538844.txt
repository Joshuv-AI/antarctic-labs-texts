TLR2 c.374C>T variant linked to natural resistance against brucellosis in Italian Mediterranean river buffalo
Authors: Andrea Fulgione, Antonio Gentile, Valentina Iovane, Vincenzo Peretti, Angela Michela Immacolata Montone, Alfredo Pauciullo, Rosanna Capparelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrucellosis poses a significant threat to human health and the livestock industry, particularly affecting small-scale farmers. Several studies have demonstrated that TLR2 can recognize the Brucella abortus (B. abortus) surface lipoproteins (i.e. Omp10, Omp16 and Omp19), thus activating the immune response. This process can be altered by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in TLR gene. This study aims to investigate whether the presence of SNPs in TLR2 could influence the resistance against brucellosis in Italian Mediterranean river buffalo, through case-control study and computational analysis. The study involved 194 Italian Mediterranean buffaloes, from three herds located in Caserta (Campania, Italy), grouped into 98 positive and 96 negative to brucellosis. Both groups, aged 40-90 months, were raised under similar environmental and management conditions to ensure consistency in the sampling. ResultsThe integration of association study with in silico analysis demonstrated that the c.374T, located in exon 2 of the TLR2 gene, could be associated with resistance to brucellosis (OR = 0.61; p-value = 0.037).ConclusionsThis study highlights how TLRs genetic variations may influence protein activity and resistance to brucellosis. The genotyping analysis combined with in silico investigation could be considered an efficient approach for detecting possible candidate marker of resistance to buffalo brucellosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05067-2.

Introduction

Brucellosis is a serious disease in farmed animals; it causes infertility in both sexes, late term abortion, decreased milk production throughout the animal’s lifespan, and it incurs considerable costs for sanitary treatments and for the reorganization of the entire herd [1–3]. These issues have caused significant economic losses for the livestock industry, particularly affecting small-scale farmers who rely on the sale of meat and dairy products. In addition, there are also indirect costs for disease management such as those of the veterinary services, vaccination, diagnostics, farmer identification, and the cost of a system for animal identification and surveillance records [3]. Despite the development and application of control and eradication plans for several years, brucellosis – caused by Brucella abortus (B. abortus) - remains a problem in different regions of South Italy such as Campania and Sicilia, and particularly in buffalo and cattle herds. The Italian Mediterranean Buffalo has been officially recognized by the Italian Ministry of Agricultural Food and Forestry Policies, and the population is monitored through a herd book (established in 1980), and from the National Association of Bubaline Species Breeders (ANASB). Actually, the population consists of 440,023 animals, and they are principally used for milk production [4, 5]. In Southern Italy, the river buffalo has significant economic importance, as its milk is used to produce buffalo mozzarella cheese PDO (Protected Designation of Origin). Campania has the highest concentration of buffalo herds in Italy, with approximately 80% of the region’s buffalo population located in the province of Caserta, an area where brucellosis incidence remains high (about 4.1% at November 2024) [6, 7]. Most of the reported genomic studies on river buffalo are mainly focused on the association of candidate genes with economic production traits, such as milk yield and composition [8–13]; while the association with diseases resistance such as brucellosis or tuberculosis is less investigated [14–19].

Many studies have demonstrated that B. abortus can be recognized by Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs) 4 or 2 [20–22] as well as a recent study has summarized the interaction between brucellosis and other TLRs [23]. Specifically, TLR2 recognizes different ligands of gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria, such as the lipoteichoic acid peptidoglycan, specific types of Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and several Brucella strains lipoproteins. Molecular cloning and sequencing studies have allowed the identification of three genes encoding the outer membrane lipoproteins (Omp10, Omp16 and Omp19) of B. abortus, able to interact with TLR2 [24, 25]. Further studies have confirmed the presence of these lipoproteins on the surface of B. abortus and their ability to induce the release of both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators [24, 26, 27]. After the pathogen recognition, TLR2 requires heterodimerization with TLR1 or TLR6 for activating the intracellular signalling [28].

In buffalo, TLR2 consists of two exons (the first not coding at all) with 2355 bp open reading frame encoding 784 amino acids [29]. It is able to recognize pathogens thanks to its structural features: an extracellular N-terminal domain containing 16 to 28 leucine-rich repeats (LRRs) which are involved in pathogen detection and heterodimerization process with TLRs like TLR1 (i.e. Dimerization Domain), and an intracellular C-terminal TIR domain (i.e. Cytosolic Domain), which is essential for triggering downstream inflammatory signaling [30]. The presence of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within TLR genes can affect the function of TLR ligands, thus potentially compromising the host susceptibility to infectious or inflammatory diseases [18, 30–32]. The bioinformatics tools and Molecular Dynamic Simulations (MDS) analysis can predict how variations in protein structure, due to the presence of SNPs, influence the susceptibility to different diseases [19, 33].

However, despite the recognized importance of brucellosis in Italian Mediterranean river buffalo, the role of TLR2 genetic variation in influencing susceptibility or resistance to B. abortus infection has not yet been investigated. In a recent study, in silico analysis (i.e. bioinformatics tools and MDS analysis) was used to predict the impact of non-synonymous SNPs (nsSNPs) on protein structure and function, before proceeding with the association study. Specifically, this approach has allowed the identification of a possible association between the two TLR4 polymorphisms c.672A >C and c.902G>C and susceptibility to brucellosis in Italian Mediterranean Buffalo [19].

Based on the above considerations, this study aims to identify TLR2 gene polymorphisms associated with brucellosis resistance in Italian Mediterranean buffalo, by using a combined approach of association study and MDS analysis, to support genetic selection strategies for disease control and to reduce both the economic burden and the health risks caused by B. abortus infection.

Materials and methods

Sampling

All DNA samples were from a previous study which enrolled 194 unrelated Italian Mediterranean Buffalo raised in three herds located in the province of Caserta (Campania region, Italy) and divided in positive (98 cases) and negative (96 controls) to B. abortus infection [19], in accordance with the official tests (i.e. buffered Brucella antigen test and complement fixation test) reported in the Annex III of h the EU Regulation 2020/689 [34] and DGRC 104/2022 of Campania Region [35]. All herds were in the same geographical area and managed under comparable extensive systems, with similar environmental conditions, management practices, and brucellosis prevalence. This sampling was carried out according to a non-probability sampling method, from November 2022 to September 2023. Cases and controls were lactating buffaloes between 40 and 90 months of age and were from the same source population; by this way all animals had a comparable level of exposure to the pathogen. Furthermore, the biosecurity measures and exposure history were similar between cases and controls, supporting the assumption of comparable infection risk. The DNA concentration and optical density (260/280 ratio) of each sample were assessed by using the Nanodrop ND-2000C Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to ensure that no significant variations occurred during storage. The A260/A280 ratios for all the DNA samples ranged between 1.8 and 1.95.

Primer design and sequencing analysis

The DNA region of the TLR2 gene (bTLR2) spanning exon 2, was amplified by end-point PCR in 20 Mediterranean river buffaloes (10 brucellosis-positive and 10 negative) using two primer pairs designed from the Mediterranean river buffalo genome sequence (GenBank accession no. PZYV01000009.1; nucleotides 69,087,912 to 69,090,267). Specifically, six samples (three brucellosis-positive and three brucellosis-negative) were randomly selected from each of the first two herds, while eight samples (four positive and four negative) were randomly selected from the third herd.

All primers were designed using DNASIS-Pro software 3.0 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) (Table 1 and Additional file 1). The PCR reaction included 200 ng of genomic DNA, 3 mM MgCl2, 200 nmol of each primer, dNTPs each at 400 µM, 0.5 U of Taq DNA Polymerase (Promega, WI, USA). The thermal protocol consisted in 1 cycle at 97 °C for 2 min; 35 cycles at 94 °C for 45 s, 60.0 °C for 45 s and 72 °C for 2 min and a final cycle at 72 °C for 10 min. All PCR products were analysed directly by electrophoresis in 1.5% agarose gel (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) in 0.5X TBE buffer and stained with Safe View Classic (Applied Biological Materials Inc, BC, Canada). The PCR products were Sanger sequenced in outsourcing on both strands by CEINGE Institute (Naples, Italy) to identify potential SNPs and they were also analysed by the allele specific-PCR (AS-PCR).

Amino acid conservation analysis and SNPs analysis

To identify the common conserved domains, a comparison between the deduced amino acid sequence of bTLR2 and the TLR2 of 20 other mammalians recoded in GenBank (Additional file 2), has been performed. Specifically, the amino acid conservation analysis was carried out by using Clustal omega software (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo) and the results were analysed by means of JalView software (version 2.11.3.2), to identify the common conserved regions showing a minimum of 50% of similarity [36–38]. Additionally, SNPs resulting in amino acid substitutions within the domain involved in heterodimerization with TLR1, were analysed for Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) to assess potential haplotype block structures [39].

Genotyping analysis

To identify the river buffalo carriers of TLR2 SNPs c.374C>T and c.1034G>A, two genotyping methods based on AS-PCR were set-up. The PCR reagents and concentrations were the same used for sequencing, while PCR protocols have been modified according to the annealing temperatures, which were specific for each polymorphism. The primers are reported in Table 2. 10% of the PCR products from each group were Sanger sequenced at CEINGE Institute (Naples, Italy).

bTLR2-bTLR1 complex homology modelling

For developing the bTLR2-bTLR1 complex by using the Swiss Model tool (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/), the Mediterranean river buffalo TLR1 amino acid sequence was deduced from the nucleotide sequence deposited in GenBank (acc. no PZYV01000022.1 from nucleotide 59,348,864 to 59,356,878). The human protein structures, retrieved from Protein Data Bank (PDB) and selected as model for this analysis were: 5d3i.1.A and 6nih.1.A for TLR2 and TLR1, respectively. The quality of the developed models was evaluated according to the following parameters: Ramachandran plot; normalized QMEAN Score; QMEAND global score. YASARA (version V.24.9.26) web Server was used to address clashes and outliers of the bTLR2, thus improving its structural integrity [40].

Docking analysis

Docking analysis was carried out to provide structural features of the heterodimerized complex bTLR2-bTLR1, essential for triggering the inflammatory response. The interaction between bTLR2 and bTLR1 was analysed under both constrained and unconstrained docking conditions by using the LZerD Server (version V.2023.20.17) [41–44]. The docking parameters were a Clustering cut-off of 4.0 and a Surface Coarsen Max Cost of 1e−2. Among the resulting models, the best one showing the lowest Ranksum score value was adopted as reference complex for the Molecular Dynamic Simulations (MDS) and Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (APBS) analyses [44].

Molecular Dynamic Simulations (MDS)

To better understand the amino acid (aa) substitution effects on the binding affinity of bTLR2 with bTLR1, the MDS analysis was carried out by means of GROMACS (v.2023.2) [45, 46]. Considering ASS (i.e. Alanine at position 125 and two Serine at position 161 and 345, respectively) as bTLR2 reference type (REF), the first step involved the analysis of the complex bTLR1/bTLR2 when a single aa substitution was present: A125V, S161T or S345N. The second step, instead, analysed the complex in which two or all aa substitutions were presents in bTLR2 protein: VT (A125V-S161T); VN (A125V-S345N); TN (S161T-S345N); or VTN (A125VS161T-S345N). All these results were compared with the bTLR1/bTLR2 no mutagenized (REF), obtained by the previously docking analysis.

To perform MDS analysis in a biological system, the OPLS-AA (Optimized Potential for Liquid Simulation All-Atom) force field was applied to each atom of the complex [47, 48] and then the complex was solvated in a water-spaced box (TIP3P). Ions were added to neutralize the charges, and the complex was then minimized to remove any steric clashes. The simulation last for 1 nanosecond (ns) under a Boltzmann pressure of 1 atmosphere (atm), a temperature of 310 K (K) and a time step of 2 femtoseconds (fs). The simulation time was considered sufficient to highlight the initial stability trends and possible differences in the interaction networks between the bTLR2 and bTLR1.

The stability of the complex was assessed by evaluating the kinetic and energetic parameters of the complex bTLR2-bTLR1. Kinetic parameters consist in Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Radius of Gyration (RoG) and Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF). Energetic parameters, instead, are potential and kinetic energies, H-bonds number between the bTLR2 and bTLR1, APBS and electrostatic analyses. All these parameters were used for characterizing the structural changes and the compactness of the complex bTLR2-bTLR1 occurring in the reference condition (REF) or in the mutagenized counterparts [45].

Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (APBS) analysis

The APBS analysis was performed to investigate the intermolecular interactions and the solvent effects in reference and mutagenized bTLR2-bTLR1 complex [49, 50]. It was estimated by using APBS Electrostatics plug-in of PyMOL (v.2.5.8) software and according to the following parameters: Amber force field, temperature at 310 K and a solvent dielectric constant of 78.000 ε [51]. The results were calculated between − 5 and + 5 kT/e.

Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) analysis

The Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) analysis was calculated using VMD Measure function (v.1.9.4). The SASA values were analysed to observe trends and trajectory over time. To visualize the solvent-accessible regions of the protein, the molecular surface was rendered in VMD using the “MSMS” plugin. Parameters for surface rendering were adjusted to match the probe radius used for SASA calculation (1.4 Å).

Statistical analysis

Allele frequencies were calculated by simple allele counting [52]. The univariate logistic regression model was performed for estimating the association of bTLR2 polymorphisms with B. abortus infection. Since the expected outcome is binary (i.e. case or control), the Odds Ratio (OR) has been adopted to evaluate a possible association between genotype and disease [53–55]. ORs and 95% confidence intervals were calculated by Fisher’s exact test using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). Values were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05 [56].

Single locus association analysis and genotypic Case/Control association test for exploring inheritance models were performed by PLINK (v. 1.9) (https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/cite.shtml) [57, 58]. The significance of the association and of the inheritance models for each polymorphism were accepted at a threshold of p-value ≤ 0.05, and the ORs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were computed.

Furthermore, to assess the cumulative effect of the risk genotypes on brucellosis susceptibility, a Genetic Risk Score (GRS) was evaluated for each animal by combining the ORs associated to the SNPs of bTLR2 of this study and those of bTLR4 from a previous study [19]. The GRS provides an estimate of the cumulative genetic risk for a specific phenotype. The calculation was carried out by adopting the natural logarithm of the OR for each risk alleles, obtained by PLINK analysis, as a weight and according to previous studies [59–61].

The GRS values were then used for Haplotype Inference Analysis carried out by using Python 3.10. This analysis was performed to verify the cumulative effect of SNPs alleles, located at two different chromosomes. Data were first converted into haplotypes format using a custom Python script that adopts an Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm (Additional file 3). This script enumerates all possible haplotype pairs consistent with the observed genotypes. The haplotype frequencies were estimated over successive iterations of the EM algorithm until convergence (when the change in frequency estimates between successive iterations was below a set up value of 10−5, which represents the predefined tolerance threshold) [62, 63]. Subsequently, individuals were stratified according to their case/control status. For each inferred haplotype, group-specific counts were derived. A chi-square test was then performed to compare the distribution of these weighted counts between cases and controls, with the level of association indicated by the corresponding p-value.

Amino acid conservation profiling and SNPs mapping

The ammino acid conservation analysis among the bTLR2 and other 20 mammalians showed a homology >98% of the TLR2 cytosolic domain, and a homology of 86% of the dimerization domain (Fig. 1 and Additional file 5). This last domain is involved in the heterodimerization process of bTLR2 with bTLR1, which is essential for the activation of the host inflammatory response. The results evidenced that the aa substitutions L5W, M18T and A560T, due to the presence of SNPs at CDS positions 14, 53 and 1678 respectively, were not selected for the consequent genotyping and in silico analyses. Specifically, the first two nsSNPs, belonged to the leader peptide-codifying region and they were not codified in mature protein, while the remaining one was in the intracellular domain (not involved in the pathogen interaction). By this way, these results indicated that only the substitutions A125V, S161T and S345N, induced by the presence of three (c.374C>T, c.482/483GC>CT, c.1034G>A) out six SNPs, were in the dimerization conserved domain which play a significant role for the protein structure and functionality [64].

Prior to genotyping analysis, the three above SNPs were selected for Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) analysis to identify potential haplotype blocks. The analysis identified a first strong haplotype block formed by c.482G>C and c.483C>T (analyzed as two distinct polymorphic loci) with high LD (D’ = 0.95; r² = 0.85); and a second haplotype block composed by c.482G>C and c.1034G>A (D’ = 0.90; r² = 0.80). In contrast, no significant LD was observed between c.374C>T and the other SNPs (D’ ≤ 0.35).

Association studies of SNPs with brucellosis resistance

The genotype distributions of c.374C>T and c.1034G>A was determined in a total of 194 buffaloes and is summarized in Table 4. For the c.374C>T, HWE was confirmed in cases (χ² = 0.80, p-value = 0.36) and controls (χ² = 1.98, p-value = 0.15). Similarly, the c.1034G>A polymorphism showed no significant deviation from HWE expectations in either cases (χ² = 1.540, p-value = 0.21) or controls (χ² = 0.89, p-value = 0.34). Referring to the c.374C>T polymorphism, the T allele was associated with resistance to brucellosis (OR = 0.61; p-value = 0.037) (Table 4). About c.1034G>A polymorphism, the G allele seems to confer protection against B. abortus infection, but it was not statistically associated to the infection (reciprocal OR = 0.74; p-value = 0.154) (Table 4). Additionally, to validate and confirm the specific amplification of DNA and the homozygous conditions for each marker, 10% of the PCR products were Sanger sequenced at CEINGE Institute.

PLINK software have confirmed the significantly statistic association of T allele with brucellosis resistance (OR = 0.61; p-value = 0.037) and the no statistical association between the c.1034G allele and the disease (p-value = 0.154). Furthermore, the genotypic Case/Control association test has evidenced that the T allele, at c.374C>T polymorphism, remained significantly associated with resistance to brucellosis in the allelic model (OR = 0.61;95% CI: 0.38–0.97; p-value = 0.037) and additive model (OR = 0.63; 95% CI 0.40–0.99; p-value = 0.042). However, neither the dominant (T-carrier [CT + TT] vs. CC: OR = 0.69; p-value = 0.14) nor recessive (TT vs. CT + CC: OR = 0.42; p-value = 0.10) models reached statistical significance, likely due to the low number of the homozygous TT genotype in cases (3/98) compared to controls (12/96). Referring to the G allele, at c.1034G>A polymorphism, the test has evidenced a non-significant protective effect in the allelic model (OR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.50–1.11; p-value = 0.154). However, analysis under a dominant model for the A allele (AG + AA vs. GG) revealed a significant association with increased risk (OR = 1.95; 95% CI: 1.03–3.70; p-value = 0.040), while the recessive model (AA vs. AG + GG) showed no effect (p-value = 0.80).

By combining the Odds Ratios (ORs) associated with the risk alleles provided by PLINK software for the above TLR2 polymorphisms, as well as those previously published for TLR4 and associated to brucellosis (i.e. c.672A>C and c.902C>G) [19], we evaluated the Genetic Risk Score (GRS) to assess the cumulative genetic risk for buffalo brucellosis across multiple loci. Data revealed that the genotypes homozygous CC-AA-CC-CC - with the first two genotypes at the bTLR2 locus (c.374C and c.1034A) and the last two at the bTLR4 locus (c.672C and c.902C) - represented the highest GRS (34.3), thus indicating a major susceptibility to brucellosis and suggesting a potential role of the haplotype CACC in the observed disease predisposition. Instead, the homozygous TT-AA-AA-GG exhibited the lowest GRS (i.e. 1.79), thus indicating a less susceptibility to disease and the potential association of the TAAG haplotype with reduced disease susceptibility. A similar condition has been also observed in the case of the CC-GG-AA-GG homozygous, which gives a GRS value of 2.65 and indicates the probable role of CGAG haplotype.

The haplotype inference analysis, carried out by a custom Python script, has confirmed the above hypotheses about the haplotype effects considering their frequencies in cases and controls group. Specifically, the analysis revealed that the CACC haplotype was more frequent in cases than controls (i.e. 31 cases vs. 15 controls) with a p-value of 0.031; the haplotype CGAG was more present in controls (15 cases vs. 30 controls) with a p-value of 0.026. Whereas the haplotype TAAG was more frequent in controls than cases (9 cases vs. 15 controls), the p-value was not statistically significant (i.e. 0.36) (Additional file 6).

The in silico analysis was carried out for predicting the possible biological effects due to the presence of the three aa substitutions (i.e. A125V, S161T and S345N) in the dimerization conserved domain. Firstly, since the PDB database does not include the crystallographic structure of river buffalo TLR2 3D protein structure, it was developed through homology modelling with human TLR2, which displayed a 61.1% similarity with the bTLR2 [40] (Fig. 2A). Clashes and outliers were removed by Chiron and YASARA web servers. The robustness of the developed protein model was also confirmed by the following parameters: Ramachandran plot, Normalized QMEAN Score and QMEAND global score (Figs. 2B and C).

After the modelling and validation of the bTLR2 protein, the heterodimerization process was investigated. Briefly, the molecular docking analysis was performed to predict the bTLR2 - bTLR1 interaction thus obtaining the dimerized complex. This analysis was conducted using LZerD software under two conditions: with constraints or without constraints (giving or not information about the amino acid interactions to the software). Among the numerous models generated, we selected the unconstrained one characterized by the lowest Ranksum Score (value of 1023). Ranksum is a ranking-based score that combines the rankings from three statistical potentials (GOAP, DFIRE, and ITScore) which assess the protein structure quality by estimating how the developed model is stable and physically realistic. This model was optimised using YASARA and selected as reference complex for the following in silico predictions [41, 42]. Later, the MDS analysis was carried out for evaluating the effects of the A125V, S161T and S345N substitutions, in the physiological conditions of a biological system like mammalian cells, during the heterodimerization process of bTLR2 with bTLR1; thus, providing information about the structural and energetic differences between the reference complex and the mutagenized counterparts. The selected simulation duration of 1 ns was found to be adequate, as the kinetic and energetic parameters analysis demonstrated that the reference complex had already reached a stable equilibrium by 510 ps (Additional file 7).

The structural differences were assessed by the following parameters: RMSD, RoG and RMSF. RMSD results evidenced a significant divergence from the REF (>90%) when all mutations (replaced ASS is replaced by VTN) were presents (Fig. 3A). It is also important to highlight that the only single aa substitution S345N was able to determine a significant divergence from the REF (>78%), thus suggesting that it could compromise the stability of the bTLR2 protein. These results were also confirmed by measuring the Radius of Gyration (RoG) which evidenced the higher value of the complexes containing all the three aa substitutions or only the S345N substitution compared to the REF (Fig. 3B). On the contrary, the A125V or S161T substitutions were found to induce the lowest divergence from the REF complex as indicated by RMSD, RoG and RMSF analyses (Figs. 3A and C). Intermediate values and similar trend were detected in the case of the remaining mutations (Figs. 3A-C).

To deeply investigate the difference between the number of H-bonds in the case of A125V or S345N substitutions, the APBS and SASA analyses were carried out at 510 picoseconds (ps), time at which the highest difference between the above two complexes has been revealed. The Figs. 5A and B showed - from the top point of view -, that there was no electrostatic potential difference at the binding pocket detected by APBS analysis; however, the total SASA was lower in the case of A125V substitution (18346Å2 respect to 19219Å2 of the S345N substitution). From a frontal perspective, Figs. 5C and D show a larger positively charged area for S345N compared to A125V. Since the bTLR1 is partially positive charged, the presence of the substitution S345N in bTLR2 caused an altered positioning which, compared to the A125V, is rotate of 19° (Fig. 5D). Probably, the reduced number of hydrogen bonds observed in the case of S345N substitution may be due to the repulsive forces between bTLR2 and bTLR1. Additionally, the S345N variant resulted in an increased average distance between bTLR2 and bTLR1 (Fig. 5E).

In summary, these simulations provide a preliminary and plausible structural prediction for the genetic associations identified in the case-control study. The A125V variant, derived from c.374T allele and probably associated with protection, appears to promote the formation of a stable heterodimer, whereas the S345N variant, associated with increased risk, disrupts the interface and compromises functionality (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Disease resistance, together with animal resilience, has recently become a key target in breeding programs across all livestock species, in response to the growing need to enhance animal welfare, reduce antibiotic use, and cope with climate change challenges. Different studies have shown that SNPs in buffalo TLRs are linked to alterations in the inflammatory response, potentially affecting resistance or susceptibility to bacterial infections and inflammatory diseases such as tuberculosis, mastitis, and endometritis, although these associations do not imply causation [18, 19, 29, 65, 66]. Among the different TLRs, TLR2 plays a key role in the recognition of Brucella abortus because it interacts with several bacterial ligands, including lipoteichoic acid, peptidoglycan, specific types of LPS, and brucella outer membrane lipoproteins (Omp10, Omp16, and Omp19), thus triggering the release of pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators and contributing to the host immune response [21–27].

The TLR2 sequencing analysis allowed us to identify 19 SNPs, already reported in previous studies [18, 29, 30]; but only three polymorphisms c.374C>T, c.482/483GC>CT, and c.1034G>A were selected for further investigation, as they lead to the amino acid substitutions A125V, S161T, and S345N within the TLR2 conserved dimerization domain. The Linkage Disequilibrium analysis evidenced a complete linkage only between c.482G>C and c.483C>T or c.1034G>A, indicating the co-inheritance as part of the same haplotype block. By this way, the c.374C>T and c.1034G>A were selected for the association study which identify a significant association between the TLR2 c.374C>T variant and reduced risk of brucellosis in Italian Mediterranean buffalo (OR = 0.61; p-value = 0.037). It is important to note that the presence of the T allele does not confer absolute resistance, but rather a statistically lower risk of infection compared to carriers of the C allele. Furthermore, the c.374C>T variant should not be considered causal, as the observed genetic association may result from a linked causal polymorphism, either within or outside the TLR2 gene, in linkage disequilibrium with the c.374C>T polymorphism.

The same c.374T allele has also been associated with the endometritis resistance in river buffalo [29], thus supporting the correlation - already investigated - between brucellosis and endometritis [67, 68]. Notably, a recent study has demonstrated that B. abortus can directly colonize the uterus and induce acute endometritis, even in non-pregnant hosts [69]. Moreover, epidemiological data reinforce the above association, demonstrating that animals with endometritis are significantly more susceptible to brucellosis, with an odds ratio (OR) of 5.5. Therefore, the SNP c.374C>T could be considered as one of the valuable genetic markers for selective breeding programs aimed at improving endometritis resistance and potentially reducing the incidence of brucellosis.

Instead, the c.1034G allele was protective against B. abortus infection, but it was not statistically associated to the infection (OR = 0.74; p-value = 0.154) (Table 4). Furthermore, it is important to note that validation in larger cohorts is necessary for both variants in order to strengthen the statistical power and robustness of the observed association for the c.374T allele, and to better clarify the potential role of the c.1034G allele.

To address potential limitations in interpreting genetic effects, we further analyzed the c.374C>T and c.1034G>A polymorphisms under multiple inheritance models (allelic, additive, dominant, and recessive). The results suggest that the protective effect of c.374T is best explained by an additive model rather than a dominant or recessive mechanism, although the small number of TT homozygotes limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding potential recessive effects.

Referring to the c.1034G>A polymorphism, the results indicated that the A allele could confer susceptibility in a dominant manner, whereas the putative protective effect of the G allele lacks statistical support in this cohort.

A previous study using the same buffalo samples identified an association between two TLR4 SNPs and brucellosis [19]; therefore, we combined those results with the findings of this study to assess the Genetic Risk Score (GRS), which estimates an individual’s overall genetic susceptibility to a given phenotype by aggregating the effects of risk-associated variants identified through association studies [59, 61]. The GRS has already emerged as a promising approach for integrating multiple genetic variants into a single predictive score, representing a valuable tool for improving risk stratification in clinical trials such as cardiovascular disease and the onset of Alzheimer’s disease [70, 71].

In this study, the GRS analysis evidenced that the animals carrying the genotypes - CC374-AA1034-CC672-CC902 – the first two genotypes at the bTLR2 locus, and the last two at the bTLR4 locus (OR = 1.621; p-value = 0.033, and OR = 1.66; p-value = 0.020, respectively) [19], were more frequently positive to brucellosis. Conversely, the buffaloes having the genotype TT374-AA1034-AA672-GG902 were less frequently positive to the disease. These findings highlight the potential of GRS-based approach to effectively capture the cumulative effect of multiple genetic variants, offering a more comprehensive assessment of individual disease risk compared to single-marker analyses. These insights pave the way for implementing marker-assisted selection (MAS) strategies aimed at improving disease resilience in buffalo breeding programs.

The GRS values were used to perform a haplotype inference analysis to identify the possible alleles combinations statistically associated with disease by using the EM algorithm, which identify all possible haplotype pairs consistent with the observed genotypes. The results demonstrated the statistically significant association of the CACC haplotype (at the homozygous state) with susceptibility to disease. Instead, the haplotype TAAG (at the homozygous state) gave a p-value not statistically significant; probably these results could be influenced by the low frequency of T allele among the samples. The above results suggest that specific haplotype block, particularly CACC in the homozygous state, may act as genetic risk markers for brucellosis. The lack of significance for the TAAG haplotype highlights, instead, the limitations of statistical power in cases of low-frequency alleles and underscores the importance of adequate sample sizes in genetic association studies.

Finally, it is important to point out that these findings should be interpreted with caution and represent a preliminary basis for further investigating the possible link between TLR2 and buffalo brucellosis. To confirm the functional relevance of the identified TLR2 SNP, additional studies are required, including analyses in larger sample cohorts and the quantification of TLR2 protein expression in both brucellosis-positive and -negative animals.

In recent years, MDS analysis has become increasingly prominent and widely adopted, especially for experimental molecular biologists. This technique could be considered an essential computational tool for investigating atomic-level interactions within protein–protein and protein–ligand systems. When integrated with experimental approaches, MDS can play a role in guiding experimental design and hypothesis generation and also for enhancing the interpretation of results. This synergistic strategy is particularly valuable in elucidating disease mechanisms, assessing the structural impact of mutations, and supporting rational drug design [72–74]. For instance, in human, the MDS analysis has been used for evaluating the interaction between cyclooxygenase-2 and four aromatic butenolides, identifying a possible drug candidate [75]. MDS has also been applied to investigate the molecular binding mechanisms between the cationic triarylmethane dye Malachite Green and human serum albumin (HSA), offering valuable insights into the interaction [76]. Further studies have used the MDS to explore the impact of aa substitutions on protein structure and function such as the S20G mutation in human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP) which is probably associated with the early onset of type II diabetes [77], as well as the T193I and R148H mutations in human prion protein associated to neurodegenerative diseases [78].

In buffalo, MDS analysis has been already adopted to investigate the structural impact of amino acid substitutions due to nonsynonymous SNPs in genes such as TRHDE or TLR4, as well as to explore the molecular interactions between peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRPs) - key innate immune pattern recognition receptors - and bacterial peptidoglycans, thereby providing deeper insights into host-pathogen interactions [19, 79, 80].

In this study, the MDS analysis was carried out for providing a possible biological plausibility associated with the presence of aa substitution in TLR2 dimerization conserved domain. Our results showed that the presence of all aa substitutions or of the substitution S345N or A125V in bTLR2, could influence both structural and energetic parameters of the bTLR2. In particular, data evidenced a different H-bonds number in the complex bTLR2-bTLR1 due to the S345N or A125V substitutions. The presence of the S345N substitution reduced the H-bonds number, while the A125V increased the number. A possible explanation could be supported by the following speculation: 1) Serine at position 345 is a polar amino acid containing a hydroxyl group in its side chain, whereas asparagine is a polar amino acid with an amide group in its side chain. This structural difference could influence the inter-protein hydrogen bonds, thus inducing local conformation changes; 2) Alanine and Valine at position 125, are both hydrophobic and aliphatic amino acids, but Valine has two additional methyl groups which probably could increase the protein folding, stability, and intra-chain H-bonds network. Additionally, the amino acid position 125 is a functional site under positive selection, suggesting its potential adaptive and functional significance [81].

The in silico results suggest that both the above substitutions may affect the stability of the bTLR2–bTLR1 heterodimer, providing additional support for a potential association between these polymorphisms and susceptibility to brucellosis in buffalo. These finding allow to hypothesize that TLR2 SNPs influence brucellosis susceptibility by altering heterodimerization dynamics, potentially modulating the host’s innate immune response to B. abortus infection.

Instead, about the substitution S161T, due to the c.482/483GC>CT polymorphism, the MDS results suggested the absence of significant effects during the heterodimerization process of TLR2 with TLR1.

It is important to highlight that in silico analyses cannot fully capture the complexity of the biological systems, and the reliability of their predictions depends on the quality of the structural data and computational models employed. Therefore, the functional implications of the identified SNPs in relation to disease susceptibility remain to be further validated through additional in vitro and in vivo studies.

Conclusions

This multidisciplinary study underscores the role of genetic variation in Toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and their potential impact on protein function and host immune response. The integration of association studies with in silico analyses, including Molecular Dynamics Simulations, provided valuable insights into the structural and functional consequences of TLR2 polymorphisms in Italian Mediterranean buffalo.

Our findings suggest that the c.374T allele, resulting in the A125V substitution, may be associated with reduced susceptibility to Brucella abortus infection, supporting its potential role as a candidate marker for disease resistance. Although these results require further validation in larger populations, they represent an advancement in the knowledge for the development of a more complete strategy for brucellosis control. The integration of genotyping data with computational modelling adds support to the observed associations and provides a framework for exploring potential functional variants.

Importantly, this approach contributes to a better understanding of host-pathogen interactions and provides a foundation for selective breeding aimed at improving disease resistance, reducing economic losses, and enhancing animal welfare.

Future studies will investigate the additional genes involved in the inflammatory pathway following pathogen recognition, will also enrol a larger number of samples to provide greater robustness to the power of association detection, and will explore the role of the c.374C>T polymorphism in TLR2 signalling and Brucella spp. intracellular persistence to provide deeper mechanistic insights.

It is also important to consider that resistance to infectious diseases such as brucellosis is a complex quantitative trait influenced by genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors. Therefore, while the identified SNPs may account for part of the phenotypic variability, they represent a step forward in deciphering the genetic architecture of disease resistance in buffalo. Finally, this study highlights the broader importance of integrating genetic, bioinformatic, and functional approaches to identify reliable markers of disease resistance. Such integrated strategies may significantly contribute to more sustainable, resilient, and welfare-oriented livestock production systems.
