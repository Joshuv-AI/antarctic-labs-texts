Background

Health professions education systems face increasing pressure to prepare a workforce capable of responding to population health needs, workforce shortages, and system disruption. [1–2] These challenges are exacerbated in active conflict zones, where educational continuity faces severe restrictions. Parallel research from other crisis-affected regions, such as Palestine, documents the profound impact of political violence on student resilience, clinical training quality, and faculty capacity, factors that must be addressed alongside curricular reform [3].

Strengthening rehabilitation and physical therapy education has become a priority in Ukraine due to rising disability prevalence, war-related trauma, chronic disease burden, and national health system transformation [4]. Evidence from other conflict-affected and low-resource settings demonstrates the importance of flexible and innovative education models to sustain health workforce training. Virtual, competency-based, and technology-enabled approaches have shown promise in maintaining educational continuity and clinical competence in fragile settings [5].

The Ukraine Ministry of Health has identified rehabilitation as a core pillar of health system recovery through the National Disability, Health, and Rehabilitation Plan, which calls for expansion of rehabilitation services and modernization of education for rehabilitation professionals [6]. In 2020, national legislation formally recognized physical therapists as independent health professionals, defined their scope of practice, and mandated alignment between educational programs and workforce needs [7]. This legal recognition was followed by expansion of the Programme of Medical Guarantees, which broadened publicly funded rehabilitation services across all levels of care and reinforced demand for a competent rehabilitation workforce [8–10]. Building on these reforms, in 2024 Ukraine introduced separate educational pathways for physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech and language therapy, replacing the previous combined “therapy and rehabilitation” model. [11–12] This pathway separation required universities to redesign curricula, clarify professional identity, and transition toward competency-based education models aligned with national standards and international frameworks such as the World Physiotherapy Education Framework [13]. Globally, countries strengthening rehabilitation and allied health education have implemented structured curriculum reform and program evaluation strategies to improve competency alignment, graduate readiness, and workforce capacity [14–17]. These initiatives highlight the importance of structured, context-sensitive tools that enable institutions to operationalize competency-based education while remaining responsive to local health system demands.

Recent assessments of Ukraine’s wartime health and rehabilitation systems highlight critical shortages in trained rehabilitation personnel, fragmented service delivery, and the absence of a coordinated national rehabilitation strategy [18]. To support Ukrainian universities in navigating this complex reform environment during wartime, four institutions implemented the Academic Program Reflection Tool (APRT), a theory-informed framework designed to guide structured program review and educational quality improvement. The APRT integrates systems theory, needs assessment methodology, stakeholder engagement principles, and the Plan-Do-Assess-Adjust improvement cycle to support institutional reflection across seven program domains [19–22]. Unlike traditional accreditation or compliance-based curriculum review processes, which primarily evaluate programs against externally defined standards, the APRT is designed as a developmental, formative tool that guides structured, domain-based reflection and supports locally driven program improvement aligned with institutional context and readiness. Therefore, this multi-institutional case study aims to describe and analyze the implementation of the APRT across four Ukrainian universities, examining the process, perceived outcomes, and transferable lessons for health professions education in crisis-affected contexts.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a multiple-case design with cross-case synthesis to examine the implementation of a structured academic program review process across four universities offering entry-level physical therapy education in Ukraine. A case study approach was selected to enable in-depth exploration of educational reform within a complex, real-world context shaped by national health system transformation and wartime disruption. Cross-case synthesis was used to identify shared themes and institution-specific differences. The study emphasizes process, institutional learning, and program development rather than measurement of intervention efficacy.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed by an Institutional Review Board and determined to be exempt from full ethical review. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent. Data were anonymized at the institutional level to protect confidentiality. The study adhered to ethical standards for educational research and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Institutional context and sample

A purposive sampling approach was used to select institutions capable of sustained engagement during wartime. This approach prioritized depth of engagement and feasibility, allowing detailed examination of institutional reform processes while acknowledging limitations in generalizability. Four Ukrainian higher education institutions offering physical therapy programs participated in this collective case: the National University of Ukraine on Physical Education and Sport (Kyiv), the Ukrainian Catholic University (Lviv), the Lviv State University of Physical Culture (Lviv), and the Khortytsia National Educational and Rehabilitation Academy (Zaporizhzhia). These institutions were selected because they were actively engaged in national efforts to revise rehabilitation education standards and represented diversity in geographic location, institutional size, resource availability, and academic structure. Although additional Ukrainian universities offer physical therapy education, participation was limited to institutions that were able to commit to a two-year reflective review process during wartime conditions. This sampling strategy prioritized depth of engagement and feasibility over national representativeness.

Participants and stakeholder involvement

Each institution identified a program leadership team responsible for participating in the APRT process. Teams typically included program directors or guarantors, clinical education directors, admissions coordinators, curriculum leads, and faculty members involved in teaching and assessment. Each team consisted of four to seven members. Self-assessment responses represented institutional consensus, developed through internal discussion rather than individual faculty scoring. Additional stakeholders contributed at key stages, including student representatives, national professional associations (e.g., Ukrainian Association of Physical Therapy), and representatives from the Ministries of Health and Education. Their involvement supported alignment with regulatory expectations, workforce priorities, and emerging professional standards.

The Academic Program Reflection Tool (APRT)

The APRT is a theory-informed academic program review and quality improvement tool developed to support reflective curriculum evaluation in rehabilitation education. The tool integrates systems theory, needs assessment methodology, stakeholder engagement principles, and the Plan-Do-Assess-Adjust quality improvement cycle. It draws on established educational evaluation frameworks and structures institutional reflection across seven domains: program philosophy, learning outcomes, curriculum, student admission and progression, faculty, resources, and program integrity (Table 1) [19–22]. The APRT was designed as a developmental and formative tool, rather than a psychometrically validated assessment instrument. It supports structured institutional reflection by prompting programs to examine curriculum alignment, teaching strategies, assessment practices, governance, and sustainability planning. At the time of this study, the APRT had not undergone formal validation and should be interpreted as a framework for guided reflection rather than as an objective performance metric.

Mentorship and facilitation

Institutions were supported throughout the process by two mentors. The local mentor had over ten years of experience in Ukrainian higher education regulation, accreditation processes, and rehabilitation curriculum development. The international mentor was a senior academic in rehabilitation and health professions education with more than twenty years of experience in curriculum reform, accreditation, and faculty development across multiple countries. Mentorship aimed to support structured reflection, contextualize international standards within Ukrainian policy frameworks, and assist institutions in translating APRT findings into actionable program improvements. Mentors facilitated workshops, guided monthly reflection sessions, and provided feedback on program documents.

Implementation process

The APRT was implemented over a two-year period using a phased and iterative approach. Initial semi-structured interviews were conducted with program leadership teams at each institution to explore perceived program strengths, challenges, and priorities. Interview topics included differentiation of physical therapy from occupational therapy curricula, alignment with international competency frameworks, preparation for national licensure examinations, clinical education quality, and faculty capacity.

Following interviews, institutions completed a baseline APRT self-assessment, which informed the agenda of an initial in-person workshop. During the first workshop, participants reviewed and refined program philosophies, examined learning outcomes, mapped curricula to national and international standards, and evaluated alignment between course objectives, teaching strategies, and assessment methods. Institutions subsequently engaged in monthly online mentoring sessions, each focused on one APRT domain. Sessions combined brief instructional content with guided institutional reflection and documentation. The time required to complete each domain varied depending on complexity; for example, program philosophy development required approximately two months, whereas curriculum mapping required approximately six months. A mid-cycle workshop in Warsaw, Poland, provided opportunities for cross-institutional learning, peer feedback, and refinement of draft program materials. A final in-person workshop in Krakow, Poland, enabled institutions to present completed APRT findings, finalize quality improvement plans, and engage with national professional and regulatory stakeholders.

Data sources

Multiple data sources were used to document institutional learning and program development, including: (1) baseline and post-implementation APRT self-assessments, (2) completed APRT worksheets across all seven domains, (3) revised program philosophies, outcome statements, curriculum maps, and assessment matrices, (4) Institutional quality improvement plans, and (5) workshop presentations, field notes, and discussion transcripts. These sources provided complementary quantitative and qualitative insights into institutional perceptions, decision-making processes, and programmatic changes.

Data analysis

Quantitative self-assessment scores were summarized descriptively to illustrate changes in institutional perceptions across APRT domains over time. Given the small number of participating institutions, statistical inference was not emphasized. To enhance trustworthiness, multiple strategies were employed. Institutional self-assessment scores were generated through consensus discussion among leadership teams, reducing individual response bias. Data triangulation was achieved by integrating multiple sources, including APRT worksheets, revised program documents, workshop transcripts, and field notes. Qualitative data were analyzed independently by two researchers using inductive thematic analysis. Initial codes were generated inductively from the data and organized into themes through iterative review. Differences in coding and interpretation were resolved through discussion and consensus to ensure consistency and reduce individual bias. Exemplar quotes from participants were used to illustrate key themes and provide transparency in interpretation. This analytic approach strengthened credibility, confirmability, and consistency of findings.

Results

Results are presented descriptively to illustrate how participating institutions interpreted their educational capacity, identified gaps, and planned program improvements during a period of health system reform and war. Outcomes should be understood as evidence of institutional learning and development rather than objective measures of educational effectiveness. Furthermore, p-values for self-assessment results are provided for completeness but should be interpreted with extreme caution due to the sample size.

Institutional self-assessment across APRT domains

Each participating institution completed baseline and post-implementation APRT self-assessments through a consensus-based process involving program leadership teams. Self-assessment items within each APRT domain were rated on an ordinal scale and domain-level composite scores were generated by summing item responses. Across institutions, self-assessment results suggested increased clarity in program philosophy, learning outcomes, and student-related policies following the APRT process. Modest gains were also reported in faculty-related domains, reflecting efforts to strengthen teaching capacity and professional development.

In contrast, ratings related to curriculum and resources remained stable or declined slightly over time. Rather than indicating reduced program quality, participants reported that structured curriculum mapping and guided reflection revealed previously unrecognized gaps in competency coverage, assessment alignment, and clinical integration. As noted by one participant,

We are very grateful for such a long support of our programs, for giving us the opportunity to reflect on our educational programs, to see how our goals and competences of graduates and program learning outcomes are achieved, how to implement such competences that are prescribed in the documents, how they are implemented and how to ensure that the graduate really has these competences and has formed clinical skills.

Similarly, the APRT process heightened awareness of constraints related to physical space, equipment availability, funding, and clinical placement capacity, which were largely beyond the direct control of program leaders. Program philosophy and professional identity

All four institutions developed revised program philosophies that articulated educational values, graduate expectations, and alignment with institutional missions, national health priorities, and international competency frameworks. Program philosophies emphasized professional identity formation, ethical practice, evidence-informed decision-making, interprofessional collaboration, and responsiveness to community health needs. The philosophy development process enabled faculty to clarify how curricular structure, teaching strategies, and assessment practices reflected shared educational priorities. Learning outcomes and assessment alignment

Participating institutions revised program-level learning outcomes to improve alignment with national higher education standards and the World Physiotherapy Education Framework. Programs developed outcome-to-assessment matrices linking graduate competencies to course-level assessments, clinical evaluation tools, and national licensure examinations. An example of revised program outcomes and associated assessment strategies is presented in Table 4. As part of this process, faculty reported decreased confidence in the validity of existing assessment methods, particularly regarding clinical performance evaluation and written examinations. This shift reflected increased critical appraisal rather than deterioration in assessment quality. Programs identified specific priorities for improvement, including development of clearer grading rubrics, standardization of clinical assessment tools, enhanced training for clinical supervisors, and expansion of performance-based assessment methods to better capture applied competence. Curriculum review and teaching approaches

Curriculum mapping to national and international competency frameworks revealed both strengths and gaps across participating programs. Universally, institutions identified areas requiring further development, including integumentary management relevant to war-related injuries, wound care, global health perspectives, and preparation for lifelong learning and continuing professional development. Faculty reviewed course syllabi and teaching approaches and noted a continued reliance on lecture-based instruction and written examinations. While practical assessments and simulated learning activities were present in most programs, structured evaluation criteria and formal grading rubrics were often limited. In response, all institutions proposed increasing the use of learner-centered pedagogies, including case-based learning, reflective practice assignments, simulation, and structured clinical reasoning exercises. Several programs also identified opportunities to integrate ethical decision-making and psychosocial considerations into clinical teaching through complex patient scenarios.

Clinical education and practice-based learning

All the programs utilized a combination of integrated and terminal clinical placements delivered through partnerships with hospitals, rehabilitation centers, and outpatient clinics. Clinical supervision models varied, with most placements involving small groups of students overseen by licensed physical therapists. Institutions reported variability in how student performance was assessed across clinical sites, including inconsistent use of evaluation forms and delays in supervisor feedback. Participants identified the need to strengthen clinical educator training, improve communication with clinical partners, and standardize evaluation criteria to enhance fairness and reliability in student assessment. Appendix C includes the notes taken during a workshop regarding these challenges across programs.

Faculty capacity and professional development

Faculty capacity remained a shared challenge across all institutions, particularly in specialized practice areas such as pediatrics, acute care, and neurological rehabilitation. Programs reported that approximately 70% to 80% of faculty delivered content aligned with current best evidence; however, participants noted challenges in supporting faculty advancement through additional clinical specialization or academic qualifications. The World Physiotherapy Program Requirements for Accreditation broadly suggests faculty have appropriate qualifications and experience in physiotherapy practice [23]. Potential solutions discussed included the use of online educational resources to support faculty in maintaining current knowledge of evidence-based practice, advancing clinical competencies, and aligning teaching with contemporary physical therapy standards and evolving professional expectations. Student-to-faculty ratios varied across institutions, and national benchmarks for staffing levels were not available. Instructional delivery was further affected by wartime disruptions, which required programs to transition from primarily in-person instruction to blended formats incorporating in-person, online, and asynchronous learning modalities.

Participants also identified financial constraints as a significant barrier to faculty recruitment and retention. Reductions in education funding and national policies requiring universities to return surplus revenue to the state under martial law limited institutional flexibility to offer merit-based salary increases or expand faculty positions. In some cases, institutions reported hiring freezes that further constrained faculty capacity. To mitigate these challenges, institutions described utilizing open-access educational resources, pursuing faculty development opportunities, and exploring strategies to recruit clinicians with advanced training to support teaching and clinical supervision.

Student admission, progression, and academic integrity

Admission requirements across programs typically included completion of a relevant undergraduate degree, academic transcripts, motivation letters, language proficiency assessment, and discipline-specific entrance examinations. Institutions noted variability in student preparedness due to differences in undergraduate training pathways, which created challenges in curriculum sequencing and competency progression. Programs reviewed student progression policies and identified concerns related to academic integrity. Programs reported ongoing efforts to strengthen policies governing student progression and academic integrity. Institutions reviewed progression criteria, including minimum passing thresholds for examinations and structured remediation processes. For example, students were required to achieve a minimum score of 61% to pass written examinations. Students scoring between 55% and 60% were permitted to retake the examination, while those who did not achieve a passing score after remediation were evaluated by an academic review commission to determine eligibility for continuation in the program. In clinical and practical examinations, safety criteria were explicitly incorporated into Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) checklists, and failure to demonstrate safe practice resulted in examination failure regardless of technical performance.

Program resources and infrastructure

Participants consistently reported resource constraints related to physical space, equipment, instructional materials, funding, and clinical placement availability. Many resource-related decisions were governed at institutional or governmental levels, limiting the authority of program leaders to implement immediate changes. Locally controlled resources included classroom-level instructional tools such as educational technologies, learning management systems, and access to online educational materials, including open-access professional and clinical resources. Faculty were generally able to modify instructional approaches and incorporate digital resources to support student learning within existing institutional platforms.

In contrast, physical infrastructure and financial resources were largely controlled at the institutional or governmental level. Departments typically did not have independent authority over budget allocation, limiting their ability to acquire new equipment, expand laboratory space, or increase faculty staffing. Participants noted that capital investments in clinical training equipment, simulation resources, and facility expansion required approval through centralized university or ministry processes. Despite these constraints, institutions identified opportunities to improve coordination of clinical agreements, strengthen long-term infrastructure planning, and advocate for increased institutional investment in rehabilitation education.

Quality improvement planning and sustainability

As a culminating outcome of the APRT process, each institution developed a formal quality improvement plan outlining prioritized recommendations, implementation timelines, responsible personnel, and indicators of progress. Plans emphasized strengthening assessment validity, diversifying teaching strategies, expanding faculty development, improving clinical education oversight, and establishing ongoing cycles of program review. Institutions reported that the APRT process fostered increased local ownership of educational reform and strengthened internal capacity for sustained quality improvement.

Discussion

This collective case study illustrates how the Academic Program Reflection Tool (APRT) supported structured institutional reflection during a period of rapid national reform and ongoing conflict. Participating programs reported increased clarity in program philosophy, learning outcomes, and assessment alignment, while structured curriculum mapping and guided review were perceived to help institutions identify previously unrecognized gaps in competency coverage, clinical education, and resource capacity. In several domains, institutional confidence decreased over time, reflecting increased critical awareness rather than regression in program quality. This pattern aligns with broader quality improvement experiences, where systematic program evaluation is reported to support institutional insight and help identify priorities for curriculum enhancement, assessment reform, and faculty development. Similar structured curriculum reform and program evaluation initiatives in Malawi, Vietnam, Ethiopia, and Uganda have demonstrated the value of context-responsive approaches to strengthening competency alignment and graduate readiness in rehabilitation and allied health education [14–17]. In the context of war-affected health systems facing workforce shortages and evolving rehabilitation demands, strengthening education programs represents a critical component of workforce and health system recovery [18]. In addition to structural alignment, competency-based reform must foster higher-order cognitive and affective capacities essential for complex clinical decision-making. Evidence from nursing education demonstrates a strong association between emotional intelligence and critical thinking disposition among students, suggesting that emotional regulation and reflective capacity enhance analytical reasoning under stress [24].

A key insight from this case was the role of structured reflection in deepening institutional awareness. In some APRT domains, institutional confidence decreased over time, particularly regarding curriculum coherence, assessment validity, and resource adequacy. Rather than indicating program regression, this shift reflects a common phenomenon in quality improvement initiatives whereby systematic review reveals previously unrecognized gaps. Institutions reported that this heightened awareness helped them articulate more precise priorities for curriculum enhancement, assessment reform, and faculty development.

In war-affected educational settings such as Ukraine, where students and educators operate under sustained psychological pressure, embedding reflective practice, ethical reasoning, and resilience-building strategies within curricula may support development of these advanced competencies. These findings align with studies from conflict-affected settings highlighting the importance of strengthening student support systems to foster resilience and coping during clinical training under conditions of instability and stress [3, 25]. Similarly, challenges related to faculty capacity extended beyond content expertise to include the ability to mentor students effectively while working under sustained stress. Research from high-acuity and conflict settings demonstrates that educator capacity is closely linked to work environment, professional quality of life, and institutional support, factors that are critical for sustaining educational quality in war-affected universities [26].

The variability observed in clinical supervision models and assessment practices across training sites mirrors findings from other conflict-affected regions. A recent mixed-methods study examining clinical learning environments among nursing students in West Bank universities reported significant disparities in pedagogical atmosphere, supervisory relationships, and clinical experiences, with variability across hospital types and clinical placements [27]. These parallels suggest that institutions in fragile or resource-constrained settings commonly report challenges related to consistency in mentorship, structured feedback, and standardized assessment tools. Together, these findings support institutional efforts to formalize clinical educator training, create consistency in evaluation criteria, and strengthen institutional oversight to improve perceived fairness and reliability across training sites.

Another notable outcome was the development of local leadership and mentorship capacity. Faculty and administrators reported assuming mentorship roles supporting occupational therapy programs within their institutions, suggesting potential for cross-disciplinary capacity building and reduced reliance on external technical assistance. Building internal expertise is particularly important in war-affected settings, where international partnerships may be unstable and institutional continuity is essential [28].

Despite these contributions, findings should be interpreted within the study’s methodological limitations. The APRT is a developmental, formative tool and its psychometric properties (e.g., validity, reliability) have not been formally established. Outcomes were based primarily on institutional self-assessment and qualitative reflection rather than objective measures of student competence, graduate performance, or patient outcomes. The small number of participating institutions limits generalizability, and ongoing national reforms and wartime disruptions likely influenced both implementation and participant perceptions. This case provides insight into the potential value of structured program reflection tools in supporting competency-based education reform in resource-constrained and rapidly evolving contexts. Future efforts should focus on: (1) longitudinal tracking of graduate outcomes post-reform; (2) continued evaluation of graduate competence, teaching practices, assessment quality and work force integration, and (3) exploration of the role of peer networks to sustain cross-institutional learning amidst ongoing instability.

Conclusion

This multi-institutional case study describes how four Ukrainian universities used structured program review to guide physical therapy education reform during war and ongoing national health system reform. The APRT served as a practical, theory-informed framework that facilitated institutional reflection, curriculum review, and quality improvement planning in a low-resource and crisis-affected context. Through engagement in the APRT process, participating institutions reported clarifying program philosophies, reviewing competency-based learning outcomes, identifying gaps in curriculum and assessment practices, and developing plans to guide ongoing educational improvement. Importantly, participants reported increased local engagement in reform processes, with faculty and program leaders assuming greater responsibility for interpreting findings and prioritizing changes. Emerging mentorship roles among participating faculty suggest potential for cross-disciplinary capacity building and reduced reliance on external partners.

Although this study does not establish the effectiveness of the APRT in improving learner performance or workforce outcomes, it suggests the feasibility and perceived educational value of structured program review as a mechanism for supporting health professions education in low- and middle-income countries and war-affected settings. The lessons derived from this case may inform other institutions seeking to strengthen education systems amid workforce expansion, regulatory change, or system disruption. This case provides practical insights into context-responsive academic program development and supports ongoing global efforts to strengthen health professions education in challenging and rapidly evolving environments.
