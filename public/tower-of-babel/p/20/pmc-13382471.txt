Seminal vesicle mixed epithelial-stromal tumor delayed local recurrence treated by salvage robotic Re-excision in a previously operated pelvis: A case report
Authors: Nhu Quang Tran, Whi-An Kwon, Jae Young Joung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeminal vesicle mixed epithelial-stromal tumor (MEST) is an exceptionally rare neoplasm. We report a 64-year-old man who developed a 7.5-cm pelvic recurrence seven years after robot-assisted radical prostatectomy and bilateral seminal vesiculectomy for a right-sided MEST. He successfully underwent robot-assisted salvage re-excision. The recurrent mass was well-circumscribed without gross invasion into adjacent organs. Pathology confirmed a borderline recurrent MEST with negative margins. This case emphasizes the potential for delayed local recurrence in seminal vesicle MEST, mandating long-term surveillance, and illustrates the feasibility of robotic salvage surgery in a previously operated pelvis.Keywords: Seminal vesicles, Mixed epithelial-stromal tumor, Neoplasm recurrence, Robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, Salvage therapy

Introduction

Primary tumors of the seminal vesicle are exceedingly rare and include both benign and malignant entities. Among the malignant subtypes, mixed epithelial-stromal tumors (MESTs), historically termed phyllodes tumors, are among the rarest.1, 2, 3, 4 Reikie et al. proposed that the term “MEST of the seminal vesicle” be used as a unified nomenclature for these biphasic seminal vesicle tumors.2 More recently, the 2022 WHO Classification defined seminal vesicle MEST as a biphasic tumor composed of stromal and benign epithelial components.3

Although surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment, recurrence can occur and long-term follow-up data are limited.2,5 Robotic surgery appears to be a feasible minimally invasive option for selected seminal vesicle tumors because the deep retrovesical location is well suited to the enhanced visualization and dexterity of the robotic platform; however, published experience remains sparse.5, 6, 7, 8.

We report a case of recurrent right seminal vesicle MEST occurring seven years after initial robot-assisted resection performed in 2019, successfully managed with robotic re-excision in 2026. This case highlights the long-term recurrence potential of this rare tumor and the feasibility of repeat robotic surgery in a previously operated pelvis.

Case presentation

Clinical history

A 64-year-old man presented in 2019 with lower urinary tract symptoms. His medical history was notable for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and obstructive sleep apnea. Upon initial evaluation, physical examination revealed an immobile, elastic hard mass on the anterior rectal wall without tenderness. Serum PSA was within normal limits at 1.0 ng/mL. Computed tomography (CT) and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 5.0 cm solid right seminal vesicle mass with a smooth contour but irregular contents; prostatic invasion could not be excluded (Fig. 1). Transrectal ultrasound-guided needle biopsy demonstrated spindle cell proliferation, with moderately increased cellularity, mild nuclear pleomorphism, 1 mitosis per 10 high-power fields, suggesting a borderline-to-malignant seminal vesicle stromal tumor.

Radiological evaluation of the right seminal vesicle mass. (A) MRI image at the time of detection, revealing a 5.0 cm solid mass in the right seminal vesicle, suspicious for prostatic invasion. (B) Surveillance pelvic MRI (June 26, 2024) demonstrating no evidence of tumor recurrence.

Because preoperative MRI could not confidently exclude prostatic invasion, the patient underwent robot-assisted radical prostatectomy with bilateral seminal vesicle excision rather than isolated vesiculectomy. Intraoperatively, the well-encapsulated mass was easily detached from surrounding structures, aside from mild biopsy-related adhesions.

Grossly, the right seminal vesicle specimen measured 5.0 × 4.0 × 4.0 cm. The tumor produced a bulging contour of the right seminal vesicle, abutted the prostate without parenchymal invasion, and showed a well-circumscribed margin with heterogeneous cut surface. Microscopic examination showed a biphasic epithelial-stromal neoplasm with leaf-like architecture, variable stromal cellularity, mild-to-moderate atypia, and low mitotic activity (1/10 HPF), consistent with MEST of the seminal vesicle, with negative surgical margins (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemistry showed CD34 positivity, focal PR positivity, low Ki-67 index, and negative staining for c-kit, ER, desmin, and SMA (Fig. 2). The prostate specimen also showed incidental adenocarcinoma, Gleason score 3 + 3 = 6, involving both lobes in the peripheral and transition zones. There was no extraprostatic extension, no seminal vesicle invasion, no lymphatic or perineural invasion, and the resection margin was negative (pT2Nx).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical features of the seminal vesicle tumor. (A) In a low-power view, a well-circumscribed tumor is confined to the seminal vesicle, and leaf-like clefts are observed. (B, C) Histologic examination demonstrated a biphasic tumor composed of stromal and epithelial components: (B) The stromal component features spindle cells in a fibromyxoid background with variable cellularity and mild-to-moderate nuclear atypia. (C) The epithelial component consists of 1-2 layers of cells with minimal atypia (D to F) Immunohistochemistry demonstrating a low Ki-67 index (D), positivity for CD34 (E), focal positivity for PR (F).

The postoperative recovery was uncomplicated; the patient was discharged after one week, with PSA dropping to <0.1 ng/mL on postoperative day 45. Following surgery, he underwent serial PSA testing every 6–12 months and annual MRI surveillance, which remained negative for local recurrence through his last scan on June 26, 2024 (Fig. 1).

At the time recurrence was detected, pelvic MRI in February 2026 demonstrated a 7.5 cm solid mass in the pelvic cavity, interpreted as recurrent MEST of the seminal vesicle, with no evidence of recurrence at the prostatectomy site and no other definite metastatic lesion in the pelvis (Fig. 3).PET-CT showed corresponding FDG uptake in the pelvic mass, supporting the diagnosis of local recurrence. At the same time, PSA remained low at 0.03 ng/mL. For metastatic evaluation, chest CT revealed two new bilateral lower lobe pulmonary nodules, measuring 4 mm in the right lower lobe and 8 mm in the left lower lobe, which were considered suspicious for metastases. However, no corresponding abnormal thoracic FDG uptake was identified on PET-CT, and their metastatic nature remained unconfirmed at the time of surgery. These nodules were therefore managed with continued surveillance (Fig. 3). After multidisciplinary discussion, robot-assisted re-excision was planned.

(D) Corresponding FDG PET-CT image demonstrating no significant FDG avidity in the right pulmonary nodule. (E) Chest CT image revealing a small nodule in the left lower pulmonary lobe (arrow). (F) Corresponding FDG PET-CT image demonstrating no significant FDG avidity in the left pulmonary nodule.

The patient underwent robot-assisted re-excision of the recurrent pelvic mass in March 2026. Intraoperatively, mild adhesion of the sigmoid colon to the abdominal wall was noted, likely related to the prior surgery (Fig. 4). The pelvic mass measured approximately 8–9 cm and originated from the posterior surface of the bladder. Importantly, there was no gross invasion into adjacent organs, and the tumor could be dissected free from the surrounding structures. Tumor dissection was carried out from the adjacent organs, and the specimen was removed through an additional 2-cm supraumbilical incision. Estimated blood loss was 20 mL, and the perioperative course was uneventful.

Intraoperative and macroscopic appearances of the recurrent pelvic mass. (A) Intraoperative image during robot-assisted re-excision showing the well-circumscribed recurrent pelvic mass. (B) Intact specimen showing a well-circumscribed, lobulated mass with surface vasculature. (C) Cut surface revealing a heterogeneous, multicystic architecture.

Grossly, the excised pelvic mass measured 10.5 × 7.2 × 4.5 cm (Fig. 4). Histopathologic examination confirmed recurrent borderline MEST with negative surgical margins. Microscopically, the tumor showed moderate cellularity, moderate nuclear pleomorphism, 1 mitosis per 10 high-power fields, absence of necrosis, absence of angiolymphatic invasion, and a pushing tumor border. Immunohistochemistry showed CD34 positivity, Ki-67 index of 5%, and negative staining for SMA, c-kit, desmin, estrogen receptor, and progesterone receptor.

Discussion

Seminal vesicle MEST remains an exceptionally rare retrovesical tumor and is often clinically difficult to recognize because its presentation is nonspecific or even incidental. When symptomatic, they most commonly cause lower urinary tract symptoms, dysuria, hematuria, hematospermia, pelvic or lower abdominal pain, constipation, or a palpable retrovesical mass on digital rectal examination.5,9 In the systematic review by Babar et al., which summarized 36 reported cases of seminal vesicle MEST from 1944 onward, the mass was palpable on digital rectal examination in 56% of patients (20/36), and concomitant prostatic adenocarcinoma was identified in 6% (2/36).5

Radiologic and pathologic evaluation may narrow the differential diagnosis, but a definitive diagnosis is often difficult before resection. CT and MRI are central to the evaluation of these tumors, with MRI being particularly useful for local characterization and surgical planning, although imaging findings are often nonspecific and may not reliably distinguish MEST from other retrovesical lesions.4 In published reports, seminal vesicle MEST usually appears as a retrovesical mass with variable solid and cystic components, more often compressing adjacent structures rather than clearly invading them.6,9,10 However, this pattern is not universal. Yu et al.11 described a massive seminal vesicle MEST with dense adherence to the rectum, bladder, and distal ureters that required more extensive extirpative surgery for complete resection. In contrast, our case mirrored the more typical pattern, as the tumor remained well circumscribed and was dissectable from the surrounding pelvic structures at both operations, without gross invasion of adjacent organs.

Because of the rarity of this entity, no dedicated NCCN, AUA, ASCO, or EAU guideline specifically addresses seminal vesicle MEST. Management in published cases has therefore been driven by case-based experience, with complete surgical excision regarded as the primary treatment strategy. Documented surgical approaches used to treat MEST have varied and included isolated complete tumor resection (if technically feasible), vesiculectomy, vesiculoprostatectomy, and cystoprostatectomy.2 Recent systematic review data suggest that surgery is the cornerstone of management, but recurrence remains possible and long-term follow-up data are sparse.5 Robotic approaches to seminal vesicle lesions — predominantly benign cystadenomas and isolated vesiculectomy — have been also reported.6, 7, 8,11 but experience in recurrent malignant or borderline MEST, particularly in a previously operated pelvis, remains anecdotal.

In the available literature, management of seminal vesicle MEST is primarily surgical. Chemotherapy or radiotherapy has only been reported in isolated cases, particularly in higher-grade or metastatic disease, and no standardized role for adjuvant treatment has been established. In the published case reports, only one patient in the report by Fain et al.12 received chemotherapy for lung metastasis, while one case reported by Son et al.13 underwent postoperative radiotherapy after surgical excision. NCCN does acknowledge a potential role for adjuvant radiotherapy in borderline or malignant phyllodes tumors, but this recommendation is derived from the breast cancer setting rather than seminal vesicle disease.14 Therefore, any extrapolation of adjuvant radiotherapy to seminal vesicle tumors should be made with caution, and its role in this setting remains uncertain.

Our case highlights three clinically important points. First, it demonstrates delayed local recurrence occurring seven years after the initial resection. Although seminal vesicle MEST is often considered a low-grade neoplasm, recurrence has also been documented in prior case reports2,13 and was identified in the systematic review by Babar et al.,5 indicating that these tumors should not be regarded as uniformly indolent. This case therefore supports the need for prolonged postoperative surveillance, as late relapse may occur despite apparently complete resection and years of negative follow-up.

Second, our case suggests that recurrent disease may enlarge substantially over a relatively short period once it becomes clinically detectable. This observation is important because it implies that, even after a long disease-free interval, recurrent seminal vesicle MEST may not necessarily follow a slowly progressive course.

Third, this case supports the feasibility of robotic salvage surgery in a previously operated pelvis. In our case, the recurrent tumor remained well circumscribed and could be dissected free from the surrounding pelvic structures without gross invasion, consistent with the relatively expansile rather than infiltrative pattern described in published reports. This feature is clinically relevant, because it suggests that selected recurrent lesions may still be amenable to repeat surgical resection, and that a robotic approach can provide excellent exposure and precise dissection in the deep retrovesical space, even after prior pelvic surgery.

This report has several limitations. Pathologic grading remains intrinsically uncertain because grading concepts for phyllodes-type tumors are largely extrapolated from breast pathology and are not standardized for seminal vesicle MEST at this distinct anatomic site. Molecular characterization was also not performed; therefore, the clonal relationship between the 2019 primary tumor and the 2026 recurrence could not be formally established, and potentially informative alterations such as MED12, TERT, or MDM2 were not assessed. In addition, post-salvage follow-up remains short, so the long-term durability of disease control has not yet been demonstrated. More broadly, the absence of comparative data is inherent to the rarity of this disease. Even so, well-documented individual cases remain valuable because they incrementally refine current understanding of nomenclature, pathology, recurrence patterns, and surgical management for this rare seminal vesicle neoplasm.

Conclusion

Recurrent MEST of the seminal vesicle is exceptionally rare. This case illustrates that delayed local recurrence may occur years after initial resection and that robot-assisted re-excision can be successfully performed in a previously operated pelvis. Complete excision remains the mainstay of treatment, and prolonged imaging surveillance should be considered after surgery.
