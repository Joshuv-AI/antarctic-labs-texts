Introduction

Triglyceride deposit cardiomyovasculopathy (TGCV) is a rare cardiovascular disorder characterized by impaired intracellular lipolysis of triglyceride (TG), resulting in TG accumulation within the myocardium and coronary artery vascular smooth muscle cells.1,2 Known causes of TGCV include both genetic and acquired deficiencies of adipose TG lipase (ATGL), the rate-limiting enzyme responsible for TG hydrolysis. TGCV is classified into 2 types: primary TGCV, associated with mutations in the ATGL genes, and idiopathic TGCV, which occurs without identifiable ATGL mutations. Patients with TGCV have a high risk of cardiovascular events, such as coronary artery disease and heart failure. Although ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death are known to occur in patients with TGCV,3, 4, 5 the characteristics of ventricular tachycardia (VT) remain unknown. To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports on TGCV have clarified the electrophysiological characteristics of VT. Herein, we present a case in which delineation of the VT isthmus using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) facilitated the identification of a VT circuit in a patient with idiopathic TGCV.

Case report

A 73-year-old man with a history of coronary artery disease was referred to our department for the treatment of VT associated with syncope. He had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia and had previously undergone percutaneous coronary intervention for the left circumflex and right coronary arteries at another hospital. Until admission, the patient had not been hospitalized for heart failure or arrhythmia. At the referring hospital, a 12-lead electrocardiogram revealed VT with left bundle branch morphology and an inferior axis (cycle length, 360 ms) (Figure 1A). Laboratory analyses revealed the following: brain natriuretic peptide, 47.6 pg/mL; total cholesterol, 148 mg/dL; TGs, 196 mg/dL; low-density lipoprotein, 83 mg/dL; and hemoglobin A1c, 9.2%. Coronary angiography revealed diffuse narrowing of the coronary arteries without significant stenosis requiring intervention (Figure 1B). The left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction was 60% on transthoracic echocardiography. Cardiac MRI showed delayed enhancement in the basal LV septum, which was remote from the area of the previously treated coronary arteries (Figure 1B). Given that cardiomyopathy of nonischemic etiology was suspected, an endomyocardial biopsy from the right ventricular (RV) aspect of the intraventricular septum and myocardial scintigraphy were performed. This finally led to the diagnosis of idiopathic TGCV, based on the following findings: (1) myocardial TG deposition confirmed by biopsy (Figure 2), (2) a decreased washout rate (<10%) on myocardial iodine-123-β-methyl iodophenyl-pentadecanoic acid single-photon emission computed tomography (Supplemental Figure 1), and (3) diffuse coronary artery narrowing observed on coronary angiography, as previously described (Supplemental Table 1).6

Before VT ablation, MR images were processed using commercially available ADAS3D software (ADAS3D, ADAS3D Medical S.L., Barcelona, Spain).7 In brief, manual tracing of the endo- and epicardial borders was performed with a subsequent semiautomatic generation of the LV shell. Based on quantitative analysis of pixel signal intensity (PSI), ADAS3D generated a total of 9 maps, each compromising 10% of the myocardium from the endocardium to the epicardium (layers 10 and 90 for the endocardium and epicardium, respectively), wherein LGE PSI was projected onto each map and coded according to a trilinear interpolation algorithm, using 40% and 60% of the maximum intensity as scar thresholds. Notably, PSI values of ≥60% were tagged as dense scar (red), ≤40% as healthy tissue (blue), and values between 40% and 60% as the border zone (intermediate color range). Corridors are defined as border zone tissue surrounded by dense scars or dense scars and anatomic barriers, which have been related to VT isthmuses in electroanatomic maps.8,9 In the present case, the ADAS3D software detected a corridor (green line) within the border zone scar located between the dense scar and the aortic annulus in the basal LV, in the subepicardial layer (70% and 80% layers from the endocardium) (Figure 3A).

During the electrophysiological study, endocardial LV and RV mapping was performed during VT using the CARTO system and a DECANAV catheter (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). VT was reproducibly induced by extrastimuli from the ventricle. The VT activation maps revealed a centrifugal pattern in both ventricles, with the earliest activation site in the right coronary cusp associated with the potential preceding 25 ms of QRS onset (Figure 3B). The presence of constant fusion indicates reentry as the mechanism of VT. Entrainment pacing from the earliest activation sites in the LV and RV showed adjacent and remote bystander responses, respectively (Figure 3C). Pace mapping was performed on both ventricles; however, it could not reproduce the morphology of clinical VT. These findings suggest that the VT isthmus is located in the intramural or epicardial myocardium, as indicated by the preprocedural ADAS3D imaging. Given the proximity of the right coronary cusp to the corridor, the first radiofrequency ablation was delivered at the right coronary cusp, which terminated the VT and rendered it noninducible (Figure 3A). This patient received an implantable cardioverter defibrillator 1 week after ablation, and no further recurrence of VT was observed after 10 months.

Discussion

The electrophysiological characteristics of VT have not been investigated previously. In our case, the electrophysiological study and ADAS3D imaging suggested that the VT originated from the basal ventricular septum. An optimal ablation site was identified based on the ADAS3D imaging, specifically, the area closest to the presumed VT isthmus, which successfully suppressed the VT.

TGCV was first reported in 2008 in a patient who underwent cardiac transplantation.1 Defective intracellular TG lipolysis caused cellular steatosis and energy failure, mainly involving vascular smooth muscle cells and cardiomyocytes. Many patients with TGCV remain undiagnosed owing to limited awareness of the disease and the necessity for specific diagnostic tests that indicate TG accumulation in the myocardium. Postmortem pathologic screening indicated that a significant number of patients with TGCV had died without any clinical diagnosis.3,10 Patients with TGCV are typically diagnosed in their mid-60s, and by 5 years, half have experienced an adverse cardiovascular event, and nearly 30% have died.3 Hirano et al3 reported that 48 of 183 patients with TGCV (26.2%) experienced ventricular arrhythmia, and 5 required hospitalization or surgery for arrhythmia during the 5-year follow-up period in their Japanese cohort of TGCV. However, their study only provided data on the incidence of VT, and comprehensive data on ventricular arrhythmias and the risk of sudden death in patients with TGCV are still lacking. Further studies are needed to investigate the risk factors for predicting future ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.

Advanced cardiac imaging using cardiac MRI can identify areas of late gadolinium enhancement, which correlate with VT isthmuses.8,11 Integration of cardiac MRI-derived PSI maps in electroanatomic maps has been shown to improve the outcomes of VT ablation.12,13 Our case also demonstrated the utility of cardiac MRI-derived PSI maps using ADAS3D software to identify the VT isthmus and determine the optimal ablation site in a patient with TGCV. In our patient, VT originated from the basal ventricular septum, as anticipated based on the preprocedural MRI. Because the VT isthmus was considered to exist in the intramural or epicardial myocardium, we ablated the site closest to the expected VT isthmus (corridor) based on cardiac MRI-derived PSI images. In the present case, delayed enhancement was observed in the basal LV septum, a region remote from the previously treated coronary arteries. TG accumulation in the myocardium or the vascular smooth muscle cells of small coronary arteries in this area might have contributed to myocardial injury. However, the relation between the VT substrate and TGCV could not be ascertained in the present case because biopsy samples were not obtained from the area of interest. Furthermore, the anatomic substrate of TGCV has never been characterized by MRI or pathologic studies. Future studies investigating the common pathogenic substrate in patients with TGCV will help clarify the characteristics of ventricular tachyarrhythmias.

Recently, marked regression of diffuse coronary atherosclerosis and recovery from heart failure have been demonstrated in patients treated with supplemental tricaprin, a medium-chain TG.14,15 Tricaprin might also be a potential therapeutic agent in patients with TGCV associated with ventricular arrhythmias. Further studies are required to clarify the characteristics of VT in patients with TGCV.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates a detailed analysis of VT originating from the basal ventricular septum in a patient with idiopathic TGCV. Preprocedural MRI facilitated the identification of ablation targets.
