How are health capital and income inequality linked? Analysis of the 2012–2018 China Labor-force Dynamics Survey
Authors: Daisheng Tang, Zhen Zhang, Lingyue Gao, Xiangbo Liu, Lanling Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to alleviate income inequality is a significant challenge faced by all countries worldwide, and disparities in health capital are one of the fundamental causes of income gaps. A thorough exploration of the relationship between health capital disparities and income gaps holds substantial practical significance. Based on the 2012–2018 China Labor-force Dynamics Survey, we employ OLS models, quantile regression, Shapley value decomposition, and Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition to provide a detailed estimation of the impact of health capital disparities on income gaps of labour. We find that health capital is a crucial driver of income increase, with its impact most pronounced at the 20th income percentile. Additionally, through Shapley decomposition, we find that health capital contributes 12.2% to overall income inequality. Although female, middle-aged and elderly, rural, and low education-level groups exhibit larger income inequality compared to their counterparts, health capital exerts a stronger influence on within-group income inequality for these disadvantaged populations. Furthermore, using Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition, we also find that health capital disparities contribute 12.8%, 12.31%, 9.83%, and 10.66% to the income gaps across gender, age, urban–rural, and education-level groups, respectively. Health capital not only significantly affects within-group income inequality but is also a key determinant of between-group income gaps. Therefore, enhancing investment in health capital, particularly for vulnerable populations, will contribute to promoting income equality and social equity.Keywords: health capital, income gaps, Shapley value decomposition, Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition

Introduction

Although some progress has been made in addressing income inequality in China, the income inequality index remains at a relatively high level (as shown by the Lorenz curve of labor income in China from 2012 to 2018 in Fig. 1). Research on income gaps continues to be an important topic for scholars (Govind 2025, Marson and Saccone 2025), including the impact of macroeconomic factors such as economic development (Yan et al. 2025), policy support (Tan et al. 2025), and resource endowments (Wang and Bai 2023) on urban–rural income gaps, as well as the influence of micro-level factors such as digital literacy (Chen et al. 2024) and educational human capital (Björkman-Nyqvist 2013) on individual income inequality. However, health capital, as a core component of human capital, has a potentially significant impact on individual income and overall income distribution (Barro 1996, Zimmer 2015, Bhattacharjee et al. 2017, Nwosu and Woolard 2017). Existing research on the relationship between health capital and income inequality is relatively limited. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a more detailed and intuitive analysis to explain the effect of health capital disparities on income gaps. This analysis is of great significance for both developing and developed countries in narrowing income inequality and promoting social equity.

Research on the relationship between health capital and income mainly focuses on two aspects. The first is the analysis of the impact of health capital on income. Some studies have found that insufficient health capital significantly reduces labor income (Berkovec and Stern 1991, Jones et al. 2020, Petersen et al. 2020, Berha et al. 2021). Additionally, research using national panel data has found a significant positive correlation between health capital and national economic growth (Barro 1996). Therefore, despite differences in the selection of health capital indicators and methods, the finding that health capital has a positive impact on income remains robust (Zimmer 2015, Nwosu and Woolard 2017).

The second focus is on the impact of health capital disparities on income gaps. Some studies have found that increased investment in physical health can significantly improve labor productivity, particularly in low-income households (Ke 2015). Moreover, the return on health capital in economically underdeveloped regions far exceeds that of other forms of capital (Strauss 1986), indicating that health capital disparities are a significant driver of income gaps. Other studies have used quantile regression to show that the effect of health capital on increasing income is stronger for low-income groups, thereby helping to reduce income gaps (Xie et al. 2020, Castillo et al. 2025). However, as there is limited research directly examining the relationship between health capital disparities and income gaps, we directly estimate this relationship to make an important contribution to the literature in this field.

Previous research has two notable shortcomings. First, existing studies exhibit limitations in measuring health capital. Health capital is multidimensional and holistic, with the World Health Organization defining health as a state of physical, mental, and social well-being—a definition widely endorsed by scholars (Lewis 2011, Tallini 2011). Mental health and social adaptation are equally important components of health capital. However, existing research often focuses on physical health when measuring health capital (Alderman et al. 2006, Chen and Zhou 2007, Bendor et al. 2020), neglecting mental health and social adaptation. Additionally, self-rated health measures are the most commonly used indicators of health capital (Zhong et al. 2024), but they are susceptible to the evaluator's cognitive abilities and environmental factors, resulting in poor objectivity (Layes et al. 2012). Second, most studies have employed quantile regression to analyze differences in health capital returns across income groups (Xie et al. 2020, Castillo et al. 2025) or used group-specific estimations to demonstrate stronger income effects of health capital in low-income households (Ke 2015). These approaches fail to directly reflect the explanatory power of health capital disparities with regard to income gaps or to intuitively illustrate the heterogeneity of health capital disparities in explaining within-group and between-group income gaps.

Therefore, this study utilizes the 2012–2018 China Labor-force Dynamics Survey (CLDS 2012–2018) to construct a health capital index from three dimensions—physical health, mental health, and social adaptation—providing a more objective measure of health capital. In addition to regression analysis of the relationship between health capital and income, we further employ Shapley decomposition and Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition to analyze the impact of health capital disparities on within-group income inequality and between-group income gaps across different groups. We find that health capital has a significant income-increasing effect, particularly for low-income individuals. We also find that health capital exerts a greater influence on within-group income inequality among disadvantaged populations, and that health capital disparities are a critical determinant of between-group income gaps across different groups.

Compared to existing studies, our study offers two marginal contributions. First, we employ a multidimensional index to comprehensively measure individual health capital levels. Different from the single-dimensional health capital indicators commonly used in most studies (Alderman et al. 2006, Chen and Zhou 2007, Bendor et al. 2020, Zhong et al. 2024), the multidimensional health capital index provides a more objective reflection of respondents’ health capital. Second, existing studies primarily use quantile regression to demonstrate that health capital has a stronger impact on low-income groups, thereby explaining how health capital can narrow income inequality (Ke 2015, Xie et al. 2020, Castillo et al. 2025). Such research did not directly estimate the impact of health capital disparities on income inequality. However, we directly estimate and discuss the impact of health capital on income inequality and income gaps by Shapley decomposition and Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition. We not only provide a novel perspective for explaining income inequality but also directly analyze the impact of health capital disparities on income gaps. Therefore, this research offers more intuitive and detailed analytical results on the role of health capital disparities in income inequality, providing valuable insights for targeted measures to reduce income inequality and promote social equity.

Conceptual framework

Health capital and health are twin concepts, but they are fundamentally distinct. Clarifying this distinction necessitates rigorous theoretical delineation. From the perspective of economic analysis, the conceptual evolution of health capital marks a pivotal theoretical shift—from viewing health as a static consumption good toward recognizing it as a dynamic productive capital asset.

In early economic research, health was predominantly treated as a final consumption good that yields direct utility (Mushkin 1962), with demand for medical services deriving from the desire to consume health itself. Becker’s human capital theory catalyzed a paradigm shift by systematically conceptualizing individual health as investible capital capable of generating future economic returns—transcending its prior framing as a mere consumption good (Becker 1965). Then, Grossman formalized this transition by constructing the health capital model, reconceptualizing health as a durable capital stock (Grossman 1972). The framework critically established that health capital possesses inherent productive properties: it is accumulated through deliberate investments, while simultaneously undergoing irreversible depreciation driven by aging and disease (Grossman 2000). This model cemented health’s dual role in economic decision-making—serving as both a consumption good and an investment good.

Thus, health constitutes an individual’s physiological and psychological state at a given moment. As a consumption good, its value lies in generating direct utility (Mushkin 1962). While health improvements can mitigate income losses caused by illness (Currie and Madrian 1999), they cannot inherently transform into productive factors that generate income growth. In contrast, health capital is an investment good that individuals use to produce ‘healthy time’ (Grossman 1972). Its fundamental attributes—productivity and returnability—derive from its role as a factor of production (Ehrlich and Chuma 1990). The returns to health capital manifest as marginal increases in income (Cutler et al. 2006, Currie 2008).

This study primarily treats health capital as an investment good, focusing on how disparities in health investments contribute to income gaps. The distinction between health capital and health is of paramount theoretical importance.

Methods

Data sources

The primary data used in this study are derived from the CLDS, a nationally representative longitudinal survey. The CLDS project conducted a pilot survey in Guangdong Province in August–September 2011. The first wave of the nationwide formal survey was launched in 2012, followed by three subsequent waves in 2014, 2016, and 2018. The CLDS sample covers 29 provinces and municipalities in China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, Tibet, and Hainan), with the survey targeting all labor-force individuals aged 15–64 years. The survey collects data at three levels: community, household, and individual, including community environment, household economic status, and individual health, education, and economic activities, providing robust data support for this study on the impact of health capital income gaps of labour. Additionally, we also utilize the China City Statistical Yearbook 2012–2018, incorporating city-level variables such as per capita GDP and the number of practicing (assistant) physicians per thousand people into the model for robustness checks to address potential omitted variable bias. The China City Statistical Yearbook only collects city-level data for prefecture-level cities and does not include macro-level data for autonomous regions. In the CLDS survey data, some samples are drawn from autonomous regions. Therefore, we manually collected macro-level data for the autonomous regions covered in the CLDS survey to supplement the dataset and avoid the loss of additional observations. It is important to note that the CLDS collects data from the year preceding the survey year. Accordingly, we also used city-level data from the year preceding the CLDS survey year as macro-level data

In the data processing procedure, we first merged the household data with the individual data using the unique individual ID, and then integrated the city-level data using the city ID. After filtering the variables required for this study, such as health, income, and education, etc., we excluded observations with missing values in any of these variables. Furthermore, to ensure the representativeness of the observations, we also deleted cities with <30 observations. We finally obtained an unbalanced panel dataset comprising 41 160 observations from 2012 to 2018.

Variables

Income

The CLDS has the question, “What is your total income from all sources”. We use the answer of this question as the income for individual laborers, with the unit is ten thousand RMB. We also deflate it to 2011 comparable prices using the CPI index. (The total income collected by CLDS2012 is the total income of individual labor in 2011, therefore it is reduced to comparable prices in 2011). Consequently, we employed the logarithm of annual income of individual laborers as a proxy variable for income (inc).

Health capital

Quantifying health capital primarily involves the following three methods. First, using basic physical characteristics as proxy variables for health capital, such as height (Chen and Zhou 2007), BMI (Alderman et al. 2006), and chronic diseases (Bendor et al. 2020). Second, employing self-rated health as the sole measure of health capital (García-Gómez et al. 2010, Zhong et al. 2024). Third, constructing an objective health capital index using multiple related indicators (Ortiz-Valverdi 2017, Zhen et al. 2024). To derive an objective individual health capital index, we use factor analysis to construct the health capital index based on physical health, mental health, and social adaptation. The health capital index we developed is methodologically credible because factor analysis is widely employed to compute health indicators (Baldwin and Johnson 1994). The specific indicators and scoring methods for each dimension of health capital, as well as the testing and computational procedures of the factor analysis, are detailed in supplementary Appendix A (see online supplementary material).

Other explanatory variables

Existing research has shown that factors such as gender, age, education level, health insurance, and urban–rural residence have a direct impact on the health capital of individual laborers (Gundersen and Kreider 2009, Chen et al. 2019, Banerjee et al. 2020, Zhen et al. 2024). Moreover, the educational level of parents also exerts an intergenerational effect on the health capital of their offspring (Case et al. 2002, Lundborg et al. 2018). Therefore, we selected gender, age, education level (edu), medical insurance (medinsure), urban–rural residence (), as well as the educational levels of both father and mother (fedu and medu) as control variables. Results

Correlation analysis of health capital and income

We examined the correlation between health capital and income using scatter plots and performed linear fit curves, as shown in supplementary Appendix B (see online supplementary material). The slope of the fitted curve between health capital and annual income is 0.616 and significantly different from 0, indicating a significant positive correlation between health capital and annual income. Furthermore, we also plotted scatter and linear fit curves for six sub-indicators of health capital and annual income, also shown in supplementary Appendix B. Among these sub-indicators of health capital, “Frequency of illness affecting daily life” and “Frequency of feeling unable to control the life” are negative indicators, with their linear fitted curves showing negative slopes, indicating a negative correlation. The other four indicators—“Self-rated health,” “Frequency of feeling confident about the future,” “Frequency of communication with locals,” and “How many friends can help you”—are positive indicators, with their linear fitted curves showing positive slopes, indicating a positive correlation. Therefore, the above analysis suggests that higher health capital is associated with higher annual income. To verify the robustness of the positive correlation between health capital and income, we also used individual wage income as an alternative measure of income. We plotted scatter diagrams of health capital and wage income, as well as scatter diagrams of the six sub-indicators of health capital and wage income, as detailed in supplementary Appendix C (see online supplementary material). The results indicate that health capital is also positively correlated with wage income, suggesting that higher health capital is associated with higher wage income. This further reinforces the robustness of the relationship between health capital and income, as does our preliminary evidence.

Shapley value decomposition of the effect of health capital on within-group income inequality

To provide an intuitive understanding of income inequality within different groups, we categorized individuals into sub-groups and plotted the Lorenz curve of income. Specifically, we considered differences in gender, age, urban–rural residence, and education level to analyze income inequality. Firstly, significant gender disparities exist between men and women in access to education, employment, healthcare, and social support, often placing women in a disadvantaged position (Backhans et al. 2007, Luo et al. 2021). Therefore, we examined heterogeneity across gender groups. Secondly, individuals aged ≥45 years are typically defined as middle-aged and elderly in the background of China, and some scholars also consider 45 years as a critical age threshold (Zhang et al. 2017, Ettman et al. 2025, Ko et al. 2025). Thus, we defined individuals aged ≥45 years as middle-aged and elderly, and those <45 years as younger, and explored heterogeneity across age groups. Thirdly, there are significant urban–rural disparities in terms of health resources, employment opportunities, and income levels (Nguyen et al. 2024, Cheng et al. 2025, Han et al. 2025). Hence, we also considered heterogeneity across urban and rural groups. Finaly, individuals with different education levels exhibit significant differences in cognitive and comprehension abilities (Fletcher et al. 2021), making educational heterogeneity another important factor for discussion. We referenced numerous studies based on Chinese background (Song et al. 2025, Yang et al. 2025, Zhang and Meng 2025), as well as China’s Compulsory Education Law enacted in 1986. Therefore, we used the completion of compulsory education as the criterion to distinguish between high and low education levels.

The Lorenz curve provides an intuitive visualization of income inequality within-groups (Wang et al. 2009). Figure 2 presents the Lorenz curves for income across different groups. [We also plotted the Lorenz curve of individual wage income, which is consistent with Fig. 2 (as shown in supplementary Appendix E, see online supplementary material).] It is evident that the Lorenz curves for male, younger, urban, and high education-level groups are closer to the line of perfect equality, indicating lower income inequality compared to female, middle-aged and elderly, rural, and low education-level groups. This suggests that disadvantaged groups experience higher levels of income inequality.

Moreover, we also analyzed the contribution of health capital to the income inequality of different groups, including gender, age, urban–rural, and education level. First, when examining gender differences, males exhibit a lower Gini coefficient of 0.450 compared to females at 0.525. Health capital contributes 8.85% to income inequality among males, whereas it contributes a substantial 32.98% among females. This stark contrast suggests that health capital plays a more pronounced role in exacerbating income inequality among females. Second, age-related analysis shows younger people have a Gini coefficient of 0.443, with health capital contributing 8% to income inequality. In contrast, middle-aged and elderly individuals have a higher Gini coefficient of 0.518, and health capital’s contribution increases to 22.42%, indicating its greater impact on older populations due to accumulated health costs and reduced earning capacity over time. Third, urban residents exhibit a lower Gini coefficient of 0.377 compared to that of rural residents at 0.517. This highlights the more significant role of health capital in income inequality in rural settings. Those with low education-levels have a higher Gini coefficient of 0.538, and health capital’s contribution is 22.04%, suggesting a slightly larger impact among low education-level individuals.

However, since this study focuses on health capital, only the detailed decomposition results for health capital are presented in the main text. Column 1 of Table 5 presents Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition results for the impact of health capital on the income disparity between males and females. The mean difference in the logarithm of annual income between males and females is 1.166, which is statistically significant at the 1% level according to a t-test. In this difference, 0.075 is attributed to disparities in health capital endowments, 0.014 to differences in the returns to health capital, and an additional 0.014 to the interaction between health capital endowments and returns. Consequently, health capital accounts for 12.8% of the gender income gaps. Columns 2–4 present similar decompositions for age, urban–rural divisions, and educational levels, respectively. For age, younger individuals have a mean log annual income that is 0.78 higher than that of older adults, with statistical significance at the 1% level. Health capital explains 12.31% of the income disparity between these age groups. In terms of urban–rural divisions, the mean log annual income for urban residents is 0.875 higher than that for rural residents, with statistical significance at the 1% level. Health capital contributes 9.83% to the income disparity between urban and rural areas. Regarding educational attainment, those with high education-level have a mean log annual income that is 0.78 higher than those with low education-level, with statistical significance at the 1% level. Health capital accounts for 10.66% of the income disparity across different levels of education. These findings highlight the pivotal role of health capital in shaping economic outcomes and underscore the need for policies aimed at improving health capital as a means to address income gaps.

Discussion

This study aims to analyze income gaps in China from the perspective of health capital, exploring the logic and rationale for achieving the goal of narrowing labor income gaps. Utilizing survey data from CLDS 2012–2018, we measure health capital of labor through physical health, mental health, and social adaptation. We empirically examine the impact of health capital on income and assess its significance in explaining both within-group income inequality and between-group income gaps. These findings have substantial implications for implementing targeted policies to reduce income inequality and improve social equity across different population groups.

Our study reveals a positive correlation between health capital and income. Further analysis using OLS estimation models confirms that this correlation is statistically significant, with a more pronounced effect on low-income groups. Through Shapley value decomposition, we identify health capital as a critical determinant of within-group income equality, particularly for disadvantaged groups. Additionally, employing Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition, we demonstrate that health capital disparities also play a substantial role in explaining between-group income gaps across various group. This research addresses gaps in the existing literature on the direct impact of health capital on income gaps.

Specifically, the statistically significant positive effect of health capital on annual income at the 1% level indicates that health capital is a crucial driver of income growth. Furthermore, the impact of health capital is most pronounced at the 20th income percentile, revealing that its role in increasing income is particularly significant for low-income populations. This suggests that health capital has substantial potential in reducing income inequality, and is consistent with the research conclusions of many scholars (Nwosu and Woolard 2017, Jones et al. 2020, Petersen et al. 2020, Berha et al. 2021). Therefore, increasing investment in health capital of low-income individuals could effectively promote an increase in their income.

Second, the Gini coefficients for income among male, younger, urban, and high education-level groups are 0.450, 0.443, 0.377, and 0.421, respectively, which are higher than those for female, middle-aged and elderly, rural, and low education-level groups, at 0.525, 0.518, 0.517, and 0.538. This indicates that within-group income inequality is larger among disadvantaged groups. However, the Shapley value decomposition results reveal that health capital contributes 12.2% to overall income inequality. Its contributions to within-group income inequality among male, younger, urban, and high education-level groups are 8.85%, 8%, 13.52%, and 20.6%, respectively, which are lower than those for female, middle-aged and elderly, rural, and low education-level groups, at 32.98%, 22.42%, 17.76%, and 22.04%. This demonstrates that health capital exerts a stronger influence on disadvantaged groups with larger income disparities. Health capital can directly or indirectly improve the economic conditions of disadvantaged groups (Ke 2015, Xie et al. 2020, Castillo et al. 2025), thereby reducing income inequality.

Third, employing Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition to analyze the impact of health capital on inter-group income disparities, we find that health capital contributes 12.8%, 12.31%, 9.83%, and 10.66% to the income gaps across gender, age, urban–rural, and education-level groups, respectively. Health capital exhibits strong explanatory power for between-group income gaps and can effectively narrow these gaps, promoting social equity.

Our study reveals that health capital not only has a significant income-enhancing effect but also plays a crucial role in reducing both within-group income inequality and between-group income gaps. These findings have important implications for improving income distribution inequality and advancing social equity in both developing and developed countries. However, this research has one limitation that warrants further investigation. Across the four rounds of CLDS surveys from 2012 to 2018, due to inconsistencies in health indicators across respondents, we had to balance the comparability of health capital measures across multiple survey waves, which precluded the construction of a more comprehensive and sophisticated health capital index.

Conclusion

Research on the impact of health disparities on income gaps has significant implications for the formulation of public health policies. The findings indicate that health capital exerts a substantial positive effect on labor income, and disparities in health capital are one of the critical factors contributing to income inequality. Moreover, increasing health capital can mitigate both within-group and between-group income gaps, thereby promoting social equity. Notably, health capital demonstrates a stronger effect in reducing income inequality among disadvantaged groups. Therefore, enhancing investment in health capital, particularly for vulnerable populations such as women, the middle-aged and elderly, rural residents, and those with low education-level individuals, will contribute to increasing labor income and fostering more equitable income distribution.

Based on the results of this study, the universal provision of basic health services and the reduction of inequities in the allocation of health resources not only improve the health capital of disadvantaged groups but also help alleviate poverty and advance social equity. From the perspective of health investment, governments should increase subsidies for medical services in low-income and vulnerable regions to address poverty induced by health issues as effectively as possible. From the perspective of health-service accessibility, efforts should be accelerated to enhance elderly care services, maternal and child health services, and the construction of health infrastructure in rural areas, thereby improving access to healthcare for disadvantaged populations.
