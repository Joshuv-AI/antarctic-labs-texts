Pulmonary indications for heart-lung transplantation: A modified Delphi survey among the French transplantation community
Authors: Eva Chatron, Benjamin Renaud-Picard, Eric Epailly, Xavier Demant, Jacques Jougon, David Boulate, Delphine Horeau-Langlard, Olaf Mercier, Elie Fadel, Anne Olland, Jérôme Le Pavec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart-lung transplantation (HLTx) is a life-saving intervention for patients with advanced cardiopulmonary disease. Its use has declined significantly in recent decades, and the pulmonary indications for HLTx remain unclear. This study aimed to explore current expert opinions and areas of agreement and disagreement on pulmonary indications for HLTx using a 2-round modified Delphi survey sent to 94 experts at 6 French HLTx centers, including pulmonologists, cardiologists, thoracic surgeons, cardiac surgeons, and anesthesiologists-intensivists. Items relevant to 9 key domains were selected by a working group based on literature reviews. Both surveys had 57 items. Consensus regarding each domain was evaluated by determining the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with an ICC >0.8 indicating strong consensus. In round 1, 40/94 experts responded. The items were scored from 1 to 9: 11 scored >7, 7 scored <3, and 39 had intermediate scores and were reformulated for the second round. The wide interquartile ranges indicated significant variability in responses, and for no domain was the ICC above 0.8. In round 2, which was offered to all experts, the response rate dropped to 25, precluding further statistical analysis. Key areas of disagreement included HLTx indications in severe PAH, right ventricular dysfunction, and congenital heart disease.These findings highlight the absence of a shared consensus and reflect the intrinsic complexity, rarity of the procedure, and variability in institutional expertise.Rather than establishing prescriptive criteria, this expert review underscores the need for individualized, multidisciplinary, and center-specific decision-making, while acknowledging ethical considerations related to donor scarcity. Future efforts should focus on multicenter data collection and predictive models integrating clinical variables and expert judgment to better identify patients who may benefit from HLTx.Keywords: Heart-lung transplantation, Pulmonary hypertension, Congenital heart disease, Transplantation ethics, Delphi method, Organ allocation

Take-home message

Pulmonary indications for heart-lung transplantation remain unclear, necessitating further research and consensus-building to optimize patient selection and improve outcomes.

Background

Heart-lung transplantation (HLTx) is a complex but life-saving option for patients with advanced cardiopulmonary disease. The frequency of HLTx has declined markedly in recent decades worldwide. In the late 1980s, over 200 heart-lung transplants were performed annually, with a peak in 1989, according to the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT).1 France has had an HLTx program since 1986, and the procedure is performed in 6 centers: the Marie-Lannelongue Hospital in the Paris region, where the volume is highest, and the University Hospitals of Bordeaux, Lyon, Nantes, Strasbourg, and Marseille (Figure 1). Since 1993, data from all centers are centralized by the Agence de la Biomédecine (the authority overseeing organ transplantation in France). The number of HLTx procedures reached a peak of 42 in 1993, then decreased drastically, dropping to 9 in 2024 (Figure 2).

Initially, idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and cystic fibrosis with severe pulmonary disease and secondary PAH were the main indications for HLTx given the lack of alternative treatments for right heart failure2 (Figure 3). HLTx was also commonly performed in pediatric patients due to the technical challenges raised by dual lung transplantation (DLTx).1, 3, 4 However, over time, DLTx has become the preferred approach for many patients who would previously have received HTLx.5 This change in indications, combined with advances in surgical techniques, improvements in the intensive-care management of right heart failure, and donor scarcity all contribute to explain the drop in the number of HLTx procedures worldwide to fewer than 100/year.1

Whereas cardiac indications for HLTx are fairly well defined,5 pulmonary indications remain unclear. Few studies have sought to update patient selection criteria to HLTx based on recent advances in transplantation medicine. To address this gap, in 2022, the French Agence de la Biomédecine created a multidisciplinary national HLTx review board and formed specialized working groups to examine current practices and develop updated, evidence-informed recommendations. One of these working groups focused specifically on pulmonary indications of HLTx.

The objective of this study conducted as part of the Agence de la Biomédecine review was to explore expert opinions on pulmonary indications for HLTx using a modified Delphi method.6, 7, 8 Rather than establishing prescriptive criteria, we aimed to identify areas of agreement and disagreement among experts, highlight persistent uncertainties, and better characterize the clinical scenarios in which HLTx is considered. Ultimately, this word aims to support multidisciplinary and patient-specific decision-making by clarifying current expert practices and identifying areas where further multicenter data are needed to improve patient selection for HLTx.

Methods

Study design

We used a modified Delphi method, which is an iterative consensus-building process in which experts complete structured questionnaires.6, 7, 8 The Delphi method is particularly well-suited for achieving a consensus when empirical evidence is lacking, as is the case for rare diseases and complex surgical procedures. With Delphi surveys, the experts provide independent feedback without being influenced by their peers, as may occur during open discussions. Also, the structured nature of the method allows a systematic analysis of expert opinions over successive rounds, thus progressively refining the consensus.

The ethical guidelines for Delphi studies were followed to ensure participant anonymity, voluntary participation, and transparent reporting.

Participants

In France, representatives of each of the 6 centers performing HLTx identified a total of 94 experts with extensive clinical expertise in HLTx, including pulmonologists, thoracic surgeons, cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and anesthesiologists-intensivists. Given the small number of HLTx procedures, selecting a representative and knowledgeable panel was crucial. We ensured diversity by including experts with different institutional backgrounds and specialties to capture a broad spectrum of opinions.

The identified experts were invited to participate in the Delphi procedure via personal invitations, multiple e-mail reminders, and discussions at national transplant meetings. The intent was to maximize engagement of the experts while maintaining the methodological rigor of the Delphi approach.

Delphi survey process

The Delphi process consisted of 2 rounds. The survey used for the first round was developed by a working group composed of 5 pulmonologists (from the Marie-Lannelongue Bordeaux, Lyon, Nantes, and Strasbourg hospitals, respectively), 3 thoracic surgeons (Bordeaux, Marseille, and Strasbourg), and 1 cardiologist (Strasbourg), with at least 1 representative from each participating transplant center. The group first identified 9 pivotal domains. Two PubMed literature reviews were then conducted to informs the discussion and identify items relevant to each of the 9 domains. The first review used the keywords “Delphi survey technique,” and “healthcare,” with no restriction on publication date. The second review focused on publications between 2010 and 2024, using the terms “heart-lung transplant” and “PAH with congenital heart disease,” or “connective tissue disease” or “sarcoidosis” or “right heart dysfunction.” During the first round, the participants rated each item on a numerical scale (1-9), where 1 indicated strong disagreement and 9 full agreement with relevance to the domain. Items whose median score was >7 were kept, and those whose median score was <3 were dropped. Items with median scores between 4 and 7 were included unchanged in the first survey, used for round 1, but were revised and reformulated for the second survey, used for round 2, as explained below. Appendix 1 lists the items in each domain for the first survey, whose total number was 57. The round-1 survey was distributed to the study participants online on February 22, 2024. Multiple email reminders were sent to nonrespondents. The round concluded on May 31, 2024.

The working group then reviewed those items that had median scores between 3 and 7 and developed a revised questionnaire (Appendix 1) for round 2. This questionnaire was sent on July 2, 2024, and responses were collected until November 1, 2024. All items were resubmitted to all participants in round 2 to assess the stability of responses between rounds. The second round focused on refining areas of disagreement, incorporating expert feedback, and reassessing controversial items. The participants were encouraged to elaborate on their responses to facilitate a deeper understanding of the differing perspectives.

Statistical analysis

We computed the median score for each item. For each domain, consensus was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). An ICC > 0.8 was taken to indicate strong consensus. The statistical analysis was conducted using R (RC core team, R foundation for statistical computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Response rates

The questionnaire was sent to 94 experts. Among them, 40 (42.6%) responded to the first round, including 4 cardiologists, 16 pulmonologists, 10 thoracic surgeons, and 10 anesthesiologists-intensivists. Of these 40 respondents to the first round, 25 (25/94, 26.6%) also responded to the second round.

Agreement about the items

At round 1, of the 57 items, 7 were invalidated (median score <3) and 11 validated (median score >7). The interquartile ranges were wide, indicating considerable variability in the responses (Figure 4). None of the 9 domains had an ICC > 0.8 (Table 1).

Despite the absence of consensus (ICC > 0.8), several trends emerged, highlighting areas where HLTx may still hold relevance. These areas include specific subsets of PAH with severe right ventricular dysfunction, certain congenital heart defects with irreversible pulmonary damage, and end-stage combined cardiac and pulmonary diseases with no alternative treatments.

Discussion

Current challenges in HLTx indications

Despite the use of a structured Delphi methodology, no consensus on the pulmonary indications for HLTx was achieved. Several challenges were identified. The field of HLTx is inherently complex since it involves the intersection of cardiac and pulmonary conditions, requiring a highly nuanced approach to patient selection. Furthermore, given the declining frequency of HLTx, many centers and clinicians have limited experience with this procedure, leading to inconsistencies in patient-selection criteria across institutions.

Lessons from recent studies on HLTx outcomes

Several recent studies investigating the outcomes of HLTx have provided insights into the evolving role for this procedure. The ISHLT registry data consistently show that HLTx recipients tend to have lower survival rates compared to lung or heart recipients.1, 9 However, in selected populations, such as patients with Eisenmenger syndrome or complex congenital heart disease, HLTx continues to demonstrate superior survival benefits compared to isolated lung or heart transplantation.1, 10, 11 These benefits suggest that HLTx still deserves consideration in certain high-risk patient subsets for whom no alternative transplantation strategies are viable.

Furthermore, emerging data on right ventricular dysfunction in end-stage lung disease indicate a need for reassessing current HLTx indications. Severe pulmonary hypertension with progressive right ventricular failure remains one of the most challenging scenarios in lung transplantation. Right ventricular dysfunction may be an underappreciated risk factor for early mortality after lung transplantation.12 Consequently, in some patients with advanced PAH and severe right ventricular dysfunction, HLTx might deserve preference over DLTx.

Heart-lung transplantation versus dual lung transplantation for end-stage pulmonary arterial hypertension

One of the most debated topics is whether HLTx or DLTx deserves preference in patients with end-stage PAH. Historically, HLTx was the preferred option, given its ability to address both the severe right ventricular failure and the irreversible pulmonary vascular disease. However, advances in perioperative management, post-transplant immunosuppression, and mechanical circulatory support have now made DLTx a viable alternative to HLTx with improved long-term outcomes in carefully selected patients.12, 13

Recent registry analyses found comparable or even superior survival after DLTx compared to HLTx in patients with PAH, assuming that right ventricular function recovers following DLTx.14, 15, 16, 17 In most patients, the right ventricle can undergo reverse remodeling once pulmonary vascular resistance returns to normal after lung transplantation.18, 19, 20 However, in patients with extreme right ventricular dysfunction, severe tricuspid regurgitation, or refractory cardiogenic shock, HLTx may still be the best approach.13, 21, 22, 23

Donor availability substantially impacts transplant decisions. DLTx allows the donor heart to be allocated to another patient, an important consideration given the dire shortage of heart transplants. However, in patients whose right ventricle is unlikely to recover after DLTx, HLTx may prevent post-operative heart failure and the need for mechanical circulatory support.23 Future research should focus on refining risk stratification models to better predict which patients with PAH will benefit more from HLTx than from DLTx.

Advances in perioperative management and post-transplant care

Another key area of research and progress is the perioperative and post-transplant management of recipients. Improvements in donor management, mechanical circulatory support, and immunosuppressive strategies have enhanced the outcomes of lung and heart recipients.13, 16, 22 Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is increasingly used as a bridge to HLTx, allowing for better preoperative stabilization and improved post-transplant recovery.24, 25, 26 However, the role for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation in determining HLTx eligibility remains unclear, and further research is needed to establish patient-selection criteria.

Novel immunosuppressive regimens and personalized immunomodulation strategies have improved long-term graft survival. Contemporary immunosuppression protocols have reduced the incidence of chronic lung allograft dysfunction, a leading cause of post-transplant mortality.21 Understanding how these advances impact HLTx indications and whether they can shift the risk-benefit ratio towards DLTx requires further investigation.

Ethical considerations in HLTx allocation

Given the increasing scarcity of donor organs, ethical considerations play a major role in determining HLTx candidacy. Organ allocation policies continue to evolve, with an increasing emphasis on maximizing overall survival while also ensuring equity in access.13 Some experts argue that HLTx should be reserved for younger patients with irreversible cardiac and pulmonary disease and no other treatment options. Others highlight the need to reconsider listing criteria for older patients, given the improved survival outcomes observed in older recipients of other solid organs over the past decade.27

Moreover, geographic disparities in HLTx access have been documented. Variability in referral patterns, transplant-center experience, and institutional policies can lead to inequities in patient selection.28, 29 Future studies should aim to address these disparities by developing standardized, evidence-based guidelines for HLTx eligibility.

Study limitations

This study presents several important limitations. First, the response rate was suboptimal, with 43% of invited experts participating in the first round and only 27% completing the second round. The reasons for non-participation remain unknown and may have introduced selection bias if experts with a particular interest or greater experience in HLTx were more likely to respond. This limitation directly impacts the representativeness and statistical robustness of our findings. Moreover, we did not collect data on the number of HLTx procedures each expert had participated in, preventing stratified analysis based on actual procedural exposure. Second, while efforts were made to assemble a diverse panel reflecting all disciplines involved in HLTx, the lack of selection criteria based on experience or case volume may have diluted the expertise relevant to the highly specific questions addressed. As a result, some participants may have felt insufficiently equipped to assess certain items, contributing to the wide variability in responses and absence of strong consensus (ICC > 0.8). Third, the online questionnaire distribution may have limited engagement. Given the current workload and time constraints faced by hospital-based physicians, email surveys are often given low priority. The structured format of the Delphi method, while valuable for avoiding groupthink, may also lack the dynamic exchange necessary to explore complex or ambiguous clinical scenarios. Fourth, this study reflects an early attempt to unify national opinions on HLTx indications. The recent establishment of a national multidisciplinary review board (RCP) in France in 2022 offers a promising opportunity to refine and disseminate best practices across centers. This RCP not only serves as a platform for shared decision-making but also contributes to the training of younger clinicians in the field of HLTx. Repeating the Delphi survey in the future among RCP participants who are actively engaged in these evaluations may yield more coherent and representative data. Lastly, the limited number of HLTx procedures performed annually in France—and globally—poses a major challenge to evidence generation. This rarity limits both individual and collective expertise, reducing the feasibility of large-scale comparative studies. It also underscores the need for international collaborations and registries to pool data and harmonize indication criteria.

Future directions

To advance our understanding of HLTx indications, researchers should perform large-scale multicenter studies that integrate hemodynamic, imaging, and biomarker data to refine patient selection. Also, machine-learning models to predict outcomes based on pre-transplant risk factors deserve evaluation as tools for enhancing decision-making.30

The low response rate in our Delphi study suggests that future consensus-developing efforts may benefit from hybrid methodologies that combine Delphi rounds with in-person expert panel discussions to ensure broader engagement. International collaboration through the ISHLT and other transplantation networks may also improve the standardization of HLTx eligibility criteria.

Conclusion

The Delphi-based expert review did not identify a shared consensus regarding pulmonary indications for HLTx, highlighting significant heterogeneity in expert opinion. Despite this lack of consensus, emerging studies and clinical experience support the continued relevance of HLTx in selected populations, particularly patients with severe pulmonary hypertension and complex congenital heart disease. Future studies should aim to integrate contemporary clinical data and expert input to improve patient selection, rather than focusing solely on consensus-building methodologies. The continued and rapid evolution of transplant medicine necessitates periodic reassessment of HLTx indications to ensure that patients most likely to benefit receive timely access to this life-saving procedure.
