Novel Relaxin Receptor RXFP1 Agonist AZD3427 in the Treatment of Heart Failure: A Phase 1a/b, First‐in‐Human, Randomized, Single‐Blind, Placebo‐Controlled Study
Authors: Kathleen Connolly, Richard George, Sami Omar, Elin Matsson, Magnus Åstrand, Magnus Althage, Daniel Pettersen, Esha Mohamed, Kelly Fang, Joao A C Lima, Mirjana Kujacic, Helena Ödesjö, Michelle Turton, Petra Johannesson, Anders Gabrielsen, Marcin Ufnal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure mortality remains high despite recent progress in pharmacological treatment. AZD3427 is a selective long‐acting analog of relaxin, a vasodilatory hormone with antifibrotic effects. We assessed the safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of AZD3427 in healthy volunteers and patients with heart failure on standard‐of‐care therapy.Methods and ResultsIn this first‐in‐human, phase 1a/b, randomized, single‐blind, placebo‐controlled study, healthy volunteers were randomized 6:2 to receive a single dose of AZD3427 or placebo by subcutaneous injection in 5 mixed‐ethnicity cohorts (5, 10, 30, 90, or 270 mg) and 1 Japanese‐descent cohort (270 mg), or by intravenous injection in 1 cohort (15 mg). After confirming safety and tolerability in healthy volunteers, 3 cohorts of patients with heart failure and left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40% and 3 cohorts with ejection fraction ≥41% were randomized 6:2 to receive 5 weekly doses of AZD3427 (5, 15, or 45 mg) or placebo by subcutaneous injection. In total, 56 healthy volunteers and 48 patients with heart failure were randomized. AZD3427 was well tolerated at all doses. After subcutaneous administration, AZD3427 was absorbed slowly, and exposure was approximately linear across the dose range. In patients with heart failure, AZD3427 terminal half‐life was 13 to 14 days and there were numerical increases in stroke volume and estimated glomerular filtration rate. No treatment‐emergent antidrug antibodies were detected.ConclusionsAZD3427 had favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profiles. Hemodynamic changes in patients with heart failure were consistent with the anticipated effects of a relaxin analog. These findings support further development of AZD3427 as a novel long‐term treatment for patients with heart failure.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT04630067.Keywords: AZD3427, HFmrEF, HFpEF, HFrEF, relaxin, RXFP1Subject Categories: Heart Failure

Heart failure is a complex and progressive medical condition that poses significant challenges in terms of mortality, morbidity, and financial burden. Contemporary standard‐of‐care treatment for heart failure encompasses beta blockers, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors, neprilysin inhibitors, and most recently, sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors. Even though these therapeutic strategies can enhance survival and improve exercise capacity, the incidence of heart failure and the associated mortality remain high. 1 , 2 The cause of heart failure is multifactorial and involves neurohormonal dysregulation, increased vascular resistance, reduced compliance of the vasculature and heart, and impaired perfusion of organs, including the kidneys. 3 Therefore, novel medications with innovative modes of action are needed to improve the management of heart failure and delay disease progression.

Relaxin is best known as a hormone produced and secreted by the corpus luteum during pregnancy, although it is also synthesized in the male reproductive system. Relaxin binds to the G protein‐coupled relaxin receptors RXFP1 (relaxin family peptide receptor 1) and RXFP2, with RXFP1 (LGR7) being the primary receptor. 4 , 5 Abundant preclinical data suggest that relaxin has vasodilatory, anti‐inflammatory, and antifibrotic effects that may be beneficial in the treatment of heart failure. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Several clinical studies investigating the effects of serelaxin, an analog of human relaxin with a short half‐life of 7 to 8 hours, have suggested a potentially beneficial pharmacodynamic profile in specific subpopulations of patients with heart failure. 12 , 13 , 14

In contrast to relaxin and serelaxin, AZD3427 is a long‐acting relaxin analog designed for long‐term weekly subcutaneous dosing. AZD3427 is a human recombinant fusion protein comprising the Fc portion of human IgG1 linked to a peptide sequence derived from human relaxin. Similar to endogenous human relaxin, AZD3427 is a selective agonist of RXFP1. 15

We assessed the safety, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity and explored the pharmacodynamic effects of AZD3427 in healthy volunteers and patients diagnosed with heart failure and established on standard‐of‐care therapy.

Methods

Overview and Objectives

This was a phase 1a/b, randomized, single‐blind, placebo‐controlled study, conducted from November 2020 to September 2022 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04630067). Investigators, participants, the study monitor, and all clinical staff were blinded to treatment assignment (except the pharmacist, clinical operations leader, and clinical research associate). The sponsor was unblinded throughout the study. Participants were randomized across 6 sites in the United States. The study was performed in 2 parts. Part A was a first‐in‐human, single ascending dose study in healthy volunteers. Part B was a multiple ascending dose study in patients with heart failure. The study was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration and relevant institutional review boards. Participants provided written informed consent before starting the study. The study followed the precepts of Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki.

The primary objective was to assess the safety and tolerability of single and multiple doses of AZD3427, including analysis of adverse events, clinical laboratory findings, vital signs, echocardiograms, and injection site reactions. Secondary objectives were to evaluate the pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity of single and multiple doses of AZD3427. Exploratory objectives were to evaluate the effect of AZD3427 on hemodynamic parameters and biomarkers in patients with heart failure. Inclusion of a cohort of patients of exclusively Japanese descent enabled evaluation of potential ethnic differences in pharmacokinetics and safety.

Participants

Part A: Inclusion Criteria for Healthy Volunteers

Eligible participants were healthy male and female volunteers of nonchildbearing potential, aged 18 to 50 years, weighing 55 to 100 kg, with a body mass index of 18 to 30 kg/m2.

Part B: Inclusion Criteria for Patients With Heart Failure

Eligible participants were male and female patients of nonchildbearing potential with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of stage C heart failure (New York Heart Association Class I to III) who had been on stable medical therapy for at least 12 weeks before screening and had documented measurements of either N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide levels greater than 15 pmol/L or brain natriuretic peptide levels greater than 10 pmol/L. Eligible participants were aged 18 to 75 years, weighing 55 to 136 kg, with a body mass index of 18 to 40 kg/m2.

Part A and B: Exclusion Criteria

Key exclusion criteria were a history of any clinically important disease or disorder, history of vascular and left ventricular aneurysms or dissections, history of drug or alcohol abuse, and previous exposure to AZD3427. Individuals with clinically significant abnormalities in clinical chemistry, hematology, coagulation, or urinalysis results were also excluded from the study, as were those with any clinically important illness, medical procedure, or trauma in the 4 weeks before enrolment.

Further inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Data S1.

Study Design

Part A: Single‐Ascending Doses in Healthy Volunteers

In Part A, healthy volunteers were sequentially randomized to 1 of 7 cohorts to receive a single dose of AZD3427 or placebo in a 6:2 ratio (Figure 1). Five cohorts of mixed ethnicity received single doses of AZD3427 5, 10, 30, 90, or 270 mg or placebo by subcutaneous injection. One cohort received a single intravenous dose of AZD3427 15 mg or placebo, and 1 cohort of exclusively Japanese descent (Japanese parents and grandparents) received a single subcutaneous dose of AZD3427 270 mg or placebo. Sentinel dosing in 2 participants was performed in each cohort, with at least 48 hours postdose observation at the study center. There were 8 follow‐up assessments (5 on‐site visits, 3 telephone calls), with the last visit taking place at least 50 days after the last dose (Figure S1).

D indicates day; EF, ejection fraction; FU, follow‐up; IV, intravenous; JPN, Japanese descent; and SC, subcutaneous.

Part B: Multiple‐Ascending Doses in Patients With Heart Failure

Part B was initiated according to the safety and tolerability profile observed in Part A. In Part B, patients with heart failure were sequentially randomized to 1 of 6 cohorts to receive multiple doses of AZD3427 or placebo in a 6:2 ratio (Figure 1). Of these, 3 cohorts comprised patients with a previous diagnosis of heart failure with left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) ≤40%; and 2 cohorts comprised patients with a previous diagnosis of heart failure with EF ≥41%. Randomization cohort was based on echocardiography assessed by investigators at screening.

Participants were randomized to receive either AZD3427 5, 15, or 45 mg or placebo by subcutaneous injection once weekly for 5 weeks (5 doses in total). Participants resided at the study center until 48 hours after the first dose and returned for 4 additional weekly doses (7 days apart). There were 7 follow‐up assessments (4 on‐site visits, 3 telephone calls), with the last visit taking place at least 50 days after the last dose (Figure S1).

Outcomes

Safety and Tolerability

All participants underwent physical examination, assessment of vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature), electrocardiography, and laboratory safety assessments, including levels of antidrug antibodies at prespecified time points throughout the study. Telemetry was performed for 4 to 6 hours on day −1 and from 30 minutes before to 48 hours after the first dose. Serious adverse events were recorded from the time of informed consent and adverse events were recorded from the time of the first dose. Adverse events were recorded at all study visits, including follow‐up visits, and intensity was graded as mild, moderate, or severe by the blinded site investigators. Causality was assessed by the blinded site investigators. Adverse events were summarized using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities vocabulary.

Pharmacokinetics

Serum AZD3427 concentration was used to establish the pharmacokinetic profile of AZD3427. In Part A, serum samples were collected predose and 4 and 12 hours postdose on study day 1, then on days 2, 3, 5, 8, 15, 29, and 50 (Figure S1). In the intravenous AZD3427 cohort, an additional sample was collected within 10 minutes of the end of injection on day 1. Serum samples on day 2 onwards were taken at approximately the same time of day as AZD3427 dosing on day 1. In Part B, serum samples were collected predose and 12, 24, and 36 hours postdose on study day 1, then predose on days 8, 15, 22, and 29, then in the follow‐up period on days 32, 57, 71, and 78 (Figure S1). Pharmacokinetic end points included observed maximum serum concentration (Cmax), area under the serum concentration–time curve, time to reach maximum serum concentration, and terminal half‐life.

Pharmacodynamics (Exploratory Objectives)

In Part B, the effects of AZD3427 on hemodynamic parameters and biomarker levels in patients with heart failure were analyzed. Echocardiographic examinations were performed predose (baseline), then at day 32 (after last dose) and day 71 (end of follow‐up). Changes from baseline in hemodynamic parameters (cardiac output, stroke volume, blood pressure) and levels of blood biomarkers relevant to heart failure and AZD3427 were assessed at prespecified time points. For pharmacodynamic end points, echocardiography metrics were assessed by blinded staff at the core laboratory (Johns Hopkins Hospital, Baltimore, MD).

Statistical Analysis

Results were analyzed and presented separately for Part A and Part B. The study was not statistically powered to assess differences in pharmacodynamic parameters. Detailed statistical methods can be found in Data S1.

In Part A, data from participants receiving AZD3427 by subcutaneous injection were analyzed and presented by cohort (AZD3427 5, 10, 30, 90, and 270 mg, and AZD3427 270 mg in participants of Japanese descent) with a pooled placebo group comprising all participants receiving placebo in these cohorts. The Japanese descent cohort was also analyzed separately by treatment group (AZD3427 270 mg and placebo). The AZD3427 intravenous injection cohort was analyzed separately by treatment group (AZD3427 15 mg and placebo).

In Part B, the EF ≤40% and EF ≥41% groups were analyzed separately. In both groups, the analysis was presented by cohort (AZD3427 5, 15, or 45 mg) with a pooled placebo group for EF ≤40% and EF ≥41%, respectively, comprising all participants receiving placebo in these cohorts.

Results

Part A: Single‐Ascending Doses in Healthy Volunteers

Baseline Demographics and Participant Characteristics

Fifty‐six healthy volunteers were enrolled and randomized in Part A, of whom 48 were randomized to receive single doses of AZD3427 or placebo by subcutaneous injection and 8 were randomized to receive a single dose of AZD3427 or placebo by intravenous injection. The majority of healthy volunteers were men, aged 22 to 50 years, and did not have Hispanic or Latino ethnicity (Table S1). Healthy volunteers who received subcutaneous AZD3427 had a median age of 31.0 years in the AZD3427 group and 37.0 years in the pooled placebo group. Healthy volunteers who received intravenous AZD3427 had a median age of 33.0 years. There were no notable differences in height or body mass index among groups (Table S1).

Four healthy volunteers had at least 1 protocol deviation (Table S2). All healthy volunteers completed the study and were included in the safety analysis and the pharmacokinetic analysis. No healthy volunteers withdrew from the study or were lost to follow‐up.

Safety and Tolerability

In Part A, no prespecified stopping criteria were met and there were no serious adverse events, deaths, or adverse events leading to withdrawal from the study (Table 1). In the subcutaneous injection cohorts (AZD3427 5, 10, 30, 90, or 270 mg, or placebo), 9 of 36 healthy volunteers (25.0%) in the pooled AZD3427 group and 2 of 12 (16.7%) in the pooled placebo group experienced at least 1 adverse event (Table 1). In the Japanese‐descent cohort (AZD3427 270 mg or placebo by subcutaneous injection), no adverse events were reported for AZD3427 or placebo. In the intravenous injection cohort (AZD3427 15 mg or placebo), adverse events were reported in 3 of 6 healthy volunteers (50.0%) receiving AZD3427 and in none receiving placebo.

Pharmacokinetics

After single subcutaneous administration in healthy volunteers, AZD3427 was slowly absorbed across the dose range (10–270 mg) with a median time to Cmax ranging from 72 to 96 hours and an absolute bioavailability of approximately 66%. After Cmax was reached, AZD3427 serum concentration declined in a multiphasic manner. Geometric mean terminal half‐life ranged from 162 to 214 hours (approximately 7–9 days) across the 30 to 270 mg dose range. According to the area under the serum concentration–time curve and Cmax, AZD3427 exposure after subcutaneous administration was approximately dose‐proportional across the dose range (Figure 2A; Table S3).

Serum concentration of AZD3427 from day 32 was not included due to deviations between actual and nominal sampling time. EF indicates ejection fraction; IV, intravenous; JPN, Japanese descent; and SC, subcutaneous.

Exposure after subcutaneous administration was similar between the AZD3427 270 mg mixed‐descent cohort and the AZD3427 270 mg Japanese‐descent cohort, according to the area under the serum concentration–time curve. However, Cmax was 1.4‐fold higher in the mixed‐descent cohort than in Japanese‐descent cohort (Table S4).

After single intravenous administration of AZD3427 15 mg in healthy volunteers, AZD3427 serum concentration declined in a multiphasic manner after reaching Cmax. The geometric mean terminal half‐life was 117 hours (approximately 5 days) (Figure 2A).

Part B: Multiple‐Ascending Doses in Patients With Heart Failure

Baseline Demographics and Patient Characteristics

Forty‐eight patients (24 with EF ≤40%; 24 with EF ≥41%) were enrolled in Part B and randomized to receive AZD3427 5, 15, or 45 mg, or placebo once weekly by subcutaneous injection. Most patients with EF ≤40% were men: 17 of 18 (94.4%) in the pooled AZD3427 group and 6 of 6 (100%) in the pooled placebo group (Table 2). In the EF ≥41% group, the proportion of male patients was lower than in the EF ≤40% group: 8 of 18 (44.4%) in the pooled AZD3427 group and 4 of 6 (66.6%) in the pooled placebo group.

In the EF ≤40% group, patients had a median age of 59.0 years, with a median weight of 99.6 kg in the pooled AZD3427 group and a median age of 65.0 years and a median weight of 90.0 kg in the pooled placebo group (Table 2). In the EF ≥41% group, patients had a median age of 63.0 years, with a median weight of 92.9 kg in the pooled AZD3427 group and a median age of 65.5 years and a median weight of 85.3 kg in the pooled placebo group (Table 2).

Twelve patients had at least 1 protocol deviation (Table S2). All patients were included in the safety analysis and pharmacokinetic analysis. Forty‐seven of the enrolled patients completed the study and follow‐up visits. One patient with EF ≤40% in the AZD3427 45 mg cohort withdrew from the study owing to an adverse event but completed the follow‐up visit.

Safety and Tolerability

In Part B, serious adverse events were reported in 2 patients with EF ≤40% in the AZD3427 5 mg cohort, 1 with back pain of moderate intensity, and 1 with COVID‐19 of moderate intensity. Neither of these serious adverse events were considered to be related to AZD3427 by the investigator. One patient with EF ≤40% in the AZD3427 45 mg cohort had an adverse event that led to discontinuation (SARS‐CoV‐2 test positive). There were no deaths. Overall, among patients with EF ≤40%, 11 of 18 (61.1%) in the pooled AZD3427 group and 2 of 6 (33.3%) in the pooled placebo group experienced at least 1 adverse event. Among patients with EF ≥41%, 14 of 18 (77.8%) in the pooled AZD3427 group and 4 of 6 (66.7%) in the pooled placebo group experienced at least 1 adverse event (Table 1).

In total, 8 adverse events of hypotension were reported in 6 patients. Hypotension was the most frequently reported adverse event in patients with EF ≥41%, occurring in 3 patients receiving AZD3427 5 mg and 2 patients receiving AZD3427 15 mg. All adverse events of hypotension in patients with an EF ≥41% were asymptomatic and of mild intensity. Four events lasted <1 hour, 2 lasted between 1 and 2 hours, and 1 lasted 12 hours. All were judged as possibly related to AZD3427 by the investigator. One adverse event of hypotension was reported in a patient with EF ≤40% in the AZD3427 45 mg cohort. This was asymptomatic, of mild intensity, and judged as not related to AZD3427 by the investigator. None of these adverse events led to discontinuation of AZD3427.

Two participants had injection site reactions, 1 patient with EF ≥41% in the AZD3427 15 mg cohort and 1 with EF ≤40% in the AZD3427 45 mg cohort, both of mild intensity. There were mild, transient reductions in hematocrit relative to placebo in both the EF ≤40% and EF ≥41% groups (Figure S2A). No other clinically relevant findings were observed for other laboratory parameters. No clinically relevant trends were observed for vital signs, physical examinations, and ECGs. No treatment‐emergent antidrug antibodies were reported.

Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics were similar among patients with EF ≤40% and those with EF ≥41% (Figure 2B; Table S5). Serum concentrations of AZD3427 declined in a multiphasic manner after reaching Cmax. The geometric mean terminal half‐life following once weekly administration of AZD3427 15 and 45 mg ranged from 315 to 325 hours (approximately 13–14 days) after the last dose (Table S5). AZD3427 exposure increased in an approximately dose‐proportional manner, with a 3‐fold increase in dose from AZD3427 15 to 45 mg and a 2.3‐ to 3.9‐fold increase in geometric mean Cmax. Exposure to AZD3427 was similar among patients with EF ≤40% and those with EF ≥41% (Figure 2B; Table S5).

Exploratory Pharmacodynamics

In patients with EF ≤40%, there were no notable differences in changes from baseline in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate between AZD3427 and placebo (Figure 3A through 3C). There were tendencies toward increases from baseline in stroke volume and cardiac output (Figure 4A and 4B) and more pronounced increases in estimated glomerular filtration rate and plasma renin levels in patients with EF ≤40% receiving AZD3427 (Figure 4C; Figure S2B).

In patients with EF ≥41%, there were also no notable differences in changes from baseline in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate between AZD3427 and placebo (Figure 3A through 3C). There were tendencies toward increases from baseline in stroke volume, cardiac output, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and plasma renin levels in patients with EF ≥41% receiving AZD3427 (Figure 4A through 4C; Figure S2B). These changes appeared more modest than those in patients with EF ≤40%.

Discussion

In this first‐in‐human phase 1 study in both healthy volunteers and patients with heart failure, AZD3427 had favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profiles. In addition, the observed changes in pharmacodynamic parameters in patients with heart failure were consistent with the anticipated effects of a relaxin analog. Relaxin is involved in cardiovascular and renal system adaptation during pregnancy. 6 AZD3427 is a long‐acting agonist of the RXFP1 receptor designed to replicate the potentially therapeutic effects of relaxin, such as vasodilation and antifibrotic action, in patients with heart failure. 7 , 8 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Previous clinical investigations of recombinant human relaxin in patients with heart failure yielded mixed results. 12 , 20 , 21 Despite promising results in earlier studies, 12 , 20 the phase 3 RELAX‐AHF‐2 (Efficacy, Safety and Tolerability of Serelaxin When Added to Standard Therapy in Acute Heart Failure) trial involving 6600 hospitalized patients with acute heart failure did not reveal any substantial clinical benefit of short‐term administration of recombinant relaxin (serelaxin). Patients who received a 48‐hour infusion of serelaxin did not experience any significant reduction in the incidence of death from cardiovascular causes or worsening of heart failure after 180 days follow‐up, compared with placebo. 21 The absence of clinical benefit in RELAX‐AHF‐2 may be attributable to the short duration of treatment with a short‐acting compound. Although short‐term treatment may produce immediate vasodilatory effects such as reduced afterload, increased cardiac output, and improved organ perfusion, it is unlikely to drive sustained favorable hemodynamic effects over time. Additionally, short‐term benefits may be offset by the potential negative impact of “rebound” effects associated with a short‐acting relaxin analog. 22 Finally, short‐duration treatment does not harness the antifibrotic effects of relaxin signaling, which hold promise in ameliorating cardiovascular disease‐related remodeling of the heart and vasculature.

In contrast to serelaxin, AZD3427 is a long‐acting RFXP1 agonist designed for long‐term treatment. In the present study, the terminal half‐life of AZD3427 was 13 to 14 days after the last dose in patients with heart failure, with exposure increasing approximately proportionally with dose across the range studied. A limitation of the study was the infrequent pharmacokinetic sampling in Part B, which may have affected the accuracy of the parameter estimates, especially in the follow‐up period. The observed pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of AZD3427 suggest that it may be possible to lower the dosing frequency to less than once weekly.

In the current study, AZD3427 was generally well tolerated, with no major safety concerns or treatment‐emergent antidrug antibodies. The most frequently reported adverse event was hypotension. All reported hypotension adverse events were mild and asymptomatic and did not result in discontinuation of AZD3427. Furthermore, no adverse events of hypotension that were judged as related to AZD3427 by the investigator were reported in the AZD3427 highest‐dose cohorts. Of the 6 participants who experienced hypotension, 5 had heart failure with EF ≥41% and 1 had heart failure with EF ≤40%. There were differences in demographics between these patient groups, with a considerably higher proportion of female participants among patients with heart failure and EF ≥41% than among those with EF ≤40%. Further investigation in a larger study is needed to determine whether this or other demographic factors affect the frequency of adverse events of hypotension.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the safety and pharmacokinetics of AZD3427. It was not powered to assess differences in pharmacodynamic parameters or dose–response relationships. Nevertheless, in participants with heart failure, there were trends toward increased stroke volume, cardiac output, and estimated glomerular filtration rate without alterations in heart rate or blood pressure, for AZD3427 compared with placebo. Given the small sample size and large variability in pharmacodynamic parameters, it is challenging to make conclusions about the dose–response relationship. However, the 15 mg dose appeared to have the greatest effects on stroke volume, cardiac output, and estimated glomerular filtration rate in the EF ≤40% group, whereas no such effects were observed in the EF ≥41% group. Taken together, these exploratory analyses of prespecified pharmacodynamic end points suggest that AZD3427 may increase renal blood flow and cardiac output, driven primarily by an increase in stroke volume. This finding is consistent with the available preclinical and clinical data regarding the hemodynamic effects of relaxin signaling.

Other changes observed with AZD3427 included elevated renin levels. Renin levels are known to increase in response to stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system, decreased blood pressure, and decreased intraglomerular pressure owing to the tubuloglomerular feedback mechanism, all of which may result from vasodilation mediated by AZD3427. 23 , 24 No AZD3427‐related adverse events related to renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system stimulation or fluid homeostasis, such as lung congestion, peripheral edema, or changes in body weight, were reported during the treatment. However, a mild drop in hematocrit was observed, which warrants further investigation to assess the significance of potential AZD3427‐induced renin release.

Conclusions

In conclusion, AZD3427 was generally safe and well tolerated in healthy volunteers and patients with heart failure. The findings of the present study support further evaluation of AZD3427 as a novel long‐term treatment for heart failure.
