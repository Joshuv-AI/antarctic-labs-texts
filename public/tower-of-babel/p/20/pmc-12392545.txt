Oncology nurses’ compliance with standard precautions: a multi-method study
Authors: Gul Hatice Tarakcioglu Celik, Ecem Ozdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo assess oncology nurses’ compliance with standard precautions (SPs) and explore factors influencing adherence to inform targeted interventions in infection prevention.DesignA multi-method methodology was used.MethodsIn the quantitative phase, 232 nurses from two oncology hospitals completed the Compliance with Standard Precautions Scale (CSPS) and a demographic questionnaire. In the qualitative phase, structured observations and semi-structured interviews were conducted with 14 purposively selected nurses.ResultsThe average SP compliance score was moderate (73.9%). Higher compliance was observed in waste disposal (86.2%), while lower rates were noted for hand hygiene (67.2%) and sharps disposal (63.2%). Age, professional experience, and recent infection control training positively correlated with compliance. A higher patient load was associated with lower adherence. Multiple regression analysis showed that both nurse age and daily patient load were significant predictors of compliance scores (F = 6.714, p < 0.01). Older age was associated with higher compliance, while a heavier patient load predicted lower adherence. Qualitative findings revealed key barriers, including time constraints, limited resources, and staffing shortages. Nurses emphasized that consistent training, managerial support, and supervision by infection control personnel facilitated better compliance.ConclusionsOncology nurses moderately adhere to SPs, with critical gaps in high-risk areas. Addressing institutional and workload-related barriers is essential for improving SP practices.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03788-1.Keywords: Compliance factors, Infection prevention and control, Oncology nursing, Standard precautions

Background

Standard precautions (SPs) are essential for preventing and controlling healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), especially in oncology settings where patients are more vulnerable due to illness or treatment. These precautions, including hand hygiene, personal protective equipment (PPE) use, equipment decontamination, safe sharps handling, and waste management, have been a cornerstone of infection control since the 1980s [1, 2]. Despite their proven effectiveness, full compliance with SPs remains challenging, even during crises like the COVID-19 pandemic, which resulted in over 7 million deaths worldwide [3].

Nurses have the most frequent and prolonged contact with patients and are central to preventing HAIs. However, research shows that although more than 80% of nurses and healthcare professionals demonstrate good knowledge of SPs, a significant proportion (12.2%-55.1%) still report poor to moderate understanding [4–6]. Compliance rates also vary widely, ranging from 53.5% to 76.68% among nurses and healthcare workers, with similar patterns observed in nursing students [7–9]. Factors influencing compliance include training, access to PPE, and workload pressures [10, 11].

Understanding the factors that influence oncology nurses’ compliance with SPs is essential to improving infection prevention and patient safety. This study aims to examine oncology nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding SPs, identify barriers and facilitators to adherence, and inform the development of targeted strategies to enhance compliance.

Methods

Design

This study utilized a multi-method design to assess oncology nurses’ compliance with SPs and identify factors influencing adherence. The research was conducted in two phases:

Quantitative phase

A cross-sectional survey was administered to measure self-reported compliance with SPs and to identify influencing factors.

Qualitative phase

Observational assessments and semi-structured interviews were conducted to explore nurses’ experiences and perceived barriers to SP adherence.

Study setting and sampling

The study was conducted at a specialized oncology training and research hospital and a university hospital providing high-complexity cancer care. The target population consisted of 420 oncology nurses. Using a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, a sample size of 201 nurses was calculated. Ultimately, 232 nurses with at least six months of bedside care experience participated in the quantitative phase. For the qualitative phase, purposive sampling was employed based on variation in years of oncology experience and work shifts. Data saturation was determined when no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews, which was achieved after the 14th interview, indicating sufficient depth and redundancy of the data.

Data collection instruments

Demographic and professional characteristics form

A researcher-developed form was used to collect demographic data (e.g., age, gender, education level) and professional details (e.g., work history, infection prevention and control training, weekly working hours, patient load).

Compliance with standard precautions scale (CSPS)

The CSPS was originally developed by Lam [12], with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.73. The Turkish adaptation of the scale was validated by Samur et al. [13], demonstrating good reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.71. The Turkish version of the CSPS consists of 20 items rated on a four-point Likert scale. Items 2, 4, 6, and 15 are reverse-scored. In scoring, one point is assigned for a response of “always” to positively worded items and for “never” on reverse-scored items; all other responses receive zero points. The total possible score ranges from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater compliance. In the present study, the scale showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.714).

Standard precautions observation checklist

Adapted from the CDC’s Infection Control Assessment and Response (ICAR) Tool [14], this checklist evaluated adherence in three areas: Hand hygiene, PPE use, and waste management (Suppl. 1). Following CDC recommendations, each nurse was observed in at least three patient rooms.

Semi-structured interview guide

Based on relevant literature and validated by experts (an oncology nurse, an infection control nurse, and a nursing academic), the interview guide explored nurses’ experiences, perceived barriers, and facilitators related to SP compliance (Suppl. 1).

Data collection procedures

Quantitative data collection

Quantitative data were collected from November 2023 to January 2024. During this period, all participating nurses completed two key instruments: The Demographic and Professional Characteristics Form and the CSPS. The demographic form collected information on the participants’ age, years of experience, educational background, and other professional characteristics. The CSPS, a validated tool, assessed nurses’ compliance with SPs.

The nurses completed both forms during their scheduled work hours, ensuring they had the time and context to provide accurate and reflective responses. The data was collected during regular nursing shifts to minimize disruption to routine care, and nurses were encouraged to provide honest and thorough responses-the quantitative data collection aimed to gather broad, measurable information about compliance levels across the sample population.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data collection took place from January to February 2024 and consisted of two primary methods: Structured observations and face-to-face interviews. These methods were used to gather in-depth insights into the nursing practices and factors influencing compliance with SPs.

Structured Observations: Between January and February 2024, structured observations were carried out during day shifts (08:00–16:00) to assess the actual SP practices of nurses in clinical settings. Observations were conducted systematically, focusing on three patient rooms per nurse per shift. The observers used a predefined checklist to record adherence to SP protocols such as hand hygiene, use of PPE, and other infection prevention measures. Field notes were taken throughout the observation period to document any additional contextual information that could support the analysis of observed practices, such as environmental factors or staff interactions that may have influenced compliance.

Face-to-Face interviews

Following the structured observations, face-to-face interviews were conducted with the observed nurses to capture their perspectives on the observed SP practices. The interviews explored factors influencing their adherence to SPs, challenges, and the underlying reasons behind their behaviors. Semi-structured interview guides facilitated open-ended discussions, allowing nurses to share their insights freely.

The interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis. This data collection method provided an opportunity to gather qualitative insights that complemented the observational data, offering a deeper understanding of the attitudes, beliefs, and experiences that shape nurses’ compliance with SPs.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and SP compliance scores. Scale reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Group differences were evaluated using t-tests or ANOVA, with post hoc Tukey tests as appropriate. Pearson’s correlation coefficient assessed relationships between variables. A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine the effects of nurses’ age, years of experience, infection control training, and daily patient load on SP compliance scores. The model was tested using the Forward Method and found to be significant. It did not have a multicollinearity problem (VIF > 10) or an autocorrelation problem (Durbin Watson = 1.894).

Qualitative analysis

Inductive content analysis was employed for qualitative data. Participants verified transcripts, read them multiple times, and independently coded them by researchers. Codes were grouped into themes through consensus, and direct quotes illustrated findings.

Trustworthiness

In qualitative research, trustworthiness is established through four key dimensions: Credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [15]. To ensure credibility, interviews were conducted directly by the researcher, and the transcripts, coding framework, and findings were confirmed with participants through member checking and consensus discussions. Transferability was supported by clearly describing the sampling strategy and participant characteristics within the methodology and results sections. Dependability and confirmability were reinforced by using evidence-based interview guides informed by existing literature on factors influencing oncology nurses’ compliance with SPs, and by employing consistent data collection methods across all interviews.

All interviews were conducted in Turkish. With participants’ consent, transcripts were translated into English. The translated texts were reviewed for accuracy and consistency by an associate professor in nursing who is fluent in both Turkish and English.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval

was obtained from the Hacettepe University Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (Approval Date: 12.10.2023; Approval ID: SBA 23/187). The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional permissions were secured, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Predictors of compliance with standard precautions

Multiple regression analysis identified two significant predictors of nurses’ compliance with standard precautions: age and daily patient load (F = 6.714, p = 0.001; adjusted R² = 0.047). The model explained approximately 4.7% of the variance in compliance scores. Each one-year increase in age was associated with a 0.065-point increase in the compliance score (B = 0.065, β = 0.151, p = 0.027). In contrast, each additional patient cared for per day was associated with a 0.064-point decrease in the compliance score (B = -0.064, β = -0.139, p = 0.042) (Table 5).

Theme diagram

Theme 1: Compliance with SPs

Compliance with SPs emerged as a central focus of the qualitative phase. It was further divided into the sub-themes of “Identifying Compliance with SPs,” “Factors Increasing Compliance Behavior,” and “Factors Decreasing Compliance Behavior.”

Subtheme: Identifying compliance with SPs

Oncology nurses described compliance with SPs as encompassing a range of protective practices to ensure patient safety and minimize HAIs. The nurses highlighted key practices such as using gloves, masks, goggles, and aprons, as well as hand hygiene, including hand washing and hand sanitizers.

One nurse emphasized the broad scope of compliance: “I can think of everything, from patient safety to the timing of drug dose administration to hand disinfection, the health of the nurse, and the cleaning staff’s proper work; it covers all of them. I mean, because it is not enough for only one person to work properly or pay attention to something in the hospital” (P3). Another nurse mentioned, “When I think of standard precautions, I think of hand washing. It is necessary to wear gloves. Wear gloves when touching the patient and their surroundings, and wash your hands beforehand. I can give examples of wearing a mask, apron, protective goggles” (P11).

These responses reflect a holistic understanding of SPs, with oncology nurses acknowledging their responsibility for their safety and patients’ safety through various preventive measures. Some nurses also mentioned the importance of more complex aspects, such as calculating drug dosages carefully to ensure proper patient care: “Practices to prevent infection, such as hand washing, using a mask, and hand disinfection before entering and leaving the patient. We use antiseptic soaps for care. We pay attention to the patient’s oral hygiene” (P12).

Subtheme: Factors increasing compliance behavior

Oncology nurses identified several key factors that enhance their adherence to SPs, the most prominent being proper control measures, availability of sufficient resources, and adequate staffing levels. A nurse highlighted the need for material supplies: “Apart from that, there should be no problems in the supplies if we comply with the precautions; disinfectant, soap, towel, gloves, and all other materials should be available. Maybe training should be held regularly, we should not forget” (P5).

The importance of a supportive work environment was also emphasized. One nurse pointed out the role of managerial support and supervision: “The equipment must be complete. Secondly, I think the management should appreciate us a little more. Nurses are always going over the deficiencies. I think harmony would increase if more things would increase our enthusiasm” (P10). Moreover, the role of infection control nurses and the presence of senior staff during shifts also increased nurses’ attention to SPs, as one participant mentioned: “Obviously, people pay more attention when the infection nurse or the nurse in charge comes. There is a tendency to take the easy way out, but it would be good if a senior nurse observed the night shifts, for example. It affects people a little bit, that is, supervision. The number of nurses should be increased” (P14).

Subtheme: Factors decreasing compliance behavior

Several challenges were cited as barriers to compliance, primarily focusing on workload intensity, lack of materials, and insufficient staff training. Nurses consistently noted the impact of having a high patient load on their ability to maintain proper precautions. One nurse stated, “The number of patients and intensive work may be the things that affect the SPs the most. The more patients we come into contact with, the more intense the environment we work in, and the more we may ignore SPs, or rather, we may neglect them” (P5).

Another nurse observed that the competency of support staff influences the quality of care and infection prevention, explaining: “It can be auxiliary staff, if they do not have enough knowledge about complying with this, of course, in terms of preventing infection… even if you comply with this, if the staff next to you does not know this, it affects in this way” (P7). The lack of material resources, such as inadequate PPE and disinfectants, also posed a significant barrier to optimal practice: “Intensity, general condition of the patient, and material” (P12).

Additionally, the emotional and physical strain from demanding work hours and the challenges of managing patients with highly infectious diseases were mentioned as factors reducing adherence to SPs. As one nurse highlighted, “If the patient has a transmission, the cause of the transmission affects compliance. For example, we pay more attention to the patient with COVID-19 and the severity of infectiousness, such as MRSA and Acinetobacter, which are highly contagious” (P12).

Theme 2: Nursing practices

The second theme, Nursing Practices, centered on nurses’ strategies for preventing and controlling infection. These strategies were categorized under the sub-theme of “Practices for IPC.”

Subtheme: Practices for IPC

The nurses described daily IPC practices, including visitor restrictions, rigorous hand hygiene protocols, patient education, compliance with aseptic techniques, and using PPE consistently. A nurse noted, “There is in-service training to prevent and control infections, but when I think about my participation, it has been a while. Our infection control nurse comes in between; she also travels around. When there is a transmission in a patient, she takes care of it” (P1). These practices highlight the importance of infection control nurses in reinforcing protocols and providing timely guidance during outbreaks.

Many nurses emphasized the importance of individualized care and patient hygiene practices. One nurse explained: “I try to do oral care as much as possible. They are tiring because the patients are weighty, their position, etc., tires you, but we try to do them as much as possible. Every day, there is a change of sheets, gowns, and wiping baths. Nappy change happens twice a day anyway” (P3). Another nurse described the importance of educating patients and their families: “Visitor restriction, we put hand sanitizers at the entrances to patient rooms, and there are gloves. Hand washing: Use disinfectant when hand washing is not available. Educating the patient. We explain the importance of hand hygiene, and we give booklets, which we also apply” (P5).

These responses emphasize oncology nurses’ multi-faceted approach to infection control, where procedural protocols and personalized patient care are key components of their practices.

Discussion

This study explored oncology nurses’ compliance with SPs through quantitative and qualitative methods. We’ll discuss the findings from each phase separately.

Quantitative data discussion

Oncology nurses in this study demonstrated a moderate level of compliance with SPs, suggesting a generally consistent yet not optimal adherence to established infection control protocols. This finding holds particular importance in oncology care, where patients with cancer face heightened susceptibility to HAIs due to immunosuppressive treatments [16]. Although adherence to SPs is critical, research consistently shows that compliance among healthcare professionals remains suboptimal-often reported at or below 70% [12, 17]. Our findings align with previous studies reporting moderate to high, yet insufficient, levels of compliance among nurses and healthcare workers (HCWs) [4, 7–9, 18–21].

Among the SP components, hand hygiene emerged as a notable area for improvement, particularly due to the inconsistent use of alcohol-based hand rubs. Non-compliance with hand hygiene presents serious risks for patients with cancer, mainly because their weakened immune systems increase their vulnerability to HAIs. HCWs’ contaminated hands are a common way pathogens, such as multidrug-resistant organisms, are transmitte. This finding reflects patterns observed in previous studies, which report that time constraints and high workloads frequently hinder proper hand hygiene practices [19, 22, 23]. Maintaining high hand hygiene compliance is essential to prevent infections in cancer care settings, where patients are especially vulnerable. Reinforcing adherence in this area is especially critical, as strong compliance with IPC measures—such as hand hygiene—has been shown to substantially reduce HAI incidence, hospital stays, and infection-related morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [24]. These findings reinforce the continued need for targeted education, supportive organizational systems, and behaviorally informed strategies to ensure consistent adherence to SPs. In oncology settings, where infection risks carry particularly serious consequences, reinforcing these practices is essential to safeguard both patient and staff health.

Additionally, while oncology nurses reported good compliance with some aspects of sharps disposal, notable gaps were identified in related practices. For instance, several nurses did not consistently follow guidelines regarding needle recapping or ensuring sharps containers were appropriately filled. This suggests that although some aspects of sharps disposal are well-executed, there is still significant room for improvement in specific behaviors. This variation is concerning and points to a need for more focused interventions. Similar findings have been documented in earlier research [7, 19, 21, 25]. Proper sharps disposal in oncology environments is crucial due to the high infection and injury risks. These dangers can be reduced by employing safety-engineered devices, providing comprehensive training, and considering the use of single-use containers to limit contamination and pathogen transmission. Continuous training and strict adherence to safety protocols are essential [26, 27]. Conducting regular audits and implementing action plans to enhance sharps management practices can reduce risks and are vital steps toward creating a safer environment for HCWs and patients.

The study also found that nurses who had received specific training in infection control practices reported higher compliance with SPs, consistent with previous research [4, 22, 28]. Importantly, our regression analysis confirmed that both age and patient load were statistically significant predictors of SP compliance. Older nurses demonstrated higher adherence (β = 0.151, p = 0.027), whereas those caring for a larger number of patients per day showed lower compliance (β = -0.139, p = 0.042). Although the model accounted for a limited proportion of variance (adjusted R² = 0.047), these results are in line with existing literature suggesting that increased workload can hinder adherence, while greater professional experience may promote more consistent infection control practices [19, 29, 30]. Future research may involve refining this model or testing alternative models to investigate other factors that affect compliance.

Oncology nurses face multifaceted challenges in implementing IPC practices, including environmental limitations, organizational constraints, and individual-level barriers. Effectively addressing these issues requires targeted strategies such as ongoing education, equitable resource allocation, and the development of clear, actionable policies. Enhancing institutional support systems and strengthening the underlying infrastructure are also critical steps toward improving the consistency and effectiveness of IPC measures. Taken together, these interventions can significantly contribute to safer care environments and better patient outcomes in oncology settings.

Qualitative data discussion

The qualitative data revealed that oncology nurses clearly recognize the importance of SPs, including hand hygiene and the use of PPE, in preventing HAIs [28]. However, several barriers to full compliance were identified, including limited institutional resources, heavy workloads, and time constraints. These challenges are consistent with previous studies and highlight the need for stronger institutional support to help nurses maintain adherence [17]. To address these issues, healthcare organizations must prioritize the development and implementation of comprehensive IPC policies tailored to the specific needs of oncology care settings [28].

Nurses emphasized that continuous education and organizational backing were essential for improving compliance with SPs. Those who had participated in regular training sessions reported stronger knowledge and better adherence, reinforcing findings from the broader literature that underscore the role of ongoing professional development in fostering consistent practice [21, 29]. Additionally, participants pointed out that challenges related to equipment availability, staffing levels, and emergency response situations could hinder compliance with SP protocols [30, 31]. These training programs need to emphasize the significance of consistently applying IPC procedures to safeguard patients and HCWs. It is also important to regularly conduct audits and updates to ensure the training remains effective and aligned with the latest guidelines.

While most nurses acknowledge the critical importance of SPs, non-compliance is often driven by factors beyond their direct control—most notably organizational constraints and resource shortages. These findings are consistent with prior research identifying inadequate staffing, limited supplies, and insufficient institutional support as persistent barriers to full adherence [18, 29, 32, 33]. Addressing these challenges requires more than individual behavior change; it demands system-level interventions. Strategies should focus on enhancing SP knowledge, delivering regular and practical training, and fostering a supportive work environment that empowers nurses to uphold infection prevention standards. In addition, continuous assessment and tailored interventions are essential to sustain improvements in compliance, strengthen a culture of accountability, and reinforce the critical role of SPs in reducing HAIs. Managers in oncology settings are responsible for fostering a supportive environment that promotes the consistent, long-term use of SPs [17].

Limitations

Like all studies, ours has a few limitations to consider. First, because some of our data were self-reported, there is a risk that participants may have overestimated their compliance with SPs, whether consciously or unconsciously. During the observation phase, we also acknowledge the potential influence of the Hawthorne effect, in which participants modify their behavior because they are aware of being observed [34]. Although efforts were made to minimize this effect by keeping observations as unobtrusive as possible, the presence of an observer may still have influenced nurse behavior. While the nurse population we conducted the qualitative interviews with was selected from volunteers, no ratings were made for their SP compliance scores. The fact that these participants generally demonstrated high adherence revealed similar underlying causes of non-compliance—namely, factors beyond their control, such as institutional barriers—during in-depth qualitative analysis.

Moreover, the study was conducted in only two hospitals in a single city, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other oncology care settings. While we achieved data saturation with 14 nurses in the qualitative phase, the relatively small and purposive sample may have excluded alternative perspectives.

A further limitation relates to our regression analysis: although it identified age and patient load as statistically significant predictors of SP compliance, the model explained only a small portion of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.047). This suggests that other unmeasured factors such as organizational culture, individual motivation, or team dynamics may also play important roles in influencing adherence.

Conclusion

HAIs are a significant issue worldwide, affecting not only patients but also healthcare workers, institutions, and society as a whole. SPs are critical to preventing these infections, especially in oncology, where patients are particularly vulnerable.

While oncology nurses in our study demonstrated moderate to high compliance with SPs, key areas such as hand hygiene and sharps disposal remain in need of improvement. The findings underscore the critical role of continuous education and practical training in supporting healthcare workers’ adherence to SP protocols. Equally important is the need to address systemic factors—such as organizational culture, resource constraints, and excessive workload—that can undermine compliance efforts. Our regression findings further support this, indicating that nurse age and daily patient load are significant predictors of compliance with standard precautions. These measurable factors can inform tailored interventions and staffing models to support infection control.

Implementing targeted strategies that integrate education, adequate resourcing, and supportive institutional policies is essential for strengthening IPC. Healthcare administrators play a pivotal role in shaping work environments that reinforce positive behaviors and remove barriers to consistent practice. By aligning organizational systems with frontline realities, institutions can enhance adherence to SPs, reduce the incidence of HAIs, and ultimately promote safer oncology care.
