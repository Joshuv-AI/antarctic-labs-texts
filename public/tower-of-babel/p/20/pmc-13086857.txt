Influence of vegetation types on soil physicochemical and biochemical properties in naturally recovering riverbank sand mining sites
Authors: Yong Qin, Tengfei Yan, Shijie Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural recovery is a vital strategy for rehabilitating degraded ecosystems, yet the mechanisms by which pioneer vegetation types differentially drive soil development in harsh environments like riverbank sand mining sites remain poorly understood. In this study, plant nutrient characteristics, soil physicochemical and biochemical properties of four vegetation types, namely, Artemisia scoparia, Saccharum arundinaceum, Imperata cylindrica, and solitary trees, in the naturally recovering sand mining sites along the upper Huai River Basin were investigated. The findings revealed a consistent “surface enrichment” effect, with soil physicochemical properties significantly better in the 0–20 cm layer than in the 20–40 cm layer across all vegetation types. The effects of vegetation types on soil properties were profound and divergent. Soil total carbon (TC) content was highest under I. cylindrica (15.24 ± 2.92 g/kg), which was 3.1 times the TC under A. scoparia. Plant total nitrogen content (TN) varied dramatically, with A. scoparia (7.16 ± 0.85 g/kg) containing approximately twice the tissue TN content of the other vegetation types; Soil multifunctionality was directly regulated by both vegetation type and soil depth, with the effects of vegetation mediated by soil chemical properties, and the effects of depth mediated by chemical properties and enzyme activities. Coarse particulate carbon was the key driver, explaining 36.5% of the variance in soil properties. Collectively, vegetation facilitated recovery via a “trading carbon for nitrogen and phosphorus” strategy, enhancing carbon cycling to overcome nutrient limitations. This study provides actionable guidance for assisted restoration by identifying I. cylindrica for rapid surface soil enrichment and solitary trees for deep structure improvement, suggesting a staged planting strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42081-2.Keywords: Vegetation restoration, Primary succession, Degraded ecosystems, Huai River, Soil multifunctionalitySubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The land degradation-induced recession in ecosystem functions, soil erosion, and loss of biodiversity has become a global concern. To address this urgent environmental crisis, the European Union has enacted the Nature Restoration Law through legislation1,2, establishing an ecosystem restoration framework focused on natural restoration. In 2015, the “Opinions on Accelerating the Construction of Ecological Civilization” issued by the General Office of the State Council of China proposed the ecological restoration policy of “mainly relying on natural restoration with artificial restoration as a supplement”. Previous studies indicated that, compared to artificial restoration, natural restoration has a significant carbon sequestration effect over the long term, and it can enhance vegetation diversity and improve site quality3–5. However, research on the mutual feedback mechanism between vegetation and soil during the primary succession of degraded sites remains limited6–8.

During the restoration process of degraded ecosystems, soil and vegetation reveal a synergistic evolutionary trend. Severely degraded sites, characterized by soil compaction, acidification, and nutrient loss, are ideal for studying primary succession9. The establishment of vegetation on degraded sites (such as sand mining) is a result of the joint effect of soil environment and primary productivity, which regulates the succession trajectory of different stages of vegetation species10. Pioneer species create nutrient-rich patches within communities through their ability to withstand extreme conditions and foster the aggregation of certain microbial groups. These patches gradually enhance surface conditions and soil fertility, facilitating the succession of plant and animal species and initiating various underground biochemical processes, including microbial activity, organic matter decomposition, and carbon–nitrogen–phosphorus cycling11. Meanwhile, vegetation also modifies soil conditions via root exudates and litter, influencing subsequent plant growth12,13. In addition, the establishment of specific vegetation in degraded sites is not only influenced by environmental and abiotic conditions, but also reflects the synergistic relationship between plant functional traits (economic spectrum) and soil nutrients14. Ding et al.15 reported that the spatial variability of productivity caused by changes in soil nutrients is mainly driven by vegetation functional traits at the gradient of wetland degradation. As such, understanding the mutual feedback patterns and driving factors between vegetation and soil during the natural restoration process is beneficial for improving the understanding of the restoration characteristics of vegetation and soil, and, ultimately, achieving the multifunctionality of the ecosystem.

As an important part of natural restoration, vegetation restoration has been widely applied to various degraded ecosystems16. Numerous studies have shown that vegetation restoration can improve soil physical properties, increase organic matter content, and enhance the input of surface litter and underground nutrients (root exudates)8,17. However, as a result of the lack of understanding of the soil-vegetation feedback mechanism during natural restoration, inappropriate vegetation selection may have a negative feedback effect on the functional restoration of degraded ecosystems9. For example, while SOC revealed a unimodal curve trend post planting herbaceous vegetation on the Mu Us sandy grassland, planting xerophytic shrubs effectively promoted the accumulation of SOC18,19. As such, the selection and configuration of vegetation is the key to restoring the functions of degraded ecosystems20,21. Despite studies shown that increasing vegetation diversity can promote the natural restoration of degraded ecosystems22, Tariq et al.23 found that monoculture of legumes should be more conducive to the restoration of the functions of desertified systems than multi-species mixed cultivation, suggesting the complex feedback relationship between soil and vegetation during the restoration of degraded ecosystems. As such, a comprehensive understanding of the mutual feedback mechanism between vegetation and soil during the natural restoration process is of great significance.

Sand mining activities often involve covering the riverbank surface, extracting the desired gravel and sand, and leaving behind large discarded gravel. This process results in sparse vegetation and nutrient loss from the soil profile, severely degrading the soil habitat quality along the riverbank. Despite these impacts, research on the reciprocal interactions between vegetation and soil during the natural recovery of sand mining sites remains limited24. The main stream of the Huai River lies at the transition between subtropical and warm temperate zones, forming part of China’s northern boundary. In its upstream section, Xinyang experienced extensive illegal sand mining prior to 2017, which severely degraded the shoreline habitat. In response, the government implemented strict remediation measures, including leveling sand piles along the banks and re-greening the shoreline by applying mud slurry mixed with grass seeds (spray seeding with an external soil layer). However, as a result of the poor nutrient content of the sandy soil in the riverbank area, the initially planted grass seeds failed to successfully take root. As a result of the humid and rainy climate conditions in the Huai River Basin, a vegetation community dominated by pioneer species has gradually formed after nearly a decade of natural recovery. This provides an ideal place for studying the coupling and synergy relationship between the plant community and soil physicochemical properties in the sand-mining sites. This study aims to: (a) quantify differences in soil physicochemical properties among multiple vegetation restoration types; (b) clarify their adaptation strategies to harsh environments; and (c) identify key factors driving soil property variation. We hypothesize that vegetation type and soil depth will jointly shape soil multifunctionality, primarily through their differential influences on soil properties.

Methodology

Study area

This study was conducted in the upper reaches of the Huai River in Henan Province, south of the riverbank (114°04′27″-114°04′46″ N, 32°21′12″-32°21′27″ E) at the end of the growing season in late August 2023 (Fig. 1). This area belongs to the subtropical and warm temperate zones, with an annual average temperature ranging from 15.3 to 15.8℃ and an annual average rainfall of 993–1294 mm. The air is humid with the annual relative humidity being between 74–78%25. This area is a typical sand-mining site. The natural recovery began in 2017, with severely sandy soil and an overall relatively flat terrain. As a result of the local spatial nutrient heterogeneity, the vegetation community is distributed in patches, mainly consisting of Artemisia scoparia, Saccharum arundinaceum, Imperata cylindrica, and scattered solitary trees.

Field sampling

Four sample plots were set up based on the distribution of different vegetation types and the size of the patches within the study area, namely, A. scoparia (H), S. arundinaceum (M), I. cylindrica (BM), and solitary trees (S). Each plot was set up with 4 replicates, totaling 16 plots. The plots were spaced at least 50 m apart to minimize interference. For the A. scoparia, and S. arundinaceum plots, 1 m×1 m quadrats were set up. In each plot, soil samples from 0 to 20 cm and 20–40 cm soil layers were collected using the five-point sampling method, and the aboveground parts were all harvested. For the I. cylindrica plot, a 5 m×5 m quadrat was set up. At each corner and the center of the plot, one I. cylindrica plant was selected, and a soil profile was dug 20 cm south of the base. Soil samples from 0 to 20 cm and 20–40 cm soil layers were collected. The aboveground part was collected from the south side of the plant. For the scattered solitary trees plot, a soil profile was dug at the south side of the trunk base of the tree, and soil samples from 0 to 20 cm and 20–40 cm soil layers were collected. The aboveground part was collected the main branches from south side. For I. cylindrica and solitary trees, sampling from the south side was standardized to control for microclimatic variation, primarily sunlight exposure, ensuring comparability across individuals for our comparative study26,27. The collected soil samples were mixed evenly and placed in sealed bags and brought back to the laboratory for determination of soil physicochemical properties. The other part was collected using the ring knife method to determine physical properties such as soil bulk density, moisture content, and porosity. The collected plant samples were washed, dried, crushed using a grinder, sieved through a 0.15 mm screen, and used to determine the nutrient element contents such as total carbon, total nitrogen, and total phosphorus in the plants.

Laboratory analyses

The pH value of the soil was determined using the potentiometric method (BPP-7800, Chian). The content of soil total carbon (TC) was determined using the potassium dichromate volumetric combined external heating method. The total nitrogen (TN) content of the soil was determined using the Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method (KN-680, China). The total phosphorus (TP) content of the soil was determined using the molybdenum-sulfur antimony colorimetric method (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Japan). The available phosphorus (AP) content of the soil was determined using the sodium bicarbonate extraction and molybdenum-sulfur antimony colorimetric method28. The carbon content, nitrogen content and phosphorus of plants were test with the same method as soil.

The nitrate nitrogen (NO3-N) and ammonium nitrogen (NH4-N) contents of the soil were determined using a continuous flow analyzer (San⁺⁺, Skalar, Netherlands). The soil sample was extracted with potassium chloride solution, and the nitrate nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen in the leachate were determined using the continuous flow analyzer. Soil bulk density (BD) was determined using the cutting-ring method29. The sample was dried and weighed, and the soil bulk density was calculated based on the mass and volume of the soil sample. Soil moisture content (SWC) was determined using the oven drying method29. Soil saturated water holding capacity (TSWC), field soil water holding capacity (FSWC), total porosity (Tpro), capillary porosity (Hpro) and non-capillary porosity (Nhpro) were determined using the immersion method17,30.

The number of mechanically stable aggregates of each particle size in the air-dried soil samples was determined by the dry sieving method. 50 g of the air-dried soil sample was weighed, and then placed on the top of the sieves with pore diameters of 5, 3, 1, 0.5, and 0.25 mm, respectively. The sieves were covered and shaken evenly for 1 min to ensure thorough sieving of the soil sample. Then, the soil on each sieve was collected and weighed. 20 g of air-dried soil samples that have passed a 2 mm sieve was weighed and placed in a 250 mL beaker. 100 mL of 5 g/L sodium hexametaphosphate solution was added into the beaker. The beaker was shaken for 10–15 min by hand then placed in a water bath shaker box with constant temperature, shaking at 150 r/min for 24 h. After the shaking was completed, the mixture was filtered through a 0.25 mm sieve, which was rinsed with ultrapure water until colorless. The portion on the sieve was collected, and defined as coarse particle carbon (T3). The portion below the sieve was then filtered through a 0.053 mm sieve, and the same steps were followed to collect the portion on the sieve, which was defined as fine-coarse particle carbon (T2). The portion below the sieve was silt-clay size carbon (T1). All samples were placed in a 60℃ oven and dried for 72 h to ensure complete evaporation of moisture. After weighing, each particle size component carbon was ground and filtered through a 0.15 mm sieve to determine its total carbon content.

Sucrase activity (SUC) was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetric method. Urease activity (URE) was determined using the phenol blue colorimetric method. Proteinase activity (Prot) was determined using the ninhydrin colorimetric method. The carbon content, nitrogen content and phosphorus of plants were test with the same method as soil.

Soli multifunctionality index (SMF) was calculated using the averaging approach33,34, which is widely used in ecosystem multifunctionality studies. The index was construct using soil properties that represent key physical, chemical, and biochemical functions critical for early ecosystem development. The multiple ecosystems including nutrient supply and cycling, carbon storage, soil structure and habitat, water regulation and enzyme function were considered for the soil multifunctionality in the study aera. In this regard, TN, TP, AP, NO3-N and NH4-N were employed to evaluate nutrient supply and cycling. TC, TC1, TC2 and TC3 were employed to evaluate carbon storage. BD, Tpro, Hpro, Nhpro and GWD were employed to evaluate soil structure and habitat. SWC and FSWC were employed to evaluate water regulation. Sucrase activity, urease activity and protease activity were employed to evaluate enzyme function. To render the different indicators comparable, each variable was Z-score standardized across all samples. The soil multifunctionality index (SMF) for each individual soil sample was computed as the arithmetic mean of the Z-scores of all selected indicator.

Data processing and analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2. Data were checked for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). When assumptions were violated, appropriate transformations (e.g., logarithmic, square root) were applied. To test the effects of vegetation type and soil depth on individual soil variables, we performed Two-way ANOVA using the aov function. When significant interactions or main effects were found (p < 0.05), Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was conducted for pairwise comparisons using the TukeyHSD function. Pairwise correlations among soil physicochemical properties were visualized using the corrplot Package.

To assess whether the overall multivariate pattern of key functional groups were associated with variation in individual soil physicochemical factors, we performed Mantel test using ggcor package. For each test, a multivariate response set was defined: Soil enzyme activity (sucrase, urease, protease), aggregate stability (GWD), Particle-size carbon distribution characteristics (TC1, TC2, TC3) and plant nutrient characteristics (TC, TN, TP). Euclidean distance matrices were calculated for each individual soil property using the vegdist function in the vegan package after Z-score standardization. The mantel test was performed with 999 permutations using the mantel function. Principal component analysis (PCA) method was applied to analyze the distribution characteristics of soil physical and chemical factors of different vegetation types by using prcomp function in vegan package. To identify the key drivers (soil enzyme activity, aggregate stability, particle-size associate carbon, and plant nutrients) and their explanatory power on soil physicochemical properties, we performed a Redundancy analysis (RDA) using the rda function in vegan package. To avoid model overfitting and select the most parsimonious model, forward selection was applied using the ordiR2step function with 999 permutations. Only variable that significantly (p < 0.05) increased the model’s adjusted R2 were retained in the final RDA model. To quantify the independent contribution of each significant predictor to the explained variance in soil properties, we performed hierarchical variation partitioning using the rdacca.hp package35.

Based on established ecological theory and our research hypothesis, we first specified a comprehensive prior conceptual model (Detail in Supplementary Method). Vegetation type (characterized by plant nutrient) and soil depth influence multifunctionality through intermediate soil properties (physical, chemical, enzyme activity). The model was fitted using the plsm package. Path coefficients were estimated via a bootstrap procedure (1000 iterations). The goodness-of-fit (GOF) index and the variance explained (R2) for endogenous latent variables were used to evaluate the final model’s explanatory power.

Differences in soil physicochemical properties among different vegetation types

Soil pH across different vegetation types at riverbank sand-mining sites was generally weakly acidic, ranging from 6.32 to 7.12, with the significant in solitary tree (6.84–7.12) than in A. scoparia (6.32–6.74). No significant differences were observed between soil layers (0–20 cm vs. 20–40 cm) except under A. scoparia, where surface soil pH was significantly lower than deep soils (Fig. 2A); Soil total organic carbon (TC) content differed significantly among vegetation types and soil depth (Table 1S). Surface soil (0–20 cm) contained significantly higher TC than deep soils (20–40 cm) across all vegetation types. TC was highest under I. cylindrica (15.24 ± 2.92 g/kg), followed by solitary trees (12.87 ± 1.67 g/kg), S. arundinaceum (5.75 ± 1.32 g/kg) and A. scoparia (4.95 ± 1.48 g/kg) (Fig. 2B); No significant differences in total nitrogen (TN) content were found between soil layers for any vegetation types, with the highest in I. cylindrica (0.49 ± 0.09 g/kg), followed by solitary trees (0.42 ± 0.06 g/kg), S. arundinaceum (0.36 ± 0.09 g/kg) and A. scoparia (0.32 ± 0.07 g/kg) (Fig. 2C); Total phosphorus (TP) content varied significantly with both vegetation type and soil depth (Table 1S). Surface soil had higher TP than deep soils in all vegetation types except A. scoparia. Discussion

The influence of different vegetation types on soil physicochemical properties in riverbank sand mining sites

Soil physical properties are important indicators reflecting the long-term natural vegetation restoration in desertified environments29,30. Numerous studies have shown that vegetation restoration can enhance soil hydrological characteristics and reduce soil BD29,36. In this study, different vegetation types were identified to promote the improvement of soil hydrological properties in sand mining sites, which was mainly reflected in the restoration effect on the surface soil. Compared with deep soils, surface soil revealed a higher Tpro, Hpro, and FSWC, as well as a lower soil BD, which was consistent with the previous research results29. This can be attributed to the concentration of plant roots in shallow soil, which affected soil porosity by occupying pore space, thereby reducing soil BD37,38. Meanwhile, the influence of vegetation types on the physical properties of soil at different depths was observed to be specific. The A. scoparia and S. arundinaceum. communities can significantly increase the soil moisture content in deep soils, which may be related to the developed shallow root systems of these two species, enhancing water infiltration and interception capacity39,40. Among different vegetation types, the I. cylindrica community maintained higher SWC and TSWC, as well as lower soil BD, which may be attributable to the fact that I. cylindrica is a shallow-rooted plant, with well-developed fine roots and high coverage of the aboveground part. This can effectively retain water and soil as well as improve soil structure. The solitary tree revealed a higher Tpho, Hpro, and FSWC, especially in deep soils, which was more prominent. This may be attributed to the trees have a straight root characteristic, with a well-developed and complete root system, effectively improving the deep soils environment and reducing infiltration rate7.

Understanding the soil nutrient status of plants is of great significance for the effective management of sand mining sites41. In this study, the contents of TC, TN, TP, and AP in the surface soil of different vegetation types were generally higher than those in the deep soils, indicating that different vegetation types have a significant “surface enrichment” effect on soil nutrients during the natural recovery process of sand mining sites. Pioneer plants tend to change the soil fertility conditions under their canopies through the input of litter, and may form vegetation patches and nutrient islands7,13. Compared to other vegetation types, the I. cylindrica community has a better enrichment ability for soil nutrients, which may be related to the high adaptability and strong resilience to the environment. I. cylindrica is a typical C4 plant with strong photosynthetic capacity and large biomass, which is often used for the rapid restoration of degraded sites, creating a higher base in its canopy and effectively promoting the C cycle42. It is worth noting that I. cylindrica is defined as an invasive species in many countries. As such, attention should be paid to controlling its invasion area in the later stage43. The nitrogen and phosphorus in the degraded site soil mainly come from soil parent material mineralization and the recycling of litter, which are the main limiting factors for plant growth and nutrient construction44. In this study, the soil under S. arundinaceum. community showed low TC, TN, TP, AP, and NO3-N nutritional characteristics. Couple with its high plant TC, but low TN, and TP (Fig. 7), the results indicate that the S. arundinaceum. community may enhance phosphorus availability root exudation from its developed fibrous root system, while its overall low-nutrient phenotype indicates a conservative nutrient use strategy45. The soil of the A. scoparia community revealed low TC, AP, and high TP, NO3-N nutritional characteristics. This may be attributable to the annual life history strategy of A. scoparia, with limited biomass accumulation and low carbon input. The lower pH of A. scoparia facilitated the combination of phosphorus with free iron and aluminum oxides in sandy soil, forming insoluble phosphate46,47 and resulting in a higher AP content.

The effects of different vegetation types on soil enzyme activity and functionality in sand mining areas

Soil enzyme activity is widely involved in the carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycles of the soil, playing a crucial role in nutrient mineralization, organic matter decomposition, and nutrient cycling48,49. In this study, the activities of sucrase, urease, and protease in different vegetation types were all found to be higher in the surface soil than in the deep soils, which is consistent with the phenomenon of nutrient enrichment in the surface soil of different vegetation types. The solitary tree community revealed an obvious advantage in soil enzyme activity, likely attributable to these late-successional species possessing well-developed soil carbon pools and established microbial communities, which can supply consistent carbon inputs through root exudates50,51. Building on the observed low-nutrient phenotype of S. arundinaceum (Sect. 4.1), its notable high soil sucrase activity suggested a compensatory mechanism. We proposed that S. arundinaceum community promote nitrogen and phosphorus mineralization ability in the impoverished environments and enhance the absorption of soil nutrients by maintaining high C cycling.

Soil multifunctionality is an important approach for comprehensively evaluating the restoration quality of degraded ecosystems52–54. In this study, different vegetation types significantly enhanced the soil multifunctionality at the surface layer, which is consistent with the previous findings regarding the impact on soil physical and chemical properties55,56. The I. cylindrica community demonstrated the best soil multifunctionality, while the solitary tree community not only maintained a high level of soil multifunctionality but also had a significant improvement effect on the underlying soil.

Synergistic relationship between different vegetation types and soil physical and chemical factors in sand mining sites

Aboveground functional traits, such as specific leaf area, leaf dry matter content, nutrient content, and C: N ratio, are typically used to reveal the aboveground responses of plants to environmental conditions57,58. Litter both directly contributes to soil C and nutrient input and determines the quality of decomposed debris, which is of great significance for regulating microbial community composition and structure as well as the accumulation of microbial residues59. In this study, the plant A. scoparia had the highest TN and TP contents and the lowest C/N ratio. Generally, a lower C/N ratio facilitates a faster decomposition rate60. As a pioneer species, A. scoparia adopts a “fast-growth, high-turnover” strategy, initiating soil nitrogen cycling through the production of nitrogen-rich litter61. The communities of S. arundinaceum. and I. cylindrica revealed the characteristics of high TC, C/N ratio, low TN, and TP, indicating that both of them are formed a unique “high carbon-low consumption” ecological adaptation strategy in riverbank sand mining sites. Carbon was preferentially allocated to the formation of structural compounds, which increased leaf tissue strength and slowed decomposition62,63.

A single species is unlikely to optimize all the functions of the soil. It is necessary to achieve collaborative restoration through functional group configuration64. In this study, during the natural restoration process of the riverbank sand mining site, four vegetation types with unique biological and ecological characteristics formed differentiated soil restoration patterns and exhibited complementarity in function. Among the four vegetation types, the I. cylindrica community showed the strongest comprehensive improvement ability and associated with significantly higher soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus contents and the functionality of the surface layer through high productivity input and a developed root network. This supported the viewpoint of Navarro-Cano et al.42 regarding the key role of high biomass plants in ecological restoration. This study further revealed that this improvement effect was mainly enriched in the surface soil (0–20 cm), and the improvement of the deep soils required the participation of woody plants. It is notable that while the S. arundinaceum had the lowest total phosphorus content (0.15 g/kg), it showed the optimal hydrological regulation function. The “low phosphorus-high water retention” characteristic may be related to its special root configuration. In general, shallow and dense fibrous roots can form stable soil aggregates and activate insoluble phosphorus through root exudates to meet their growth needs, providing a new perspective for understanding the adaptation strategies of C4 plants in poor phosphorus environments. A. scoparia, as a pioneer species, accelerated the nitrogen cycle via high protease activity and low C/N ratio litter, creating a nitrogen-rich environment for subsequent species. By contrast, the solitary tree improved the profile structure through deep roots, increasing the porosity of the 20–40 cm soil layer. The distinct and complementary effects of the four vegetation on soil indicates a functional successional sequence. This pattern aligns with the facilitation model of ecological succession65,66, where early colonizers modify the environment in ways that favor later arrivals. Specifically, the high-nutrient litter of the pioneer A. scoparia likely facilitated the establishment of later species, while the deep rooting of solitary trees represents a later successional strategy. Thus, the observed pattern can be metaphorically viewed as a functional ‘relay’, where each group performs a specialized role at different stages or in different niches, collectively driving ecosystem recovery. This suggests that, for early restoration environments, prioritizing species with complementary functional traits and temporal succession might be as important as, or even more important than, simply increasing species richness.

Major factors of soil physicochemical properties of different vegetation types in riverbank sand mining sites

Our findings support and refine our initial hypothesis. As predicted, the four vegetation types created distinct soil properties signatures. However, the hypothesized direct link from specific plant traits (e.g., plant C: N) to soil properties was not the predominant pathway. Instead, the PLSM analysis revealed a more intergrade mechanism: both vegetation type and soil depth were primary drivers, but they influence soil multifunctionality through distinct pathways. Soil depth primarily regulated multifunctionality by modifying soil chemical property and enzyme activity, while vegetation type exerted its effects through chemical property. This underscores that in early primary succession, the vertical stratification of plant effects and the legacy of depth-related process are at least as critical as the specific nutrient economy of the plants in determining restoration outcomes67; The soil physicochemical properties of degraded sites are the result of the combined effects of environmental, climatic, vegetation, and animal biological and non-biological conditions. Meanwhile, the soil physicochemical properties also exert an influence on the nutrient cycling and material accumulation, thereby altering the surrounding environment. Previous studies indicated that vegetation restoration is essential for achieving the climax community stage50. In this study, coarse particle size carbon was the main factor influencing the soil physicochemical properties of different vegetation types in riverbank sand mining sites. Carbon, as the basic nutrient substrate for various organisms, was the core step of vegetation restoration in degraded sites. Coarse particle size carbon was considered the more active fraction of soil carbon, characterized by a short residence time and a high propensity to participate in various nutrient cycles68,69. This indicates that the rapid cycling of the active carbon component during the primary succession stage at riverbank sand mining sites is a key element for maintaining regional ecology and is the main driving force for different vegetation types to adopt corresponding adaptation strategies. Combined with the discussion on the nutritional strategies of different vegetation types, it can be inferred that different vegetation types during the primary succession stage of sand mining sites influenced soil physical properties, regulated plant nutritional characteristics and enzyme activities, enhanced the soil carbon cycling process and overcame the barren sandy environment. These processes benefited vegetation to adapt to the nutrient-poor sandy environment by adopting a “carbon-for-nitrogen-and-phosphorus” strategy70, thereby gradually improving the regional environment.

This study was based on a space-for-time approach and a single sampling time point, which may not fully capture successional dynamics. While we identified key associations, the underlying microbial and biochemical mechanisms warrant deeper investigation. The proposed staged restoration strategy required field validation to assess its practical efficacy.

Conclusion

This study investigated and identified the differential impacts and potential synergistic patterns of different vegetation types on the soil physicochemical properties in a naturally recovering riverbank sand mining site. The findings of this work both deepened our understanding of the self-repair process of degraded ecosystems and provided an important theoretical basis for artificial restoration. Pioneer vegetation influenced the development of soil physicochemical properties through life cycle traits and nutrient-use strategies. Specifically, I. cylindrica enhanced surface carbon and nitrogen content, S. arundinaceum improved hydrological functions, A. scoparia accelerated nitrogen cycling, solitary tree strengthened deep soil structure. These species, through their complementary functional traits and sequential roles, provide a stratified restoration frame for degrade ecosystems. This highlights that, for the restoration of similar severely degraded sites, the temporal sequencing of species with complementary function. It is important to remain vigilant about the invasive potential of highly competitive species (e.g., I. cylindrica) and to recognize that successful deep soils restoration relies on the structural support provided by woody plant root systems.
