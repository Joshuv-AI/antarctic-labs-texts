Acute clinical adverse events associated with the LUCAS chest compression system
Authors: Syed Shaheer Ali, Konmal Ali, Aviva Cohen, Robert Kropp, Sara Sakowitz, Yas Sanaiha, Nichole Bosson, Peyman Benharash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMechanical cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) devices, such as the Lund University Cardiac Assist System (LUCAS), were developed to reduce variability in the quality of chest compressions and improve outcomes of cardiac arrest. With increasing adoption of mechanical CPR devices, understanding the nature and frequency of device-related complications is critical to ensuring patient safety.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of all adverse events involving the LUCAS 1.0, 2.0, 2.1, 2.2, 3.0 and 3.1 devices from 2015 to 2024 Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) maintained by the US Food and Drug −Administration. Reports were manually reviewed, categorized by device subtype, and further stratified by type of complication. Events were grouped into device- or patient-related categories to identify trends and potential causes.ResultsA total of 255 adverse events were reported, with an increase from 12 events in 2015 to 33 in 2024. Most events were associated with LUCAS 1.0 (50.2 %) and LUCAS 2.0 (27.1 %). The most frequent device-related issues involved device defect (53.7 %) and power failure (26.7 %). Patient-related complications included soft tissue injury (22.0 %), internal organ injury (20.4 %), and chest compression interruption (13.7 %). Malfunctions such as abrupt stoppage, loss of suction, or misalignment were frequently noted as contributing factors.ConclusionReported adverse events associated with LUCAS use include device malfunction and deployment issues, with serious patient harm including death or organ injury, in some cases. As mechanical CPR becomes more widely adopted, knowledge of the most common complications can inform operator training and potential mitigating strategies to minimize adverse outcomes.

Introduction

Despite advances in resuscitation and out of hospital emergency medical services, only 15 % of the more than 500,000 U.S. individuals experiencing cardiac arrest each year, survive to hospital discharge.1, 2, 3 With focus on quality of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), prior work has noted significant variability attributable to rescuer fatigue, inconsistent performance, and interruptions in compressions.4, 5, 6 More recently, mechanical CPR devices such as the Lund University Cardiac Assist System (LUCAS) have been introduced in order to standardize chest compressions and improve clinical outcomes.3, 7

However, studies on the clinical safety and effectiveness of the LUCAS remain sparse and have yielded mixed findings. In a randomized trial, use of LUCAS resulted in similar survival with good neurologic outcome when compared to manual compressions.8, 9 This was further supported with a subsequent meta-analysis of 8501 subjects, in which Liu et al. found the LUCAS not to improve the odds of achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) or survival, compared to manual CPR.10 Karasek and colleagues noted significantly reduced 30-day survival among out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients treated with the device.11 Despite the lack of data demonstrating direct patient benefit, mechanical CPR device use are increasingly employed. This is likely driven by other benefits, including freeing up clinical staff for other tasks, facilitating continuous compressions during patient movement, and increasing safety of prehospital personnel during transport.

Given the increasing integration of mechanical CPR devices into clinical practice and the lack of data demonstrating improved patient outcomes, further evaluation of potential adverse events is warranted. In the present work, we used the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database to quantify LUCAS-related adverse events across the U.S.

Methods

This was a retrospective analysis of all records for the LUCAS 1.0, 2.0, 2.1, 2.2, 3.0, and 3.1 chest compression device manufactured by Jolife AB and Physio-Control Inc. available in the MAUDE database between April 12th, 2015 to December 25th, 2024. The MAUDE registry is a publicly accessible database maintained by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and contains all reported adverse events involving medical devices for the prior decade.12 Reporting is mandatory for manufacturers and user facilities, but voluntary for clinicians and consumers. MAUDE contains over 4 million reports of device-related adverse events and receives approximately 2000 new entries daily.13 Given the deidentified nature of the MAUDE registry, the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Los Angeles determined this study to be exempt from full review.

We identified adverse events using a search term “LUCAS” from MAUDE. S.S.A and A.C performed a manual review of the free-form text for each reported event to categorize the event type (patient- or device-related). S.S.A further categorized device complications as Deployment or Mechanical Issues, Device Defect, and Power Failure, while patient-related complications were classified as Chest Compression Interruption, Fractures, Internal Organ Injury, Soft Tissue Injury, Hemorrhage/Bleeding, and Other/Unknown (Supplementary Table 1).14 Events such as mortality were included only if the submitter’s narrative attributed the outcome to LUCAS use, either explicitly or through reported device malfunction temporally linked to the event. Furthermore, adverse events were stratified by LUCAS subtype. We excluded duplicate records (n = 4) and those with missing key data (n = 3), including events from 2025 (n = 3) (as the year is incomplete) or records lacking a documented event date (1.04 %). Categorization of outcomes (death, injury, malfunction) was based on FDA-defined MAUDE classifications and the submitter’s narrative description. We did not independently assign causality; rather, we report events as documented in MAUDE, recognizing that patient outcomes such as death may reflect the underlying critical illness in addition to, or instead of, device use.

The primary endpoint of this study was the frequency and type of complications related to the LUCAS use as reported in MAUDE. We performed descriptive statistics with frequencies and proportions.

Patient outcomes of mortality and injury comprised 33.3 % and 45.5 % of reported incidents, respectively (Table 1). The volume of reported adverse events steadily increased with a sharp rise in 2021 coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic in the U.S. (Fig. 1). Across all LUCAS subtypes, there were 85 cases entailing mortality with common reasons including device failure leading to an abrupt stop in chest compressions, device freezing as a result of alarms, and damage to patient organs. Fractures to the ribs and spine were also reasons for patient mortality.

Upon further stratification of adverse events by LUCAS device type, the LUCAS 1.0 demonstrated greatest frequency of mortality (18.0 %) and injury (23.9 %), while the LUCAS 2.0 exhibited the highest rate of malfunction (8.6 %) (Table 1).

Discussion

In review of reports to the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience database in the U.S, this analysis provides the first nationwide report of adverse events associated with LUCAS and elucidates patterns of device failure and patient injuries. We further noted an increase in reported adverse events over the past decade, with a sharp rise beginning in 2021, reflecting increased use of mechanical CPR devices coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, when clinicians adopted these systems to minimize infection risk, reduce physical fatigue, and maintain continuous compressions during resuscitations.15

While the exact use numbers for the LUCAS family of devices remains unknown, other investigators have reported its widespread adoption in both prehospital and hospital settings.8, 16 However, neither national datasets such as the MAUDE nor Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (CARES) provide total yearly counts of mechanical CPR use, limiting our ability to calculate event rates and reinforcing that observed increases may reflect reporting practices as much as device risk.17 Although it is important to note that LUCAS 1.0 was introduced around 2003 as the sole gas-powered device, followed by LUCAS 2.0 in 2008, with later versions such as the LUCAS 3.0 released beginning in 2015.18 Given this timeline, earlier models, particularly the LUCAS 1.0, likely had longer clinical use as well as variation in mechanical design, which may contribute to the higher frequency of adverse events reported for these devices. Among reported patient complications, soft tissue injury, internal organ injury, chest compression interruption, and fractures were common complications. This is consistent with prior reports, which have noted rib fractures to occur in 78.8–91.4 % of patients after LUCAS-assisted CPR, compared to 64.6–75.9 % following manual compressions, with no significant difference in rates of sternal fracture or fatal injuries.9, 19, 20 Moreover, visceral injuries such as liver lacerations, hemothorax, and pulmonary trauma have been documented and may be exacerbated by improper device positioning or movement during resuscitation.7, 21 Couper and colleagues further report the AutoPulse device also found serious visceral injuries in 11.6 % of cases vs. 7.4 % with LUCAS and 6.4 % with manual CPR, underscoring adverse events are not unique to LUCAS but may differ in frequency depending on device design.22

It is unknown whether the increased complications observed with mechanical CPR devices represents an increased risk of injury over manual compression, or if survival bias may increase detection of these injuries. Notably, randomized trials such as PARAMEDIC (n = 4471) and LINC (n = 2589) have demonstrated that while serious device malfunctions are rare, rib and sternal fractures occur more frequently with mechanical CPR, consistent with our MAUDE findings.8, 23 Chun and colleagues highlight the importance of precise anatomical placement and device setup to minimize soft tissue trauma and avoid underperformance.21 Monitoring LUCAS positioning throughout the resuscitation, including marking the skin to indicate the initial position of the suction cup, and use of the shoulder strap to maintain the position, are practices that can be emphasized in training. Additionally, device complications such as power failure, loss of suction, and malalignment may abruptly interrupt compressions or reduce their effectiveness,24 potentially impacting clinical outcomes. As mechanical CPR becomes more commonly used in resuscitative efforts, structured training and simulation-based education are essential to optimize application and mitigate risk.

Limitations

The MAUDE database is based on free-form writing that may not fully represent clinical situations in its entirety. Compliance with reporting as well as reporter identity is unknown, as only manufacturers, importers, and user facilities are mandated reporters, while clinicians and other end users are not. Variability in reporting practices and underreporting of complications may affect the comprehensiveness of the data. Additionally, international use and adverse events are not captured. Although the number of overall adverse events has increased in MAUDE, we are unable to quantify a rate of adverse events given the total use of LUCAS chest compression devices are unknown. Supplemental data, such as the AHA Get With The Guidelines–Resuscitation registry, reported 2232 mechanical CPR cases between 2011 and 2019, providing some context regarding national mechanical chest compression usage. However, future work is warranted to adequately capture LUCAS utilization in the United States.25 Finally, patient injuries and poor outcomes may be the result of chest compressions regardless of the method used (mechanical or manual) and the patient's baseline critical illness or injury. There is no reporting of manual compression-related injuries in the MAUDE database for comparison.

Conclusion

In the past decade, 255 device-related adverse events were reported for the LUCAS. As mechanical CPR becomes more widely adopted, knowledge of the most common complications can inform operator training and potential mitigating strategies to minimize adverse outcomes. Specifically, training should emphasize correct device placement and monitoring, while design improvements aimed at preventing suction loss and power failure could further reduce risk. Prospective data collection can better inform the actual complication rate of these devices and their impact on patient outcomes.
