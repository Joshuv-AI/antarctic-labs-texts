Micro/nano-structured titanium surfaces promote endothelial responses and angiogenic activity under zoledronic acid exposure
Authors: Pugeng Li, Hanyu Sun, Xiaoyu Wang, Xuan Li, Qingci Kong, Feilong Deng, Yan Wang, Zhengchuan Zhang, Xiaolin Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental implants are widely used to restore oral function, maintain mastication, and improve long-term quality of life. However, patients receiving high-dose bisphosphonates (BPs), such as zoledronic acid (ZOL), are considered clinically high-risk due to the potential development of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ). Impaired angiogenesis caused by BPs is a key pathogenic factor of MRONJ and an essential role for early wound healing, infection control, and long-term osseointegration of dental implants. Therefore, understanding whether titanium micro/nano-scale topographies can promote angiogenic responses under ZOL exposure is essential for improving implant outcomes in this high-risk population.MethodsTwo micro/nano-structured surfaces were fabricated on selective laser melting (SLM) titanium: (i) SAH: sand-blasted alkali-heated; (ii) SAO: sand-blasting and acid-etching (SLA) followed by anodic oxidation. Conventional SLA surface served as positive control. Under 5 µM ZOL exposure, endothelial morphology, viability, cytoskeletal organization, tube-forming ability, and angiogenic gene and protein expressions were evaluated on different titanium surfaces in vitro. A rat model receiving high-dose intravenous ZOL was used to assess peri-implant vascularization and early osseointegration among mini-implants with different surface topographies in vivo.ResultsMicro/nano-structured (SAH and SAO) surfaces comparably improved endothelial spreading, cytoskeletal integrity, viability, and tube formation compared with SLM and SLA surfaces in the presence of ZOL. Consistently, both SAH and SAO surfaces upregulated the expression of angiogenesis-related genus, including PECAM-1, ICAM-1, vWF, and Ang-1. At the protein level, Ang-1 expression was upregulated on these micro/nano-structured surfaces, while apparent differences in PECAM-1 levels were not observed. In vivo, micro/nano-structured implants showed a higher density of CD31-positive microvessels and a tendency toward improved bone-implant contact and early bone formation despite high-dose ZOL administration.ConclusionsMicro/nano-structured titanium surfaces can enhance endothelial function and promote peri-implant angiogenesis under ZOL exposure challenge. These findings suggest their potential to support better peri-implant vascularization and safer implant rehabilitation for patients receiving high-dose bisphosphonates.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08220-x.

Background

Dental implants are widely regarded as a predictable and effective treatment option for restoring masticatory function and aesthetics. However, for patients receiving high-dose antiresorptive therapy, particularly intravenous bisphosphonates (BPs) such as zoledronic acid (ZOL) or denosumab for osteolytic tumors and bone metastases, implant placement is considered clinically high-risk [1, 2]. This concern primarily arises from the potential development of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ), a rare but severe complication characterized by exposed maxillofacial bone that fails to heal for more than eight weeks [3, 4]. Evidence indicates that implant-associated MRONJ in cancer patients receiving high-dose BPs frequently occurs within the first three years after implant placement [5], with considerable negative influence on implant-supported oral rehabilitation. In addition, the management of MRONJ is complex, unpredictable, and often prolonged, meaning that it may adversely affect implant survival and bring heavy treatment burden for patients. Consequently, major dental organizations, including European Federation of Periodontology (EFP), consistently classify high-dose BPs therapy as a high-risk factor and recommend extreme caution or avoidance of implant surgery in this patient population [1, 4, 6, 7].

In clinical practice, these risk considerations often lead clinicians to choose removable dental prostheses instead of implant-supported restorations for patients receiving high-dose BPs. Although removable dentures can avoid the risk of MRONJ triggered by implant surgery, they are partly associated with discomfort, reduced masticatory efficiency, challenges in hygiene maintenance, and impaired nutritional intake, ultimately influence long-term quality of life [8, 9]. Moreover, with recent advances in novel therapies, the median survival of oncology patients receiving antiresorptive treatment has increased [10, 11]. Consequently, it is necessary to explore safer and feasible strategies that may allow high-risk patients to benefit from the functional advantages of fixed implant-supported restorations.

The pathogenesis of MRONJ is widely recognized as multifactorial. In addition to the well-described suppression of bone remodeling, accumulating evidence indicates that impaired angiogenesis, persistent inflammation or infection, immune dysregulation, and individual genetic susceptibility all contribute to disease development [4, 12]. Among these mechanisms, insufficient angiogenesis at the surgical site has received particular attention, as adequate vascular supply is essential for tissue repair, infection control, and early osseointegration following implant placement [13–15]. Notably, ZOL exhibits potent anti-angiogenic effects even at low concentrations. In vitro, it inhibits endothelial cell proliferation, migration, tube-formation capacity, as well as key pro-angiogenic signaling pathways, including EGFR/Akt/PI3K [16, 17]. These effects are further corroborated in vivo, where animal models consistently show reduced vascularization within healing bone [18, 19]. Clinical evidence aligns with these findings: ZOL significantly and persistently reduces circulating angiogenic factors such as VEGF and PDGF in cancer patients [20], and BPs treatment has been shown to increase apoptosis of circulating endothelial progenitor cells, thereby impairing angiogenesis and contributing to avascular necrosis [21]. Collectively, these anti-angiogenic effects create a biologically compromised peri-implant environment, which results in inadequate neovascularization, diminished infection control, impaired bone healing, and an increased susceptibility to MRONJ in patients receiving ZOL who undergo dental implant therapy. Therefore, identifying strategies to enhance peri-implant angiogenesis is critically important.

Surface modification of titanium implants has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance peri-implant vascularization and support early healing. Micro- and nano-scale topographies on titanium surfaces have been shown to promote angiogenesis through multiple mechanisms. At the cellular level, micro/nano-structured surfaces improve endothelial cell behaviors, including adhesion, proliferation, and migration, which is attributed to their favorable topography, wettability, and hydrophilicity [22–25]. In addition, these surface modifications can modulate macrophage polarization toward the pro-healing M2 phenotype, which is associated with angiogenic and tissue-regenerative functions, including increased secretion of platelet-derived growth factor that further stimulates endothelial activity [26, 27]. Consistent with these in vitro findings, in vivo studies demonstrate that micro/nano-structured titanium surfaces enhance early neovascularization and osseointegration in animal models [23, 24, 26, 28–30]. Despite these encouraging outcomes, a critical knowledge gap remains: it is still unclear whether the pro-angiogenic properties of micro/nano-structured surfaces are preserved under ZOL exposure, a condition known to markedly impair endothelial function.

Therefore, this study aims to construct pro-angiogenic micro/nano-scale topographies on titanium surfaces and evaluate their effects on angiogenesis under ZOL exposure both in vitro and in vivo. By investigating endothelial behavior and angiogenic potential within this inhibitory microenvironment, we seek to provide a theoretical and experimental basis that may support safer implant rehabilitation for oncology patients receiving high-dose BPs and potentially reduce their risk of developing MRONJ.

Methods

Experimental groups and specimen preparation

The titanium discs (diameter 20 mm, thickness 1 mm) and mini-implants (diameter 1.8 mm, length 5 mm) were designed with SolidWorks® 12.0 software (Dassault Systèmes, USA) and fabricated via a selective laser melting (SLM) system (SLM Solutions GmbH, Germany), following a previous study [29]. The as-fabricated SLM specimens served as the blank control group.

The positive control group selected the clinically prevalent sand-blasted, large-grit, acid-etched (SLA) topography. This was achieved by sand-blasting the SLM samples with 250 μm ZrO₂ particles under 0.8 MPa pressure, followed by acid-etching in a heated (95 °C) mixture of H₂SO₄ and HCl (H₂O/HCl/H₂SO₄, 2:4:3, by volume) for 15 min.

For the experimental groups, two different micro/nano-scale topographies were fabricated: (i) the sand-blasting, alkali-heating (SAH) group, where sand-blasted specimens were treated in a stirred 5 M NaOH solution at 80 °C for 8 h; and (ii) the SLA/anodic oxidation (SAO) group, where SLA specimens underwent anodic oxidation in 0.5 wt% hydrofluoric acid at 20 V for 45 min. All specimens were ultrasonically cleaned and sterilized before further use. In summary, the study included four groups: the SLM (blank control), the SLA (positive control), the SAH, and the SAO groups.

Surface characterization

Surface topography of the four groups was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; model SU-70, Hitachi, Japan) and the surface elemental compositions were analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS; model X-Max 7, Oxford Instruments, UK) coupled with SEM. Surface roughness parameters, including the arithmetic mean height (Sa) and the root mean square height (Sq), were determined using a three-dimensional optical profilometer (UP-WLI, Bruker, USA). Water contact angles were measured by the sessile drop method using a contact angle goniometer (OCA40 Micro, DataPhysics Instruments, Germany).

In vitro cell studies

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (OriCell® Cat. # HUVEC-20001) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (China). The cells were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM; ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S), and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (all from ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂, and the medium was refreshed every 24 h. HUVECs at passages 3–6 were seeded onto titanium discs from the four surface groups in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with complete ECM containing 5 µM zoledronic acid (ZOL; Macklin, China) and cells were further cultured for 48 h before subsequent analyses. This concentration was selected to simulate clinically relevant ZOL levels in the alveolar bone (0.4-5 µM) [31], as it effectively impairs HUVEC angiogenic functions while preserving sufficient cell viability for subsequent assays [32].

Cell viability

After 48 h of ZOL exposure, the discs were gently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transferred to new 12-well plates. Cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 3 h of incubation, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm (Sunrise™, Tecan, Switzerland).

Cell adhesion and morphology assay

Following 48 h of ZOL treatment, the samples were gently washed with PBS and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the fixed cells were dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%). After sputter-coating with gold, the cellular morphology and adhesion were observed using a FE-SEM (model SU-70, Hitachi, Japan).

Cellular skeleton

After 48 h of ZOL treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cytoskeleton and nuclei were subsequently stained with Actin-Tracker Green (Beyotime, China) and DAPI (Beyotime, China), respectively. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (model FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

LIVE/DEAD staining

After 48 h of ZOL treatment, Live/Dead Cell Double Staining Kit (Calcein AM/PI; BestBio Science, China) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol to visualize live and dead HUVECs. The stained cells were immediately observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (model FV3000, Olympus, Japan). The percentages of live and dead cells were quantified from the acquired images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Tube formation assay

The in vitro vascularization potential of HUVECs was evaluated using a Matrigel tube formation assay. Following the treatment of ZOL for 48 h, HUVECs were harvested from the titanium discs. Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (BD Biosciences, USA; Cat. No. 354234) was pipetted into pre-chilled 96-well plates after overnight thawing at 4 °C and gelled at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, 1.5 × 10⁴ harvested HUVECs in ECM were seeded onto the solidified Matrigel. After 3 h of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere, tubular structures were observed. Images from three random fields per well were captured using an inverted microscope. Three random fields per well were imaged using an inverted microscope, and the number of master junctions and total tubule length were quantified with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Following the ZOL treatments described in Cell culture, total RNA was isolated from HUVECs using the RNA Quick Purification Kit (Yishan Biotechnology, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, China). Quantitative PCR was subsequently performed using the Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China) on a QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The thermal cycling protocol comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. A melting curve analysis was conducted from 65 °C to 95 °C to confirm amplification specificity. The relative mRNA expression levels of PECAM-1, ICAM-1, vWF, and Ang-1 were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. All primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Following the 48 h ZOL treatment, total protein was extracted from HUVECs using M-PER mammalian protein extraction reagent (Thermo Scientific, USA) containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein extracts were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Merck, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ang-1 (Abcam, UK), PECAM-1 (CST, USA), and GAPDH (CST, USA). Following standard washing procedures, the membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using a western blotting detection kit (Advansta, USA). Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Thermo Scientific, USA).

In vivo experiment

Animal model construction

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University (Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2021-000814). Twelve female Sprague-Dawley rats (six weeks old, 200–220 g; MGI:5651135) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China. The rats were housed three per cage in standard polycarbonate cages within a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. They had ad libitum access to a standard laboratory rodent diet and water to support normal growth.

The in vivo experimental timeline is illustrated in Fig. 1. Bilateral maxillary first molars were surgically extracted from all rats on day 1 to simulate the delayed dental implant placement in clinical practice. After a 4-week healing period, ZOL (MedChemExpress, USA) was administered via tail vein injection at 0.2 mg/kg, three times per week for 8 weeks. The dose was individualized for each administration based on the body weight measured immediately prior to injection. This protocol simulated high-dose intravenous clinical administration in humans [33, 34]. At week 12, the rats were randomly assigned to the four groups (SLM, SLA, SAH, and SAO). Bilateral implant surgeries were then performed in the healed extraction sockets under anesthesia induced by pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). The implant sites were prepared with a surgical drill unit, and the implants were manually inserted, resulting in a total of n = 6 implants per group. Following surgery, the rats were housed separately by treatment group. and received intramuscular penicillin (80,000 U/rat) for 3 days to prevent infection. Eight weeks after implant placement, all rats were euthanized. Bilateral maxillary specimens, each containing an implant, were collected. These were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and stored in 70% ethanol for subsequent analysis.

Micro-CT

The maxillary specimens containing the implants in situ were scanned using a high-resolution micro-computed tomography system (SkyScan 1276, Bruker, Germany). The scans were performed in the transverse plane with the following parameters: an isotropic voxel size of 15 μm, a source voltage of 100 kV, a current of 200 µA, and a 0.4° rotation step. Each scan generated approximately 910 contiguous image slices per specimen. The acquired projection images were reconstructed and spatially aligned using Dataviewer software (v.1.5.6.2, Bruker, Germany). A cylindrical region of interest (ROI), defined as a 150 μm-wide ring surrounding the implant surface, was selected for analysis. Three-dimensional models of the bone and implant within the ROI were reconstructed using Mimics software (v.19.0, Materialise, Belgium). Bone morphometric parameters, including the bone-implant contact (BIC) ratio, bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), were quantified from the ROI using CTAn software (v.1.20, Bruker, Germany).

Histological analysis

To evaluate bone formation, hard tissue sections were prepared using maxillary specimens containing the implants in situ. Briefly, the specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and infiltrated with methyl methacrylate (MMA) resin under vacuum at 4 °C. After polymerization, the blocks were sectioned along the alveolar ridge crest plane into 200 μm slices using a precision saw (IsoMet™ 1000, Buehler, USA) and subsequently polished to a final thickness of 50 μm. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a standard protocol with Harris’s hematoxylin and eosin Y. Following dehydration and clearing, they were mounted with a neutral resinous medium and digitized using a slide scanner (Aperio AT2, Leica Biosystems, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

The distribution of blood vessels around the implant site was assessed by CD31 immunohistochemistry on the peri-implant tissue. After careful implant removal, samples were fixed, decalcified, and paraffin embedded. Section (4 μm) were cut along the alveolar ridge crest plane using a microtome (Leica RM2255, Germany). The peri-implant area was defined as the soft and hard tissues directly surrounding the implant cavity.

For immunohistochemistry, sections were processed for CD31 staining. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit anti-CD31 primary antibody (Abcam, UK; Cat. No. ab182981, 1:500 dilution). This was followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB development. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the slides were mounted and captured using a slide scanner (Aperio AT2, Leica Biosystems, USA).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro and in vivo data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 24.0; SPSS Inc., USA). Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was evaluated, and p-values were consistently reported and denoted as p > 0.05 (ns, not significant), p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), or p < 0.001 (***). All graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Surface characterization

Macroscopically, the titanium discs (20 mm in diameter) exhibited distinct metallic colors following the different surface modification treatments (Fig. 2a). Microscopically, distinct surface morphologies were observed across the four groups (Fig. 2b). The SLM surface was characterized by a coarse texture with irregularly shaped, partially melted titanium spheres, while the SLA surface exhibited micron-scale irregular concavities and ridges. Hierarchical micro/nano-structured surfaces were successfully constructed on the SAH and SAO specimens. Specifically, the SAH surface displayed shallow pits under low magnification and a biomimetic nano-network under high magnification, with a gradient in pore size from the periphery to the central regions. In contrast, the SAO surface featured uniformly distributed nanotubular structures.

EDS analysis confirmed titanium as the predominant element across all groups, followed by oxygen (Fig. 2d, Table S1). Trace amounts of sodium and carbon were exclusively detected in the SAH group, attributable to residues from the sodium hydroxide treatment. No other extraneous elements were detected in the other groups.

The three-dimensional surface topography and roughness parameters (Sa, Sq) are shown in Fig. 2c and e, and 2f. The specific quantitative values for these roughness parameters are detailed in Table S2. Quantitative analysis showed the SAH group exhibited significantly lower Sa and Sq values, whereas the SLM group showed the highest roughness. Statistical analysis confirmed that both SAH and SAO surfaces were significantly smoother than the SLM and SLA surfaces (p < 0.001).

Water contact angle measurements revealed marked differences in surface wettability (Fig. 2g and h, Table S3). The SAH and SAO groups exhibited superhydrophilic behavior, with contact angles of 5.52 ± 0.53° and 4.75 ± 0.71°, respectively. In contrast, the SLA surface was significantly more hydrophobic, with a contact angle of 120.13 ± 16.97° (p < 0.0001).

Cellular responses of HUVECs to different titanium surface topographies in a ZOL microenvironment

Cell adhesion and morphology

The adhesion and morphology of HUVECs cultured on the four types of titanium surfaces under ZOL exposure were examined by SEM (Fig. 3a). While filopodia-like protrusions were observed in all groups, the degree of cell spreading and attachment quality varied markedly with surface topography. Cells on the micro/nano-structured SAH and SAO surfaces exhibited the broadest spreading and a plumper morphology. In comparison, cells on the SLA surfaces displayed more polygonal and elongated shapes, whereas those on the SLM surfaces appeared least spread and were often loosely attached to unmelted titanium spheres.

Cytoskeleton evaluated by CLSM

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed the differential response of the cytoskeleton to surface topography under ZOL exposure (Fig. 3b). Cells on the SAH and SAO surfaces maintained well-spread, polygonal morphologies with clearly defined actin filaments. Furthermore, these surfaces supported higher cell densities compared to SLM and SLA surfaces, demonstrating their enhanced capability to promote cell adhesion and growth in the presence of ZOL.

Live/dead viability assay

The viability of HUVECs under ZOL treatment was significantly influenced by surface topography (Fig. 3c). The micro/nano-structured SAH and SAO surfaces demonstrated superior performance, supporting significantly higher cell densities with lower percentages of dead cells compared to SLM and SLA surfaces. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3d) confirmed this advantage, with the SAH group exhibiting the lowest cell mortality (0.32%).

Quantitative cell viability (CCK-8 assay)

Quantitative assessment using CCK-8 assay revealed that surface topography significantly affected HUVEC viability under ZOL exposure (Fig. 3e). The SAO surface supported the highest cell viability, followed by the SAH surface, both significantly higher than the SLM group (p < 0.001). This indicates that the specific micro/nano-topographies of SAO and SAH are more favorable for endothelial cell maintenance when ZOL is present.

Angiogenic response of HUVECs on different titanium surface topographies under ZOL exposure

Tube formation assay

The micro/nano-structured surfaces demonstrated superior capacity to support angiogenic function in the presence of ZOL (Fig. 4a). While the SLA surface facilitated better capillary-like network formation than SLM, both SAH and SAO surfaces promoted more complex and well-developed tubular structures than SLA, as reflected by increased tube junctions, total tube length, and branching points (Fig. 4b). Among the four groups, the SAO surface showed the strongest pro-angiogenic effect, with statistically significant enhancements (p < 0.05).

Gene expression analysis by Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

At the molecular level, the micro/nano-structured surface modifications upregulated the expression of pro-angiogenic genes in HUVECs adherent to the titanium surfaces in the ZOL microenvironment (Fig. 4d). The SAH and SAO surfaces significantly upregulated the expression of PECAM-1, ICAM-1, vWF, and Ang-1 compared to SLM and SLA surfaces (p < 0.001). Notably, the SAO surface was associated with the highest expression levels of vWF and Ang-1, significantly higher than the SAH group (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively).

Protein expression analysis by Western blot

To further investigate the angiogenic response, Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate specific protein levels (Fig. 4c). Consistent with the gene expression trends, the SAH and SAO surfaces promoted the protein expression of Ang-1 relative to the SLA and SLM groups under ZOL exposure. Meanwhile, apparent differences in PECAM-1 protein band intensities were not observed among the different titanium surfaces.

In vivo peri-implant angiogenesis and osseointegration under ZOL treatment

A total of 24 implants were placed bilaterally in the maxillary molar regions of rats received ZOL treatment. One implant from the SLM group was lost on the first postoperative day, while the remaining 23 implants maintained stability throughout the 8-week healing period. No clinical evidence of MRONJ was observed in any experimental group.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining for CD31 was performed to evaluate peri-implant angiogenesis under the influence of ZOL. In the stained sections, CD31-positive endothelial cells were identified by distinct brown staining. This staining clearly outlined the microvessels within the peri-implant tissues (Fig. 5a). Observation of the stained sections suggested a trend toward improved vascularization around implants with micro/nano-structured surfaces. The SAH and SAO groups showed a tendency for higher density and wider distribution of CD31-positive microvessels compared to the SLM and SLA groups. This finding is consistent with the pro-angiogenic tendencies previously observed in cellular studies of these surface topographies.

Micro-CT analysis

Micro-CT analysis was performed to visualize the three-dimensional structure of the bone-implant interface (Fig. 5b). Although quantitative evaluation of bone-implant contact (BIC) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) revealed no statistically significant differences among the four groups, the SAH and SAO groups exhibited a noticeable upward trend in these bone parameters (Fig. 5c, d), which may suggest a modest positive response despite variability. Analysis of trabecular microstructure parameters, including trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, Fig. 5e), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, Fig. 5f), and trabecular number (Tb.N, Fig. 5g), showed no statistically significant differences across groups.

Histological analysis

Histological staining revealed distinct patterns of bone-implant integration among the four surface groups (Fig. 5h). The SLM group exhibited limited bone integration, characterized by a wider soft tissue interface and noticeable fibrous tissue encapsulation around the implant, along with the presence of unmelted titanium spheres. In contrast, the SLA, SAH, and SAO surfaces showed more favorable osseointegration. Among them, the SAH and SAO groups presented the most pronounced bone-implant contact, with newly formed bone more extensively contact to the implant surface. These observations indicate that micro/nano-structured surface modifications may promote a more intimate bone-implant interface under ZOL exposure.

Discussion

In this study, micro/nano-scale structures (SAH and SAO) were successfully fabricated on SLM titanium surfaces, and their effects on angiogenesis were evaluated under ZOL exposure. Overall, the results showed that both SAH and SAO surfaces supported more favorable endothelial morphology and function than the conventional SLA surface, and this was accompanied by higher expression of angiogenesis-related genes. In vivo, these micro/nano-scale surfaces also exhibited a modest increase in peri-implant microvessel density and a trend toward improved osseointegration. Taken together, these findings suggest that micro/nano-structured surfaces can enhance endothelial activity and local angiogenic responses even under the antiangiogenic challenge of ZOL, thereby potentially supporting improved early implant healing.

Angiogenesis is a fundamental step in early implant healing and osseointegration [35, 36], yet it can be severely compromised by bisphosphonate (BP) therapy. Zoledronic acid (ZOL), a third-generation nitrogen-containing BP, is widely used to prevent skeletal-related events in malignancies and metastatic bone disease due to its strong inhibition of osteoclastic bone resorption [37, 38]. However, ZOL also exhibits direct antiangiogenic effects, impairing endothelial proliferation, migration, cytoskeletal organization, and vascular signaling pathways [39–43].

The pharmacokinetic properties of ZOL further exacerbate its impact on the jawbone. BPs preferentially bind to hydroxyapatite and accumulate in bones with high remodeling rates [44, 45], and the alveolar bone of the jaws exhibits greater turnover due to repeated microtraumas from teeth and masticatory forces [46]. As a result, higher local concentrations of ZOL can be incorporated in the jawbone microenvironment. Clinical evaluations indicate that ZOL concentrations in the saliva and alveolar bone of treated patients range between 0.4 and 5 µM [31]. To accurately simulate this clinical microenvironment in vitro, a concentration of 5 µM was utilized in this study. Based on previous in vitro studies, this concentration significantly impairs endothelial tubule formation and invasion [32], while its time-dependent inhibition of cell viability still preserves a sufficient number of cells for subsequent functional assays.

This combination of antiangiogenic activity and high local ZOL concentration creates a microenvironment in which not only early wound healing and angiogenic responses are compromised, but also the long‑term capacity for new bone formation and vascular support is diminished. Reduced vascularization after implant placement can limit nutrient and oxygen delivery to the regenerating bone, inhibit the recruitment and migration of osteogenic cells, and reduce immune cell trafficking to the site [47–49]. These effects delay bone formation and weaken local defenses against microbial challenge, ultimately impairing implant integration and stability. In summary, the multifactorial effects of ZOL present a major challenge for implant success in patients receiving high-dose BP therapy.

In the present study, we simulated clinically relevant bone-level ZOL accumulation corresponding to high-dose intravenous regimens [31, 50, 51]. Consistently, both SAH and SAO surfaces supported better endothelial morphology, cell spreading, cytoskeletal organization, and angiogenic gene expression compared with conventional SLA surfaces. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted without ZOL, in which micro/nano-topographies enhanced focal adhesion maturation and integrin-mediated cell adhesion, thereby promoting endothelial function [26, 52–54]. Furthermore, the superhydrophilic properties of SAH and SAO surfaces may also enhance endothelial attachment and spreading. Alkali-heat or anodic oxidation treatments have been shown to result in highly hydrophilic titanium surfaces, thereby facilitating more effective cell adhesion [55, 56]. Previous studies have reported that increased surface wettability can facilitate the adsorption of adhesion proteins, which in turn support cell adhesion and focal adhesion formation [57, 58].

To further explore the underlying mechanisms, the interplay between the biochemical toxicity of ZOL and the specific surface topography of the implant must be considered. ZOL can exert its antiangiogenic effects by inhibiting the mevalonate pathway, which depletes isoprenoids required for the prenylation of small GTPases (such as Rho and Rac) [59, 60]. Because these GTPases are master regulators of actin dynamics, their inhibition leads to cytoskeletal collapse, thereby severely impairing endothelial cell migration and the physical assembly of capillary-like tubes [41, 61].

Furthermore, cellular behavior is highly sensitive to microenvironmental physical cues. The specific micro/nano-topographies and superhydrophilicity of SAH and SAO surfaces can facilitate the adsorption of extracellular matrix proteins and subsequently promote integrin clustering [62, 63]. Such cell-material interactions may initiate mechanotransduction cascades, including the activation of Focal Adhesion Kinase (FAK). Previous studies indicate that FAK signaling contributes to cytoskeletal reorganization [64]. This structural remodeling may assist cells in partially overcoming the ZOL-induced GTPase blockade, thereby maintaining their migratory capacity. Additionally, these mechanical signals can stimulate downstream survival effectors, such as the PI3K/Akt pathway [52].

This integrin-mediated mechanical compensation is reflected in the transcriptional and translational profiles observed in this study. Even under continuous ZOL exposure, the micro/nano-structured surfaces significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of crucial pro-angiogenic factors (Ang-1, vWF) and intercellular junction molecules (PECAM-1, ICAM-1) relative to the unmodified surface (SLA). At the protein level, the marked upregulation of Ang-1 further confirms the activation of vessel maturation and stabilization pathways. Meanwhile, apparent differences in PECAM-1 protein band intensities were not observed among the groups. This result might reflect temporal delays in protein translation or complex post-transcriptional regulation under severe ZOL-induced stress. Despite this, the functional restoration of endothelial migration and tube-forming capacity suggests that topography-induced cues can support local microvascular networks against pharmacological challenges.

The in vivo results further corroborated these findings. CD31 immunohistochemical staining indicated a tendency toward higher peri-implant microvessel density around SAH and SAO surfaces, consistent with the enhanced angiogenic behavior observed in vitro. Given that systemic ZOL administration reduces bone blood vessel density, including in alveolar bone, mainly by inhibiting endothelial proliferation and microvascular remodeling [65–67], the relatively enriched CD31-positive vessels around micro/nano-structured implants are notable. These surfaces may provide a more favorable microenvironment for endothelial survival and early neovascular formation, similar to previous findings in non-ZOL models, where micro/nano-structures promote vascular infiltration [24, 68]. It should be noted that one implant (out of a total of 24) experienced spontaneous loss on the first postoperative day, which was most likely attributable to insufficient primary stability rather than biological failure. Inadequate insertion torque may compromise early mechanical anchorage [69], particularly in ZOL-treated bone with potentially altered remodeling dynamics. This isolated event was therefore considered a technical factor and did not affect subsequent biological analyses.

Although micro-CT analysis did not detect statistically significant differences in bone parameters among groups, both BIC and bone BV/TV exhibited a consistent upward trend around SAH and SAO implants. Several factors may account for the absence of statistical significance, including the potent antiresorptive effects of high-dose ZOL, which markedly suppress bone turnover [70], the relatively short observation period that may limit the detection of early or subtle changes in mineralized bone structure, and the limited sample size, which may reduce the statistical power to detect modest but biologically relevant differences in peri-implant bone parameters. These nonsignificant but favorable trends in bone parameters derived from micro-CT analysis are biologically consistent with the enhanced peri-implant vascularization. Enhanced neovascularization can improve local nutrient and oxygen supply, facilitate the recruitment and differentiation of osteogenic precursors, and promote early bone matrix deposition, which collectively contribute to slight improvements in peri-implant bone formation [47]. Histological observations further revealed more direct bone-implant contact. Considering that angiogenesis precedes new bone formation, the vascular advantage of micro/nano-surfaces likely contributes to slightly improved early bone response under ZOL influence, linking enhanced angiogenesis to potential osseointegration benefits. Overall, these in vivo results indicate that micro/nano-scale surface modifications can partially mitigate the vascular inhibitory effects of ZOL, thereby contributing to a favorable trend toward improved early osseointegration.

In contrast, the untreated SLM surface showed the least favorable outcomes throughout the study. In vitro, endothelial cells on SLM were sparsely distributed and showed limited spreading. In vivo, SLM implants were surrounded by more fibrous tissue and had weaker bone contact. These effects are likely attributable to the inherent roughness and presence of unmelted titanium spheres on the SLM surface, which create microenvironments that limit cell growth [71]. These findings indicate that, although SLM technology allows high-precision, customizable implant fabrication [72, 73], post-processing modifications such as micro/nano-scale structuring are necessary to achieve optimal biological performance.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. As an initial exploratory investigation, the in vivo sample size was relatively limited, and increasing the number of animals in future studies will help confirm observed trends. Future work will also include additional time points during healing periods and more detailed vascular analyses to further validate and refine these findings.

In summary, this study demonstrates that micro/nano-scale surface modifications enhance endothelial and angiogenic responses under ZOL exposure, supporting early peri-implant healing in patients receiving high-dose BP therapy. These findings provide preliminary evidence for optimizing implant surface design to improve safety and outcomes in this high-risk patient population.

Conclusion

Under ZOL exposure, micro/nano-structured titanium surfaces (SAH and SAO) improved endothelial morphology, viability, and specific angiogenic gene and protein expressions in vitro. These improvements were accompanied by a trend toward enhanced peri-implant vascularization and osseointegration in vivo. These results highlight the potential of such micro/nano-structured surfaces to support endothelial activity and local angiogenic responses under BPs challenge.
