Computational screening of natural compounds from traditional Persian medicinal plant for the finding of potential antidepressant
Authors: Aryan Rahbar, Seyed Soheil Tabibian, Hamid Madanchi, Sajjad Ahmad, Ramtin Naderian, Najmeh Hemmatian, Seyyed Bozorgmehr Hedayati, Milad Hatamian, Samira Sanami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depression is one of the most common mental disorders, and more than half of individuals with depression have reduced or discontinued antidepressant use due to side effects. Medicinal plants are safer, cheaper, and more readily available than synthetic medications. This study will employ bioinformatics to assess the impact of compounds discovered in plants used in traditional Persian medicine (TPM) on key proteins involved in depressive pathways. Compounds derived from plants used in TPM for the treatment of depression were identified and subsequently evaluated for ADME properties and toxicity. The potential target genes of these compounds, along with genes associated with depression, were identified, and the intersecting genes were selected to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Molecular docking analysis of the plant compounds and their key target proteins was conducted, followed by the selection of the most effective compound based on molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. In this study, 23 genes were identified as target genes of the plant compounds, and 582 genes associated with depression were identified, nine of which were shared, including ABCB1, AKT1, CAT, CDH1, CYP2B6, ESR1, ESR2, PPARG, and TRPV1. The PPI network identified ABCB1, AKT1, CDH1, ESR1, and PPARG as key proteins. Among them, ABCB1 and AKT1 exhibited favorable docking energies with carnosic acid. MD simulations further revealed the stability of the ABCB1–carnosic acid and AKT1–carnosic acid complexes. Our findings indicate that carnosic acid may represent a potential therapeutic option for the treatment of depression. However, further in vivo and clinical trials are required to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53032-2.

Introduction

Depression is one of the most widely diagnosed mental disorders, and it not only costs money but also results in significant psychological and interpersonal distress, along with social repercussions1. It causes chronic sadness, persistent low mood, cognitive impairment, and a loss of interest. Depression has a significant impact on individual, physical, and social functioning. It is also associated with profound grief, hopelessness, and emptiness. It may eventually result in an inability to feel pleasure, psychomotor dysfunction, sleep and appetite difficulties, difficulty concentrating, and suicidal ideation2.

An estimated 4% of the global population is affected by depression. The prevalence is 5.7% among adults (4.6% in men and 6.9% in women) and 5.9% among individuals aged 70 years and older. Approximately 332 million individuals worldwide experience depression3. Depression manifests around 1.5 times more often in women than in men. Globally, almost 10% of pregnant women and those who have just given birth suffer from depression4.

Current biological models of depression characterize it as a neural network disorder characterized by changes in widely dispersed brain regions5. Antidepressants that promote synaptic plasticity include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)6. These pharmaceutical therapies are regarded as first-line pharmacological therapies for individuals suffering from major depressive disorder (MDD)7 and anxiety disorders8. Despite guidelines advocating for a comprehensive biopsychosocial approach and research demonstrating the need for psychological therapy, social support, and exercise, pharmaceutical treatment is frequently required in situations of moderate to severe depression. Although antidepressants are effective, SSRIs and SNRIs have been linked to a number of side effects, including appetite suppression, weight loss, somnolence, vertigo, fatigue, cephalalgia, increased suicidal ideation, nausea/vomiting, sexual dysfunction, and an increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events9–11. Although individual adverse effects of antidepressants differ, they frequently appear with significant frequency, intensity, and burden12,13.

Due to significant side effects, over half of individuals with depression have begun lowering their antidepressant dosage or have completely stopped taking psychiatric medications14. The exorbitant expense of these pharmaceuticals, combined with their negative effects, has prompted individuals to explore alternatives derived from medicinal plants15. Medicinal plants, frequently perceived as safer, more cost-effective, and more accessible than synthetic medications, have traditionally served as significant medicinal agents in ethnomedicine16. Traditional medicine systems’ written records include lists of drugs that have been used for centuries. These can be important resources for identifying potential novel medicines for conditions that currently lack an effective treatment. Traditional Persian medicine (TPM) is one example of a long-standing medical system that still exists today17. By the late nineteenth century, Western medicine had superseded this practice in Iran. Although medical professionals were prohibited from employing its therapeutic practices, scholars preserved valuable knowledge in their studies. Local herbal shops continue to provide relief for many Iranians, maintaining the survival of TPM18.

Computational approaches are cost-effective and efficient and are capable of investigating a broad spectrum of chemical diversity, thereby facilitating the identification of novel lead compounds with therapeutic potential in various disorders19. In recent years, computational methodologies have been increasingly popular among researchers as an efficient and reliable means of screening pharmaceuticals for various illnesses. Molecular docking elucidates the binding affinity and interaction mechanisms between phytochemicals or drugs and their protein targets20. Numerous medicinal plants were advocated in TPM for the treatment of depression. This study aims to perform a bioinformatic analysis of the effects of compounds in these plants on a group of proteins that are predicted to be involved in essential mechanisms of depression that have been identified as optimal therapeutic targets. Methods

Identification and ADME/toxicity evaluation of plant compounds

Medicinal herbs effective for treating depression in TPM were selected based on a comprehensive review of the available literature using a targeted search of keywords including “medicinal plant”, “herbal compounds”, “plant compounds”, “depression”, “Iran”, and “TPM”. The compounds in the plants were determined through a search in the Chemical Entities of Biological Interest (ChEBI) database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chebi/). ChEBI is a free dictionary of molecular entities focused especially on “small” chemical compounds21. The SwissADME web tool (http://www.swissadme.ch/) was used to evaluate the physicochemical properties and estimate ADME parameters (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion)22. Lipinski’s rule of five (RO5) was used to predict the compounds’ drug-likeness. RO5 states that poor absorption or permeation is more likely when there are more than 5 hydrogen bond (H-bond) donors, 10 H-bond acceptors, the molecular weight (MW) is greater than 500 and the calculated Log P (CLogP) is greater than 5 (or MlogP > 4.15)23. According to RO5, an orally active drug can have no more than one violation of these conditions. Additionally, compounds were screened for key physicochemical properties, including flexibility (rotatable bonds < 9), polarity (20 Å2 < topological polar surface area [TPSA] < 130 Å2), and saturation (0.25 < fraction of sp3 carbons < 1)22. The ProTox 3.0 server (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) assessed ADME-filtered plant compounds for toxicity criteria such as hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and cytotoxicity. The ProTox 3.0 server uses molecular similarity and machine-learning models to predict 61 toxicity endpoints, including acute toxicity, organ toxicity, clinical toxicity, molecular-initiating events (MOE), adverse outcomes (Tox21) pathways, a variety of other toxicological endpoints, and toxicity off-targets24.

Identification of shared genes targeted by plant compounds and associated with depression

The Search Tool for Interacting Chemicals (STITCH) database (http://stitch.embl.de/), with “Homo sapiens” species settings, was used to identify potential gene targets for compounds previously screened for ADME properties and non-toxicity. The STITCH database contains information about known and predicted interactions between chemicals and proteins. The interactions include direct (physical) and indirect (functional) links25. Genes associated with depression pathogenesis were identified using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene), and the Venny 2.1 software (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) was used to find shared genes between the two groups26.

Construction and analysis of protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

The PPI network of the common target genes was built using the Search Tool for Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) v12.0 (https://string-db.org/)27. The parameters used to construct the PPI network were defined based on the “Homo sapiens” organism, with a minimum interaction score set at medium confidence (0.400). The interaction results were downloaded as a TSV file and imported into Cytoscape 3.7.2 software for the identification of main target proteins and further network analysis28. The NetworkAnalyzer plug-in in Cytoscape was used for analysis, and topological parameters including degree and betweenness centrality were employed to identify key target proteins in the PPI network.

Retrieval and preparation of protein and ligand structures

Crystallographic structures of key proteins selected in the previous step were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/)29. Each protein crystal structure was chosen based on fewer missing residues, the presence of a co-crystal ligand, and the highest possible resolution. To prepare the protein, we initially conducted homology modeling by SWISS-MODEL software (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) to fill the missing residues in the crystal structure of the target protein, using the parent sequence as a template30. Subsequently, we eliminated all co-crystal ligand, heteroatoms, and associated crystal water molecules from the structure using UCSF Chimera program (version 1.10.2) to ensure that the pre-bound molecules had no effect on the plant compounds’ binding affinity31. The addition of hydrogens and charges (AMBER ff14SB) to target proteins was conducted using the Dock Prep tool in UCSF Chimera program. Energy minimization was conducted using UCSF Chimera software (version 1.10.2) with default parameters, including steepest descent steps of 1000, steepest descent step size of 0.02 Å, conjugate step gradient of 10, conjugate steps of 0.02 Å, and update interval of 100. Subsequently, the prepared proteins were saved in PDB format for further analysis. The plant compounds targeting key proteins were obtained in SDF format from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)32.

Molecular docking and interactions analysis

Molecular docking-based virtual screening was performed to identify plant compounds with a high binding affinity for the target proteins, employing Autodock vina33 in PyRx 0.8 software34. The receptor and ligand file formats for molecular docking were prepared in PDBQT format. Active sites were defined as locations inside the target protein structure that were less than 5 Å from the co-crystal ligands. To validate the docking process, we redocked the co-crystal ligand with the target proteins. Compounds with low root mean square deviation (RMSD) values and more negative docking score than the target protein’s co-crystal ligand were chosen for further analysis. Furthermore, the positions and orientations of ligands on receptor active sites were visualized using the UCSF Chimera program (version 1.10.2), and receptor-ligand interactions on 2D maps were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer 202535.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Following molecular docking procedures, the best docked complexes were examined using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The MD simulation was conducted using the Assisted Model Building with Energy Refinement (AMBER) 18 package36. To produce the topology files, the tLEAP interface of the AMBER 18 package was employed, and the primary coordinates were derived from the unbound receptors and docked complexes. The General Amber Force Field (GAFF)37 and ff99SB were used as the computational force fields, and the systems were solvated using three-point transferable intermolecular potential (TIP3P) water. Sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl−) ions were introduced to equilibrate the system’s overall charge. The docked protein complex was minimized using 1500 steepest descent steps followed by 1000 conjugate gradient steps to avoid steric conflicts. Langevin dynamics were employed for system heating over a duration of 10 picoseconds (ps)38, succeeded by 100 ps of equilibration in the canonical (NVT) ensemble. Throughout the production run, hydrogen bonds were restricted employing the SHAKE algorithm39. The system’s temperature was gradually increased from 0 to 300 K over 200 picoseconds (ps) while maintaining constant volume, followed by equilibration at constant pressure. The production run lasted for 200 ns (ns). The final MD trajectories of the unbound receptor and docked complexes were evaluated. Subsequently, we performed trajectory analysis to assess the structural alterations throughout the simulation, focusing on the RMSD, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), free energy landscape (FEL), and dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) parameters for free receptors and docked complexes.

MM-PBSA/GBSA binding free energy calculation

The docked complexes’ binding free energies were calculated using the molecular mechanics generalized Born surface area (MM-GBSA) and molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) approaches40. A total of 450 frames, separated by 0.2 ns, were extracted from the entire MD trajectory and analyzed using MM-PBSA calculations with the MMPBSA.py module41 of the AMBER18 package.

Results

Identification and ADME/toxicity evaluation of plant compounds

Following a literature review, 11 medicinal plants, including Crocus sativus42–49, Melissa officinalis50,51, Rosa damascena51–56, Lavandula angustifolia57–63, Valeriana officinalis64,65, Hypericum perforatum66–70, Matricaria chamomilla71–76, Silybum marianum77, Scrophularia striata78, Zingiber officinale79–82, and Rosa canina83 were chosen and submitted into the ChEBI database to identify their chemical components. The identified compounds’ physicochemical properties were determined using the SwissADME web tool. The compounds demonstrated RO5 properties (H-bond donors, H-bond acceptors, MW, and MlogP), as well as flexibility, polarity, and saturation qualities, as indicated in Supplementary Tables S1–S11. Only compounds that matched all RO5 criteria or violated only one rule while maintaining sufficient flexibility, polarity, and saturation qualities (Supplementary Table S12) were chosen for toxicity evaluation. Supplementary Table S13 shows the toxicity parameters of the plant compounds as evaluated by ProTox 3.0 server, including hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and cytotoxicity.

Identification of shared genes targeted by plant compounds and associated with depression

Target genes were identified using the STITCH database for plant compounds previously accepted as appropriate for ADME properties and toxicity. For lots of plant compounds, no target genes were identified, while for others, multiple target genes were identified. This database identified 9, 1, 5, 1, 5, 2, and 5 target genes for the plant compounds carnosic acid, cis-3-hexenyl isovalerate, falcarindiol, pinocarveol, ethylzingerone, rel-2alpha,3beta,23-trihydroxy-12,17-dien-28-norursane, and zingerone, respectively (Supplementary Table S14). In total, 582 genes associated to depression have been reported in NCBI, and the list is shown in Supplementary Table S15. Construction and analysis of PPI network

A PPI network was built by uploading the common target genes to STRING v12.0. The interactions between the targets consisted of nine nodes and 17 edges, with each node representing a protein and the edges showing protein–protein interactions. The most connected node in this network was AKT1, which was shown to be a core node that directly interacts with a number of genes, including CDH1, PPARG, ESR1, ESR2, TRPV1, and ABCB1. Furthermore, ESR1 and ESR2 demonstrated robust interactions with both AKT1 and one another. ABCB1 was primarily linked to the network through interactions with CYP2B6 and AKT1. TRPV1 showed the least connectivity in the network but still remained relevant to the overall network structure, while CAT showed no connectivity in the network (Fig. 3A). Cytoscape analysis revealed that the PPI network consisted of a single major cluster. Five proteins were selected to be key proteins based on degree > 3 and betweenness centrality > 0. These proteins include ABCB1 (ATP binding cassette subfamily B member 1), AKT1 (AKT serine/threonine kinase 1), CDH1 (E-cadherin), ESR1 (estrogen receptor 1), and PPARG (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma) (Table 1, Fig. 3B).Fig. 3PPI analysis. Molecular dynamics simulation

Assessing RMSD in MD simulation enables the evaluation of structural stability, equilibrium, and alterations within the system over time84. During the initial 20 ns, all three systems exhibited a rapid increase in RMSD; following that, the RMSD values showed minimal variation. The average RMSD values for apo-ABCB1, the ABCB1-carnosic acid complex, and the ABCB1-elacridar complex were 11.08, 11.23, and 10.57 Å, respectively. The ABCB1-elacridar complex had the smallest amplitude of fluctuations and the lowest maximum RMSD value of 13.08 Å. The results demonstrate that elacridar binding enhances the structural stability of ABCB1 relative to its apo form and the complex with carnosic acid (Fig. 9A). RMSF measures the flexibility of individual residues, indicating the amount to which they fluctuate during an MD simulation85. RMSF analysis demonstrated that ligand binding significantly decreased the overall flexibility of ABCB1 compared to the apo form (average RMSF = 4.23 Å). ABCB1-elacridar and ABCB1-carnosic acid complexes had an average RMSF of 3.08 Å and 3.27 Å, respectively (Fig. 9B). Rg quantifies the compactness of a molecular structure, often a protein86. Rg analysis indicated that the overall compactness of ABCB1 remained constant across all systems during the 200 ns simulation. The apo-ABCB1, ABCB1-elacridar complex, and ABCB1-carnosic acid complex had average Rg values of 30.25, 30.36, and 30.24 Å, respectively. FEL analysis is employed to identify stable conformations and energy transition states of macromolecules87. The FELs of apo-ABCB1, the ABCB1-elacridar complex, and the ABCB1-carnosic acid complex were analyzed using the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) to examine the influence of ligand binding on the system’s dynamic behavior and conformational stability. The FEL of apo-ABCB1 exhibits a deep and narrow minimum (approximately 7–8 kcal/mol) distributed throughout a wide region, especially along PC1. The broad basin indicates that ABCB1 has high conformational variability and intrinsic flexibility in the absence of ligands (Fig. 9D). The ABCB1- elacridar complex shows the most significant change in FEL. The energy basin along the PC1 and PC2 axes becomes more defined, deeper, and narrower, indicating a significant reduction in structural flexibility (Fig. 9E). The ABCB1-carnosic acid complex appears to have a deeper and slightly denser minimum depth than apo-ABCB1, as well as a more regular energy distribution with less dispersion along PC2. This reduction in structural space shows partial stabilization of a dominating conformational state (Fig. 9F).

DCCM analysis is a computational method used in MD simulations to improve the understanding of protein dynamics by evaluating critical atoms’ cross-correlated motions. The method generates a two-dimensional map that depicts positively correlated, negatively correlated (anti-correlated), and uncorrelated motions between atoms88. The DCCM map in this study is presented as a heatmap, with red, blue, and white indicating positive correlation, negative correlation, and no correlation, respectively. The DCCM analysis was conducted to compare the intrinsic dynamics of apo-ABCB1 with the ABCB1-elacridar complex and the ABCB1-carnosic acid complex. In apo-ABCB1, the red blocks in the DCCM map had a clear positive correlation along the diagonal, signifying cohesive intra-domain stability (Fig. 9G). The DCCM map of the ABCB1-elacridar complex revealed distinct and larger anti-correlated areas, particularly in the center inter-domain zones. It also revealed a significant shift in the usual positive correlation blocks observed in the apo-ABCB1. These results demonstrate that elacridar significantly alters the dynamic network of ABCB1, leading to a lack of coordinated domain motions (Fig. 9H). The DCCM map of the ABCB1-carnosic acid complex showed an increase in positive correlations in specific regions, particularly in the lower-right quadrant of the matrix, as well as a decrease in the intensity of select blue areas (indicating reduced anti-correlation). While the overall block arrangement remained largely unchanged, a slight asymmetry developed in comparison to the apo-ABCB1 (Fig. 9I).

Apo-AKT1 showed an average RMSD of 2.38 Å, while the AKT1-carnosic acid and AKT1-capivasertib complexes had average RMSDs of 2.10 Å and 2.21 Å, respectively. The AKT1-carnosic acid complex had the lowest average RMSD (2.10 Å) and the lowest maximum RMSD (2.91 Å), indicating that the binding of carnosic acid to AKT1 enhances its stability compared to its apo form or AKT1-capivasertib complex (Fig. 10A). RMSF analysis indicated that ligand binding significantly decreased AKT1’s flexibility compared to the apo form (average RMSF = 1.16 Å). The average RMSF for AKT1-carnosic acid complex was 1.10 Å, while the AKT1-capivasertib complex had an average RMSF of 0.98 Å (Fig. 10B). The Rg analysis revealed that the overall compactness of AKT1, in both the apo and bound states, varied little across the 200 ns simulation. The FEL analysis reveals that apo-AKT1 has multiple substantial energy minima in PC1 and PC2. This suggests that AKT1 can be organized in a number of stable or quasi-stable conformations while in the apo state. The various energy wells and their uneven distribution suggest that the apo-AKT1domains are highly flexible (Fig. 10D). The FEL of the AKT1-capivasertib complex exhibits a significant, deep, and concentrated energy minimum. A more uniform energy level indicates that the ligand efficiently reduced structural fluctuation and increased the complex’s stability (Fig. 10E). The AKT1-carnosic acid complex has a significant reduction in conformational space compared to the apo state; yet, many energy minima with moderate to substantial depths persist (Fig. 10F). This implies that, while carnosic acid binding increased the protein’s structural stability, the protein may still access multiple quasi-stable states. Carnosic acid promotes relative stability of the AKT1 structure; nevertheless, its inhibitory effect is likely less potent than that of capivasertib.

The DCCM map of apo-AKT1 is predominantly made up of scattered blue and gray patches, indicating that the mobility of different portions of the protein is anti-correlated (Fig. 10G). The red patches on the DCCM map of the AKT1-capivasertib complex have increased while the blue section has decreased, indicating that capivasertib binding causes a correlation of movements between various areas of the AKT1 protein (Fig. 10H). The DCCM map of the AKT1-carnosic acid complex shows more gray and blue patches, indicating that carnosic acid has a lower stabilizing effect on AKT1 protein movement coordination than capivasertib (Fig. 10I).

Discussion

Depression is frequently associated with other medical disorders. Pharmacological treatments that modify neurotransmitter levels are the foundation of traditional depression treatment; however, they can have unwanted side effects, including headaches, sexual problems, addiction, seizures, and even suicide89. According to a systematic literature review, these adverse effects were reduced in 45% of studies that used herbal drugs for the same purposes90.

Traditional medicine has long recognized plants’ medicinal potential, and various diseases and disorders have found alleviation through the use of plant-derived remedies91,92. Despite the effectiveness of these medications when provided as concentrated plant extracts, the isolation and purification of active compounds is crucial in modern medicine93. A novel method for discovering plant-derived compounds has evolved, supported by network pharmacology and powerful computer algorithms. Recent advances in computational approaches have created several opportunities for the virtual optimization of target binding affinity and the identification of phytoconstituent therapeutic targets, which have the potential to speed up and simplify the drug discovery process94.

This study assessed the ADME characteristics and toxicity of natural compounds from 11 medicinal plants used in TPM to treat depression to evaluate their pharmacological potential. Fortunately, the majority of the compounds were non-toxic and passed the specified thresholds for critical ADME criteria, indicating their potential as suitable drug candidates, and then the target genes of these compounds were identified. The findings indicated that a compound could affect multiple target genes and different compounds may share a common target gene. After eliminating duplicates, 23 genes were identified as target genes for the compounds. A total of 582 genes associated with depression retrieved from NCBI, sharing nine genes with the target genes of plant drugs, namely ABCB1, AKT1, CAT, CDH1, CYP2B6, ESR1, ESR2, PPARG, and TRPV1. Based on the PPI network of proteins produced by these genes, we identified five key proteins: ABCB1, AKT1, CDH1, ESR1, and PPARG.

The ABCB1 gene is located on chromosome 7 (7q21.12) and encodes P-glycoprotein (P-gp)95, a 1280-amino-acid transporter. P-gp is primarily expressed in the blood–brain barrier (BBB), lower gastrointestinal tract, liver, placenta, and CD56 lymphocytes, and it plays a key role in modulating drug bioavailability96. As an efflux pump, P-gp protects the brain from various medicines or neurotoxic compounds such as glucocorticoids and amyloid beta97, as well as limiting the concentration of several antidepressants. ABCB1 variation has been linked to antidepressant efficacy and side effects in small-scale investigations. Although cognitive impairment in major depressive disorder predicts poor treatment outcomes, the genetic consequences of ABCB1 have not been studied in patients with cognitive impairment98.

AKT1 is a gene located on chromosome 14 (14q32.33), coding for protein kinase B (PKB) called AKT1; it is a key protein in the PI3K/AKT pathway that regulates essential cellular functions including survival, growth, and differentiation. This pathway is vital for the neurological system, influencing neuroplasticity, neuronal survival, and neurogenesis, all associated with mood regulation and depression99. Mutations in the AKT1 gene may lead to the dysregulation of this system, potentially contributing to psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and depression100. In the study by Yang et al., the genotype and allele distribution of the AKT1 gene in 461 Chinese Han patients with depressive disorder were investigated using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and direct sequencing techniques. According to the findings of this study, AKT1 polymorphisms are associated with the severity of depression, anxiety symptoms, and suicide attempts in individuals with depressive disorders101.

The CDH1 gene, located on chromosome 16 (16q22.1), encodes E-cadherin, a transmembrane protein that mediates Ca2⁺-dependentcell-cell adhesion. E-cadherin is a member of the classical cadherin family, also called type 1 cadherins102. The external region of E-cadherin functions as an adhesion anchor, binding to cadherins on other cells103, while the intracellular region interacts with catenins (e.g., α- and β-catenins) and other regulatory proteins104. E-cadherin, a protein involved in adhesion junctions and Wnt signaling, interacts with β-catenin, which has been related to affective disorders and anxiety in clinical settings and animal models105. Ketamine, a new rapid-acting antidepressant, has been shown in vitro to reduce E-cadherin expression106. Similarly, fluoxetine, a classic antidepressant, inhibits CDH1-mediated cell adhesion107.

The ESR1 gene, located on chromosome 6 (6q25.1), encodes the estrogen receptor alpha (ERα). During pubertal development, the expression patterns of ERα in the brain change108. Protein and mRNA levels were detected in the cortex prenatally, peaking in the cortex and hippocampus throughout early postnatal life and dropping during puberty into adulthood109. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the ESR1 gene may influence the expression and function of ERα, altering estrogen signaling in emotion-related regions and potentially influencing susceptibility to internalizing mental disorders like depression and anxiety110,111.

The PPARG gene, located on chromosome 3 at 3p25.2, encodes the nuclear receptor PPARγ112. The PPARγ system is strongly active during stress113, and it appears to have a significant role in depression, particularly in one of the most pronounced concurrent conditions, central and peripheral inflammation114. The PPARγ system has a considerable influence on suppressing many types of inflammation, which could explain why PPARγ agonists have become relevant agonists for depression115.

The docking score for the ABCB1 protein with carnosic acid is more negative than that of its co-crystal ligand, elacridar. The interaction map revealed that LEU204, ILE267, TRP200, PHE271, VAL959, SER799, ALA802, ASN264, PHE738, and PHE962 residues of ABCB1 protein interacted with both ligand (carnosic acid) and co-crystal ligand (elacridar). The docking score for the AKT1 protein with carnosic acid is also more negative than that of its co-crystal ligand, capivasertib. According to the interaction map, LYS18, GLY17, PHE298, LEU16, TYR89, ALA90, THR71, THR151, ASP152, VAL24, MET87, ALA37, and MET141 residues of the AKT1 protein interacted with both the ligand (carnosic acid) and the co-crystal ligand (capivasertib). The majority of interactions involving ABCB1 and AKT1 proteins with their ligands and co-crystal ligands are van der Waals in nature. Although van der Waals interactions are individually weak, their abundance results in the ligand being densely packed into the protein binding pocket, providing a substantial surface complement116. This indicates that the binding is predominantly reinforced by hydrophobic and dispersion forces, and may be strong due to the presence of hydrogen bonds and other interactions.

The MD simulation results for the AKT1-carnosic acid and ABCB1-carnosic acid complexes indicate that the system is approximately in equilibrium, with the receptor proteins oscillating around a stable conformation rather than altering orientation. However, carnosic acid’s binding to the AKT1 protein is predicted to be more stable than its binding to the ABCB1 protein. The negative net energy in MM-PBSA/GBSA implies that carnosic acid is preferentially and persistently bound to both ABCB1 and AKT1 proteins. Furthermore, the presence of van der Waals interactions in the 2D maps of both the ABCB1-carnosic acid and AKT1-carnosic acid complexes is consistent with a greater contribution of van der Waals energy to total energy in MM-PBSA/GBSA.

Carnosic acid is a diterpene compound known as a polyphenol because it contains a phenolic group117. Carnosic acid provides a wide range of medical benefits, according to numerous studies. Carnosic acid has been shown to have antioxidant, antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anti-obesity, anticancer, and neuroprotective effects117. This compound has potent anti-cancer activity, and various in vitro and in vivo pharmacological studies have revealed numerous molecular targets associated with carnosic acid activity, including enzymes, kinases, apoptotic proteins, transcription factors, growth factors, oncoproteins, tumor suppressor genes, receptors, and other proteins involved in cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, migration, and invasion118. Microglia-mediated inflammation in the human brain has been linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD)119, and carnosic acid has been proposed as a potential treatment for these disorders due to its anti-inflammatory effects120.

Several studies have been conducted to evaluate the effect of carnosic acid in the treatment of depression. In the study by Azhar et al., the effect of carnosic acid on post-stroke depression-like behavior and the expression of fibroblast growth factor 9 (FGF9) was evaluated. The results indicate that carnosic acid exhibits antidepressant-like effects in post-stroke depressed rats, accompanied by reduced expression of hippocampal FGF9121. The study conducted by Samy et al. demonstrated that carnosic acid treatment substantially alleviated depressive symptoms and restored the disrupted redox state in ovariectomized (OVX) female Balb/c mice that exhibited depressive-like behavior122. Our computational results also revealed new insights into the interaction processes of carnosic acid with two key proteins (ABCB1 and AKT1) involved in the development of depression; however, further in vivo and clinical studies are required for validation.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the potential of carnosic acid, a compound derived from plants used in TPM for the treatment of depression, such as Valeriana officinalis and Melissa officinalis, as a drug candidate. This compound is non-toxic and has positive properties, including RO5 compatibility, acceptable ADME profiles, and a high affinity for the ABCB1 and AKT1 proteins, which are important in depression. These characteristics make it suitable for further investigation, including in vivo studies and clinical trials aimed at drug development.
