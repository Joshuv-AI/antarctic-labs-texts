A comparison of the double-locking loop stitching technique and traditional repair techniques for partial-thickness bursal-side rotator cuff tears: randomized controlled trial
Authors: Weipeng Zheng, Qirun Liu, Yuke Song, Zhihao Liao, Zhijun Liu, Sheng Chen, Suming Zheng, Zhiyong Yi, Xudong Huang, Hewei Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis prospective randomized study compared the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing arthroscopic repair for partial-thickness bursal-side rotator cuff tears (PT-BRCTs) using the double-locking loop stitching technique (DLLST) versus traditional conversion-to-full-thickness repair techniques.MethodsThe study included 110 patients with PT-BRCTs exceeding 50% of tendon thickness. They were randomly assigned to two groups during surgery. Ninety-four patients completed follow-up (Group I: DLLST, n = 43; Group II: traditional repair, n = 51). Clinical outcomes, including range of motion (ROM), visual analog scale (VAS), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Constant score, and modified SF-12 quality-of-life scores, were assessed preoperatively, at 3 months, and final follow-up. Postoperative MRI evaluated tendon integrity.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvements in all clinical parameters (P < 0.05). At final follow-up, Group I exhibited superior ASES (91.25 ± 2.88 vs. 89.95 ± 3.24, P = 0.042) and Constant scores (91.19 ± 2.73 vs. 90 ± 2.76, P = 0.040), along with faster pain reduction at 3 months (VAS: 2.19 ± 1.33 vs. 2.67 ± 0.99, P = 0.023). No significant differences were observed in SF-12 scores (P > 0.05). MRI confirmed complete tendon healing in all cases.ConclusionBoth techniques provided satisfactory functional recovery and pain relief for PT-BRCTs exceeding 50% of tendon thickness. However, DLLST achieved faster functional restoration while preserving tendon integrity, suggesting its potential as a biomechanically advantageous alternative.Keywords: Partial-thickness, Bursal-side rotator cuff tears, The double-locking loop stitching technique, Traditional conversion-to-full-thickness repair techniques, Clinical outcomes, American shoulder and elbow surgeons score

Introduction

Rotator cuff injuries have garnered increasing research interest, with a multitude of suture techniques emerging. However, no consensus has been reached on the optimal method [1, 2]. While most studies and treatments focus on full-thickness rotator cuff tears, partial-thickness tears, especially those on the bursal side, are often overlooked and remain controversial in terms of the best treatment approach [3, 4]. Generally, partial-thickness tears are converted to full-thickness tears before repair, which can damage normal cuff tissue. Typically, repairs are considered for partial-thickness tears exceeding 50% of tendon thickness, although this decision is influenced by patient age, activity level, tendon quality, and surgeon skill [5].

Surgical options for partial-thickness tears include arthroscopic debridement or repair, with or without acromioplasty. Some surgeons advocate converting the tear to a full-thickness tear and then repairing it using traditional methods, with satisfactory clinical results reported [6, 7]. Others support the use of trans-tendon repair techniques, which preserve the intact bursal-side cuff tendon [8, 9]. The double-locking loop stitching technique (DLLST), as a type of trans-tendon repair, is expected to restore the rotator cuff footprint while preserving the integrity of the bursal side. The DLLST has evolved from double-row repair and transosseous tunnel fixation techniques. Its core mechanism involves the integration of medial row anchors with lateral row bridging fixation, establishing a dual-layer locking construct. In contrast, conventional full-thickness rotator cuff repair techniques, with single-row and double-row anchor fixation as the most representative approaches, have a well-documented developmental trajectory: the concept of single-row anchor fixation, a cornerstone of these techniques, can be traced back to the early era of arthroscopic surgery. Over the past few decades, this class of repair methods has undergone a continuous minimally invasive evolutionary process, transitioning sequentially from traditional open repair to mini-open repair, and ultimately advancing to the current all-arthroscopic repair paradigm—an evolution driven by the clinical demand for reduced surgical trauma, accelerated postoperative recovery, and improved patient compliance. However, no studies have compared the clinical outcomes and tendon integrity between the DLLST and traditional repair techniques for partial-thickness bursal-side rotator cuff tears (PT-BRCTs). The aim of this study was to prospectively compare the clinical outcomes and tendon integrity potential of arthroscopic double-locking loop stitching and traditional repair techniques. We hypothesized that for PT-BRCTs exceeding 50% of tendon thickness, arthroscopic repair would provide satisfactory improvements in shoulder function and pain relief, regardless of the technique used.

Methods

This study included 110 patients who underwent arthroscopic repair for PT-BRCTs between January 2022 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and all patients provided informed consent. PT-BRCTs were diagnosed via preoperative MRI and confirmed during arthroscopic surgery. The depth of the tear and the size of the exposed footprint area were determined using known-sized arthroscopic instruments (a 4.5-mm diameter full-radius shaver and a probe with a tip diameter of 4 mm).

Inclusion criteria: ① patients with trauma or chronic cumulative injury, presenting with clinical symptoms such as shoulder pain and limited activity, with ineffective non-surgical treatment (conservative treatment for more than 3 months), and preoperative physical examination consistent with manifestations of rotator cuff injury, such as positive Jobe's test and Neer's test; ② MRI examination showing partial-thickness tear of the rotator cuff, Snyder Description Type III (more severe rotator cuff injury that includes fraying and fragmentation of tendon fibers, often involving the entire surface of a cuff tendon [most commonly the supraspinatus]; usually < 3 cm) or Snyder Description Type IV (very severe partial tear that usually contains, in addition to fraying and fragmentation of tendon tissue, a sizable flap tear and often involves more than one tendon) [10, 11]; ③ patients willing to follow up and cooperate, with a follow-up duration of more than 1 year.

Exclusion criteria: ① previous history of other shoulder surgery or joint infection; ② history of previous upper limb nerve injury or deltoid dysfunction, and severe shoulder instability; ③ according to MRI images, the standard for fatty infiltration in the subscapularis muscle is Goutallier type IV; ④ full-thickness rotator cuff tears; ⑤ poor cardiopulmonary function for surgery; ⑥ history of mental illness and inability to cooperate well; ⑦ patients who have no intention of rehabilitation or cannot be followed up.

During the study period, 110 patients with partial-thickness cuff tears who met the inclusion criteria underwent arthroscopic treatment at this institution. Patients were randomly assigned to two groups based on the repair technique. Randomization was performed by an independent researcher using a computer-generated random sequence, and the code for each patient's group assignment was revealed to the surgeon at the time of surgery. Group I underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair using the DLLST. Twelve patients withdrew from the study due to skin allergy (1 case), postoperative corticosteroid injection into the joint (3 cases), loss to follow-up (1 case), non-compliance with rehabilitation training (3 cases), or refusal to participate (4 cases), leaving 43 patients who completed the follow-up after surgery. Group II underwent traditional arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Four patients withdrew from the study due to postoperative corticosteroid injection into the joint (2 cases), loss to follow-up (1 case), or refusal to participate (1 case), leaving 51 patients who completed the follow-up after surgery. All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team at one institution. No patients received corticosteroid injections into the shoulder joint during the postoperative rehabilitation period.

All patients completed a survey that included information on symptom duration, trauma history, and other demographic details, such as age, gender, and dominant arm. All patients followed the same postoperative rehabilitation protocol. Clinical assessments were regularly conducted preoperatively, at 3 months postoperatively, and at the final follow-up. Postoperative clinical and radiological outcomes were evaluated and compared between the two groups. Shoulder range of motion was measured using a goniometer. Functional outcomes were assessed using the VAS, ASES, and Constant shoulder scores. Finally, patient satisfaction with surgery was evaluated using the 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12) [12]. Data collection for clinical assessment and functional outcome tests was performed by an assistant physician who was blinded to the surgical procedure. All patients underwent preoperative MRI to identify partial-thickness rotator cuff tears and postoperative MRI at follow-up to assess tendon integrity. MRI results were evaluated by a musculoskeletal radiologist who was not involved in the study and was blinded to the surgical procedure (Fig 1).

Surgical technique

Patient positioning and surgical approach

The surgery was performed under brachial plexus and general anesthesia. Patients were secured in a lateral decubitus position using a pelvic fixation device on a standard operating table, with the affected shoulder facing up. The non-operated arm was positioned in abduction, with the knees flexed and a sponge cushion placed between them. The head was kept in a neutral position. Under anesthesia, the range of motion and adhesion of the affected shoulder were first assessed and manually released if necessary. The limb was then placed in a traction device with a shoulder distractor applying 6.6 pounds of traction force. The trunk was tilted backward by approximately 30°, with the affected limb abducted at about 70° and flexed forward by 20°. After positioning the patient, bony landmarks and surgical approaches of the shoulder joint were marked with a marker pen. Typically, four surgical approaches were used: anterior, anterolateral, lateral, and posterior approaches. The posterior approach was usually established about 1–2 cm medially and 1–2 cm inferior to the posterolateral corner of the acromion (approximately at the position of the posterior “soft spot”), serving as the viewing portal. Under observation through the posterior approach, a guide needle was inserted above the lateral part of the subscapularis muscle and medial to the bicipital tendon sheath to establish the anterior approach, which is generally located at the lateral edge of the coracoid process. The anterolateral approach was usually established about 2 cm lateral to the anterolateral corner of the acromion. The lateral approach was established about 1 cm lateral to the midportion of the acromion. Additional approaches could be used as required during the surgery (Fig. 2).

Glenohumeral joint assessment

With the posterior approach as the viewing portal, the arthroscope was inserted. Using the anterior approach as the working portal, an arthroscopic shaver or radiofrequency plasma knife was inserted. The anatomical landmarks were identified in the following order: bicipital tendon, subscapularis tendon, long head of the biceps tendon, superior labrum, labrum–ligament complex, humeral head, articular surface of the rotator cuff, and posterior structures of the shoulder joint. These structures were sequentially inspected and debrided or repaired as needed. If the long head of the biceps tendon was inflamed or torn, an anterosuperolateral approach was established, and the biceps tendon was managed according to the patient’s age and activity level, either by tenotomy or tenodesis. If the joint capsule was severely adhered, it was released, and other intra-articular lesions were addressed.

Subacromial decompression and acromioplasty

After completing the procedures in the glenohumeral joint, the arthroscope was withdrawn, and the puncture cone was reinserted. The arthroscope was then inserted into the subacromial space along the posterior edge of the acromion. In the subacromial space, a shaver (Arthrex, Florida, America) and a radiofrequency plasma knife (Arthrex, Florida, America) were used to remove hypertrophic synovium and inflamed adhesions. The impingement between the rotator cuff and the acromion was observed under arthroscopy. Further exploration was conducted to identify osteophytes on the greater tuberosity of the humerus and the coracoacromial ligament, as well as the rotator cuff tear. For patients with Neer type II and III acromion and osteophytes at the insertion of the coracoacromial ligament, acromioplasty was performed. The hypertrophic coracoacromial ligament was first trimmed with a radiofrequency device, followed by the insertion of a bur from the lateral or anterolateral approach. The lateral and anterior edges of the acromion were gradually shaved from outside to inside and from front to back, shaping the undersurface of the acromion and rounding the lateral edge of the acromion.

Double-locking loop stitching technique

DLLST was used to repair the rotator cuff in Group I patients. The anterior, anterolateral, and lateral approaches were used for surgical manipulation. A blue cannula (Arthrex, Florida, America) was placed through the anterolateral approach. The first step involved using a suture shuttling device (Arthrex, Florida, America) to pass the first FiberWire® suture horizontally through the torn rotator cuff tissue, from the medial edge of the tear, starting from the articular side and passing through to the subacromial space (Fig. 3a). The second step involved passing the other free end of the suture through the rotator cuff tissue at a position approximately 10 mm away from the first exit point. The middle part of the suture formed a loop on the surface of the rotator cuff (Fig. 3b). In the third step, the second FiberWire® suture was passed through the rotator cuff tissue in the same manner. The exit point of the first suture was approximately 5 mm away from the entry point of the second suture (Fig. 3c). In the fourth step, the two free ends of the first FiberWire® suture were pulled through both loops (Fig. 3d). In the fifth step, the two free ends of the second FiberWire® suture were pulled through its own loop (Fig. 3e). In the sixth step, the free ends of both FiberWire® sutures were tightened to form a double-locking loop and double-triangular structure (Fig. 3f). In the seventh step, the four free ends of the sutures were fixed with a single SwiveLock® C anchor (Arthrex, Florida, America), which was inserted into the outer lower part of the greater tuberosity, forming a triangular “pyramid”-shaped suture bridge (Fig. 3g).

Traditional rotator cuff repair technique

The traditional double-row suture bridge repair technique was used to repair the rotator cuff in Group II patients. When using the traditional repair technique, a shaver was used to convert the partial tear into a full-thickness tear from the corresponding bursal side. After completing the partial tear of the rotator cuff, the greater tuberosity was decorticated with a round burr to remove soft tissue from the footprint area. A double-suture anchor and a SwiveLock® C anchor (Arthrex, Florida, America) were placed in the footprint area of the greater tuberosity for suture bridge repair.

Postoperative rehabilitation

The rehabilitation program is typically divided into four stages, with gradually increasing load training for the shoulder joint in each stage. The goal of the first stage (0–4 weeks) is to reduce stress on the repaired tendon and promote early healing. The rehabilitation method involves wearing a shoulder abduction brace in daily life and strengthening active exercises of adjacent joints such as the hand, wrist, forearm, and elbow. Appropriate passive shoulder movements are also performed. The goal of the second stage (4–8 weeks) is to provide certain stress stimulation to the tendons, prevent early joint adhesion, and promote the recovery of proprioception of the shoulder joint. The rehabilitation method involves active shoulder joint training and the establishment of a correct movement pattern. The goal of the third stage (8–12 weeks) is to strengthen the muscle strength of the rotator cuff muscles, improve the stability of the shoulder joint and scapula, and establish a good exercise pattern. The rehabilitation method involves resistance strength training for the peripheral shoulder muscles. The goal of the fourth stage (12 to 16 weeks) is to restore daily living abilities as much as possible and return to exercise. The rehabilitation method involves further strengthening of strength training and special training for returning to the sports field [13, 14].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. Quantitative variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between the two groups were made using the independent samples t-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies, and comparisons between groups were made using the χ2 test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January 2022 and December 2023, a total of 94 patients with PT-BRCTs underwent surgical treatment. Of these, 43 patients underwent the DLLST (Group I), and 51 patients underwent traditional rotator cuff repair (Group II). The mean follow-up duration was 15.11 ± 1.75 months in Group I and 16.03 ± 4.77 months in Group II, with a mean age of 58.79 ± 8.96 years in Group I and 57.24 ± 9.83 years in Group II. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of categorical variables (P > 0.05). Table 1 provides the demographic data of the patients.

Clinical outcomes

Both groups showed significant improvements in shoulder range of motion, VAS scores, shoulder function scores, and SF-12 quality-of-life scores preoperatively and postoperatively (Table 2). However, Group I experienced faster pain relief at the 3-month follow-up compared to Group II (VAS score: Group I, 2.19 ± 1.33; Group II, 2.67 ± 0.99; P = 0.023). At the final follow-up, both groups achieved pain relief, with no significant differences found between the groups (P = 0.802). Group I had a preoperative mean forward flexion of 101.43 ± 18.59°, abduction of 90.47 ± 23.79°, external rotation of 46.26 ± 13.52°, and internal rotation of 38.98 ± 7.92°. These ranges of motion improved significantly at the final follow-up (all P < 0.05), with forward flexion reaching 137.47 ± 29.37°, abduction 137.42 ± 25.54°, external rotation 66.86 ± 14.62°, and internal rotation 58.42 ± 6.85°. Group II also showed significant improvements in forward flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation at the final follow-up (all P < 0.05). At the final follow-up, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of range of motion (P > 0.05).

Functional outcomes improved significantly in both groups after arthroscopic repair. At the final follow-up, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of quality-of-life scores (P > 0.05). MRI scans of some patients showed complete structural integrity of the repaired subscapularis tendon after surgery (Fig. 4). No retears were found on imaging or based on symptoms. Discussion

Rotator cuff injuries are among the most common causes of shoulder pain and functional impairment, with an increasing incidence in the middle-aged and elderly populations [15, 16]. For PT-BRCTs exceeding 50% of tendon thickness, most do not heal spontaneously and are prone to progression to more severe full-thickness tears over time. Therefore, surgical intervention is often preferred by most surgeons [17, 18]. This study demonstrates that regardless of the repair technique used, arthroscopic repair of PT-BRCTs provides satisfactory improvements in shoulder function and pain relief. The DLLST not only achieves complete tendon integrity, but also results in slightly faster recovery of shoulder function. Traditional arthroscopic techniques involve converting partial tears to full-thickness tears before repair. This method has been reported to yield satisfactory clinical outcomes and high patient satisfaction [19, 20]. Kamath et al. supported the arthroscopic conversion of partial-thickness rotator cuff tears to full-thickness tears because this approach allows better access to the tendon footprint and removal of degenerative tissue, which may facilitate tendon freshening and promote healing [21]. In this study, the 51 patients treated with the traditional method experienced a significant reduction in pain intensity from 5.76 ± 1.52 preoperatively to 0.78 ± 0.78 at the final follow-up, with ASES scores improving from 40.98 ± 14.96 to 89.95 ± 3.24 and Constant scores from 40.88 ± 12.88 to 90 ± 2.76. Converting partial tears to full-thickness tears for arthroscopic repair is a relatively straightforward method for restoring the tendon footprint. However, despite the satisfactory clinical outcomes of this traditional technique, it often involves resecting structurally sound bursal-side cuff tissue, and the integrity of the repaired tendon is a major concern. Moreover, disrupting the integrity of the cuff tissue may lead to non-anatomical restoration of the tendon footprint, thereby altering the normal biomechanical properties of the rotator cuff.

With advancements in arthroscopic repair techniques, partial tears of the rotator cuff can now be repaired without compromising tendon integrity. Trans–tendon repair techniques make it possible to anatomically restore the entire tendon–bone structure of the footprint while preserving the intact bursal layer of the torn cuff. The DLLST is a type of trans-tendon repair. In this study, the 43 patients treated with the DLLST were satisfied with the clinical outcomes and achieved complete tendon integrity. However, some studies have reported clinical discomfort in patients with partial-thickness bursal-side tears after trans-tendon repair. Recent studies have concluded that shoulder discomfort after trans-tendon repair is caused by suture traction on the bursal layer, leading to imbalanced tension within the tendon. Although there are no clinical studies on the ideal surgical technique for PT-BRCTs, cadaveric studies have shown that preserving the intact cuff tendon provides better biomechanical properties [22, 23]. In a cadaveric study comparing trans-tendon repair and full-thickness conversion repair for PT-BRCTs, trans-tendon repair was found to have significantly smaller gap formation and higher maximum load to failure. This study indicates that trans-tendon repair is biomechanically superior to repair after conversion to a full-thickness tear [23].

Depending on the type, severity, and degree of fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles, different surgeons may choose different surgical plans based on personal experience and technical skills. The ideal suture repair method should be simple, economical, and robust, achieving the best suture effect with the fewest anchors and sutures. The trans-tendon loop suture technique, which has recently gained popularity, has been widely used in the repair of rotator cuff injuries. Studies have shown that this technique is highly successful in repairing partial-thickness rotator cuff tears and small to medium-sized full-thickness tears, reducing the rate of retear after surgery [24, 25]. Moreover, the loop structure, which uses fewer anchors and no knots, significantly reduces the probability of anchor dislodgement and increased tear size due to excessive suture tension. The DLLST, which is a trans-tendon suture, only requires the use of traditional suture instruments. It forms a double-locking triangular structure through double-suture traction, providing a gathering and fixation effect on the torn area without the need for a complicated knot-tying process.

The DLLST uses a single outer-row anchor fixed in the footprint area. The procedure is clear and simple, reducing surgical time and making it easier for beginners to master. This repair technique essentially involves creating two overlapping locks with two FiberWire® sutures in the partially damaged area of the rotator cuff and fixing the damaged area to the footprint using a single SwiveLock® C anchor. This suture technique overcomes many of the problems associated with other repair techniques. When suturing, two FiberWire® sutures are passed through the damaged area of the rotator cuff, forming two locks. These two locks create a “double loop lasso”, distributing the cutting force of the suture on the tendon and avoiding stress concentration at a single suture point, which can lead to suture cutting through the tendon. Mitchell et al. [26] studied the medial meniscus posterior horn in cadaveric knee joints and found that a similar “double lock loop” suture technique provided immediate failure load comparable to that of the natural state of the medial meniscus posterior horn after suture. The DLLST uses horizontal mattress sutures and vertical longitudinal sutures that intersect to prevent suture cutting through the tendon while providing strong fixation. Moreover, this suture technique creates a woven suture bridge in the damaged area of the rotator cuff, forming a “pyramid” shape.

From a biomechanical perspective, adequate tendon–bone contact area and good biomechanical fixation are two important factors for rotator cuff healing after repair. Enhancing these factors can improve the healing rate after rotator cuff repair [27]. The “pyramid” structure provides greater contact area between the tendon and the greater tuberosity, which is important for promoting postoperative healing of the rotator cuff. The DLLST can produce a suture bridge-like fixation effect with simple sutures and without knot-tying, resulting in a smooth surface of the rotator cuff that reduces the possibility of postoperative impingement and friction. In particular, for larger partial-thickness rotator cuff tears, where conversion to a full-thickness tear for repair would cause significant damage and other suture techniques are not applicable, the DLLST shows greater advantages. First, the large “pyramid” structure provides sufficient tendon–bone contact area, similar to a double-row repair. Second, the anatomical restoration of the footprint does not damage the tendon or affect blood supply, providing better contact pressure and tendon compression, similar to the suture bridge technique. Third, the unique “pyramid” shape gives the structure more stability and superior performance. From an economic standpoint, compared to double-row or suture bridge techniques, the DLLST uses only one anchor and conventional operating instruments. The simple procedure reduces surgical time and costs, offering higher cost-effectiveness.

There is currently a lack of sufficient clinical data on the clinical outcomes of loop suture techniques. The first clinical reports on the originally described loop suture technique were recently published by Miyazaki et al. [28] and Hawi et al. [29]. Unlike our study, they used a single loop suture structure rather than a double-locking loop structure. Moreover, they did not use functional scores, health-related, or disease-specific quality-of-life scores to assess patients' clinical outcomes after repair. Opsomer et al. [30] reported that the double-layer lasso loop repair technique is an effective method, but it is relatively complex and cumbersome, not suitable for partial rotator cuff tears or for beginners. The DLLST is similar to the lasso-mattress stitch rather than the simple lasso-loop stitch. Although the DLLST has not yet been biomechanically studied, Liodakis et al. [31] found in a biomechanical study that the lasso-mattress and simple-cinch stitches had similar biomechanical properties, with significantly higher tensile loads required for failure than the lasso-loop stitch. This indirectly suggests that the DLLST also has good suture fixation effects, but this needs further biomechanical research.

Limitations

The use of SwiveLock® C anchors and FiberWire® sutures for arthroscopic repair of partial rotator cuff tears can achieve good clinical outcomes and tendon–bone healing. However, our study has several limitations: ① cases were not grouped and observed based on the size of the partial rotator cuff tear. Snyder type I and II cases have not been studied. It is currently not possible to assess the suture effectiveness of this technique for full-thickness small to medium-sized tears. ② Isolated partial rotator cuff tears have a relatively low incidence rate. Postoperative functional recovery is not only affected by the rotator cuff tear but also by the repair of other shoulder joint structures. This study did not fully consider the impact of repairs of other structures on shoulder function. ③ However, these data were collected from patients treated by surgeons at the same hospital. This study may be limited by selection bias inherent to its single-center design.④ The follow-up period was relatively short. ⑤ Most patients had low demands for activities of daily living.

Conclusion

Both techniques provided satisfactory functional recovery and pain relief for PT-BRCTs exceeding 50% of tendon thickness. However, DLLST achieved faster functional restoration while preserving tendon integrity, suggesting its potential as a biomechanically advantageous alternative.
