Causal correlation between inflammatory cytokines and hypertrophic scar based on two-sample Mendelian randomization
Authors: Lujia Mao, Juan Huang, Yixun Zhang, Weiqiang Zhang, Xiaoxiang Wang, Ronghua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

HS is a histopathological status that occurs during the wound healing process, characterized by abnormal changes in normal skin tissue1. Typically, it presents as red, raised scars at the site of healing2. HS is a common complication in wound healing and is marked by irregular shape, firmness, erythema, excessive fibrosis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition3. When hypertrophic scars are large and lack elasticity, they can severely restrict the movement of various body parts, such as joints, limbs, and facial areas, significantly affecting both the appearance and quality of life of patients4. Although the precise processes that contribute to the creation of hypertrophic scars are not yet completely understood, studies have suggested that abnormal fibroblast migration, excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix, and prolonged inflammatory responses are closely associated with HS formation, particularly chronic inflammation5, 6, 7. Thus, regulating the mechanisms associated with HS formation has become a key focus of current research on its prevention and treatment.

Currently, treatment options for hypertrophic scars include pressure therapy, localized corticosteroid injections, laser therapy, radiation, and surgery8, 9. However, the overall effectiveness remains suboptimal. A significant amount of investigation has revealed that many inflammatory factors and cytokines, such as interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-6, IL-10 and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), play crucial roles in regulating fibroblast proliferation and metabolism during wound healing10, 11, 12. CCL4 can attenuate fibrosis progression by modulating cell-associated pathways, thereby reducing fibrosis and inflammatory responses13. Despite these therapeutic efforts, once hypertrophic scars have formed, non-surgical treatments are often unable to fully remove them, highlighting the importance of early intervention and preventive measures.

Inflammatory cytokines exert immunosuppressive effects by modulating the activity of immune cells infiltrating hypertrophic scars14, 15. Persistent pro-inflammatory responses may impair wound healing, while both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines are essential during acute healing of wounds16, 17, 18. Therefore, progressive modulation of both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines may represent an effective tactic to promote the healing of chronic wounds. These cytokines play critical roles in both the acute and chronic inflammatory responses involved in wound healing and in regulating hypertrophic scar formation19. A deeper understanding of their mechanisms of action will enhance our comprehension of the pathophysiology of hypertrophic scarring. However, research on the specific roles of individual inflammatory cytokines whenever hypertrophic scars are formed remains limited, so it is crucial to investigate their mechanisms and therapeutic potential further in order to develop innovative treatment strategies. This study compares the effects of CCL4 and IL-2 on hypertrophic scar formation to reveal their potential causal relationship with scar development.

This study utilises Mendelian randomization (MR) to overcome all of these challenges. MR is a method of statistical analysis that investigates the causal associations between factors related to exposure and target diseases using genetic variants as instrumental variables20. In contrast to conventional observational research and trials with randomized control, MR offers significant advantages by reducing confounding bias and ethical limitations21. The objective in the present research is to figure out whether inflammatory cytokines and HS are causally related. Specifically, we will use genetic variants correlated to these cytokines as instrumental variables to consider to infer the possible impact of inflammatory cytokines on the development of HS, providing a theoretical basis for developing of novel treatment strategies meant to enhance the clinical management of hypertrophic scarring.

Materials employed and methodology

Research framework

The current research looked into the causal connection between inflammatory cytokines and HS utilizing a TSMR technique. We utilized a two-sample MR design, leveraging summary-level data from GWAS to obtain genetic data on inflammatory cytokines and HS. Inflammatory cytokines were treated as the exposure variable, while HS was the outcome variable. A Diagrammatic sketch of the general MR design is presented in Fig. 1. We formulated three core hypotheses for the genetic variants: (1) The variable of exposure (inflammatory cytokines) is substantially correlated with single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as suggested by the association hypothesis; (2) SNPs in the exposure-outcome pathway are unaffected by other variables which is called the Independence hypothesis; (3) Exclusivity hypothesis: The exposure variable is the only means by which that SNPs influence the result, no additional pathways are involved22, 23.Fig. 1Overview of the assumptions and analysis of Mendelian randomization.

Overview of the assumptions and analysis of Mendelian randomization.

Data sources

For GWAS analysis, SNPs corresponding to inflammatory cytokines were chosen as instrumental variables (IVs). Data on 41 inflammatory cytokines were obtained from a meta-analysis of GWAS conducted in three separate population cohorts, comprising 8293 Finnish individuals. These cohorts included the Young Finns Study (YFS), FINRISK 1997, and FINRISK 200224. The HS data came from the Finngen information set that contains 766 cases and 207,482 controls, all of which have European ancestry(data source: https://www.finngen.fi/en)25. These diverse and comprehensive data sources provided a solid foundation for a thorough investigation into how inflammatory cytokines and HS are related.

Instrumental variable selection

To determine possible IVs, we first selected SNPs associated with inflammatory cytokines. We selected SNPs with a significant association (P < 5.0 × 10−5 with the exposure variable from the GWAS data. Suitable instrumental variables must meet strict quality control criteria, including maintaining an r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window to reduce linkage disequilibrium (LD) effects and ensure SNP independence. Additionally, we used PhenoScanner V2 to further validate the selected SNPs, ensuring that the exposure variable is the only way in which these SNPs influence the result. Finally, we calculated the F-statistic (F=β2/SE2), where β is the effect size and SE is the standard error, ensuring that the F-statistic exceeds 10 to avoid weak instrument bias26.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Our study employed a two-sample MR analysis to examine the causative relationship between inflammatory cytokines and the risk of HS. Common MR analysis methods IVW was the core and most frequently used approach27. Other methods such as MR-Egger regression, weighted median method and so on, which were used for supplementary analyses. The IVW technique offers a total evaluation of the impact of inflammatory cytokines on the risk of HS by combining the Wald estimates obtained from every SNP. The IVW estimate is the inverse-variance weighted mean of the ratio estimates from all valid instrumental SNPs, assuming that all SNPs are valid instruments (or null if there is no overall bias). MR-Egger regression can estimate causal relationships even when no valid instruments are present. The weighted median technique can produce reliable estimates even in cases when as much as 50 % of the instruments are invalid. Each and every outcome in this study was presented as an odds ratio (OR) with 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI).

We conducted sensitivity evaluations to evaluate the MR analysis results' robustness. First, we excluded genetic variants that posed a risk of pleiotropy either biologically or statistically. After that, we concentrated on analyzing the MR-Egger regression's intercept term; if the intercept is close to zero, it suggests that there is no horizontal pleiotropy in our bidirectional MR analysis. We further assessed heterogeneity in the IVW approach by employing Cochran’s Q statistic along with funnel plots, which helped further test the consistency and reliability of the outcomes. Finally, we conducted reverse TSMR assessments to explore the presence of reverse causality. The reverse analysis followed the same procedure as the forward TSMR analysis.

Given the importance of statistical significance in interpreting results, we presented the results with relative 95 % CI to focus more on the magnitude, direction, and uncertainty of causal effects.

Experimental validation

Cell culture

The HS tissue samples utilized in this investigation came from the First People's Hospital of Guangzhou, with the collection process adhering to the moral guidelines specified in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the hospital's medical ethics council. Fibroblasts were isolated from HS tissue of patients and cultured to the 6th generation. These cells were kept in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) with antibiotics (Servicebio, China) and 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) added. Cell culture was performed at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, and every two days, the medium was changed. When the cell density was between 70 and 80 percent, passage was carried out.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

TRIzol reagent was utilized to extract total RNA, and RevertAid reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was applied to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). SYBR Green RT-PCR (Takara Bio, Japan) was utilized to perform quantitative PCR on the cDNA, with ACTIN as the reference gene. The comparative Ct method (2−△△CT) was used for calculating relative expression levels. Western Blotting (WB)

Protease inhibitor-containing RIPA buffer(Beyotime, China) was used to extract the total protein from HaCaT cells. A BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was implemented to measure the protein concentrations. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated on a 10 % SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5 % BSA and incubated with the primary antibody for a whole night at 4 °C. For one hour, the secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature. Bands of proteins were observed utilizing a gel imaging system (AI800, GE, USA) and analyzed with ImageJ software. The antibodies used were IL-2 (bs-10274R, 1:1000, Bioss), CCL4 (bs-1046R, 1:1000, Bioss), and GAPDH (A19056, 1:10,000, Abclonal).

Immunofluorescence assay

For immunofluorescence experiments, HaCaT and fibroblasts have been fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by PBS washing and 0.1 % Triton X-100/TBST permeabilization. Non-specific antigen binding sites were blocked using 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) to prevent background binding. Following the blocking step, the cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C to ensure optimal binding and specificity for the target antigens. The cells were then incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (goat anti-rabbit, Affinity, 1:500) for one hour at room temperature. Cells were subsequently treated with a secondary antibody linked to horseradish peroxidase (HRP), specifically goat anti-rabbit from Affinity, at a dilution of 1:500. This incubation was carried out for a duration of one hour at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Servicebio, China) for 10 min. Using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), allowing for high-resolution imaging of the stained nuclei.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and analyses were carried out by means of R software (version 4.3.3). To assess the differences between two groups, a Student’s t-test was employed. In this study, a P-value of < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant for all statistical tests.

Ethical statement

All summary-level data employed in this research were anonymized and publicly accessible data, and accessible for download. Moreover, all GWAS data involved in current research were sanctioned by the corresponding institutional ethics committees.

Results

Instrumental variables within TSMR

We performed quality control on the SNPs derived from GWAS. At the genome-wide significance level being 5 × 10−8, only 9 out of 41 inflammatory cytokines were significantly associated with three or more SNPs. For further MR analysis, we established the significance threshold to P < 5.0 × 10−5, r2 < 0.001, and kb = 10,000 to guarantee a sufficient amount of SNPs. We then selected SNPs significantly related to the exposure variables based on the resulting data. Weak instrumental variables weren't utilized, as indicated by the F-statistic values for the associated SNPs for each inflammatory cytokine being larger than 10.

Results of a TSMR analysis

Through TSMR analysis, the results demonstrated a strong correlation between inflammatory cytokines and HS risk. Both IVW and MR-Egger analyses showed that the combined effect of 9 SNPs related to interleukin-2 (IL-2) was greater than zero (Fig. 2A), suggesting that IL-2 acts as a deleterious exposure factor. Specifically, increased IL-2 gene expression has been correlated with a greater risk of developing HS. Experimental validation results

We performed experimental validation on fibroblasts isolated from normal skin and hypertrophic scar tissues. qRT-PCR results showed that IL-2 expression was higher in HS tissues compared to normal skin, whereas CCL4 expression was higher in normal skin tissues than in HS tissue. These experimental results suggest that IL-2 and CCL4 could serve as potential biomarkers for hypertrophic scarring and may be important therapeutic targets for treating HS. Our study found that the anti-inflammatory cytokine CCL4 was expressed at lower levels in HS tissue, while the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-2 was upregulated. Specifically, IL-2 was highly expressed in scar tissue, while CCL4 showed higher expression in normal tissue. These findings imply that pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-2, may promote scar formation by driving the inflammatory response. Therefore, reducing inflammation in scar tissue could represent an effective strategy for treating hypertrophic scars.

Discussion

In this study, we applied the TSMR approach and leveraged large-scale GWAS data to assess if inflammatory cytokines and HS are causally related. What we found revealed that the formation of hypertrophic scars is linked to the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-2, while the anti-inflammatory cytokine CCL4 is associated with the suppression of HS formation. Pleiotropy and heterogeneity tests confirmed the robustness of our results, with consistent directions of effect observed across different MR analysis methods, such as the IVW estimation28. This research offers innovative insights and methodologies for understanding the pathogenesis of HS and offers a theoretical foundation for further exploration of the pathological mechanisms involved in its formation.

HS are a common type of skin fibrosis, primarily caused by excessive inflammation and ECM deposition during the wound healing process29. The typical clinical manifestations of HS include erythema, pain, restricted movement, and even cosmetic disfigurement30. Beyond physical appearance, HS can also lead to negative psychological outcomes such as depression and anxiety, significantly impairing the quality of life of affected individuals31, 32, 33. Inflammatory cytokines are essential for the healing of wounds and subsequent chronic inflammation, according to recent studies34. With a deeper understanding of inflammatory cytokines, controlling the ratio of anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory factors and mitigating the inflammatory response at the wound site holds promise for reducing the incidence of hypertrophic scars35. Currently, treatments for HS include steroid injections, laser therapy, and surgical excision, but clinical outcomes remain limited, and relapse is often observed36, 37, 38. Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of HS and developing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies remain important clinical challenges.

Accumulating evidence suggests that HS shares tumor-like characteristics and is closely linked to dysregulated39, 40, 41. Inflammatory cytokines not only directly induce fibroblast proliferation but also stimulate connective tissue generation. Both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines are essential for acute wounds healing, and their balanced regulation is crucial for wound repair and minimizing HS formation42, 43. Numerous cytokines and growth factors, such as TGF-α, TGF-β, IL-1, and TNF-α, contribute to wound healing by modulating fibroblast growth and metabolism44. However, the precise mechanisms by which inflammatory cytokines contribute to HS formation remain to be further elucidated. Pro-inflammatory cytokines exacerbate the inflammatory response and promote scar formation, suggesting that reducing inflammation could be a viable strategy for improving hypertrophic scar healing45. Understanding the pathogenesis of HS, particularly the functions of cytokines that promote and inhibit inflammation, has important clinical implications.

One of the main factors thought to be responsible for the formation of HS is excessive inflammation. Anti-inflammatory therapies have been shown to be important strategies for preventing hypertrophic scars46. Compared to normal skin, IL-2 levels in hypertrophic scar sufferers' tissues are noticeably higher. IL-2, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, is involved in various inflammatory diseases and contributes to fibrosis-related conditions, including scar formation47. Previous studies suggest that IL-2 may promote scar formation through mechanisms such as enhancing the inflammatory response, excessive ECM production, and inhibiting fibroblast migration, invasion, transformation, and apoptosis48, 49. However, CCL4 is thought to be a possible HS protective factor. Research has demonstrated that CCL4 supports wound healing and preserves skin homeostasis in physiological settings, potentially reducing HS formation by modulating the inflammatory response50. we validated IL-2′s local expression in HS tissues, and further revealed that its interaction with CCL4 influences fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition. However, the role of CCL4 in hypertrophic scarring remains underexplored, and more research is needed to validate this finding, including larger numbers of samples and deeply mechanistic investigations.

By using the TSMR approach, this study minimizes the effects of confounding factors and reverse causality, while utilizing publicly available large-scale GWAS summary data that cover a broad population. Compared to traditional observational studies, the TSMR method provides higher statistical power and reliability. Our innovative findings of the divergent roles of IL-2 and CCL4 in HS formation offer new avenues for the treatment and intervention of hypertrophic scars51. However, the research has certain drawbacks. First, since the analysis used summary statistics rather than raw data and lacked detailed demographic and clinical information on the study patients, subgroup analysis could not be performed. Second, the GWAS data primarily came from European populations, which may introduce ethnic bias. Adjustments to the analysis method may be necessary when studying Asian or other populations. Third, only 41 inflammatory factors were included in this study, which may restrict the range of our results. Future studies ought to expand the range of inflammatory factors to explore their roles in HS formation. Lastly, although we investigated the roles of IL-2 and CCL4, the underlying mechanisms of their effects still require further exploration. Future studies should increase sample sizes and conduct multi-level, multidimensional analyses to provide an even more thorough explanation of how inflammatory cytokines function in HS formation and offer more theoretical support for the mechanistic research on hypertrophic scars.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Mendelian randomization was employed in this entire research to explore the relationship between 41 inflammatory cytokines and hypertrophic scars. By utilizing the TSMR method, we innovatively found that IL − 2 facilitates the formation of HS, whereas CCL4 hinders it. The presented findings offer new causal evidence regarding the mechanisms of scar formation. By doing so, it overcomes the drawbacks of traditional observational studies, which are susceptible to confounding factors, thereby enriching the cytokine regulation theories in the realm of scar research. Also, it uncovers the anti − scarring effect of CCL4, pinpointing potential targets for the clinical development of scar intervention strategies centered around the IL − 2/CCL4 pathway (such as CCL4 mimetics and IL − 2 antagonists). Ultimately, future investigations can verify their interaction mechanisms via in − vitro experiments and carry out large − scale cohort research to explore their viability as biomarkers for scar risk. This would progress the prevention and treatment of scars from empirical methods towards precision medicine.
