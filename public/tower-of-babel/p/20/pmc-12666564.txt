A β-lactamase inhibitory protein mutant displays high potency and a broad inhibition profile due to an altered binding mode with β-lactamases
Authors: Paola Rivera, Shuo Lu, Dignite Ngango, Banumathi Sankaran, Bidadi Venkataram Prasad, Timothy Palzkill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
β-lactamase enzymes inactivate β-lactam antibiotics, leading to drug resistance. The β-lactamase inhibitory protein (BLIP) is a naturally occurring inhibitor of β-lactamases, with inhibition constants (Ki) ranging from picomolar to micromolar values. For example, BLIP inhibits CTX-M-14 β-lactamase with a Ki of 330 nM, whereas the Ki for CTX-M-15 is 3 nM, despite CTX-M-14 and CTX-M-15 sharing 83% sequence identity. We used a genetic screen to identify a BLIP mutant, E73W, that potently inhibited CTX-M-14. Subsequent purification and testing of BLIP E73W revealed that it is a potent, broad-spectrum inhibitor of class A β-lactamases. We determined structures of BLIP E73W in complex with the CTX-M-14, CTX-M-15, and TEM-1 β-lactamases to investigate the basis of the broad-spectrum inhibition. Previous structures of BLIP in complex with several class A β-lactamases revealed that β-lactamase active site residue Tyr105 is found in an altered rotamer conformation. Also, in the case of the BLIP–CTX-M-15 complex, an altered conformation of the active site 103 to 106 loop is observed. In contrast, the BLIP E73W–β-lactamase complexes did not show the altered conformations of Tyr105 or the 103 to 106 loop. Instead, the mutant's mechanism involves BLIP Trp73 trapping Tyr105 against the wall of the active site in a similar conformation as in the apoenzyme. Interestingly, the E73W mutant binds the apo-enzyme conformation in all the BLIP E73W–β-lactamase complexes. Binding to the apo-enzyme conformation, which is expected to be highly populated in solution, as well as enhanced hydrophobic interactions of Trp73 with β-lactamases are possible explanations for the high potency and broad-spectrum inhibition.

β-lactams are the most prescribed class of antibiotics worldwide. Since the introduction of penicillin in the 1940s, additional classes of β-lactam antibiotics, including cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams, have been introduced to battle antimicrobial resistance (1). However, resistance is widespread to these agents, and drug development is not keeping pace with antimicrobial resistance. The cyclic amide β-lactam ring is conserved among all β-lactam antibiotics and is required for antimicrobial activity (1). A major resistance mechanism is the bacterial production of β-lactamases that catalyze the hydrolysis of β-lactam antibiotics. There are four classes of β-lactamases (A, B, C, and D) based on sequence homology and the identity of their active site amino acids (2). Class A β-lactamases are similar in sequence and structure and were first classified by Ambler et al. based on sequence conservation (3, 4, 5).

The class A β-lactamase family includes extended-spectrum β-lactamases such as CTX-M and variants of the TEM- and SHV-type enzymes. These enzymes are of clinical interest as they can hydrolyze oxyimino cephalosporins and monobactams (6, 7, 8). Furthermore, class A carbapenemases such as the widespread KPC-2 enzyme are a particular concern in that they can hydrolyze all classes of β-lactams, including the last-resort carbapenems such as imipenem and meropenem (6, 9). Finally, class A enzymes from Gram-positive bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus BlaZ β-lactamases, are a widespread source of resistance (10).

Many antimicrobial agents are natural products (11). Streptomyces clavuligerus is a soil bacterium that produces various antibiotics such as cephalosporin-type molecules and the widely used class A β-lactamase inhibitor, clavulanic acid (12). S. clavuligerus also produces the β-lactamase inhibitory protein (BLIP) that potently inhibits class A β-lactamases with inhibition constants ranging from micromolar to high picomolar (13, 14). For example, BLIP inhibits SHV-1 β-lactamase with a Ki of 1000 nM, CTX-M-14 β-lactamase with a Ki of 330 nM, CTX-M-15 with a Ki of 3 nM, and TEM-1 with a Ki of 0.5 nM (15, 16, 17, 18, 19). BLIP is a 17.5 kDa protein with two 76 amino acid tandem domains and a relatively flat structure (17). The available structures of class A β-lactamases in complex with BLIP reveal that the concave surface of the inhibitor binds to the convex surface of the enzyme, and a loop from each of the two BLIP domains extends into the β-lactamase active site. Given the wide range of inhibition potency shown by BLIP for class A β-lactamases, the question is what controls the binding specificity leading to picomolar or nanomolar as opposed to micromolar or weaker inhibition potency? X-ray structures are available for BLIP in complex with several β-lactamases, including TEM-1, SHV-1, KPC-2, and CTX-M-15 (15, 17, 20, 21). In all these structures, β-lactamases contain a protruding loop region that includes residues 103 to 106, and the side chain carboxylate oxygens of BLIP Glu73 hydrogen bond with the main chain NH groups of β-lactamase residues 105 and 106, which requires a conformation of the 103 to 106 loop that places the NH groups in the proper, exposed position for hydrogen bonding (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, in these structures, the side chain of Tyr/Trp105, which forms a wall of the active site in the apoenzymes, is found in an altered rotamer conformation angling down the active site.

Support for the importance of the conformation of the β-lactamase 103 to 106 loop in BLIP binding and specificity has come from the observation that in the apo-enzyme structure of CTX-M-15 β-lactamase, the peptide backbone between the residues Asn104 and Tyr105 orients so that the direction of the carbonyl oxygen of Asn104 and main-chain NH of Tyr105 are altered 180o compared with that in the TEM-1, SHV-1, and KPC-2 apoenzymes and the BLIP complexes with these enzymes (Fig. 1D) (15). This conformation difference results in the NH group of Tyr105 being inaccessible for hydrogen bonding with BLIP Glu73. However, in the structure of BLIP bound to the CTX-M-15 enzyme, the peptide backbone at Asn104 and Tyr105 reorients to the conformation observed in the TEM-1, SHV-1, and KPC-2 apoenzymes and BLIP complexes, which places the Tyr105 NH in position for hydrogen bonding, suggesting the orientation of the peptide bond linking Asn104 and Tyr105 allowing hydrogen bonding to BLIP Glu73 is critical for potent inhibition. Further support for this hypothesis was provided by the observation that the apo-enzyme structure of a natural variant of CTX-M-15 with an N106S substitution has a Asn104–Tyr105 backbone conformation like that in the TEM-1, SHV-1, and KPC-2 apoenzymes, and all BLIP–β-lactamase complexes with the Tyr105 NH positioned to hydrogen bond with BLIP Glu73 (22). As predicted if this conformation is important for BLIP binding, the CTX-M-15 N106S enzyme is inhibited by BLIP with enhanced potency compared with CTX-M-15 (15).

Here, we utilized a genetic screen to search libraries of BLIP mutants for those that potently inhibited CTX-M-14 β-lactamase. The original goal was to develop BLIP variants that had high affinity specifically for CTX-M enzymes as a tool for diagnostics. We identified the BLIP E73W mutant that potently inhibits both CTX-M-14 and CTX-M-15. However, we discovered that the mutant also potently inhibits all other class A enzymes tested. Structural results of BLIP E73W in complex with the CTX-M-14, CTX-M-15, and TEM-1 β-lactamases revealed that the mutant employs a different binding mode that is not dependent on the conformation of the 103 to 106 loop of β-lactamase. The structures highlight an alternate binding mode of BLIP that traps a β-lactamase conformation that closely resembles the structures of β-lactamase apoenzymes, suggesting that the higher potency may be related to the BLIP E73W mutant binding a β-lactamase conformation that is highly represented in solution as well as enhanced hydrophobic interactions of Trp73 with the highly conserved active site Tyr105 of β-lactamases.

Results

BLIP inhibits class A β-lactamases with Ki values ranging from micromolar to high picomolar (15). Although BLIP potently inhibits CTX-M-15 β-lactamase, with a Ki of 2.9 nM, it is a relatively weak inhibitor of CTX-M-14, with a Ki of 330 nM. The difference in BLIP inhibition potency between these enzymes is interesting, considering they share 83% sequence identity (15). We were intent on identifying BLIP variants that potently bound all CTX-M enzymes to shed light on the determinants of binding specificity to aid in the development of potential diagnostic tools.

Discovery of the BLIP E73W mutant as a potent, broad-spectrum inhibitor

We utilized a genetic screen to identify BLIP mutants with high affinity for CTX-M-14 β-lactamase. The screen consisted of inserting the gene for CTX-M-14 β-lactamase into the pyrF gene on the chromosome of Escherichia coli to maintain a stable, low copy number and introducing BLIP mutants into the strain on a plasmid (23, 24). Inhibition of β-lactamase in E. coli by a BLIP variant reduces the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ampicillin in an agar plate assay (23, 24). We previously used this approach to identify BLIP variants that bind to the KPC-2 carbapenemase but not the TEM-1 β-lactamase (23). For this study, we introduced the gene for CTX-M-14 into the same site on the chromosome in the pyrF gene as previously done with KPC-2 and TEM-1 to create E. coli strain TP159 (25).

BLIP mutants were introduced into E. coli TP159, and the MIC for ampicillin was determined for individual clones. The BLIP mutants tested were previously generated by codon randomization mutagenesis to create libraries of random mutants for positions Tyr50, Glu73, Lys74, Trp112, and Arg160 (24). These sites were chosen based on previous observations that mutations at these positions are often associated with changes in BLIP binding specificity (25, 26). We identified the BLIP E73H, E73Y, and E73W mutants from the Glu73 codon-randomized library that exhibited a lower ampicillin MIC than that observed for the TP159 strain containing the plasmid encoding WT BLIP. Each of these BLIP mutants was expressed and purified, and the Ki for inhibition of CTX-M-14 β-lactamase was determined using the colorimetric β-lactam substrate nitrocefin as a reporter. The resulting data were fit to the Morrison equation, which takes into account the KM of the enzyme for nitrocefin (see the Experimental procedures section) (23). Consistent with the results of the MIC assay, the Ki values for the BLIP mutants were lower than that for WT BLIP, and the E73W mutant was most potent with a Ki of 5 ± 1 nM. Based on its 60-fold higher potency than WT BLIP, we focused on the BLIP E73W mutant for further characterization.

Biochemical and structural characterization of BLIP E73W binding interactions

We next examined the inhibition potency of BLIP E73W versus other class A β-lactamases to assess its specificity profile (Table 1, Fig. 2) (see the Experimental procedures section). The E73W mutant inhibited the CTX-M-15 enzyme with a 20-fold enhancement in potency relative to WT BLIP (Table 1). Furthermore, BLIP E73W inhibited the TEM-1 β-lactamase with eightfold greater potency and KPC-2 with sevenfold improved potency. Finally, WT BLIP weakly inhibits SHV-1 β-lactamase with a Ki of 1600 nM, whereas BLIP E73W shows 600-fold enhanced potency (Table 1). Therefore, while WT BLIP exhibits a wide range in inhibition potencies for these enzymes (0.2–1600 nM), the E73W variant displays a broad spectrum of activity and is a potent inhibitor of all enzymes tested, with Ki values in the narrow range of 0.03 nM to 2.4 nM (Table 1). As indicated above, we used inhibition constant determinations (Ki) to measure BLIP potency and assume this also is reflective of the binding affinity between BLIP and β-lactamases. Consistent with this assumption, it has been shown previously by our group and others that Ki values and KD values of WT BLIP and mutants determined by on and off rates as well as isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) are similar (18, 23, 27, 28).

To further validate our findings, we performed ITC experiments to measure binding constants of various class A β-lactamases to BLIP WT and to BLIP E73W (Fig. S1). A binding constant of 325 nM was obtained by ITC for BLIP with CTX-M-14, which is consistent with the inhibition constant of 340 nM determined for this pair. In addition, the binding constant obtained from ITC experiments (1.2 μM) for BLIP with SHV-1 closely matches the Ki obtained from inhibition assays (1.6 μM). For BLIP and BLIP E73W interactions with β-lactamases that have low nM Ki values, we were not able to accurately measure binding affinities. Overall, the binding affinities obtained through ITC, although a small sample size, support the accuracy of the inhibitor assays as an indicator of affinity.

The molecular basis of the broadly enhanced potency of BLIP E73W is of interest, and we addressed this question by structural analysis of BLIP E73W–β-lactamase protein complexes using X-ray crystallography (Table 3). CTX-M-15 β-lactamase was cocrystallized with BLIP E73W, and the structure of the complex was determined at 1.6 Å resolution (Figs. 3F, 4D and S3). As noted, we previously determined the structure of the complex of CTX-M-15 with WT BLIP, revealing a conformational change in the residue 103 to 106 region of the protruding loop of β-lactamase whereby the orientations of Val103 and Asn104 as well as of Asn104 and Tyr105 are altered because of changes in their phi–psi angles. In addition, the Tyr105 side chain adopts an altered rotamer conformation with the side chain angled down the active site (15) (Figs. 3A, 4A and 5G). It was found by previous molecular dynamics simulations that these changes are critical for BLIP binding in that they allow the formation of hydrogen bonds between the side-chain carboxylate oxygens of BLIP Glu73 and the main-chain NH of β-lactamase residues Tyr105 and Asn106 (15) (Figs. 3A and 5G). These hydrogen bonds are found in all BLIP–class-A β-lactamase structures (Fig. 3, A–D). Interestingly, the conformational change of the CTX-M-15 protruding loop seen in the structure of the enzyme in complex with WT BLIP, wherein the Val103–Asn104 and Asn104–Tyr105 residues have altered orientations, is not observed (compare Fig. 5G and 5B). Presumably, tight binding of BLIP E73W nevertheless occurs because there is no requirement for positioning of the main-chain NH of residues 105 and 106 for hydrogen bonding to BLIP Glu73. Instead, BLIP Trp73 engages in hydrophobic interactions with a potential pi–pi stacking interaction (∼3.7 Å) with β-lactamase residue Tyr105 (Figs. 3F and 4D). We also determined the structure of CTX-M-14 β-lactamase in complex with BLIP E73W (Figs. 3E, 4F and S3). The structure is very similar to the CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W structure with minimal change in the conformation of the protruding loop of CTX-M-14 compared with the apo-enzyme structure, including the orientation of the carbonyl and amide groups of the peptide bond linking Asn104–Tyr105 (Fig. 5, A and D). As with the CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W structure, in the CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W structure, Trp73 extends into the active site and traps the β-lactamase Tyr105 side chain against Pro107 (Figs. 3E, 4F). Furthermore, as observed in the CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W structure, the position of the Tyr105 side chain is shifted toward the wall of the active site compared with its position in the apoenzyme, and the Tyr105 hydroxyl forms a new hydrogen bond with the main chain C=O of Tyr129 that is not present in the apoenzyme (Figs. 6 and S2). There is no structure of CTX-M-14–BLIP available for comparison. Still, based on the weak potency of BLIP inhibition, it likely maintains the same conformation of the protruding loop structure as in the apoenzyme, which is not conducive to hydrogen bonding with BLIP Glu73.

We next determined the structure of BLIP E73W in complex with TEM-1 β-lactamase (Figs. 3G, 4E and S3). The structure is similar to the CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W and CTX-M-14–BLIP E73W structures with little change in the conformation of the protruding loop of TEM-1 compared with the apo-TEM-1 structure (Fig. 5, C and F). However, note that the 103 to 106 region of the TEM-1 apoenzyme protruding loop structure is in a different conformation than that of the apo-CTX-M-15 and apo-CTX-M-14 (compare Fig. 5F with Fig. 5, D and E). In the TEM-1 structure, because of differences in phi–psi angles of Asn104–Tyr105, the orientation of the peptide unit linking these residues is altered from that observed for the CTX-M enzymes. Specifically, the carbonyl oxygen of Asn104 is pointed down into the loop, and the NH of Tyr105 is oriented up becoming accessible for hydrogen bonding. In the TEM-1 structure, the main-chain NH groups of Tyr105 and Asn106 are positioned optimally for forming hydrogen bonds with the carboxylate of Glu73 in WT BLIP, which is consistent with the potent inhibition of TEM-1 by WT BLIP (Fig. 2). In contrast, in the structure with BLIP E73W, such hydrogen bonding interactions are not possible as Trp73 extends into the active site pushing the Tyr105 side chain against Pro107 as observed for the BLIP E73W–CTX-M structures (Fig. 4E). The observation that the protruding loop of the TEM-1 enzyme has a different conformation than that of the CTX-M enzymes in the complexes with BLIP E73W indicates that the mutant BLIP does not discriminate between β-lactamases based on the 103 to 106 region of the protruding loop as does WT BLIP. This contributes to the broad binding profile of BLIP E73W compared with WT BLIP.

Removal of β-lactamase Tyr105 leads to broad spectrum inhibition by BLIP and BLIP E73W

It is evident from the cocrystal structures that the rotamer conformation of Tyr-105 of β-lactamase distinguishes the inhibition modes of BLIP–β-lactamase versus BLIP E73W–β-lactamase complexes since the conformation of the Tyr105 side chain is similar among all WT BLIP structures and among all BLIP E73W structures but different between these sets of structures. Furthermore, interestingly, while the conformation of the Tyr105 side chain in the WT BLIP–β-lactamase structures is very different compared with the apoenzymes, it is similar in the BLIP E73W–β-lactamase structures and the β-lactamase apoenzymes. One possibility is that the Tyr105 side chain is an obstacle for WT BLIP binding, and its positioning is crucial to avoid steric clashes with the BLIP 48 to 51 and 138 to 142 loops that insert into the β-lactamase active site (17). By this hypothesis, removal of Tyr105 in the form of a Y105A mutation would enhance WT BLIP binding and lead to broad inhibition potency since the BLIP loops would have more ready access to the β-lactamase active site. To test this prediction, we constructed the Y105A mutation in the CTX-M-15, CTX-M-14, and TEM-1 enzymes and determined inhibition constants (Ki) for WT BLIP. Consistent with our hypothesis, WT BLIP inhibited CTX-M-15 Y105A with 50-fold enhanced potency relative to WT CTX-M-15. Similarly, WT BLIP inhibited CTX-M-14 Y105A with 250-fold enhanced potency compared with WT CTX-M-14 and inhibited TEM-1 Y105A with 3.6-fold enhanced potency relative to that observed for WT TEM-1.

In the BLIP E73W structures in complex with CTX-M-15, CTX-M-14, and TEM-1 β-lactamases, the BLIP Trp73 residue traps Tyr105 against the wall of the active site in a position similar to that in the β-lactamase apostructures. Therefore, it is not clear that Tyr105 is an obstacle for BLIP E73W binding and may actually contribute to binding energy. The Ki values for BLIP E73W inhibition of the β-lactamase Y105A mutants are similar to the values with the WT β-lactamases (Table 1).

Taken together, these results highlight that the BLIP E73W mutant takes advantage of a different binding mode with the active site to achieve broad specificity and potent inhibition of class A β-lactamases.

Discussion

Here, we identified the BLIP E73W mutant that exhibits high potency and broad inhibition of class-A β-lactamases. Furthermore, we aimed to determine the molecular basis for the broad potency. Previous structures of BLIP in complex with β-lactamases showed hydrogen bonds between BLIP Glu73 and the main-chain amide NH groups of β-lactamase residues 105 and 106, which requires a conformation of the 103 to 106 loop that presents the NH groups in the proper orientation for hydrogen bonding. This conformation pre-exists in the apo-enzyme structures of TEM-1, SHV-1, and KPC-2 but not for the CTX-M-14 and CTX-M-15 enzymes. The previously determined structure of BLIP in complex with CTX-M-15 β-lactamase showed that the 103 to 106 region of the enzyme undergoes a conformational change upon BLIP binding such that the phi–psi angles of the 103 to 106 region are similar to those found for the 103 to 106 loop in other available BLIP–β-lactamase structures where the residue 105 and 106 NH groups are properly oriented by hydrogen bonding to BLIP Glu73 (15). Coincident with the 103 to 106 conformational change, the side chain of Tyr105 assumed an alternate rotamer conformation that matched the position of Tyr105 in all known BLIP–β-lactamase structures (Fig. 6).

In contrast, the BLIP E73W does not hydrogen bond to the residue 105 to 106 NH groups, but rather, the new Trp73 residue extends over the 103 to 106 loop into the β-lactamase active site and packs the β-lactamase Tyr105 residue against the wall of the pocket in a position very similar to that found in class A β-lactamase apoenzymes. The broad specificity of BLIP E73W is due at least in part because it is agnostic to the conformation of the 103 to 106 loop of β-lactamase. The enhanced potency may result from hydrophobic and possible pi–pi interactions between BLIP Trp73 and β-lactamase Tyr105 and the fact that the inhibitor binds a β-lactamase conformation that is similar to that found in the apoenzymes, that is, a highly populated conformation (30) (Fig. 6).

All available BLIP–β-lactamase structures show a change in rotamer conformation of Tyr105 in the BLIP complexes compared with the β-lactamase apoenzymes (15, 17, 20, 21) (Fig. 6). Based on this, we hypothesized that the side chain of Tyr105 may require precise placement in WT BLIP–β-lactamase complexes, and a different conformation as that in the apoenzyme, to avoid steric clash. This predicts that removing β-lactamase Tyr105 may enhance WT BLIP binding and inhibition potency by avoiding clashes. Consistent with this, Y105A substitutions of CTX-M-15, CTX-M-14, and TEM-1 resulted in enhanced inhibition potency of WT BLIP. In contrast, Trp73 of the BLIP E73W mutant makes hydrophobic interactions and traps β-lactamase Tyr105 against the wall of the active site and may contribute to the enhanced potency of the BLIP mutant. Consistent with this hypothesis, additivity analysis of double mutants showed that the β-lactamase Y105A substitution antagonized the effect of the BLIP E73W mutation for all three β-lactamases tested. This further suggests that a key property of BLIP E73W is that it binds the β-lactamase apoenzyme “as is” and does not require conformational changes.

Proteins sample many conformations, and the X-ray structure is a snapshot of a low-energy conformation (30, 31, 32, 33). Furthermore, it is proposed that the proportion of the population occupied by any given conformation is directly related to its energy, with high-energy conformations having low representation and low-energy conformations being more highly populated (30, 34). We propose that the conformation of β-lactamase in complex with BLIP E73W, which is similar to the apo-enzyme structure, is of lower energy and thus more highly populated in solution than the altered rotamer conformation of Tyr105 seen in the complexes of WT BLIP with β-lactamases, which is consistent with the higher potency of BLIP E73W.

Our structural results indicate that BLIP E73W shows a different binding mode to β-lactamase than WT BLIP. There are numerous examples of mutations in binding partners that result in changes in the binding mode in the protein–protein interaction. An early example was a structure of a human growth hormone mutant with several substitutions that resulted in multiple changes in the protein–protein interaction interface with the human growth hormone receptor (35). Thus, multiple binding modes of a protein–protein interaction are accessible via a small number of amino acid changes in interface amino acids, as observed here with the BLIP E73W mutant.

The high potency and wide range of inhibition of BLIP E73W suggest that it would be an excellent broad-spectrum β-lactamase inhibitor. However, the permeation of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria is a significant challenge for its application as a therapeutic (36, 37). Nevertheless, advances in adjuvants for permeation or other progress in moving large molecules through the outer membrane may lead to therapeutic practicality in the future (38, 39, 40). Furthermore, the BLIP E73W protein could provide a broad-spectrum diagnostic reagent for rapid detection of class A β-lactamases in clinical isolates of bacteria.

Experimental procedures

Genetic screening of BLIP libraries

The E. coli TP159 strain was constructed as previously described (24, 25). Briefly, the CTX-M-14 gene was introduced on the chromosome in the pyrF gene using the recombineering strain E. coli SW102 (24, 41). The BLIP random libraries were constructed in the plasmid pGR32 (19), as previously described (24). The BLIP random libraries were transformed into TP159, and the cells were spread onto LB agar plates containing 12.5 μg/ml chloramphenicol. Clones with low-ampicillin MICs were identified by inoculating single colonies into LB media in 96-well plates and were further tested with the ampicillin E-test strip method to determine an exact ampicillin MIC (Liofilchem MTS). After MIC testing, clones with low-ampicillin MICs were sequenced to assess the mutagenesis present.

Protein expression and purification

CTX-M-14, CTX-M-15, TEM-1, KPC-2, SHV-1, and PC1 were expressed in the pET28a vector as previously described (15). The CTX-M-15 Y105A, CTX-M-14 Y105A, and TEM-1 Y105A mutations were introduced using the QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis protocol (42) with Phusion DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The DpnI restriction enzyme was added to remove the methylated DNA template. The product was transformed into E. coli XL1 Blue electrocompetent cells (Agilent). Single colonies were picked for plasmid extraction, and the constructs were sequenced by Sanger sequencing to confirm the mutations.

CTX-M-14, CTX-M-15, TEM-1, KPC-2, SHV-1, and PC1, along with their respective Y105A mutants, were expressed in the pET28a vector with an N-terminal 6× His-tag followed by a tobacco etch virus (TEV) cleavage site. The plasmids were electroporated into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells and cultured in Luria broth media containing 25 μg/ml kanamycin at 37 °C with 200 rpm agitation. At an absorbance of 0.6 at 600 nm, the cultures were induced to 0.2 mM IPTG and incubated for 20 h at 23 °C with 200 rpm agitation. The next day, cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM sodium phosphate buffer [pH = 7.4], 300 mM sodium chloride, Xpert Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Solution [GenDE POT], and 20 mM imidazole). The proteins were lysed by sonication in intervals to prevent sample heating. The cell lysate was spun down, and the supernatant was passed through a prepacked HisTrap-FF Ni Sepharose 6 column (Cytiva). The His-tagged proteins were eluted with an imidazole gradient (0–100% of 500 mM imidazole). The purity of the protein fractions was evaluated by SDS-PAGE followed by Bio-Safe Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 staining. Pure fractions were pooled, and imidazole was removed using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units (MilliporeSigma) for buffer exchange into 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0) and 150 mM sodium chloride buffer. The protein concentrations were determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm.

His-tagged TEV protease was used to cleave the N-terminal His tag of all β-lactamases incubating overnight at 4 °C. After His-tag cleavage, the protein mixture was passed through a gravity column containing Cytiva Ni Sepharose 6 fast flow resin to capture the His-tagged TEV protease as well as any remaining uncleaved β-lactamase. The protein concentrations were determined again by measuring absorbance at 280 nm.

BLIP was expressed in the Thermo Fisher pPINKα-HC expression vector, according to the PichiaPink Expression System Manual. The 6× His-tagged BLIP gene was amplified from the pGR32 vector by PCR using the forward and reverse primers 5′-GTGCATCCTGGCGCCCACCACCACCACCACCACGCGGGGGTGATG-3′ and 5′-CAGCACGCAGGGTACCGAGATTATACAAGGTCCCACTGCCGC-3′, respectively. The PCR fragment and pPINKα-HC vector were digested with StuI and KpnI. Digestion products were ligated using T4 DNA ligase and incubated overnight at 16 °C. The ligation product was then transformed into E. coli XL1 Blue (Agilent). The expression vector was then sequenced by Sanger sequencing to confirm the presence and alignment of the BLIP gene in pPINKa-HC. The expression vector was then linearized with SpeI and introduced into electrocompetent yeast cells by electroporation. The presence of the cloned vector was confirmed by PCR amplification of the gene insert using extracted genomic DNA as a template, extracted with the Zymo Research YeaStar Genomic Extraction Kit. The His-BLIP pPINKa-HC construct was expressed in the Thermo Fisher PichiaPink Strain #2 (ade2, pep4). The cells were cultured in 1 l of buffered glycerol-complex medium (BMGY) at 30 °C for 3 days with 200 rpm agitation, feeding the culture every 24 h with methanol to 0.5%. The culture was centrifuged, and the supernatant containing the expressed protein was saved for further purification. The supernatant was then passed through a prepacked HisTrap-FF Ni Sepharose 6 column (Cytiva), followed by a column wash (25 mM sodium phosphate [pH 7.4], 300 mM sodium chloride, and 20 mM imidazole), and eluted with an imidazole gradient (0–100% of 500 mM imidazole). The His-tag was not cleaved from BLIP.

The purity of the purified proteins was evaluated by SDS-PAGE followed by Bio-Safe Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 staining. The purified proteins were then concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter units (MilliporeSigma). The protein concentrations were determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm.

Ki determination for BLIP inhibition

The Ki of BLIP for class A β-lactamases was determined using purified proteins as previously described (15, 25, 43). The β-lactamase was incubated for 20 min with increasing concentrations of BLIP. Then the colorimetric substrate nitrocefin was added, and the rate of hydrolysis was monitored at 482 nm. The initial rates of hydrolysis were fit using a simple linear regression, and the slopes were normalized by the rate of hydrolysis in the absence of BLIP. These values were expressed as fractional activity and were plotted against the increasing BLIP concentrations. The concentration of the reporter substrate, nitrocefin, was kept as low as possible relative to the KM of the enzyme for nitrocefin hydrolysis to minimize the impact of competition of substrate with inhibitor. In addition, the data were fit to a form of the Morrison equation that includes the competition effect of the concentration of nitrocefin used in the assay relative to the KM of the enzyme for the nitrocefin (Equation 2). The data were fitted using the Morrison equation for tight binding inhibitors to obtain the Ki using the software GraphPad Prism, version 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc) (44, 45) (Equation 2). MIC determination

Bacterial E. coli strain SW102 with β-lactamase genes cloned into the pyrF gene on the chromosome was used as previously described (21). The strains used here were E. coli TP112, TP140, TP159, TP160, and TP161, which contain TEM-1, SHV-1, CTX-M-14, KPC-2, and CTX-M-15 in the bacterial chromosome, respectively. Each strain was transformed with plasmid pGR32 encoding for BLIP WT or BLIP E73W. An overnight culture of each strain was used for MIC determination using the Liofilchem ampicillin MIC test strips. Briefly, the overnight culture was diluted 1:200 in LB media and spread onto an LB agar plate. After 5 min of drying, the ampicillin MIC test strip was placed on the agar plate and incubated overnight at 30 °C.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Binding enthalpy measurements were done using a VP-ITC isothermal titration calorimeter from MicroCal LLC. Experiments were carried out by using purified proteins dialyzed in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH = 7.0) and 150 mM sodium chloride. BLIP WT (50 μM) was titrated into CTX-M-14 β-lactamase (5 μM). BLIP WT (160 μM) was titrated into SHV-1 (160 μM). All ITC experiments were done at 30 °C. The data were analyzed using the manufacturer’s software Origin 7.0 (MicroCal LLC) with an ITC processing add-on module.

Crystallization of protein complexes

The hanging drop vapor diffusion method was used to obtain protein crystals. The protein samples used for crystallization were in 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0) and 150 mM sodium chloride. The proteins were mixed in a 1:1 M ratio and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to facilitate complex formation. Hanging drops were set up using the Mosquito liquid handling robot in a 96-well plate, with one drop per well, a total volume of 200 nl per drop (100 nl protein sample + 100 nl liquid from well). The final protein concentration of the mixture was 15 mg/ml. CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W crystals were obtained with 0.1 M Mes (pH 6.5) and 25% (w/v) PEG 4000 (Nextal PEGs Suite). TEM-1–BLIP E73W crystals were obtained with 0.1 M magnesium chloride, 0.1 M sodium acetate (pH 4.6), and 25% (w/v) PEG 400. CTX-M-14–BLIP E73W crystals were obtained with 0.2 M calcium chloride, 0.1 M sodium acetate (pH 5), and 20% (w/v) PEG 6000. The crystallization conditions used for the Protein Data Bank (PDB) structures referenced in the article (1JTG, 7S5S, 2G2U, and 3E2L) share some similarities with the conditions we used for our crystals. The majority contain some form of PEG. While ours vary from 400, 4000, and 6000, the structures in the PDB have crystallized mostly in PEG 8000. Most crystals seem to form at pH 6.5, but there is variability in the crystals we obtained, with pH ranging from 4 to 6.5. In addition, there are similarities in space groups of the crystals. The CTX-M-15–BLIP E73W crystal structure was determined with a similar space group as the CTX-M-15–BLIP structure (C 1 2 1) (15) (Table S1). However, CTX-M-14–BLIP E73W was in space group P 2 21 21. TEM-1–BLIP structures in the literature are in either the P 21 21 21 or P 1 21 1 space groups (Table S1). Consistent with this, the TEM-1–BLIP E73W mutant crystalized in the P 1 21 1 space group (Table S1).

Crystals were harvested at room temperature, and a 1 μl drop was added to the hanging drop from a cryoprotectant solution made from 15 volumes of glycerol and 85 volumes of liquid from the well. Crystals in their harvest loops were plunged in liquid nitrogen for storage and shipping.

Crystallography data processing and refinement

The diffraction data were collected at the Berkeley Center for Structural Biology using the Advanced Light Source synchrotron 8.2.1 beamline (Table 3, Fig. S4). Data were collected at cryotemperatures for crystal protection during data collection. The CCP4i suite, version 7.1 (46) was used for indexing and integration (iMosfilm) (47), scaling and merging (SCALA) (48), molecular replacement (PHASER, version 2.1.2) (49), and initial refinement (REFMAC) (50). Molecular replacement for the CTX-M–BLIP complexes was done using the CTX-M-15–BLIP complex structure (PDB ID: 7S5S) as the search model. More refinement was done using the PHENIX software (51), and manual examination of the structures was done using the Crystallography Object Oriented Toolkit program (COOT) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20383002/) and MolProbity (52). Hydrogen bonding interactions and structure analysis were done using UCSF ChimeraX, v1.6.1 (53).
