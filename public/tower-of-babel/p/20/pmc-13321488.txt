A novel SPI1 variant (c.566T > C (p.Ile189Thr)) possibly associated with autosomal dominant agammaglobulinemia in a Chinese girl
Authors: Ping Wu, Jing Zhao, Zilong Yu, Hongwei Li, Zhenwei Liu, Yinghui Peng, Zhe Cai, Dehui Chen, Chengyu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAgammaglobulinemia is a rare primary immunodeficiency disorder characterized by deficient B lymphocyte development and reduced serum immunoglobulin levels, leading to recurrent infections. Autosomal dominant agammaglobulinemia (AD-AGM) related to SPI1 gene mutations is extremely rare, with limited reported cases worldwide.Case presentationA 13-year-old Chinese girl was admitted with recurrent respiratory infections for over 6 years and acute cough/fever lasting 1 week. Laboratory tests showed significantly decreased IgG (3.94 g/L) and IgA (0 g/L) levels, with a B lymphocyte ratio (CD3−CD19+)/ lymphocytes) of 0.03% and absolute B cell count of 1 cell/µL. Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous SPI1 variant (c.566T > C (p.Ile189Thr)) inherited from her asymptomatic mother; her father and younger brother did not carry the variant. The variant is annotated as “of uncertain significance (VUS)” according to ACMG criteria, supporting a potential diagnosis of AD-AGM associated with the SPI1 variant. Literature review indicated that the c.566T > C mutation in the SPI1 gene had not been previously reported. Additionally, we summarized the clinical and genetic characteristics of patients from different continents. The patient received anti-infection therapy, intravenous gamma globulin infusion, bronchoalveolar lavage, and symptomatic treatment (e.g., antipyretic, antitussive, expectorant, and nutritional support), with significant clinical improvement. Long-term follow-up (12 months) showed no severe recurrent infections with monthly gamma globulin maintenance.ConclusionThis report describes a novel SPI1 variant (c.566T > C, p.Ile189Thr) in a Chinese girl with clinical features of AD-AGM. The variant has not been recorded in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) or Chinese reference databases. The novel SPI1 variant expands the genotypic spectrum of agammaglobulinemia and provides a potential candidate for further functional and therapeutic research. Whole-exome sequencing may facilitate early identification of such rare variants in patients with suspected immunodeficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06498-4.Keywords: Novel mutations, SPI1 gene, Agammaglobulinemia

Introduction

Agammaglobulinemia, a primary immunodeficiency disease, is mainly characterized by a severe reduction or absence of B lymphocytes in peripheral blood and hypogammaglobulinemia [1, 2]. Clinically, patients present with recurrent bacterial infections, particularly of the respiratory tract, which can be life-threatening if untreated [3–5]. X-linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA) is one of the most common primary immunodeficiency disorders in children, exhibiting X-linked recessive inheritance, with most patients being male [6–8]. XLA is caused by mutations in the gene encoding Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) on the X chromosome, disrupting B cell differentiation and maturation [9]. Most patients presenting with recurrent bacterial infections, significantly reduced or absent serum immunoglobulins, and decreased circulating B lymphocytes [10, 11]. BTK mutations are detectable in 80% − 90% of clinically diagnosed cases [12, 13], including single-nucleotide deletions, substitutions, insertions, frameshift, nonsense, and missense mutations [14, 15].

Beyond XLA, autosomal dominant and recessive forms of agammaglobulinemia also exist [16, 17]. Research on autosomal dominant or recessive agammaglobulinemia began later. Due to rarity, such studies are challenging but have made progress in recent years. Through studies of agammaglobulinemia patients, international scholars have identified multiple autosomal recessive mutations, including those in µHC, PIK3R1, TCF3, and SLC39A7 [18–21]. These mutations impair genes regulating early B cell differentiation and function, triggering agammaglobulinemia. Patients with autosomal recessive agammaglobulinemia (ARA) exhibit earlier onset and higher susceptibility to severe complications [22, 23]. Here, we report a Chinese girl with clinical features of AD-AGM carrying a novel SPI1 variant, aiming to provide clinical and genetic insights for this rare disorder.

Clinical information

Patient characteristics

A 13-year-old girl was admitted to our hospital due to a cough and fever lasting 1 week. She had a history of recurrent lower respiratory tract infections since 2 years of age (approximately once every 2 months) and was hospitalized in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for severe pneumonia 2 months prior. She was born prematurely at 34 weeks of gestation (birth weight 2.6 kg) to non-consanguineous, healthy parents with no family history of genetic diseases.

Genetic testing

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was conducted for the patient and her family using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, with an average exome coverage of 150×. Genetic analysis revealed that the patient harbored a heterozygous variant in the SPI1 gene, designated as c.566T > C (HG19 reference: chr11:47377025), which leads to a missense amino acid substitution of isoleucine to threonine at position 189 (p.Ile189Thr) (Fig. 1A-C). This variant was inherited from her mother, who had normal immunological indices (IgG 11.2 g/L, IgA 1.8 g/L, B cell count 286 cells/µL). The variant was not detected in her father or younger brother. The variant is not found in gnomAD—with an allele frequency of 0 and no reported homozygous carriers—nor in Chinese reference gene databases. In silico predictions further shed light on its potential impact: SIFT classifies it as “damaging” (score = 0.01), PolyPhen-2 as “probably damaging” (score = 0.998), and MutationTaster as “disease-causing” (score = 1.0). Additionally, the variant resides within the evolutionarily conserved ETS domain of the PU.1 protein, supported by a high PhyloP score of 5.23, indicating strong conservation across species. However, under the ACMG criteria, this variant is classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). This classification arises due to two key factors: the absence of functional validation to confirm its pathogenicity, and a conflicting inheritance pattern—specifically, the variant was identified in the proband’s mother, who remains asymptomatic.

Treatment results and prognosis

The patient received anti-infection therapy (piperacillin-tazobactam and voriconazole), intravenous gamma globulin infusion, bronchoalveolar lavage, and symptomatic support (antipyretics, antitussives, expectorants, and nutritional support). Her temperature normalized within 72 h, and cough/expectoration significantly improved. Inflammatory markers, cytokines and chest/paranasal sinus CT findings showed marked resolution before discharge. During 12 months of follow-up, she received monthly intravenous gamma globulin infusions (6 g) at a local hospital. No recurrent severe infections (e.g., sepsis, meningitis) occurred, and her growth and development gradually normalized.

IGHM biallelic mutations (e.g., c.433G > A, c.258G > A) are high prevalence in European (Sweden, Spain, Italy), American, and Argentinean populations. While ETS domain variants uniformly associate with B cell defects, variability exists in age at onset (childhood vs. adolescence) and infection severity—potentially linked to modifier genes or environmental factors (consistent with our study’s asymptomatic carrier finding).

Discussion

Clinical and genetic correlation

This case presents a Chinese girl with clinical features of AD-AGM (recurrent infections, B cell deficiency, hypogammaglobulinemia) carrying a novel SPI1 variant (c.566T > C, p.Ile189Thr). SPI1 mutations are known to cause AD-AGM via PU.1 haploinsufficiency, which disrupts B cell differentiation by impairing chromatin accessibility in bone marrow B cells [34, 41]. CRISPR-based in vitro studies have validated the critical functional roles of the SPI1 gene and its encoded PU.1 protein, while also uncovering that B cells exhibit high sensitivity to perturbations in PU.1 activity. Notably, Knox et al. (2025) analyzed 134 SPI1 variants in their research and emphasized a key finding: heterozygous missense variants localized to the ETS domain of SPI1—including the c.566T > C variant identified in our study—are strongly associated with the development of agammaglobulinemia [42]. Previous reports, such as Daddali et al. (2025) and Miskovic et al. (2024), have described similar clinical phenotypes (recurrent respiratory infections, B cell absence) in patients with SPI1 variants [43, 44]. However, the current case differs in that the variant was inherited from an asymptomatic mother, which contrasts with simple autosomal dominant pathogenicity.

Interpretation of the SPI1 Variant (c.566T > C (p.Ile189Thr))

The SPI1 variant identified in this study, designated as c.566T > C (p.Ile189Thr), is classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. In vitro/in vivo functional validation is needed to confirm altered PU.1 transcriptional activity or B cell differentiation defects. Additionally, the identification of this variant in an asymptomatic carrier-the proband’s mother-raises important considerations regarding penetrance. This finding suggests the possibility of incomplete penetrance, a phenomenon consistent with prior reports of SPI1-related AD-AGM, where penetrance has been estimated to be approximately 80% [42, 43]. Incomplete penetrance in monogenic disorders often reflects the influence of additional modifying factors, and while this variant’s presence in an asymptomatic individual aligns with the known variable expressivity of SPI1-associated diseases, it also introduces ambiguity about its pathogenic potential. Notably, alternative explanations for this discrepancy cannot be ruled out: modifier genes (e.g., other loci involved in B cell signaling or immune regulation) may mitigate the deleterious effects of the SPI1 variant in the carrier, or environmental triggers (e.g., infection history, lifestyle factors, or exposure to certain medications) could be required to unmask the pathogenic phenotype in genetically predisposed individuals. Further investigation into these potential modifiers—such as genome-wide association studies to identify co-segregating genetic variants or retrospective analyses of environmental exposures in affected vs. asymptomatic carriers—is therefore warranted to clarify the variant’s clinical relevance.

Collectively, the VUS classification underscores the need for complementary studies to resolve the ambiguity surrounding this SPI1 variant. Functional assays to assess PU.1 activity and B cell differentiation capacity in the context of the p.Ile189Thr substitution, combined with expanded genetic screening of affected families and larger cohorts of AD-AGM patients, will be critical to reclassify the variant and elucidate its role in disease pathogenesis. Additionally, long-term follow-up of the asymptomatic carrier may provide valuable insights into whether the variant confers latent disease risk or if its expression is truly neutral under current conditions.

Limitations

Apart from the lacking of functional validation (e.g., in vitro PU.1 transcriptional activity assays, B cell differentiation experiments) to confirm the pathogenicity of the variant, the small sample size (single case) limits the generalizability of the findings. And the role of modifier genes or environmental factors in disease manifestation remains unclear. Studies should include functional testing of the variant and multi-center collaboration to collect more cases for comprehensive genetic and clinical analysis. And future research should also address quality of life and mental health in agammaglobulinemia patients. Long-term treatment and life restrictions may induce anxiety or depression; studies should explore psychological support and social care strategies to improve quality of life, and optimize treatments to minimize disruption, facilitating social integration.

Clinical implications

Whole-exome sequencing is a valuable tool for identifying rare variants in patients with suspected primary immunodeficiency, especially when conventional testing (e.g., BTK mutation analysis) is negative. For patients with recurrent infections and B cell deficiency, genetic testing for SPI1 and other autosomal agammaglobulinemia-related genes (e.g., TCF3, NFKB2) should be considered. However, interpretations of VUS variants must be cautious, and functional validation is essential to establish causality.
