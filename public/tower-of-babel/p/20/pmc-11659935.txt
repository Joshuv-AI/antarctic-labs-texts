Stellabody: A novel hexamer‐promoting mutation for improved IgG potency
Authors: Clarissa A. Whitehead, Bruce D. Wines, Anna M. Davies, James M. McDonnell, Halina M. Trist, Sandra E. Esparon, P. Mark Hogarth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryAdvances in antibody engineering are being directed at the development of next generation immunotherapeutics with improved potency. Hexamerisation of IgG is a normal physiological aspect of IgG biology and recently described mutations that facilitate this process have a substantial impact upon monoclonal antibody behavior resulting in the elicitation of dramatically enhanced complement‐dependent cytotoxicity, Fc receptor function, and enhanced antigen binding effects, such as targeted receptor agonism or microbe neutralization. Whereas the discovery of IgG hexamerisation enhancing mutations has largely focused on residues with exposure at the surface of the Fc‐Fc and CH2‐CH3 interfaces, our unique approach is the engineering of the mostly buried residue H429 in the CH3 domain. Selective substitution at position 429 forms the basis of Stellabody technology, where the choice of amino acid results in distinct hexamerisation outcomes. H429F results in monomeric IgG that hexamerises after target binding, so called “on‐target” hexamerisation, while the H429Y mutant forms pH‐sensitive hexamers in‐solution prior to antigen binding. Moreover, Stellabody technologies are broadly applicable across the family of antibody‐based biologic therapeutics, including conventional mAbs, bispecific mAbs, and Ig‐like biologics such as Fc‐fusions, with applications in diverse diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are a well‐established and still rapidly expanding class of therapeutics that have revolutionized the treatment of a range of human diseases including cancer, inflammation, autoimmunity, and infectious diseases. They form the basis of a highly valuable industry and currently over 100 antibody‐based therapies are approved for marketing in the USA and/or Europe by the FDA/EMA, with a further 1200 being investigated in clinical trials. 1 While many formats of antibody therapeutics are being explored for clinical use, including bispecifics and antibody‐fusion proteins, the majority of FDA‐approved therapeutic antibodies are formatted as a typical full‐length IgG consisting of two fragment antigen‐binding (Fab) arms linked to a fragment crystallisable (Fc) region. 2 In this format, mAbs can exert their therapeutic effects through multiple mechanisms. These include direct neutralization of the target antigen via the Fab arms, which can act also as surrogate ligands wherein the two Fab arms induce surface receptor signaling by crosslinking of their target receptor. Importantly, the Fc region is responsible for much of the biological activity of IgG antibodies and can initiate a variety of pro‐or anti‐inflammatory responses of the innate and adaptive immune systems which, depending on the context, include activation of pro‐inflammatory Fc receptors, anti‐inflammatory Fc receptors, or the classical pathway of serum complement. 3 In addition to triggering the above‐mentioned effector functions, the Fc region is also responsible for other key biological activities of IgG antibodies including half‐life 4 , 5 , 6 and certain intracellular actions, such as IgG opsonised target degradation mediated through TRIM21. 7 , 8

The IgG Fc region comprises of two domains: the CH2 and CH3 domains. The CH2 domain contains the binding sites for complement C1q and the IgG Fc receptors (FcRs/FcγRs). FcγRs on immune cells are aggregated by complexed IgG, triggering cellular effector responses. Pro‐inflammatory responses of IgG‐FcγR engagement include cytokine release, as well as antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) or antibody‐dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) of IgG‐opsonised targets. Alternatively, the Fc can also modulate responses by coengagement of a specialized inhibitory FcR (FcγRIIb) with ITAM‐based signaling receptors. These include the B cell antigen receptor (BCR) resulting in the modulation of B cell activation or pro‐inflammatory activating‐type FcRs thereby modulating IgG immune complex activation of myeloid cells (reviewed elsewhere 9 ). C1q is the hexameric, antibody‐binding component of the C1 complex of the complement system. It is comprised of 18 polypeptide chains forming six heterotrimeric collagen‐helices that each end in a globular head. The collagenous stalk associates with the protease components of C1; C1r, and C1s. Each globular head can bind to an IgG Fc at the CH2 domain of IgG, each contributing to the avid binding of complexed or aggregated IgG. This activates C1 and thereby initiates the classical complement cascade, resulting ultimately in the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which is the pore‐forming lytic machinery of the cascade. The consequential lysis of opsonised targets is known as complement‐dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and is tightly regulated to limit host damage. 10

Importantly, the ability to elicit the main IgG‐dependent effector functions, involving FcγR and complement, varies between different IgG subclasses. IgG1 and IgG3 are generally considered the most potent, while IgG2 and IgG4 are considered the least potent triggers of these immune effector responses. IgG2 potency has been considered as restricted to the activation of only the His131 allelic form of FcγRIIa, although the activation of other FcγRs in situations of high opsonisation and high avidity interactions has been indicated. 11 Since IgG4 binds avidly to the inhibitory FcγRIIb, it likely plays a role in modulating the proinflammatory responses of the activating‐type FcγR of innate immune cells and also the activation of the BCR. 12 Despite restricted receptor interactions and low affinity, these subclasses can none‐the‐less trigger some potent responses that are of high clinical importance. 1 , 13

As antibodies rely on their Fc region to elicit effector functions, Fc protein‐engineering is an approach to manipulate the effector responses and develop mAbs with improved clinical efficacy. Mutations in the CH2 region that directly impact binding to C1q and FcγRs are well documented and have been reviewed thoroughly elsewhere, while mutations in the CH3 domain have been comparatively less explored. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

Recently however, several point mutations, particularly in the CH3 and adjacent CH2 residues, have been identified in the Fc region that mediate distinct effects on antibody behavior and, despite being distal from the C1q and FcR binding sites in CH2, dramatically alter the elicitation of effector responses. 18 , 19 Of particular interest are mutations that promote the self‐association of IgG molecules after they have bound to antigen. Such “on‐target” oligomerization is a normal feature of antibody biology that has important implications for engineering enhanced therapeutic mAbs. Indeed, the organization of antigen‐bound IgG molecules into ordered hexameric assemblies facilitates interactions between antibodies and C1q, and so the activation of the classical complement cascade. 18 , 20 , 21 Other mutations in this region of the Fc instead promote the assembly of IgG into hexamers in solution in the absence of antigen at physiological pH. These preformed oligomers of mutated IgG are pH‐sensitive, and oligomerisation can be reversed at lower pH.

In this review, the novel functional and structural implications of substitutions at histidine 429 (H429) in the CH3 domain of human IgG that form the basis of Stellabody technology (see Figure 1) will be discussed and compared to other hexamer facilitating mutations. The use of the Stellabody mutation will also be explored in the context of different promising therapeutic applications, its performance compared to unmodified mAbs, and its compatibility with various established engineering techniques.

IgG ON‐TARGET HEXAMERISATION: A PHYSIOLOGICAL PROCESS

Early studies of IgG self‐association

IgG molecules exist as monomeric proteins in the circulation but form immune complexes upon the binding of antigens in solution and will similarly opsonise antigenic surfaces. Early studies of the formation of insoluble immune complexes from soluble antigens described a role for antigen crosslinking by the Fab regions, but surprisingly also discovered a role for weak Fc‐Fc interactions in nucleating these immune precipitation reactions. 22

Such Fc:Fc interactions also occur between IgGs bound to surfaces, including cell surfaces. 18 Diebolder et al., showed that this Fc‐mediated self‐associative behavior of IgG was a feature of efficient binding and activation of the C1 complement complex. Indeed, anti‐CD20 mAb‐mediated CDC was inhibited in the presence of a peptide that bound the CH2‐CH3 interface, indicating that Fc‐Fc interactions at this site were important for CDC. 18 Furthermore, complement activation by serum IgG is increased when the C‐terminal K447 of the heavy chain is removed via the activity of serum carboxy‐peptidase, thereby removing a repulsive charge effect that inhibits hexamer formation. 23 This and subsequent work have revealed that the Fc portions of IgG molecules can normally undergo an ordered oligomerisation, more specifically hexamerisation, upon binding to permissive antigenic surfaces. This physiological “on‐target” IgG oligomerisation is driven by noncovalent interactions between neighboring Fc regions of individual IgG molecules and can result in distinct ordered hexameric complexes of six antigen‐bound IgG molecules. The crystallographic structure of the anti‐HIV human IgG1 antibody, b12, provided a description of this normal IgG hexamerisation interface. 24

IgG hexamerisation facilitates efficient binding of C1q

Elegant studies using cryogenic electron tomography, cryogenic electron microscopy, and high‐speed atomic force microscopy have together revealed important insights into the relationship between the IgG‐Fc hexamer and the antibody recognition subunit of the C1 complex, C1q. 18 , 20 , 21 , 25 IgG hexamers on target surfaces were shown to provide six optimally positioned binding sites for C1q, one per Fc in the hexamer, that enables the efficient engagement of the six globular heads of C1q. This efficient recruitment of C1q by IgG hexamers activates the classical complement pathway, resulting in the assembly of the channel‐forming MAC which may result in the lysis of cellular targets. 25 In this manner, IgG hexamers on opsonized target cell surfaces provide natural high‐avidity binding sites to efficiently recruit C1q and activate the classical complement cascade. Accordingly, while the binding affinity of each C1q globular head to monomeric IgG is weak (∼104 M‐1), avid binding of the C1q hexamer to immune complexes or heat‐aggregated IgG is greatly increased because of avidity effects and estimates of avidity/apparent affinity have been determined (∼108 M‐1–109 M‐1). 18 , 26 , 27 Although hexameric assemblies of IgG are a platform for highly efficient binding of C1q and the subsequent activation of C1 and the complement pathway, lower order IgG oligomers are reported to still activate complement to varying degrees, whereas monomeric and dimeric IgG are unable to activate complement. 21 , 28

HEXAMERISATION IN THE DIFFERENT HUMAN IgG SUBCLASSES

While most studies that have examined hexamerisation were conducted using human IgG1, other IgG subclasses also form ordered hexamers. A structural study of IgG3 provides insights into the function of this subclass. 25 For example, only one Fab arm of IgG1 is documented to bind an antigenic surface when forming hexamers, 18 owing potentially to structural constraints imposed by the short upper hinge of IgG1. In contrast, IgG3 possesses a flexible upper hinge that permits bivalent Fab binding to dense arrays of epitopes and a longer core hinge that results in extended display of the hexameric Fc platform. 25 Such bivalent binding and the positioning of the hexameric IgG3‐Fc platforms at 22 nm above the cell surface, 25 as opposed to 11 nm for IgG1, 18 likely enables better access to C1q and FcγRs. This will contribute to IgG3 binding C1 and, in certain environments, some FcγRs more strongly than IgG1. 12 , 29

Although the interacting interface by which IgG‐Fc is assembled into hexamers is defined, the manner of IgG opsonisation, including the assembly of hexameric complexes, will be influenced by Fc‐independent factors which may impact the efficiency of hexamerisation and the subsequent activation of effector functions. 29 These other factors include IgG subclass, as well as the size, density, accessibility, orientation and flexibility of the target epitopes. 21 Such variables have been shown in other systems, to influence IgG potency in initiating effector functions such as ADCC. 30 , 31 During the development of mAb‐based immunotherapies, these factors are often not easily predicted and are either only revealed empirically or remain obscure, while favorable combinations of these variables give rise to strong complement fixation by a particular mAb therapeutic. One strategy to overcome the limitations imposed by such variables may be the development of combinations of mAbs recognizing different epitopes and perhaps comprising different IgG subclasses that together can result in opsonising mAb preparations that efficiently form Fc hexamers.

STRUCTURAL FEATURES OF THE IgG HEXAMERISATION INTERFACE

The capacity of immunoglobulins to assemble into oligomeric structures is evolutionarily ancient. 32 Through avidity effects, the naturally oligomeric (pentameric) nature of secretory IgM imparts greater antigen binding capacity and provides an efficient scaffold for activating the complement system. 33 The hexameric ring‐like assembly used by IgG CH2 (Cγ2) and CH3 (Cγ3) domains to bind and activate complement is remarkably similar in terms of its overall architecture to that of secretory IgM comprising a pentamer of CH3 (Cμ3) and CH4 (Cμ4) domains and J chain (Figure 2A). 24 , 34

The IgG hexamer is exemplified by the intact b12 antibody crystal structure (PDB code 1HZH) 24 that arises from crystal packing interactions, but similar hexameric assemblies have been observed for the intact 2G12 antibody (PDB 4NHH) 35 and deglycosylated IgG4‐Fc (PDB 4D2N). 28 In the b12 mAb, the hexamer interface between neighboring molecules buries a surface area of ~800Å2 and involves contacts between the CH2 and CH3 domains. Residues that form the hexamer interface are part of a promiscuous binding site, or interaction “hot spot”, 36 , 37 that engages FcRn, 4 TRIM21, 7 rheumatoid factor, 38 staphylococcal protein A 39 , 40 and many other pathogen IgG‐binding proteins. 41 , 42

The hexamer interface is characterized by a series of shallow pockets and depressions on its surface (Figure 2B,C) and as the interface involves interactions between two identical chains of IgG‐Fc, each type of interaction is found twice at a single interface. Extensive mutagenesis analysis by a number of groups 18 , 19 , 43 has confirmed the biological relevance of this crystallographic Fc‐Fc interface, the effects of which are described in detail below.

Contacts at the Fc‐Fc interface

At the centre of the interface between the neighboring CH2 domains, the AB loops in each CH2 domain contact one another. I253 in one AB loop packs against I253 from the other AB loop. I253 in one chain occupies a shallow pocket created by L251, M252, and I253 (CH2 AB loop), H310 and L314 (CH2 EF loop), and H435 (CH3 FG loop) in the other chain (Figure 2C). At the edge of the pocket, M252 in one chain contacts N434 in the CH3 domain of the other chain.

At the interface between the neighboring CH3 domains, Y436 side chains pack against one another and T437 contacts Q438 in the other chain. H433 and N434 (CH3 FG loop) in one chain occupy a depression formed by the following residues in the other chain (Figure 2B): K248 (CH2 AB loop), E380 and E382 (CH3 β‐strand C), G385, Q386 and P387 (CH3 CD loop) and Y436 (CH3 β‐strand G). At the edge of the interface, E345, which is part of the linker between the CH2 and CH3 domains, contacts the CH3 CD loop in the other chain (Figure 2C). Like the CH2‐CH2 domain interactions, each type of CH3‐CH3 domain interaction is observed twice at the interface, once for each chain.

Salt bridges influence the Fc‐Fc interface

Two intramolecular salt bridges influence the behavior of the Fc‐Fc interface by stabilizing the interface between CH2 and CH3 domains. One salt bridge is between K338 in the CH2‐CH3 domain linker and E430 in the CH3 domain while the other is between K248 (CH2) and E380 (CH3) and as noted above E380 also forms part of the buried surface area of the Fc:Fc interface.

The CH2‐CH3 ball and socket

The CH2:CH3 interface includes a “ball and socket” joint that permits movement of the CH2 domain relative to the CH3 domain. L251 in the CH2 domain forms the “ball” and M428, E430, and H435 in the CH3 domain form the “socket”, with H429 at its base 44 (Figure 2D–F). Interestingly, as noted above, E430 and H435 form part of the network of intra‐ and intermolecular contacts but this is not the case for H429 as described in detail below.

Engineering Fc‐Fc interactions by mutation of surface residues

The b12 mAb crystal structure has been used to guide the selection of residues to engineer enhance IgG hexamerisation. Diebolder et al. mutated residues that are found in the CH2‐CH3 linker and on the CH3 domain surface, at, or close to, the Fc‐Fc interface including most notably E345, E430, and S440 (Figure 2D), 18 the functional effects of which are discussed elsewhere below. Zhang et al., in a similar approach, mutated residues K248 and T437 on the CH2 and CH3 domain surfaces (Figure 2D). 19

Mutation of K248 (K248R; CH2) or E430 (E430G; CH3) disrupts intramolecular salt bridges with E380 (CH3) and K338 (CH2‐CH3 linker), respectively, affecting interactions across the CH2‐CH3 interface. In contrast, the E345R, S440Y 18 and T437R 19 mutations directly enhance Fc‐Fc interface interactions.

Engineering Fc‐Fc interactions by H429 modification

In a novel approach to enhanced hexamerisation that underpins Stellabody technology, different substitutions of H429 were made, notably phenylalanine (H429F) and tyrosine (H429Y). 43 Unlike the surface residues targeted in other studies, H429 is buried, lying largely in the interior of the CH3 domain beneath the “socket” residues. Thus, H429 does not form any direct intramolecular contacts with CH2 or intermolecular contacts with CH3. That is, H429 is not a direct participant in the CH2‐CH3 interface or the Fc‐Fc interface. As a result, the structural basis for the functional effects of the H429F mutation is less obvious than direct CH2‐CH3 or Fc‐Fc contact residues.

Modeling the effect of H429 modification on the Fc‐Fc interface

To investigate the basis for the hexamerisation properties of Stellabody technology, a model for an IgG1‐Fc region that contained a H429F mutation was generated using AlphaFold2. 45 When compared to the b12 hexameric assembly (Figure 2E,F), the predicted model reveals that replacement of the H429 with the bulkier phenylalanine residue leads to small‐scale and larger‐scale conformational changes involving parts of the Stellabody molecule that form the hexamer interface. In the predicted model, a shift in the CH2‐CH3 domain linker, accompanied by a larger‐scale movement of the CH2 domain, suggests a mechanism by which better packing interactions could form between residues E311 (CH2 EF loop) and S254 (CH2 AB loop) in neighboring CH2 domains. Furthermore, part of the CH3 domain FG loop is shifted in the Stellabody molecule. When viewed in the context of the b12 hexamer, H433 and N434 (CH3 FG loop) in one Fc molecule are closer to E380 and K248 in the neighboring CH3 and CH2 domains, respectively, which could also lead to more favorable contacts. Notably, the “ball and socket” joint is present in the H429F mutant and the salt bridge between E430 and K338 is preserved.

However, constructing a hexamer using a predicted model for Stellabody technology generates steric clashes between other regions in neighboring CH2 domains, suggesting that additional conformational changes within the Fc region, perhaps involving the CH2 domains, might be involved in hexamer formation. Indeed, Zhang et al. have noted that hexamer formation may require particular relative dispositions of the CH2 and CH3 domains. 19 Furthermore, smaller‐scale movements upon hexamer formation would not be unprecedented, given that the CH2 AB loop undergoes a small shift in both the b12 24 and deglycosylated IgG4‐Fc 28 hexamers compared with monomeric IgG‐Fc molecules. The structural effects of the H429F mutation thus may be more complex than those suggested by the predicted model and await experimental validation.

CONTROLLING HEXAMERISATION

On‐target hexamers and mixed hexamers

In recent years, several mutations in the Fc region have been identified that enhance Fc‐Fc interactions and consequently the ability of IgG to form “on‐target” hexamers (Table 1). These modified IgG antibodies are monomers in solution and assemble into ordered hexamers only once bound to target antigen. It should be noted that other strategies, outside of the scope of this review, have been utilized to facilitate IgG oligomerisation, including integration of the IgM μ‐tailpiece fused to an IgG backbone. 46 , 47 , 48

Mutation of other residues that contribute to the intermolecular Fc‐Fc interface have also been identified that enhance on‐target hexamerisation. As touched upon briefly above, Diebolder et al., carried out a mutagenesis analysis of residues at, or close to, the crystallographic Fc‐Fc interface of the b12 mAb, and identified mutations such as E345R, E345K, E430G, S440Y that were found to enhance IgG hexamerisation. 18 , 49 IgG antibodies comprising either E345K or E430G (the basis of HexaBody technology) were shown to be monomers in solution and displayed an enhanced capacity for on‐target hexamerisation, while a small percentage (1.2%) of E345R mutants formed hexamers in solution; that is, prior to contact with antigen. Interestingly, the substitution of any other amino acid at position E345 and E430 increased the propensity of IgG molecules to oligomerise, as inferred from enhanced activation of complement, suggesting that the glutamic acid residues at these positions may uniquely function to limit IgG Fc:Fc interaction. 49 , 50

Zhang et al., also described two mutations, T437R and K248E, that have little impact on antibody behavior by themselves, but instead work synergistically to enhance on‐target IgG oligomerisation when both are introduced into the same IgG molecule. 51 Together, these mutations enhanced the activity of an anti‐OX40 agonist mAb, but less effectively than the E345R mutation. 51 This dual mutant IgG was a monomeric protein, but demonstrated superior association on target cells measured by a proximity assay, and improved complement activation.

It is also important to note that H429 and also K248, E345, E430 and T437 are conserved in all human IgG subclasses, and interestingly H429 in all Ig classes, making these suitable candidates for modification in the development of a range of biologics containing varied Fc components.

As mentioned previously, the C‐terminal residue of normal IgG is the positively charged K447, which inhibits Fc‐Fc association of neighboring IgGs through charge repulsion and thereby suppresses CDC potency. 23 Building on this, the introduction of either a positively charged S440K or negatively charged K439E residue near the C‐terminus of IgG also inhibited hexamer formation of the IgG molecules carrying the same charge‐repulsion mutation. 18 An innovative use of this observation was its application in the targeting of distinct antigens with mixtures of two hexamerising‐enhanced mAbs. The K439E mutation in one mAb complemented the S440K mutation of the second and therefore, by charge steering, directed the assembly of the two mAbs into mixed hexamers of dual specificity. Since hexamerisation and CDC activity in an individual mAb is suppressed by a charge repulsion mutation, the mixing of mAbs carrying distinct, but complementary, charge mutations provides a means to selectively direct the on‐target assembly of hetero‐hexamers. These mAbs, which require two distinct antigens for hexamer assembly, can deliver potent complement‐mediated killing which is highly specific. Killing is thus directed to cells expressing the two distinct antigens while cells that express only one of the antigens are spared. 18 , 52

Preformed hexamers

In addition to point mutations, and combinations of mutations, that promote the on‐target hexamerisation of IgG antibodies, other mutations have been identified that promote the self‐association of IgG in solution (“preformed” hexamers). The oligomerisation of IgG molecules in solution functionally mimics IgM in providing higher avidity for the neutralization and agglutination of soluble targets. 53 As such, preformed IgG hexamers may offer advantages in specific therapeutic applications, such as virus neutralization and clustering of targeted receptors.

Combining hexamer‐enhancing mutations has been shown to increase the propensity of IgG molecules to form hexamers in solution, as double mutants (e.g., E345R/E430G) produce a population of 7%–13% preformed hexamer and triple mutants (e.g., E345R/E430G/S440Y) produce a population of 50%–80% preformed hexamer. 49 , 50 The extent of oligomerization of these mutant antibodies is also concentration‐dependent. 50

Replacement of histidine at position 429 with tyrosine (H429Y) induces the formation of noncovalent preformed IgG oligomers (as represented in Figure 3B). 43 Interestingly, this contrasts with the H429F mutation which produces monomeric IgG in solution with enhanced on‐target hexamerisation capabilities. These effects are independent of the variable domain in mAbs and were also observed in an Fc‐fusion protein. 43 Given the nonpolar nature and similar structure of tyrosine (in H429Y) and phenylalanine (in H429F), these differences in biophysical nature are remarkably determined solely by the presence of a hydroxyl group in the tyrosine of the H429Y mAb.

It is noteworthy that this assembly into hexamers in solution (preformed hexamers) in the absence of antigen is sensitive to pH, as oligomeric H429Y species are formed at neutral pH 7.4 while monomers predominate at pH 5.0 (unpublished). A similar inhibition of self‐association into preformed hexamers has also been noted for the triple mutant RGY at pH 5.0. 54

ASSOCIATION, ALLOSTERY, AND Fc FUNCTION IN HEXAMERISATION

An IgG immune complex or IgG opsonised surface presents an array of Fcs that activates Fc‐multimer sensing effector molecules, such as C1 and cell surface FcγRs. Such antigen‐bound IgGs can present a high concentration of colocalized Fc regions, and so their relatively weak Fc interactions become functionally relevant. 55 In this associative process, Fc‐Fc interactions may organize the colocalized Fcs into ordered arrays, such as hexamers (Figure 3A). However, while this accounts well for most of the behavior of these systems, some experiments have suggested conformational allostery may also play a role. 56

As indicated above, modeling and structural studies suggest that the CH2 domains are altered to accommodate Fc‐Fc hexamer interactions, which may also impact FcR function (see below). The Fc‐Fc hexamerisation interface also requires movement of the AB loop to accommodate the interaction of I253 with the neighboring Fc (H310 and I253) without steric clash. 28 Indeed, antigen binding may transmit allosteric effects through the hinge to alter Fc conformation, 57 which raises the possibility that antigen recognition may play a role in on‐target hexamer assembly. Techniques such as hydroxyl radical and deuterium exchange reaction mapping by mass spectroscopy have detected secondary interactions of the Fab with FcγRs 58 , 59 and FcRn, 60 so from such effects antigen binding induced allosteric effects need to be discerned. In one such mapping experiment, peptide antigen binding by an intact IgG resulted in changes in CH2‐CH3 residues that bind FcRn, 61 many of which also participate in the Fc‐Fc interface. Similarly binding of soluble FcγRIIIa to intact IgG1, which occurs in the hinge proximal area of the CH2 domains, resulted in detectable changes in residues in the CH2‐CH3 interface. 59 Another study demonstrated Fc changes that increased the binding of low affinity FcγRs upon antigen binding, but also mapped changes in the CH2 β‐strand A and the CH3 FG loop/β‐strand G (residues F241‐M252 and V427‐L443, respectively) including residues comprising the L251‐”ball”, K248, and the “socket” (M428, E430, H435) 44 , 62 that also influence the Fc‐Fc hexamer interface.

In sum, given the increasing evidence for antigen binding‐induced allosteric changes in the Fc, there is a possibility, yet unconfirmed, that in addition to effects on low affinity FcγR binding, antigen binding allosterically affects IgG hexamerisation.

APPLICATIONS OF HEXAMERISING MUTATIONS I: mAb EFFECTOR FUNCTIONS

Complement activation and CDC

CDC mediated by the classical pathway of the complement cascade is an important immune effector response that results in the lysis of target cells, including pathogenic micro‐organisms and tumor cells, and is a fundamental mechanism of action for several therapeutic mAbs against such targets. 3 , 63 Recent antibody engineering efforts have focused on exploiting this powerful mechanism with the incorporation of hexamer‐enhancing mutations that dramatically improve C1q binding and thereby transform ineffective mAbs into potent activators of CDC. 18 , 19 , 43 , 64

As shown in Figure 1A, the Stellabody H429F mutation of the anti‐CD20 mAb rituximab, dramatically improves C1q binding to lymphoma cells opsonised with the mAb, which in turn leads to potent CDC activity (Figure 1B). Similarly, Stellabody H429F mutation of the anti‐HER2 mAb, trastuzumab, enhanced C1q binding on HER2‐positive ovarian cancer cells (Figure 1C), indicating that enhancement of complement action is not restricted to one target. Indeed, the ability of hexamer‐enhancing mutations to improve C1q binding and subsequently induce CDC is broad in its application, with mutations that enhance on‐target hexamerisation such as H429F, E430G, and E345R shown to dramatically improve the CDC potency of mAbs directed against a wide array of antigens, including targets in blood cancer (CD20, CD38, CD52), solid tumors (HER2, EGFR), as well as viruses such as HIV and SARS‐CoV‐2. 49 , 64 , 65 , 66

This strategy to induce potent CDC by facilitating hexamer assembly exploits the avid binding of hexameric C1q by IgG hexamers and is distinct from other approaches that use mutations directly at the C1q binding site to enhance IgG affinity for C1q 67 , 68 (reviewed by Damelang et al. 17 ). A recent comparison of both approaches revealed that the complement‐mediated lysis of tumor cells by anti‐EGFR mAbs was more effectively enhanced by hexamer‐enhancing mutations, compared to mutations that enhanced IgG affinity for C1q (S239D/H268F/S324T/I332E). 69 Therefore, the enhancement of C1q binding avidity via hexamerising mutations may present a dominant approach to improve CDC.

Therapeutic advantages of enhanced CDC by hexamerising mutations

Hexamer‐enhanced mAbs possess several key advantages as CDC‐inducing therapeutics compared to conventional mAbs. Firstly, the antigen expression threshold for complement activation is lowered for hexamer‐enhanced mAbs, which increases the likelihood that patients with low target expression may be successfully treated with a mAb (Cook et al. 65 and CAW BDW PMH unpublished). This is a promising advance in overcoming a lack of response to current mAb therapies where target antigen expression is reduced naturally or as a consequence of treatment. For example, rituximab or ofatumumab treatment reportedly leads to the reduction of CD20 expressed on the surface of chronic lymphoid leukemia (CLL) cells as a result of trogocytosis, thereby decreasing the efficacy of further rounds of antibody treatment. 70 , 71 Secondly, as hexamerising mAbs induce potent CDC of target cells at lower concentrations of mAb treatment, less antibody is required to achieve maximal killing, thereby reducing dosing requirements. 49 , 72 Thirdly, unlike conventional mAbs, hexamer‐enhanced mAbs can also efficiently induce CDC when complement components are limited, as robust CDC is mediated by E340G mutants when C9, a critical component of the MAC, is limiting. 65 Furthermore, the exhaustion of complement is another key mechanism of resistance to mAb therapies. 71 , 73 Hexamer‐enhanced mAbs may mitigate the reduced complement potency in these resistant patients, overcoming a lack of CDC by conventional mAbs and thereby leading to better patient outcomes. Future studies will benefit from assessing the superior efficacy of hexamer‐enhanced mAbs and their potential to resensitize refractory patients to mAb therapy. Lastly, the introduction of a hexamer‐enhancing mutation in a type II anti‐CD20 mAb, such as 11B8, can endow it with the ability to stimulate CDC in addition to retaining its capacity to induce ADCC and apoptosis that are typical of these type II mAbs, thus providing multiple mechanisms of target cell killing. 49

Consequently, these point mutations have been largely explored in the context of enhancing complement system activation, however the ability to promote IgG self‐association through hexamerising mutations is currently being explored across other therapeutic applications including improved receptor signaling, enhanced virus neutralization, and altered FcγR‐mediated effector functions. Highlighting the gaining traction and promise of this technology, a first‐in‐human trial is currently underway using assess hexamerising anti‐CD38 in the treatment of relapsed refractory multiple myeloma (GEN3014, NCT04824794).

Hexamerising mutations and CDC in the different IgG subclasses

As H429, and K248, E345, E430, T437 are conserved in all human IgG subclasses, hexamer‐enhancing point mutations at these positions may have the potential to improve the CDC potency of those IgG subclasses that lack CDC potency. 74 The hexamerising mutations E430G and E345K were recently shown to strongly improve the C1q binding to an IgG2 on highly opsonised beads (both in the presence and absence of the C1r2s2 proteases that, together with C1q, form the C1 complex), but in this setting of high antigen density, had little effect on binding by IgG1 and no effect on binding by IgG3 that were already capable of avid C1q binding without the aid of hexamerising mutations. 64 Similarly, these mutations were found to enhance the binding of IgG4 to the fully assembled C1 complex, suggesting that the poor interactions of surface bound IgG4 with C1 may be overcome upon the introduction of hexamerising mutations. These studies suggest that for subclasses with poor intrinsic CDC, the hexamerisation likely provides a “more stable” platform for C1q binding in situations of high target densities. Indeed, hexamer‐enhancing mutations such as H429F (Stellabody) and E345R (HexaBody) increase the CDC activity relative to unmodified equivalents of IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4 on target cell lines in the presence of normal human serum, confirming the utility of hexamerising mutations in all human IgG subclasses. 18 As each subclass possesses unique properties that impact target binding, and FcγR engagement, such an approach enables the development of a broad range of hexamer‐enhanced mAbs that differ in functional profiles and are optimal for different therapeutic contexts.

mAb cooperation and heterohexamers for enhanced CDC activity

Hexamer technologies confer or enhance CDC by individual mAbs, and this activity may be enhanced further by the simple targeting of multiple antigens with mixtures of hexamerising mAbs. For example, two Stellabody H429F mutant mAbs that target distinct epitopes on HER2 were found to cooperate to enhance C1q binding, and this effect was particularly enhanced when concentrations of each antibody were limiting (Figure 1D). This demonstrates cooperative synergy in mixtures of hexamer‐enhanced mAbs directed at different target epitopes or antigens, and such mixed hexamer approaches can thus enhance killing.

As mentioned previously, the introduction of complementary charge steering mutations near the C‐termini of two hexamerising mAbs can direct hexamer formation specifically to targets expressing both antigens. 18 , 52 This approach demonstrated highly selective targeting of CD20/CD52‐positive lymphoma cells, as CDC was induced by mixed hexamers with specificity for both CD20 and CD52, while T cells expressing only CD52 alone were spared. 52 This improved specificity via incorporation of charge steering modifications into cooperating pairs of hexamerising antibodies could have other applications, for example receptor clustering for signal amplification.

FcγR‐mediated effector functions (ADCC and ADCP)

While the enhancement of hexamerisation augments the ability of mAbs to induce potent CDC, the impact on FcγR‐mediated effector functions is currently less studied. Surface bound IgG can also stimulate immune responses via engagement of FcRs that are expressed on almost all types of innate effector cells such as monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, neutrophils and other myeloid cells, and constitutes a major mechanism of action of antibodies. 9 Emerging evidence indicates that hexamerising mutations also affect mAb induced FcγR functions.

On‐target hexamers and FcγR‐mediated effector functions

mAbs comprising the E430G, E345K, E345R, or the T437R/K248E double mutation that promote on‐target hexamerisation enhance the activation of FcγRIIIa, as inferred from receptor activation reporter assays 19 , 51 or functional assays of ADCC activity using PBMCs. 49 , 72 There is also evidence for enhanced activation of FcγRs by Stellabody H429F modified mAbs (Table 1).

A recent study by Gehlert and colleagues examined the functional properties of a CD19 therapeutic mAb enhanced for both CDC (E345K) and FcγRII/FcγRIII‐mediated (S239D/I332E) effector functions. Using whole blood as a source of complement and effector cells, this study highlighted that strong complement deposition by E345K hexamer‐enhanced mAbs may have a dominant effect over FcγR engagement. 75 However, while CDC was the dominant effector function at high mAb concentrations (2 μg/mL) of hexamer‐enhanced mAb, ADCC was the prominent mechanism at low antibody concentrations (0.016 μg/mL). 75 This likely reflects the physiology of the engagement of effector function during the natural antigen‐specific antibody response or during the administration of therapeutic mAbs as antibody concentrations rise. Antibody concentrations are low early in responses or mAb infusion and targets are likely to be opsonised sparsely. FcR activation may be dominate at these low concentrations whereas as responses, or infusions progress and antibody concentrations rise, greater opsonisation density overcomes limitations or distance constraints on hexamer formation, and CDC is thus potent. Given the complexity of human FcγR and complement biology, further investigation into the impact of on‐target hexamerisation on altered FcγR interactions effector functions and their relationship to complement activation, particularly under physiologically relevant conditions, is warranted.

Preformed hexamers and FcγR‐mediated effector functions

On‐target hexamers maintain the ability to stimulate FcγR‐mediated effector functions upon binding to surface antigen, as indicated above for IgG and also demonstrated with two ACE2‐Fc virus decoy Fc fusion proteins modified with either the Stellabody H429F or the HexaBody E340G mutations (Table 1). However, mutations that promote the formation of hexamers in solution (i.e., before contact with antigen), also known as preformed hexamers, may behave differently.

The H429Y mutation that promotes the assembly of hexamers in solution does not affect FcγRIIIa activation when incorporated into IgG1 antibodies directed against cell surface antigens, such as CD20 or HER2 (Table 1). However, this was not the case for the preformed hexamers of H429Y‐modified ACE2‐Fc SARS‐CoV‐2 viral decoy proteins which failed to bind FcγRIIIa and, accordingly, failed to activate cells via FcγRIIIa. 43 One possible explanation for this ablated FcγR engagement is the unique nature of this fusion protein. The H429Y mutation in the CH3 domain and the context of the physically large ACE2 ectodomain that replaced the Fab arms, may combine to adversely affect distal sites on the same molecule, such as the FcγR binding site within the CH2 domain. Indeed, our model (Figure 2) and the crystallographic data reported by Zhang et al., 19 suggest that structurally optimal hexamerisation requires some change in the relationship between the CH2 domains within an Fc. Thus, in this large fusion protein, the structure of the H429Y‐mutated Fc may be altered in such a way as to favor the formation of IgG hexamers in solution while impeding FcγR engagement.

APPLICATIONS OF HEXAMERISING MUTATIONS II: SIGNAL AMPLIFICATION

The use of enhancing IgG Fc hexamerisation extends beyond improving antibody‐dependent effector functions. Since mAbs and Fc fusion proteins can target cell surface or soluble molecules, effects on signaling and trafficking are possible. Indeed, antibodies directed at receptors can act as surrogate ligands through Fab arm‐mediated crosslinking of target receptors, which in turn can initiate receptor signaling resulting in a cellular response.

Since an individual IgG antibody can only crosslink two targets, induction of receptor signaling may require additional “super”‐crosslinking to achieve agonistic responses. One such mechanism in vivo is the “scaffolding” of target‐bound mAbs by the FcγRs of adjacent cells. These cells present their FcγRs as a large array that crosslinks the Fc regions of the target‐bound mAbs. While such FcγR scaffolding may be useful, or even necessary, in the mechanism of action of agonistic mAbs, it can also be associated with severe adverse in vivo responses of “super‐agnostic” mAbs. 13 , 76 Modifying mAb affinity for FcγRs is one means of controlling the strength of scaffolding, and therefore agonism, allowing weak agonistic mAbs to be enhanced while avoiding toxicities associated with intrinsically strong agonists. 77 However, an alternative to FcγR scaffolding is the use of hexamer enhancing mutations. Antibodies assembled into hexamers are dodecavalent and would therefore potentially crosslink up to 12 targets. Hexamerising mutations may thus enable mAbs to be exploited to enhance the action of agonist antibodies or even confer agonism on poor‐ or non‐ agonistic mAbs, thus providing an alternate method for tuning agonism for avoiding toxicity.

Boosting signal agonism via on‐target hexamers

The tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF) has been largely explored as therapeutic targets of agonistic mAbs modified with hexamerising mutations. Hexamer enhancement of TNFRSF agonists has included an anti‐OX40 mAb (TNFRSF4 or CD134), 19 , 51 death receptor five specific mAbs (DR5, also known as TNFRSF10B, CD262 or TRAIL‐R2), 78 and a glucocorticoid‐induced TNFR–related protein (GITR, TNFRSF18, or CD357) mAb, as well as a GITR‐Ligand Fc fusion protein. 79

Indeed, the Stellabody H429F mutation that enhances on‐target hexamerisation can transform ineffective DR5‐targetted mAbs into potent inducers of apoptotic cell death, by enhanced activation of the apoptotic pathway (Figure 1E and unpublished), showing that clustering is necessary, but that apoptosis can be induced without the need for FcγR scaffolding. Furthermore, this effect is independent of IgG subclass, with both IgG1 and IgG2 backbones being capable of guiding DR5 into functional clusters through on‐target hexamerisation. Interestingly, a mixture of two H429F mutant IgG2 molecules targeting non‐overlapping epitopes on DR5 (TDR5 and BDR5, respectively) further boosted the induction of apoptosis, suggesting that a mixed hexamer approach can improve receptor clustering, in addition to C1q binding (as previously shown in Figure 1D). Similar enhancement of apoptotic signaling has been reported for the E430G hexamerising mutation when incorporated into DR5 agonistic mAbs. 80 , 81

Receptor clustering in this context is therefore mediated by two factors: the binding of IgG Fab arms to target receptors, and the interaction of IgG Fcs with each other during on‐target hexamerisation. Our observation that hexameric Stellabody IgG2‐based mAbs (Figure 1E) are also potent inducers of DR5‐mediated apoptosis provides a means of enhancing signaling using a mAb with low effector function thereby reducing the need for in vivo FcR‐scaffolding and its attendant risks. A similar approach is the introduction of mutations in the CH2 region of hexamer‐enhanced mAbs that ablate C1q and/or FcγR binding without compromising signal amplification.

Stabilization of on‐target hexamers by C1q and FcγR

Clearly, hexamerisation provides a mechanism for increasing antibody‐mediated agonism that is not reliant on FcγR scaffolding. However, in some cases, such as weak agonism, such crosslinking may serve to further stabilize the hexameric mAb‐receptor complexes. Indeed, crosslinking with FcγRIIb was shown to further boost the agonism of an anti‐OX40 antibody carrying the T437R/K248E hexamerising mutations, with a three‐fold increase in OX40 activity when target cells and mAb were cocultured with Raji cells expressing FcγRIIb. 19 In addition, C1q has also been shown to play a role in stabilizing these hexameric mAb‐receptor complexes for further improvement in signaling using E430G‐modifed DR5 agonistic mAbs. 81 Importantly, for mAbs where scaffolding (i.e., FcγR crosslinking) is important in their mechanism of action, the use of hexamer enhancing mutations may offer advantages in circumstances where FcγR‐expressing cells may be in short supply in the tumor microenvironment, particularly as C1q is present at relatively high levels in most human tumors. 82

Therefore, the magnitude of signaling induced by hexamer‐enhanced mAbs and potential therapeutic responses in vivo can be moderated by the choice of IgG backbone. As such, hexameric IgG1 can be utilized to amplify signaling responses that are enhanced further by interaction with effector cells or molecules. Alternatively, a response can be nuanced by using IgG2 with equipotent intrinsic signaling but with reduced capacity for effector engagement, and thus without additional scaffold promoted signaling. Additionally, mutated forms of these backbones for increased or ablated effector function would likely provide even greater control for the modulation of responses.

APPLICATIONS OF HEXAMERISING MUTATIONS III: VIRUS AND MICROBIAL NEUTRALIZATION

Hexamerising mutations in virus neutralization

Broadly neutralizing antibodies

As hexamer‐enhancing mutations can be applied to any immunotherapeutic protein comprising an Fc region, these mutations have also been explored in the context of virus neutralization across a variety of formats, including broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) as well as decoy receptor Fc‐fusion proteins.

The capacity of bNAbs to engage Fc‐mediated effector functions to kill live virus or virus‐infected cells is enhanced by hexamer‐enhancing mutations. 66 , 83 In vivo protection by an E430G modified hexamerising bNAb (10–1074) against a simian‐HIV challenge in non‐human primates was confounded by its more rapid plasma clearance compared to the unmodified bNAb. Notwithstanding the reduced half‐life, plasma breakthrough viremia occurred at lower concentration of the hexamerising bNAb, indicating a protective role for enhanced complement activity. 83

Hexamerising mutations in viral “decoy” Fc‐fusion proteins

Much of the application of hexamerising mutations has been to IgG antibodies or bispecific IgG antibodies, but a unique study has explored the use of the Stellabody H429F and H429Y mutations in Fc‐fusion proteins comprising a “decoy” viral entry receptor. 43

The use of the viral‐entry receptor as a decoy offers a potential universal approach to neutralize “antibody escape variants” of viruses which are still susceptible to neuralisation by the entry receptor decoy. This approach has been used to develop a variety of SARS‐CoV‐2 decoys comprising the principal entry receptor, angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), fused to human IgG‐Fc, which have demonstrated protective activity against several SARS‐CoV‐2 variants in human ACE2 transgenic mouse models. 84 , 85 Furthermore, by fusing such decoy receptors to the Fc region of human IgG, these proteins are endowed with both virus neutralization capacity and Fc‐mediated effector functions (such as ADCC, ADCP, and CDC), as well as a prolonged serum half‐life through Fc interactions with FcRn. 86

The protective activity of viral decoy Fc‐fusion proteins may be further enhanced when a hexamer‐enhancing mutation is added to increase the potency of Fc‐mediated effector functions while also retaining neutralization activity. This was shown for ACE2‐Fc decoy proteins comprising the Stellabody H429F on‐target hexamer‐enhancing mutation, which demonstrated equivalent neutralization potency to ACE2‐Fc decoys with an unmodified Fc, but exhibited remarkably enhanced complement mediated lysis of target cells expressing the SARS‐CoV‐2 Spike protein. 43

As decoy receptor‐Fc fusion proteins are engineered to mimic the viral entry receptor, they compete with the native cellular receptors in a stoichiometric manner, potentially limiting neutralization potency, meaning that high doses may be required to achieve therapeutic benefit. Therefore, Fc engineering approaches that increase avidity for the target may provide an advantage. The construction of Fc‐fusion proteins carrying the Stellabody H429Y mutation that produces preformed oligomers represents one such method to enhance avidity. ACE2‐Fc fusion proteins formatted with the H429Y mutation show greatly enhanced SARS‐CoV‐2 neutralization potency, although these proteins lost the ability to bind to FcγRs and activate FcγRIIIa. 43 Such an immunotherapeutic protein with increased virus neutralization and abrogated FcγR activation may be useful as an antiviral agent in certain therapeutic contexts, such as severe COVID‐19 cases where excessive release of inflammatory cytokines are associated with poor patient outcome. 87 Similar approaches using the E430G mutation have been used in other Ig formats to neutralize arenaviruses and enhance CDC function. 88

These examples highlight the utility and versatility of hexamer‐enhancing mutations in Fc‐fusion proteins, with therapeutic applications extending across several mechanisms, which therefore warrant further investigation as novel immunotherapeutic agents.

Hexamerising mutations in microbe neutralization

Enhanced clearance of microbes via on‐target hexamers

Infections caused by Gram‐positive bacteria are a major health burden causing morbidity and death, and control of these pathogens is challenging due to complex vaccinology and antibiotic resistance. While therapeutic mAbs provide an alternate therapeutic approach, these pathogens have mechanisms to evade antibody mediated immunity, but the use of mAbs with hexamer‐enhancing mutations may be a useful countermeasure. Streptococcus pneumoniae, for example, is resistant to killing mediated by antibodies directed against its capsular polysaccharide and is not susceptible to lysis by the MAC and the classical complement pathway. However, the incorporation of E345K or E430G mutations that enhance on‐target hexamerisation into anti‐capsular mAbs confers potent C1q binding, complement activation, fragment deposition, and opsonophagocytic killing. Hexamerising mutations were also functional in the context of IgG3, and less so IgG2 (i.e., IgG1 > IgG3 > IgG2). Interestingly, the E345K mutation in the IgG2 and IgG3 subclasses was more potent than E430G, contrasting with IgG1 where the E345K and E430G modifications were equipotent. Treatment with an anti‐capsular IgG1‐E345K formatted mAb provided hexamerisation‐enhanced protection from bacteraemia and death, presumably via enhanced clearance by opsonic phagocytosis, in mice challenged intranasally with S. pneumoniae. 89

Staphylococcus epidermidis is an important cause of hospital acquired infections in neonates that is poorly controlled using intravenous Ig. E345R hexamerising mutations had differing effects on the potency in three S. epidermidis‐specific IgG1 mAbs. The unmodified mAb, rF1, activated complement strongly and was little improved by the hexamerising mutation. In contrast the mAb CR5133 was strongly improved by E345R mutation and potent complement fixing activity was conferred on the otherwise poorly active mAb, CR6453. 90 This example nicely illustrates the complexity of the complement activating capacity of individual mAbs, and shows that poor activity can be broadly overcome by hexamerising mutation.

In contrast to Gram‐positive pathogens, Neisseria sp are susceptible to MAC lysis. The E430G hexamer mutation of a mAb specific for Neisseria gonorrhoeae lipooligosaccharide enhanced complement lysis, neutrophil opsonophagocytic killing, and protection from colonization in in vivo mouse models. Notably N. gonorrhoeae possesses effective complement evasion capacities, targeting factor H and C4 binding protein, but these were not sufficient to evade protection conferred by the hexamer‐enhanced mAb. 91 , 92

Pathogen anti‐hexamerisation strategies to evade immune‐mediated clearance

Interestingly, some parasites have evolved anti‐hexamerisation strategies to evade IgG‐mediated clearance by serum complement. 93 The surface expression of P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) on infected erythrocytes is restricted to protrusions known as “knobs” which, due to the distance between neighboring knobs, can hinder the hexamerisation of PfEMP1‐bound IgG. Consequently, this prevents activation of the classical complement cascade, even when hexamer‐enhanced mutants (E430G and E345R) were used. The authors noted that on‐target hexamerisation might be achievable with hexamer‐enhanced polyclonal IgG preparations, although this was not examined. Furthermore, hexamer‐enhancing mutations were only introduced into the IgG1 subclass. Additionally, protein A and protein G are bacterial proteins that also serve in a pathogen strategy for evasion of CDC. By binding to the CH2‐CH3 interface in the antibody Fc region, these proteins impair hexamer formation to inhibit complement activation, thus preventing the host's immune system from targeting and clearing pathogens. 94

APPLICATIONS OF HEXAMERISING MUTATIONS IV: BISPECIFIC ANTIBODIES (bsAbs)

bsAbs incorporating mutations to enhance on‐target hexamerisation

bsAbs are designed to recognize two distinct epitopes, either on the same or a different target molecule. The dual targeting capabilities of bsAbs have offered novel avenues for therapeutic targeting including directing effector cells to kill target cells, linking different soluble antigens, the simultaneous blockade of two signaling pathways, or by enabling unique modes of binding to target molecules such as biparatopic bsAbs that bind to distinct epitopes on the same target (reviewed in Ma et al. 95 ).

A variety of methods have been developed to generate bsAbs, with platforms such as knob‐in‐hole 96 , 97 or DuoBody 98 typically used to generate bsAbs with full length Fc regions. These methods use complementary heavy chain mutations in the CH3‐CH3 interface that favor heterodimerisation. Knob‐in‐hole pairing involves the substitution of a small amino acid with a larger in the CH3 domain of one heavy chain (T366W) to form a bulky “knob” while several residues are replaced with a smaller ones (T366S:L368A:Y407V) in the complementary heavy chain to form a “hole”, as originally described by Ridgway. 99 More recently, this method was paired with electrostatic charge steering mutations in the VH/VL and CH1/CL to direct the assembly of appropriate heavy and light chain combinations, facilitating the efficient production of bsAbs in single cells. 96 By comparison, the DuoBody platform utilizes controlled Fab‐arm exchange (cFAE) whereby IgG1 molecules with matched CH3 mutations (namely, K409R and F405L) can be dynamically recombined upon the reduction of heavy chain disulfides by 2‐MEA. 100

Mutations that enhance on‐target hexamerisation, such as Stellabody H429F, show remarkable compatibility with established bispecific engineering techniques, indicating that these mutations do not interfere with the critical CH3 mutation sites required for bispecific assembly. For example, the H429F mutation enhances the CDC potency of a biparatopic anti‐CD37 bsAb produced using “knob‐in‐hole” pairing and charge steering mutations (Figure 1F). This suggests that the enhanced CDC activity mediated by the formation of on‐target hexamers is maintained in bsAbs comprising H429F, with this particular bsAb targeting two non‐overlapping epitopes on CD37. Hexamer‐enhancing mutations are also compatible with the DuoBody platform, as exemplified by DuoHexaBody‐CD37; a biparatopic anti‐CD37 bsAb containing the E430G mutation. 101

bsAbs vs. heterohexamers

Using IgG hexamers, it is possible to achieve bi‐specificity (or even greater oligo‐specificity) in a hexameric complex in mixtures of conventional mAbs carrying hexamerisation mutations. Indeed, in some contexts, mixtures of hexamer‐enhanced conventional mAbs may confer superior activity compared to bsAbs with a hexamer enhancing mutation. In the case of DR5, an E430G‐modified biparatopic bsAb is less efficient at inducing cell death than a mixture of the individual mAbs carrying the E430G mutation that promotes the formation of on‐target hexamers. 81 This comparatively reduced potency of the bsAb format presumably reflects less efficient crosslinking of DR5 by the bispecific Ab. Indeed, this difference in activity related to antibody format was not observed in the use of a hexamer‐enhanced anti‐CD37 biparatopic bsAb, which induced greater CDC than mixtures of the mAbs. 101

The influence of epitopes on the antibody format for optimum hexamer formation is further highlighted by a biparatopic bsAb targeting HER2, zanidatamab, which has an unmodified Fc. Zanidatamab exhibits unprecedently high anti‐HER2 CDC activity, which is reported to result from the unique topology of its interaction with HER2 that promotes hexamer formation without any enhancing modification of the Fc. 102 The asymmetric format of zanidatamab, wherein one heavy chain has a conventional Fab and the other a scFv, also is utilized in CD20 bispecifics highly potent for CDC. 103 [Correction added on 22 Nov 2024, after first online publication: Reference #66 was corrected to #103.] In these and in other studies, such as Pande et al., 66 high complement activity is inferred to result from hexamer formation.

Therefore, mutations that enhance on‐target hexamerisation are compatible with bsAbs generated with different methods, however the activity of bsAbs with these mutations compared to mixtures of hexamer‐enhanced mAbs to each specificity may be target‐dependent and effects should therefore be evaluated on a case‐by‐case basis. Thus, as research continues to reveal the full potential of hexamerising mutations, there is a need for further investigation into the context of different target antigens and bsAb formats for hexamer‐enhanced bsAbs. Such research would provide additional insights into the efficacy and mechanisms of action of hexamer‐enhanced bsAbs across the many areas in which this technology could be applied.

CONCLUSION

Mutations that facilitate IgG hexamerisation offer a powerful, yet simple and highly adaptable, approach to overcome the limitations of current mAb therapies and pave the way for the development of a broad range of potent, next‐generation antibody‐based therapeutics. Much research and translational activity has focused on the impact of hexamerisation technology on the enhancement of CDC, while other functional effects are also apparent.

Complement activation by the ring of IgG‐Fc hexamers is strikingly like the overall topology of pentameric IgM, well known for efficient complement fixation. Mutations such as H429F drive the assembly of IgG into these “IgM”‐like mimics on the target. Mutations that strongly increase the Fc‐Fc interaction decouple target recognition from hexamer formation so that hexamers form in solution. In such instances, such as the H429Y mutation, oligomers are preformed independently of antigen binding. These may be considered as “IgM”‐like molecules in solution in contrast to those like H429F which become “IgM”‐like only once bound to their target.

The classical complement pathway is a powerful effector arm of the immune system which is regulated at multiple levels. At the level of initiation of CDC, IgG hexamerisation, apart from its clear biomedical applications, is another example of how multivalency, and possibly allostery, harnesses intrinsically very low affinity interactions to achieve the graded activation of a potent immune effector function. As such, the hexameric Fc platform of normal or mutated IgG1, or IgG3, likely represents the “physiological ceiling” for achievable CDC initiation without further mutagenesis to improve direct binding contacts. IgG‐hexamers and C1q thus exemplify a matching of ligand symmetry and recognition molecule symmetry.

An additional and coincidental consequence of exploiting the natural tendency of IgG for hexamerisation is the enhancement of signal transduction by agonistic mAbs. Just as effector function can be enhanced by hexamer mutations, so too can agonism of antibodies that act as surrogate ligands for receptors, most notably mAbs recognizing TNFR superfamily members, including DR5. As is the case for C1q and IgG hexamers in enhanced CDC, a compatible hexameric symmetry of these mutated mAbs and the trimeric TNFRSF members, which also exist as hexagonal signaling arrays, may underly the enhanced potency of these agonist mAb hexamers.

Though less investigated FcγR activation is also enhanced by some hexamerisation mutations. These will improve or confer FcγR effector potency of therapeutic mAbs in vivo for functions such as ADCC and ADCP by activating‐type FcγR or improve immunomodulation by FcγRIIb.

These hexamerising technologies provide antibody engineers with a choice of type of hexamerisation by simple amino acid substitution(s). The choice of mutation can selectively direct on‐target hexamerisation or, by different mutation, preformed hexamerisation which demonstrates the great versatility of these simple yet powerful hexamerising technologies.

For many uses, on‐target hexamerisation of mAbs may be preferred for particular applications especially where circulating monomeric IgG is desirable prior to hexameric assembly on the target, for example in enhancing complement activation on cell surfaces in oncology or autoimmunity. Alternatively, preformed “in solution” hexamers may be preferred where their oligovalency provides binding avidity advantage over monomeric IgG, for example in the enhanced neutralization of viruses and likely other multivalent soluble entities.

The use of hexamer technology has considerable practical applications and implications, and mutations that facilitate IgG hexamerisation offer a novel opportunity to overcome the limitations of current mAb therapies and thereby extends their utility into new therapeutic areas where use of conventional mAbs is considered ineffective. Hexamer technology potentially expands the suite of mechanisms contributing to the activity of antibody‐based therapeutics, reducing dosing requirements due to higher potency of target cell killing, and enhancing therapeutic efficacy on low abundance targets that are not amenable to treatment with conventional mAbs. For example, in our experience, one great advantage of hexamerising mAbs is dramatically demonstrated for low abundance targets. Stellabody modified clinically approved CD20 mAb is almost 10‐times more potent on primary lymphoma cells with low CD20 levels than standard‐of‐care mAbs (unpublished).

The application of hexamer technology also extends beyond uses in mAbs. In principle, any therapeutic biologic comprising a CH3 domain may benefit from use of hexamerising mutations such as H429F. The hexamer dependent improvement in, or conferment of, therapeutic potency has been demonstrated in bsAbs, but also interestingly in Fc fusion proteins. Fc‐fusions are not typically recognized as using immune effector function as part of their mechanisms of action. Clearly, hexamer mutation strategies are compatible with established antibody engineering techniques, which highlights the versatility and expansive potential capabilities of this innovative technology.

IgG hexamerising strategies use potentially powerful technologies, but they are influenced by non‐Fc factors. Indeed, the varied outcomes from enhancing hexamerisation in different IgG mAbs, that are otherwise identical in their constant regions, indicates the influence of antigen on the efficiency of hexamer formation. The sum of influences such as epitope density and occupation, topology, and mobility will all affect the permissiveness of hexamer formation for each unique mAb. In most clinical settings, single mAbs are used and these factors that constrain the presentation of Fcs, and so limit hexamer formation, may well limit the effectiveness of single mAb therapies. In the case of natural polyclonal IgG responses, more of these antigen variables will be broadly sampled so permitting hexamerisation to efficiently contribute to polyclonal IgG potency.

Thus, therapeutic strategies using mixtures of two or more mAbs, an approach not typically used in approved therapeutics, mimics the natural polyclonal antibody response and may provide hexameric platforms for complement activation or other mAb actions. As we have advocated previously for the use of mixtures of mAbs for FcR function enhancement, 25 so too mixtures of hexamerising mAbs, with or without other Fc modifications, likely provide greater control and potency for therapy than the use of single mAb preparations.

A further extension of this dual/oligoclonal approach to mAb therapy is the ability to use charge repulsion/charge steering mutations to direct the assembly of mAbs of different specificity into multi‐specific heterohexamers, which extends the utility of hexamer technology to directing effector responses that are highly specific for targets expressing multiple antigens.

The engineering of IgG hexamerisation is an exciting avenue for the improvement of therapeutic mAbs since it (i) is applicable to all IgG subclasses, (ii) is compatible with independent engineering of C1q and FcγR binding sites in CH2, (iii) can be incorporated into a variety of antibody formats, (iv) exhibits cooperative behavior in mixes of mAbs, (v) allows for the specific targeting of multiple antigen expressing targets, and (vi) potently increases the activity of some agonist mAbs, notably mAbs targeting TNFRSF members.

Hexamerising technologies will likely emerge as a significant advance in the development of novel, customized and potent antibody and antibody‐like therapeutics, leading ultimately to better patient outcomes.
