Comparative evaluation of diaphragmatic and myocardial navigation in coronary magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Dan Wang, Yinshuang Yang, Miao Yu, Junye Yao, Lu Han, Yang Duan, Aiyuan Li, Kai Xu, Xiaopeng Wang, Shuguang Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA) is limited by respiratory motion artifacts, for which diaphragmatic navigation (dNAV) is commonly applied. Myocardial navigation offers a direct motion-tracking alternative, but its performance in different anatomical placements remains underexplored. This study aimed to compare the image quality and clinical feasibility of diaphragmatic and myocardial navigation approaches at 3.0 T.MethodsThirty-three healthy volunteers underwent CMRA with four navigators: dNAV, left ventricle navigation (LvNAV), right atrial navigation (RaNAV), and apex navigation (ApNAV). Acquisition efficiency, scan duration, success rate, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) were analyzed for major coronary segments: right coronary artery (RCA), left anterior descending artery (LAD), and left circumflex artery (LCX). Two observers independently scored image clarity and sharpness (4-point scale) for segments. Interobserver agreement was assessed via intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsLvNAV and RaNAV both achieved a 100% technical completion rate (30/30). This was higher than the technical completion rates for ApNAV (97%, 29/30) and dNAV (90%, 27/30), with the latter two groups experiencing failures due to navigator drift. Scan durations showed no significant differences (dNAV: 478.70±104.68 s; LvNAV: 469.62±102.64 s; P=0.332). LvNAV yielded the highest SNR and CNR (P<0.05). Proximal and mid segments of the RCA, LAD, and LCX showed comparable visualization between dNAV and LvNAV (P>0.05), whereas distal segment visibility was reduced mainly with RaNAV and ApNAV, with dNAV and LvNAV preserving better distal visualization. Coronary visualization rates differed significantly (χ2=76.563, P<0.001), with dNAV and LvNAV outperforming other techniques.ConclusionsMyocardial navigation, particularly LvNAV, yields superior image quality and visualization performance comparable to dNAV, representing a promising alternative under conditions requiring enhanced motion stability. Personalized navigator selection based on respiratory patterns may further optimize CMRA outcomes.Keywords: Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA), myocardial navigation, diaphragmatic navigation (dNAV), motion compensation, image quality

Introduction

Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA) has emerged as a promising non-invasive technique for coronary artery disease (CAD) assessment (1). Unlike computed tomography angiography (CTA), CMRA is free from ionizing radiation and iodine-based contrast use. CMRA inherently suppresses calcification-induced blooming artifacts through intrinsic low proton signal characteristics in T1 and T2 weighted sequences (2). This feature enables more accurate detection and assessment of coronary artery lumen stenosis. However, widespread clinical adoption of CMRA remains limited by persistent challenges in mitigating physiological motion artifacts-particularly dual interference from cardiac contraction and respiratory motion. Optimizing CMRA acquisition strategies to enhance image quality and diagnostic accuracy remains a critical area of ongoing research.

Respiratory motion compensation represents a critical bottleneck in CMRA. Although electrocardiographic (ECG) gating effectively compensates for cardiac motion, traditional breath-hold strategies are impractical for CMRA due to prolonged acquisition time (>20 seconds). Consequently, free-breathing CMRA techniques incorporating with diaphragmatic navigation (dNAV) have become the predominant approach (3). dNAV restricts data acquisition to the quiescent phases of the respiratory cycle, enabling free breathing during scanning while enhancing feasibility and patient comfort. However, dNAV efficacy is compromised by irregular respiratory patterns, such as variations in breathing rate and depth, as well as the small and tortuous nature of coronary arteries (4). Furthermore, dNAV is suboptimal due to its reliance on unidirectional (superior-inferior) diaphragmatic motion tracking, which cannot sufficiently capture multi-directional cardiac displacement or non-rigid deformations of the heart and surrounding structures (5).

To overcome these limitations, myocardial navigators have been developed to track cardiac displacement directly (6). By aligning motion correction with true cardiac motion, myocardial navigation may enhance acquisition efficiency and image fidelity. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of both diaphragmatic and cardiac-based respiratory navigation for free-breathing coronary magnetic resonance (MR) angiography; however, myocardial navigation has generally been treated as a single entity without systematic comparison of different intra-cardiac navigator locations (7). Real-time MR observations have shown substantial inter-individual variability in diaphragm-coronary motion coupling during free breathing, supporting the need for navigator strategies that more directly reflect cardiac respiratory displacement (7). Given the distinct motion characteristics of the left ventricular wall, right atrium, and cardiac apex, it remains unclear how myocardial navigator placement influences coronary motion tracking and image quality. This study aimed to systematically evaluate diaphragmatic and myocardial navigators—including left ventricular, right atrial, and apical placements—assessing their effects on image quality, acquisition efficiency, and clinical feasibility at 3.0 T. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1914/rc).

Methods

Study population

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital (Approval No. XYFY2024-KL534-01). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. From January 2024 to November 2024, a total of 33 healthy volunteers (17 males, 16 females; age range, 25–50 years) were recruited. The inclusion criteria were as follows: absence of congenital or acquired heart disease, no metallic implants, normal heart rate and rhythm, and no contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Exclusion criteria included MRI contraindications, arrhythmia, claustrophobia, and a heart rate >85 beats per minute. After applying exclusion criteria, 30 participants were ultimately enrolled, and each participant underwent CMRA acquisition using all four navigation techniques, with the acquisition order randomized using a lottery-based method. Pre-examination preparation

Participants were instructed to avoid consuming substances that could elevate heart rate, such as alcohol, strong tea, caffeinated beverages, and phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil), for 12–24 hours before the examination. They were also required to fast for at least 2 hours before the scan to ensure gastric emptying and reduce gastrointestinal motion artifacts. During the examination, participants were placed in a supine position with head-first entry into the scanner, with both upper limbs resting naturally alongside the body. To optimize respiratory motion control, a respiratory gating airbag was secured at the point of maximal respiratory excursion on the abdomen using an abdominal belt, ensuring effective and precise respiratory gating. Participants underwent pre-scan breathing training to maintain a steady and consistent respiratory pattern, ensuring uniform respiratory amplitude throughout the scan. Additionally, four non-magnetic ECG electrodes were accurately placed on the precordium, and ECG calibration was performed to ensure clear R-wave detection and accurate cardiac triggering. If the R-wave signal was suboptimal, electrode positioning was adjusted until a stable trigger signal was obtained. During the preparation phase, participants were informed of key procedural details to alleviate anxiety, as reducing stress was considered beneficial for maintaining a stable heart rate and improving the success rate of the scan.

Scanner and imaging protocol

A 3.0T superconducting MRI system (Elition X, Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) equipped with a fully digital 32-channel phased-array body coil and a spinal array coil was used for CMRA acquisition. The scanning protocol began with three-plane localizer imaging, followed by a cine imaging sequence in a true coronal plane passing through the diaphragm and myocardium. A breath-hold-free real-time cine acquisition was required to cover 2–3 respiratory cycles and multiple cardiac cycles to ensure precise localization of the diaphragm and myocardium and to identify the end-expiratory quiescent phase for subsequent navigator placement and gating window definition. Subsequently, a 100-phase bright-blood cine sequence of the four-chamber view was acquired to determine the quiescent phase of coronary motion and to define the optimal data acquisition window. The trigger timing and acquisition duration for the four navigator techniques were kept consistent. Diaphragmatic and myocardial navigations were implemented using a volume-tracking (Gate and Track) technique, in which a cylindrical navigator excitation volume dynamically adjusted the imaging volume according to real-time motion of the tracked structure. Finally, a non-selective 3D-balanced turbo field echo (3D-BTFE) sequence was used for coronary MRI in the coronal plane, with each participant undergoing random assignment to one of the four navigator techniques. Scan duration was recorded for each technique.

3D-BTFE sequence parameters: field of view (FOV): 280 mm × 413 mm; matrix size: 200×295; voxel size: 1.4 mm × 1.4 mm × 1.4 mm; repetition time (TR): 2.5 ms; echo time (TE): 1.25 ms; flip angle: 70°; number of excitations (NEX): 1; compressed sensing (CS) factor: 4.5. Subjective image quality assessment

The CMRA images from the four navigator techniques were transferred to the Philips IntelliSpace Portal workstation for post-processing. Image reconstruction was performed using curved planar reformation (CPR). Two experienced radiologists with more than 10 years of expertise in MR diagnostics independently evaluated the images. The coronary artery segmentation was based on the American Heart Association (AHA) 15-segment model.

For this study, eight key segments from three major coronary arteries were selected for evaluation (8):

Image segments receiving a Grade III or higher were considered to have high-quality images with reliable diagnostic value.

Objective image quality assessment

Objective image assessment was conducted based on the 2014 Society of Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (SCCT) coronary artery segmentation guidelines (1). To minimize partial volume effects and artifacts caused by small vessel diameters, regions of interest (ROIs) of identical size were placed at specific anatomical locations: aortic arch (AO) at the origin of the RCA, proximal and mid RCA, proximal LAD and proximal CX.

Signal intensity (SI) was measured for the AO and the three main coronary arteries. Care was taken to avoid vessel walls, adipose tissue, plaques, and stenotic regions when positioning the ROIs.

For noise measurement, an ROI of equal size was placed in the shoulder muscle at the same imaging level as the aortic ROI. The standard deviation (SD) of the muscle SI was used as the noise value.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Quantitative data that followed a normal distribution and passed Mauchly’s test of sphericity were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed data or those that did not meet sphericity assumptions were expressed as median [Q1; Q3] and analyzed using the Friedman non-parametric test to determine whether there were statistically significant differences in SNR and CNR among the three major coronary arteries. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage) and compared using the χ2 test. Post-hoc analyses were corrected using the Bonferroni method to adjust for multiple comparisons.

The inter-observer agreement between the two radiologists was assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC): ICC ≤0.40: Poor agreement, 0.40< ICC ≤0.75: Moderate to good agreement, 0.75< ICC ≤1.00: Excellent agreement. The final subjective image quality score was determined by averaging the scores assigned by the two radiologists. Coronary visualization rates were compared among the four navigation techniques, and differences in coronary visualization based on navigator placement were analyzed using the χ2 test. A P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Subjective image quality assessment

After excluding two participants due to claustrophobia and one due to frequent coughing, 30 volunteers [mean age 32±7.79 years, 16 (59%) female] successfully underwent CMRA scans using all four navigator techniques, with an average heart rate of 70.5 beats per minute and an average body mass index (BMI) of 22.63 kg/m2.

During the imaging process, three cases in the dNAV group and one in the ApNAV group exhibited navigator drift over time. However, all participants in the LvNAV and RaNAV groups successfully completed the scans without issues. The technical completion rate of LvNAV and RaNAV were both 100% (30/30), which was higher than ApNAV (97%, 29/30) and dNAV (90%, 27/30). Navigator drift affected four individual navigator scans, including two scans (dNAV and ApNAV) in the same participant. To ensure a standardized comparison among the four navigator techniques, a final cohort of 27 volunteers was included in the analysis. The scan durations for the four navigator techniques were as follows: 478.70±104.68 s for dNAV, 469.62±102.64 s for LvNAV, 494.76±96.77 s for RaNAV, and 478.00±97.62 s for ApNAV. The differences in scan time among the four groups were not statistically significant (F=1.156, P=0.332). Acquisition efficiency differed significantly among the four navigation techniques (F=11.941, P<0.001). The mean acquisition efficiencies were 52.8%±3.83% for dNAV, 53.1%±3.73% for LvNAV, 51.1%±4.05% for ApNAV, and 51.3%±3.39% for RaNAV. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons demonstrated significant differences between dNAV and ApNAV, dNAV and RaNAV, LvNAV and ApNAV, and LvNAV and RaNAV (all P<0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed between dNAV and LvNAV or between ApNAV and RaNAV.

Each of the 27 volunteers underwent four separate CMRA scans, generating a total of 81 coronary arteries per group and 216 major coronary segments for analysis. According to the image quality grading system, segments with scores of 3 or higher were classified as having excellent visualization, whereas those with lower scores were deemed suboptimal. Based on the mean scores assigned by two independent radiologists (Table 1), 178 coronary segments (82.4%) in the dNAV group received a score of 3 or higher, compared to 175 segments (81.0%) in the LvNAV group, 115 segments (53.2%) in the RaNAV group, and 118 segments (54.6%) in the ApNAV group. A statistically significant difference in coronary visualization rates was observed among the four navigator techniques (χ2=76.563, P<0.001). Both dNAV (82.4%) and LvNAV (81.0%) demonstrated significantly higher visualization rates compared to RaNAV (53.2%) and ApNAV (54.6%). However, there was no significant difference between dNAV and LvNAV in terms of overall coronary visualization rates.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive comparison of four navigation strategies for free-breathing CMRA. LvNAV and RaNAV achieved higher success rates than conventional dNAV and ApNAV, with LvNAV demonstrating the best image quality metrics, including SNR and CNR, while maintaining similar scan times across all approaches. These findings indicate that myocardial navigation, by directly monitoring cardiac motion with less respiratory interference, can more effectively enhance CMRA stability and image quality.

The results align with previous literature underscoring the importance of navigation techniques in reducing respiratory motion artifacts (10-12). Conventional dNAV is limited to craniocaudal low-frequency respiratory motion, which is insufficient for accommodating the high-frequency, periodic, and complex three-dimensional motion of the heart (13). In contrast, the advantages of myocardial navigation are consistent with the advancement of self-navigation techniques (14,15), which improve imaging efficiency by directly tracking target organ motion. LvNAV, by tracking left ventricular motion, captures the heart’s intrinsic displacement more accurately than RaNAV or ApNAV, which may miss subtle myocardial movements due to their anatomical placements (11,16). The left ventricle’s robust contractility provides a stable reference for motion correction, enhancing SNR and CNR, as evidenced by our results (Table 2), whereas RaNAV’s reliance on right atrial motion, which is less synchronized with coronary motion, introduces higher variability (17). Similarly, ApNAV’s apical positioning may fail to account for torsional heart movements, leading to suboptimal distal coronary visualization (18). The distal segment degradation in RaNAV and ApNAV aligns with prior findings that non-ventricular navigators struggle with coronary displacement during systole (10,19,20). In contrast, while dNAV is limited to tracking craniocaudal low-frequency respiratory motion of the diaphragm, its comparable visualization performance in proximal coronary segments (LAD_pro, RCA_pro, LCX_pro; P>0.05) suggests retained clinical utility in patients with regular respiration. Furthermore, the 100% technical completion rate of LvNAV indicates stable navigator performance without drift, in contrast to dNAV, whose performance may be affected by diaphragm–heart motion decoupling. These insights highlight the need for navigator selection based on cardiac motion fidelity. Moreover, this study found that left ventricular myocardial navigation significantly improved both technical completion rate and image quality, supporting recent strategies for precise cardiac motion correction and image quality enhancement (21,22). Nevertheless, our findings suggest that myocardial navigation may outperform existing self-navigation techniques in vascular clarity improvement (4,23). These results highlight the pivotal role of navigator positioning selection in motion compensation technologies.

From a clinical perspective, LvNAV may represent a promising option in scenarios requiring enhanced motion robustness, given its stable navigator performance and higher proportion of diagnostic-quality coronary segments observed in this study, while dNAV retains advantages in routine applications for its operational simplicity. It should be noted that CS reconstruction alters noise characteristics, and therefore conventional SNR and CNR definitions are not strictly quantitative in a physical sense. In the present study, these metrics were used for relative comparison under identical reconstruction conditions and were interpreted in conjunction with diagnostic image quality outcomes rather than as absolute measures of performance. RaNAV and ApNAV require technical refinements such as gating window optimization to enhance reliability. These findings not only provide evidence-based guidance for personalized navigator selection but also establish a foundation for subsequent research targeting populations with arrhythmias or respiratory dysfunction.

This study is limited by its small sample size and the exclusive inclusion of healthy volunteers. The lack of disease-specific subgroup analyses and standardized parameter tuning for myocardial navigation also restricts the clinical scope. Validation in larger and more diverse patient cohorts—including those with arrhythmia or structural heart disease—would enhance generalizability. Future multicenter studies are needed to establish robust, generalizable protocols. Additionally, integrating artificial intelligence for real-time motion prediction (24) or developing multimodal navigation techniques (25) could further reduce coupled respiratory and cardiac motion interference.

Conclusions

This study compared the performance of different navigator placements in CMRA and demonstrated that myocardial navigation, particularly LvNAV, provides significant advantages in improving technical completion rate and image quality. In clinical practice, selecting an appropriate navigation technique based on a patient’s respiratory pattern is recommended to optimize CMRA imaging outcomes. Future research should further explore the optimal parameter settings for myocardial navigation and its potential applications in complex clinical scenarios to enhance its diagnostic utility.
