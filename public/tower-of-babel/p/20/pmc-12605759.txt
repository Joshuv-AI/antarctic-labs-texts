Stereotactic Step-by-step Dissection of the Anterior Transpetrosal Approach: A Three-dimensional Photogrammetry-based Educational Tool for Neurosurgeons
Authors: Yoshifumi TAKATSUME, Shunsuke SHIBAO, Takashi HORIGUCHI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Skull base surgery requires precise spatial recognition; however, existing educational tools remain limited. To address this issue, we developed a step-by-step stereotactic three-dimensional anatomical model of the anterior transpetrosal approach using digital photogrammetry. Cadaveric dissection was subsequently performed in 17 steps, of which 10 were reconstructed into three-dimensional models. The images were processed using 3DF Zephyr and Blender to create interactive three-dimensional portable document-format models, enabling dynamic anatomical visualization. The three-dimensional models provided high-resolution visualization of the complex skull base structures, including the trigeminal nerve, trochlear nerve, geniculate ganglion, semicircular canal, and internal carotid artery. Additionally, they supported keyhole surgery simulations by allowing users to manipulate and explore anatomical structures from multiple perspectives. However, the compatibility and performance varied depending on the device and software used. This study demonstrated the feasibility of a three-dimensional photogrammetry-based educational tool for skull base anatomy. By providing interactive three-dimensional visualization, this tool may complement existing surgical training methods and enhance anatomical understanding. However, further studies are required to evaluate its practical impact in structured educational settings.

Introduction

Recent advancements have significantly refined the surgical approaches to the skull base, with cadaver dissection facilitating widespread dissemination. However, the limited number of actual skull base surgery cases underscores the necessity of developing alternative educational tools to enhance neurosurgeons' understanding of the skull base anatomy. Although cadaveric dissection remains the gold standard for anatomical education, its availability is limited, and traditional two-dimensional (2D) representations fail to capture the spatial complexity of some regions.1,2)

Skull base surgery requires surgeons to develop transparent 3D spatial recognition to visualize structures obscured by overlying tissues. Recent advances in neurosurgical training have emphasized the role of interactive three-dimensional (3D) visualization tools, including augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR), in enhancing spatial comprehension and procedural planning.1-4) Additionally, the concept of “keyhole surgery” is vital, as it allows maximal observation of targeted structures by altering the visual axis. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and imaging, the intricate anatomy of the skull base continues to pose challenges, particularly in approaches such as the anterior transpetrosal approach (ATPA), in which precision is crucial.5) The complex interplay among vital structures necessitates a profound spatial understanding to avoid complications.

We hypothesized that stereotactic step-by-step dissection, which enables the sequential visualization of the surgical field at each anatomical layer from multiple viewing angles, would benefit learning by simulating skull-based surgeries. Existing educational resources often lack the ability to comprehensively visualize step-by-step stereotactic anatomy as well as the dynamic changes occurring during an ATPA. This knowledge gap hinders surgical training and can affect patient outcomes.

This study aimed to address this gap by developing a 3D step-by-step anatomical model of the ATPA using digital photogrammetry.6) Digital photogrammetry is increasingly being applied in anatomical education to generate high-fidelity 3D models from cadaveric specimens. Further, this method has been increasingly explored across various surgical fields, including orthopedic surgical procedures, and has been applied to reconstruct anatomical structures for educational purposes.7) By leveraging this technique, our model provides a detailed, interactive visualization of the surgical field, enabling surgeons to explore complex anatomies from various perspectives. Furthermore, it highlights the critical neurovascular structures, aiding in surgical planning and risk assessment. By providing a comprehensive educational tool, this study aimed to enhance surgical training and improve patient safety during skull base surgery.

Technical Notes

Preparation

We used the remains of those who, before death, provided informed consent for body donation as educational and research material and provided their consent after learning how they would be used. This study of human cadavers was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with approval granted by the Board of Ethics at Keio University School of Medicine (Approval No. 20070026, 20210091). Efforts were made to follow all local and international ethical guidelines and regulations regarding the use of human cadaveric donors in anatomical research.8) Formalin-fixed cadaver heads were used in this study. To enhance the visualization of vascular structures, red silicone was injected into the internal carotid and vertebral arteries, while blue silicone was injected into the internal jugular vein.

Pre-dissection preparation

Silicone injections were administered to the head of the cadaver to effectively delineate the vasculature. To prevent movement during imaging, the head was secured using a Mayfield three-point fixation device. Following this preparation, all the sections were dissected under a microscope.

Surgical procedure and step-by-step dissection

A modified ATPA was performed through a frontotemporal craniotomy to secure sufficient illumination of the surgical field. The dissection progressed through 17 stages, from the skin to the subdural space of the posterior cranial fossa. Of these, 10 key stages were selected for inclusion in the 3D photogrammetry-based model (Fig. 1).

1. Epidural detachment of the middle cranial fossa dura mater (Fig. 1a).

2. Ligation of the middle meningeal artery (MMA) (Fig. 1b).

3. Detachment of the greater superficial petrosal nerve (GSPN) occurred interdurally, with the exposure of Kawase's triangle bordered by the GSPN, trigeminal nerve impression, and arcuate eminence (Fig. 1c).

4. Removal of Kawase's triangle (Fig. 1d).

5. Ligation of the superior petrosal sinus and tentorium cerebelli (Fig. 1e).

6. Removal of the middle cranial fossa dura mater (Fig. 1f).

7. Exposure of the dura mater from the internal auditory canal to the geniculate ganglion of the facial nerve (Fig. 1g).

8. Opening of the dura mater of the internal auditory canal, tympanic cavity, and superior semicircular canal (Fig. 1h).

9. Removal of the trigeminal nerve (Fig. 1i).

10. Exposure of the C6 segment of the internal carotid artery (Fig. 1j).

Image acquisition and processing

Images were acquired using a Sony ILCE-6000 camera mounted on a Meta Corporation Motion Control Camera (MCC-K6) equipped with a Micro Nikkor 55 mm F2.8 lens. Photographs were taken 40 cm from each specimen. To create the stereoscopic images, 3 photographs were captured from each viewpoint: one centered and 2 shifted by 20 mm to the left and right, respectively. The MCC-K6, with its computer-controlled movement, ensures precise and reproducible camera positioning. At each dissection step, 36 photographs were captured from 12 viewpoints, including stereo pairs, by systematically moving the camera across 3 horizontal and 4 vertical steps at 15° intervals (Fig. 2). Twelve high-resolution images were acquired for each step, while cross-polarization photography effectively minimized reflections, ensuring image quality suitable for 3D reconstruction.9)

3D model reconstruction

The 3D models were generated from the captured images using the 3DF Zephyr software (3DFLOW, Verona, Italy), producing textured obj files. A 3D model of each layer was created using the 36 photographs.

Blender software (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, Netherlands) was used to refine the 3D models by cropping unwanted elements and aligning steps using the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration and alignment plugin.10) This alignment process utilizes common anatomical features present in each layer to ensure accurate registration.

The photogrammetry process generated textured 3D models with a resolution of approximately 8,000 × 8,000 pixels and 700,000 faces. A comparison of the stereo images extracted from the 3D models and the original stereo photographs demonstrated excellent fidelity in terms of anatomical detail and depth perception. The fine structures, including delicate threads and neurovascular elements, were accurately reproduced. Although the models were constructed from 36 viewpoints (12 primary viewpoints and stereo pairs), the resulting 3D models allowed seamless navigation and visualization from any arbitrary angle. Areas not captured in the original photographs are represented as voids in the 3D models. Furthermore, ICP-based alignment enabled precise registration of the steps, facilitating dynamic visualization of the dissection sequence.

Creating 3D portable document format

MeshLab (https://www.meshlab.net/)11) was applied to export 3D models as Intermediate Data Text Format (IDTF) files. The IDTF files were manually edited to combine the layer names with the corresponding 3D objects into a single file. The combined IDTF file was converted into a u3d file suitable for embedding in a portable document format (PDF) using IDTFconverter.exe (https://sourceforge.net/projects/u3d/). Finally, Adobe Acrobat software was used to create an interactive 3D PDF, displaying each anatomical layer sequentially. The camera settings within the 3D PDF were configured to provide optimal visualization of the dissected structures.

Results

The cadaveric specimens demonstrated excellent tissue preservation, and pre-dissection preparations were completed without complications. A modified ATPA was successfully performed, providing high-resolution visualization of the skull base in a sequential, step-by-step manner (Fig. 1 and Supplemental Video 1).

During the initial stage of dissection, epidural detachment of the middle cranial fossa dura mater was performed, enabling identification of the MMA at the foramen spinosum (Fig. 1a). Subsequent ligation of the MMA was clearly demonstrated at the convergence point with the dura (Fig. 1b). As the dissection advanced medially, the GSPN, was detached interdurally, allowing exposure of Kawase's triangle, which was bordered by the GSPN, trigeminal nerve impression, and arcuate eminence (Fig. 1c). Following this, the removal of Kawase's triangle provided direct access to the posterior fossa dura mater (Fig. 1d). The superior petrosal sinus and tentorium cerebelli were then ligated, exposing the course of the trigeminal nerve from its origin at the pons to its entry into Meckel's cave, as well as adjacent structures such as the trochlear and abducens nerves (Fig. 1e). Further removal of the middle cranial fossa dura mater enhanced the visibility of these deep neurovascular elements (Fig. 1f). Progressing deeper, the dura mater overlying the internal auditory canal was exposed, enabling visualization of the geniculate ganglion of the facial nerve (Fig. 1g). Opening of the dura covering the internal auditory canal, tympanic cavity, and superior semicircular canal revealed the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves within the canal and the cross-section of the semicircular canal (Fig. 1h). The trigeminal nerve was subsequently removed, revealing the abducens nerve as it traversed Dorello's canal toward the cavernous sinus (Fig. 1i). Finally, the C6 segment of the internal carotid artery was exposed, deep to the GSPN and adjacent bony structures (Fig. 1j).

Sequential visualization of each step enhances the ability to understand spatial relationships between anatomical structures, supporting “transparent 3D structural recognition” (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Video 2). Specifically, key structures such as the trigeminal nerve, trochlear nerve (Fig. 3a), geniculate ganglion (Fig. 3b), superior semicircular canal, and C6 segment of the internal carotid artery (Fig. 3c), which are crucial for meticulous dissection during Kawase's triangle removal, were clearly demonstrated.

The ability to manipulate the viewing angle within each step enhanced the spatial perception of the ‘keyhole surgery,' thereby simulating the effect of altering the optical axis of the microscope during surgery (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Video 3). Notably, the abducens nerve, which was initially obscured by the trigeminal nerve, became visible when the viewing angle was adjusted (Fig. 4a). Similarly, the root exit/entry zone of the facial/vestibulocochlear nerve complex, which is typically hidden, was revealed by manipulating this perspective (Fig. 4b).

Anatomical structures observed depending on the viewing axis (view-axis-dependent anatomical differences). In direction a left side, the abducens nerve (VI) is not visible. However, when the viewing axis is adjusted from above to below, as seen on the right side (positions 1 to 4), the abducens nerve becomes observable. In direction b, left side, the exit points of the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves (VII, VIII) from the brainstem are not visible, when the viewing axis is adjusted to an anterosuperior perspective, as in b right side (positions 7-12), these exits become visible.

3D PDF creation and validation

A 3D PDF incorporating the layered anatomical models was successfully created (Supplemental PDF and Videos 1-3). The model allows interactive manipulation of anatomical layers, including toggling visibility and adjusting transparency. To ensure performance across standard desktop systems, the model was optimized by reducing texture resolution and polygon count. Compatibility was confirmed only on Adobe Acrobat Reader for Windows and Mac OS, as other PDF viewers and mobile applications do not support 3D model rendering.4,12)

Discussion

Challenges in anatomical education for skull base surgery

Skull base surgery involves complex anatomical structures that require detailed anatomical knowledge and advanced spatial awareness before surgery.13) Surgeons must accurately comprehend the three-dimensional anatomy of the surgical field, including the surrounding structures, within narrow operative windows. This understanding is critical for identifying and preserving vital vessels and nerves hidden deep within the surgical field, which is essential for ensuring safe surgical procedures.14-16)

Cadaver-based dissection training is beneficial for improving surgical skills; however, access to such training is limited due to the scarcity of cadaveric specimens, particularly outside university hospitals.13) Additionally, although clinical training allows for the sequential step-by-step progression of surgical procedures from the surface inward, it does not enable a reverse step-by-step review of the procedure.

Conventional 2D videos and anatomical atlases are limited by their flat and fixed perspectives. Even with multiple images, learners are unable to observe anatomical structures from the diverse viewing angles they wish to explore. In this study, we developed the “Stereotactic, Step-by-Step 3D Photogrammetry-Based Educational Tool” as an anatomical learning tool. This tool enables reverse, step-by-step reviews of surgical procedures and observations from multiple perspectives, thereby addressing the limitations of traditional educational methods.

Stereotactic, step-by-step dissection

This Stereotactic, Step-by-Step, 3D Photogrammetry-Based Educational Tool reproduces the intricate anatomical structures of the skull base using high-resolution 3D models, thereby offering a level of detail and interactivity that surpasses traditional educational resources. The current model, created using photogrammetry, accurately reconstructed the complex anatomical structures of the skull base within the areas captured by the camera. This model, which allows for 3D learning through diverse technologies such as AR and VR, enables surgeons to explore the surgical field from various angles, identify critical neurovascular structures, and virtually simulate surgical procedures. In this study, the model is integrated into a versatile 3D PDF format. Because of the substantial amount of data required for 3D models, 3D PDFs tend to have large file sizes. To address this, it is necessary to carefully select the number of layers to include, reduce the number of faces in the model, and reduce the resolution of the textures. Additionally, 3D PDFs have limitations regarding viewing environments, as they can only be accessed using the desktop version of Adobe Acrobat software. Even within Adobe Acrobat, 3D PDFs cannot be viewed in a browser-based version or on smartphones and tablet applications. Furthermore, the performance of the computer used to view the 3D PDF can affect playback, and customizing the mouse controls is challenging.

These constraints reduce the versatility of 3D PDFs. Despite these limitations, the widespread adoption of the PDF format offers significant advantages. Without specialized applications or equipment, 3D PDFs can promote visual understanding and enable interactive operations. Moreover, the ability to implement security measures such as password protection offers an additional advantage when utilizing anatomical visuals, ensuring controlled access, and safeguarding sensitive content. Given these features, 3D PDFs have considerable potential as effective learning and communication tools in medical education and clinical settings.4,12,17)

This tool enables users to change the viewing angles at each layer, providing an intuitive experience of how the surgical field appears under different optical axes of the microscope. This is particularly relevant in minimally invasive “keyhole” surgeries. They can also select specific layers of interest and compare changes in the surgical field when transitioning from forward to reverse dissection, and vice versa.

A significant advantage of this tool is its accessibility, which enables learning irrespective of time or location. By continuously observing each layer, users can grasp the spatial relationships between deeper anatomical structures, thereby fostering a transparent understanding of 3D anatomy.

Traditional anatomical teaching tools using 2D static images are inherently constrained by the angle of image acquisition. Learners are limited to viewing structures from a fixed perspective, which fails to replicate the dynamic, multi-angled visualization required in real surgical settings. Particularly in skull base and microsurgery, surgeons often adjust the optical axis of the microscope to inspect deep anatomical layers from different directions.

In contrast, the photogrammetry-based 3D models developed in this study enable users to explore the surgical field freely, even from angles not originally photographed. This capability replicates the act of ‘looking around corners,' which is critical during keyhole surgery.

Furthermore, the use of ICP alignment between dissection stages enabled seamless switching between pre- and post-manipulation views. Importantly, this alignment can be achieved post hoc, even if the images were captured without rigid fixation or motorized camera control. This opens the possibility of creating step-by-step 3D surgical education tools from intraoperative photographs, thereby extending the utility of this technique beyond the laboratory and into clinical practice.

Stereotactic, step-by-step visualization of the anatomy via the ATPA

The ATPA effectively accessed the petroclival regions. However, a comprehensive understanding of the relevant anatomy is essential for safe application. Some key anatomical structures encountered during the ATPA include the MMA, GSPN, arcuate eminence, trigeminal nerve impression, trigeminal nerve, trochlear nerve, abducens nerve, facial nerve, and cochlear nerve. In this study, step-by-step stereotactic dissection was used to successfully identify these critical anatomical structures.

The MMA is encountered to the most lateral extent during extradural dissection. Hemostatic techniques such as removal of the outer portion of the foramen spinosum and transection of the MMA at its convergence with the dura have been reported.18) These techniques were replicated using a stereotactic, Step-by-Step Dissection method.

GSPN: The GSPN serves as a crucial landmark and aids in protecting critical structures during the drilling of Kawase's triangle.19) Facial nerve monitoring enhances nerve preservation.

Trigeminal Nerve: The trigeminal nerve is a central structure in the ATPA surgical field and requires careful attention during posterior fossa dural incisions.20,21) By transparently overlaying the pre- and post-incision layers, the trajectory of the trigeminal nerve can be visualized preoperatively, thereby facilitating safe dissection.

Trochlear Nerve: The trochlear nerve courses along the tentorial edge and requires special attention during tentorial incisions. Overlapping the pre- and post-incision layers with transparency allows preoperative visualization of the course, ensuring a safe incision.

Abducens Nerve: This nerve is located in the inferior portion of the ATPA surgical field. In a step-by-step stereotactic dissection, adjusting the viewing axis from superior to inferior facilitates identification.

Facial and Cochlear Nerves: Surgical manipulation of the ATPA was performed anterior to the facial and cochlear nerves to minimize the risk of injury to these structures. However, careful attention to the geniculate ganglion of the facial nerve is critical during drilling in the Kawase's triangle. Stereotactic Step-by-Step Dissection provides a clear understanding of the positional relationships between these structures. Additionally, the origins of the facial and cochlear nerves in the brainstem were confirmed by adjusting the viewing axis from anterior to posterior.

Overall, this stereotactic, Step-by-Step dissection approach enhances spatial awareness and facilitates safe surgical procedures by enabling visualization of critical structures at each stage of the ATPA. Although this study presents a novel approach to anatomical education using 3D photogrammetry, its full impact on surgical training has yet to be formally assessed. Future research should therefore include structured evaluations involving learners, such as pre- and post-training assessments, to determine their educational effectiveness. Additionally, comparative studies using traditional educational methods, including cadaveric dissection and 2D anatomical atlases, will be valuable for defining their role in neurosurgical training.

Conclusion

Step-by-step stereotactic dissection of the ATPA was performed to serve as the foundation for the development of a 3D Photogrammetry-Based Educational Tool. This tool facilitates the transparent 3D visualization of anatomical structures, aids in the study of skull base surgery, and enables flexible adjustments to the viewing axis. Detailed anatomical models have the potential to enhance education on surgical anatomy.
