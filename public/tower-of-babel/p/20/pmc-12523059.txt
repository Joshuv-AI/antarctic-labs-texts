Effects of exercise alone or combined with dietary restriction on leptin and adiponectin in overweight or obese individuals: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Xianyang Xin, Yongqing Guo, Longyan Liu, Qinzheng Liu, Jun Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effects and ranking of different exercise modalities, with or without dietary restriction, on adipokine levels (leptin and adiponectin) in overweight and obese individuals. Additionally, the independent contribution of dietary restriction to adipokine level improvement was analyzed.MethodsA systematic search of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published between January 2000 and November 2024 was conducted in Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and EBSCO databases. A network meta-analysis (NMA) was performed to integrate the results.ResultsA total of 87 RCTs were included. The regulatory effects of different exercise modalities and their combinations with dietary restriction on adipokine levels varied. Nearly all interventions reduced leptin levels and increased adiponectin levels. Among them, dietary restriction combined with resistance training (D + RT) exhibited the most significant effect on reducing leptin levels. Regarding adiponectin, dietary restriction combined with combined exercise (D + COM) showed the greatest improvement. Notably, dietary restriction combined with exercise (D + Ex) was more effective in reducing leptin levels, whereas the mechanisms underlying adiponectin enhancement were more complex, suggesting that HIIT, RT, and COM alone may be preferable choices.ConclusionExercise interventions, particularly when combined with dietary restriction, are more effective in reducing leptin levels, while HIIT, COM, and RT alone appear to be more effective in increasing adiponectin levels. These findings provide scientific evidence for personalized obesity management strategies and highlight the synergistic role of exercise and dietary restriction in adipokine regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01316-x.

Background

The prevalence of overweight and obesity is increasing rapidly worldwide and has become a major risk factor for various metabolic diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [1–3]. Leptin and adiponectin, as key adipokines, play essential roles in these metabolic processes. Leptin is closely associated with energy homeostasis, appetite regulation, reproductive function, tumorigenesis, and autoimmune diseases [4–7]. Similarly, adiponectin is critical for metabolic regulation and energy intake, while also exhibiting anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects and improving insulin sensitivity [8–10]. Circulating adiponectin levels serve as an important biomarker of adipose tissue health [10]. In obese individuals, dysregulated adipose tissue secretion results in excessive leptin production and reduced adiponectin levels [11–14], Elevated circulating leptin levels, leptin resistance, and hypoadiponectinemia are closely linked to the pathophysiological risks of obesity and CVD, potentially leading to overweight, obesity, CVD, and other metabolic disorders [15, 16]. Therefore, effectively regulating adipokine levels is of significant clinical importance in preventing and managing obesity-related diseases.

Exercise training (Ex) and dietary restriction (D) are first-line obesity interventions that promote negative energy balance [17–20]. The resulting weight loss is closely associated with improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors [21–24]. In addition, either exercise intervention or dietary intervention alone also exerts multiple beneficial effects on individuals with obesity or overweight [25–28]. Numerous studies suggest that exercise combined with dietary restriction (D + Ex) may exert synergistic effects, leading to greater health improvements following the intervention [29–32]. Several meta-analyses have demonstrated that both exercise training and dietary restriction (Ex or D) alone can effectively reduce leptin levels and increase adiponectin levels [33–36], However, the effects of D + Ex appear to be comparable to those of either intervention alone. A recent meta-analysis [17], indicated that in overweight and obese populations, exercise alone (Ex) was less effective than dietary restriction (D) or combined exercise and dietary restriction (D + Ex) in reducing leptin and increasing adiponectin levels. Furthermore, D + Ex was not superior to D alone, suggesting that dietary restriction plays a key role in modulating leptin and adiponectin concentrations. However, different types of exercise training may exert distinct effects on leptin and adiponectin [37–39]. Not all exercise modalities are necessarily less effective than dietary restriction, and specific combinations of exercise and dietary restriction may lead to differential effects, potentially optimizing the modulation of adipokine levels. Nevertheless, these findings remain controversial.

A study conducted in obese individuals reported that aerobic exercise combined with dietary restriction (D + AE) significantly increased adiponectin levels, whereas no significant change was observed in the dietary restriction (D) alone group [40]. Another study found that combined exercise with dietary restriction (D + COM) was less effective in improving leptin levels than combined exercise (COM) alone [41]. However, some studies suggest that exercise combined with dietary restriction is more effective than dietary restriction alone. A study in obese women reported that resistance training combined with dietary restriction (D + RT) was significantly more effective than resistance training (RT) alone in reducing leptin levels [42]. Thus, this study aims to systematically evaluate the effects of different exercise interventions (with or without dietary restriction) on adipokine levels in overweight and obese populations through a large-scale network meta-analysis (NMA). By analyzing the effectiveness of various combined interventions, this study seeks to identify the optimal intervention strategy and investigate key factors influencing treatment outcomes. The findings will provide scientific evidence and evidence-based intervention strategies for obesity management, offering valuable insights for clinicians in developing personalized intervention plans.

Methods

This study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [43]. The review is registered with PROSPERO under the ID 2024-CRD42024605944 (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/).

Search strategy

We conducted a search in databases including Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, combination of MeSH and Emtree terms and keywords were used. All RCTs published in English between 1999 to October 2024. To avoid missing any eligible studies, we examined the reference lists of systematic reviews published in the past three years [7, 8, 17, 44] and performed a manual search to supplement relevant research. The complete strategy search for each database is available in Supplementary 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study followed the Participants, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design (PICOS) principles to establish the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

(1) The study population included overweight or obese adults and children. For adults, the European classification defines overweight as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² and obesity as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², while the Asian classification defines overweight as BMI ≥ 24 kg/m² and obesity as BMI ≥ 28 kg/m². For children and adolescents, overweight and obesity were determined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) standards [45] or nationally recognized criteria in the respective countries [46].

(2) The study included at least one experimental group in which participants underwent an organized exercise training program lasting a minimum of 4 weeks. The types of exercise interventions included in this study (detailed in Supplementary 3) were Aerobic Exercise (AE), Resistance Training (RT), Combined Exercise (COM), High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT), Dietary Restriction (D), Dietary Restriction with Aerobic Exercise (D + AE), Dietary Restriction with Resistance Training (D + RT), Dietary Restriction with Combined Aerobic and Resistance Training (D + AE + RT), Dietary Restriction with High-Intensity Interval Training (D + HIIT), and Control Group (CON).

(3) The control group included participants who did not receive any exercise intervention or only engaged in light stretching activities. Control groups adhered to a sedentary lifestyle, maintaining their previous physical activity levels. Studies involving two or more exercise intervention types or those without a control group but allowing direct data comparisons between intervention methods were also included.

(4) Outcome Measures: Studies that assessed leptin and adiponectin levels before and after exercise interventions were included.

(5) Study Design: Only RCTs were included.

(6) Exclusion Criteria: Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: ① Non-English language publications; ② Theses, conference abstracts, or records without full-text availability; ③ Studies with unavailable outcome data, where the corresponding author could not be contacted; ④ Duplicate publications; ⑤ Studies involving special populations (e.g., pregnant women, postoperative patients, etc.).

Literature screening

During the initial screening phase, all retrieved records were imported into reference management software (EndNote X9) to eliminate duplicates. Two researchers (XYX and YQG) then independently screened titles and abstracts to identify all potentially relevant studies, reaching out to authors when necessary to obtain missing data. Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were independently confirmed and assessed by the same two researchers (XYX and YQG), with any disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third expert (JX) if needed. Finally, the two independent researchers (XYX and YQG) reviewed the full texts of the included articles to make the final inclusion decisions. Inter-rater agreement between the two reviewers was assessed using Cohen’s κ statistic, with an initial κ of 0.83, indicating almost perfect agreement [47]. Any disagreements were subsequently resolved through discussion according to the pre-specified protocol.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently and in a double-blinded manner by two researchers (XYX and LYL), using a self-designed statistical table based on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The extracted data included: (1) first author, (2) publication year, (3) intervention details (sample size, sex, age, BMI, and body weight for each group), (4) intervention characteristics (exercise modality, duration, frequency, and session length), and (5) outcome measures. The outcome measures included pre- and post-intervention values for both the intervention and control groups. If the full text did not provide the necessary outcome data, the corresponding author was contacted for additional information. If the data could not be obtained, the study was excluded.

Risk of bias assessment and certainty of evidence in the included studies

The risk of bias was assessed by two researchers (XYX and YQG) using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias (RoB) Tool [48]. This assessment included evaluation of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Discrepancies were resolved by a third researcher (JX). Risk of bias assessment and certainty of evidence in the included studies. The overall risk of bias for each study was assessed comprehensively according to the standard [49], and the certainty of evidence for the main outcomes in the network meta-analysis was evaluated using the GRADE approach [50].

Statistical analysis

To account for the baseline differences, this study conducted effect size pooling using the change in mean and standard deviation (SD) before and after intervention. The mean difference and SD of the change were calculated using the following formulae [51–53]. The first step involved calculating the difference in means:

where is the raw mean difference, is the reported mean post-intervention, and is the reported mean pre-intervention [54]. Then the SD of the change in means is calculated as follows [54]:

where is the SD of the difference in means, is the SD from pre-intervention, is the SD from post-intervention, and is the correlation coefficient [54]. Correlation coefficients for pre- and post-intervention were rarely reported in the included studies and were generally assumed to be r = 0.50, as suggested by the Cochrane Handbook [54].

This study follows the PRISMA guidelines for network Meta-analysis [55]. Using a frequentist framework [56], we applied a random-effects model in Stata 16.0 to calculate the pooled effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Due to the heterogeneity of measurement units across outcomes, standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the measure of effect size.

A network plot was constructed to visualize the relationships among exercise interventions, with lines connecting nodes representing direct comparisons between interventions. The thickness of the lines is proportional to the number of studies, and node size reflects sample size. Consistency within each closed loop was assessed by calculating inconsistency factors and their 95% CI [57]. An inconsistency model was used to test for inconsistencies; when P > 0.05, a consistency model was applied [58]. Consistency of both direct and indirect comparisons was examined using node-splitting method, with p > 0.05 indicating non-significant inconsistency. Intervention effects across exercise types were ranked and compared using the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) [59]. SUCRA values range from 0 to 100, with 100 indicating the most effective intervention and 0 indicating the least effective [60]. Publication bias and small-study effects were examined using a funnel plot.

Results

Literature screening process

A total of 3,753 relevant studies were identified through database searches, including PubMed (n = 249), Embase (n = 1,268), Cochrane Library (n = 1,202), Web of Science (n = 956), and EBSCO (n = 78). Additionally, two studies were obtained through other sources. After importing the records into Endnote X software, 1,425 duplicate studies were removed. Subsequently, two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts, excluding 2,084 studies. After full-text review, 159 additional studies were excluded. Finally, 87 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in this study. The literature screening process is presented in Fig. 1.

Basic characteristics of included studies

This study included all English-language RCTs published between January 2000 and November 2024. The detailed characteristics of the included studies are provided in Supplementary 4. Geographically, most studies were conducted in the United States (n = 24), Iran (n = 17), and South Korea (n = 5). A total of 64 studies focused on leptin and adiponectin levels. Regarding participant characteristics, 34 studies included only female participants, 29 studies included only male participants, and 24 studies included both male and female participants. In terms of comorbid conditions, 23 studies involved participants with diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), knee osteoarthritis, metabolic syndrome, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), dyslipidemia, or hepatitis.

Quality assessment results

The risk of bias assessment results are presented in Fig. 2, with detailed evaluations provided in Supplementary 6. Overall, 83 studies clearly reported their randomization procedures, and 49 studies reported allocation concealment. Only three studies employed double-blinding, and one study used blinded outcome assessment. Additionally, 71 studies reported attrition rates, with a dropout rate exceeding 20% considered high risk (unless intention-to-treat analysis was performed). Among them, 13 studies had a dropout rate of over 20%. Regarding other sources of bias, two studies were classified as high risk due to small sample sizes (fewer than 10 participants in any group), lack of supervision, and potential conflicts of interest. Based on the risk of bias assessment criteria adapted from previous research [49]: each study was categorized as follows: High-quality studies: No high-risk items and ≤ 3 unclear-risk items (n = 63). Moderate-quality studies: One high-risk item or ≥ 4 unclear-risk items (n = 21). Low-quality studies: All other cases (n = 3).

Inconsistency assessment

The study evaluated inconsistency across outcome measures using the loop inconsistency test, inconsistency model, and node-splitting method. The loop inconsistency test indicated that for leptin, all triangular loops demonstrated no significant inconsistency (P > 0.05). However, for adiponectin, inconsistency was observed in only four loops: COM-D + COM-D, AE-COM-HIIT, D + AE-D + RT-D + COM, and COM-D + RT-D + COM, while all other loops exhibited good consistency (Supplementary 8). The inconsistency model showed that both leptin and adiponectin had P-values > 0.05, indicating no significant inconsistency; thus, the consistency model was adopted for further analysis (Supplementary 8). The node-splitting analysis demonstrated that direct and indirect evidence was consistent for all outcome measures (P > 0.05), ensuring the reliability of the results (Supplementary 8). Supplementary 9 provides the forest plots for adipokine comparisons.

Discussion

This study is the first to employ a network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare the effects of different exercise modalities, with or without dietary restriction, on leptin and adiponectin levels in overweight or obese individuals. By synthesizing RCTs investigating the effects of exercise and dietary restriction on these adipokines, we found that nearly all intervention methods reduced leptin levels and increased adiponectin levels. However, HIIT did not significantly affect leptin levels, and D + RT and D + HIIT had no significant impact on adiponectin levels. Additionally, we observed that in terms of leptin regulation, the combination of dietary restriction and exercise (D + Ex) was more effective than exercise alone (Ex). However, for adiponectin regulation, the effects were more complex. Some standalone exercise modalities (Ex) were more effective than their dietary restriction plus exercise (D + Ex) counterparts, such as HIIT and RT showing greater effects than D + HIIT and D + RT. Conversely, some D + Ex interventions had greater effects than their exercise-only equivalents, such as D + COM and D + AE outperforming COM and AE, possibly due to the aerobic component in both COM and AE. These results suggest that dietary restriction enhances leptin regulation, whereas its combination with aerobic exercise further boosts adiponectin levels, while its combination with HIIT or RT may attenuate adiponectin regulation. By including a large number of primary studies, this research integrates direct and indirect evidence to analyze the differential effects of exercise modalities.

Previous studies have shown that exercise can lower leptin levels and increase adiponectin levels in obese individuals [34, 37, 38], However, discrepancies remain in network meta-analysis results. Wang et al. [61] found no significant differences in adiponectin improvement between AE, COM, RT, and HIIT groups compared to the control group, possibly due to insufficient study inclusion. Subsequent NMAs have suggested that exercise significantly influences leptin and adiponectin levels in overweight or obese individuals, and different exercise modalities exert varied effects on these adipokines. Tan et al. [37] focused exclusively on overweight and obese women and found that COM significantly reduced leptin levels, whereas other exercise forms did not. Additionally, COM and HIIT significantly increased adiponectin levels, while other exercise forms were ineffective. Del Rosso et al. [38] reviewed only AE, RT, and COM, reporting that all three significantly reduced leptin levels, with RT having the most pronounced effect. Furthermore, AE and RT significantly increased adiponectin levels, while COM had a weaker effect. Chen et al. found that COM and AE had the best effect on reducing leptin, with HIIT also showing significant effects. Meanwhile, AE had the best effect on adiponectin, followed by COM and HIIT, whereas RT had no significant effect on either adipokine. Compared to these studies, our research included a broader range of exercise interventions, and our results indicate that RT was most effective for leptin regulation, while HIIT and RT were most effective for adiponectin regulation.

Additionally, our findings suggest differences in the regulatory effects of dietary restriction on leptin and adiponectin. In the SUCRA rankings, dietary restriction ranked third for leptin regulation but only fifth for adiponectin regulation, placing it below standalone HIIT, RT, and COM. A previous meta-analysis [62] reported that fasting and dietary restriction significantly reduced leptin levels but had no statistically significant impact on adiponectin levels, even showing a downward trend. Although that study included populations beyond overweight and obese individuals, its findings align with our NMA results, offering critical insights into our research. These findings suggest that dietary restriction may be more effective in lowering leptin levels but less effective in increasing adiponectin levels. This discrepancy may arise from the differential sensitivity of leptin and adiponectin to dietary restriction. Leptin levels respond more acutely to long-term dietary restriction, primarily influenced by fat mass reduction. Dietary restriction leads to a significant and immediate decline in leptin levels, representing a rapid physiological response [63]. Conversely, adiponectin secretion depends more on adipose tissue quality rather than quantity. Its regulation is influenced by fat mass reduction, improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced inflammation [64]. Therefore, combining dietary restriction with exercise may further enhance leptin reduction, while the increase in adiponectin levels is subject to multiple factors.

Changes in leptin levels are primarily mediated through reductions in body weight and fat mass, achieved via exercise or dietary restriction [38], or through modifications in the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis due to hunger or appetite changes [7]. A meta-analysis by Rostas et al. [65] reported that the beneficial effects of exercise interventions on leptin levels are closely associated with weight loss. Therefore, we hypothesize that D + RT, D, D + AE, and RT may be more effective in modifying body composition by reducing fat mass while increasing muscle mass, thereby exerting a greater impact on leptin regulation.

Additionally, research suggests that leptin reduction is more strongly linked to appetite changes than exercise demands. This reduction does not necessarily occur after exercise programs consuming 30% of total daily energy expenditure, but rather in response to food intake and appetite regulation [66, 67],. Recent studies indicate that long-term exercise can modulate appetite-related hormones and eating behaviors in overweight and obese individuals [68]. Kawano et al. [69] found that weight-bearing exercise exerts a stronger appetite-suppressing effect than non-weight-bearing aerobic exercise, which may explain why RT has a more pronounced impact on leptin levels. However, there is currently a lack of comparative studies on the effects of different exercise modalities on appetite regulation, highlighting an important avenue for future research.

Adiponectin is a key anti-inflammatory biomarker, and its reduction is associated with obesity-related metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerosis [64, 70]. Changes in adiponectin levels are mainly adaptive responses that contribute to reducing inflammation, lowering fat mass, regulating androgen metabolism, and improving insulin sensitivity. Among exercise modalities, COM, HIIT, and RT are the most effective in increasing adiponectin levels, likely due to their ability to stimulate metabolic activity, enhance insulin sensitivity, promote fat oxidation, reduce fat storage, and improve cardiovascular health. Some researchers propose that cardiorespiratory fitness plays a mediating role in inflammatory biomarker changes [38]. Previous studies demonstrated that HIIT significantly improves VO₂max in obese individuals compared to other exercise modalities [71–73]. Therefore, the impact of HIIT on adiponectin levels may be attributed to enhanced cardiorespiratory function. Similarly, a network meta-analysis by Chen et al. [39] found that HIIT was the most effective intervention for improving multiple inflammatory biomarkers. The relatively weaker effects of aerobic exercise (AE) on leptin and adiponectin levels compared to other interventions may be due to lower-intensity interventions in the included studies. Many studies in our meta-analysis involved walking-based interventions. Previous research suggests that exercise programs exceeding 70% of maximal aerobic capacity (VO₂max) effectively reduce baseline leptin levels, particularly in individuals with high BMI [74–77]. Furthermore, the limited effect of AE on adiponectin levels may also be influenced by confounding factors.

Clinical implications and limitations

This study has significant clinical and practical implications, while also presenting certain limitations. Through a large-sample network meta-analysis, we systematically compared the effects of different types of exercise (with or without dietary restriction) on leptin and adiponectin levels in overweight or obese individuals, providing reliable evidence for obesity management. The results showed that dietary restriction plays a key role in reducing leptin, whereas specific forms of exercise (such as high-intensity interval training and resistance training) are more effective in increasing adiponectin. This suggests that, in clinical practice, individualized interventions can be developed based on the imbalance of adipokines: for obese individuals with elevated leptin levels, dietary control should be prioritized; whereas for those with low adiponectin levels, greater emphasis should be placed on exercise interventions. These findings offer important guidance for clinicians in designing targeted weight loss and metabolic regulation strategies, contributing to the optimization of interventions for obesity and related metabolic diseases.

However, this study has several limitations. First, due to the limited sample size of the included studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses based on sex and age, even though these factors may influence intervention outcomes. Additionally, in the inconsistency analysis of adiponectin-related studies, four closed loops showed inconsistency, and for certain intervention types (such as HIIT and D + HIIT), the number of high-quality studies was limited, which could affect the robustness of our findings. Overall, the GRADE evidence levels for most intervention comparisons ranged from low to high quality, with the evidence quality supporting the SUCRA rankings for leptin and adiponectin outcomes being moderate. This means that while the network meta-analysis provided a ranking of the relative effects of different interventions, our confidence in some of the evidence is limited.

Second, this study did not account for the potential influence of dietary composition on leptin and adiponectin levels. Previous research suggests that specific dietary components, such as high-protein diets, play an important role in regulating leptin levels [78]. Variations in dietary interventions could contribute to discrepancies in study results. Future research should integrate different dietary factors to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the combined effects of exercise and diet on adipokine regulation in obese individuals. Additionally, further investigations should explore specific exercise and dietary intervention combinations to support personalized obesity management strategies. Finally, since the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of different interventions, we did not conduct an in-depth analysis of safety (i.e., adverse events). We believe that this represents an important direction for future research and warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive comparison of different exercise modalities and their combinations with dietary restriction in regulating leptin and adiponectin levels in overweight or obese individuals. Our findings suggest that dietary restriction combined with exercise is more effective in reducing leptin levels, while its effects on adiponectin regulation are more complex. This study provides empirical support for obesity management strategies: ①For individuals with high leptin levels, dietary restriction should be prioritized in their intervention plans. ②For individuals with low adiponectin levels, exercise interventions should be emphasized, with a focus on COM, HIIT, and RT.。.
