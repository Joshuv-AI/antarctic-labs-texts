Utilizing a co-production strategy to identify multi sectoral priorities for enhancing preparedness against extreme weather events in vulnerable District Badin, Sindh-Pakistan
Authors: Wardah Ahmed, Narjis Fatima Hussain, Mohammad Waleed Ikram, Shoaib Jagirani, Saima Shahnawaz, Sasha Memon, Khurram Gondal, Zahid Memon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 2022 floods in Pakistan affected 33 million people and destroyed over 1,400 health facilities, highlighting limited disaster management capacities, systemic fragility, and the need for improved preparedness and resilience to extreme weather events (EWE). Introduction

Climate change is presently considered one of the “greatest public health threats” and is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of health, with complex implications for health systems, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [4, 30]. Climate hazards in LMICs not only affect disease patterns but also strain workforce, supply chains, and health governance; core pillars of climate-resilient health systems [38]. Pakistan, despite contributing less than 1% to global greenhouse gas emissions [20], ranks 5th as per the report on the Global Climate Risk Index [13]. The country’s 40% population is frequently exposed to extreme weather events (EWEs) [19] such as floods, heatwaves, droughts, and coastal storms all of which have direct and indirect effects on health systems [2, 32].

The 2022 floods in Pakistan affected 33 million people and destroyed over 1,400 health facilities, highlighting systemic fragility, thus underscoring the need for improved extreme weather events (EWE) preparedness and local resilience due to limited government capacity. Among the most climate-affected areas in Pakistan is Badin, a coastal district in Sindh province. Badin frequently experiences flash floods, saline water intrusion, water logging, and rising temperatures challenges that have been largely exacerbated by infrastructural developments like the Left Bank Outfall Drain (LBOD). In particular, Tando Bago, one of Badin’s sub districts (Fig. 1), records the highest Climate Vulnerability Index (CVI) score in Sindh (0.72) [11]. These events not only damage physical infrastructure, but also severely disrupt the delivery of essential health services, increase vector- and water-borne disease outbreaks, and reduce access to maternal and child healthcare, particularly in 2022 floods [3]. Vulnerable groups, such as lower-income groups, women, children, and lower-caste communities, face the most pronounced impacts due to systemic exclusion and limited mobility, and are at higher risk of morbidity and mortality [16]. Badin exemplifies how environmental stressors intersect with gender and caste inequities, amplifying health disparities.

Study setting: Tando Bago subdistrict-Badin district of Sindh Province, Pakistan

The World Health Organization [38] and Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [17] have both called for urgent transformation of health systems into climate-resilient, equity-focused institutions capable of anticipating, responding to, and recovering from climate shocks. Despite this urgency, conventional disaster management and health system responses remain inadequate in such contexts. Institutional silos, short-term planning, poor dissemination of early warnings, and top-down relief efforts often fail to address the complexity of climate-health interactions [18]. Health systems tend to focus on immediate medical relief, while overlooking the structural, intersectoral drivers of vulnerability [14]. The recent scoping review of 98 studies from high income countries found limited evidence linking vulnerability assessment to concrete evacuation and sheltering decisions, revealing a persistent gap between conceptual frameworks and operational practice [8]. These limitations highlight the need for approaches that are inclusive, anticipatory, and tailored to local realities. Within Pakistan, the National Climate Change Policy [25] and the National Research Council [26] call for decentralized, multisectoral adaptation approaches, yet few mechanisms exist to guide how this should be achieved with various implementation barriers [1, 3, 23].

This study explores the use of a co-production strategy to identify multisectoral priorities for enhancing preparedness against EWEs in Badin. It challenges the idea of beneficiaries as passive recipients of aid and instead repositions them as active contributors to planning and governance. In recent years, co-production has gained traction as a means to embed local knowledge in health systems, address power asymmetries, and foster sustainable, context-sensitive interventions [12, 15]. Applied within the framework of climate adaptation, co-production enables a shift from reactive disaster management to proactive resilience-building. This shift is particularly useful in areas like Badin, where slow-onset disaster, such as salinization and disease spread, coexist with rapid shocks such as floods and infrastructure collapsing [31]. Furthermore, Pakistan’s national climate policies while progressive in language, lack operational mechanisms for integrating health, water, and disaster management sectors at the district level.

The findings presented in this paper are rooted in the lived experiences of climate-affected communities in Badin and shaped through collaborative tools such as problem tree analysis, visioning exercises [24] and retrospective response analysis [6].

There is significant opportunity to explore the potential of multisectoral approach, based on local priorities, to prepare for and adapt to climate-related risks. It demonstrates how co-production, when operationalized as an inclusive, structured process, can help build trust, bridge knowledge gaps, and produce actionable priorities that are both legitimate and feasible. These lessons are not only applicable to Badin but are also relevant for other climate-vulnerable regions globally seeking to localize adaptation and resilience strategies. Through a qualitative case study approach, this research seeks to understand how co-production can surface local priorities, foster cross-sectoral collaboration, and contribute to more responsive and inclusive preparedness planning.

Methods

Study setting and design

The coastal district of Badin, Sindh province, Pakistan, was purposively selected as a sentinel district representing the compound climate-health vulnerabilities typical of coastal Sindh. As reported in the Pakistan National Human Development Report (2017), Badin’s Human Development Index (HDI) stands at 0.4, placing it within the low-development category. Large segments of its population, particularly in the sub-districts, live in poverty, exhibit low adaptive capacity, and are highly dependent on climate-sensitive socioeconomic activities. Geographically, these sub-districts lie in Pakistan’s southern region, between 23°43′ and 25°26′ N and 67°05′ and 68°45′ E. Badin was divided into five sub-districts: Badin, Golarchi, Matli, Talhar, and Tando Bago.

A qualitative case study design [7] was employed in this study, grounded in a participatory research framework [37]. It was conducted in Tando Bago subdistrict of Badin, from July to November 2023.

A qualitative approach was utilized because it allows for an in-depth exploration of complex, context-specific issues like vulnerability, institutional fragmentation, and lived experience of climate risk. This is especially important in regions like Badin, where the formal health system overlaps with informal community networks, and where local narratives and perspectives are frequently unrecognized and excluded in the policy making process. Furthermore, the case study design was well suited to capturing the dynamics between actors at multiple governance levels and documenting processes over time.

Guided by the core principles of co-production, including shared ownership, inclusivity, and practical applicability, the study utilized a participatory research approach. Co-production, in this context, refers to the collaborative generation of knowledge and solutions by communities, government actors, health practitioners, and researchers. The process was designed to encourage dialogue, critical reflection and collaborative problem-solving across different stakeholders with active involvement of all stakeholders.

Stakeholder engagement

Recruiting and sampling strategy

Participants were selected using a combination of purposive and criterion-based sampling strategies [27, 28]. Purposive sampling guided the selection of participants across three governance levels-district, health system and community to ensure vertical representation and triangulation of perspectives. Criterion-based inclusion ensured that all participants had direct experience with climate-health vulnerabilities and institutional roles relevant to EWE preparedness. At the community level, snowballing sampling complemented purposive selection to reach marginalized and hard to access groups including women from flood affected areas and lower-caste community members, who are typically underrepresented in formal consultations. This multi-strategy sampling approach is consistent with the principle of participatory research, which prioritizes inclusivity and contextual relevance over statistical representative [37].

Study participants were reached through local project team member (SJ) who were socially and culturally positioned to engage the stakeholders across three interrelated levels.

Participants characteristics and inclusion criteria

District-level stakeholders

Included officials and planners from the district disaster management authority, Department of Health, Environment Protection Agency (EPA), Agriculture Department, and Irrigation Department. The participants were included if they were actively responsible for intersectoral planning and response coordination at the policy level.

Health systems-level stakeholders

Included public health facility in-charges, Lady Health Worker (LHW) supervisors, Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health (RMNCH) coordinators, medical officers (MO) from basic health unit (BHU), and rural health centers (RHC). These stakeholders actively operated at the service delivery interface and possessed firsthand knowledge of operational constraints during EWEs.

Community-level stakeholders

Included residents of flood-affected areas, ensuring gender balance and inclusion of youth representatives, local leaders, and women’s group members. Special attention was given to including voices from disadvantaged social groups, particularly those underrepresented in public decision-making. Community level participants were included if they were residents of flood-affected areas within the sub district and had experiences at least one EWE. Participants under 18 years of age were excluded. The participant’s characteristics are tabulated in Supplementary File 1.

Data collection methods

The verbal consent was obtained after receiving the letter of support from Department of Health, Government of Sindh. Data collection was conducted by team of four researchers. Two researchers served as facilitators (WA and NH) for co-production workshops, while third (SG) managed documentation including anonymous written exercises and visual outputs. A fourth researcher (SJ) a local liaison fluent in Sindhi and familiar with community local context, support cultural mediation and ensured inclusivity across all session. The whole workshop proceedings were not recorded; however, the anonymous written exercises were kept as the data source. The few video excerpts were recorded and translated as per the participant voluntary participation. Written outputs from workshop exercise were stored as physical documents in a locked cabinet at the project office and subsequently digitized. Data sources were intentionally linked to specific inquiry objectives: workshop-based participatory tools (problem tree analysis, visioning exercises, retrospective response analysis) were the primary source of identifying community and institutional priorities, barriers, and proposed solutions. Visual aids, locally translated materials, and culturally adapted methods were used to promote easy understanding. KII were conducted by two researchers (NH and other qualitative research trained research manager), with one leading the interview and other taking filed notes). Audio recordings from KIIs were stored on password protected devices, with access restricted to the core team. Transcripts were anonymized prior to analysis with participants identified by designation rather name. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English. The interviews were guided by the interview script can be found in Supplementary File 2. Triangulating workshops, interviews, and participatory exercises strengthened credibility by capturing both consensus and divergence. Multiple qualitative and participatory data collection methods were used.

Stakeholder co-production workshops

The three workshops brought stakeholders together to collaboratively identify problems, share experiences, and co-design practical solutions through guided participatory exercises. Workshops typically lasted 2–3 h and were conducted in Sindhi. Facilitators ensured equal participation by encouraging round-robin contributions, small group breakout discussions, and anonymous voting in ranking exercises.

Key informant interviews (KIIs)

The KII conducted with nine government officials, health staff, NGO representatives, and traditional leaders to explore existing preparedness systems, challenges, and institutional roles. The participants were also part of workshops and usually interview scheduled on the same day or next day for 30–45 min at the project office.

Participatory tools

To facilitate meaningful dialogue and ensure structured outputs, the following co-production tools were used:

Problem tree analysis

This helped participants identify immediate symptoms, underlying causes, and root drivers of climate-health vulnerability. For example, water contamination was linked to both infrastructure failure and governance gaps.

Visioning exercises

Participants described their ideal future scenario for health and climate resilience and worked backward to define necessary steps, actors, and investments.

Retrospective response analysis

Participants were asked to reflect on their most recent EWE experience (floods 2022), including preparedness actions, institutional response, and outcomes. This allowed for grounded assessment of strengths, failures, and missed opportunities.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed a hybrid inductive-deductive analysis approach [5]. The data was coded, and then thematic analysis was conducted. Coding was conducted using a hybrid approach: pre-defined codes were derived from the WHO [38]. Climate-Resilient Health Systems Framework and from Towards Climate-Resilient Health System in Pakistan A framework of Action [33]. These frameworks informed thematic codes covering domains such as early warning system, intersectoral coordination, health workforce capacity, and community resilience.

To enhance analytical rigor, the coding was conducted independently by two researchers and cross-validated, additionally codes were developed inductively from the data. Discrepancies were resolved through peer debriefing sessions. Microsoft excel software was used to manage and organized the data. Themes were then developed by clustering related codes, reviewing them against the full data set, and refining them through an iterative process. The thematic and coding framework can be found in Supplementary File 1. Themes were triangulated across stakeholder levels and validated during follow-up interviews with selected participants. This feedback loop served as both a form of member checking and co-interpretation. To ensure analytical rigor, four quality criteria guided the study throughout [22]. Credibility was enhanced through methodological triangulation(workshops, KIIs, and participatory tools), member checking via follow-up interviews with selected participants, and prolonged engagement with local stakeholders over five months. Dependability was supported by maintaining an iterative methodological and analysis decisions, including the coding and thematic framework, filed notes and debriefing records. Confirmability was addressed using reflexivity notes and documented their positionality and potential biases, particularly given the power differentials inherent in co-production processes. The transferability maintained by providing contextual descriptions of the study setting, sampling approach and participating to similar climate -vulnerable contexts.

Results

The findings from this study are organized into four interlinked thematic areas that emerged from triangulated data across community, health system, and district-level stakeholders, integrating insights from both workshops and interviews These themes reflect both the lived experience of extreme weather events (EWEs) in Badin.

The stakeholder workshop was conducted prior to the interviews. Data from workshops and interviews yielded complementary insights. Workshop-based participatory exercises generated collective, consensus-based priorities and co-designed solutions through structured dialogue among diverse stakeholders.

In contrast, key informant interviews provided individual, in-depth perspectives on institutional mandates, resource constraints, inter-departmental coordination challenges, and personal experiences navigating EWE preparedness within existing governance structures. Interviews allowed for candid reflection on systemic gaps, power dynamics, and barriers to implementation that were less visible in group settings. The results highlight not only risks but also point to collaboratively identified multisectoral preparedness framework for the development of local solutions.

Theme 1: Climate-related health risks

Across all stakeholder levels, participants identified a consistent and alarming rise in health issues linked to climate-induced events-particularly floods, heatwaves, and water contamination. Health risks included both communicable and non-communicable conditions, with vulnerable populations disproportionately affected.

Community members reported increased prevalence of diarrheal diseases, malaria, dengue, and skin infections during flood seasons. Frontline health workers noted that outbreaks were often compounded by the inaccessibility of health services and disrupted supply chains for medicines and safe water.

A female community representative in Tando Bago shared:

Only 20% of people were aware of the floods. We had no warning and nowhere to go

Health staff from basic health units (BHUs) confirmed that service delivery during EWEs was largely reactive and frequently overwhelmed. In many areas, post-EWE responses arrived days or even weeks after the event, exacerbating community distress and delaying treatment.

Mental health stress, though less frequently discussed, emerged as a cross-cutting concern, especially among women and children facing displacement, loss of livelihood, and uncertainty. Despite this, there were no formal mental health services available in the area.

Theme 2: Inefficient communication and coordination

There was near-universal agreement that early warning systems (EWS) in Badin were either non-existent or ineffective. Community participants reported receiving flood alerts late, if at all, and health staff indicated they were often unaware of upcoming EWEs until they were already underway.

Many facilities lacked basic preparedness protocols. Health units in flood-prone zones were built on low-lying land, rendering them inaccessible during high water. Most lacked backup power, alternative water supplies, or contingency planning for service continuity.

A KII respondent from the health department noted:

We receive some information from the Met Office, but there’s no formal channel to distribute this to local communities or even to our clinics.

The disconnect between meteorological services, disaster response agencies, and the health system was evident. District officials admitted that coordination structures existed only on paper and were not activated until EWE had already begun.

Theme 3: Water insecurity and contamination

Water quality and availability were consistently raised as a central health and survival issue. Participants described widespread reliance on contaminated open wells, ponds, and canals, many affected by arsenic, salinity, and sewage overflows, particularly after flooding events.

Communities linked much of this to the mismanagement of the Left Bank Outfall Drain (LBOD), which has caused widespread environmental degradation and increased flood frequency in low-lying areas.

Health workers reported repeated spikes in water-borne illnesses after every flood, with little capacity to test water sources or distribute chlorine or filtration kits. Women participants described having to walk several kilometers to fetch relatively cleaner water, often with children accompanying them.

“Our drinking water smells like salt and chemicals. My children have stomach infections all year,” said a mother in workshop

Water testing infrastructure was absent in most health facilities, and there was no budget line for preventative WASH measures. This not only heightened the risk of disease but also undermined public trust in local authorities and the formal health system.

Theme 4: Governance and lack of inclusivity

Stakeholders highlighted widespread frustration with governance systems and service delivery in Badin. Community-level participants expressed deep distrust in local authorities, citing corruption, poor planning, and lack of responsiveness. Women and lower-caste participants felt particularly excluded from formal planning processes and rarely received targeted health support during or after EWEs.

One community elder share that:

Medicines arrive, but we never see them. They’re sold in the market

District-level officials acknowledged institutional silos between health, disaster management, and municipal departments, admitting that plans were often made in isolation. Relief distribution was described as politicized, with aid going primarily to those with local political connections. There was lack of system in place for community feedback, grievance, or participatory budget planning, further reinforcing marginalization.

The workshops also revealed latent community capacities, such as local women leaders managing informal relief efforts, youth volunteering for rescue work, and community-based health educators who could be mobilized for future preparedness campaigns.

Participants expressed strong interest in remaining involved in ongoing planning efforts. There was a shared sense of ownership and optimism that future adaptation efforts would be more effective if designed collaboratively.

Co-produced multisectoral preparedness framework

Despite the challenges, the co-production process enabled stakeholders to surface practical, multisectoral priorities for improving preparedness (Fig. 2). This framework is organized based on the interconnected three-tier domains that work together to address health and climate-related challenges. The health domain highlights four priority health conditions directly influenced by water and climate factors: malaria, dengue diarrhea and skin diseases. The water domain comprises essential eater -related interventions: clean drinking water provision, drainage systems, community water behaviors, and sanitation infrastructure. The capacity domain describes the critical support systems required for implementation: competent staff, community education sessions, medicine supplies, infrastructure/shelters, and ambulance services. The strength of the framework lies in its interconnectedness, that depicts how water intervention impact directly on health outcomes, while capacity-building elements support both water and health interventions. This ensures that improvement in one domain reinforces and maximizes the outcomes in the others and makes the system comprehensive for earl warning and response to climate-sensitive health threats. The priorities identified by stakeholders are as follows:

Co-produced multi sectoral preparedness framework

Priority 1: Strengthening resilience capacities for climate-responsive healthcare

Improving communication channels between climate and health services, as well as enhancing technical, local, and contextual competencies of health care professionals.

Priority 2: Water Security and health resilience

Participants identified clean water’s importance and its correlation with health outcomes. Notably, enhancing water-related behaviors and knowledge emerged as a critical coping mechanism essential for addressing this priority.

Priority 3: Enhancing practical approaches for preparedness

Participants were in favor of utilizing both traditional and innovative channels for communication, early warning systems, decision support tools, and community mobilization.

Discussion

The study shows that co-production successfully brought together local stakeholders to address climate related challenges in Badin, a climate vulnerable district of Sindh, Pakistan. To comprehensively analyze these findings and their broader implications, the discussion section structured around key finding-reframing resilience from static concept to dynamic, global alignment of our local findings and structural implementation challenges, finally operational strength and limitation encountered during implementation, providing practical insights for scaling and embedding co-production approaches in similar low-resource settings.

Key finding- reframing resilience

The important finding was a major shift in thinking: instead of focusing only on building infrastructure like roads and drainage systems, participants prioritized people-centered, community focused that are more practical and impactful solutions. These included community-based early warning systems, clean drinking water access, mobile health services focusing on gender sensitivity and cross-sectoral staff training. These reflect a broader reframing of resilience that centers not just on “hard” systems but on social infrastructure, communication, and trust.

Global alignment of the findings

This aligns with broader shifts in the global health adaptation field, where increasing attention is being paid to the “software” of health systems governance, local leadership, information flows, and social cohesion. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report [17] notes that transformative adaptation is unlikely to occur without shifts in power, participation, and institutional culture.The findings support this: participants often cited institutional distrust, corruption, and exclusion as core vulnerabilities, not just lack of roads or clinics.

The results align with literature highlighting the value of co-production in climate adaptive health systems. Studies from South Asia, in Bangladesh, India and Nepal, have demonstrated the collaborative planning between local governments, community groups with local indigenous knowledge, and front-line health workers leads to more context-sensitive and financially sustainable disaster responses [9, 29]. These studies echo the current study observation that trust, shared ownership and the integration of local knowledge enhance the acceptance of adaptation strategies. This also reinforces emerging global policy discussions, which stress that adaptation must shift from being top-down to community-led. Co-production in this view, is not just tool for disaster resilience but an evidence-informed strategy for social transformation [21, 34]. However, some studies argue for the scalability and sustainability of co-production in context of weak governance. For example, researcher argue that without structural changes, co-production could pose risk especially when local voices are included in planning but excluded from decision making [10]. Additionally, there was a claim that co-production worked in relatively well resources or donor-supported projects, nevertheless our work shows that co-production can be meaningful even in low-resource settings, provided there is deliberate effort to align institutional roles and build trust [23]. Although co-production generated locally relevant and operational relevant priorities, climate-health governance in Pakistan remains fragmented across sectors, with limited coordination between health, agriculture, environment protection and disaster management [35]. Participants acknowledged limitations: lack of funding to implement ideas, bureaucratic inertia, and the risk of token participation. Frequent transfers of administrative staff affect continuity essential for relational trust that facilitates collaborative efforts. To institutionalize co-production, changes are needed in planning processes, monitoring frameworks, methods and tools. Current planning cycles in the districts often lack flexibility for participatory approaches, and departmental silos prevent the monitoring of outcomes [3, 36]. Alignment with the existing administrative structures, development of institutional champions and widening the scope of evaluation in line with the global commitments may provide an avenue for making incremental progress.

Operational strengths and limitations of co-production approach

A key strength of this study lies in its use of co-production as both a methodological approach The qualitative case study design further enabled in-depth exploration of real-world dynamics, allowing us to capture power dynamics, trust, and learning that are often lost in large-scale quantitative analyses. The findings contribute to the limited but growing body of evidence that co-production is viable and valuable in resource-constrained settings. Furthermore, multisectoral framework highlights local priorities and provides insights into building climate-resilient healthcare services by integrating expertise from health, social, and climate sciences. Currently, the project team are now in the process of co-creating local solutions addressing various challenges at the intersection of climate and health.

However, the study also has some limitations. Co-production processes are inherently shaped by interpersonal dynamics and institutional culture, which can vary significantly even within the same country. There is also a risk of bias being introduced by the dual role of the research team as both facilitators and observers in the co-production process. Although reflexive practices were built into the methodology to mitigate this, full objectivity is inherently challenging in participatory research [37]. Participants were reached through local project team members and existing social networks, which may have introduced selection bias toward individuals already engaged in community activities or positive disposed towards health system actors. In workshops and interviews, social desirability bias may have led participants to frame responses in ways they perceived as favorable to researcher. To mitigate these biases, the anonymous data, the separate community only workshop was conducted without government presence, used trusted local facilitators, triangulated across multiple data sources, and asked open-ended, non-leading questions. However, these biases cannot be eliminated, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Finally, while the study suggests that co-production can succeed in low-resource settings, longer-term follow-up is needed to assess the sustainability and institutionalization of the adaptive capacities built during the project.

Recommendations and policy implications

As Pakistan updates its National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and health sector climate strategies, the Badin experience provides a practical and doable district-level model might be integrated in the existing health system. The findings of this study support several key recommendations:

Institutionalize co-production within district extreme weather event (EWE) and health planning by embedding tools such as stakeholder mapping and visioning into official workflows.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that co-production approaches grounded in inclusive and iterative stakeholder engagement can enhance the trust, shared ownership, and sustainability of climate-health preparedness at the district level. The findings offer a replicable planning model for operationalizing climate adaptation in existing health systems. Despite the structural and implementation challenges the study offers a potential multisectoral preparedness framework for embedding climate resilience into routine sectoral coordination and service delivery mechanism. Future research should undertake long-term follow-up evaluation to assess the effectiveness and system level implications of co-produced adaptation models, particularly in strengthening multisectoral approach.
