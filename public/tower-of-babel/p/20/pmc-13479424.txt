Integrated isothermal shift assay and multi-omics identify melittin as a novel EGFR-targeting peptide to suppress NSCLC
Authors: Chenhui Zhong, Jietao Gong, Lisha Wei, Rong fang Xie, Peiying Shi, Hong Yao, Jianyong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic agents. Melittin (MEL), a bioactive peptide, exhibits potent antitumor activity; however, its clinical translation is limited by an incomplete understanding of its molecular mechanism. Furthermore, the development of peptide-based therapeutics is often constrained by the lack of systematic approaches to identify their specific cellular targets.MethodsTo address these challenges, we established a multi-dimensional framework integrating biophysical validation with omics analysis. We evaluated the specific anti-NSCLC efficacy of MEL in vitro and in a PC9 xenograft mouse model. The direct molecular target of MEL was deconvoluted using a combination of isothermal shift assay (iTSA), cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), and bio-layer interferometry (BLI), supported by AlphaFold3-based structural modeling. Mechanistic insights were obtained through quantitative proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling of tumor tissues, followed by validation using shRNA-mediated gene knockdown.ResultsMEL demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against NSCLC cells and suppressed tumor growth in vivo, with minimal toxicity toward normal human bronchial epithelial cells. Isothermal shift assay and CETSA revealed that MEL treatment significantly enhanced the thermal stability of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), indicating direct target engagement. This interaction was further confirmed by BLI, which showed high binding affinity (KD = 18.6 nM), and structural modeling predicted a specific interaction interface. Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses indicated that MEL binding inhibited EGFR phosphorylation, thereby attenuating the downstream ERK and STAT3 signaling cascades and inducing autophagy-dependent apoptosis. Importantly, the antitumor effects of MEL were significantly attenuated in EGFR-knockdown cells, confirming its dependency on this target.ConclusionsThis study identifies EGFR as a direct molecular target of MEL in NSCLC, supporting the potential of MEL as a novel peptide-based EGFR inhibitor. Furthermore, our integrated biophysical and omics strategy provides a valuable methodological reference for systematic peptide target identification, thereby facilitating clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08358-w.Keywords: Melittin, Non-small cell lung cancer, EGFR, Target

Background

Lung cancer is broadly classified into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer. NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of newly diagnosed lung cancer cases and is characterized by rapid tumor cell proliferation and a high metastatic potential [1, 2]. Most patients present with advanced-stage disease at diagnosis. For the approximately 57% of cases involving metastasis, the prognosis remains poor, with a 5-year relative survival rate of only 6%. While the current standard of care encompasses surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [3, 4], curative surgery and chemoradiotherapy are rarely feasible for this demographic. Furthermore, although targeted therapies have revolutionized NSCLC management, their long-term efficacy—along with that of traditional chemotherapeutic agents—is frequently compromised by narrow therapeutic windows, severe systemic toxicity, and the emergence of drug resistance [5–7]. Consequently, the development of novel, effective, and safe anticancer agents remains an urgent priority.

Melittin (MEL), the predominant bioactive constituent of bee venom, is a linear, cationic, amphipathic peptide composed of 26 amino acids that typically adopts an α-helical structure [8, 9]. This distinctive conformation confers a broad spectrum of biological activities. Accumulating evidence indicates that MEL can modulate critical signaling axes, such as NF-κB and MAPK, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects [10] and inducing apoptosis in various solid tumors. Investigation into the antitumor mechanisms of MEL has been extensive. In addition to its well-documented capacity to exert direct cytotoxicity through physical disruption of tumor cell membranes [11], MEL modulates tumor biology through pleiotropic regulatory effects. These include the induction of cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, as well as the suppression of angiogenesis and metastasis. At the molecular level, MEL has been reported to regulate metabolic axes (e.g., SREBP1/FASN and HSF1/PDK3) and inhibit invasion-related matrix metalloproteinases. Furthermore, it attenuates the activation of critical oncogenic signaling cascades, including the PI3K/AKT, RAS/ERK, and JNK pathways [12–14]. These concerted inhibitory effects fundamentally disrupt the functional integrity of tumor cells, thereby suppressing aggressive malignant behaviors such as invasion and metastasis [15]. However, the direct cellular targets responsible for initiating these effects have not yet been systematically characterized. Therefore, precise identification of these interaction partners is critical to advancing peptide-based therapeutics, providing the necessary structural and mechanistic basis for future drug discovery.

Traditional target identification strategies, such as activity-based protein profiling (ABPP), typically necessitate chemical modification of the parent molecule [16, 17], which risks compromising the bioactivity or steric conformation of peptides. To circumvent this limitation, the isothermal shift assay (iTSA) has emerged as a robust, label-free strategy for proteome-wide target identification method [18]. As an extension of the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) [19], iTSA detects changes in protein thermal stability to study protein-ligand interactions and dynamic post-translational modifications. Its label-free approach enables unbiased, proteome-wide target identification, making it particularly suitable for the target deconvolution of natural products [20]. Therefore, we employed iTSA technology to conduct a large-scale investigation of potential MEL target proteins. Candidate targets identified through this screening were subsequently validated using complementary target confirmation techniques, including CETSA and bio-layer interferometry (BLI) [21]. AlphaFold3 was employed to model the complex formed between MEL and its putative target protein, aiming to characterize the binding interface, key amino acid residues, and interaction forces at the atomic level [22]. Additionally, global proteomics and phosphoproteomics analyses were performed to map the downstream intracellular proteomic changes induced by MEL. Functional validation further confirmed alterations in the associated signaling pathways.

Herein, utilizing this label-free proteomic strategy, we identified epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) as the principal molecular target mediating the antitumor efficacy of MEL in NSCLC. Our results demonstrate that MEL directly binds to EGFR, effectively suppressing the receptor autophosphorylation and consequently attenuating the phosphorylation of downstream effectors STAT3 and ERK. Concurrently, MEL treatment was found to trigger autophagy-dependent cell death, thereby arresting malignant proliferation. Collectively, these findings not only clarify the molecular basis of MEL’s antitumor activity as a natural peptide but also establish a methodological framework for the target deconvolution of bioactive natural products.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

MEL, gefitinib, and puromycin were purchased from Yuanye Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RPMI-1640 medium, high-glucose DMEM, trypsin solution, phosphate-buffered saline, and penicillin–streptomycin were obtained from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China), and fetal bovine serum was provided by ExCell Bio, Inc. (Taicang, China). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were supplied by Solarbio (Beijing, China). RIPA lysis buffer for tissues and cells, a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). The anti-GAPDH antibody (Cat# GB11002-100) was acquired from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Primary antibodies against LC3B (Cat# ET1701-65), EGFR (Cat# ET1603-37), phospho-EGFR (p-EGFR; Cat# ET1604-44), Bax (Cat# ET1603-34), Bcl-2 (Cat# ET1702-53), phospho-ERK (p-ERK; Cat# ET1610-13) and phospho-STAT3 (p-STAT3; Cat# ET1607-39) were sourced from HuaAn Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China). ERK (Cat# YM8336) and STAT3 (Cat# YM8325) were sourced from Immunoway Biotechnology (California, USA). Recombinant human EGFR protein (Cat# 10001-H08H) was obtained from Sino Biological, Inc. (Beijing, China).

Cell culture

The human NSCLC cell lines PC-9 and A549, as well as the normal human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Lung cancer cells and normal bronchial epithelial cells were grown in either RPMI 1640 or high-glucose DMEM, each supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. All cultures were maintained in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c nude mice aged 4–6 weeks were purchased from Wushi Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Fuzhou, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of Fujian Medical University. Animals were kept at a maximum of six mice per cage, with free access to autoclaved chow and water. The animal room was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of 40–60% and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science (ICLAS) and the national Guide for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare of China. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the College of Animal Sciences, Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University (Approval No. PZCASFAFU23024).

MTT assay

Cells were plated in 96-well plates at 1 × 10^4 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. They were then incubated with MEL at final concentrations of 0.25–10 µg/mL for 24–48 h. After drug exposure, MTT solution was added to each well and the plates were further incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The supernatant containing MTT was carefully discarded, and the formed formazan crystals were solubilized in DMSO. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

DAPI staining

PC-9 cells were cultured in 6-well plates and treated with MEL (4, 5, or 6 µg/mL) for 24–48 h. After treatment, cells were rinsed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Following additional PBS washes, cells were incubated with DAPI solution (Beyotime) for 5 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclear morphology was then observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti).

PC9 xenograft model and treatment

PC-9 cells were collected and resuspended in PBS at a density of 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL. An aliquot of 200 µL of this suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of BALB/c nude mice. Given the potent hemolytic activity and non-specific cytotoxicity associated with MEL, intratumoral injection was employed as a highly effective and safe administration route. This approach facilitates the attainment of high localized drug concentrations within the tumor microenvironment while concurrently minimizing systemic toxicity and damage to off-target organs. When xenograft volumes reached about 300 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 per group): three MEL treatment groups receiving intratumoral injections of 1, 3, or 5 mg/kg once daily for 14 days (The dosage was determined based on previous studies [23]). A positive control group administered gefitinib (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, twice weekly); and a vehicle group given intratumoral saline once daily. Mice were monitored daily for changes in body weight, coat condition, and general activity, with all observations systematically recorded. At the experimental endpoint, the mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation following carbon dioxide CO2 anesthesia. Tumors were excised, carefully dissected free from adjacent tissues, and weighed. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were recorded three times per week with digital calipers, and volumes were calculated using the equation V = ½ × (L × W²). Throughout the study, body weight and overall health status were monitored, and tumor growth curves were generated based on serial volume measurements.

Isothermal shift assay (iTSA)

Sample Preparation: xenograft tumor tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails) using a tissue homogenizer. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 30 min at 4 ℃ to remove debris. The protein concentration of the supernatant was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. For the iTSA, supernatant proteins were divided into two groups: vehicle control and MEL-treated (10 µg/mL). A thermal challenge was performed at 58 ℃ for 4 min to induce the aggregation of thermally unstable protein. After heating, the samples were immediately cooled on ice and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 20 min to isolate the stabilized soluble protein fraction.

Protein Digestion and Desalting: Protein extracts were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreito at 56 °C for 1 h and subsequently alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. Trypsin digestion was performed using the Filter-Aided Sample Preparation method as previously described. Following overnight incubation, the peptides were collected by centrifugation at 13,000 ×g for 10 min and concentrated via vacuum drying. Finally, the samples were desalted using C18 desalting columns and stored for subsequent analysis.

LC-MS/MS Analysis: Desalted peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (Buffer A) and analyzed using a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled with a nanoElute 2.0 system. A total of 200 ng peptides were loaded onto an AUR3-15075C18 analytical column (25 cm×75 μm, 1.7 μm, IonOpticks) maintained at 50 °C. Separation was achieved at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with a 60-min gradient: 5% to 28% Buffer B (100% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid) over 45 min, followed by an increase to 80% Buffer B over 5 min, and a 5-min hold at 80% Buffer B. Data acquisition was performed in diaPASEF mode with a scan range of 349–1229 m/z and an isolation window of 40 Da.

Data Processing and Bioinformatics: The DIA raw data were processed using Spectronaut 19.0 with BGS Factory Settings. For protein identification, the false discovery rate was controlled at 1.0% at both the precursor and protein levels. Decoy databases were generated using a mutated strategy. Protein quantification was conducted using the average peak area of the top 3 precursors with an FDR < 1.0%. All search results were aligned against the UniProt Homo sapiens database. The resulting protein group tables were initially filtered to ensure data quality. Quantitative data underwent missing value imputation and median normalization to account for technical variations. Differential protein expression analysis was performed using Student’s t-test, where candidate proteins were identified based on the thresholds of |log₂FC| > 0.15 and p < 0.05. Disease ontology (DO) enrichment analysis identified cancer-associated genes, and the R packages ‘clusterProfiler’ and ‘DOSE’ were used. Disease followed by protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction via STRING for lung disease-related targets.

Prediction of the EGFR-MEL binding site by alphaFold3

The amino acid sequence of the human EGFR was retrieved from the UniProt database (Entry: P00533). This sequence of MEL (GIGAVLKVLTTGLPALISWIKRKRQQ) corresponds to that of the biologically active MEL conformation as determined by NMR spectroscopy (PDB ID: 6DST), thereby confirming its native structure. The complex structure of EGFR and MEL was predicted directly using AlphaFold3 (version: af3-server). The two sequences described above were submitted to the AlphaFold3 online server with all parameters set to their default configurations. The docking results were visualized by PyMOL.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Tumor lysates were incubated with/without MEL (10 µg/mL) at gradient temperatures (45–65 °C) for 4 min. Thermal stability of EGFR was assessed by Western blot.

Bio-layer interferometry (BLI)

Biotinylated EGFR was prepared by incubating EGFR with biotin (1:5 molar ratio) for 2 h at 25 °C, followed by removal of excess biotin using size-exclusion chromatography (Zeba™ Spin Columns, Thermo Scientific). Binding kinetics were analyzed at 30 °C using an Octet RED96 instrument (ForteBio). Super streptavidin biosensors were hydrated in kinetics buffer for 20 min, loaded with biotinylated EGFR (600 s), and equilibrated. Reference-corrected binding responses were measured against MEL (40–200 nM). Wavelength shift data were processed using Octet Data Analysis Software v9.0.

Tandem mass tag (TMT)-based proteomic profiling

TMT Labeling and High-pH Fractionation: Sample extraction and protein digestion were performed as described in the DIA proteomics section. For quantitative analysis, peptides were labeled using the TMTpro 16plex Label Reagent Set (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [24]. The labeled peptides were pooled, lyophilized, and subsequently fractionated via high-pH reversed-phase HPLC using an Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Scientific) equipped with an XBridge C18 column (2.1 mm×150 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters). Separation was performed using a 40-min linear gradient of 5% to 45% Solvent B (80% ACN, 20 mM ammonium formate, pH 10.0) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. A total of 12 fractions were collected and dried for further analysis. Nano-HPLC-MS/MS Analysis: Each fraction was resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with an Easy-nLC 1000 system. Peptides were separated on a 50 cm analytical column (75 μm i.d., Monoche, Japan). The mass spectrometer was operated in Data-Dependent Acquisition mode with a 3.5 s cycle time. Full MS scans were acquired at 60 K resolution, followed by HCD MS/MS scans at 30 K resolution. Dynamic exclusion was implemented for 45 s. The raw MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 for protein identification and TMT-based quantification. Differential protein expression was evaluated with hierarchical clustering using thresholds of |fold change| ≥ 1.5 and p < 0.05. For phosphoproteomics, enriched phosphopeptides from identical samples were analyzed analogously, with additional phosphopeptide enrichment prior to MS detection [25].

Western blot analysis

Cells and tumor tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 30 min), protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked, incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, and then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized by ECL and quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Detection of autophagy

Cellular autophagy was assessed with monodansylcadaverine (MDC) staining according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Beyotime). Lysosomes in living cells were labeled with LysoTracker Red following the supplier’s instructions (Beyotime).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues from the model and high-dose MEL (5 mg/kg) groups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm sections. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and treated to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After serum blocking, sections were incubated with primary and then secondary antibodies. Signals were developed with DAB, followed by nuclear counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting. Images were acquired, and positive staining was quantified using ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded tumor sections were deparaffinized in xylene and graded ethanol, then subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for 20 min. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 2 h, sections were incubated with primary antibodies, washed with PBS, and then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the sections were mounted and imaged by fluorescence microscopy.

EGFR-knockdown cell lines

PC-9 cells were infected with lentiviral particles encoding either a control shRNA (shCtrl) or one of three EGFR-targeting shRNAs (Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd.). After 72 h, infected cells were selected in puromycin-containing medium for 48 h to generate stable lines. EGFR knockdown was verified by Western blot analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5). The data are presented as means ± SEM. The normality of the data was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. For normally distributed data, statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test (for multiple groups). For non-normally distributed data, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Antitumor efficacy of MEL against NSCLC in vitro and in vivo

This study systematically evaluated the antitumor activity of MEL against NSCLC both in vitro and in vivo. MTT assays showed that MEL exhibited significant time- and dose-dependent proliferation inhibition on NSCLC cell lines PC9 and A549 at concentrations above 2 µg/mL, demonstrating MEL’s selective cytotoxicity and antitumor efficacy against lung cancer cells (Fig. 1A-B). In contrast, MEL inhibited less than 50% of BEAS-2B cells at concentrations below 8 µg/mL(Fig. 1C). DAPI staining revealed increased fluorescence intensity indicative of apoptosis or necrosis in MEL-treated cells after 24 h, while most cells died and fluorescence decreased significantly after 48 h, confirming MEL-induced cell death in NSCLC (Fig. 1D). In a nude mouse xenograft model, MEL significantly suppressed tumor growth without affecting body weight, indicating effective in vivo antitumor activity with low systemic toxicity (Fig. 1E). Tumor volume was notably reduced at 3 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg, supporting an antitumor effect (Fig. 1F-G).

MEL’s target identification

To identify the direct cellular targets of MEL in cancer, we employed the iTSA (Fig. 2A). As shown in Figs. 2B and 7951 proteins were identified by iTSA. Relative to the untreated control group (0 µg/mL MEL), exposure to MEL resulted in the stabilization of 779 proteins, as reflected by marked alterations in their abundance levels. Enrichment analysis based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) further indicated that a substantial proportion of these MEL-responsive proteins are predominantly involved in pathways related to the muscle cell cytoskeletal framework. (Fig. 2C). Disease ontology (DO) enrichment highlighted genes related to lung diseases, from which lung cancer-associated genes were further analyzed (Fig. 2D). Notably, EGFR, KRAS, and GFPT2 emerged as key proteins, with EGFR recognized as a critical oncogenic target in cancer cells (Fig. 2E). Based on this, we hypothesized that MEL may interact with EGFR. We employed AlphaFold to predict the binding mode between EGFR and MEL. Our prediction indicated that MEL was likely to bind to the extracellular domain of EGFR, with the binding site involving key amino acid residues such as Cys-215, Ile-213, and Arg-165 (Fig. 2F). This was confirmed by CETSA using lung tumor tissue lysates, where MEL significantly protected EGFR from temperature-induced denaturation (Fig. 2G-H). In addition, biolayer interferometry (BLI) confirmed that MEL directly binds to EGFR, with a dissociation constant (KD) of 18.6 nM, reflecting a strong binding affinity between them (Fig. 2I).

MEL modulates the proteomic landscape in NSCLC cells

To explore the intracellular signaling pathways affected by MEL, we conducted comprehensive proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses. As shown in Fig. 3A-B, tumor tissues from mouse xenografts of PC9 cells underwent proteomic profiling. By comparing the protein profiles of the control and MEL-treated groups, 154 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were detected (Fig. 3C). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that these DEPs were predominantly associated with biological processes (BP) such as coagulation and wound repair, localized to cellular components (CC) including the collagen-rich extracellular matrix and various vesicular structures, and involved in molecular functions (MF) such as glycosaminoglycan binding and serine-type peptidase activity (Fig. 3D). KEGG pathway enrichment further indicated that MEL markedly influenced several cancer-related pathways, notably proteoglycans in cancer, IL-17 signaling, and ECM–receptor interaction pathways, all of which are known to contribute critically to the modulation of apoptotic processes in tumor cells (Fig. 3E). DO enrichment analysis further highlighted multiple cancer-related genes, including those associated with lung adenocarcinoma (Fig. 3F).

In order to capture global phosphorylation dynamics influenced by the experimental treatment, we first applied principal component analysis to analyze variability and sample coherence within the phosphoproteomic data. The PCA results demonstrated clear clustering and minimal intra-group variation, indicating strong reproducibility and a well-defined biological response (Fig. 3G). Building on this, comprehensive phosphoproteomic profiling was conducted, which led to the identification of a significantly expanded set of differentially phosphorylated proteins (DPPs) between the compared conditions. As shown in Fig. 3H, these DPPs reflected extensive alterations in phosphorylation status, suggesting widespread signaling pathway modulation in response to the treatment. Volcano plot analysis identified 77 upregulated and 182 downregulated phosphoproteins in MEL-treated samples compared to controls (Fig. 3I). Phosphoproteomic GO enrichment analysis revealed that entries related to the cytoskeleton and actin, such as actin filament organization, regulation of actin filament, and actin binding, were significantly enriched, indicating that MEL treatment strongly affected cytoskeletal dynamics. Additionally, significant enrichment of RNA splicing-related terms suggested that MEL could also regulate post-transcriptional splicing processes (Fig. 3J). Since KEGG enrichment analysis yielded only two significant pathways, these were not presented in the main text. To further investigate EGFR-associated alterations, we analyzed the phosphorylation levels of EGFR-related differentially expressed genes in the omics dataset (Fig. 3K). EGFR regulated cell proliferation, migration, and survival through pathways such as RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK (MAPK) and PI3K-AKT-mTOR upon activation. Inhibition of EGFR activation by MEL results in decreased phosphorylation levels of downstream proteins, including BRAF (a key kinase in the MAPK pathway), MTOR, and RPTOR (core components of the mTORC1 complex), thereby suppressing cell growth and migratory capacity. CBLB, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, is involved in the ubiquitination and degradation of EGFR, modulating the intensity of EGFR signaling. MEL may indirectly affect the function and expression of CBLB by reducing EGFR activity, further enhancing the negative regulation of EGFR signaling [26, 27].

MEL mediates apoptosis and autophagy through targeting EGFR/STAT3 signaling

To further clarify the mechanism by which MEL induces cytotoxicity, we monitored changes in apoptosis- and autophagy-associated protein levels in PC9 cells. MEL exposure (5–6 µg/mL) for 24–48 h significantly upregulated the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio (p < 0.05), implicating mitochondrial apoptotic signaling (Fig. 4A-B). Concurrently, MEL enhanced autophagic activity, as evidenced by a significant increase in the LC3-II/I ratio (Fig. 4C), which was further confirmed by MDC staining (Fig. 4D). At the molecular level, MEL treatment notably decreased the total EGFR protein levels. Besides, after 48 h of treatment with higher concentrations (6 µg/mL), a significant reduction in EGFR phosphorylation (p-EGFR) was observed (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E-F). In the downstream effector protein STAT3, we observed a marked decrease in p-STAT3 levels, while total STAT3 remained unchanged, further confirming the specific inhibition of this signaling pathway (Fig. 4E and G). These results indicate that MEL triggers apoptotic cell death and enhances autophagic activity in NSCLC cells, most likely by directly or indirectly modulating the EGFR/STAT3 signaling axis.

MEL suppresses tumor growth in vivo by inhibiting EGFR signaling

In vivo, the antitumor effects of MEL were consistent with the cellular results, further supporting its role as an EGFR pathway inhibitor. IHC analysis revealed that MEL treatment significantly downregulated p-EGFR levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 5A-B). Western blot analyses revealed a marked decrease in the abundance of both total EGFR and its phosphorylated form in the MEL-treated groups (Fig. 5C-E). MEL administration also resulted in substantial reductions in total ERK and p-ERK protein levels, together with a notable decline in p-STAT3 expression (Fig. 5F-J). Collectively, these alterations suggest that MEL exerts a broad inhibitory effect on the EGFR-driven signaling axis in vivo. Moreover, MEL exposure led to a significant increase in the LC3-II/I ratio within tumor tissues, indicative of a pronounced induction of autophagy (Fig. 5F and K). Immunofluorescence staining further showed a significant increase in the LC3B fluorescence in tumor tissues from MEL-treated mice (Fig. 5L-M). Collectively, these in vivo results confirm that MEL exerts its anti-cancer effects by inhibiting EGFR phosphorylation, thereby attenuating downstream ERK and STAT3 signaling, and ultimately promoting autophagic activity in NSCLC tumors.

Construction of EGFR knockdown PC9 cell line and targeting effect of MEL

We constructed an EGFR knockdown PC9 cell line to assess the targeting effects of MEL. After infecting PC9 cells with lentivirus, Western blot analysis identified shEGFR-1 as the cell line for subsequent experiments (Fig. 6A). As shown in Fig. 6B, the anti-cancer efficacy of gefitinib, used as a positive control, was significantly attenuated in shEGFR-1 PC9 cells after 48 h of treatment, reflecting a diminished therapeutic response upon target depletion. Similarly, MEL at a concentration of 6 µg/mL also exhibited a diminished anti-cancer effect on shEGFR-1 PC9 cells after 24 and 48 h of treatment (Fig. 6C). To further investigate the effects of MEL and gefitinib on autophagy, we used a lysosomal probe to assess autophagic activity following treatment with both drugs. As shown in Fig. 6D and E, the shCtrl group exhibited prominent red fluorescence after 6 µg/mL MEL and 12.5 µg/mL gefitinib treatment, indicating that both drugs induced autophagy. However, in the shEGFR group, the fluorescence intensity after treatment with either gefitinib or MEL showed no significant change, with no notable difference compared to the control group (Fig. 6E).

Subsequently, we examined the expression of EGFR-associated proteins in the shEGFR group (Fig. 6F). Following exposure to 6 µg/mL MEL or 12.5 µg/mL gefitinib for 24 h, the protein levels of EGFR and p-EGFR showed no obvious change relative to the corresponding control group (Fig. 6G).However, the expression of STAT3 and p-STAT3 was markedly downregulated (Fig. 6H). These results suggest that MEL, similar to gefitinib, exerts its anti-cancer effects, in part, by targeting EGFR.

Discussion

The development of therapeutics for NSCLC is crucial for improving patient survival. This study first revealed the antitumor effect of MEL against NSCLC in vitro and in vivo. In this study, gefitinib was employed as a positive control against NSCLC. It is noteworthy that Gefitinib was administered systemically to simulate its clinical application, whereas MEL was applied locally to circumvent its rapid systemic clearance and potent hemolytic toxicity. Despite the differences in administration routes and schedules, our findings provide a robust validation of MEL’s antitumor efficacy, offering a meaningful comparison with established EGFR-targeted therapy.

Natural small molecules and peptides derived from traditional medicine represent valuable chemical resources for anti-cancer drug discovery [28]. However, a major obstacle in natural product research is the lack of well-defined drug targets. In NSCLC, EGFR mutations occur in 10–50% of cases, leading to constitutively enhanced tyrosine kinase activity [29]. These mutations activate downstream signaling pathways without ligand binding, thereby driving uncontrolled cell proliferation, suppressing apoptosis, and promoting angiogenesis and metastasis [30]. Currently, therapeutic approaches targeting EGFR in NSCLC rely primarily on monoclonal antibodies—such as cetuximab and panitumumab—and small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors. However, their clinical benefits are frequently undermined by the rapid and widespread development of resistance.

Consequently, inhibiting EGFR kinase activity holds significant therapeutic value, especially for patients with EGFR-TKI resistance [31]. Our previous study demonstrated that bee venom enhanced the inhibitory efficacy of the EGFR-TKI gefitinib against NSCLC via EGFR-mediated autophagy [32]. The potential for overcoming drug resistance through this combination could offer significant therapeutic benefits. Given that MEL is the primary component of bee venom [33], the effect of MEL on EGFR in NSCLC warrants further investigation.

Reports have shown that MEL possesses potent anti-cancer activity across various tumor types, as well as notable anti-inflammatory properties [34, 35]. However, its direct molecular targets have remained largely undefined. Using the iTSA, we identified EGFR as a direct binding target of MEL. BLI analysis results demonstrated that the dissociation constant (KD) was 18.6 nM. In comparison, the KD value of gefitinib was approximately 50 ± 6 nM, indicating that MEL exhibits a markedly stronger binding affinity for EGFR [36]. EGFR is frequently overexpressed in solid tumors and modulates autophagy through several key pathways, including the EGFR-mTOR, EGFR-RAS, EGFR-Beclin1, EGFR-STAT3, and EGFR-LAPTM4B [37, 38]. As a central metabolic checkpoint, mTOR serves as a potent negative regulator of the autophagic machinery by phosphorylating and inactivating the ULK1 initiation complex. Attenuation of mTOR signaling eliminates this inhibitory phosphorylation, promoting the assembly of ULK1 with Beclin-1 and other core ATG proteins to trigger autophagosome formation [39]. Within EGFR signaling, the RAS-RAF-ERK (MAPK) cascade can function as a regulator of autophagy; it obstructs autophagic flux primarily by activating mTORC1 and inhibiting the nuclear translocation of the transcription factor TFEB [40]. Consequently, suppressing this pathway relieves the inhibitory block on autophagy. Furthermore, the majority of STAT3 resides in the cytosol, the primary site of autophagy. Cytoplasmic STAT3 inhibits autophagy via its SH2 domain, which binds to the C-terminus of protein kinase R (PKR), thereby preventing the phosphorylation of eIF2α―a critical step in the LC3B and ATG5 initiation cascade [41]. Thus, the inhibition of STAT3 facilitates autophagic induction. Our phosphoproteomic analysis of MEL-treated NSCLC cells revealed significant suppression of BRAF and mTOR phosphorylation. Moreover, MEL reduced STAT3 phosphorylation levels in both in vitro and in vivo tumor models. These findings suggest that by binding to and inhibiting EGFR, MEL induces sustained hyper-activation of autophagic flux, likely triggering a transition from cytoprotective autophagy to autophagy-dependent cell death. While our current study elucidates this upstream regulatory axis, future investigations utilizing specific autophagy inhibitors, such as 3-methyladenine, or targeted genetic knockdown of ATG proteins are required to definitively establish the functional indispensability of this pathway in MEL-mediated cytotoxicity.

We constructed an EGFR knockdown PC9 cell line to assess the targeting effects of MEL. MEL at a concentration of 6 µg/mL exhibited a diminished anti-cancer effect on shEGFR-1 PC9 cells. And MEL had no obvious autophagic activity against shEGFR-1 PC9 cells. In EGFR-silenced cell lines, MEL treatment notably failed to alter the protein abundance of EGFR or p-EGFR compared to controls; however, it successfully suppressed the expression of both total and phosphorylated STAT3. The persistent inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation by MEL, even in EGFR-knockdown cells, strongly suggests the involvement of an EGFR-independent mechanism. This phenomenon can be elegantly explained by the amphiphilic nature of the MEL peptide. Beyond specific receptor binding, MEL is well-recognized for its ability to physically insert into the lipid bilayer, leading to the loss of cell membrane integrity and the perturbation of lipid rafts. Given that lipid rafts serve as essential physical platforms for the assembly and activation of numerous signaling cascades, their global disruption by MEL can effectively dismantle signal transduction in a manner independent of specific receptor-ligand interactions. Consequently, the observed STAT3 suppression likely results from the synergistic effect of direct EGFR targeting and a generalized attenuation of membrane-associated signaling induced by biophysical membrane disruption.

While iTSA identified EGFR as the predominant target, it also revealed other noteworthy candidates such as KRAS and GSTP1 [42]. Recent studies have further demonstrated that MEL targets NRP-1 to attenuate pulmonary fibrosis and inhibits NSCLC metastasis via USP10 targeting, underscoring its inherent polypharmacological properties [43]. Among these diverse candidates, we prioritized EGFR based on spatial rationale: as an amphiphilic peptide, MEL predominantly accumulates at the plasma membrane, rendering transmembrane receptors with exposed extracellular domains the most physically accessible targets. Intracellular proteins like KRAS likely serve as secondary targets, engaged only following peptide internalization. These intracellular proteins may participate in a synergistic network that bolsters the overall therapeutic efficacy of MEL. Specifically, while EGFR initiates the signaling cascade at the cell surface, KRAS functions as a critical downstream molecular switch [44]. In many malignancies, KRAS mutations or overactivation frequently drive resistance to upstream EGFR inhibitors [45]; however, the multi-targeted action of MEL may circumvent such resistance. This multifaceted interaction establishes a robust inhibitory network wherein the suppression of EGFR limits initial activation, while the subsequent engagement of secondary targets ensures a sustained attack on intracellular signaling hubs. Consequently, this polypharmacological approach positions MEL as a potent agent capable of overcoming the inherent limitations of highly specific yet easily bypassed monotherapies.

Despite the promising findings presented in this study, certain limitations remain and warrant further investigation. The selectivity Index for MEL in A549 cells was low, indicating a narrow therapeutic window for the free peptide. Systemic administration of the free peptide frequently results in non-specific cytotoxicity and rapid degradation by serum proteases. To address these limitations and improve clinical feasibility, the development of sophisticated delivery systems is paramount. Utilizing nanocarriers such as liposomes or polymeric nanoparticles can effectively sequester the peptide, thereby preventing premature interaction with erythrocytes and protecting it from proteolytic cleavage [46]. These delivery platforms not only enhance the stability of the peptide but also widen its therapeutic window by facilitating passive accumulation in tumor tissues through the enhanced permeability and retention effect. Alternatively, localized delivery routes including intratumoral injections or implantable hydrogels present viable strategies to achieve high local drug concentrations, while circumventing systemic toxicity. Furthermore, given its origin as a natural venom component, the intrinsic immunogenicity of the peptide remains a critical concern for repeated therapeutic dosing. The introduction of foreign biological molecules often triggers robust immune responses, potentially leading to the rapid clearance of the peptide or severe hypersensitivity. Strategies designed to enhance stability, particularly surface modifications like PEGylation or complete encapsulation within stealth nanomaterials, play a dual role. These approaches not only improve the pharmacokinetic profile but also sterically hide the immunogenic epitopes from immune cell recognition. Future translational efforts must therefore prioritize these advanced formulation strategies to fully harness the anti-cancer efficacy of the peptide within a safe and clinically viable framework.

In conclusion, this work uncovers EGFR as the critical molecular lynchpin for MEL’s bioactivity. By demonstrating that MEL targets EGFR to modulate autophagic flux and arrest tumor growth (Fig. 7), this study provides a solid theoretical and mechanistic foundation, positioning MEL as a promising lead compound for next-generation targeted therapies.

Melittin, a cationic amphipathic peptide, selectively targets EGFR in NSCLC cells. It binds to EGFR with high affinity (KD = 18 nM), inhibits its phosphorylation, and suppresses downstream STAT3 and ERK signaling, leading to potent antitumor effects

Conclusion

This study reports the identification of EGFR as a direct protein target mediating the anti-NSCLC activity of the natural peptide MEL. Our data indicate that the antitumor activity of MEL is mainly mediated by blocking EGFR phosphorylation, which in turn attenuates downstream oncogenic signaling cascades and triggers both apoptotic cell death and autophagy in tumor cells. In addition, we constructed a comprehensive landscape of MEL-regulated intracellular protein networks and established a curated target repository using the iTSA platform. Taken together, these findings highlight MEL as a potential lead molecule for developing peptide-derived anti-cancer agents and offer important mechanistic clues for advancing NSCLC-targeted therapies.
