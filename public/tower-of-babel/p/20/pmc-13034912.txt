Analyzing factors associated with sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Wonseon Shin, Moon Jin Lee, Jiyoun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia is a critical health issue among older adults that leads to reduced muscle mass, strength decline, and impaired physical function. It is associated with falls, fractures, decreased quality of life, and increased mortality. Accurate diagnosis and management of sarcopenia are essential for the healthcare of the elderly. This study aimed to identify the key factors associated with sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults in Korea and assess their relative importance in contributing to the development of diagnostic criteria tailored to the Korean population. A total of 581 individuals aged ≥60 years were recruited from welfare centers and nursing facilities. Sarcopenia was assessed using indicators such as handgrip strength (HGS), 5-time sit-to-stand, short physical performance battery (SPPB), and appendicular skeletal muscle. Multiple linear regression and receiver operating characteristic analyses were conducted to determine the relative importance and diagnostic accuracy of associated factors. The SPPB, HGS, age, body mass index, 30-second chair stand test, plantar flexion strength, and 2-minute walking test were significant factors related to sarcopenia. Among these, the SPPB score (β = −0.332, P < .001, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.786, cutoff = 9.5) and HGS (β = −0.294, P < .001, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.834, cutoff = 17.75) were the strongest predictors of sarcopenia, highlighting the importance of physical function in diagnosing sarcopenia. This study systematically evaluated key indicators for diagnosing sarcopenia and provided valuable data for establishing diagnostic criteria suitable for older adults in South Korea. These findings emphasize the necessity of incorporating physical function fitness assessments into the diagnosis of sarcopenia to improve early detection and intervention.

1. Introduction

Sarcopenia is a serious health issue closely associated with aging, and its significance has grown with global aging trends. This disease is characterized by loss of muscle mass and decline in muscle strength, with severe adverse outcomes, such as functional impairment, falls and fractures, reduced quality of life, and a shortened lifespan.[1] Accurate diagnosis, prevention, and management of sarcopenia have emerged as critical challenges for elderly healthcare and well-being.

The development of sarcopenia is influenced by a complex interplay of multiple factors. Increased age, nutritional imbalance, lack of physical activity, hormonal changes, and chronic diseases contribute to its development.[2] Recent studies have analyzed the role of body composition, physical function indicators, and functional impairment biomarkers in sarcopenia,[3,4] and found that demographic and physical fitness factors play important roles in its development[5,6]; however, the relative importance of these parameters in the progression of sarcopenia has not been sufficiently elucidated.

Despite advances in the definition and diagnostic criteria of sarcopenia, there is still a lack of universal consensus on tools and criteria to assess it. Various diagnostic guidelines, including the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS),[7] the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP),[1] and the National Institutes of Health,[8] have proposed different diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia. Although these diagnostic criteria are generally designed for specific regions, applying these region-specific guidelines across diverse populations raises concerns regarding cross-population validity.[9] Chiu et al evaluated sarcopenia prevalence using 9 diagnostic criteria from different regions and reported a wide range of prevalence rates from 4.8% (Foundation for the National Institutes of Health) to 16.1% (EWGSOP).[9] Such variability indicates that estimates of sarcopenia prevalence can differ significantly depending on the definition applied. Therefore, it is essential to develop appropriate assessment tools and diagnostic criteria that reflect the characteristics of the Korean population to enhance the clinical applicability of sarcopenia diagnosis. Identifying major factors influencing the occurrence of sarcopenia is an urgent priority.

This study aimed to address the limitations of the previous research by analyzing various factors related to the diagnosis of sarcopenia, identifying the most relevant factors, and evaluating their relative importance. We aimed to establish accurate and reliable diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia in the Korean population to improve early diagnosis and prevention of sarcopenia in an aging society.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.[10] This study included 581 older adults aged 60 to 98 years who were in facilities such as welfare centers and geriatric hospitals in the metropolitan area of South Korea. In addition, the sample was recruited from February to June 2023 through a voluntary sampling method. Additionally, a priori power analysis using G*Power software (3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-University, Düsseldorf, Germany) indicated that a minimum of approximately 200 participants would be needed to detect a moderate effect size (f2 = 0.15) with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.95. Therefore, the sample size of 564 participants used in this study was more than sufficient to ensure adequate statistical power. The participants were selected based on the absence of severe diseases, disabilities, or physical activity limitations. Individuals who were unable to perform handgrip strength (HGS) tests or the short physical performance battery (SPPB) for sarcopenia diagnosis were excluded, as shown in Figure 1. All participants provided written informed consent either personally or through their guardians. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gachon University (approval number: 1044396-202309-HR-188-01) on October 26, 2023, and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Subject flow diagram.

2.1.1. Diagnosis of sarcopenia

Participants were assessed for sarcopenia according to the 2019 AWGS diagnostic criteria.[7] The criteria included low muscle strength (HGS < 28 kg for men and <18 kg for women), low physical performance (5-time sit-to-stand [5TSTS] ≥ 12 seconds or SPPB ≤ 9), and low muscle mass (appendicular skeletal muscle [ASM]/ht2 < 7.0 kg/m2 for men and <5.7 kg/m2 for women). The participants were categorized into 3 groups: normal, possible sarcopenia, and sarcopenia (including both sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia), as shown in Figure 2. Possible sarcopenia was defined as low skeletal muscle strength or physical performance; sarcopenia was defined as low skeletal muscle mass, low muscle strength, or poor physical performance; and severe sarcopenia was defined as low skeletal muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance.[7]

Diagnostic process of sarcopenia based on the AWGS 2019. AWGS = Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia.

2.2. Measurements

Patient demographics were analyzed (weight, skeletal muscle mass, body fat mass, body mass index [BMI], and body fat percentage) using a body composition analyzer (InBody720, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea), while height was measured using a stadiometer (BMS330, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Sarcopenia diagnostic factors were assessed based on the AWGS 2019 algorithm and included the calf circumference, SARC-F, SARC-Calf questionnaire (SARC-Calf), HGS, 5TSTS, SPPB, and ASM. The ASM was calculated using the following formula after measuring body composition: skeletal muscle (kg) = 0.244 × body weight + 7.80 × height − 0.098 × age + 6.6 × sex + race − 3.3.[11]

Physical function tests were conducted, namely the plantar flexion strength (PFS, microFET®2, Hoggan Scientific, LLC, Salt Lake City),[12,13] dorsiflexion strength, single-leg stance, 30-second chair stand test (30CST), 4-meter walk test, chair sit and reach test, 2-minute step test (2MST), and timed up and go (TUG) test.

Disease factors were assessed using measurements of the blood pressure (BP), hemoglobin A1c SARC-Calf levels, and skin autofluorescence. Data on medical history (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, sleep disorders, and diabetes) were collected using a self-administered questionnaire.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). Continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance, and categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test (χ2 test). Continuous and categorical variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and n (%), respectively. Sarcopenia was set as the dependent variable, while the independent variables included age, sex, body measurements (6 factors), sarcopenia diagnostics (7 factors), physical function (8 factors), and disease-related factors (7 factors). A stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent variables most strongly associated with sarcopenia. To control for confounding variables, key demographic factors (age, sex, and BMI) and physical function indicators were included in the regression model. Variance inflation factor was calculated to assess multicollinearity, and all selected variables had variance inflation factor < 5, indicating no severe collinearity issues. Additionally, residual plots were examined to assess homoscedasticity and the normality of residuals, confirming that the assumptions of the regression model were satisfied. The independence of residuals was assessed using the Durbin–Watson test, and no significant autocorrelation was detected (Durbin–Watson = 1.892). To explore subgroup differences, separate regression analyses were conducted for age groups (60–69 years, 70–79 years, and ≥80 years) and sex (male vs female) to determine whether the factors associated with sarcopenia varied across demographic groups. Additionally, interaction terms (Age × BMI, HGS × sex) were included in multiple linear regression models to assess moderating effects. However, no statistically significant interactions were found (P > .05), suggesting that the associations between independent variables and sarcopenia were consistent across subgroups. A total of 564 participants were included in the study. No missing data were observed for key variables, including sarcopenia diagnostic indicators and physical function tests. Minor missing values (<5%) in disease-related variables (e.g., self-reported medical history) were addressed using complete case analysis (listwise deletion), as the proportion of missing data was minimal and unlikely to introduce bias. For significant variables identified in the regression analysis, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to evaluate their predictive power for sarcopenia. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and cutoff values were obtained from the receiver operating characteristic analysis. Sensitivity, which represents the ability to correctly identify sarcopenia cases, was used to assess the diagnostic performance of the selected variables. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

4. Discussion

This study makes a significant contribution to the literature by analyzing factors related to sarcopenia that can serve as new indicators for the diagnosis of sarcopenia in older Korean adults. The results confirmed that physical function, BMI, and age were significant predictors of sarcopenia. BMI is generally recognized as a standard indicator of obesity and is widely used as a predictor of metabolic diseases. According to Fan et al, the BMI of the sarcopenia group (21.48 ± 1.73) was significantly lower than that of the normal group (24.77 ± 2.43; P < .001),[14] which is consistent with the U-shaped relationship between BMI and mortality, wherein individuals with moderate BMI tend to have a longer lifespan than those with extremely high or low BMI.[15] In this study, the BMI (β = −0.211, P < .001) showed a negative correlation with sarcopenia, congruent with previous research that identified low BMI as a risk factor for sarcopenia; however, since BMI is calculated as the ratio of weight to height, it does not distinguish between body fat and muscle mass. Although a low BMI may indicate a sarcopenic state due to muscle loss caused by underweight conditions, additional studies are required to validate its reliability as an indicator in older adults.

Sarcopenia is accompanied by a decrease in muscle mass and strength as well as physical function.[16] The Sarcopenia Definitions and Outcomes Consortium has emphasized the importance of muscle function over muscle mass in diagnosing and evaluating sarcopenia.[17] Similarly, the AWGS 2019 and EWGSOP 2 recommend evaluating physical performance using 3 (SPPB, 6-minute walk test [6MWT], and 5TSTS)[7] and 4 (4-meter gait speed, SPPB, TUG, and 400-meter walk test)[1] assessments, respectively. The Korean Working Group on Sarcopenia mandates the use of SPPB for physical function assessment, and suggests gait speed or TUG as alternatives when the SPPB cannot be performed.[18]

Among the 30 predictor variables analyzed in this study, 7, including SPPB, were identified as key predictors of sarcopenia in Korean older adults (Table 3). Five of these variables were related to physical function, with SPPB (β = −0.332, AUC = 0.786) and HGS (β = −0.294, AUC = 0.834) emerging as the most powerful predictors. The SPPB and HGS are fundamental tools for assessing physical function and muscle strength in sarcopenia diagnosis. The SPPB evaluates balance, lower limb strength, and mobility.[19] According to Brown et al, individuals with declining or persistently low SPPB scores are about 4 to 5 times more likely to develop mobility disability compared to those with stable, higher scores.[20] These findings highlight that physical function assessments, such as the SPPB, reflect daily living capabilities and serve as important indicators of independence and quality of life in older adults. The HGS, a proxy for upper-body strength and overall muscle strength, is strongly associated with frailty and mortality.[21] McGrath et al reported that men with lower HGS had a higher risk of developing physical disabilities in the future.[21] This study validated the independent significance of the HGS and SPPB, confirming their reliability in the diagnosis and assessment of sarcopenia.

Sit-to-stand tests are not only measures of strength but also indicators of endurance, balance, and mobility.[22] The 5TSTS is widely used to assess frailty and sarcopenia, and can be performed as a standalone test or as part of the SPPB. In contrast, the 30CST is a component of the senior fitness test and evaluates both muscular endurance and cardiorespiratory fitness.[22] The findings of this study revealed that the 30CST (β = −0.165, P < .001) was a better predictor of sarcopenia than the 5TSTS (β = 0.038, P = .579), which supports previous research indicating that 30CST is a reliable method for identifying the risk for sarcopenia[23]; however, the cutoff value for the 30CST in this study (11.5 repetitions) was lower than that reported in previous studies (≤17 for men, ≤15 for women). Because the 30CST is influenced by muscle strength and endurance, factors such as age and sex play crucial roles in its outcomes. The lack of sex-specific classification and demographic limitations in this study may have contributed to the lower performance threshold compared with previous studies. Future research should refine the cutoff values by incorporating demographic factors based on prior studies.

One of the most notable findings of this study is the inclusion of PFS as a predictor of sarcopenia, which previous studies have rarely reported. The plantar flexor muscles play crucial roles in posture maintenance and locomotion.[24] Isometric PFS is moderately associated with dynamic balance in older adults, but has little to no correlation with static balance.[25] PFS is closely related to balance control (dynamic reactive balance), forward fall recovery (dynamic reactive balance), and walking speed (dynamic proactive balance). Specifically, the relative effort during gait was higher in the plantar flexor muscles than in the hip and knee extensors. A high relative effort indicates an insufficient reserve capacity to generate additional torque in challenging situations, such as balance recovery after a loss of stability.[26] Older adults utilize a greater proportion of their plantar flexor torque generation capacity than younger adults, and this reliance increases in individuals with lower strength. During aging, type II muscle fibers, which are crucial for force generation in the early phases of muscle contraction, are more affected than type I fibers,[27] which aligns with the fact that the gastrocnemius, a primary agonist of plantar flexor contraction, is predominantly composed of fast-twitch fibers. The plantar flexor muscle-tendon unit is a key site for energy storage and release during gait, contributing significantly to forward propulsion[28]; hence, weakness of the plantar flexors leads to inefficient push-off and reduced gait speed.[26] These findings suggest that PFS is not only a predictor of sarcopenia but also a crucial indicator of functional fitness in older adults. Further research incorporating ultrasound and electromyography is required to validate the effectiveness of PFS measurements.

Finally, sarcopenia is associated with cardiorespiratory fitness in older adults. According to the AWGS 2019, the 6MWT (<1.0 m/s) is recommended for assessing low physical performance, and is commonly used to evaluate aerobic capacity and endurance in various clinical conditions, including sarcopenia, chronic heart failure, and chronic pulmonary disease[29–31]; however, this test requires a relatively large space and extended testing time.[30] To address this limitation, the 2MST has been proposed as an alternative because it requires minimal space, shorter testing duration, and no specialized equipment. Surapichpong et al compared the 2-minute marching test and the 6MWT in adults (mean age: 29.6 years) and found that the estimated VO2 max values were comparable (41.00 ± 3.95 mL/kg/min vs 40.65 ± 3.98 mL/kg/min).[32] Haas et al examined whether the 2MST could serve as a substitute for the 6MWT in assessing functional status and exercise capacity in older adults aged 64 to 77 years. The results showed no significant differences between the 2 tests in terms of heart rate, oxygen saturation, perceived exertion, and BP, which led to the conclusion that the 2MST is a safe alternative to the 6MWT for submaximal fitness assessment.[33] In this study, the 2MST was significantly lower in the sarcopenia group than in the other groups (P < .001) and was significantly associated with sarcopenia (β = −0.097, P = .032, AUC = 0.758); hence, the 2MST is a useful tool not only for evaluating aerobic capacity, but also for predicting sarcopenia risk. Lin et al reported that the sarcopenia group demonstrated lower 2MST performance than the non-sarcopenia group.[6] Chien et al and de Oliveira et al found that aerobic fitness levels were significantly lower in individuals with sarcopenia than in those with normal skeletal muscle mass. They also reported that both lean mass and quadriceps strength contributed to a decline in aerobic capacity,[34,35] which suggests that age-related declines in muscle strength and mass are closely associated with reduced aerobic fitness in older adults. From a physiological perspective, the relationship between decreased aerobic capacity and sarcopenia can be explained as follows: sarcopenia may reduce the capillary density in muscle fibers, limiting the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the muscles, which in turn impairs exercise capacity; loss of muscle mass leads to increased fat infiltration within the muscles, altering muscle metabolism, and reducing the efficiency of aerobic energy production; and a decline in the mitochondrial oxidative capacity, which is significantly associated with muscle strength and quality, contributes to this relationship.[36] Aging-induced reductions in mitochondrial density and function can lead to DNA damage, disrupt calcium regulation, and generate reactive oxygen species, which negatively affect muscle fiber structure and function, and ultimately contribute to decreased muscle force production.[37] These physiological mechanisms highlight the critical role of aerobic capacity in the diagnosis, assessment, and prediction of sarcopenia; hence, the development of comprehensive exercise programs that incorporate various training modalities is essential for preventing sarcopenia and frailty in the aging population.

Despite these significant findings, this study had several limitations. First, the sample size was not sufficiently large to represent the entire Korean older adult population. The findings of this study may not be fully generalizable to all older adults in Korea, particularly those residing in rural areas or those with different socioeconomic backgrounds. Further studies with larger and more diverse populations, including individuals with different levels of physical activity and frailty, are needed to validate these findings. Second, although some assessment tools used in this (e.g., the PFS and 2MST) are commonly utilized in physical function evaluations among older adults, they have been relatively underutilized in sarcopenia research. Therefore, further studies are warranted to establish their reliability and validity as assessment tools for sarcopenia. Third, this study did not differentiate between sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia, highlighting the need for future research to refine the classification criteria for more precise analysis.

Finally, in clinical practice, the integration of these simple and validated physical performance measures – such as SPPB, HGS, 30CST, and PFS – can enhance the early detection of sarcopenia and allow for the timely initiation of tailored exercise and rehabilitation interventions. This approach may help maintain or improve functional independence and prevent adverse outcomes, such as falls, hospitalization, and institutionalization, in older adults. Additionally, the incorporation of aerobic fitness assessments underscores the importance of multidimensional intervention strategies that address both muscular and cardiorespiratory health, ultimately supporting healthy aging and reducing the burden of age-related disability.

5. Conclusion

This study analyzed key factors for diagnosing and assessing sarcopenia in community-dwelling older adults in South Korea and evaluated their relative importance. Our findings identified the SPPB, HGS, age, BMI, 30CST, PFS, and 2MST as the primary factors associated with sarcopenia, which highlight the significance of physical function in the early diagnosis and prevention of sarcopenia. These findings contribute to the development of diagnostic criteria and assessment tools tailored to the characteristics of the elderly Korean population. Future research should further investigate the causal relationships between these factors and sarcopenia, while considering sex differences for a more comprehensive understanding of this disease.
