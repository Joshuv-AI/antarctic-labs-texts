Structural and biochemical insights into the antibiofilm activity of cationic lipoamino acid ester against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Aleena Pious, Esackimuthu Paramasivam, Subham Preetam, Anbazhagan Veerappan, Fuad Ameen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three-dimensional structures, primarily derived from X-ray crystallography, are crucial for understanding the solid-state properties of active pharmaceuticals and for enabling structure-based drug design. Herein, we report the first single crystal X-ray structure of cationic lipoaminoacid ester [(2-((N-(2-methoxy-2-oxoethyl)tetradecanamido)methyl)-1-methylpyridin-1-ium iodide, QPyN14Ge]. QPyN14Ge crystallized in the triclinic space group P1̄. QPyN14Ge has a highly ordered and coherent packing arrangement due to tail-to-tail interdigitation of hydrophobic layers, stabilization by planar trans-amide head groups, controlled flexibility at the distal ends of the hydrocarbon chains, and heavy-atom anchoring by iodine near the polar region. QPyN14Ge exhibits antimicrobial activity against Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus by compromising membrane integrity. Molecular docking reveals that QPyN14Ge binds to the Staphylococcal protein FemX, which is responsible for peptidoglycan biosynthesis. Molecular dynamics simulations support the formation of a stable, energetically favourable FemX-QPyN14Ge complex, suggesting its potential as a cell wall biosynthesis inhibitor. QPyN14Ge shows strong antibiofilm activity at the MIC and can inhibit biofilm formation (40%) even at sub-MIC levels. Confocal microscopy shows that sub-MIC QPyN14Ge reduces the biofilm thickness from 11.88 ± 1.16 µm to 7.01 ± 0.95 µm. The antibiofilm mechanism is attributed to the inhibition of major virulence factors of MRSA, such as cell surface hydrophobicity, slime synthesis, exopolysaccharide production, and staphyloxanthin production. The findings suggest that ability of QPyN14Ge to form interdigitated molecular packing, in which molecules insert themselves between lipid bilayers, thinning and compromising membrane integrity, resulted in antibacterial and antibiofilm activities.

Introduction

Lipoamino acids (LAAs) are a class of amphiphiles widely used in food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic formulations due to their low toxicity, high biodegradability, and versatile properties as biosurfactants and active ingredients.1 They are characterized by a structure that features both a hydrophobic fatty acid chain (lipo) and a hydrophilic amino acid group, which gives them an amphiphilic nature. These types of LAA conjugates are used to enhance the oral absorption of drugs, such as peptides, β-lactam antibiotics, and alkaloid-based molecules.2,3 The conjugation of amino acids to a fatty acid alters lipophilicity, thereby influencing the membrane permeability of the synthesized molecule.4N-palmitoylated amino acids and N-stearyl amino acids function as potential biostatic additives.5 Ricinoleic acid-based lipoamino acid derivatives are demonstrated to have antibacterial, antifungal, and antibiofilm properties. The study demonstrates that the addition of amino acids tunes molecular lipophilicity and enhances antimicrobial activity.6 Methyl oleate grafted with amino acids using thioglycolic acid as a spacer group showed activity against Gram-positive bacteria and fungal strains.7 The study shows that the antibacterial activity of derivatives of heterocyclic amino acids varies significantly.7 Recent findings have established the structure–activity relationship for some LAA molecules, but their molecular packing in three dimensions remains limited.8,9 Many amphiphiles are highly flexible and form complex mesophases (like micelles, vesicles, or liquid crystals) rather than well-ordered single crystals.10–12 Structural information obtained from single crystals by X-ray diffraction can provide initial structural input (e.g., bioactive conformation) for 3D-QSAR studies.13–15 Recently, we reported the synthesis of cationic N-acylthiazolidine and their antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria.16 In this paper, we report the single crystal structure of lipoamino acid class molecule, 2-((N-(2-methoxy-2-oxoethyl)tetradecanamido)methyl)-1-methylpyridin-1-ium iodide (QPyN14Ge) and describe its molecular packing.

The application of QPyN14Ge in inhibiting Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus biofilms and its interaction with Factors Essential for Methicillin-resistance (Fem) proteins were investigated. Bacterial biofilms are recognized as a crucial factor in drug resistance, as they shield bacteria from antibiotics and the immune system by forming a protective matrix, resulting in persistent, hard-to-treat infections on medical devices and tissues.17 MRSA is renowned for its ability to form biofilms, resist antibiotics, evade the immune response, and cause persistent infections.18 MRSA-associated antibiotic-resistant infection has been linked to over 100 000 deaths globally.19 The Fem protein family contributes to the drug resistance in Staphylococcus aureus.20 Fem transpeptidase proteins are responsible for cross-linking peptidoglycan, a key component of microbial cell growth, division, and structural integrity. The Fem family of proteins, composed of FemX, FemA, and FemB, contributes to cell wall formation by sequentially adding glycine residues to Lipid-II to form the mesh-like structure of peptidoglycan. FemX is involved in initiating the first glycine addition to the lysine in the A-E-K-A-A pentapeptide stem of Lipid-II.21 There are currently no known human homologues of FEM proteins. Therefore, they represent a promising target for the development of highly selective antimicrobial drugs to tackle bacterial resistance with a lower risk of host toxicity.22 QPyN14Ge binding to FemX was assessed by molecular docking; biofilm inhibition activity was determined; and the mechanism of action was discussed.

Materials and methods

Single crystal X-ray diffraction

The amphiphile, 2-((N-(2-methoxy-2-oxoethyl)tetradecanamido)methyl)-1-methylpyridin-1-ium iodide was synthesised from the starting material glycine ester using reported procedure (Scheme 1).16 Briefly, one equivalent of glycine methyl ester hydrochloride, dissolved in 10 mL methanol, was allowed to react with five equivalents of pyridine aldehyde in the presence of 2.5 equivalents of sodium cyanoborohydride. After 24 h, the reaction mass was extracted with ethyl acetate and saturated NH4Cl. The crude product in the organic layer was dried, dissolved in DCM, and reacted with 1.2 equivalents of myristoyl chloride in the presence of 3 equivalents of triethylamine. After 1 h, the reaction mass was extracted with ethyl acetate and saturated NaHCO3. The product in the organic layer was purified by column chromatography. The yield of the methyl N-(pyridine-2-ylmethyl)-N-tetradecanoylglycinate was 75%. The obtained product was quaternized by reacting with 2 mL methyl iodide in 10 mL acetonitrile for 48 h. The precipitate formed was collected and washed repeatedly with acetone and hexane to remove impurities. The purified QPyN14Ge yield is 95%.

Antibacterial activity

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of QPyN14Ge against MRSA was determined by resazurin microtiter plate assay (REMA). Briefly, QPyN14Ge was serially diluted in a 96-well plate, inoculated with 0.1 OD MRSA (ATCC43300), and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Then, each well was mixed with 50 µL of resazurin and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The color change from blue to pink was noted as MIC.26 The ability of QPyN14Ge to permeate the cell membrane was assessed by propidium iodide uptake assay.27 About 0.5 OD cells were treated with 15.63 µM QPyN14Ge for 2 h and stained with 0.5 mM propidium iodide. The stained cells were visualized in confocal microscopy using a red filter. Cell shape and membrane damage were assessed by scanning electron microscopy.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of FemX was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 6SNR).28 All crystallographic water molecules and heteroatoms were removed using AutoDock Tools, ADT 1.5.7. Polar hydrogens were added, and Kollman united atom charges were assigned. Missing side-chain hydrogens were added according to physiological pH, and the final structure was saved in PDBQT format for docking. The ligand structure was built and energy-minimized using the MMFF94 force field. Rotatable bonds were detected, and Gasteiger partial charges were assigned in ADT. The minimized and optimized ligand was exported as a PDBQT file. The FemX active-site cavity was defined based on previously reported functional residues involved in peptidoglycan bridge synthesis. A docking grid box was centered at: X = 100.7, Y = −80.19, Z = −61.600. The box dimensions were selected to fully encompass the catalytic pocket and allow complete exploration of the substrate-binding cleft. Molecular docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina version 1.2.0.29 The exhaustiveness parameter was set to 8 to balance computational efficiency and pose sampling. Multiple binding poses were generated and ranked by predicted binding affinity (kJ mol−1). The top-scoring pose was selected for structural and interaction analysis. Docked complexes were visualized using PyMOL 2.5 and Discovery Studio Visualizer. All residues interacting within 4.0 Å of the ligand were considered for analysis. Hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, van der Waals interactions, alkyl and π-alkyl interactions, and carbon–hydrogen bonds were quantified using Discovery Studio's 2D interaction maps. The best-ranked docking pose of the FemX-QPyN14Ge complex was selected for subsequent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to evaluate its stability.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The topology and coordinate files for QPyN14Ge were generated using the Automated Topology Builder (ATB) and incorporated into the MD simulations of the docked FemX-QPyN14Ge complex using GROMACS.30 The complex was solvated in a rectangular simulation box containing the equilibrated three-point TIP3P water model. Counter ions (Na+ or Cl−) were added as required to neutralize the system.31 Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm to remove steric clashes and unfavorable contacts, reducing the maximum force to below 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−1. The minimized system was subsequently equilibrated in the constant–volume, temperature (NVT) and constant–pressure, temperature (NPT) ensembles.32 A production MD simulation was conducted for 100 ns. Finally, the binding free energy of the FemX – QPyN14Ge was calculated using the Molecular Mechanics-Poisson Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) method.33

Antibiofilm activity

The MIC of QPyN14Ge against MRSA was 15.63 µM. Thus, biofilm inhibition was monitored at a sub-MIC (7.81 µM) using light microscopy. Briefly, 1% MRSA cells in Tryptic Soy broth (TSB) containing 0.5% glucose were allowed to grow on glass slides placed in a 12-well plate. After 3 h of incubation at 37 °C in a static incubator, the MRSA biofilm-attached glass slides were treated with QPyN14Ge and incubated for the next 24 h at 37 °C in a static incubator. The slides with biofilm without treatment served as the control. After 24 h, the glass slides were washed with PBS buffer (10 mM sodium phosphate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) to remove any planktonic cells. Then, 0.1% crystal violet (CV) was added and incubated for 20 min. Excess crystal violet dye was removed using PBS buffer wash, and the plates were imaged using light microscopy.

Ring biofilm inhibition assay

Typically, 1% MRSA in 2 mL TBS with 0.5% glucose was inoculated into a glass tube and incubated statically for 3 h at 37 °C. Then, the cells were exposed to 7.81 µM QPyN14Ge and incubated for 24 h in a static incubator at 37 °C. After incubation, the culture was carefully discarded and washed with PBS to remove any adherent planktonic cells. After washing, the test tubes were stained with 0.1% CV for 20 minutes and washed with PBS to remove unbound CV, and air-dried in an inverted position. Ring formation was observed at the air-liquid interface in untreated glass tubes.

Exopolysaccharide production assay

EPS was quantified using TSB medium, which supports MRSA EPS production as the main biofilm component. Briefly, MRSA was cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in 24-well plates, with and without QPyN14Ge. After 24 h, the plates were rinsed with PBS buffer to remove any adherent planktonic cells. The EPS was extracted using 650 µL of sulfuric acid and 125 µL of phenol (5% v/v). The final volume of the extract was adjusted to 1 mL using distilled water, and then its absorbance at 490 nm was measured.

Microbial adherence to hydrocarbons assay

The microbial adhesion to hydrocarbons in the absence and presence of QPyN14Ge was assessed to determine cell surface hydrophobicity. Briefly, 0.5 OD MRSA was treated for 2 h at 37 °C with QPyN14Ge. After 2 h of treatment, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and then resuspended in 1 mL PBS. About 1 mL of toluene was added to the PBS suspension, vortexed vigorously for 10 min, and allowed to phase-separate. The aqueous phase was separated, and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm. The following equation was used to determine the percent hydrophobicity. % hydrophobicity = [1 − (OD600nm after vortexing)/(OD600nm before vortexing)] × 100.

Slime production assay

The effect of QPyN14Ge on slime production was qualitatively assessed by Congo red agar (CRA) plate assay. The CRA plate was prepared in 3.7% BHI broth containing 0.8% Congo red, 1.5% agar, and 5% sucrose. Untreated control and QPyN14Ge-treated MRSA were streaked onto a CRA plate and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a static incubator.

Staphyloxanthin production assay

To evaluate the staphyloxanthin production, 1% MRSA was grown in 10 mL TSB broth containing 0.5% glucose in the presence and absence of QPyN14Ge. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the treated cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min and washed with PBS. The color difference in the pelleted cells was recorded as a photograph. Then, the cells were resuspended in 2 mL and incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C. After 24 h, the samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant absorbance was recorded at 465 nm. The absorbance value was measured at 465 nm, and the percentage of staphyloxanthin was calculated. Staphyloxanthin inhibition % = (control A465 nm − treated A465 nm)/control A465 nm).

Structural analysis

Analysis of molecular connectivity and electron density confirms that the heteroaromatic ring in QPyN14Ge is present as a pyridinium cation. At the same time, the iodine atom functions as a non-coordinating iodide counter-ion rather than a covalently bound substituent. The iodide anion occupies an interstitial position within the polar region of the lattice, where it engages in electrostatic and weak contact with the cationic pyridinium headgroup, thereby stabilizing the ionic assembly. The correction to the structural interpretation establishes the compound as an ionic pyridinium-iodide salt, in which the cationic aromatic headgroup, the flexible hydrophobic tail, and the iodide counter-ion collectively define the molecular geometry, packing interactions, and overall lamellar organization of the crystal. The relatively high residual electron density peak of 1.59 e Å−3 near the iodine atom contributes to a high residual density ratio. Residual peaks of this magnitude are typical in iodine-containing materials due to Fourier truncation effects and inadequate absorption correction caused by heavy scatterers. The residual density does not correspond to any chemically relevant atom position, and hence is regarded as acceptable within the framework of a heavy-atom structure.

Fractional coordinates and isotropic displacement parameters reveal the expected mobility gradient commonly seen in long acyl chains (see Table S1). The head-group atoms (N, O, carbonyl C) exhibit moderate atomic displacement parameters (Ueq) values. In contrast, the methylene units in the central and terminal regions of the chain show progressively larger displacement parameters. The trend is further supported by anisotropic displacement values, where Ueq increases along the hydrocarbon tail, particularly for terminal carbons (see Table S2). Such behaviour is consistent with an extended, all-trans alkyl chain, in which molecular flexibility increases with distance from the more rigid amide head group.

Bond angles around the amide nitrogen and carbonyl carbon (118–125°) further support sp2 hybridization and a strongly planar peptide-like geometry (see Table S4). Within the aliphatic chain, C–C distances and C–C–C angles fall within normal ranges for sp3-hybridized carbons, with no distortions or bond compression. These values clearly demonstrate that the alkyl chain retains its expected zig-zag, tetrahedral all-trans geometry.

Torsion-angle evaluation provides the most direct evidence of the molecular conformation (see Table S5). Nearly all dihedral angles along the hydrocarbon chain lie between ±175° and ±180°, including representative torsions such as C00P–C010–C012–C013 = 177.7°, C012–C013–C014–C016 = −178.3°, and C018–C019–C01D–C01P = 179.4°. These values confirm that the entire hydrophobic region adopts a fully extended all-trans conformation, analogous to the conformations reported for N-myristoylglycine (NMG) and N-palmitoylglycine (NPG).35 No gauche defects, kinks, or localized distortions are present. Similarly, the torsion angles around the amide linkage (e.g., N004–C00D–C00O–N00A = −177.4°, N006–C00I–C00M–N008 = 174.0°) demonstrate a trans orientation of the carbonyl oxygen and amide N–H group. This arrangement mirrors the amide geometry of NMG and NPG and reflects the stability of the anti-periplanar configuration commonly favoured in peptide-like systems.

Hydrogen atoms were refined using standard riding and rotating-group models, with no abnormal displacement parameters or geometric inconsistencies (see Table S6). Increasing U values toward the end of the hydrocarbon tail again corroborate enhanced mobility in the terminal region. The absence of disorder or alternative conformers reinforces the conclusion that QPyN14Ge crystallizes in a highly ordered, linear conformation.

The unit cell of QPyN14Ge has a calculated volume of 2737.3(4) Å3, which is consistent with its triclinic P1̄ crystal system and the presence of four molecules per unit cell (Z = 4). The relatively large cell volume arises from the combined effects of the long C14 hydrocarbon chain, the incorporation of a heavy iodine atom, and the packing arrangement in which two crystallographically independent molecules occupy the asymmetric unit. Such a volume is typical of extended N-acyl derivatives, where the all-trans conformation of the hydrophobic chain results in an elongated molecular shape, requiring greater lattice spacing along the c-axis. The observed unit-cell volume is therefore fully consistent with the molecular dimensions, conformational linearity, and packing efficiency of QPyN14Ge, aligning with structural trends seen in related long-chain N-acylglycines such as N-myristoylglycine.

Intermolecular packing analysis

The intermolecular packing of QPyN14Ge is characterized by a highly organized lamellar arrangement governed by the extended all-trans conformation of the C14 hydrocarbon chains and the planar amide headgroups shown in Fig. 2A. Within the triclinic P1̄ lattice, molecules assemble into parallel sheets in which the acyl chains align closely along the long crystallographic c-axis, as reflected by the chain length and the unit-cell dimension a = 26.405 b = 26.405 c = 26.405 Å. This alignment produces a dense, side-by-side arrangement of the alkyl chains, maximizing van der Waals interactions and minimizing steric repulsion. The torsion-angle pattern showed that nearly all C–C–C–C dihedrals were between ±175° and 180°, indicating that the chains retain a fully extended geometry, enabling uniform packing across the hydrophobic layers. This interlayer interdigitation is a hallmark of amphiphilic crystalline solids and contributes to overall lattice cohesion. The thermal displacement parameters support this packing model: low Ueq values near the headgroup indicate rigid, well-anchored polar layers. In contrast, progressively higher Ueq values at the terminal carbons indicate increased conformational freedom in the hydrophobic tails. This gradient is consistent with a lamellar framework featuring rigid polar cores and flexible chain terminals.

(A) Packing diagram of PyN14Ge indicating the interdigitated arrangements. (B) Close-up view of the H-bonding in the polar region showing the involvement of two water molecules.

The hydrogen bonding pattern in the crystal lattice viewed down the c-axis and b-axis is shown in Fig. S2 (see Fig. S2A and B). At the polar interface, the amide headgroups cluster into distinct layers that organize the lattice. An analysis of the crystal structure of QPyN14Ge revealed the presence of five intramolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions, which contribute to stabilization of the molecular conformation. The hydrogen bonds were identified based on donor–acceptor distances (D⋯A), hydrogen–acceptor separations (H⋯A), and D–H⋯A bond angles. Interestingly, the water molecules act as bridging units, connecting iodide molecules and forming a cooperative hydrogen-bond relay motif that significantly stabilizes the crystal packing. As a consequence of these interactions, two independent molecules are present in the asymmetric unit. The close up-view of the hydrogen bonding interaction is shown in Fig. 2B. A strong hydrogen bond is observed between Of(D)and O7(A)(Of–H⋯O7), with a donor–acceptor distance of 2.909 Å, an H⋯A distance of 2.068 Å, and a bond angle of 169.84°, indicating a nearly linear and well-defined interaction. Similarly, a hydrogen bond between O9(D) and Oe(A)O9–H⋯Oe) shows a D⋯A distance of 2.892 Å, an H⋯A separation of 2.051 Å, and a D–H⋯A angle of 170.15°, consistent with strong hydrogen bonding. Additional moderate hydrogen-bonding interactions are present between Oe(A) and Cn(D)(Oe–H⋯Cn), with a D⋯A distance of 3.251 Å and a bond angle of 135.57°, and between I(D) and Of (A)(I1–H⋯Of), where the donor–acceptor distance is 3.663 Å and the bond angle is 156.53°. Another hydrogen bond involving Oe and I2 exhibits (Oe–H⋯I2) D⋯A separation of 3.675 Å and an angle of 162.40°, characteristic of weak to moderate hydrogen bonding. However, strong classical N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds (<2 Å) are limited due to steric orientation, but the trans-amide configuration (N004–C00D–C00O–N00A ≈ −177.4°; N006–C00I–C00M–N008 ≈ 174.0°) produces a planar, conjugated head group capable of forming directional dipolar and short–contact interactions with neighboring molecules. These interactions stabilize the orientation of molecules within each polar layer. The presence of the heavy iodine substituent, positioned near the headgroup region, further enhances structural coherence by serving as an electrostatic and steric anchor, reinforcing molecular registry across inversion-related molecules in the P1̄ space group. Overall, QPyN14Ge exhibits a highly coherent and tightly ordered packing arrangement, driven by (i) the linear, all-trans molecular geometry, (ii) stabilization through planar trans-amide headgroups, (iii) heavy-atom anchoring by iodine near the polar region, (iv) tail-to-tail interdigitation of hydrophobic layers, and (v) controlled flexibility at the distal ends of the hydrocarbon chains. The formation of interdigitated structures can be explained by considering the mismatch between the hydrophilic head group and lipophilic tail. When the bulky pyridinium head group is in proximity to the head region, the linear lipophilic tail may be forced to create voids in the hydrophobic part. The lipid tails could fill these voids through interdigitation, a more favorable energetic state. This form is frequently observed in gel-phase membranes.36 It is more common in membranes containing saturated, symmetric phospholipids, especially in the presence of foreign molecules such as alcohols, anesthetics, and surfactants.37–39

Antibacterial activity

QPyN14Ge shows antibacterial activity against MRSA with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 15.63 µM (see Fig. S3). The effect of QPyN14Ge on bacterial membrane integrity was assessed by propidium iodide (PI) staining. The PI is a cell-impermeable dye; it only enters cells with compromised membranes, and therefore dying, dead, and necrotic nucleated cells stained with PI generate red fluorescence.40 MRSA subjected to QPyN14Ge treatment showed red fluorescent cells, suggesting the loss of membrane integrity (Fig. 3A). Scanning electron microscope imaging shows that QPyN14Ge treatment disrupts the cell wall, resulting in the loss of normal, smooth coccoid structure (Fig. 3B). The rupture of the cell membrane causes the release of intracellular contents and accounts for the cell death.

Molecular docking of QPyN14Ge with FEM proteins

Given the QPyN14Ge membrane-damaging activity, we conducted molecular docking simulations to investigate its binding to the FemX transpeptidase proteins. FemX is responsible for peptidoglycan polymer cross-linking, a key component of microbial cell growth, division, and structural integrity. The FemX crystal structure employed in this study (PDB ID: 6SNR) lacks a co-crystallized ligand or inhibitor, making traditional redocking-based binding-site validation difficult. Therefore, the docking pocket was selected based on the reported substrate-binding/catalytic groove of FemX and was further supported by CASTp binding-pocket prediction.41,42 CASTp analysis identified a prominent pocket in the central groove region, which overlapped with the reported functional/catalytic region. Accordingly, the docking grid was defined around this CASTp-predicted pocket. The predicted QPyN14Ge binding pose was positioned within this pocket and docked using AutoDock Vina version 1.2.0. Docking analysis revealed the QPyN14Ge exhibited a binding affinity to FemX with a docking score of −16.74 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 4).

(A) 3D docked pose of QPyN14Ge with FemX, and (B) 2D diagram showing the amino acid of FemX involved in intramolecular interactions with QPyN14Ge.

The docking pose revealed a conventional hydrogen bond with Gly321 and Met196 and a non-conventional carbon–hydrogen bond with Thr199, Gly200, and Gly355 (Fig. 4). These residues are located within the central groove where FemX positions the donor substrate for pentaglycine bridge formation. The involvement of Gly321 is particularly significant, as it lies close to a region critical for substrate alignment. Although the number of strong hydrogen bonds is limited, their spatial distribution indicates that the ligand is correctly oriented within the FemX catalytic cavity. A strong salt bridge was observed between the QPyN14Ge and Asp203. This residue is part of the negatively charged region that stabilizes substrate engagement during the initial glycine addition step. Salt bridge formation is energetically favorable and provides strong electrostatic anchoring. This interaction compensates for the relatively low number of classical hydrogen bonds and reinforces the ligand's placement in a catalytically relevant region. Additionally, QPyN14Ge showed extensive hydrophobic interactions with residues Phe205, Phe354, Tyr317 (π–alkyl interactions), Leu24, Leu319, Leu370, Pro414 (alkyl interactions). These interactions indicate that the lipid-like extension of the ligand occupies the hydrophobic channel of FemX that normally stabilizes glycine derivatives. Aromatic interactions with Phe205 and Tyr317 are particularly noteworthy, as these residues frequently appear in FemX–ligand complexes and contribute substantially to hydrophobic stabilization. The hydrophobic complementarity strongly supports ligand accommodation within the binding cleft and suggests that the long aliphatic chain helps maintain pocket occupancy. Further, van der Waals contacts with Ala322, Gly204, Tyr318, and Lys374 stabilize the complex by minimizing steric repulsion. These nonspecific interactions contribute cumulatively to the ligand's stability within the cavity, and their uniform distribution around the ligand indicates that the compound fits smoothly into the active-site contours without causing steric strain. The binding potential of QPyN14Ge to the FemX catalytic site suggests that QPyN14Ge may interfere with substrate positioning, inhibit the activity of Fem proteins, and prevent cell wall formation. Further optimization of the chemical scaffold, with improved rigidity and increased hydrogen bonding without sacrificing hydrophobic anchoring, would yield a QPyNAGe-class drug to treat MRSA infection.

Molecular dynamics simulation of QPyN14Ge with FemX

Having observed the binding of QPyN14Ge with FemX, we went on to study the structural stability and conformational integrity of FemX and the FemX-QPyN14Ge docked complex using molecular dynamics simulation. The FemX and FemX-QPyN14Ge complex was subjected to a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation. It analyzed the backbone root–mean–square deviation (RMSD), root–mean–square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). RMSD profile showed that FemX and the FemX-QPyN14Ge complex systems equilibrated within the initial phase of the simulation (Fig. 5A). Apo FemX exhibited fluctuations between 0.20–0.6 nm, indicating intrinsic flexibility. In contrast, the FemX-QPyN14Ge complex maintained comparatively stable deviations (0.30–0.5 nm), suggesting that ligand binding does not induce structural destabilization and supports its conformational stability.

Analyses of MD simulation trajectories of 100 ns. (A) RMSD, (B) Rg, (C) SASA, and (D) RMSF. Black line – apo FemX and red line – FemX-QPyN14Ge complex.

The overall compactness evaluated from Rg analysis indicates that the FemX showed moderate structural flexibility (Rg ∼2.62–2.76 nm) while maintaining global compactness. The QPyN14Ge bound form showed slightly reduced fluctuations (2.60 to 2.72) as compared to the apo form (Fig. 5B). No significant expansion or unfolding events were observed in either system. The stable Rg profile of the ligand–bound complex suggests that QPyN14Ge binding enhances structural compactness and contributes to overall conformational stability. The solvent exposure of the apo and bound forms throughout the simulation was assessed by SASA analysis (Fig. 5C). As shown, apo FemX fluctuated between 207 and 221 nm2, with a slight late-stage reduction, suggesting transient compaction. Comparable values of 208–222 nm2 were observed for the FemX-QPyN14Ge complex, indicating that QPyN14Ge binding does not induce significant conformational expansion and preserves overall structural integrity. The RMSF analysis revealed that most residues fluctuated below 0.20 nm in both systems. A prominent flexible region (240–270 residues) showed reduced fluctuation in the ligand-bound complex compared to the apo form, indicating stabilization of loop regions upon QPyN14Ge binding. Noteworthy, the core and active-site residues remained structurally stable throughout the simulation.

To overcome the accuracy gap between molecular docking and free energy perturbation, the MM/PBSA method was employed to calculate the binding free energy by averaging interaction energies across MD snapshots. The MM/PBSA analysis revealed that the FemX-QPyN14Ge complex exhibited favorable binding, with a total binding free energy of −291.57 kJ mol−1. The energy values associated with electrostatic (−283.68 kJ mol−1) and van der Waals interactions (−82.52 kJ mol−1) support their roles as primary driving forces for complex formation. It is noteworthy that despite the polar solvation energy (86.07 kJ mol−1) resisting binding, the non-polar SASA energy (−11.44 kJ mol−1) enhanced the complex's stability. Per-residue MM/PBSA decomposition analysis identified several key residues contributing to ligand binding (see Fig. S4). Asp203 showed the highest favorable contribution (−56.5867 kJ mol−1), followed by Asp353 (−26.4738 kJ mol−1) and Asp22 (−22.6312 kJ mol−1), indicating strong stabilizing interactions. In contrast, Arg74 (25.0848 kJ mol−1) and Lys374 (36.0518 kJ mol−1) exhibited positive energy contributions, suggesting unfavorable or destabilizing effects on ligand binding. Overall, negatively charged aspartate residues predominantly contributed to complex stabilization. The formation of a stable, energetically favorable FemX-QPyN14Ge complex under simulated physiological conditions, as studied by MD simulation, suggests that QPyN14Ge may inhibit FemX function and prevent MRSA cell wall biosynthesis. The lack of a co-crystallized inhibitor, docking, and MD data should be viewed as supporting computational evidence for a potential FemX-mediated mechanism rather than definitive experimental evidence of FemX inhibition.

Antibiofilm activity of QPyN14Ge

MRSA is renowned for its ability to form biofilms, resist antibiotics, evade the immune response, and cause persistent infections. To overcome MRSA antibiotic resistance, this process must be targeted by preventing adhesion, disturbing the matrix, or promoting dispersion. A good biofilm agent is expected not to affect the growth and metabolic activity of the microorganism.43 Thus, biofilm inhibition was assessed at sub-MIC levels using a crystal violet quantification assay. At 7.81 µM (0.5X MIC), QPyN14Ge showed a maximum 40% of biofilm inhibition in MRSA. The qualitative analysis of MRSA biofilm architecture using microscopic techniques reveals that QPyN14Ge treatment reduces the biofilm-covered surface area compared to the untreated control (Fig. 6A). A Confocal microscopy image of biofilm formed on a glass slide was visualized by staining with acridine orange (Fig. 6B). Consistent with the light microscopy imaging, the biofilm inhibition by QPyN14Ge treatment was observed. The biofilm thickness was reduced from 11.88 ± 1.16 µm to 7.01 ± 0.95 µm. In addition to surface biofilm, aerobic bacteria like MRSA form biofilm at the air-liquid interface to gain access to aeration and liquid nutrients. QPyN14Ge treatment greatly diminishes the air-liquid ring biofilm formation on the glass surface (Fig. 6C). The effect of QPyN14Ge on MRSA growth and viability was assessed on a Nutrient agar plate. The growth of MRSA control and QPyN14Ge treatment at sub-MIC was found to be unchanged (seeFig. S5). Interestingly, growth inhibition was observed above 7.81 µM, indicating that QPyN14Ge inhibits biofilm formation at sub-MIC. The Resazurin microtiter plate assay showed that cells are metabolically active at sub-MIC (see Fig. S3). The findings suggest that the QPyN14Ge treatment at sub-MIC levels can exhibit true antibiofilm activity, thereby preventing the activation of stress-response pathways that frequently lead to antibiotic resistance.

QPyN14Ge inhibits a virulence factor involved in biofilm formation

MRSA produces slime, an extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) layer that serves as a virulence factor, promoting adhesion, increasing bacterial colonization of host tissue, enhancing bacterial persistence, and developing resistance to host immune responses.44 Hence, the slime production was qualitatively analyzed by the Congo red agar (CRA) plate method. The slime-producing MRSA appears black with a white periphery on a CRA plate. Interestingly, QPyN14Ge treatment at 7.81 µM showed reduction in the black colored colonies, revealing the ability of QPyN14Ge to reduce the slime production (Fig. 7A). In addition, EPS quantification assay revealed that QPyN14Ge concentration dependent inhibition of EPS production and exhibited 34% EPS inhibition at sub-MIC (Fig. 7B). The findings suggest that QPyN14Ge could affect the biofilm architecture and induce bacterial sensitivity to external factors. Bacteria with high surface hydrophobicity attach more securely to surfaces, initiating the initial stage of biofilm formation. Therefore, the cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH) of MRSA with and without QPyN14Ge was evaluated using the microbial adhesion to hydrocarbons (MATH) assay. The treatment resulted in a concentration-dependent decrease in CSH. The untreated cells showed 46.5% CSH, which was reduced to 31.2% at sub-MIC treatment, suggesting that QPyN14Ge inhibits cell adhesion and biofilm development (see Fig. S6). Staphyloxanthin, a carotenoid pigment, acts as an antioxidant, helping protect MRSA against host-mediated immune responses.45 QPyN14Ge treatment reduces golden-yellow pigment production compared to the control (see Fig. S7). Quantitative spectroscopic analysis showed a dose-dependent reduction in staphyloxantin production. In particular, 7.81 µM QPyN14Ge inhibited staphyloxanthin by 55%, suggesting that QPyN14Ge inhibits the antioxidant virulence factor and prevents biofilm formation (Fig. 7C).

Based on the crystallographic findings, we hypothesized that QPyN14Ge's ability to self-assemble into an interdigitated structure in the solid phase would be implicated in antibacterial and antibiofilm activity. The interdigitated structure is frequently observed in specific cellular stress conditions. This structure is associated with longer cell junctions and is known to alter paracellular conductance or leakiness, thereby increasing the permeability of ions, solutes, and macromolecules.46Bacillus subtilis produces iturin A, a cyclic lipopeptide whose ability to form an interdigitated structure within lipid bilayers has been linked to antifungal activity.47 Devanand et al. demonstrated that the membrane-active protein Nogo-66 induces interdigitation in dimyristoylphosphocholine membranes.48 Frewein et al. showed interdigitation-based ordering and disordering in asymmetric membranes.49 Alcohol and surfactants are known to induce interdigitation in membranes. Having learned about interdigitation from the literature, we propose a plausible mechanism for QPyN14Ge activity in which the cationic head group facilitates recognition of the bacteria's negative surface charge, enables QPyN14Ge insertion into the bacterial membrane, and triggers interdigitation within the bacterial membrane. This process reduces the membrane hydrocarbon core width, thereby directly affecting membrane permeability, stability, and function. The stress induced by QPyN14Ge, through the formation of interdigitated membranes, may inhibit the production of virulence factors (EPS, slime, staphyloxanthin) required for their survival and biofilm formation. However, further biophysical studies with model membranes are warranted to support the proposed involvement of QPyN14Ge interdigitated structure in the antimicrobial activity.

Conclusion

In summary, the three-dimensional structure of the QPyN14Ge amphiphile was obtained through X-ray crystallography. The molecule adopts interdigitated packing in the crystal lattice. QPyN14Ge disrupts MRSA membrane integrity and displays antibacterial activity. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation reveal that QPyN14Ge binds to FemX enzymes, indicating its potential as a cell wall biosynthesis inhibitor. QPyN14Ge inhibits the key virulence factor involved in MRSA biofilm formation. The antibacterial and antibiofilm activities can be linked to an interdigitated structure formed by the insertion of QPyN14Ge into the membranes. These structures could cause membrane thinning and disrupt the membrane function. Future research aimed at studying the interaction of QPyN14Ge with membranes will provide detailed insight into the role of an amphiphile interdigitated structure in antibacterial activity. The findings indicate that molecular architectures could aid in the development of next-generation antimicrobials.
