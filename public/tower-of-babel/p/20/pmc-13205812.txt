LsMAPK6 Phosphorylates the LsCO Protein to Enhance Its Stability and Transcriptional Activity, Promoting Floral Transition Upon High Temperatures in Lettuce
Authors: Tingzhen Wang, Mingjia Liu, Yufeng Tian, Qingqing Tan, Jiaxuan Wang, Huifang Mu, Chaojie Liu, Huiyu Wang, Ning Liu, Yingyan Han, Jinghong Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh temperatures significantly accelerate the timing of floral transition, namely, bolting and flowering, in lettuce, which results in severe loss of marketable yield. Thus, understanding the genetic regulation of floral transition is of great interest to plant biologists and lettuce breeders. Here, we show that mitogen‐activated protein kinase (LsMAPK6), whose expression and phosphorylation are stimulated by elevated temperatures, plays a positive role in the floral transition. The lsmapk6 mutants exhibit delayed bolting and flowering, whereas LsMAPK6 overexpression accelerates lettuce bolting. LsMAPK6 physically interacts with LsCO. Knockout of LsCO, to a large extent, phenocopies lsmapk6 mutants, strongly indicating that they function in the same genetic pathway. Mechanistically, LsMAPK6 phosphorylates LsCO at residue serine‐258, resulting in the enhanced transcriptional activity and protein stability of LsCO and consequently the activation of LsFT‐mediated flowering signalling pathways, which was confirmed by phospho‐inactive and mimic analyses. Collectively, these findings reveal that the LsMAPK6‐LsCO signalling module fine‐tunes the timing of floral transition upon high temperatures, demonstrating that LsMAPK6 is a potential target for breeding lettuce cultivars adapted to global warming.Keywords: CONSTANS, floral transition, high temperature, lettuce, MAPK6

Introduction

Lettuce ( Lactuca sativa L.), a globally popular vegetable, is cultivated primarily for its edible rosette leaves. According to the FAO statistical yearbook 2023, the planting area of lettuce, together with chicory, has reached 1261.4 k hectares, with an estimated annual yield of 28.08 million tons (FAO 2024). It is generally accepted that lettuce is native to the Caucasus Mountains and was domesticated in Mediterranean‐Caucasus regions (Cao et al. 2024; Wei et al. 2021). Accordingly, lettuce thrives in cooler climates; conversely, high temperatures, especially in summer climates in China, are unfavourable to lettuce production because the heat environment causes accelerated floral transitions, namely, premature bolting and flowering processes, in lettuce plants (Han et al. 2021; Ryder 1996). Consequently, premature bolting poses a major challenge for the lettuce industry, as it halts leaf biomass accumulation, compromises quality by inducing leaf bitterness, and leads to substantial economic losses (Hu et al. 2022; Pouteau and Albertini 2009; Rosental et al. 2021). Therefore, understanding the genetic and molecular mechanisms governing thermally induced floral transition is critical for breeding lettuce cultivars resistant to high temperatures.

Floral transition in flowering plants is regulated by a sophisticated network that integrates endogenous signals (e.g., flowering hormones and plant age) and environmental cues (e.g., photoperiod and temperature) (Bao et al. 2020; Conti 2017; Li et al. 2024). Genes responsible for floral transition have been extensively studied in model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana , where CONSTANS (CO), a B‐box zinc finger transcription factor, serves as a central integrator of photoperiodic signals (Romero et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023). CO promotes flowering under long‐day conditions by activating the expression of FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), which encodes the mobile florigen protein from light‐sensing leaves to shoot apices, where the floral transition is initiated (Pin and Nilsson 2012). As revealed by recent studies, CO activity is finely regulated by posttranslational modifications, particularly phosphorylation, affecting CO stability under specific conditions (Takagi et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2025). For example, the phosphorylation of CO by two glycogen synthase kinase‐3 (GSK3s), that is, SHAGGY‐like kinase 12 (SK12) and BRASSINOSTEROID‐INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2), promotes CO degradation via the E3 ubiquitin ligase CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1), thereby delaying flowering under noninductive conditions (Chen et al. 2020; Ju et al. 2023). Conversely, blue light stabilises CO through the cryptochrome‐mediated inhibition of COP1, ensuring the precise timing of floral transition under favourable photoperiods (Zou et al. 2021). However, whether the subcellular localization and DNA‐binding capacity of CO proteins can be affected by phosphorylation has not yet been reported.

The timing of floral transition in lettuce is particularly critical because of its considerable impact on marketable yield. As a result, the identification of genes associated with the transition to reproductive growth is of great interest to horticultural researchers and lettuce breeders. The gibberellin (GA) and auxin signalling pathways are tightly linked to lettuce bolting (Liu et al. 2018; Wang, Li, Li, et al. 2022). Emerging evidence indicates that LsRGL1, which encodes a DELLA protein in lettuce, delays plant bolting by suppressing LsGA3ox and LsYUC4 expression (Wang, Luo, Sun, et al. 2022). Our recent study revealed that LsARF3, an auxin response factor, mediates thermally induced bolting by directly activating LsCO expression, suggesting that auxin pathways are essential for lettuce bolting (Li et al. 2022). The expression of LsFT is also elevated by heat stress, and the transient silencing of LsFT by RNA interference results in late bolting in lettuce (Chen et al. 2017; Fukuda et al. 2011). In addition to reverse genetic approaches, recent landmark studies identified two key regulators of bolting time in lettuce through a map‐based cloning method. In late‐bolting lettuce, LsKN1, a KNOTTED1‐like homeobox protein, inhibits LsGA20ox1 while increasing LsRGA1 expression, which leads to impaired GA biosynthesis and signalling and consequently late bolting (Chen, Qi, et al. 2025). LsKN1 also interacts with LsOFP6, which acts as a safeguard to prevent excessive GA suppression during floral transition (Chen, Qi, et al. 2025). A natural allele of LsBLH2, which encodes a BEL1‐like homeodomain protein, also confers late bolting in lettuce. Subsequent studies suggested that LsBLH2‐LsOFP6‐LsKNAT3 forms a regulatory module that fine‐tunes GA levels across developmental stages (Qi et al. 2025). These findings underscore the importance of GA homeostasis as a central regulatory mechanism of lettuce bolting; nevertheless, the genetic basis of bolting time and the upstream regulators of phytohormone pathways in lettuce have not been elucidated.

Mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades are evolutionarily conserved signalling modules that transduce both environmental and developmental signals into cellular responses (Bigeard and Hirt 2018; Sun and Zhang 2022). In plants, MAPK6 is one of the central components of abiotic stress responses, including those to drought, salinity and extreme temperatures; it phosphorylates transcription factors, ion channels and enzymes to increase plant adaptation to various environmental stresses (Zhang and Zhang 2022). Similarly, MAPK signalling is closely associated with the regulation of plant development. For example, the MAPK6‐mediated phosphorylation of MYC2, a bHLH transcription factor, negatively regulates root elongation in Arabidopsis (Takahashi et al. 2007). Additionally, proper stomatal patterning relies on MAPK3/MAPK6 cascades, which result in the phosphorylation and thereby inactivation of transcription factors such as Arabidopsis speechless (AtSPCH) (Yang et al. 2015). In lettuce, transcriptomic analyses of thermally treated stem apices revealed that high temperatures activate MAPK signalling pathways, suggesting their potential role in stress responses and floral transition under heat conditions (Wang, Liu, Wu, et al. 2022). Although the GSK3 kinases SK12 and BIN2 have been identified as regulators of CO stability in Arabidopsis (Chen et al. 2020; Ju et al. 2023), we cannot exclude the possibility that other kinases, such as MAPK6, may play specialised roles in the bolting and flowering processes of lettuce or other plant species.

In this study, the expression of LsMAPK6, as well as its phosphorylated form, increased in response to high temperatures. lsmapk6 mutants exhibited delayed bolting and flowering, whereas LsMAPK6 overexpression accelerated plant floral transition, confirming that LsMAPK6 is a positive regulator of lettuce bolting. Yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H), Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP), the luciferase complementation assay (LCA) and Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays demonstrated that LsMAPK6 directly interacted with LsCO. The knockout of LsCO phenocopied lsmapk6 mutant lines, thereby indicating that they might be in the same genetic pathways. LsMAPK6 phosphorylated LsCO at the residue of Ser‐258, which likely increases its transcriptional activity. A dual‐luciferase assay suggested that phosphorylated LsCO activated LsFT expression by binding to its promoter. A phosphomimetic LsCO (S258D) could promote LsFT activation, whereas a phosphorylation‐deficient mutant (S258A) decreased the activation. Together, we propose that the LsMAPK6‐LsCO‐LsFT signalling module might mediate thermally induced flowering and bolting in lettuce.

Results

LsMAPK6 , With Preferential Expression in the Stem and Other Reproductive Organs, Is Strongly Induced by High Temperatures

In a previous study, we reported that LsMAPK6, the ortholog of Arabidopsis MAPK6 (Figure S1a), was expressed in all vegetative organs, particularly abundantly expressed in stems and stem apex (the top 0–0.5 cm top segments), implying its potential roles in stem elongation and differentiation (Wang, Liu, Tian, et al. 2022). To further confirm the expression patterns of LsMAPK6 in all functional organs, qRT‐PCR analyses were conducted with different tissues and organs. The results showed that LsMAPK6 was abundantly expressed in the seed, mature flower and stem, whereas its expression levels were much lower in the lateral root and premature achene (Figure S1b). Thus, the role of LsMAPK6 might be correlated with stem growth as well as reproductive development. To further investigate the LsMAPK6 expression patterns at the stem apex, we explored the expression changes of LsMAPK6 at different times post‐heat treatments by the qRT‐PCR method. The results showed that heat stress could significantly enhance the expression of LsMAPK6, and the expression levels reached the peak at 8 days post‐heat (DPH) treatment when GB‐30 lettuce started bolting (Figure 1a,b). We also investigated the translation and phosphorylation patterns of LsMAPK6 with customised anti‐LsMAPK6 antibodies and anti‐phosphorylated‐LsMAPK6 antibodies. Consistent with the findings from qRT‐PCR analyses, LsMAPK6 protein was accumulated with the duration of heat treatments, and interestingly, the phosphorylated forms of LsMAPK6 were also increased accordingly (Figure 1c,d), suggesting that high temperature stimulated the accumulation of LsMAPK6 and promoted its phosphorylation. Together, these results suggested that LsMAPK6 expression is strongly induced at transcriptional and translational levels in the stem apex of heat‐treated lettuce plants, and phosphorylated LsMAPK6 might be associated with lettuce floral transition.

Knockout of LsMAPK6 Results in the Delayed Floral Transition Under High Temperatures

To explore the role of LsMAPK6, we generated lsmapk6 mutant lines via the CRISPR/Cas9 genome‐editing approach. The Cas9/sgRNA construct with synthetic sgRNAs targeted to the first exon of LsMAPK6. And after confirmation by PCR sequencing (Figure S2a–c), multiple chimeric T0 plants that contained various out‐of‐frame insertion and deletion alleles were generated in lettuce. These T0 plants were allowed to set self‐pollinated seeds (T1 seeds), and then the T1 plants that exhibited an approximately 3:1 segregation ratio of the Cas9 transgene were genotyped by sequencing the PCR products of target regions. Three homozygous T2 plants, lsmapk6‐L9, lsmapk6‐L10 and lsmapk6‐L94, whose genomes were introduced with a premature stop codon to the LsMAPK6 locus, were obtained for phenotypic analyses. Furthermore, western blot analyses suggested that expression and phosphorylation levels of LsMAPK6 proteins were significantly decreased in lsmapk6‐L9 mutants (Figure 2a, Figure S3a,b).

We first observed the bolting and flowering phenotypes of the six‐week‐old seedlings of the lsmapk6 and wild type (WT) plants that were subjected to thermal treatments. The results showed that compared with the WT, three lsmapk6 lines, lsmapk6‐L9, lsmapk6‐L10 and lsmapk6‐L94, delayed bolting by 12, 10 and 16 days, budding time by 13, 15 and 27 days, and flowering time by 5, 9 and 25 days, respectively (Figure 2b–d). Likewise, the stem length of the lsmapk6 mutant lines was significantly shorter than that of the WT plants, and the length difference between WT and lsmapk6 kept increasing with thermal treatments (Figure 2e). Microscopy analyses suggested that, at 8 DPH, the stem apex of the lsmapk6 mutants exhibited a prominent shape, the inflorescence meristem (IM) was wrapped by the leaf primordium (LP), and no floral primordium (FP) could be observed; conversely, the stem apex of WT plants terminated in a flat, round shape, suggesting that the early differentiation of IM occurred at the stem apex. These observations indicated that the lsmapk6 mutants were still in the vegetative growth stage, whereas WT plants initiated floral transition after 8‐day thermal treatments (Figure 2f). At 24 DPH, we observed that the inflorescence apex of the WT plants protruded upward, and small protrusions were found around the IM, indicating the beginning of differentiation of the bract primordia and the early stage of bract scale differentiation (Figure 2g). Simultaneously, the stem apex of the lsmapk6 mutants became flat and round, which indicates the initiation of lsmapk6 IM at 24 DPH. Taken together, the absence of the LsMAPK6 significantly delayed the bolting of lettuce under high temperatures.

Overexpression of LsMAPK6 Promotes Lettuce Bolting and Flowering

To get insight into the function of LsMAPK6 in lettuce, three homozygous overexpression (OE) lines of LsMAPK6, OE‐L21, OE‐L24 and OE‐L32, were obtained (Figure 3a). qRT‐PCR analysis indicated that expression levels of LsMAPK6 were significantly higher in these LsMAPK6‐OE lines. Furthermore, we found that the protein levels of LsMAPK6, unphosphorylated or phosphorylated, were all increased significantly in OE lines (Figure 3b and Figure S3a,b). At room temperature (RT), the OE‐L21, OE‐L24 and OE‐L32 plants initiated bolting at 50, 52 and 40 days after sowing (DAS), and started budding at 137, 142 and 120 DAS, respectively. As compared to the WT plants, the average bolting, budding and flowering time of these OE lines were 79.6, 41.6 and 75.5 days earlier than WT plants, suggesting that LsMAPK6 overexpression significantly promoted the floral transition in lettuce (Figure 3c–e). We examined the dynamic changes of stem length at different time points and found that the stem length of the LsMAPK6‐OE lines was significantly greater than that of the WT plants at 60 DAS, and the length differences kept increasing with bolting processes (Figure 3f). Microscopy examination of the stem apex at 52 DAS revealed that the stem apex of the WT plants presented a protruding hemispherical shape, suggesting that these plants had not yet initiated the floral transition. Conversely, the stem apex of all three OE lines presented a flattened shape, and the LP wrapped the IM, which indicated the initiation of floral transition at 52 DAS (Figure 3g). Therefore, these findings indicate that the overexpression of LsMAPK6 could promote floral transition, implying that LsMAPK6 might be a positive regulator in lettuce bolting.

Next, we examined the expression patterns of flowering genes, such as LsCO, LsFT, LsSOC1 and LsLFY, in the LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 lines. The results suggested that, as compared to the WT plants, the transcript levels of these flowering genes was increased in LsMAPK6‐OE lines but were decreased in lsmapk6 lines (Figure S3c–f). Thus, LsMAPK6 might promote lettuce bolting by directly or indirectly regulating the expression of these flowering genes.

To evaluate the effects of LsMAPK6 on the agronomic traits, we also conducted field experiments with LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 lines. Interestingly, the results showed that, in terms of flesh weight per plant, crop yield, 1000‐seed weight and seed setting rate, no significant differences could be observed between LsMAPK6‐OE, lsmapk6CR and WT plants (Figure S4a–d). So far, our results strongly indicate that the knockout of LsMAPK6 did not bring a growth penalty to lettuce growth, which implies that these lsmapk6 mutants generated in these experiments might be potential germplasms for the development of bolting‐resistant lettuce.

LsMAPK6 Protein Interacts With LsCO

To investigate how LsMAPK6 promotes bolting and flowering, we used LsMAPK6 as bait to screen against the lettuce cDNA yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) library constructed with stem apex tissues. Among the 36 candidate interacting proteins identified from the screening (Table S1), a lettuce B‐box transcription factor (Lsat_1_v5_gn_2_86121) was of particular interest because it shares high sequence similarity to Arabidopsis CONSTANS, a well‐known regulator during the floral transition process. Using its protein sequence, we blasted against the lettuce genome (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/) and identified 12 CO‐like genes. Phylogenetic analysis of protein sequences of these CO and CO‐like proteins revealed that, in addition to Lsat_1_v5_gn_2_86121, there is another lettuce CO‐like (Lsat_1_v5_gn_4_175001) in the same clade; however, sequence analysis suggested that the B‐Box domain on its N‐terminal is disrupted by large deletions, implying its inability to interact with DNA or proteins. Like Arabidopsis CO, Lsat_1_v5_gn_2_86121 has two conserved B‐boxes at the N‐terminal and one CCT domain at the C‐terminal, and they share 46.1% identity (Figures S5 and S6). Thus, we hypothesized that Lsat_1_v5_gn_2_86121 might be the ortholog of AtCO and is referred to as LsCO ( Lactuca sativa CONSTANS).

We cloned the full‐length of LsCO and conducted a one‐to‐one Y2H analysis to validate the interaction between LsMAPK6 and LsCO. Indeed, the Y2H experiments suggested that co‐transformants of pGBKT7‐LsMAPK6 and pGADT7‐LsCO grew on SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp (QDO) plates (Figure 4a), confirming that LsMAPK6 specifically interacted with LsCO. As well, the subcellular localization results demonstrated that LsMAPK6‐GFP fusion proteins were localised in the nucleus of tobacco cells. As expected, when tobacco leaves were co‐transformed with LsMAPK6‐GFP and LsCO‐RFP (fused with a red fluorescent protein tag) constructs, both GFP and RFP fluorescence were observed in the nucleus (Figure 4b). Meanwhile, an in planta interaction analysis was tested by Co‐IP assays, and the results demonstrated that LsMAPK6 could interact with LsCO in plant cells (Figure 4c). Furthermore, the LCA results showed that the combination of LsMAPK6‐nLUC with LsCO‐cLUC restored the catalytic activity of luciferase in the co‐infected N. benthamiana leaves, which was similar to the positive control, AtbZIP63‐nLUC with AtbZIP63‐cLUC (Figure 4d). To further validate the interaction between LsMAPK6 and LsCO proteins, a construct containing LsMAPK6 and the N‐terminus of yellow fluorescent protein (nYFP) was made, as well as the constructs with the LsCO and C‐terminus of YFP (cYFP), to conduct BiFC assays. The results suggested that only co‐transformation with LsMAPK6‐nYFP and LsCO‐cYFP could produce fluorescence in tobacco cells (Figure 4e). Together, these findings strongly indicate that LsMAPK6 interacts with LsCO.

LsMAPK6 Phosphorylates LsCO at Serine‐258 to Enhance Its Stability

To investigate whether LsCO is a substrate of LsMAPK6, we first analysed the phosphorylation levels of LsCO in the stem apexes of LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 lines. Phos‐tag mobility shift assay revealed that the shifted bands could be detected in all transgenic and control samples, implying that LsCO could be phosphorylated (Figure 5a). Compared to the WT, the relative content of phosphorylated LsCO was decreased by 4.3 times in lsmapk6, while increased by 1.8 times in LsMAPK6‐OE lines, indicating that LsMAPK6 might be the major protein kinase responsible for LsCO phosphorylation in lettuce. Interestingly, the shifted bands could not be completely abolished in lsmapk6 lines, which indicated that other protein kinases (Figure 5b), in addition to LsMAPK6, might also contribute to LsCO phosphorylation. Thus, we conclude that LsCO phosphorylation, to a large extent, depends on LsMAPK6 kinase.

Furthermore, an in vitro kinase assay was conducted to test whether LsCO was an LsMAPK6 substrate. Constitutively active LsMAPK6 (CA‐LsMAPK6), which results from two mutations (G213D; A217E) in the conserved domains, was used for detecting in vitro phosphorylation of LsCO. Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining was used to visualise protein extracts, and autoradiography was used to identify phosphorylated proteins. The results showed that LsCO was indeed phosphorylated by CA‐LsMAPK6 in vitro (Figure 5c). Further, a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) of LsCO phosphopeptides was conducted to identify the phosphorylation site(s) of LsCO by LsMAPK6. We observed that the Ser‐258 residue in LsCO is directly phosphorylated by LsMAPK6 (Figure 5d). Interestingly, sequence alignment with CO and CO‐like proteins suggested that Ser‐258 is relatively conserved in Arabidopsis and lettuce (Figure S6), which implies that the same modification might occur in other CO proteins. Therefore, these results indicated that LsMAPK6 can phosphorylate LsCO both in vitro and in vivo, and the phosphorylation site of LsCO is the residue Serine‐258.

Previous studies have reported that, in Arabidopsis, the phosphorylated CO is the substrate of the 26S proteasome via COP1 (Chen et al. 2020; Zou et al. 2021). Thus, we further investigated how the abundance of LsCO was affected by Ser‐258 phosphorylation in lettuce. First, we compared the protein stability of LsCO in LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 transgenic lettuce plants, and WT lettuce plants were used as controls. Our results show that the protein degradation rate of LsCO was comparable in LsMAPK6‐OE and WT lettuce extracts, whereas LsCO in the lsmapk6 extracts became more sensitive to degradation (Figure 5e). Next, to evaluate the in planta effects of Serine‐258 phosphorylation on LsCO stability, we analysed the abundance of MBP‐LsCO, MBP‐LsCOS258A and MBP‐LsCOS258D in WT and lsmapk6 mutant backgrounds. The results showed that, in the WT background, the phosphomimetic (LsCOS258D) proteins accumulated to relatively higher levels than those of LsCO wild type or phosphor‐interactive (LsCOS258A) forms (Figure 5f), suggesting that Ser‐258 phosphorylation could improve the LsCO stability. In contrast, the degradation of LsCO proteins was much slower in all these extracts treated with MG132, a 26S proteasome inhibitor. As expected, the similar degradation tendency of dephosphorylated and phosphorylated LsCOs was observed in lsmapk6 background (Figure 5f). Taken together, our findings suggest that the phosphorylation of LsCO on Ser‐258 by LsMAPK6 could enhance its protein abundance and stability, thereby facilitating the CO‐mediated floral transition in lettuce.

LsCO Is a Positive Regulator of Floral Transition in Lettuce

The expression patterns of the LsCO in lettuce GB30 were examined by qRT‐PCR to evaluate its transcript accumulation in different tissues or under heat stress. The results revealed that, like AtCO, the transcript of LsCO was highly accumulated in the floral organs, such as anthesis flowers and flower buds, and its transcripts could also be detected in the root and seed. Interestingly, high expression of LsCO could be detected in the stem, with the highest expression levels in the stem apex, suggesting a potential role in stem development (Figure S7a). When lettuce plants were grown under high temperatures, the LsCO transcription increased significantly at the stem apex and reached its peak at 16 DPH (Figure S7B), suggesting that high temperatures could activate the expression of LsCO in lettuce. Consistent with the mRNA changes, immunoblot analysis demonstrated that the accumulation of LsCO proteins increased upon the heat treatments, indicating that heat treatments could promote LsCO accumulation in lettuce (Figure 6a). Next, we examined LsCO protein translation patterns in the selected LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 lines under high temperatures, and the results revealed that LsCO accumulation exhibited the same patterns as LsMAPK6 in the transgenic lines, implying that LsCO might be one of the substrates of LsMAPK6 (Figure 6b). Collectively, these findings suggest that elevated temperatures could stimulate the LsCO expression at transcriptional and translational levels, and that LsCO might be one of the substrates of LsMAPK6 under heat treatments.

To explore the role of LsCO during floral transition, we generated two lsco knockout mutants (with 1‐bp and 4‐bp deletions at the first exon) via the CRISPR/Cas9 genome‐editing approach (Figure 6c; Figure S7c,d). Under HT, the bolting time of lsco mutants were delayed by 7.5 days compared to WT plants, which usually began bolting at 8 DPH (Figure 6d). Similarly, the emergence of budding and flowering was delayed by 22 and 19 days, respectively, as compared with WT plants under high temperatures (Figure 6e,f). Paraffin sections revealed that floral differentiation in WT plants initiated at 8 DPH, whereas mutants still maintained vegetative growth (Figure 6g,h). The morphology of lsco lines strongly suggested that the knockout of LsCO inhibited the floral transition.

We also developed three LsCO‐overexpressing lines (OE‐L5, OE‐L11 and OE‐L23) (Figure S8a). qRT‐PCR confirmed significantly greater LsCO expression in these OE lines compared to the WT plants (Figure S8b). Under normal growth conditions, the average bolting time of these OE lines was 63 days, 29 days earlier than WT plants (Figure S8c); the average time of budding and flower emergence was 17 and 14 days earlier than WT plants, respectively (Figure S8d,e). Paraffin sections revealed that the stem elongation of these OE lines began at around 61 DAS, while the WT plants remained in the vegetative growth phase at this time (Figure S8f,g). Together, these results demonstrate that knockout of LsCO delays floral transition, leading to late bolting and flowering, whereas overexpression of LsCO accelerates these processes. Thus, LsCO acts as a positive regulator of floral transition in lettuce, similar to its ortholog in Arabidopsis.

LsMAPK6 Functions Synergistically With LsCO to Modulate the Bolting of Lettuce

To explore the genetic interaction between LsMAPK6 and LsCO in regulating bolting, LsCO was transiently overexpressed or silenced in the six‐week‐old LsMAPK6‐OE plants (Figure 7a,b). qRT‐PCR analysis suggested that the LsCO expression levels were increased or decreased in the corresponding transgenic plants. As compared to the WT plants, the expression levels of LsCO were increased by 1.2‐fold and 1.5‐fold in LsMAPK6‐OE and LsCO transient overexpression in the LsMAPK6‐OE background (LsMAPK6‐OE/LsCO OE) plants, respectively; on the contrary, the LsCO expression level was decreased by 19.7% in LsCO transient silencing in the LsMAPK6‐OE background (LsMAPK6‐OE/LsCO SL) plants (Figure 7c).

The alternation of LsCO expression levels brought severe effects on the stem elongation in the LsMAPK6‐OE plants grown (Figure 7a,b). At 24 days post‐agroinfiltration (DPI), down‐ and up‐regulation of LsCO in LsMAPK6‐OE lines resulted in shorter and longer stems as compared to the WT stems. At 81 DPI, the average stem length of LsMAPK6‐OE/LsCO OE plants was 26.5 cm and 11.4 cm longer than that of WT and LsMAPK6‐OE plants, respectively. Conversely, the average stem length of LsCO silencing lines was 2.15 cm and 4.72 cm shorter than WT and LsMAPK6‐OE plants (Figure 7b,d). Additionally, we found that LsFT expression levels were significantly higher (2.9‐fold) in LsCO‐OE lines but reduced by 48% in lsco mutants, as compared to the WT plants, suggesting that the LsCO‐LsFT signalling cascade might be evolutionarily conserved in lettuce (Figure 7e). Thus, these results indicate that LsMAPK6 could promote stem elongation by regulating the expression and protein stability of LsCO in lettuce, and LsFT may act as a downstream target of LsCO and function synergistically with LsMAPK6 and LsCO to regulate bolting in lettuce.

Transactivation of Phosphorylated LsCO by LsMAPK6 Might Promote Floral Transition Through the LsCO‐LsFT Signalling Cascade

As LsCO and LsFT expression levels were correlated with LsMAPK6 expression in transgenic lines (Figure S3c,d), we further investigated their expression patterns in LsMAPK6‐OE plants where LsCO expression was transiently up‐ or down‐regulated. As predicted, the expression levels of LsCO and LsFT were significantly increased by 3.4‐fold and 2.1‐fold, respectively, in LsMAPK6‐OE/LsCO OE plants compared to the LsMAPK6‐OE plants; on the contrary, reduced expression levels of LsCO and LsFT were observed in the LsMAPK6‐OE/LsCO SL plants (Figure 7f). Consistent with the co‐expression analyses, yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays demonstrated that LsCO could bind to the LsFT promoter (Figure 8a), indicating that LsFT might be the direct target of the transcription factor LsCO. To further identify the binding sites of LsCO in the LsFT promoter region, we aligned five CORE or CORE‐like cis‐acting elements within the LsFT promoter sequence and examined the interaction between LsCO and these elements using the pLacZi Y1H system. The results demonstrated that the CORE2‐2 ‘TGTGG’ cis‐acting element starting at −1212 bp could interact with LsCO (Figure 8b,c). Considering that LsFT promotes lettuce bolting under heat stress (Chen et al. 2017), our results support the notion that the LsCO‐LsFT signalling node might be evolutionarily conserved in lettuce and that the activity of this signalling node might be influenced by LsMAPK6‐mediated LsCO phosphorylation.

To explore the regulatory mechanism of LsMAPK6‐LsCO‐LsFT signalling node, we next analysed the binding ability of LsCO to the LsFT promoter regions via the dual‐luciferase reporter assays. The results showed that strong luciferase fluorescence signals were detected in tobacco leaves co‐transformed with LsFTpro::LUC and p62SK‐LsCO, and LsCO increased the activity of the LsFT promoter by 4.6‐fold (Figure 8d–f), demonstrating the ability of LsCO to promote LsFT transcription.

To further evaluate the effects of LsCO phosphorylation on its transcriptional activity, we also constructed effector vectors containing either LsCOS258D, a constitutive phosphomimic form, or LsCOS258A, a non‐phosphorylated form. In the dual‐luciferase experiments, no luciferase fluorescence signals were detected in the tobacco leaves co‐transformed with single effectors, such as p62SK‐LsMAPK6 or p62SK‐LsCO S258A , indicating that neither could activate the expression of LsFTpro::LUC reporter. Co‐transformation of p62SK‐LsCO S258A and p62SK‐LsMAPK6 vectors did not result in the transactivation of LsFTpro::LUC reporter, whereas individual LsCOS258D effector significantly activated expression of LsFTpro::LUC reporter, similar to the two combinations, LsCOS258D + LsMAPK6 and LsCO + LsMAPK6 (Figure 8d–f). These observations strongly suggest that LsMAPK6 phosphorylates LsCO at Ser‐258, which is critical for the transactivation of LsFT. As the flowering inductive function of the florigen FT has been well documented in higher plants, we speculate that the elevated temperatures enhance the expression of LsMAPK6 and LsCO in lettuce stems, and at the same time, LsMAPK6 directly interacts with and phosphorylates LsCO at Ser‐258, which enhances the transcriptional activation capability of LsCO and ultimately drives lettuce bolting and flowering upon heat stress.

Discussion

LsMAPK6 Integrates Environmental Cues and Developmental Signals, Which Highlights Its Potential Application in the Breeding Program

Heat extremes, more frequently and intensely occurring in the summertime, become the major threat to the sustainable production of the lettuce industry. To survive the changing environment, plants evolve diverse MAPK‐substrate modules as a molecular switch that transduces temperature signals to plant responses at cellular, molecular and physiological levels (Jagodzik et al. 2018). In this study, we show that the expression of LsMAPK6, both in shoot apices and rosette leaves, is activated by elevated temperatures. LsMAPK6 overexpressing lines displayed much higher tolerance to heat stress compared to wild‐type lettuce, indicating that LsMAPK6‐mediated signalling cascades play significant roles in plant adaptation to heat stress. In Arabidopsis, AtMAPK6‐mediated phosphorylation of AtMYB15, a transcriptional repressor, reduces its binding affinity to the promoters of target genes such as AtCBF, thereby enhancing plant tolerance to chilling injuries (Kim et al. 2017). Thus, our observations are in good agreement with previous studies that MAPK6 is the key regulator in plant adaptation to abiotic stresses.

Simultaneously, we noted that overexpression of LsMAPK6 significantly accelerated the timing of floral transition even under normal growth conditions, indicating its potential roles in the regulation of lettuce bolting and flowering. Loss‐of‐function mutants of LsMAPK6 exhibited hindered transition, which further supports that LsMAPK6 is implicated in this crucial event of reproductive development. Indeed, numerous interconnections between MAPK signalling and plant development have been reported, supporting that MAPK6, as well as MAPK3, serve as upstream regulators of morphogenesis such as hypocotyl elongation, cotyledon opening, root growth, etc. For example, the key bHLH transcription factor AtPIF3 is one of the substrates of AtMAPK6, and upon red light exposure, the phosphorylated AtPIF3 proteins are more unstable, which has a negative effect on cotyledon open angle (Xin et al. 2018). Another example is that AtMKK3‐AtMAPK6 and AtMYC2 form a feedback regulatory loop that mediates the Arabidopsis seedling development under blue lights (Takahashi et al. 2007). The chromatin modifier GRAIN WEIGHT 6a (GW6a), which controls grain size and yield in rice, was reported to be downstream of MAPK, and OsMAPK6 stabilises GW6a by phosphorylation during grain development (Bai et al. 2024). Despite these discoveries, MAPK6 is reported to be associated with other floral development processes, including ovule development, pollen development and floral abscission (Guan et al. 2014; Su et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2017). However, there is no experimental evidence that MAPK6 is involved in the floral transition process in plants. In this study, the LsCO, LsSVP, LsLFY and other flowering genes exhibited correlated expression patterns in LsMAPK6 overexpression and knockout lines, and morphological observation confirmed that the floral transition was influenced by the perturbation of LsMAPK6 transcription in these lines. Therefore, we speculate that LsMAPK6 might be involved in the floral transition under heat stress.

Additionally, the morphology looks indistinguishable between lsmapk6 and wild‐type (GB30) lettuce plants at their vegetative growth stage, though we did find that lsmapk6 lines display late bolting. As GB30 is an elite lettuce cultivar that exhibits moderate resistance to disease and high tolerance to chilling injury, the lsmapk6 lines generated in this study are expected to be developed into valuable lettuce germplasms. Taken together, our findings reveal that LsMAPK6 might integrate the heat stress and flowering signals in plants and that LsMAPK6 is a potential target for the generation of late‐bolting lettuce.

LsMAPK6 Interacts With and Phosphorylates LsCO at High Temperatures

Floral transition is regulated by complex regulatory networks that monitor environmental changes and modify reproductive growth accordingly (Conti 2017). Among these components in the flowering network, the transcription factor CO is one of the key regulators (Yu et al. 2025). Interestingly, we identified that LsMAPK6 in vivo interacts with LsCO by Y2H, LCA and BiFC experiments; in planta LsMAPK6‐LsCO interaction was further confirmed by co‐immunoprecipitation assays. Consistent with the protein–protein interaction analyses, LsMAPK6 and LsCO co‐localised in the nucleus, strongly implying they might form a protein complex that functions in regulating the transcription of target genes. qRT‐PCR analyses suggest that LsMAPK6 and LsCO share similar expression patterns, where they were stimulated by high temperatures, especially at the stem apex. Therefore, these results suggest that LsCO might be phosphorylated by LsMAPK6 when lettuce is challenged by heat stress.

In Arabidopsis, AtCO directly triggers the expression of AtFT, which directly activates a number of flowering genes to initiate floral transition (Pin and Nilsson 2012). Consequently, it is not surprising that phosphorylation of CO proteins plays a pronounced role in regulating the initiation of plant bolting and flowering. For example, the phosphorylated AtCO is much more susceptible to degradation by COP1, which delays Arabidopsis flowering under LD conditions (Jang et al. 2008; Zuo et al. 2011). Accordingly, we speculate that LsMAPK6‐mediated phosphorylation of LsCO might also alter its activity and/or stability. Using Anti‐phosphoSer/Thr antibodies, we detected that the phosphorylation levels of LsCO were increased in LsMAPK6‐OE lines, but dramatically reduced in lsmapk6 lines. PhosTag‐based analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of LsCO, the phosphorylated and natural forms, were decreased in the absence of LsMAPK6, whereas both were increased with the LsMAPK6 overexpression. An LC–MS/MS analysis further demonstrates that LsMAPK6 phosphorylates LsCO at amino acid residue Serine‐258. Hence, these findings have led to the notion that high temperatures might promote the LsMAPK6‐mediated phosphorylation of LsCO, consequently regulating the bolting and flowering in lettuce plants.

Phosphorylation of LsCO at Ser‐258 Increased Its Stability and Transcriptional Activity, Thereby Promoting Lettuce Bolting and Flowering

Previous studies have demonstrated that CO stability is modulated by different interacting partners, such as E3 ubiquitin ligases COP1 and HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENE 1 (HOS1), whilst GSK3 kinase could affect CO stability by protein phosphorylation in Arabidopsis (Chen et al. 2020; Laubinger et al. 2006; Lazaro et al. 2012; Lian et al. 2011). Arabidopsis SHAGGY‐like kinase SK12 interacts with and phosphorylates AtCO at Thr‐119, and the phosphorylation contributes to the degradation of AtCO, thereby postponing the flowering time (Chen et al. 2020). A recent study found that AtBIN2 negatively regulates Arabidopsis flowering through phosphorylating AtCO at Thr‐280, which reduces the stability of AtCO proteins (Ju et al. 2023). Despite the lack of direct biochemical evidence, Sarid‐Krebs proposes that the phosphorylation site might be located on the C‐terminal of AtCO, probably at the residue Ser‐303 (Sarid‐Krebs et al. 2015). These investigations support that phosphorylated AtCO is the preferred substrate for degradation, although the identified phosphorylation sites are different. However, there remains in debate whether phosphorylation could enhance the stability of the CO protein. In this study, in vivo and in vitro analyses confirmed that LsMAPK6 could phosphorylate LsCO at the residue Ser‐258. It is noteworthy that, compared with previously identified phosphorylation sites, Ser‐258 is a relatively conserved position among CO and CO‐like proteins in lettuce and Arabidopsis. We found that the stability of LsCO proteins was increased in LsMAPK6 OE lines as compared to the wild‐type plants; conversely, their stability was reduced in LsMAPK CR lines. Furthermore, the in planta analyses with the phosphomimetic (LsCOS258D) and phosphor‐interactive (LsCOS258A) indicate that phosphorylation at Ser‐258 functions as a molecular switch for the LsCO stability, enhancing it in the S258D variant and attenuating it in the S258A variant. Considering that transcript and protein accumulation of LsMAPK6 was stimulated by heat treatments, our results support the notion that Ser‐258 phosphorylation of LsCO could increase its stability under high temperatures. Importantly, the Ser‐258 site is present in the C‐terminal region of the LsCO protein, near the CCT domain that is responsible for CO degradation (Valverde 2011). Hence, we speculate that the modification at Ser‐258 might not be limited to lettuce. It is worthwhile investigating whether the same modification of the CO protein could occur in other plant species, such as Arabidopsis.

In addition, we also found that the transcriptional activity of LsCO was improved in phosphorylated LsCO under high temperatures. When LsCO was mutated to LsCOS258D, the transcription activity became more robust, implying that LsMAPK6‐mediated phosphorylation of LsCO could effectively promote the LsFT expression under heat conditions. Thus, these results support that Ser‐258 phosphorylation is also essential for the transcriptional ability of LsCO, whose expression is improved by elevated temperatures. Here, the LsMAPK6‐LsCO‐LsFT module provides a plausible hypothesis for thermally induced bolting in lettuce (Figure 9). In this scenario, high temperatures trigger the MAPK6 signalling cascades when lettuce plants undergo heat weather. As a result, the LsMAPK6 stabilises the LsCO via phosphorylation at Ser‐258, which leads to the accumulation of LsCO proteins; simultaneously, the modification also enhances the transcriptional activity of LsCO. The increase of phosphorylated LsCO facilitates the upregulation of flowering genes, including LsFT, LsSVP, LsLFY, etc., and the acceleration of floral transition in lettuce. Taken together, our results reveal a fine‐tuning transcriptional cascade where LsMAPK6 phosphorylates LsCO at Ser‐258 in the heat‐stressed lettuce plants.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

The materials used for phenotypic analysis in this study were the easy‐bolting lettuce ( Lactuca sativa L.) variety GB‐30. All transgenic plants generated in this work were also produced in the GB‐30 background. Lettuce seeds were germinated on filter paper for 48 h and then transferred into 10‐cm‐diameter pots filled with nutrient soil. Plants were grown in a climate‐controlled greenhouse under a 14/10‐h light/dark cycle with a light intensity of 12 000 lx. For the heat stress treatment, plants were subjected to temperatures of 33°C ± 2°C (day) and 25°C ± 2°C (night). Control plants were maintained at 20°C ± 2°C (day) and 13°C ± 2°C (night).

To generate overexpression (OE) lines, the full coding sequences of LsMAPK6 and LsCO were cloned into the PRI101‐GFP vector and transformed into GB‐30 lettuce via Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation (Li et al. 2022). For the CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knockout (CR) lines of LsMAPK6 and LsCO, two optimal sgRNAs targeting the first exon of each gene were designed using the CRISPR‐P 2.0 web tool (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/), with off‐target effects excluded by BLAST analysis (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer‐blast/). The synthesised sgRNAs were ligated into the pKSE401 vector, and CR lines were obtained through Agrobacterium‐mediated genetic transformation. Mutant lines (lsmapk6 and lsco) were identified by PCR and sequencing (Chen, Chen, et al. 2025). The sgRNAs and the sequences required for obtaining transgenic plants are reported in Table S2.

For virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) vector construction, a 400‐bp fragment within the coding sequence of LsCO was amplified and inserted into Agrobacterium GV3101. Six‐week‐old LsMAPK6‐OE plants were infiltrated with the VIGS‐mediated silencing and overexpression vectors for phenotypic complementation assays (Wang, Liu, Tian, et al. 2022). The primers used are reported in Table S2.

RNA Isolation and qRT‐PCR Analysis

Tissue‐specific expression was performed on lettuce plants at the reproductive stage, characterised by firm apical inflorescences, open middle flowers and developing basal buds. Samples included taproots, lateral roots, stems, young leaves, old leaves, flower buds, mature flowers, premature achenes and seeds. To examine gene expression during bolting under heat stress, stem apices (top 0–0.5 cm segments) were harvested at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 24 h post‐heat treatments (HPH), as well as at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 24 days post‐heat treatment (DPH). The second new leaves of six‐week‐old lettuce were sampled to assess gene expression across different transgenic lines. For VIGS‐treated plants, leaves were sampled at 7 days post‐agroinfiltration (DPI) for subsequent transcriptional level profiling. Three biological replicates were established, each consisting of tissues pooled from three to four plants.

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA Isolation kit (Huayueyang Biotech, Beijing, China) with modifications (Li et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2022). First‐strand cDNA was synthesised using TransScript Uni All‐in‐One First‐Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). qRT‐PCR was prepared with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Japan) and was conducted with the CFX96 System (Bio‐Red) under the protocol: 95°C for 30 s, followed by 39 cycles at 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 30 s and then 95°C for 10 s. Three replicates were performed for each sample, and the 2−△△Ct method was used for data analysis. All primers used for fluorescence quantification are shown in Table S2.

Yeast Two‐Hybridization (Y2H) and Yeast One‐Hybridization (Y1H) Assays

For Y2H screening and validation, the CDS of LsMAPK6 was cloned into the pGBKT7 bait vector and transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae Y2HGold. This construct served as bait to screen a cDNA library generated from lettuce stem apices. For pairwise validation, the CDS of LsCO was inserted into the pGADT7 prey vector. The pGBKT7‐LsMAPK6 and pGADT7‐LsCO plasmids were then co‐transformed into Y2HGold. Protein interactions were confirmed by growth on double dropout plates (SD/−Leu/−Trp; DDO) and quadruple dropout plates (SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp; QDO). The following controls were included: a positive control (co‐transformation of pGBKT7‐53 and pGADT7‐T), a negative control (co‐transformation of pGBKT7‐Lam and pGADT7‐T) and a blank control (co‐transformation of empty pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors) (Qiu et al. 2025).

For Y1H assays, the promoter sequence of LsFT was cloned into the pHIS2 vector. The pGADT7‐LsCO and pHIS2‐LsFTpro constructs were co‐transformed into Y187 yeast strain. Binding activity was assessed by growth on triple dropout plates (SD−Trp/−Leu/−His; TDO) supplemented with 5 or 20 mM 3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (3‐AT). Controls consisted of a positive combination (pGADT7‐53 with pHIS2‐p53) and a negative control (empty pGADT7 with pHIS2‐p53) (Chae et al. 2024). To identify cis‐acting elements within the LsFT promoter region that respond to LsCO, a yeast one‐hybrid assay was performed using the pLacZi system. The plasmid pJG was used to express LsCO to create the prey vector. The LsFT promoter region contains CORE2 and P1 elements. Following the addition of 3‐base protective sequences, the cis‐elements were tandemly repeated three times to enhance the reporter signal and were homologously recombined into the pLacZi vector. Both constructs were then co‐transformed into yeast strain EGY48 and selected on mediums lacking uracil and tryptophan (SD/‐U‐T) supplemented with X‐gal. Three parallel tests were conducted on each interaction in three independent experiments. Controls consisted of a positive combination (pJG‐DREB1A with pLacZi‐P1) and a negative control (empty pJG with pLacZi) (Lin et al. 2019).

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP), Subcellular Localization Analysis, Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) Assay and Luciferase Complementation Assay (LCA)

For the Co‐IP assay, the CDSs of LsMAPK6 and LsCO were cloned into the p1300‐35S‐3XFLAG and p1300‐GFP vectors, respectively. The constructs 35S::FLAG‐LsMAPK6 and 35S::LsCO‐GFP were co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves for 72 h. The IP products and the input samples were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), and target proteins were detected by Western blotting using anti‐FLAG (1:1000, Sigma–Aldrich F1084) or anti‐GFP (1:1000, Abcam ab32) antibodies (Fiil et al. 2008).

For subcellular colocalization determination, the CDSs of LsMAPK6 and LsCO were fused to p1300‐GFP and p1300‐RFP vectors, respectively (Schornack et al. 2009). Fluorescent signals were detected using a laser scanning confocal microscope (SP8, Leica, Germany), with excitation wavelengths set at 488 nm for GFP, 405 nm for DAPI and 532 nm for RFP. For the BiFC assays, the CDSs of LsMAPK6 and LsCO were inserted into the pUC‐SPYNE and pUC‐SPYCE vectors, respectively. The positive control is bZIP63, which can form homodimers on its own (Walter et al. 2004). After agroinfiltration, YFP fluorescence was detected using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Germany) with an excitation wavelength of 514 nm (Chen et al. 2013).

For the LCA, the CDSs of LsMAPK6 and LsCO were inserted into the p1300‐nLUC and p1300‐cLUC vectors, respectively. After 72 h infiltration, tobacco leaves were injected with 1 mmol/L D‐luciferin potassium substrate (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for the qualitative detection of luciferase activity by a charge‐coupled device (CCD) imaging system (Night SHADE evo, Berthold, Germany). The detection wavelength was 560 nm (Lang et al. 2019). The primers used in the analyses are listed in Table S2.

Protein Extraction, Immunoblot Analysis and Recombinant Protein Purification

To analyse the protein expression dynamics during high‐temperature‐induced bolting in lettuce, stems of 15 plants were taken as mixed samples at 0, 4, 8 and 16 DPH. For transgenic lines, stems were collected from the line showing the most pronounced phenotype at the six‐leaf‐one‐heart stage. Fresh tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen, and total protein was extracted. The protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method (Bradford 1976). Antibodies used were as follows: anti‐HIS (HIS‐Tag (D3I1O) XP Rabbit mAb #12698; 1:1000 dilution), anti‐β‐ACTIN (β‐Actin (D6A8) Rabbit mAb #8457; 1:1000 dilution), anti‐MBP (Myelin Basic Protein (D8X4Q) XP Rabbit mAb #78896; 1:1000 dilution), goat anti‐rabbit lgG antibody (Goat Anti‐Rabbit lgG H&L pAb, PTM‐6252; 1:10 000 dilution), anti‐LsCO (1:500 dilution; produced by PTM Biolabs Inc., Hangzhou, China), anti‐LsMAPK6 and anti‐phosphorylated‐LsMAPK6 (1:1000 dilution; Beijing Protein Innovation Co. Ltd., Beijing, China).

For recombinant proteins purification, the CDS of LsCO and the constitutively active form CA‐LsMAPK6 (G213D; A217E) were inserted into the pGEX‐6p1 and pET‐28a vectors, respectively, and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3). After the position of the band with small‐scale expression was detected by SDS–PAGE, abundant protein expression was induced. Western blot analysis was performed with an anti‐His and anti‐GST antibody to identify the purified protein and determine its concentration (Zou et al. 2021).

In Vitro and In Vivo Phosphorylation and Phosphosite Analyses

In vivo phosphorylation analysis was conducted as previously described (Xing et al. 2023). To examine LsMAPK6 phosphorylation in lettuce, protein extracts were prepared from stem apices of WT plants subjected to HT or RT conditions at 0, 4, 8 and 16 days, as well as from WT, LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 lines. Phosphorylated LsMAPK6 was detected by immunoblotting using a specific anti‐phosphorylated‐LsMAPK6 antibody. To assess the phosphorylation status of LsCO in lettuce, total protein was extracted from the stem apices of WT, LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 lines. Proteins were separated by both standard SDS‐PAGE and Phos‐tag gel electrophoresis, followed by immunoblotting with anti‐phosphoSer/Thr and anti‐GFP antibodies, respectively. Parallel samples treated with calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (CIP) served as dephosphorylation controls.

For in vitro phosphorylations, 1.5 μg of recombinant LsCO protein was incubated with 0.5 μg of constitutively active CA‐LsMAPK6 in kinase reaction buffer containing 0.2 mM ATP and 5 μCi [γ‐32P] ATP, which was separated through SDS–PAGE and detected by CBB staining, and phosphorylation signals were detected by autoradiography (Typhoon FLA 9500) (Ma et al. 2018). For phosphorylation site identification, the target protein band was excised from the gel, subjected to tryptic digestion, and analysed by LC–MS/MS (Zou et al. 2021).

Cell‐Free Protein Degradation Assay

Cell‐free protein degradation assay was performed as previously described (Zeng et al. 2025). Total proteins were extracted from six‐week‐old lettuce plants, including WT, LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 plants using a commercial Plant Total Protein Extraction Kit (Coolaber, Beijing, China). Purified recombinant LsCO‐MBP was added to equal amounts of WT, LsMAPK6‐OE and lsmapk6 total proteins, as well as 10 mM ATP. Purified recombinant LsCO‐MBP, LsCOS258A‐MBP and LsCOS258D‐MBP were added to equal amounts of WT and lsmapk6 total proteins, as well as 10 mM ATP. The mixture was incubated at 25°C for different time intervals. The concentration of proteasome inhibitor 50 mM MG132 was included as controls, and the commercial antibody anti‐MBP was used in this experiment.

Dual‐LUC Assays

For Dual‐LUC assays, phosphosite mutants of LsCO (LsCO S258A and LsCO S258D ) were generated using a site‐directed mutagenesis kit (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan), and the mutations were verified by Sanger sequencing. The corresponding cDNA sequences (LsMAPK6, LsCO, LsCO S258A and LsCO S258D ) were cloned into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector, and the LsFT promoter sequence was inserted into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector. Agrobacterium GV3101 carrying these constructs was co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves, with effector‐to‐reporter bacterial suspensions mixed at a 10:1 (v/v). After 72 h incubation, dual‐luciferase activities were quantified using the dual‐luciferase reporter assay system (GloMax 20/20 Luminometer, Promega, Madison, USA), which was used to measure the ratio of firefly luciferase activity (560 nm) to Renilla luciferase activity (465 nm) (Jiang et al. 2022).

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Duncan's test for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05), with Student's t‐test applied for pairwise comparisons. Data visualisation and graph construction were executed in OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). All figure panels were prepared and assembled in Adobe Photoshop 2022 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) and Microsoft PowerPoint. Schematic diagrams were created with FigDraw (https://www.figdraw.com). All experiments incorporated three independent biological replicates.
