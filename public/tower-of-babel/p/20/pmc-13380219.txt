Kanwa and Niky induced partial saponified emulsions in highly concentrated ionic medium: Mechanism of emulsion formation and stability for food applications
Authors: Stève Djiazet, Laurette Blandine Mezajoug Kenfack, Digambar Kavitake, Horliane Nzali Ghomdim, Franklin Kégah Ngoualem, Palanisamy Bruntha Devi, Eric Serge Ngangoum, Lilian Karcher, Michel Linder, Prathapkumar Halady Shetty, Clergé Tchiégang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study elucidates the formation mechanism and stability of a non-conventional alkaline colloidal system termed partially saponified emulsion (PSE), prepared using two alkaline salts, Kanwa and Niky, as emulsifying agents. The mechanism leading to the formation and stability of the non-conventional alkaline emulsion prepared in high ionic strength emulsion, is discussed herein. This study examined the various complex chemical reactions and interactions, as revealed by phenomenological microscopic observations, zeta potential of oil droplets (−100 to +150), Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy. The zeta potential results showed the coexistence of oil droplets with variable overall charges, both negative and positive, that fluctuate over time in solution. The IR and Raman spectroscopy showed the occurrence of new absorption bands at 1565.25 and 1554.24 cm−1 then 1161.1659 cm−1 for Raman, theoretically attributed to partially saponified triglycerides. TLC, staining test on white paper, NMR and microscopy observations confirmed the presence of oil or emulsion properties. The outcomes of this pilot study provide theoretical mechanistic insights into alkaline-based emulsions and highlight their potential as functional, naturally derived emulsifying systems for food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical applications.

Introduction

Emulsions are multiphase colloidal systems with broad applications in food, pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and chemical industries. In foods, emulsions underpin the texture, stability, and functionality of products (Goodarzi & Zendehboudi, 2019; Niu et al., 2025). Emulsion-based food products are produced through emulsification processes using surfactants, emulsifying agents, or stabilizers (Niu et al., 2025). There exists a variety of emulsion-based food products: milk (natural milk, plant-based milk products like soybean, coconut, etc.…), (Braun et al., 2019), beverages (Molet-Rodríguez et al., 2018), meat emulsions (Santhi et al., 2017), mayonnaise, salad dressing including hollandaise sauce (Rognså et al., 2017; Yuan, Wei, et al., 2025), and tahini (Szpinak et al., 2022). The mechanistic model of a good number of them are not established. The pH of these food matrixes varies from extremely acidic to moderate alkaline, depending on the surfactant or emulsifying agent used.

There exists a diversity of such surface-active materials (Zhao et al., 2025). The chemical composition, structure, and physicochemical properties of these surface-active materials determine droplet interactions, phase behaviour, and functional attributes of the emulsion (Zhao et al., 2025). While conventional emulsifiers are well studied, non-conventional or naturally occurring alkaline emulsifying systems from mineral or botanical sources remain poorly understood. Thus, the need of carrying out serious chemical analysis to determine their composition, chemical and physical properties. They can introduce unique ionic and pH-dependent effects that modulate droplet surface charge, interfacial structure, and bulk rheology (Ho & Ahmad, 1999; Li et al., 2020). The blend of fatty acid soap, mono, di, triglycerides ethoxylate possibly present in partially saponified emulsion (PSE) have been reported to act as surfactants (Cox & Weerassoriya, 2000); but there are still misperceptions about the formation process and stability mechanisms of similar types of emulsion systems (Goodarzi & Zendehboudi, 2019).

Depending on the pH and the presence of cations, the electronic interactions convey another functionality to the oil droplets in the emulsified colloidal system, besides the known functionalities in conventional emulsions (Ho & Ahmad, 1999). Alkaline food-based emulsions like PSE are however seldom or almost not reported in the literature (Lunestad et al., 2018). Ho and Ahmad (1999) demonstrated zeta potential inversion in palm oil emulsions between pH 7 and 11, emphasizing the importance of electrokinetic effects under alkaline conditions. Similarly, Djiazet, 2021,Djiazet et al., 2022) in the preliminary studies on PSE reported high conductivities of 8–12.35 mS/cm in nah poh. This emulsified colloidal system is notable for its rich mineral content, potential health-promoting properties (Djiazet, 2021). The food-based emulsion was used in 2020 as one of the local and early solutions for the prevention and treatment of Covid-19 pandemic in Cameroon. Its increasing commercialization rate extended even to Western markets is a reason to pay attention to the study of this food matrix. Earlier, in many reports, it was either qualified as an emulsion (Abdou Bouba, 2009; Mouafo & Blaise, 2013; Ngoualem et al., 2020) or as a product of saponification (Ekosse, 2010). But these previous works did not discuss the mechanism of formation and stability of the colloidal system as prepared by the indigenous people, known to be the starting point for preparing yellow achu soup (Mboseh et al., 2024; Ngoualem et al., 2020; Ngwasiri et al., 2021), nah poh), sauce jaune (Abdou Bouba, 2009; Mouafo & Blaise, 2013). It therefore, appears very interesting to conduct studies to understand the mechanism of formation and stability of such a colloidal system, by developing mechanistic models linking the alkaline matrix and plausible ionic interactions.

Two alkaline materials (kanwa and niky) were used as emulsifying agents in this study. Kanwa is a natural evaporite mineral from soda lake deposits while niky is a potassium carbonate-rich substance obtained by calcination of Musa paradisiaca or Musa acuminata peels. In this study, they were characterized (chemical composition and some physicochemical properties) and applied to produce partially saponified emulsions (PSE), which were assessed through spectroscopic, electrokinetic analyses, and microscopic observations.

Materials and methods

A.: CuCl2.2H2O, Daejung reagents chemicals: FeCl3 (99%) BDH Laboratory supplier: (CH3COO)2Ni.4H2O (98%), CrCl3.6H2O (93%), (CH3COO)2Pb.3H2O (99.5%), Acetone 99.5% E. Merch, Germany: Cd (NO3)2.4H2O (99%), KBr 99.99%, Deuterated chloroform 99.8%.

Kanwa was purchased from local vendors in Ngaoundere Adamawa, Cameroon. niky and palm oil were bought from Food Market in Bamenda, Northwest region of Cameroon.

Physicochemical characterization of emulsifying agents (kanwa and niky)

Solution of each emulsifying agent was prepared at 1 % (w/v) and at saturation concentration (25% w/v for kanwa and 29% w/v for niky) then used for physicochemical analysis. The ionic species (anions and cations) composition in solution was determined. Also, the pH, conductivity, total dissolve solids, solubility, turbidity, and all alkaline constituents were determined for both emulsifying agents (Li et al., 2024).

Preparation of palm oil emulsion with kanwa or niky

The ratio/proportions of constituents (oil, water, emulsifying agent) were optimized and selected using central composite design (supplementary data: S1, S2, S3, S4). Palm oil emulsions were prepared with 24%, 19% and 14% of palm oil, with precise mass of emulsifying agent (Kanwa or niky: 1.1, 1.65, 2.2 g) dissolved in distilled water. The constituents were mixed and stirred using an electric hand blender (Moulinex: 350 W, mixing intensity 7000 W/L; 2 blades mixing configuration 7 cm of diameter) at the required time and temperature (central composite design) in a thermostatic water bath. The obtained emulsified colloidal systems were used for various analyses.

Cations assessment in emulsifying agent solutions

Cations (Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Fe3+, Cr3+ and Zn2+) were determined in prepared stock solutions of kanwa and niky. After dissolution in 4 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid (37%), each solution was filtered into a 10 mL volumetric flask and the volume was made up with deionise water. Cations assessment was done by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICPMS) (NYSE: PKI, ISIN:US7140461093) (Enamorado-Báez et al., 2013).

Anions assessment

Anions determined in the samples of emulsifying agents were chlorides (Cl−), phosphates (PO₄3−), carbonates (CO32−), and bicarbonates (HCO3−). Chlorides and phosphates were determined using spectrophotometric methods at 530 nm developed by Bertolacini and Barney (1957) and Jeffery et al. (1989) respectively, while carbonates and bicarbonates were assessed by acid-base titration.

Measurements of pH, conductivity, total dissolve solids, solubility, turbidity and alkaline constituents

pH, conductivity, total dissolve solids measurements

The pH, conductivity and total dissolve solids were measured with a benchtop conductivity meter LBCM-A11 A modified method based on conductivity measurement over time was developed and adopted for the study of PSE, after several trials (Li et al., 2024). Previous works on the food matrix (yellow achu soup, and sauce jaune) did not addressed the ionic strength and other aspect reported herein (Mouafo & Blaise, 2013; Mboseh et al., 2024; Ngoualem et al., 2020). The conductivity of a précised volume (50 mL) of partially saponified emulsions prepared with distilled water at the temperature that the conductivity had to be measured (experimental design) was determined. The electrode of the conductivity meter was placed in the vial immobilised in a water bath at the required temperature (experimental design). The conductivity values were recorded after every 60 s (1 min) for a duration of 20 min. Preliminary trials for different emulsions samples showed that after 20 min the conductivity was stable, or almost. The values were corrected using the following formula:

(KTref) =KT1+αT−Tref

Where, -KT is the conductivity measured at the temperature T (°C),

-KTref is the corrected value of conductivity at the reference temperature,

- α is the conductivity temperature coefficient (°C) (0.025) for electrolytes,

-T is the temperature at which the measurement in °C is recorded,

-Tref is the reference temperature in °C.

The emulsion stability factor was calculated (S4) and use to study the stability of PSE.

Solubility, turbidity and alkaline constituents

Turbidity was measure using Turbidity meter (NTU, Eutech Instruments turbidimeter TN-100), the solubility was determined with the methodology used by Saidou et al. (2015). The entire alkaline constituents were determined by acid/base titration.

Microscopy and viscosity of emulsions

In this primary study, the microscopic monitoring was qualitative and descriptive. Freshly prepared emulsions were introduced in the cavity of slide and monitored at 10 and 40× using Nikon eclipse TS100 (Japan) microscope, equipped with a light filter and a device for light polarisation, to improve the quality of images. Emulsions were also monitored using the inverted phase contrast microscope Nikon Eclipse TE2000-E Inverted Fluorescence Motorized Microscope Pred Ti2-E (Digambar et al., 2020). Due to the volume of work, the droplet sizing technics would be discussed in the upcoming manuscripts. The viscosity of the emulsified product was measured using Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA) following the method used by Prow & Metzger, 2005.

Droplet size distribution and zeta potential

Immediately after emulsification, the colloidal suspension was placed in the Zetasizer and the measurements were conducted at 25 °C using dynamic laser light scattering with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Zeta potential was determined using two distinct analytical methods. Malvern Zetasizer is limited in determining the charges of individual particles in suspension, low density solutions and ZetaCDA® does give the results in the form of curve. For the Malvern Zetasizer 10 mg of dehydrated emulsion were diluted in 50 mL of deionized water (Chudasama et al., 2022) and secondly ZetaCDA® was used to determine the stream zeta potential of individual oil droplets (size ˃ 50 μm) in the freshly prepared emulsion as described in the section 2.3 (Wang et al., 2024).

FT-IR, Raman spectroscopy, XRD, UV spectroscopy, and TLC

FT-IR spectroscopy

Fourier-Transformed Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) is used to study the functional groups in various organic and inorganic samples (Nandiyanto et al., 2019). After preparing the emulsions, they were air dried in a ventilated oven at 35 °C. About 5 mg of dried emulsion were employed for the FT-IR analysis withing 400–4000 cm−1 wavelength range at a resolution of 4 cm−1 using Thermo Nicolet: 6700 (Souza et al., 2020).

Raman spectroscopyof dehydrated emulsionsand XRD of emulsifying agents

Raman spectroscopy was used as a complementary analysis to FT-IR to study the chemical modification occurring during the emulsification process. The analysis was done by placing about 5 mg of sample on the slit and scanned between 0 and 3500 cm−1 using Raman confocal spectroscopy (RENISHAW, Wotton-under-Edge, United Kingdom). (Haynes et al., 2005). XRD analysis was performed on the samples of kanwa and niky, to have an idea the texture and elemental constitution.

UV spectroscopy, and TLC

A Thermo scientific Evolution 220 spectrophotometer was used to record the UV–visible absorption spectra of the emulsion and oil, diluted in acetone (Ameur et al., 2025). Major compounds in palm oil and PSE emulsion were separated by TLC test on aluminium plates recovered by silica 60 RP18 F254s (Merk, Darmstadt, Germany). Five (5) % ethyl acetate in petroleum ether was used for elution and the plate was revealed by phosphomolybdic acid.

NMR

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (13C NMR) for the dehydrated colloidal emulsion and that of palm oil were recorded at 20 °C using a Bruker DRX Advance 400 MHz spectrometer to see the effect of the emulsification process on ester functional groups of triglycerides. About 10 mg of dehydrated colloidal emulsions or palm oil were dissolved in 0.5 mL of 99.96% CDCl3 (Deuterated chloroform) in NMR tubes and scanned with Bruker AV III 500, 400 MHz Fourier transform-nuclear magnetic resonance Spectrometer.

Statistical analysis

Results obtained were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Stat graphics centurion was used for variance analysis, compare the means of values obtained from the analysis of kanwa or niky and PSE using the Duncan multiple range test. Experiments were performed done in triplicate and analysed at 95% level of probability. The curves were plotted using origin software.

In lake deposits, major minerals are natron, trona, thermonatrite, magnesite and several other minerals. The review reports sodium contents between 2.6 and 32.3% for kanwa, and potassium between 1.54 and 26% for plant ashes. These values are comparable to those of Na+ for kanwa and K+ for niky in Table 1.

Anions in emulsifying agents

Anions present are Cl− (530 μg/g), H2PO42−, (3.8601 μg/g) CO32− (348 mg/g) and HCO3− (421 mg/g) for kanwa and Cl− (135 mg/g), H2PO42−, (1.38 mg/g) and CO32− (346 mg/g) for niky. The values are also closed to those reported by Ngoualem et al. (2020). HCO3− was not detected in niky but is reported to be present (0.12 to 17.8%) (Ngoualem et al., 2020). The composition of kanwa makes it more soluble in water (0.99 g/100 g) than niky (1.05 g/100 g) at 1%. Consequently, there are more dissolved solids in the solution of kanwa 11.00 ppt than niky 9.51 ppt. The high concentration of dissolved solids makes kanwa to be more conductive 14.10 mS/cm than niky 12.37 mS/cm, but less alkaline (pH 10.04) than niky (pH 11.97). The high alkalinity could be attributed to H2PO42− ions present in high quantity in niky (346 mg/g).

Total alkalinity of emulsifying agents

The titration of these solutions to determine the total alkalinity using HCl and phenolphthalein showed almost similar values for kanwa (1.10 N) and niky (1.06 N) for the same mass (0.5 g) dissolved in distilled water. The difference observed is attributed to the species and concentrations of ions present in solutions (CO32−; HCO3−, H2PO42−) as well as OH- ions released by metal ions in niky solution or dissociation of water molecules. It is also due to the fact that for the same masse of emulsifying agent, kanwa has more soluble particles in solution (11.00 ppt) than niky (9.51 ppt). The trend was the same when the solutions were studied in their saturation concentrations Table 1.

Microscopy of PSE

The colloidal suspensions produced using kanwa and niky were studied using the optical microscope and the dehydrated emulsion by Scanning Electron Microscope. The emulsions were prepared using three different processes for kanwa and for niky and observed at two different magnifications (10× and 40×). The oil droplets distribution analyses were preferred over the results of the optic microscope scale bar. The experiments were selected from an experimental design set (central composite design), used previouslyto standardise the preparatory processes. The factors studied were oil/water ratio, the masse of kawa, the stirring duration and the operating temperature. The levels were 14 to 24% (v/v) for oil/water ratio; 1.1 to 2.2 g for emulsifying agent, 30 to 60 s for the stirring duration and 25 to 35 °C for the operation temperature. The responses measured were the apparent viscosity and the conductivity which was then used to calculate the emulsion stability factor using the following formular:ESF=Conductivity emulsifying agent solution−ConductivityPSEat0minConductivityPSEafter20min−ConductivityPSEat0min

The results are presented as supplementary data (S1, S2, S3, S4). The microscopic observations were done at the ages of the cover-slide used to cover the convex slide containing the PSE. The PSE observed under optic microscope have the same physical appearance for kanwa and niky.

Microscopy for PSE from kanwa

The micrographs depicted on Figs. 1A to 1I are for PSE prepared using kanwa. The oil droplets are generally yellow in colour, spherical or round shape particles disseminated in the colloidal system. At 10×, they look like aggregates of solid elements with small spots of shining particles. At 40×, the shining particles are well observed as yellow spherical or round droplets. Bigger oil droplets at the same magnification are found in Fig. 1B, H, and C. These observations confirm that the size distribution of the droplets depend on the process used to prepare the PSE, (Li et al., 2020).

Microscopy for PSE from niky

Emulsion prepared using niky tend to yield bigger oil droplets (Fig. 1L, J to 1R). It is worth mentioning that from the edges of the slide to the centre, the appearance of the emulsion becomes less visible under the microscope (Fig. 1K and G). At magnification 40×, it becomes difficult to see individual oil droplets when the observation was focussed towards the centre of the slide (Fig. 1F).

Scanning electron microscopy and photo of PSE

The dehydrated PSE samples were observed under scanning electron microscope and the images obtained are those on Fig. 1s, t, u, v and w at different magnifications. The morphology of PSE prepared using kanwa and those prepared using niky had the same appearance under scanning electron microscope. The images show that during dehydration, the particles are ordered in rode-like shape, probably due to the electric properties of the PSE. The scanning electron microscope images also show that after drying, the resulting product is amorphous. A sample of the PSE is presented on Fig. 1Z, having a yellow colour.

Analysis of the apparent viscosity of PSE

The apparent viscosity of the PSE results is illustrated on Fig. 2A and B. Fig. 2A show that emulsion prepared using kanwa is generally very highly viscous (180 to 260 Cp) than emulsion produced using niky (23 to 24.9Cp), irrespective of the process used. These observations could be due to the differences at the level of the atomic radii of potassium (2.43 Å) and sodium (1.90 Å) ions which make sodium (890 mg/g in kanwa) to have more affinity with water molecules than potassium. Potassium fatty acids are known to be highly soluble in water and have lower melting point compared to sodium soap (Alum., 2024). The low viscosity of the PSE produced with niky (211 mg/g) could be due to the bigger atomic radius of potassium which is about 1.3 times bigger than that of sodium. This property of potassium atom makes the resulting PSE produced with niky more fluent (Alum., 2024; Remko & Rode, 2006). The differences of viscosity between the PSE samples prepared with kanwa could also be explained by the proportions of oil and water used. It is reported that the oil/water ratio significantly influence the viscosity of emulsions (Farah et al., 2005; Pajouhandeh et al., 2016; Soleimanpour et al., 2013; Ishigami et al., 2022). Another factor that modifies the viscosity is the concentration of carbonate, bicarbonate and dihydrogen phosphate ions in the various PSE samples. Droplet size distribution of PSE

The oil droplets size distribution of the PSE is presented on Fig. 2D and E. Fig. 2D is the particle size distribution of PSE prepared with kanwa using three processes and Fig. 2E is PSE prepared with niky through three processes. The results of the particle size distribution show that the process of preparation influences the size distribution of oil droplets. High temperatures (35 and 40 °C) favour the formation of bigger oil droplets (Fig. 2D and E), especially when niky is used as emulsifying agent Binks and Rocher (2009).

The average oil droplets for PSE produced using kanwa were around 450 nm for the processes conducted at 25 °C and around 615 nm at 40 °C while PSE produced using niky were about 495 nm when prepared at 25 and 35 °C. Regardless the nature of the emulsifying agent, the particle size distribution was bimodal and bidisperesed (Fig. 2D, E). The micrographs of Fig. 1A to 1R confirm that the PSE contains two sets of oil droplets populations visually. Li et al. (2014) attributed the occurrence of large droplets to high dispersed phase volume fraction; that is high volume of water compared to oil.

Determination of the stability of PSE by conductivity measurement

The present study showed that, the destabilisation of the PSE could be evaluated by measuring the electric conductivity of the colloidal system as shown on Fig. 2C. In fact, during the destabilisation process, the conductivity increases with time. The increase of conductivity is due to the separation of the organic phase from the liquid phase. The separation is very slow and at the chemical level, it is explained by the movements of ions from the organic particles to the aqueous phase (videos 1 and 2). The movements stop when the thermodynamic equilibrium is attained by the 20th min (Fig. 2C) in the case of kanwa and niky as studied in this work. Fig. 2C was obtained by calculating the mean values of three different measurements for each of the points on the graph. The instability of the PSE is determined by the hight (H) separation at the beginning of the measurement 0 min and the plateau between the 15th and the 20th min on Fig. 2C. Fig. 2C was further linearised by calculating the inverse value of each point to obtain Fig. 2F. Fig. 2F shows that the points do not form a perfect straight line in this case. However, the R2 which is 0.98 enabled us to validate the method developed, which was then used to study the stability of the PSE system by calculating the emulsion stability factor.

Zeta potential of non-diluted and dissolved dehydrated PSE in distilled water

Zeta potential of non-diluted PSE

The zetapotential of the non-diluted PSE was determined using the ZetaCDA® to have details on the zeta potential of detected particles in the form of oil droplets, in the colloidal suspension as depicted on Fig. 3A, B and C in the concentrated state. The zeta potential was recorded at 5, 10 and 15 min on the same sample to study the change in potential of the oil droplets with time. The choice of the duration was done based on the results of Fig. 2C, showing the time withing which the electrical conductivity increases the most. The results show that in the colloidal system, there coexists both negatively and positively charged particles. On this Figure, the units 0 to 10 materialize the number of particles having a particular charge. At unit nine for example, there is one point (particle) +30 mV, and one point (particle) at −30 mV. Zeta potential of dissolved dehydrated PSE in distilled water

The conventional method of determining the zetapotential was also used to evaluate the overall zeta potential for complementary information. The analysis was done using dehydrated PSE (10 mg) which was solubilised in large volume of water (50 mL) and measured using Malvern zetasizer (Fig. 3D). The metal dissociation results from the solubilization of the dehydrated PSE in large volume of water. Physico-chemical characterization of PSE

FTIR spectroscopy

The FT-IR spectra of palm oil, PSE produced using kanwa as well as niky were done and represented in Fig. 4A. Looking at the FT-IR, there is a stretch at 1565.25 and 1554.24 cm−1 for the dehydrated PSE samples (lyophilized). These stretches are assigned to carboxylic acid salt formed with sodium and other metal ions (R-COO− +Na) (Nandiyanto et al., 2019; Filiopoulou et al., 2021). Irrespective of the lent of the hydrocarbon chain, the carboxylic acid salt peak appears towards this region. It can also be due to the interaction of the polar regions of monoacylglycerol (MAG), diacylglycerol (DAG) and triacylglycerol (TAG) with cations in solution, after formation of a tetrahedral intermediate or not as demonstrated by Gómez-Bombarelli et al. The stretch is attributed to potassium fatty acid salt R-COO− +K. Potassium ions are predominant metals present in niky (Table 1). Gocen et al., 2017 identified FTIR stretches at 1560 and 1423 cm−1 as correspond to salts of carboxylic acid of linoleic potassium and sodium salts. These salts of carboxylic acid are used as food additives in food industries. Complementary analysis to assign peaks would be given in forthcoming manuscripts.

Raman spectroscopy

The result shows a stretch at 1161 cm−1 for emulsion produced using kanwa and niky as well as at 1527.74 cm−1, indicating two different species of carboxylic acid salts (Fig. 4B). The two species of carboxylic acid salts are probably free fatty acid salts and carboxylic acid salts produced with non-hydrolysed carboxylic acid function, after the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate at the level of ester bonds Gomez Bombarelli et al. (2013). Gocen et al., 2017 attributed band values of 1560 to COO− and 1562 for COO−. These stretches are however absent in palm oil. Gracia et al. (1994) reported peak for potassium soaps at 1450 cm−1 showing that metal potassium is formed in both PSE systems (kanwa and niky). There is a concordance in the FT-IR and Raman analyses to support the existence of at least two types of carboxylic acid salts in the PSE. However, it was noticed while doing this work that after reducing the ionic strength by adding deionized water a quick reversion of the PSE happen and is materialized by the formation of oil layer at the surface of the system (S6, Fig. 2F and H). This supports the hypothesis of the formation of the tetrahedral intermediate by the hydrophilic part of non-hydrolysed triglycerides following the dissociation of cations from the carboxylic acid after reducing the ionic strength, giving rise to oil at the surface Gomez Bombarelli et al. (2013).

Thin layer chromatographic, X ray diffraction (kanwa and niky), and UV visible spectrum of PSE

TLC analysis of PSE

Thin layer chromatographic (TLC) result (Fig. 4C) was done to put into evidence the presence of partially hydrolysed triglycerides after interaction with emulsifying agent and palm oil. The results are similar for the two emulsifying agents and show five spots for PSE as well as palm oil. The differences in the spot's migration levels show the production of new compounds in the PSE.

Spot 1 correspond to carotenoids (pigments) and is present in both palm oil and PSE (rf: 0.8 to 1 respectively). Spot 3 in palm oil attributed to saturated triglycerides (rf: 0.7 to 0.9). Spot 2 and 3 in PSE are assigned to saturated triglycerides, diglycerides and monoglycerides salts. Spot 4 in palm oil matches with unsaturated triglycerides, monoglycerides and diglycerides (rf: 0.3 to 0.7). Spots 4 and 5 in PSE which are monoglycerides, diglycerides and triglyceride salts. Spot 5 in palm oil refers to phenolic compounds and gums (0.05 to 0.3) (Mahilika & Piraman, 2013). This observation supports the hypothesis of the formation of different types of carboxylic acid salts during the emulsification process. Detailed analyses would be conducted to support these results, using less complex system.

UV analysis of PSE

The UV visible spectrum of palm oil and PSE were recorded to understand the effect of alkalinity and ionic strength on the UV spectrum of palm oil (Fig. 4F). The UV spectrum shows a broad peak of absorption at 450 nm for both palm oil and PSE, corresponding to carotenoids (de Souza Guedes et al., 2021). There was a reduction on the carotenoids content, and the reduction is probably due to loss of double bonds caused by oxidation of carotenoids. This results also shows that the carotenoids are not totally oxidized after reaction with alkaline emulsifying agents, despite the occurrence of the yellow colour. The yellow colour is not an indication of total degradation of carotenoids. It is more related to their hydration in water and partial oxidation.

XRD analysis kanwa and niky

The X-ray diffraction of the two emulsifying agents presented on Fig. 3D (kanwa) and 3E (niky) were performed for complementary information on their composition to understand their interaction with oil and water when PSE is prepared. Results show that kanwa has a crystalline nature and niky is amorphous. The amorphous character of niky is due to its hydroscopic nature. The X-ray of kanwa confirms the presence of different minerals materialized by the following peaks: trona (10, 15 to 20, 27.5, 57,67.5, 73), natrite (29, 36, 40, 41, 46.5), natron (29, 47.5), nahcolite (30, 34, 35) (Eskosse et al., Ozkan, 2016). The high conductivity and TDS of kanwa in Table 1 are supported by results of XRD. It is highly made up of minerals that can easily solubilised in water, compared to niky. The XRD of niky shows that it contains impurities as well as mineral elements.

Evidence of fatty acid salt formation in PSE

The result of the experiment is presented in supplementary data S6. Fig. S6B is the hexane fraction containing monoglycerides salts, diglycerides salts and triglycerides. After cooling the content of the round bottom flask (Fig. S6B), the two layers were separated and characterized by Raman spectroscopy (S6A). Raman spectrum (S6A) shows a difference in the concentrations of various constituents in the upper and lower layers, illustrated by the intensities of the stretches. The stretches are that of different types of triglycerides, diglycerides (diglycerides salts), monoglycerides (monoglycerides salts) and fatty acids. This result shows that the emulsifying agents could react selectively with some triglycerides or fatty acids (Gharby, 2022). Fig. S6D is the solid material remaining in the Soxhlet extractor chamber. Other analyses not discussed in this paper showed that the retained product is fatty acid salt (soap) based on its solubility in water and its foaming properties. The fact of it remaining in the extractor chamber shows that it was not soluble in hexane (organic solvent). The soap content obtained with various emulsifying agents was estimated and presented on S6C. Soap is therefore produced during the process of preparation of PSE.

Evidence of oily fraction in PSE

More than 80% of the lyophilized PSE (niky, kanwa) was washed by hexane and represent the non-saponified oil (triglycerides) or partially saponified fraction (diglycerides, monoglycerides). After reducing the ionic strength of freshly prepared PSE by adding demineralized water, oil droplets having red colour reappeared and suspended at the surface of the beaker (Supplementary data Fig. 6F and G). The dehydration of PSE change from yellow to bright red. The dehydrated colloidal suspension leaves a translucid spot on white sheet of paper (Supplementary data 6H, 6I, 6 J, 6 K). These are proves that not all the triglycerides present are hydrolysed to form fatty acid salts. The colloidal suspension is therefore, a mixture of non-saponified triglycerides, diglycerides, monoglycerides and fatty acid salts in aqueous solution.

NMR analysis

The results are given as supplementary data (S7). Due to the presence of different molecules and minerals in the samples, a comparative evaluation was preferred due to the complexity of the emulsion system. The comparison of S7A, S7B, and S7C shows similar stretches in palm oil and the dehydrated colloidal suspension (Kanwa and niky) with variations in the intensity. This result supports the fact that, there are still triglycerides in the colloidal suspension, and the presence of fatty acid salts is elucidated (S6C; S6D). The process used to produce this colloidal suspension is most often at temperatures below 60 °C and for a short duration. In these conditions incomplete saponification process leads to the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate (Gomez Bombarelli et al., 2013), that is stabilized by cations present in the medium. The stabilisation of incomplete saponified acylglycerols contributes to the functionalization of the surface of oil droplets by producing acylglycerols with different charges at the surface of oil droplets. The diacylglycerols produced are functional molecules which are known to play a role in the management of some chronic metabolic diseases (Lai et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025).

Proposed reaction mechanisms leading to partially saponified emulsion system

During the 22nd international conference of nutrition in Japan in 2022, the relationship between health claims of indigenous foods, chemistry, and the scientific evidence supporting the functionality were raised and encouraged. The case of this study enriches the list of existing and known alkaline foods like green tea, soda, Lutefisk, etc. (Lunestad et al., 2018) recommended in nutrition. The chemical attack can either be on the functional carbon of the esterified fatty acid or a carbon atom of the glycerol backbone (electrophile). The electrons attacking can be that of the double bond of the carbonate, bicarbonate, dihydrogen phosphate ions or hydroxyl ions (nucleophile). The attack can either result or not to the hydrolysis of the ester function (Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2013). The same reactions happen when the oil droplet is made up of diglycerides and monoglycerides. The OH of the glycerol simply interacts with a cation in the system (Weng & Elliott, 2014). The mechanism emphases on the case where the ester bond is not completely hydrolysed but is stabilized by various cations in the reaction medium and subsequent reactions. Summary of interactions between the different constituents to ensure the stability of oil droplets in the partially saponified emulsion

Fig. 6 is a summary of illustration of probable interactions that happen at the surface of an oil droplet in PSE produced with kanwa or niky. On carbon 1 the ester function interacts with the carbonate ion. The carbonate ion then interacts in turn with cations (calcium and magnesium), which in turn interact with water molecules to ensure stability (Yuan, Yang, & Liu, 2025). Rings A, B, C, and D summarise the different possibilities of reactions that take place at the surface of oil droplets in the colloidal system to ensure its stability. Glycerol salts could also be produced after hydrolysis of the TAG (Yoshimura et al., 2025). In the case of the formation of diglyceride and monoglycerides, the hydroxyl of the glyceride interacts with cations also.

On carbon 2, the oxygen of the ester function of triglyceride interacts with cation (sodium), the ester function is also attacked by carbonate ion and stabilized with monovalent cations (sodium and potassium). On carbon 3, the carbonate ion attacks a carbon of the ester function and is in turn stabilized by cation (sodium) to form a tetrahedral carbon by interaction of an oxygen of the ester function with a cation (magnesium). The same magnesium interacts with an oxygen related to the ester function of carbon 4 to establish a chain between oil droplets in the system (Yuan, Wei, et al., 2025). Still on carbon 4, the water molecule interacts with the carbon of the ester functional group. The water molecule interacts in turn with carbonate ions linked to the ester function of carbon 5. On carbon 5, the carbon of the ester function interacts with the phosphate group of phospholipids in the system. The phosphate group is then stabilized by metal ions (sodium and potassium). On carbon 6, hydroxyl ion in solution attacks the carbon of the ester function. Carbon 7 and 8 are the free fatty acids that fit their hydrophobic part in the interior of the oil droplet, and the hydrophile part remains at the surface, interacting with cations (sodium and potassium) in the aqueous phase. The formation of glycerol salts is also possible.

The summary of the data of these studies shows that the reversibility of the alkaline non-conventional food emulsion into oil after modification of physicochemical parameters (Suplementary data, 6E to 6 K) results from the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate at the level of the carbon of the ester bonds by cations, carbonate bicarbonate or hydroxyl ion interaction (Majumder et al., 2025). They enable the oil droplets in the suspension to survive by folding and protect the droplets and the structure of non-hydrolysed tri, di, and monoglycerides (Cox & Weerassoriya, 2000), giving the yellowish milky and stable appearance when dydrated. The proposed reactions are postulated as those happening on triglycerides and derivatives. The reaction happening with other oil constituents (carotenoids, tocopherol…) are not taken into consideration here. Depending on the nature of the surfactant or emulsifying agent used, the surface of emulsion systems could become functionalized (Cionti et al., 2022; Courrégelongue & Baigl, 2025; Ho & Ahmad, 1999; Li et al., 2020). The oil droplet core therefore interacts with surface-active compounds (metal ions, charged organic compounds like proteins, polysaccharides, exopolysaccharides, or modified triglycerides) at the functional sites (Ho & Ahmad, 1999; Li et al., 2020; Tabatabaei et al., 2022). This enables them to interact (electronic, hydrogen, hydrophobic, Vander vaal forces) with other elements of the colloidal system like oxygen/carbon of esterified triglycerides, phosphate groups, fatty acids (Drescher & Hoogevest, 2020; Li et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021), glycerol (Weng and Elliott, 2014), and water molecules.

Conclusion

This work puts into evidence the coexistence of soap and emulsion in partial emulsified emulsion system. Partially saponified emulsion (PSE) described in this study produced in strong alkaline and highly concentrated ionic conditions result from on a complex electrostatic interaction of triglycerides, diglycerides, and monoglycerides, glycerol and fatty acids. Diglycerides, and monoglycerides; glycerol and fatty acids are produced in situ. The incomplete saponified oil droplet and the charge density of cationic micelles surrounding oil droplets in the PSE insures the stability of the emulsion system. The free fatty acid salts and some cations adsorb onto the oil droplet surface, and the entire oil droplets constitute mobile charged particles in the colloidal system. Further studies would elucidate the mechanism, other applications and their contributions on health.
