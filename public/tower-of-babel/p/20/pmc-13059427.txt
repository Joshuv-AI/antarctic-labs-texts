Association between healthy lifestyle and risk of chronic kidney disease: a prospective cohort study of 0.5 million Chinese adults
Authors: Yilin Chen, Huan Zhou, Siqing Wang, Lingqiu Dong, Yi Tang, Jiaxing Tan, Wei Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a fast growing global public health concern, with lifestyle factors contributing significantly to its risk. The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between a healthy lifestyle and the risk of incident CKD.MethodsWe examined healthy lifestyle and CKD risk in 502,621 China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) participants. Six low-risk lifestyle factors were assessed: 1) non-smoking or quitting for at least 6 months, 2) drinking alcohol weekly, 3) engaging in moderate or greater physical activity, 4) following a healthy diet, 5) maintaining a body mass index (BMI) of 18.5–23.9 kg/m2, and 6) having a waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) of < 0.90 for men and < 0.85 for women. Adequate physical activity was defined as achieving at least the gender-specific median level of total metabolic equivalent task hours per day. A Simplified Healthy Eating Index score in the highest quartile indicated a healthy diet.The proportion of CKD cases that could have been prevented was estimated using the population attributable risk percent.ResultsIn multivariable-adjusted analyses, all six low-risk lifestyle factors—never smoking or quitting not for illness, drinking alcohol weekly, maintaining a healthy diet, having a normal BMI, and possessing a normal WHR—were independently linked to a reduced risk of CKD. Participants with a minimum of five low-risk lifestyle factors demonstrated a 47% decrease in the relative risk of CKD compared to individuals without any low-risk lifestyle factors. Approximately 39.8% (95% CI: 31.5–47.8%) of CKD cases are attributable to an unhealthy lifestyle.ConclusionsA healthy lifestyle significantly reduces the incidence of CKD, demonstrating the long-term benefits of lifestyle changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26728-3.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is among the world's fastest-growing chronic noncommunicable diseases, representing a significant and enduring hazard to human health. Based on the data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 report, the worldwide prevalence of CKD has increased to 9.1%, accounting for over 697 million cases and approximately 1.2 million fatalities each year globally [1]. In China, the burden is notably substantial, as the sixth China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance indicates that approximately 82 million adults are impacted by CKD, presenting considerable economic and social challenges for the healthcare system [2]. With an aging population and a high prevalence of chronic diseases, the prevention and management of CKD has become a significant concern for the public health system.

Consequently, the identification and thorough investigation of potential risk factors for CKD possess considerable scientific importance in preventing the onset of the disease and delaying its progression.Established risk factors, including diabetes and hypertension, are widely recognized. Nevertheless, modifiable lifestyle factors present a vital opportunity for primary prevention. Indeed, individual healthy behaviors—such as abstaining from smoking [3], drinking alcohol appropriately [4], being physically active [5], eating a healthy diet (assessed herein using the Simplified Healthy Eating Index, SHEI, a validated tool adapted to Chinese dietary patterns) [6, 7], and maintaining low body fat levels [8]—have each been linked to a lower risk of CKD.

While the impact of individual lifestyle factors is recognized, recent studies from Western populations, such as those utilizing the Life's Essential 8 (LE8) and Life's Crucial 9 (LC9) scores in the UK Biobank and US NHANES, have begun to quantify the combined benefit of multiple healthy lifestyle factors on CKD risk [9, 10]. However, comprehensive evidence regarding this synergistic effect, derived from a large-scale prospective cohort within the general Chinese population, remains limited. Given the distinct genetic background, lifestyle patterns, and disease burdens in China, it is critical to investigate this association in a representative cohort. We hypothesized that a composite healthy lifestyle score, integrating multiple modifiable factors relevant to the Chinese context, would be independently linked to a markedly lower risk of incident CKD, and that a considerable proportion of CKD cases could be attributed to suboptimal lifestyle behaviors.

Therefore, utilizing data from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) study, this study aimed to: 1) investigate the independent and combined associations of six key low-risk lifestyle factors with incident CKD, and 2) estimate the population attributable risk (PAR) of CKD associated with non-adherence to a healthy lifestyle.Findings from this study are expected to provide robust evidence for formulating lifestyle-based primary prevention strategies against CKD in China.

Method

Study population

The China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) is among the largest prospective studies globally, recruiting over 500,000 adults from five rural and five urban regions in China.The study design and methodologies have discussed this in detail previously [11, 12].In brief,during 2004–2008 at baseline, permanent residents aged 35 to 74 years, without significant disabilities, from all preselected communities or villages were invited to participate in the study. The CKB encompasses 512,724 individuals at baseline, including a small number outside the designated age range, all of whom provided informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention and the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee at the University of Oxford.This study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

In this study, we excluded people with CKD (n = 7574), cancer (n = 2578), or missing BMI values (n = 2) at baseline, resulting in a total of 502,621 participants for analysis.

Assessment of lifestyle factors

LLifestyle data were collected at baseline through a comprehensive electronic questionnaire administered during face-to-face interviews at local community assessment centres. The interview process was conducted by trained, full-time staff with formal health-related qualifications (e.g., as nurses or public health professionals), who were specifically recruited for the CKB study. To ensure data quality and participant comprehension across the ten diverse study areas, all interviewers underwent a standardized, centralized training program, and the interviews were conducted in the participant's local dialect or Mandarin Chinese. This rigorous process was designed to minimize miscommunication and ensure the accuracy of self-reported data.

We evaluated lifestyle status through six modifiable factors: smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) [13–17]. The research questions regarding smoking encompassed whether all participants were smokers, the types and daily quantity of cigarettes consumed by current smokers, and the duration since cessation and reasons for quitting among former smokers. Questions on alcohol consumption encompassed participants' frequency of drinking, the types of alcoholic drinks regularly consumed, and the quantity of alcohol ingested on a typical day over the last 12 months. Dietary consumption was measured using a qualitative food frequency questionnaire. Adherence to a traditional Mediterranean diet was not assessed in this study. Instead, we created a Simplified Healthy Eating Index (SHEI) specifically for the CKB cohort. The SHEI's food components were conceptually informed by proven healthy dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, which has been linked to a lower risk of CKD [18, 19].These principles stress the consumption of nutritious foods (such as fresh fruits, vegetables, legumes, and fish) while limiting less healthy options (such as red meat and processed meals).Our SHEI was based on a nutritional evaluation method previously developed and validated for the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) [7]. As a result, our SHEI rated fresh vegetables, fresh fruits, beans, fish, and eggs as positive, whereas red meat and salted vegetables received a negative rating. In the CLHLS cohort, a higher SHEI score indicated predictive validity due to its strong correlation with lower all-cause mortality. The detailed scoring methodology and point allocation for each food component are provided in Supplementary Files 2 and 3. Physical activity was assessed using an interviewer-administered questionnaire, the questions for which were adapted from previously validated studies and refined after a CKB pilot study [20, 21].This instrument collected detailed information on the type, frequency, and duration of activities across four domains—occupation, commuting, housework, and leisure time—over the preceding 12 months. The methodology for assigning metabolic equivalent of task (MET) values and calculating domain-specific, intensity-specific, and total physical activity levels (in MET-h/day) has been described in detail in prior CKB publications [5, 22]. In a reproducibility sub-study (n≈1,300), the intraclass correlation coefficient for total physical activity was 0.59, indicating good reliability [23].Measurements of human body markers, including height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference, were conducted using standard instruments and methodologies. The BMI is calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared. WHR represents the ratio of waist circumference to hip circumference.

According to prior research, the following are the definitions of the six low-risk lifestyle categories.The low-risk category for smoking is characterized as never smoking or quitting not for disease for at least 6 months. The low-risk category for alcohol consumption is characterized by individuals who drink alcohol weekly. The categorization of ‘drinking alcohol weekly’ as a low-risk factor in this study was based strictly on the observed association with incident CKD risk in our cohort data (see Results, Table 2), where current weekly drinkers showed a lower hazard ratio compared to never regular drinkers. This definition should not be interpreted as a general public health endorsement of alcohol consumption. Our analysis aimed to objectively report the association as it appears in our data, within the specific context of CKD risk. For the purpose of defining a low-risk lifestyle, participants with an SHEI score in the first quartile (highest quarter) of the distribution were classified into the low-risk dietary category.The low-risk category for physical activity comprises individuals who participate in gender-specific median or greater levels of physical activity. The low-risk category for BMI was defined as having a BMI ranging from 18.5 to 23.9 kg/m2 [24, 25]. The low-risk category for WHR, is defined as individuals with a WHR of less than 0.90 for males and less than 0.85 for women [26].

Ascertainment of incident chronic kidney disease

Incident CKD cases were identified through linkage with local disease and death registries, augmented by the national health insurance claim databases, which currently encompass approximately 98% of participants. These databases record detailed information on hospitalizations; approximately 4–5% of participants experience at least one hospital admission annually, with supplementary data acquired through active follow-up.For this analysis, CKD was defined to include diabetes with renal complications (E10.2, E11.2, E12.2, E13.2, E14.2), hypertensive renal disease (I12, I13), glomerular diseases (N03, N04, N05, N07), renal tubulo-interstitial diseases (N11, N12, N13, N14, N15), and renal failure (N18, N19). It is important to note that this case definition specifically excludes hospitalizations primarily coded for acute kidney injury (ICD-10: N17).

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the sample were summarized as percentages for categorical categories and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables.Person-time was computed from baseline to the date of CKD diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the last follow-up (1 January 2017), whichever occurred first.Cox proportional hazards models were employed to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), utilizing age as the underlying time scale, stratified by 5-year age groups and ten survey areas.In multivariate models, the analysis of a single lifestyle factor incorporated five additional lifestyle factors, including age, gender, education, income, and the presence of diabetes and hypertension at baseline.In our primary multivariable models, we adjusted for baseline prevalent diabetes and hypertension to estimate the association of lifestyle factors with CKD independent of these strong clinical risk factors. We also conducted sensitivity analyses without adjusting for them to assess the total effect of lifestyle. Simultaneously, we investigated whether the correlation between lifestyle and CKD risk differs by gender and residence. Furthermore, to analyze the linear trend of these associations, we incorporated the median of each category as a continuous variable in the examination of individual lifestyle factors, and the quantity of low-risk lifestyle factors as a continuous variable in the analysis of the association between the number of low-risk lifestyle factors and CKD. We incorporated interaction terms into the statistical model and used the likelihood ratio test to compare models with and without the interaction terms, assessing the interaction with gender or residence.We assessed potential multicollinearity between correlated variables (particularly BMI and waist-to-hip ratio) by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs). All VIF values were below 2.0, well under the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a substantial concern in our multivariate models.

We estimated the PAR% to quantify the proportion of CKD cases that could have been prevented if every participant had maintained a healthy lifestyle, assuming a causal relationship [27, 28].PAR% provides a unified measure by integrating the effect size of each risk factor, its prevalence, and the potential reduction in exposure. This analysis was conducted using a binary classification of participants into low-risk and non-low-risk categories for each lifestyle factor, and further stratified by gender, residence, and the presence of diabetes or hypertension to evaluate subgroup-specific impacts.

R software (version 4.3.2) was used to conduct all statistical analyses.Statistical significance was set at 2-tailed p < 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with incident CKD in the first 2 years of follow-up and further adjusting for marital status and rheumatoid arthritis at baseline had no significant effect on the primary outcome(Supplementary Table 7;Supplementary Table 8;Supplementary Table 9). In a sensitivity analysis excluding former drinkers (who may have quit due to health concerns), the association between current weekly drinking and CKD risk was attenuated (HR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.73–1.02; Supplementary Table 10). In a sensitivity analysis that did not adjust for prevalent diabetes and hypertension (Supplementary Table 11; Supplementary Table 12), the associations between lifestyle factors and CKD risk were generally stronger than those in the primary model.

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study of Chinese adults, adherence to a low-risk lifestyle—defined as never smoking or quitting not for disease for at least 6 months, moderate frequency of drinking, engaging in high-intensity physical activity, following a healthy diet, and maintaining a normal BMI and a low WHR—was significantly correlated with a decreased risk of CKD. Participants with five or more low-risk lifestyle factors had a 47% lower relative risk of CKD compared to those with none.If the observed association is causal, one-third of CKD in this cohort over a median follow-up time of 10.1 years is attributable to unhealthy lifestyles.

Our study extends the current evidence in several key aspects. To our knowledge, this investigation is one of the first and largest prospective studies to comprehensively quantify the combined effect of six modifiable lifestyle factors on CKD risk in a general Asian population. While prior studies in Western populations have established the value of composite lifestyle scores like Life's Essential 8, our findings provide crucial, population-specific evidence from China, where dietary patterns, physical activity modes, and disease burdens differ substantially [9, 10]. Furthermore, by incorporating both BMI and WHR, we offer a more nuanced assessment of adiposity's role. Most importantly, our estimation of the PAR% provides a quantifiable metric for public health planning, suggesting that a significant portion of CKD cases in China could be prevented through lifestyle modifications.

Our results align with a prior meta-analysis involving 2,755,719 participants, which indicated that heightened vegetable consumption (21% reduction in risk), elevated physical activity (18% reduction in risk), and moderate alcohol intake (15% reduction in risk) mitigate the risk of developing CKD [29].Our findings indicate that while each lifestyle factor had a distinct impact on CKD risk reduction, participants who adhered to a higher number of healthy lifestyle factors experienced a more significant reduction in CKD risk compared to those who followed fewer healthy lifestyle factors.

This study examined the individual contributions of various lifestyle factors and their cumulative effects in a substantial sample. Our findings indicate that smoking, drinking alcohol, and physical exercise are most strongly associated with the incidence of CKD. Both former and present smokers exhibit an elevated risk of CKD, with current smokers experiencing a significantly heightened risk proportional to their smoking quantity, as evidenced by multiple studies [30]. Regarding the categorization of alcohol consumption as a lifestyle factor, we acknowledge that defining ‘weekly drinking’ as low-risk based solely on CKD outcomes is conceptually nuanced and requires careful interpretation.The relationship between alcohol consumption and CKD is intricate. A previous study revealed an inverse association between alcohol consumption and the risk of CKD. The risk of CKD was 0.60 (0.42–0.86) for heavy drinkers (more than 210 g/week) in comparison to nondrinkers [31].Our data indicated a diminished risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) among individuals who consumed alcohol weekly compared to non-drinkers. The sensitivity analysis excluding former drinkers provided important context: the apparent protective effect of weekly drinking was attenuated, and current daily drinking was associated with increased CKD risk. This suggests that former drinkers may include "sick quitters" whose elevated risk influenced the reference group in our main analysis. Our investigation revealed no significant correlation between the risk of CKD and current drinkers, with the exception of individuals who consumed 30–60 g of alcohol daily.It is essential to recognize that even light to moderate alcohol consumption may elevate the probability of adverse outcomes like cancer [32]. Moreover, based on the CKB, studies indicate that male daily drinkers exhibit a 30–40% elevated risk of acquiring alcohol-related cancers and cirrhosis in comparison to non-daily drinkers [33]. Consequently, regarding overall health, we will adopt caution in our advice on alcohol consumption and do not recommend heavy drinking.Concerning the investigation of obesity and CKD, numerous studies have concentrated on the impact of obesity on renal function in CKD patients, but limited research has been performed on obesity and the onset of CKD. A Korean cohort study identified both BMI and WHR as independent risk factors for the development of CKD, with WHR being more significant than BMI due to its indication of fat distribution as a proxy for central obesity [34].

PAR% quantifies the cumulative effect of risk factors and serves to evaluate the prospective influence of preventative efforts on public health. Our research indicates that nearly 40% of CKD incidence is attributed to unhealthy lifestyle, signifying that 40% of CKD cases could potentially be mitigated through the adoption of a healthy lifestyle. A Study have shown that adverse dietary factors, weight, physical activity, social networks, and tobacco use account for up to 13% of CKD incidence in individuals with type 2 diabetes [35]. A study examining vascular cardiovascular disease in the Chinese population presented a comparable definition of a healthy lifestyle, indicating that two-thirds of major coronary events, two-fifths of ischemic heart disease, and two-fifths of ischemic stroke in this cohort could have been prevented through adherence to a healthy lifestyle [36].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively assess the relationship between several lifestyle factors and the onset of CKD in a Chinese population. The study's strengths were a prospective design, substantial sample size, extended follow-up period, and comprehensive adjustment for relevant confounders. Our findings highlight implications for practice, policy, and research. Clinically, they support integrated lifestyle counseling in primary care to address smoking, diet, activity, and alcohol use collectively for CKD prevention. For public health policy, the high population attributable risk suggests that lifestyle promotion should be a national priority, informed by campaigns and environments that enable healthier choices. Future research should prioritize trials of multifactorial lifestyle interventions and longitudinal studies to track habit changes and elucidate underlying biological mechanisms.

Several limitations should be noted in this research. First, the evaluation of lifestyle factors relied on self-reporting at baseline, which is susceptible to measurement inaccuracies and social desirability bias, despite the use of trained interviewers and objective physical measurements to mitigate this. Second, as lifestyle factors were assessed only at baseline, they may not capture changes in habits over time. Third, there may have been an under-ascertainment of pre-existing CKD at baseline, potentially leading to an overestimation of CKD incidence during follow-up. However, risk estimates remained largely unchanged after excluding participants who developed outcomes within the first 2 years.

Conclusion

This large prospective study indicates that abstaining from smoking or stopping without illness, moderate frequency of drinking, engaging in high-intensity physical activity, following a healthy diet, keeping a normal BMI, and achieving a low WHR—can mitigate the risk of CKD.This study indicates that comprehensive lifestyle modifications have a lasting influence on illness prevention.
