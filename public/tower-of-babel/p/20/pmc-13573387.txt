Association of emerging anthropometric ratios with arterial stiffness risk measured by pulse wave velocity: insights from a middle-eastern population
Authors: Mobina Imannezhad, Susan Darroudi, Maryam Saberi-Karimian, Habibollah Esmaily, Mohsen Moohebati, Simin Rashidian, Sara Ghazizadeh, Gordon A. Ferns, Majid Ghayour-Mobarhan, Maryam Alinezhad-Namaghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArterial stiffness (AS), a strong risk factor for atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease (CAD), is typically assessed using pulse wave velocity (PWV). This study takes a unique approach by exploring the relationship between novel anthropometric indices and AS in a large cohort study.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted during Phase II of the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorders (MASHAD) study, a 10-year cohort (2010–2020). Out of the original 9704 participants at the beginning of the MASHAD cohort study, 310 healthy participants, 206 individuals without confirmed cardiovascular disease (CVD), and 307 individuals with confirmed major cardiovascular events were randomly selected. PWV test was conducted using a specialized SphygmoCor XCEL System, and participants were grouped into two CVD and two non-CVD groups based on the median PWV < 6.9 or ≥ 6.9.ResultA total of 823 individuals took part in the study. The CVD group with PWV ≥ 6.9 m/s had the highest mean age of 51.37 ± 7.07 years. Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that with each unit increase in weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) and visceral adiposity index (VAI), the odds of AS increased by 25.7% [OR: 1.257 (1.002–1.576)] and 13.9% [OR: 1.139 (1.022–1.271)] (P-value = 0.048 and 0.019, respectively). Additionally, as lipid accumulation product (LAP) increased, the AS decreased by 0.4% [OR: 0.996 (0.993–0.999)] (P-value = 0.01). Moreover, height and arm-to-height ratio (AHR) resulted in a 3.7% [OR: 1.037 (1.003–1.073)] and 6.4% [OR: 1.064 (1.002–1.131)] increase in PWV risk (P-value = 0.034 and 0.044). In the CVD-, PWV ≥ 6.9 group, VAI posed a 16.4% [OR: 1.164 (1.051–1.289)] (P-value = 0.004) increase in AS with a one-unit increment.ConclusionWWI and VAI showed significant associations with AS in CVD patients. VAI was also linked to AS in non-CVD patients, with a stronger association than height and AHR. This contributes significantly to our understanding of vascular health. Equally noteworthy is the protective association of LAP against AS in non-CVD individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01338-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including heart attacks and stroke, are widespread health issues that pose a significant threat to human life around the globe, with a staggering number of deaths attributed to these conditions each year [1]. In 2021, approximately 20.5 million people died due to CVD [2] and about half deaths in Iran are related to CVD, adding to this statistic annually [3]. Atherosclerosis is a crucial cause of CVD [4], characterized by the accumulation of fat plaques and inflammatory factors [4, 5], leads to the hardening of vessel walls and arterial stiffness (AS) [5], which is a powerful predictor of CVD [6] and endothelial function [7]. Various methods can evaluate vascular status, AS, and endothelial function, including pulse wave velocity (PWV) [6]. PWV measures AS between two points on the vessel [8] and is used to assess AS [9, 10].

The relationship between obesity and CVD is well documented [11]. Obesity can be evaluated using traditional anthropometric indices such as body mass index (BMI), waist-to-height-Ratio (WHtR), and waist circumference (WC) [12, 13]. While BMI commonly reflects overall obesity, WHtR and WC are better predictors of central obesity and visceral fat [14]. Novel anthropometric indices such as body adiposity index (BAI), weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), abdominal volume index (AVI), body roundness index (BRI), a body shape index (ABSI) have been discovered in recent decades and are more effective in predicting visceral obesity and obesity-related diseases [15–19]. Additionally, lipid accumulation product (LAP) and visceral adiposity index (VAI) are two valuable visceral fat indicators and central lipid accumulation markers [20, 21]. It is well-known that there is a remarkable link between VAI, LAP, and cardiovascular events [20–22].

Other anthropometric indicators, like neck circumference (NC) [23], neck circumference to height ratio (NHR) [24], wrist circumference (WrC) [25], hip circumference (HC) [26], and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) [27], have also shown a positive relationship with CVD and mortality.

Previous research has reported a significant relationship between different anthropometric indices and AS [28–31]. However, to our knowledge, previous research has not comprehensively examined the relationship between various novel and height-related anthropometric indices together with the odds of AS, and besides, indices such as HC, WrC, and MUAC divided by height, which are utilized for the first time in the adult population. Previous research on arm to height ratio (AHR), hip to height ratio (HHR) and wrist to height ratio (WrHR) mainly focused on children and adolescents, declaring a significant relationship with determining overweight and obesity [32], metabolic risk [33], childhood hypertension detection [34] and predicting metabolic syndrome, diabetes [35] and CVD [36] in children and adolescent population. This innovative approach is the focus of our study and also fills the research gap, aiming to examine the relationship between different novel anthropometric indices and the AS using PWV.

Method

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study was derived from the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorders (MASHAD) study, a cohort from northeast Iran that began in 2010 and ran until 2020, consisting of 9704 individuals collected from 3 regions of Mashhad city in Iran [37]. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) individuals aged between 35 and 65 years, (II) possessing the physical and mental ability to engage in the clinical examination, and (III) no plans to relocate from the area after the initiation of the study. Exclusion criteria included individuals with incomplete clinical or laboratory data, an acute kidney injury diagnosis at baseline, or a history of major cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction, stroke within the six months before enrollment.

Of the 9704 participants, 1715 individuals were lost to follow-up due to migration, non-respondence, and reluctance. 432 participants passed away, and finally, 7560 remained and continued the study. Of this population, 1,000 individuals were randomly selected due to budget limitations to participate in the study, and ultimately, 823 cases took part (individuals who were excluded had consumed coffee, did not fast, engaged in vigorous physical activity, or sexual activity in the last three days). Diabetes was defined as fasting blood sugar ≥ 126 mg/dl or the use of glycemic-lowering medications or insulin. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or using antihypertensive drugs.

PWV measurement

The PWV test was meticulously performed using the SphygmoCor XCEL System (version 8, AtCor Medical, Sydney, Australia). All SphygmoCor measurements were conducted by a single trained operator to minimize inter-observer variability. The operator underwent training and performed a calibration phase before data collection to ensure consistency in the measurement process. Prior to the test, participants were instructed to fast for a minimum of 6 h and refrain from alcohol, tobacco, or caffeine for at least 12 h [38]. After a rest period of 10–15 min, the test was conducted non-invasively in two steps to ensure the most accurate results. PWV was categorized as median PWV < 6.9 and PWV ≥ 6.9. Since the average values of PWV ​​depend on the recruited population and the mean age of participants [39], as the evidence has been suggested [40], we used our population median PWV value as a cut-off point in the analysis. Participants were divided into four groups: CVD- & PWV < 6.9, CVD+ & PWV < 6.9, CVD- & PWV ≥ 6.9 and CVD+ & PWV ≥ 6.9.

Ethics

Participants provided written informed consent before undergoing the baseline assessment. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Science (IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1403.260 with grant ID: 4022017). Also, study was conducted according to Helsinki Declaration principles.

Anthropometric measurements

Height was measured with a measuring tape with a precision of 0.1 cm, ensuring accuracy without shoes or a hat. Participants were instructed to stand with their feet against the wall and their head in a horizontal position. Weight was measured with the least amount of clothing and with a precision of 0.1 kg using a Seca scale. BMI was calculated using the formula: weight (kg)/height2 (m2). WC was measured with a measuring tape at the narrowest area between the iliac crest and lowest rib. WHtR was calculated by dividing waist circumference (cm) by height (cm). HC was measured with a tape around the widest part of the hip, and HHR was calculated as hip circumference (cm)/height (cm). MUAC was measured with a tape with a precision of 0.1 cm. AHR was calculated by dividing MUAC by height. WrC was measured by holding out right wrist while a tape was wrapped around it with an accuracy of 0.1 cm, and WrHR was computed as WrC (cm)/height (cm). NC was measured above the carotid cartilage in a straight line, and NHR was calculated by NC (cm)/height (cm).

Other novel anthropometric indices were calculated using specific formulas as follows:

BRI = 364.2 − 365.5 × √ (1 − (((WC/2π)2)/[(0.5 × height)]2))

AVI= [2 cm (waist)2 +0.7 cm (waist-hip)2]/1,000.

WWI = WC [cm]/√weight[kg].

BAI= [HC (cm)/height (m) 1.5] – 18.

LAP: Men: (WC [cm] − 65) × (TG concentration [mmol/L])

Women

(WC [cm] − 58) × (TG concentration [mmol/L])

VAI: Men: (WC (cm)/[39.68+(1.88×BMI)] × (TG (mmol/L)/1.03) × (1.31/HDL (mmol/L)).

Women

(WC (cm)/[36.58+(1.89×BMI)] × (TG (mmol/L)/0.81) × (1.52/HDL (mmol/L)).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). For comparing qualitative parameters, the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used, and for comparing quantitative parameters, the one-way ANOVA test was applied. Mean ± SD was used for continuous data, and frequency (%) was used for categorical data. Additionally, normality was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Bonferroni correction test was used to minimize the risk of type I errors and enhance the rigor of the study. Multinomial regression analysis was applied to determine the relationship between anthropometric indices and PWV in four different groups. Data was adjusted for age, sex, diabetes, and hypertension. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

This study recruited a total of 362 males and 461 females who were categorized into 4 groups (total = 823). 39.9% were in the CVD-, PWV < 6.9 group, 9.8% in the CVD+, PWV < 6.9 group, 22.8% in the CVD-, PWV ≥ 6.9 group, and 27.5% in the CVD+, PWV ≥ 6.9 group. The CVD+, PWV ≥ 6.9 group had the highest mean age of 51.37 ± 7.07 years old, and the CVD-, PWV < 6.9 group had the lowest mean age of 45.27 ± 7.09. Significant differences were observed in the prevalence of diabetes and hypertension between groups (P-value < 0.001). Individuals with CVD and higher PWV had the highest values of BRI (5.73 ± 1.9) and AVI (5.73 ± 1.9). Furthermore, non-CVD individuals with higher PWV had the highest means of VAI (2.9 ± 2.1), AHR (0.20 ± 0.05), and WHtR (0.59 ± 0.07). Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Adjusted regression analysis

Table 3 depicts the adjusted model of multinomial regression analysis. After adjusting for different confounding variables, WWI and VAI showed a significant positive association with AS odds and also the odds of CVD (CVD+, PWV ≥ 6.9). For every unit of WWI increment, the odds of AS and CVD increased by 25.7% [OR: 1.257 (1.002–1.576)] (P-value = 0.048) and 13.9% for VAI increment [OR: 1.139 (1.022–1.271)] (P-value = 0.019). Other anthropometric indices showed no significant relationship with higher PWV and AS odds (P-value > 0.05). VAI was associated significantly with higher PWV and AS odds in individuals without CVD. As VAI increased, the odds of atherosclerosis in non-CVD patients increased by 16.4% [OR: 1.164 (1.051–1.289)] (P-value = 0.004). Height and AHR also linked to AS but not as much as VAI, increased the odds by 3.7% [OR: 1.037 (1.003–1.073)] and 6.4% [OR: 1.064 (1.002–1.131)] respectively (P-value = 0.034 and 0.044). In non-CVD individuals, LAP was marginally associated with lower odds of higher PWV and vascular aging [OR: 0.996 (0.993–0.999)] (P-value = 0.01). However, in CVD patients, LAP had a marginal positive association with the odds of AS [OR: 1.008 (1.001–1.014)] (P-value = 0.02). In both groups of CVD-, PWV ≥ 6.9 and CVD+, PWV < 6.9, the remaining anthropometric indices did not show a significant association with odds of AS (P-value > 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between various anthropometric indices and AS odds in a cross-sectional study. The main findings of the study showed that WWI and VAI were significantly associated with AS (PWV ≥ 6.9) in individuals with CVD. The association of WWI was stronger than VAI, with higher PWV (25.7% vs. 13.9%). Additionally, VAI had a positive significant association with higher PWV in non-CVD individuals. Furthermore, height and AHR showed a significant positive relationship with vascular aging and higher PWV in non-CVD patients. LAP acts as a protective factor for the odds of AS in non-CVD patients (0.4%), but it may worsen CVD in CVD + patients with lower PWV. Although our study shares some conceptual similarities with the studies conducted by Ataie et al. [41] and Sobhani et al. [38], there are notable distinctions that highlight its novelty. First, both previous studies were limited to university staff populations and primarily examined surrogate markers of AS—such as central blood pressure and mean arterial pressure—rather than directly assessing PWV as an established marker. Additionally, their anthropometric analysis focused only on a few conventional indices, including BMI, ABSI, VAI, and BRI, without investigating height-related or novel composite ratios.

In contrast, our study was conducted on a broader urban adult population, enhancing generalizability. More importantly, we introduced and evaluated emerging anthropometric indices such as AHR and HHR for the first time in the context of AS. These novel indices may offer distinct predictive value by capturing aspects of body composition and fat distribution not reflected in traditional indices. Taken together, these methodological and analytical differences underscore the added value of our study and its contribution to expanding the body of knowledge regarding non-invasive predictors of arterial stiffness.

Consistent with our results, Xiong et al. reported that WWI was positively associated with ba-PWV in hypertensive patients and considered a good intervention for treating AS [42]. Also, Gong et al. conducted a study on 1406 diabetic patients and revealed that WWI had significant correlation with ba-PWV [30]. Li et al. conducted a study based on the Shaanxi cohort and reported that WWI was a good predictor of AS compared to BMI [43]. Another study by Zhao et al. on 2256 hypertensive and obstructive sleep apnea found a J-shaped relationship between WWI and CVD, demonstrating that higher values of WWI increase the incidence of CVD [44]. A possible mechanism could be defined as WWI is an indicator of visceral fat and eventually fat accumulation [45]. Fat deposition is the accumulation of white adipose tissue and activates inflammatory pathways, producing adipokines such as adiponectin, leptin, and interleukin 6 [46–48]. These inflammatory mediators cause endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, insulin resistance (IR), and incident AS [49]. Additionally, WWI is linked to diabetes [50], hypertension [51, 52], and metabolic syndrome [53], and these cardiometabolic disorders evidently accelerate AS incidence.

A cross-sectional study by Choi et al. on 2647 adults demonstrated that VAI significantly associated with mean ba-PWV and AS and can be a useful tool for CVD risk assessment in the Korean population [40]. Additionally, Ataee et al. reported that VAI was strongly correlated with AS among BMI subgroups in males, measured by cf-PWV among 5921 Iranian adults [41]. Moreover, Bagyura et al. conducted a cross-sectional study on 460 participants, showed higher VAI was associated with higher coronary artery calcium score which is linked to coronary atherosclerosis [54]. There is an evident link between VAI and visceral adiposity in Asian population [48]. Increased VAI is associated with higher amounts of fat storage in body and consequently increase of free fatty acids and eventually resulted in systemic inflammation, insulin sensitivity and vasculopathy [55, 56]. Fat accumulation also leads to IR, which is a factor in atherosclerosis susceptibility. In fact, insulin plays an important role in endothelial function and vasodilation. However, IR leads to hypertrophy, vessel fibrosis, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Additionally, IR can induce inflammation in the body, ultimately leading to AS [57, 58].

Our study supported taller height as a risk factor for AS, increasing the risk by 3.7%. However, El-Bikai et al. declared that AS increases in short-statured older participants [59]. Also, our research revealed no significant association between BMI, WHtR, and AS odds. In line with our study, Hernandez-Martinez et al. showed that WHtR and BMI did not significantly associate with AS assessed by PWV [60]. Other researchers, measuring BMI as an anthropometric parameter, reported no significant relationship between BMI and PWV [40, 61, 62]. In contrast, Lou et al. conducted a cross-sectional study on 3201 diabetic patients and reported that WHtR was positively associated with ba-PWV [63].

On the contrary, our study showed no significant relationship between anthropometric indices such as BAI, BRI, ABSI, AVI and BMI and AS. However, Choi et al. reported that ABSI does significantly correlate with ba-PWV after adjustment for age in the Korean population [40]. Additionally, Zhang et al. demonstrated that ABSI, WHtR, and BRI were significantly associated with AS in the population divided by sex [28]. Also, Li et al. conducted a study in the overweight and obese Chinese population, reported that BRI is closely associated with AS, while ABSI has a weaker relationship with AS [64]. Furthermore, the association of WHtR with PWV and AS has been noted in several studies, showing a strong correlation with AS [61, 65]. These studies were conducted in an age range between 18 and 59 years, categorized based on BMI between 18 and 30 kg/m2, or included special populations such as the hypertensive individuals. In terms of BMI, Sobhani et al. [38] and Zou et al. [66] reported a significant positive association between BMI and PWV. Sobhani et al. conducted their study on a population of university employees, which was different from our study population which was drawn from an urban population across different areas of Mashhad city. Zou et al. carried out subgroup analysis based on BMI, and also did not consider CVD-related comorbidities such as diabetes or hypertension. Our study did not show a significant relationship between PWV and NHR, but a study by Yang et al. was the first to show a positive and significant relationship between NHR and ba-PWV risk in the Chinese adult population [67].

LAP appears to play a dual role in our study. It served as a protective factor against higher PWV and AS odds, but it was positively associated with lower PWV in CVD patients (CVD+, PWV < 6.9). Two studies confirmed a significant positive relationship between LAP and ba-PWV in patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension [68, 69]. Moreover, Papathanasiou et al. studied 770 participants with a median age of 62 years old and reported that higher LAP quartiles were associated with adverse CVD events [70]. No previous study reported an inverse association between LAP and PWV, but a study related to mortality conducted by Bozorgmanesh et al. on the Tehran lipid and glucose study found that LAP was inversely associated with all-cause mortality after adjusting for different confounders [71]. The phenomenon known as the “obesity paradox” may affect our observations. This phenomenon was reported by Gruberg and his colleagues who noted better survival rates of overweight/obese patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention compared with underweight patients. Although obesity is a crucial risk factor for CVD incidence, some CVD- related outcomes may have a better prognosis in overweight/obese patients [72]. The obesity paradox is evident in individuals with class I obesity [73]. This phenomenon may explain differences in LAP results.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has investigated the relationship between HHR, WrHR, and AHR with AS. Therefore, our study was pioneering in exploring this issue. We found no significant relationship between HHR and WrHR with PWV and AS odds (P-value > 0.05). However, AHR showed a 6.4% increase in the odds of AS in non-CVD individuals. As mentioned earlier, studies in the fields of HHR, AHR, and WrHR have primarily been conducted in the children and adolescent population, and their association with overweight, obesity, diabetes, and metabolic status has been investigated [32–35, 74]. Research indicates that the upper arm reflects upper body fat distribution, and evidently, upper body subcutaneous fat is related to visceral fat significantly and is associated with metabolic disorders [75, 76]. Besides, upper body subcutaneous fat is involved in more lipolysis, leading to an increase in free fatty acids in the body [77]. subsequently increases the amounts of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), IR, and inflammation. These factors collectively lead to AS [78, 79].

Strengths and limitations

Our study is the first to comprehensively evaluate the relationship between various novel anthropometric indices with PWV and AS odds in the Iranian population. Additionally, it was conducted in a large cohort database, and some novel anthropometric indices were assessed for the first time. However, limitations include the small sample size (N = 823); secondly, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, we were unable to establish causality. Also, there is a need for future investigations to gain a better understanding of these indices and their associations, as well as the fact that we did not check for other AS indicators such as the augmentation index (AIx).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a significant association between WWI, VAI, and higher PWV regarding odds of AS in CVD patients, and WWI was found to have a relatively better association with AS, suggesting that WWI may be a practical index for identifying AS in this population. In non-CVD individuals, height, AHR, and VAI were significantly linked to higher PWV, with VAI representing the largest effect size among these factors. Additionally, LAP showed marginal association with higher and lower PWV in CVD and non-CVD patients, showing a weak association that warrants further investigation. These findings indicate that these indices may be valuable tools in identifying individuals with higher PWV and a higher chance of AS, potentially assisting in the prevention and management of these conditions. Further research into novel height-related anthropometric indices, such as HHR, AHR, or WrHR, is recommended for better insight and awareness. Additionally, longitudinal study designs should be conducted to establish a causal relationship between anthropometric indices and AS incidence. Moreover, the potential role of sex in the relationship between anthropometric indices and AS should be explored.
