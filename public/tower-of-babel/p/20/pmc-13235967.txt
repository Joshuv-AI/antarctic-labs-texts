Drowning in the silver tsunami: Geriatric patient proportion impact on trauma outcomes
Authors: Sebastian Boland, Liling Lu, Tamara Byrd, David Silver, Joshua B Brown
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The volume-outcome relationship in trauma centers is well established, including geriatric volume. With an increasing geriatric population, it remains unclear whether a tipping point exists for these complex patients who overwhelm centers. Our objective was to evaluate whether the proportion of geriatric patients relative to total volume impacts outcomes.Methods:This retrospective cohort study analyzed patients aged >15 years from the Trauma Quality Program Patient Use File dataset between the years 2017 and 2021. We calculated the center-level annual geriatric proportion of total adult patient volume. Generalized additive mixed models evaluated nonlinear effects between geriatric proportion and mortality for geriatric and nongeriatric patients adjusted for demographics, vitals, injury characteristics, center characteristics, frailty, and comorbidities.Results:We included 3,989,267 patients from 605 centers. Increasing geriatric proportion was associated with improving mortality for geriatric patients until reaching thresholds of 65% of total volume, where mortality plateaued. Importantly, at a geriatric proportion of 61%, mortality for nongeriatric adults began to increase (adjusted odds ratio per 5% increase: 1.04; 95% confidence interval: 1.01–1.08). Geriatric Charlson Comorbidity Index was related to nongeriatric mortality in the highest quartile of geriatric proportion (adjusted odds ratio: 1.18, 95% confidence interval: 1.11–1.25).Conclusion:Center-level geriatric proportion is an important predictor of both geriatric and nongeriatric trauma outcomes. At higher proportions of geriatric patients, the loss of the volume-outcome benefit and even increase in mortality suggests the medically complex geriatric population may overwhelm centers, leading to worse outcomes among all patients. These findings may inform trauma system planning to optimize care of our geriatric and nongeriatric patients.

Introduction

The relationship between patient volume and outcomes (ie, the volume-outcome relationship) has been well documented among a range of health disciplines, and favorable patient outcomes have been associated with higher patient volumes in both the pre-hospital and in-hospital setting for traumatically injured patients, including geriatric patients.1–7 However, most model a linear relationship that assumes infinite increases in volume result in additional outcome benefits, which is unlikely at the extremes of volume. Thus, the limits of this volume-outcome relationship have not been explored, and it is unknown whether there exists an inflection point after which additional patient volume no longer portends improved outcomes or even results in worse outcomes as centers begin to experience resource constraints. Identifying ideal center patient volumes that maximize the benefit of available resources provides stakeholders with critical information for designing highly efficient trauma systems. In addition, defining the volume-outcome relationship at both the highest and lowest extremes of patient volume is useful for establishing evidence-based standards that can be used for trauma center accreditation purposes.

Traumatic injury impacts patients of all ages, with the predominant injury mechanism varying among age groups. Although trauma has historically affected younger populations, the mean age of trauma patients rose from 54 to 60 years from 2000 to 2011.8 Older adults are more prone to low-energy mechanisms such as ground level falls, with falls comprising approximately 66% of all traumatic injuries for patients aged 65–84 years compared with approximately 4% for patients aged 18–44 years.8 They also tend to have more comorbid conditions than younger patient groups, presenting a clinical challenge for busy trauma services. As our population ages, the proportion of geriatric patients continues to grow, indicating that this challenge will likely continue to grow as well. Some trauma centers use age criteria as a part of their trauma activation protocols, with several studies advocating for age-related activation criteria to reduce undertriage in this vulnerable group.9,10 However, the impact of this additional geriatric trauma volume on trauma centers is unclear. Concerns about the ability of trauma centers to care for medically complex geriatric patients are not unfounded, suggesting caution in the widespread adoption of isolated age-related trauma activation criteria. By further delineating the impact of specifically geriatric volume metrics on the volume-outcome relationship present among trauma centers, we can better understand how to best serve this vulnerable population and provide resources to centers that may otherwise be overwhelmed by this growing group. We hypothesize that the volume-outcome relationship for geriatric patients is not linear, with a plateau of the outcome benefit and even potential worse outcomes among high extremes of geriatric volume. We also hypothesize that extremes of geriatric trauma patient volume will negatively impact nongeriatric patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study used the Trauma Quality Program Patient Use File (TQP-PUF; formerly known as the National Trauma Databank) dataset from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2021. This dataset captures patients across a variety of trauma centers (levels I–V) in the United States, providing a heterogeneous national sample for study. This dataset contains in-hospital patient data as well as patient demographics and medical history, which allows for a nuanced view of each patient's hospital course after injury. Trauma center data are also available in this dataset, allowing for comparison among centers. This study was deemed exempt by the University of Pittsburgh institutional review board.

Patients older than 15 years who were treated at a level I–III trauma center after injury were included. Level IV and V centers were excluded as these centers have substantially fewer capabilities than level I–III centers, and most injured patients were treated at a level I–III center. Patients who were missing hospital disposition, underwent withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, or were treated at a level IV or level V trauma center were excluded from analysis. We ultimately excluded patients who underwent withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy to avoid “penalizing” outcomes that are concordant with patient wishes and likely are differentially present in geriatric patients. Before exclusion for mortality analyses, we compared rates between age groups. Our primary outcome of interest was in-hospital mortality rate. We evaluated mortality rates in both geriatric (aged ≥65 years) and nongeriatric adults (aged 16–64 years) to understand the impact of geriatric patient volume metrics on both geriatric and non-geriatric trauma patient outcomes.

Geriatric volume metrics

Each center's total adult volume was calculated as the mean number of patients aged >15 years recorded in the dataset for each center annually over the 5-year study period. Similarly, the mean annual geriatric volume (AGV) was calculated for each center. Using this information, the mean annual geriatric proportion (AGP), which was the percentage of patients over 65 years of age treated by the center relative to all adult trauma patients, was also calculated.

Missing data

All variables except hospital teaching status had missingness of <10% of observations, and therefore complete case analysis with no imputation was performed. Hospital teaching status had missingness of 23%; however, because no variables in the dataset were reliably associated with hospital teaching status to accurately impute missing values, we chose not to impute this variable to avoid introducing systemic bias.

Statistical analysis

Because we hypothesized that the association between mortality and geriatric volume metrics is nonlinear, we used generalized additive mixed models to evaluate the association between AGV and AGP and in-hospital mortality. These models were adjusted for patient age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), Injury Severity Score (ISS), Abbreviated Injury Score for each body compartment, frailty (as represented by the modified frailty index-5), interfacility transport status, admission Glasgow Coma Scale, systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, and each institution's teaching status, trauma level, operational model (for profit, not for profit, and governmental), hospital size, and non-geriatric patient volume. We included a random effect for centers to account for clustering within centers.

We also compared the characteristics of patients and secondary outcomes, including length of stay (LOS) and failure to rescue (FTR, defined as in-hospital mortality after a major in-hospital complication according to prior work11–13) among centers by AGV and AGP quartiles.

In addition, each patient's CCI was calculated using the comorbidities captured by TQP-PUF. Each center's median CCI was recorded for both the geriatric patient population and the whole cohort (including patients younger than 65 years). The relationship between center CCI (both geriatric and whole cohort) and mortality was evaluated using linear regression. All model effect estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A double-sided P value of ≤.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata (18/SE; StataCorp, College Station, TX) or R (version 2024.04.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Sensitivity analyses

A sensitivity analysis that included only level I and II trauma centers was performed as level III centers are less likely to have access to geriatric-specific care teams and palliative care teams that have important capabilities for providing care to injured older adults. In addition, because blunt injury is far more common among older adults than their younger counterparts, a sensitivity analysis including only patients with blunt injury mechanism was performed. Finally, a sensitivity analysis that included patients discharged to hospice in the overall mortality rate was performed to assess differences between in-hospital mortality and total mortality as hospice discharge may include a nontrivial proportion of geriatric patients. Generalized additive mixed models were generated for both geriatric and nongeriatric patients for each sensitivity analysis, similar to the main analysis.

Results

In total, 3,989,267 adult patients treated at 605 centers after traumatic injury were included. Patient characteristics can be found in the Table, stratified by age cohort. Approximately one-third of the population was geriatric patients. Patients in the geriatric population tended to have greater ISS, more comorbidities, and higher in-hospital mortality rates than the nongeriatric adult population. Geriatric patients were also significantly more likely to have an advance directive (7.3% vs 0.4%, P < .001), although the content of the directive was not available in the dataset. Among the entire trauma cohort, geriatric patients were more likely to undergo withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (3.2% vs 1.0%, P < .001), though, as noted above, these patients were ultimately excluded from the mortality analysis. As predicted, greater AGV and AGP both were associated with improved survival rates for geriatric patients until a threshold of 65% AGP or 1,261 AGV was reached, after which the survival rate plateaued, suggesting a loss of the volume-outcome benefit. Specifically, at an AGP of 65% (Figure 2, A), further increases in AGP were no longer significantly associated with reduced odds of geriatric mortality (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] per 5% increase: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.98–1.02; Supplementary Figure S1), whereas at 61% AGP (Figure 2, B), mortality among nongeriatric adult trauma patients began to increase (aOR per 5%: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.01–1.08; Supplementary Figure S2).

In terms of AGV, surpassing 1,261 annual geriatric patients predicted a loss of the volume-outcome benefit for geriatric patients. At this point, further increases in AGV were not associated with a decrease in geriatric mortality (P > .05). At 1,511 annual geriatric patients (Figure 3, A), a 5% increase in AGV was associated with greater odds of geriatric in-hospital mortality (aOR per 5%: 1.01; 95% CI: 1.00–1.01). However, AGV was not significantly associated with nongeriatric mortality at any point (Figure 3, B), suggesting that AGP contributes more significantly to the volume-outcome relationship than AGV for nongeriatric patients.

When comparing the highest quartile AGV and AGP centers with the lower quartiles, only CCI was significantly greater for both high AGV and high AGP centers (highest AGV quartile: coefficient 0.18; 95% CI: 0.17–0.18; P < .001; highest AGP quartile: coefficient 0.21; 95% CI: 0.21–0.22; P < .001). ISS, LOS, FTR, and mortality were not significantly different in the highest AGV or AGP quartiles when compared with the lower quartiles.

The association of center median geriatric CCI and mortality was also explored among centers in the highest quartile for AGP and AGV. Among the highest quartile of AGV, center median geriatric CCI was positively associated with mortality for geriatric patients (aOR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.00–1.10; P = .036) but not for nongeriatric patients (aOR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.98–1.08; P = .255). Among the highest quartile of AGP, geriatric CCI was positively associated with mortality for both geriatric (aOR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.12–1.21; P < .001) and nongeriatric patients (aOR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.11–1.25; P < .001). CCI was also predictive of rates of advance directive (aOR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.22–1.24, P < .001) and rates of withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (aOR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.20–1.22, P < .001).

Among patients treated at level 1 or 2 trauma centers only in sensitivity analyses, the volume outcome mortality benefit was lost at 60% AGP for geriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–1.01; Supplementary Figure S3, A) and 51% for nongeriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00; Supplementary Figure S3, B). In terms of AGV, the mortality benefit was lost at 1,773 patients for geriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00; Supplementary Figure S4, A). Similar to the main analysis, AGV was not significantly related to nongeriatric mortality (Supplementary Figure S4, B).

Among patients with a blunt injury mechanism, the volume outcome mortality benefit was lost at 61% AGP for geriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.96–1.01; Supplementary Figure S5, A) and 54% for nongeriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.97–1.01; Supplementary Figure S5, B). In terms of AGV, the mortality benefit was lost at 1,886 patients for geriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–1.00; Supplementary Figure S6, A). Again, similar to the main analysis, AGV was not significantly related to nongeriatric mortality (Supplementary Figure S6, B).

When mortality was expanded to include patients discharged to hospice, AGP was not related to mortality for geriatric patients (P > .05; Supplementary Figure S7, A). Among nongeriatric patients, the mortality benefit was lost at 50% (aOR per 5%: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.99–1.01; Supplementary Figure S7, B). In terms of AGV, the mortality benefit was lost at 512 patients for geriatric patients (aOR per 5%: 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.00; Supplementary Figure S8, A). AGV was again not significantly related to nongeriatric mortality (Supplementary Figure S7, B).

Discussion

Our study finds that the outcome benefits associated with a trauma center's increasing AGV and AGP plateau and ultimately decrease when reaching higher thresholds for geriatric patients. Importantly, increasing AGP was also associated with increased mortality among nongeriatric patients above a given threshold. These findings were robust across several sensitivity analyses, particularly the impact of AGP on nongeriatric outcomes. This represents 3 novel findings: first, there exists a limit to the volume-outcome relationship for injured geriatric patients. Second, the adverse consequences of an excessively high AGP affect all patients, not only geriatric patients. And finally, AGP may be a more sensitive indicator of the benefit or detriment portended by a center's volume-outcome relationship than AGV.

In addition, identifying the positive association between CCI and mortality among the highest AGP suggests that the medical complexity of these patients may contribute to the worse outcomes among centers with high values.

Our findings align with other similar studies, supporting the existence of a volume-outcome relationship among injured older adults. We expand on our current understanding of this volume-outcome relationship by exploring extremes of AGV and AGP. The volume-outcome relationship among geriatric trauma patients is well documented, with most studies focusing on geriatric volume as a driver of mortality benefit. Several studies have additionally evaluated geriatric proportion as an independent driver of mortality benefit, finding that higher geriatric proportion centers tend to have improved outcomes for geriatric trauma patients.2–7 However, no study has tested the limits of AGV or AGP on the volume-outcome benefit. Our group has identified an AGP threshold of 68% in geriatric patients and only 58% for nongeriatric patients above which outcomes tend to worsen. These thresholds were similar among multiple populations, including patients treated at level 1 or 2 trauma centers and patients with blunt injuries. These findings provide a general target that centers may use to evaluate the degree of benefit (or lack thereof) provided by the volume-outcome relationship.

Prior studies have considered only geriatric mortality, and to date, no studies have tested the association between AGV/AGP and nongeriatric mortality. Our findings that nongeriatric trauma patients are sensitive to high AGP and that nongeriatric patients experience worse outcomes at a lower AGP than geriatric patients underscore the importance of considering the balance of geriatric and nongeriatric patients. Seeking additional resources at centers with high AGP may serve to offset some of these effects.

Center AGP has been previously identified as an important predictor of geriatric mortality in multiple studies of both trauma and emergency general surgery populations.2–7,14–16 The association between AGV and mortality is less clear, with some studies finding an association between AGV and mortality1–4,7,16 while others finding no association.14,15,17 Our study finds that AGP is more sensitive to patient outcomes than AGV, as evidenced by the lack of association between AGV and nongeriatric mortality. Although absolute center volumes have a relationship with outcomes overall, centers with high geriatric volumes may have even greater nongeriatric volumes and therefore would not be considered a “high AGP” center. Using AGP provides information about both geriatric and nongeriatric patients and appears to have a stronger relationship with patient mortality than AGV alone.

Our sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the identified AGP thresholds were similar among patient subgroups, including patients treated at a level 1 or 2 trauma center and patients with blunt injuries. Including hospice discharges in the total mortality resulted in loss of the relationship between AGP and mortality for the geriatric population but maintained a similar threshold for nongeriatric patients. This further underscores the finding that nongeriatric patients may be more sensitive to changes in AGP than their geriatric counterparts.

Our exploratory analysis of high-volume centers, which found that center median geriatric CCI was significantly higher among the highest quartile of AGP and AGV and was associated with mortality in these centers, suggests that medical complexity may be a driver of the increased mortality seen among these centers. Patients with significant comorbidity are more likely to be transported to a higher level of care after injury, concentrating the most comorbid patients within higher volume centers. As a greater proportion of a center's trauma census is made up of geriatric patients, with unique injury patterns, physiologic limitations, and multiple comorbidities, trauma providers may become overwhelmed by this group of patients with complex needs. In focusing attention on this growing group, nongeriatric patients may receive relatively less attention, which may be a mechanism partially driving the worse outcomes identified within this population.

Collectively, these findings have several implications. First, trauma centers should assess and benchmark their AGP and evaluate opportunities to mitigate potential adverse effects among all patients. Although it may not be possible for centers to control the number of geriatric patients injured within their catchment area, regional strategies to equitably distribute injured geriatric patients can be explored within the trauma system. Further, field triage criteria for trauma center transport or trauma team activation criteria based solely on age should be approached with caution.

Institutional efforts to address the growing geriatric trauma population have varied, with some centers implementing geriatric trauma protocols or even establishing dedicated geriatric trauma teams. Results of these efforts have been mostly favorable, with improvements in LOS, geriatric service consultation rates, and nonskilled nursing facility discharge disposition rates; however, an association with mortality reduction has not been demonstrated.18–20 Establishment of geriatric-specific protocols is an important step in standardizing the care of this complex group of patients, but caution must be exercised so that risk of over-correction into a culture of geriatric overtriage is minimized. Hasty attempts to improve outcomes by lowering the threshold for geriatric trauma activation may have the opposite effect, further burdening busy trauma services with an unmanageably high AGP, with a resulting decline in both geriatric and nongeriatric outcomes. Instead, trauma systems should focus on developing evidence-based geriatric trauma protocols that consider a center's resources and experience, coupled with regional efforts to distribute geriatric trauma in a manner that maintains a manageable AGP among centers. Because the geriatric population is only growing, opportunities may exist to collaborate with geriatricians. Developing service models and best practices that may consult with a geriatric service or separately incorporate them in a geriatric service line may help to manage the medical complexity of these injured patients.

Several key limitations of this study should be noted. First, the use of a national trauma database affords access to a large, diverse patient population with traumatic injuries. However, because largely only trauma centers are included in this dataset, patients treated at a nontrauma center after injury are not reflected in the analysis. Trauma center distribution throughout the United States is not uniform, and regions with fewer trauma centers have a greater proportion of injured patients treated at nontrauma centers and therefore are under-represented among our population. Further, the retrospective nature of our study limits our ability to draw causal conclusions about the relationship between AGV/AGP and patient outcomes. Our analyses are adjusted for many important patient factors, but some relevant variables may not be captured. For example, timing of in-hospital complications is not included in this dataset. This limits our FTR analysis, as it is impossible to determine the duration between the complication and mortality. Particularly among geriatric patients, there is a complex interplay around advance directives, withdrawal of care, and discharge to hospice that are difficult to capture in this dataset. As noted, we evaluated the rate of documented advance directives but do not have data on the content of these. We ultimately excluded withdrawal of care but evaluated hospice discharge as a total mortality outcome, although the latter may still represent concordance of patient and family wishes on a longer time horizon than withdrawal of care and thus, we use this as a sensitivity analysis rather than our primary outcome. Finally, center-level information about geriatric trauma services/consultation or care protocols is not available, which may also impact outcomes.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable information about the volume-outcome relationship for injured adults. Continued study of AGP and its relationship to patient outcomes is needed, as well as implementation studies of geriatric trauma protocols to inform best practices in the standardization of geriatric trauma care.

In conclusion, we demonstrate a volume-outcome relationship for geriatric trauma patients as well as the limits of this relationship in terms of not only AGV but also the proportion of geriatric patients relative to total volume, which appears to have a stronger association with patient outcomes than geriatric volume alone. Importantly, this study found evidence of worse outcomes among not only geriatric patients but also nongeriatric patients at high values of AGP. It also identified medical complexity, as represented by CCI, as a potential driver of poor outcomes at the highest AGV/AGP centers. These findings have implications for trauma system resource allocation and catchment strategies to improve outcomes for both geriatric and nongeriatric trauma patients.
