Digital health technologies to improve access to comprehensive primary health care in remote Northern Territory, Australia: qualitative findings from thought leader interviews
Authors: Vishnu Khanal, Emily Saurman, Nicki Newton, Kureisha Wilson, Josielli Comachio, Karina Coombes, Alexandar Puruntatameri, Hilda Bert, Amy Von Huben, Sarah Norris, Meredith Makeham, Michelle Dickson, Sean Taylor, Paul Burgess, Tamsin Cockayne, Jeff Robson, David Reeve, David Edwards, Alison Lloyd, John Wakerman, Tim Shaw, Deborah Russell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting literature shows the potential of digital health technologies (DHTs) to improve access to Comprehensive Primary Health Care (CPHC) by overcoming various challenges to care provision. However, the usefulness of DHTs in the remote Northern Territory (NT) Australian context to support the delivery of CPHC has rarely been explored. This study explores thought leaders’ perspectives about the key challenges to accessing CPHC services and the role of DHTs in overcoming these challenges in remote NT.MethodsIn-depth interviews were conducted with 17 participants between February 2023 and February 2024 who were working or had previously worked in remote NT, including in leadership, governance, and management roles with the NT Health Department or Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services. Thematic analysis was conducted using a deductive approach based on the theory of access.ResultsKey challenges to accessing CPHC in remote NT include workforce issues (staff shortages, high staff turnover, poor continuity of care), the large distances and high costs of providing care, and issues related to how health care services were organised and delivered. Participants highlighted the potential of DHTs for improving challenges relating to provider availability, continuity of care, and high service delivery costs incurred by health services. Challenges to implementing DHT solutions were also raised which were broadly classified as technical and non-technical. Poor digital infrastructure, intermittent connectivity, and the lack of interoperability of systems between healthcare organisations were key technical challenges. Low rates of digital device ownership and low digital literacy of community members, together with limited local availability of skilled staff to support DHT solutions, and the lack of flexibility in the way appointments were organise were identified as key non-technical challenges.ConclusionsThere was optimism that DHTs could substantially improve CPHC access in remote NT. Telehealth, used where clinically and culturally appropriate and in combination with face-to-face care using a hybrid approach, could potentially improve remote patients’ access to a range of healthcare providers, reduce patients’ and providers’ need to travel and improve continuity of care. This will necessitate increased investment in training local Aboriginal people to support communities’ access to DHTs and improve the quality and cultural safety of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03480-6.Keywords: Digital health, Telehealth, Remote health, Primary care, Aboriginal health

Background

Poor access to primary health care in remote and very remote communities is a well-known and long-standing issue in Australia [1, 2]. The small proportion of Australia’s population living in remote areas (1.2%) or very remote areas (0.8%) are widely dispersed across Australia’s considerable land mass. About 32% of the population living in remote and very remote communities identify as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander peoples (respectfully referred to as Aboriginal in this paper) [3]. The Australian Institute for Health and Welfare (AIHW) 2021 statistics show that people living in remote and very remote areas experience 1.2- and 1.5-times higher mortality than those living in major cities [3]. Living in remote and very remote areas is also associated with a higher burden of disease, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension and chronic kidney disease [3]. Comprehensive primary health care (CPHC) is extremely important for delivering equitable preventive, promotive, and rehabilitative health services in a cost-effective way to remote populations [4]. CPHC is the delivery of a broad range of essential healthcare services from the first point of contact to enabling equitable access to health and wellbeing for all residents in the community. It goes beyond just providing clinical services [5]. Challenges to accessing healthcare services in remote and very remote communities include limited health literacy, poor resourcing, inadequate infrastructure (e.g. scant public transport and unsealed roads), reliance on intermittent outreach visits (via hub and spoke service models) for most medical specialist, allied health and General Practitioner (GP) services, and limited resident health professionals available to deliver care on site [1, 2].

It is widely assumed that telehealth can improve access to CPHC, particularly for those living in remote and very remote communities, because of its ability to overcome proximity, transport, and local provider availability issues. Telehealth has been used to improve access to health care and support provider-provider communication during urgent or emergency care situations across remote Australia for decades [6, 7]. In recent years, technical improvements and increases in available bandwidth have enabled patients to access telephone and video calls with specialists, General Practitioners (GPs), and Allied Health Professionals [8–10]. Other digital health tools, such as text messaging, have also been used by Aboriginal populations to access asynchronous health care [11]. However, these initiatives have primarily been delivered to people living in regional and major cities in Australia, with minimal reports of their use in remote and very remote areas [12].

The use of digital health technologies (DHTs), including telehealth, in remote CPHC settings can be resource-intensive and increase the administrative workload for remote staff [8, 13]. Previous studies have highlighted a range of barriers and facilitators to using DHTs from remote CPHC staff members’ perspectives [8, 9]. However, there remains a lack of evidence about the perceptions of health leaders, providers, and managers about how DHTs could be used to improve access to CPHC in remote communities. It is also important to understand how remote health professionals respond to new technologies because their readiness is essential to the successful implementation of any DHTs in the remote healthcare system. Therefore, this study aimed to address these evidence gaps by exploring the perceptions of thought leaders about: (i) the key challenges to accessing CPHC services in remote Northern Territory (NT); (ii) the role of DHTs in overcoming these challenges; and (iii) the key challenges to adopting DHTs.

Methods

This study is part of a larger program of research [14] being conducted with four remote Aboriginal communities in the NT (July 2022 to June 2026) to co-design, implement or optimise, and evaluate various DHT initiatives [15]. The data used in this manuscript were derived from key stakeholder interviews conducted during the initial phase of the research. The research project was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Northern Territory Department of Health and Menzies School of Health Research (HREC Approval Number: 2022–4275). Administrative approvals were also obtained from the Research Governance Office, NT Health (EFILE 2023/8720), and the Boards of participating Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services (ACCHSs).

Study setting

About 26% of the NT population identifies as Aboriginal (compared to 3% across Australia) [16]. In remote and very remote NT communities, where more than 90% of residents identify as Aboriginal [16], most CPHC services are delivered by either NT Health or ACCHSs.

Participants and recruitment

Invited participants comprised ‘thought leaders’ working in leadership, governance, and management roles, with responsibilities or experience in remote NT Health or ACCHSs. Thought leaders were purposively selected for their in-depth understanding of remote CPHC and recruited via health service nomination, self-nomination at professional development events, or nomination by project advisory group members.

Interview guide and data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted either face-to-face or online via Zoom or Microsoft Teams between February 2023 and February 2024. The semi-structured interview guide was informed by Saurman’s adaptation of Penchansky and Thomas’s theory of access [17, 18]. While a core set of questions (provided in Supplementary Table 1) was consistently used, the guide was iteratively refined throughout the data collection process to reflect emerging insights from concurrent data analysis.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed by a professional transcriber [19], and checked for accuracy by the research team. Transcripts were imported into NVivo for analysis, and thematic analysis was conducted following the six steps as outlined by Braun and Clark [20]. A deductive approach was used to analyse thought leader perceptions about (i) the key challenges to accessing CPHC services; and (ii) the role of DHTs in overcoming these challenges [21]. These data were coded to six dimensions of access: accessibility (geography, distance, and transport); availability (supply of health services relative to the need for CPHC services); acceptability (consumer and provider attitudes and beliefs); affordability (direct and indirect costs relative to the ability to pay), adequacy/accommodation (organisation of services), and awareness (communication and understanding of health information) [17, 22]. Four transcripts were independently thematically analysed by VK, NN, and ES, who met frequently to discuss and review themes. VK then analysed the remaining transcripts, with NN and ES reviewing and approving the final coding. All authors reviewed the findings to ensure they were relevant and sound. Any disagreements with coding or interpretation of findings were resolved by discussion amongst the researchers.

Thought leader perceptions of key challenges to the successful implementation of DHTs were thematically analysed using a deductive approach guided by previous DHT-related studies [23, 24]. These themes included technical challenges (digital infrastructure, data structure and heterogeneity) and non-technical challenges (patient factors, health service factors, public and societal factors, and policy factors).

Reflexivity statement

Members of the research team brought expertise in remote primary health care, Aboriginal health, health services research, digital health, public health, and implementation science. The team included Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal researchers and practitioners, some with long-standing relationships with remote NT health services. These backgrounds supported contextual interpretation but may also have shaped assumptions about the value and feasibility of DHT. Multiple researchers were involved from the conceptualisation of research questions and interview tools, as well as in the interpretation of qualitative data, to minimise bias.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Data were collected from 17 thought leaders, of whom ten were female. Participants’ ages ranged from 20 to 60 years. Thought leaders had between three and more than 20 years of experience in the NT healthcare system. Participants’ professional backgrounds were diverse, including rural General Practitioners (GPs), Aboriginal Health Practitioners (AHPs), Remote Area Nurses (RANs), health service leaders, managers and coordinators, and community leaders. Two participants identified as Aboriginal. All participants were still working in the NT.

Key challenges to accessing CPHC and how DHTs can help

Accessibility

Most remote Aboriginal communities have resident primary healthcare staff (usually RANs, sometimes with Aboriginal Health Workers/Practitioners) who work from a local clinic to deliver CPHC. Some PHC providers perceived that getting to a local clinic for care wasn’t too problematic for those who lived in the community:

“I think the distance is not so great in terms of, we have a large number of remote clinical settings for primary healthcare delivery across Northern Territory. …And distance is often breached [overcome] by having a presence in those remote communities, and those probably have their clinics.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

“Because yeah, I think the model, even the fly in, fly out model is not going to capture nearly enough [to meet need for health care].” (participant 3, nurse educator)

However, other participants noted that a lack of access to reliable private transport, poor road conditions, a lack of paved footpaths, and extreme weather can each make it difficult to safely reach the clinic. This was particularly challenging for those with mobility issues such as older people and people with disabilities:

“But there’s a cohort of our clients who have mobility impairment, older people, younger people, mums with prams, just getting there [to PHC clinic] physically is an issue sometimes… There’s also less access [mostly no access] to public transport, and there’s poorer public infrastructure.” (participant 2, allied health professional).

While in some communities, clinics employ drivers to pick people up and bring them to the clinic, which helps improve accessibility, this is resource intensive and not always possible:

“[in] the smaller communities ,.. a driver will go out and try and locate the people within the community, obviously if that’s a viable thing.” (participant 3, nurse educator)

DHTs such as telehealth were viewed positively by community (and providers) for its ability to help overcome distance barriers by reducing the resources, effort, and time required to travel to receive (and deliver) CPHC services:

“So, if it’s not necessary to go to Darwin like, for those appointments, it would be great if we could sit with her in front of a screen.” (participant 17, community leader)

“Oh, I think a lot of telehealth could be done with a lot of the people [who are currently] travelling. Unless they physically need something done in Darwin, like a CT or an MRI or,...But definitely I think it would work well. … this would reduce the person having to travel and stress and all those other things, and cost too.” (participant 1, GP)

Availability

A key access challenge in remote clinics was high staff turnover, which resulted in clinics being understaffed and heavy reliance on short-term agency and locum staff. This, in turn, was viewed as undermining interpersonal continuity of care and the establishment of trusting relationships between PHC staff and patients:

“Continuity is a major one, with very frustrating levels of staff turnover, particularly affecting the nursing workforce.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Thought leaders felt that increased regular employment of trusted local Aboriginal Health Workers/Practitioners, together with greater retention of RANs was needed to improve access:

“The big thing that I think will improve access to primary healthcare for residents in remote communities is having trained Aboriginal Health Practitioners and [Remote Area] nurses available in their community on a reliable basis. It doesn’t have to be fulltime everywhere, but it needs to be regular and reliable.” (participant 1, GP)

The lack of resident allied health professionals in remote communities and reliance on infrequent intermittent outreach visits which were insufficient to meet demand for their services was a major challenge for delivering CPHC:

“There’s many allied health specialties that visit and they have a small number of visits every year. So, there is insufficient time for those allied health practitioners to see all the people eligible for their services.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

DHTs such as telehealth were perceived by participants as having potential to augment the availability of CPHC services, for example, in remote homelands which generally are too small in size to have resident health professionals or a clinic. In these circumstances telehealth could enable a single clinician to provide consultations in numerous communities during a single session. In contrast, in-person consultations are confined to patients within a single community, and clinician time is curtailed by the necessity for the health professional to travel to and from the community:

“Here’s a setting where we can’t have an onsite [GP or RAN]. Like – it’s that coverage – we can suddenly cover all 12 communities at any one [time], all the time. And not just that, we [clinicians] are available.” (participant 6, public health nurse, coordinator)

Telehealth was also perceived as having the capacity to offer almost an instantaneous availability of a PHC provider, without there necessarily being a requirement for additional clinical staff to support the patient:

“So, it [telehealth] gives instant coverage, you don’t even need one of our Remote Area Nurses… I get lots of calls just from a patient, just out there on their own and them ringing through, and then they call me and then we often …have video call.” (participant 6, Public Health Nurse, coordinator)

Acceptability

A key aspect of acceptability raised by participants related to cultural safety issues. Services by PHC service providers not previously known to patients were hindered if there were no local Aboriginal staff available to support initial engagement and communication with patients:

“We [visiting Allied Health Professionals] don’t have the option of using the Aboriginal Health Practitioner as an intermediary or as an initial agent to facilitate an initial meeting, so that’s harder…so, I think a local workforce is really a major problem with the health services in remote.” (participant 2, allied health professional)

“So, it can be a bit daunting for people to go into the clinic – even though it’s their clinic and their community – and see people who they’ve never met before, essentially.” (participant 2, allied health professional)

DHTs were seen as being helpful in building and sustaining positive healthcare relationships. Thought leaders who were already using telehealth spoke about how telehealth helped them maintain relationships with their patients, though :

“when rapport is already in place and I understand that person and their family and their needs, it’s quite easy to have a consultation over digital means…So, the continuity is one area digital technologies can improve.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Participants highlighted that having good interpersonal skills facilitated provider-patient interactions, irrespective of whether the consultation was in-person or using telehealth:

“I guess that the doctors, if they’ve got a good bedside manner and, you know, you can build a rapport with person quite easily over [video call],… you can see their whole face, you can, it’s almost just like having a normal discussion face-to-face pretty much.” (participant 17, community leader)

Clinicians perceived that being unable to perform a physical examination when one was indicated reduced acceptability of telehealth, although workarounds were also suggested:

“I think it [telehealth] would work for everyone, apart from the ones if, like I said, unless the doctor really needs to do an actual physical exam. But we [nurses] can do it for them in front of them…” (participant 13, RAN)

Higher levels of acceptability of DHTs amongst younger populations could be used to engage a population subgroup that was otherwise harder to reach. Sending well-crafted health messages using SMS, for example, to promote youth health and wellbeing:

“If we had an option about sending health promotion messages, again using the text reminder, not doing too much, maybe one message … and in language. Not overdoing it but gently pushing some message. I mean, if we can get through to them, at that [younger] age.” (participant 13, RAN).

Furthermore, community members’ propensity to use certain DHT platforms and tools was seen as something that could be leveraged to improve health and health literacy, for example by sharing targeted text message reminders or videos:

“…So, if we could make the content that we have really connect with people’s phones,… Small videos, YouTube, things that can easily be dropped into community Facebook pages.”(participant 10, allied health professional)

“…text message is very helpful for appointment reminders, and medication reminders. I believe text message to remind appointment has value to reduce DNAs [did not attend appointments] as that helps patients to get organised.” (participant 16, allied health professional)

Affordability

Two aspects of affordability, notably costs to the healthcare system and costs to consumers (relative to abilities to pay), emerged as distinct access challenges. The key affordability issue for the health system was the expense of transporting patients to regional centres or further afield for consultations, investigations and management, which comprised a large chunk of remote PHC clinic budgets:

“And then there’s yeah…sometimes people need to be seen by specialists or be flown out to secondary care… the expenses are quite astronomical and often beyond the budget of any health service or government.” (participant 9, GP)

“…You know, [if] they don’t turn up…we would still have to pay for that bush bus [or flight]. With the price of fuel now, that’s a lot of money out of your [PHC clinic’s] budget, in and out.” (participant 13, RAN).

Funding for allied health outreach services to remote communities was reported to be inadequate and a major barrier to the provision of CPHC:

“I think …They’re just not funded adequately to do everything they’re expected to do or that they want to do for their community, or the community wants from them.” (participant 6, public health nurse, coordinator)

For consumers, affordability of sundry indirect and opportunity costs when travelling for healthcare were a key access challenge:

“Travelling there, coming into town [Darwin]…They don’t have actually any money to them because of that financial stress that they have in community. No money meaning not even at any money, no dollar, no nothing. But traveling to Darwin [city], so if the PATS (patient assistance travel system) bus doesn’t turn up to pick them up, they don’t even have money to get taxi.” (participant 15, Aboriginal Health Practitioner)

In small communities that are far from PHC clinics, such as homeland communities, getting to a clinic is often difficult and even if a vehicle were available resources are needed to cover costs such as fuel and food.

“…from a financial perspective, it depends on how severe your presentation type is. So, somebody may go “Well, actually do I want to spend the money to buy fuel to drive to the clinic or do I want to buy food?” (participant 7, public health professional and manager)

Participants felt that DHTs could not address all consumer affordability concerns. Personal ownership of and access to DHT devices and connectivity may be limited due to poverty:

“…But also, poverty means that people will not necessarily have access to video consultations on their personally controlled device.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

However, participants believed that DHTs could help reduce health service costs, for example by reducing costs of clinician travel to remote communities:

“… if they’re employing a full-time GP to do this, that’s a cost and they’re going to work out the costs of flying out to a community when you could just do a telehealth with them. For us, [saving] 1,200 flight or several hundred dollars [for a road] trip, that’s a significant cost.” (participant 9, senior GP)

While it was widely acknowledged that setting up for DHTs and paying for personnel involved an initial cost, there was an overall agreement that it would eventually improve affordability:

“if telehealth was to come online, and it would pay for itself in the long run.” (participant 15, Aboriginal Health Practitioner, RAN, coordinator)

Adequacy (accommodation)

The way services are organised and offered by the health system was identified by consumers as a barrier to accessing health services. Long waits to see health care providers caused distress and agitation for patients:

“She [mother-in-law] gets agitated when she has to wait too long [to be seen]… So, and waiting, waiting for the doctor, she gets annoyed.” (participant 17, community leader)

Thought leaders highlighted that there was an ongoing mismatch between how the services are organised and people’s ability to use those services. For example, outreach services (e.g., allied health) are scheduled with very limited flexibility. When there are local events of cultural significance such as funerals, visits may need to be cancelled and may be unable to be rescheduled:

“it’s not uncommon for there to be a significant cultural event such as a senior person that’s passed away or a funeral that would normally necessitate the closure of a clinic. And it’s unfortunately not uncommon for that to coincide with a visiting service that only comes once or twice a year.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

From a health service perspective, local health staff coordinate a range of acute and specialty-related health services for patients. This was described to take a considerable amount of staff time and detrimentally affect their ability to provide adequate CPHC services.

“…a lot of them [local PHC clinic staff] I think are quite frustrated with that because they see that they’re spending a large amount of their time coordinating acute services and not delivering primary health services. It’s a bit of a double-edged sword there.” (participant 5, manager)

Thought leaders felt that DHTs could help improve a range of organisational issues. For example, DHTs could improve clinician access to patient health information:

“…having access obviously to the person’s medical health records during that consultation is paramount so that you can understand. Because people may not be able to explain what’s wrong with them or they mightn’t be able to understand what’s wrong with them, to share that information back at you.” (participant 7, public health professional and manager)

Consumers were hopeful that DHTs could be used for scheduling appointments (rather than usual ‘walk-in’ consultations) and thereby reduce patient wait times:

“if you had that scheduled time in a telehealth appointment where you’re just able to sit down and no wait time, it would be really, I think for her [participant’s mother] it would be quite good like…” (participant 17, community leader)

Participants also suggested that DHTs could streamline the referral system using electronic forms:

“It should all just be consolidated and coordinated within one electronic system …” (participant 2, allied health professional)

Awareness

Key issues included low consumer health literacy including and poor understanding of medications or prescribed interventions, and lack of timely and accurate clinical information for clinicians while attending patients.

‘… medication really but they’re all different. Some people are not compliant, they forget to take their medication. Some of them just don’t come for the blood checks…there’s a few that’s just not compliant at all. (participant 12, RAN)

‘…people need to understand that if you get given some tablets, if I only take them for the first day or two and the pain goes away, I might throw away the tablets and go “I don’t need these anymore”…that comes back down to the education and the support’ (participant 7, public health professional and manager)

Poor communication within the health system as well as between the health system and patients was an important barrier to care:

“Then we had an incident yesterday, a lady had to go in for an appointment, but there wasn’t any communication. She had two appointments, so she didn’t even know about that, and we didn’t know about that. So, it was a [poor] communication for the hospital to here [PHC clinic].” (participant 12, RAN)

Both consumers’ and healthcare providers’ levels of understanding of the health system and the many care pathways were also identified as a challenge:

“But also, significant gaps in terms of understanding the healthcare system and how to navigate the healthcare system are often underpinned by different languages and cultural worldviews and pervasive socioeconomic disadvantage.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Thought leaders felt that DHTs could be used to help improve both general and health literacy:

“So, I think that [digital technology] would be a big thing of general education, in just general education, and the second one is health specific education to give people an awareness of what’s the right steps to do.” (participant 7, public health professional and manager)

Mobile applications and text messages could be used to provide reminders to patients about taking their medications and attending appointments, as well as for increasing health literacy:

“My general feeling is that mHealth will be the way to go, that people will have access on their phones, or maybe it’s an app that reminds them about the medication that’s due today or their check-up’s due or the specialist is coming tomorrow and they need to come to clinic, “If you’ve got diabetes, watch this video”. (participant 12, RAN)

mHealth (i.e., use of smartphone to access health care) might be particularly useful for raising awareness of sensitive issues:

“It’s [Technology] great education for young ladies as well,…So, I think the more information, the more technology around that, I think the better, you know. Absolutely. Because the ladies don’t necessarily always want to go up to the clinic … yeah.” (participant 17, female, community leader)

Participants felt that DHTs could improve communications with patients. For instance, a digital community notice board to inform consumers about outreach services in advance of visits and remind them again at the time of the visit:

“I think it really is an opportunity for us to improve people’s access to health services and health information if we do it right. …provide information and videos about services available, people visiting, like what visiting services are coming over the next month and allow people to have something that they can explore and checkout.” (participant 2, allied health professional)

“But maybe if we get free WiFi in community, maybe we can ping out alerts or something, I don’t know. If that someone’s [health provider’s] in town, then we can just tell everyone …” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Key challenges to the successful use of digital health technologies

Participants highlighted a range of technical and non-technical challenges to using DHTs. The following sections briefly outline these challenges, with Supplementary Table 3 providing additional illustrative quotes.

Technical-related challenges

Participants shared their observations that the lack of public and personal digital infrastructure in remote NT is a key barrier to successfully implementing digital health solutions. Patients in remote communities may still be dependent on public payphones, which are not video-enabled:

“A lot of remote health outstations still have basically a free Telstra phone booth.” (participant 1, GP).

“I think a lot of the time the deficiency is in the remote outstations and you’re lucky if you’ve got a phone.” (participant 1, GP).

Participants further highlighted the issues associated with connectivity in remote communities as a major issue:

“But mostly, the barriers with a lot of our earlier forays into particular video conferencing have been the instability of the platforms, the bandwidth available in remote communities, mostly on satellite and terrible connections feeds.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Almost all participants with experience of the previous NT Health telehealth platform (i.e. Cisco telehealth platform) reported challenges with highly restrictive security settings making the system difficult to use, and interoperability issues, for example when communicating with ACCHS:

“So older versions of telemedicine equipment had complicated ways to log in and get through the firewall of NTG, even within the network. And that’s exacerbated when you’re working in a community-controlled setting, trying to get through the NTG firewall to connect and talk to a specialist colleague who works for the Northern Territory government.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Non-technical challenges

Poor digital literacy was perceived as a key barrier affecting remote patients’ ability to benefit from DHTs:

“And then there’s digital literacy. So very familiar with Aboriginal friends that I’ve known and very vulnerable to all sorts of digital literacy issues, whether that be scams or viruses, or content that’s not appropriate. As you know, it’s very overwhelming.” (participant 11, GP and senior manager)

Significant health system-specific barriers were also noted, with time-consuming processes deterring staff from using telehealth and heavy workloads limiting their ability to take time to improve workflows and processes:

“They [the PHC clinic staff] know a particular process [to attend telehealth] is terrible. They don’t like it, but they don’t have capacity to just stop for half an hour and say, “This process is rubbish. Well, how are we going to change it?” That’s what people who are overwhelmed do. They don’t have time to stop and say, “Let’s fix this.” (participant 5, manager)

Discussion

This study examined the key challenges to accessing CPHC, how DHTs could help improve access and key challenges to the successful use of DHTs in remote NT. Findings were presented using six dimensions of access: accessibility, availability, acceptability, affordability, adequacy/accommodation, and awareness. Together, these findings provide insight into the complex interaction between workforce, geography, infrastructure, cultural factors, and digital capability in shaping access to care in remote NT settings.

Accessibility

DHTs are well-suited to address critical accessibility issues by reducing the need for patients and providers to undertake lengthy travel across NT’s vast landmass to seek and provide CPHC, respectively. This is particularly important for Aboriginal people living in very remote homelands, where there is generally no local community PHC clinic nearby. Accessibility to CPHC is further challenged by a lack of private and public transport from and within communities and homelands. This limits people’s ability to independently travel to their local clinic as well as to regional hubs where GPs and allied health services are located when not providing outreach. Our findings are consistent with other studies from the NT which have reported that telehealth increases health service accessibility in remote Aboriginal settings [8, 25]. Telehealth in these studies was used to complement face-to-face services and reduce the need to travel and discomfort and associated with the travel.

Availability

Primary health care staff availability was a major issue affecting access to CPHC services. High staff turnover was associated with low interpersonal continuity of care which undermines development of trusting patient-provider relationships. This is consistent with previous research in remote NT quantifying extremely high remote staff turnover rates in both NT Health and ACCHS clinics and impacts on community members’ engagement with PHC services [26–28]. Our findings showed that thought leaders were optimistic that DHTs can help improve the availability of CPHC from GPs and Allied Health Professionals in remote NT communities. This, too, is consistent with findings from an earlier study from the NT [25], which demonstrated that telehealth helped improve both GP availability and continuity of care. Telehealth also reduced travel time for GPs which increased time available for consultations. Low availability of trusted local Aboriginal staff members in remote communities also meant that cultural and language support may not be available during consultations. This aligns with other research showing that the vast majority of remote community members speak English as a secondary language and find it difficult to attend telehealth with their providers unless a support person is present [8, 29].

Affordability

Patients and health services struggled with affordability of costs associated with travelling for care. For patients, this could mean a trade-off between seeking health care and being able to feed their family. Other studies have similarly revealed how poverty, together with higher living costs in remote communities can detrimentally affect health care [30, 31]. There is also a substantial body of literature which demonstrate that low income households, especially those living in remote locations, are forced to spend more on their electricity, phone bills and other utilities [32]. This means that even smaller incidental costs for care can be a significant barrier. Study participants perceived that DHTs have the potential to reduce health system costs associated with accessing health care in remote areas. This is consistent with the NT virtual care strategy (2023) which suggests that 14% of visits could be delivered using telehealth, thereby reducing by $21 million health-related travel costs in the NT each year [33]. The findings are also aligned with evidence that telehealth can also reduce personal travel costs, including fuel, accommodation, and lost wages due to absence from work, and help reduce other indirect costs, such as having to arrange for someone to look after kids or pets [6, 9].

Acceptability

Thought leaders indicated that acceptability of CPHC required that care was culturally appropriate, supported by local Aboriginal staff and provided by someone known to the patient. Extant literature suggests, similarly, that health services are acceptable when they are delivered close to home, by someone community-members know and trust, and when there is a support person (for interpretation of language or medical jargon) so the patients can follow up with questions [8, 34]. In our study, thought leaders identified that telehealth could help improve acceptability as it enabled clinicians to maintain relationships with patients using a hybrid model of care. Other studies have likewise found that video conferencing supports continuity of care [25, 35]. However, optimising acceptability of DHTs will require increased local Aboriginal workforce development in remote communities so that Aboriginal health workers are available to support culturally safe and effective communication between patients and providers [36].

Adequacy (accommodation)

Health system processes, whereby remote health services generally offered ‘walk-in’ consultations, were problematic for patients because of the long waits often required before they were seen. This aligns with Liddle et al.’s [27] findings that wait times negatively impacted community members’ experiences accessing their local clinic. Inflexibility in the scheduling of intermittent outreach services was also a challenge reported by thought leaders, as outreach teams couldn’t easily pivot to a different date if visit dates became culturally inappropriate. This could mean that patients had to wait many months until the team’s next outreach visit. Other studies similarly report access to Allied Health Professionals in remote communities is challenging and supported by flexible service delivery [27, 37]. Participants felt that DHTs could help address service organisation issues by facilitating improved care coordination, reduced paperwork, easier sharing of medical information and greater flexibility of service delivery. Previous studies [10, 25] from remote NT communities have reported that telehealth enabled shared decision-making with families, whilst electronic medical records (or eHealth records) supported clinical decision-making and promoted continuity of care [38].

Awareness

Low consumer health literacy, suboptimal adherence to the recommended management plan, and limited provider consumer understanding of different care pathways within the health system were stated as key awareness issues. Thought leaders perceived that DHTs could play a significant role in improving communication. This finding is similar to those from a recent systematic review of global literature, which reported that DHTs such as telemedicine were likely to increase interprofessional communication and collaboration among different health groups [39]. Improving access to health information using DHTs was regarded as essential to uplifting health literacy and the understanding of complex health systems. In the NT and very remote Australian context, telehealth was also noted as beneficial in supporting the ongoing education of the remote clinic-based staff when they attend telehealth with specialists and other clinicians [8, 9]. Such consultations offer a unique learning opportunity that is important given the generalist nature of remote health care.

Challenges to the successful use of digital health technologies

A number of technical and non-technical challenges, including poor digital infrastructure, intermittent device ownership and health system readiness, were highlighted by participants. While these challenges are not unique to the digital health space [8, 13, 23, 40] they must be taken into account and addressed proactively for sustainable DHT implementations to improve health service delivery in remote settings.

Given the lack of transport within and to the community, limited personal vehicle and digital device ownership in the remote NT communities, on-the-ground coordination to support telehealth was identified an important aspect to improve access to health services, including those delivered via DHT which is comparable to the findings from previous studies [8, 9]. At times, the clients were also not comfortable talking to someone on the screen, had language barriers, had little established relationships with the clinicians in the session, or had technical issues [8, 9, 41]. The remote clinic staff would have to set up telehealth and sit with the clients to assist with the consultation. This would take considerable time and resources away from a regular working day [8]. Therefore, for digital technology-enabled care to work well in the remote NT communities, the patient-end support needs to be well-resourced; and proactive steps need to be taken to curtail the impact of increased workload on local clinic staff [40–42]. A further characteristic of the current digital health models in the NT remote context is their dependence on champions where an entire model is dependent on one or two personnel. When these personnel leave their job or are transferred to another location, use of DHTs either ceased or slowed down until a person more supportive of DHTs restarted the initiative all over again [43]. This indicates that a systems level approach to implement and sustain telehealth is essential.

Implications of current findings

Technology-enabled models of care offer substantial opportunities to improve access to CPHC for diverse remote NT populations through a range of impacts on accessibility, availability, affordability, acceptability, adequacy (accommodation) and awareness. There are a number of implications of the current findings. First, the successful implementation of DHTs will require remote PHC workforce changes (new or redesigned roles such as coordinators; increased Aboriginal workforce pathways; increased availability/capacity of PHC staff) to support community-level consumer engagement. Second, technologies should be easy to use for both primary health care clinic staff and patients (or families who help patients) so the use of DHTs can be sustained in the long run. Third, any resources saved through telehealth should be reinvested into local clinics to strengthen patient support at the community level. This should be linked with capacity building of the primary health care workforce and improving the institutional capacity of the PHC clinics/services.

Fourth, further research is needed to assess the economic impacts of telehealth, including potential unintended consequences for remote health infrastructure and workforce sustainability. Finally, when adopting technology-enabled models of care, it is essential that DHTs are not used as a short-term or “band-aid” solution to the remote health workforce crisis, do not undermine efforts to build and sustain a critical mass of the remote workforce, and do not divert resources from essential infrastructure in remote communities, including connectivity, health facilities, and transport.

Study limitations

While this study included key stakeholders working in remote health across the NT, in both government and the Aboriginal community-controlled sector, and purposively included strong representation from clinicians, leaders and community members, we acknowledge that there is substantial variation across remote Aboriginal communities in Australia. This includes difference in language, culture, kinship groups, education attainment, and personal and public access to infrastructure, as well as regional and jurisdictional differences in policy and health service conditions. Therefore, the findings may not be generalisable to other remote settings in Australia (and may even not apply to some NT remote communities). A further limitation was that thought leaders tended to delve deeper into the issues that were closely related to telehealth (or mHealth) rather than to broader opportunities to use DHT to improve CPHC (e.g. use of artificial intelligence, use of remote sensors etc.). This may reflect constraints related to our sampling strategy, however, it was also our intention to use the information from these interviews to co-design digital health interventions in remote communities, so that may have also influenced participant responses.

Conclusion

This study adds to existing knowledge by examining key challenges in accessing primary health care services in remote NT communities and how DHTs could address some of these challenges using a framework comprising six domains of access. The study also explored key challenges to using DHTs to deliver primary health care services in complex cross-cultural environments such as those found in remote NT. Telehealth, used where clinically and culturally appropriate and in combination with face-to-face care using a hybrid approach, could potentially improve remote patients’ access to a range of healthcare providers, reduce patients’ and providers’ need to travel and improve continuity of care. While it was the general perception that DHTs could help reduce overall health system expenditure, it was suggested that increased resourcing of PHC was needed in remote communities, as local teams were needed to support DHT use at the patient-end. This will necessitate increased investment in training local Aboriginal people to support communities’ access to DHTs and improve the quality and cultural safety of care. Despite their widespread acceptance of technology-enabled models of care, participants cautioned that DHTs should not be viewed as a single solution for the broad range of CPHC access issues in remote NT communities, and that challenges of using DHTs exist across various layers of health service delivery. Nevertheless, there was optimism that DHTs could substantially improve healthcare access in the NT.
