Spinal Subdural Hematoma After Dual Antiplatelet Therapy
Authors: Nitharsha Prakash, Vinod Kumar Balakrishnan, Sankaran S. Ramesh, Preetam Krishnamurthy, G. Sudhir, Kailash Karthik, Sindhu Reddy Dwarampudi, Harilalith Kovurireddy, Adhiyaa N.S.S., Sharath Shanmugam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNontraumatic spinal subdural hematoma is an extremely rare occurrence. This case highlights the challenging management strategy for this situation.Early Reports SummaryA 77-year-old woman developed nonspecific back pain after percutaneous coronary intervention, with progressive neurological deficits. Imaging demonstrated subdural hematoma in the lumbar spine. She was managed with steroids and withholding of antiplatelet drugs.DiscussionA shared-decision making approach after informing the patient of the risks and benefits, along with multidisciplinary support, was crucial in reaching the desired outcome.NoveltyThere are only 3 prior case reports of similar situations; none of them describe subdural hematoma, and there is no clear management directive.Take-Home MessagesVigilance is needed regarding atypical bleeding presentations after percutaneous coronary intervention. A patient-centric, multidisciplinary approach enabled optimal outcomes in this situation.

Bleeding complications secondary to dual antiplatelet therapy after coronary interventions are reported to have an estimated frequency of 1.7%.1 Most of the bleeding occurs in access sites, although they can occur anywhere. We describe a patient who developed bleeding in one of the most unusual sites, thereby complicating management. This report highlights the need for high clinical suspicion for bleeding, even when presented with unrelated symptoms.Take-Home Messages•Spinal subdural hematoma, albeit rare, can occur after PCI, manifesting as low back pain, loss of bladder sensation, weakness of limbs, respiratory muscle weakness, and numbness based on the level of the lesion.•This case highlights the role of a multidisciplinary, patient-centric approach in choosing a management strategy when a high risk of adverse prognosis is anticipated.

Case Summary

Our patient, a 77-year-old woman with a history of diabetes, systemic hypertension, and dyslipidemia, presented with chest pain and shortness of breath on exertion over the previous 10 days. On presentation, the patient was hemodynamically stable, and systemic examination was unremarkable. Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm, with T-wave inversions in V4 to V6. Echocardiogram showed normal chamber dimensions, mid and apical inferior, inferolateral wall hypokinetic, mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction with grade I diastolic dysfunction (ejection fraction: 50%), with no significant valvular lesions. Serial cardiac enzymes were within normal limits. The patient was diagnosed with coronary artery disease–acute coronary syndrome–unstable angina.

In view of the patient's clinical picture, she underwent coronary angiography, which showed double-vessel disease (distal left anterior descending artery: 80% stenosis, proximal left circumflex artery: 80% disease). Successful percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was performed from the proximal left circumflex artery to the major obtuse marginal branch using a 2.75 × 18 mm drug-eluting stent, and successful plain-old balloon angioplasty was performed to the distal left anterior descending artery.

Postprocedure, the patient was initiated on clopidogrel 75 mg once daily and aspirin 75 mg once daily along with other medications. Adenosine diphosphate aggregometry was reported as 61.4, after which clopidogrel was switched to ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily. The patient remained stable, with no new episodes of chest pain. Screening echocardiography at this time showed no mechanical complications and no pericardial effusion. The patient initially experienced some low back pain postprocedure, and this was managed with analgesics.

On day 2 after PCI, the patient's back pain persisted. Computed tomography aortogram was performed, and dissection of the aorta was ruled out. On day 3, the patient noticed difficulty in passage of urine and weakness in the left lower limb. Upon consulting the neurology team, the patient was advised for magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and whole spine. Both ticagrelor and aspirin were promptly discontinued. The clinical findings were discussed with a spine surgeon, a neurologist, and the patient attenders. Further management along the lines of decompressive surgery versus medical management, as well as the risks, benefits, and outcomes anticipated in both therapeutic options were discussed, and the patient attenders opted for a conservative line of management. Hence, pulse dose of steroids was given. The patient was continued on dexamethasone in view of hematoma over the next 3 days and was changed to oral therapy along with limb physiotherapy. Over the course of treatment, there was a reduction in focal neurological deficit, and she was able to walk with support, with recovery of power of the left lower limb. In consensus with the neurologist and the spine surgeon, the patient was reinitiated on single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 75 mg once daily).

At 2 months after discharge, the patient showed complete neurological recovery, she was able to comfortably walk without support, and her bladder and bowel functions returned to normal. She is still on single antiplatelet therapy, with no episodes of angina or shortness of breath.

Discussion

Acute spinal subdural hematoma is an extremely rare disease that can result in devastating neurological dysfunction. It is usually caused by trauma, spinal surgery, or lumbar puncture. It can also spontaneously occur with arteriovenous malformations, neoplasms, and coagulation disorders. In this report, our patient had no such etiology, but the hematoma merely occurred after initiation of aspirin and ticagrelor, in spite of the cautious choice of antiplatelet agents considering her age. Generally, management is either with surgical decompression or a conservative approach, with more favorable outcomes from surgical decompression.2,3

However, in our patient post-PCI, the decision for continuation of antiplatelet therapy as well as the management of the hematoma became a complicated one. With regard to antiplatelet therapy, there was a need to balance the risk of in-stent thrombosis versus worsening neurological dysfunction. It was decided to de-escalate to single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin) and wait and observe.

Regarding neurological outcomes, the risks of neurological intervention from the use of antiplatelets in the preceding days were assessed. An informed decision was made after having explained the consensus of the multidisciplinary observation, including the risks and benefits of the procedure to the patient. It was decided to go with the conservative approach, and this patient responded well.

Novelty of the Submission

There are only 3 published reports of similar cases.2, 3, 4 Two of the cases opted for surgical intervention and one was managed conservatively. Two of the cases opted for surgical intervention and achieved optimal outcomes. None of the 3 cases are reports of subdural hematoma, hence this case is one of the first reported to our knowledge.

Future Directions

This case underscores the need for heightened awareness toward atypical bleeding complications, especially in the elderly. Reporting of similar cases should be encouraged to aid in making informed evidence-based decisions in the management of such cases.

Conclusions

This case presented a unique challenge with regard to the identification of bleeding secondary to antiplatelets from an uncommon site. Although it manifested as mild pain in the beginning, it was only identified when neurological deficits where noted. This calls for a higher degree of suspicion when symptoms that occur uncommonly after PCI are noticed. It also highlights the role of a multidisciplinary approach in choosing the management strategy in a patient-centric manner, which helped us reach an optimal outcome.
