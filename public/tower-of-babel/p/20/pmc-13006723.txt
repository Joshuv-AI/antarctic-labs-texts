Quantitative microbial risk assessment with microbial source tracking for enteric pathogens in southwest Wisconsin private wells
Authors: Tucker Burch, Joel P Stokdyk, Joe Heffron, Sarah A Opelt, Aaron D Firnstahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrivate wells supply drinking water for many households, and their contamination by fecal microbes presents a risk of acute gastrointestinal illness (AGI). Risk is thought to vary by contaminating fecal source, but specific associations with fecal source are unknown for most private wells in the United States. This study characterizes AGI risk in Grant, Iowa, and Lafayette counties in southwestern Wisconsin. AGI risk was assessed for 10 viral, bacterial, and protozoan pathogens detected in private wells using quantitative microbial risk assessment. Exposure assessment was based on sampling 138 private wells in the study area, and risk was associated with fecal source by microbial source tracking (MST). Median Monte Carlo estimates indicated 4450 AGI cases/year in the study area associated with drinking water from contaminated private wells (95% confidence interval: 90–37,990). Most annual cases were associated with detection of human MST markers, including median estimates of 2550 associated with only human markers and 880 with human and livestock MST markers detected together. Note that 50 AGI cases/year were associated with detection of only livestock markers, and nearly 1000 cases were estimated to occur in the absence of detectable MST markers. This study characterizes AGI risk for households served by private wells in southwestern Wisconsin. It illustrates that human fecal sources can predominate risk associated with drinking water from private wells, even in a rural region characterized by substantial agricultural activity. MST can characterize the relationship between risk and fecal source; contributions of fecal sources to risk cannot be assumed from land use.

INTRODUCTION

Private wells are an important source of drinking water for many households, including 23 million in the United States, which is approximately 17% of all US households (Murray et al., 2021). Private wells are especially important in rural areas, where they often provide the primary source of water for household use. Agricultural and residential land use often overlap in rural areas, which creates potential for contamination of private wells by fecal pathogens (Borchardt et al., 2021). Fecal sources in these settings include effluent from household septic systems that infiltrates through the soil profile to the aquifer, nearby livestock manure lagoons, and land‐applied livestock manure (Borchardt et al., 2021; Stokdyk et al., 2025). Enteric pathogens from these sources can be transported through the soil profile by precipitation and groundwater recharge, contaminate the underlying aquifer, and present a risk of acute gastrointestinal illness (AGI) when consumed in drinking water from private wells (Burch et al., 2021). Water quality in private wells is not regulated at the federal level in the United States, and many private well owners consume their tap water without prior treatment for microbial contaminants (Lavallee et al., 2021; Malecki et al., 2017).

Private well water has been linked to childhood diarrhea, enteric Salmonella infections, and emergency department visits for AGI (Borchardt et al., 2003; DeFelice et al., 2016; Denno et al., 2009), and consumption of untreated groundwater is a risk factor for waterborne disease (Wallender et al., 2014). However, the magnitude of this risk is unknown for most areas served by private wells in the United States. To our knowledge, only three US studies have been published. These studies report rates of 3.2 (DeFelice et al., 2016) and 26 (Burch et al., 2021) AGI cases, and 58 gastrointestinal infections (Gitter et al., 2023) per 1000 people per year associated with drinking water from private wells. However, with few studies from which to draw conclusions, our current understanding of AGI risk in US private wells is unclear and incomplete. This is particularly relevant given the necessarily limited scope of each study. DeFelice et al. (2016) address only the most severe cases of AGI, Burch et al. (2021) limit their estimates to a geographic region known for its combination of fractured bedrock aquifer, thin overlying topsoils, and high livestock density, and Gitter et al. (2023) focus on flood‐impacted wells.

This study characterizes AGI risk in the three‐county region of Grant, Iowa, and Lafayette counties in southwestern Wisconsin. These counties are home to 90,322 residents collectively, where 45% rely on private wells (18,000 private wells total) (USEPA, 2023). Land use is dominated by agriculture, particularly cattle but also pigs and poultry, and all participating households were served by a private septic system or holding tank (Stokdyk et al., 2025). Fractured limestone and dolomite form the uppermost geologic layer in most of the area, and wells access groundwater from two bedrock aquifers (an upper and lower aquifer) that are separated by a shale aquitard (Stokdyk et al., 2025). Study objectives were to (1) assess AGI risk and total AGI cases associated with 10 viral, bacterial, and protozoan waterborne pathogens in private wells, (2) categorize AGI risk and cases by contaminating fecal source (human sewage and livestock manure from cattle and pigs), and (3) categorize AGI risk and cases based on the aquifer supplying private wells (upper vs. lower). AGI risk and cases were estimated using quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA) based on site‐specific quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) data for pathogen detections reported by Stokdyk et al. (2025). Study results inform regional public health and water quality interests and contribute to our basic understanding of AGI risk in private wells and its distribution with respect to fecal source and hydrogeology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Overview

QMRA included hazard identification, exposure assessment, dose‐response assessment, and risk characterization. Hazard identification and exposure assessment were based on pathogen data for the study area summarized in Stokdyk et al. (2025), who conducted two phases of sampling in the study region. The first phase consisted of two synoptic events to assess nitrate, coliform, and Escherichia coli contamination (November 9–10, 2018 and April 11–12, 2019). Homeowners collected samples from a sterilized tap outdoors or near their pressure tank after flushing for 5 min (Stokdyk et al., 2025); these samples (n = 840) were analyzed for nitrate and the fecal indicator bacteria (FIB) total coliforms and E. coli. The second phase consisted of four follow‐up events (April 9–13, August 5–8, and November 4–7, 2019, and March 3–6, 2020) to assess pathogen contamination in a subset of wells found positive for high nitrate (>10 ppm) and/or FIB in the first phase. Pathogen concentrations were measured in pairs (n = 138 each) of large‐volume (range = 492–1374 L) dead‐end ultrafiltration (DEUF) and small‐volume (1 L) grab samples collected by trained technicians from flame‐sterilized taps after flushing for 1–2 min. The QMRA used pathogen concentrations from DEUF samples because these samples accounted for the majority of pathogen detections (82 of 88 total detections) (Stokdyk et al., 2025); pathogen concentrations from 1‐L grab samples are analyzed separately for comparison purposes only. Additional details of sample collection and analysis are reported in Stokdyk et al. (2025).

QMRA calculations were stratified by fecal contamination source, where contamination source was identified based on the co‐occurrence of detected pathogens with microbial source‐tracking (MST) markers specific to human, bovine/ruminant, and pig fecal sources. Due to few pig detections, the latter two MST categories were combined into a single livestock category. Furthermore, some wells were contaminated by a mixture of human and livestock fecal sources, while others contained pathogens but no detectable MST markers from any source. Thus, fecal source categories in the QMRA included human only, livestock only, human and livestock together, and none detected.

QMRA calculations were also stratified by the source of well water, which was determined by the position of the well's open interval (area of the well borehole that is not cased, where water enters the well) (Stokdyk et al., 2025). Well construction reports were obtained for 98 study wells, and when available, wells were classified by the aquifer(s) in which the open interval occurred, either upper, lower, or both. Note that 37 wells with an unknown open interval aquifer and three wells constructed in sand and gravel were excluded from this analysis.

Hazard identification

The hazard considered was AGI due to waterborne pathogens detected in private wells from the study area. Pathogens detected and included in QMRA were human adenovirus, human enterovirus, norovirus (genogroups GI and GII combined), Campylobacter jejuni, enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (EPEC), Salmonella spp., Cryptosporidium hominis, Cryptosporidium parvum, ungenotyped Cryptosporidium spp., and Giardia. Pathogens were detected by qPCR as described previously; qPCR inhibition was evaluated in every sample, and 95% limits of detection were determined for each assay (Stokdyk et al., 2025).

Additional qPCR analyses were conducted for QMRA to identify Cryptosporidium species in samples testing positive for the genus‐level assay (n = 48) (Mary et al., 2013). Species included in these follow‐up analyses were C. baileyi, C. bovis, C. galli, C. meleagridis, C. suis, and C. ubiquitum (Li et al., 2015; Mary et al., 2013; Nakamura et al., 2014; Stroup et al., 2006). None of these species were detected.

Exposure assessment

Exposure assessment estimated mean daily pathogen dose and the number of study area residents exposed to contamination for open interval aquifer and fecal source, with analysis of these two factors treated independently. It was assumed that residents unexposed to contamination were at zero risk for exposure to fecal‐borne pathogens in private wells.

The number of residents exposed was estimated as Ni = W × F × Si × T, where W is the number of private wells in the study area (17,985) (Murray et al., 2023), F is the proportion of private wells contaminated by coliforms and/or nitrate, Si is the proportion of F associated with fecal source i (or aquifer i), and T is the number of people served per well per day (2.28) (Murray et al., 2023). The quantity F was determined from Stokdyk et al.’s (2025) first phase of sampling, combining independent estimates for fall (F fall = ∼0.42) and spring (F spring = ∼0.27) with equal probability to represent the entire year (Table S1). The quantity of Si for open interval aquifer was determined based on analysis of well construction reports. The quantity of Si for fecal source was determined based on Stokdyk et al.’s (2025) second phase of sampling, where MST concentrations were quantified by qPCR. Human‐specific MST targets were adenovirus groups A–F, human enterovirus, Bacteroidales‐like Hum M2, human Bacteroides (HF183), and C. hominis (Green et al., 2014; Jothikumar et al., 2005; Leon, 1990; Mary et al., 2013; Monpoeho et al., 2000; Shanks et al., 2009). Bovine‐specific MST targets were ruminant Bacteroides, bovine adenovirus, and bovine enterovirus (Gibson & Schwab, 2011; Mieszkin et al., 2010; Wong & Xagoraraki, 2010). Pig‐specific MST targets were pig Bacteroidales 1 and pig Bacteroidales 2 (Mieszkin et al., 2009).

The mean daily pathogen dose (Dij ) was calculated for each combination of fecal source (or aquifer) i and pathogen j. Dij was estimated as Dij = Cij × V, where Cij is mean pathogen concentration, and V is per capita daily water consumption (USEPA, 2011) (Table S2). Cij is expressed as infectious units per liter of water (e.g., oocysts/L for Cryptosporidium) and derived from qPCR‐based measurements using dose harmonization factors for each pathogen (Hj ):

where C qPCR, ij is in units of genomic copies/L, and Hj has units of genomic copies per infectious organism (Table S3) (Corsi et al., 2016; Kundu et al., 2013; J. P. Stokdyk et al., 2019, 2020; J. Jonsson et al., 2009; Stokdyk et al., 2025). C qPCR, ij was set equal to zero for non‐detects. Dose harmonization was not performed for norovirus since the underlying dose‐response data are based on qPCR measurements (Teunis, Moe, et al., 2008).

Dose‐response assessment

Dose‐response assessment was based on published models (Table S4). Morbidity ratios were used to derive illness endpoints for models predicting infection endpoints, including for adenovirus (Crabtree et al., 1997; Haas et al., 1993), enterovirus (Haas et al., 1993; Mena et al., 2003), norovirus (Teunis, Moe, et al., 2008; M. J. Messner et al., 2014), C. jejuni (Medema et al., 1996; Schmidt et al., 2013), C. parvum (DuPont et al., 1995; USEPA, 2005), and Giardia (Burch, 2020). Outbreak‐based models were used for EPEC (Teunis, Ogden, et al., 2008) and Salmonella spp. (WHO, 2002) because these models likely represent wild‐type pathogens and natural host populations. The C. hominis model has been validated against outbreak data (T. Burch, 2019), and the Giardia model integrates data from outbreaks with feeding trial data (Burch, 2020). Two models were used for norovirus to account for uncertainty in its dose‐response (Schmidt, 2015; Van Abel et al., 2017), and a correction factor for the proportion of seropositive hosts among typical norovirus host populations was also used (Lindesmith et al., 2003). Similarly, multiple models were used for unknown Cryptosporidium spp. to account for broad uncertainty in dose‐response when the species of Cryptosporidium cannot be identified (Burch et al., 2024).

Risk characterization

Daily risk by pathogen (Pij ,daily) was calculated from Dij (Section 2.3) using selected dose‐response models (Section 2.4). Annual risk by pathogen was calculated as Pij ,annual = 1 − (1 − Pij ,daily)365. Annual AGI cases by pathogen were calculated as Yij ,annual = Pij ,annual × Ni (Ni described in Section 2.3). Total AGI cases per year across all pathogens in each study stratum were calculated as Yi ,annual = ∑jYij,annual, and total AGI cases study‐wide were calculated by summing Yi ,annual with respect to fecal source. Relative risk by fecal source (or open interval aquifer) was calculated as (Yi ,annual/Ni )/(Y ref,annual/N ref), where Y ref,annual and N ref are total AGI cases and persons exposed in a reference category and Yi ,annual and Ni are as previously defined. Reference categories were “livestock only” and “lower only” for fecal source and open interval aquifer, respectively.

Computations were carried out in 2‐dimensional Monte Carlo (2DMC) simulations using R version 4.5.0 with the package mc2d (Pouillot & Delignette‐Muller, 2010; R Core Team, 2025). Each 2DMC simulation consisted of 4 million iterations, with 2000 iterations in the variability dimension and 2000 iterations in the uncertainty dimension. Variability and uncertainty in pathogen concentrations were estimated by bootstrap sampling (Table S2). Variability and uncertainty in all other inputs came from prior publications (Tables S1–S4). 2DMC outputs are summarized using arithmetic means in the variability dimension and medians with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) in the uncertainty dimension (Pouillot & Delignette‐Muller, 2010).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the effects of major assumptions in the QMRA (Supporting Information Section S1), including implicit assumptions that (1) 100% of water consumption occurs at home and (2) 100% of the exposed population is susceptible to disease. A third sensitivity analysis was conducted to investigate the effect of using small‐volume (1 L) samples to characterize pathogen exposure instead of DEUF.

RESULTS

AGI cases, persons exposed, and AGI risk by fecal source

Median QMRA estimates indicated 4450 cases of AGI per year in the study area due to consuming drinking water from contaminated private wells. The majority of estimated AGI cases (3430) were associated with detection of human fecal sources, including 2550 associated with detection of only human MST markers and 880 associated with detection of human and livestock MST markers together (Table 1). Nearly 1000 cases were estimated to occur in the absence of detectable MST markers, and 50 were associated with detection of livestock MST markers only (Table 1).

The median estimate for persons exposed in the study area was 13,360, and the distribution of cases by fecal source corresponded to the number of persons exposed in each category, except the human only category had fewer exposures but more estimated AGI cases than the category “none detected” (Table 1). The largest number of exposures was associated with the fecal source category “none detected” followed by “human only” (5790 and 3900, respectively). Relatively few people were exposed in the category “livestock only” (1370; Table 1).

Cumulative annual AGI risk was highest when human fecal sources were detected compared to “livestock only” and “none detected” (Table 1). Cumulative annual AGI risk was highest for human only (median = 0.65 AGI cases/person‐year; Table 1), nearly 20 times higher than “livestock only.” This difference was significant at the 90% confidence level (90% CI: 1.01–668.24; Table S5). AGI cases, persons exposed, and AGI risk by open interval aquifer

Regarding the source of private well water, median estimates indicate that wells open to the lower aquifer were associated with more exposures than the upper aquifer, but wells open to the upper aquifer accounted for more AGI cases per year (Table 2). In fact, estimated AGI cases appeared to decrease as a function of open interval aquifer, with wells open to the upper aquifer accounting for the highest median AGI cases per year (1750), wells open to the lower aquifer accounting for the lowest median AGI cases per year (640), and wells open to both aquifers being intermediate between these two categories (Table 2). AGI cases per person‐year followed the same trend (Table 2), and median risk for wells open to the upper aquifer only was more than four times higher than median risk for wells open to the lower aquifer only (0.62 compared to 0.15, respectively; Table 2). However, this difference was not significant (Table S6). Median risk for wells open to both aquifers was nominally higher than median risk for wells open to the lower aquifer only (0.47 vs. 0.15, respectively; Table 2), but this difference was also not significant (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

Risk of AGI associated with consumption of private well water

Even in countries with broad access to safe drinking water, the risk of AGI from drinking water commonly exceeds health targets (Lee et al., 2023). Private wells are a common source of drinking water in the United States (Murray et al., 2021), but the disease burden from consumption of groundwater from private wells is not well characterized. Microbial contamination of groundwater is influenced by land use, geology, and other factors that can vary by location and setting (Allen et al., 2017; Engström et al., 2017; Murphy et al., 2020). Specific examples from studies of private wells include agricultural land use, livestock operations, nearby septic systems, bedrock depth, well construction, precipitation, and groundwater recharge (Borchardt et al., 2021; Stokdyk et al., 2025). For a rural, agricultural region with high reliance on private wells, this study estimated 0.33 cases of AGI per person‐year associated with consumption of untreated well water (Table 1). Ten human and zoonotic pathogens detected in well water contributed to the estimated disease burden, including viruses, bacteria, and protozoa. Cryptosporidium contributed the most estimated cases of AGI (Table 3), consistent with prior work (Tollestrup et al., 2013; Burch et al., 2021). Findings illustrate a range of microbial hazards relevant to private well water and show that fecal contamination associated with rural land use was an important determinant of AGI risk.

Influence of fecal source on AGI risk associated with drinking water

Fecal source is understood to influence AGI risk for waterborne pathogen exposures (Boehm & Soller, 2020; Brown et al., 2017; Soller et al., 2010; Soller et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2020). Different fecal sources contain unique combinations of pathogens (Soller et al., 2010) and can also have distinct distributions on the landscape (Borchardt et al., 2021), such that pathogens originating from each source can be affected by fate and transport processes to varying extents before reaching points of human exposure.

Application of MST within QMRA allows investigation of relationships between risk and fecal source in natural settings. Prior work with this approach found a relatively high level of risk associated with bovine manure during a ∼1‐year observational study of private wells in Kewaunee County, Wisconsin. Bovine manure‐associated exposures in that study accounted for an estimated 230 AGI cases per year in a population of 830 persons exposed (Burch et al., 2021), equivalent to an annual risk of 230/830 = 0.28 AGI cases/person‐year, and these cases accounted for 76% of the 301 total cases estimated county‐wide. In contrast, human sewage‐associated exposures accounted for 12/390 = 0.031 AGI cases/person‐year, while exposures associated with unknown fecal sources accounted for 59/1790 = 0.033 AGI cases/person‐year (Burch et al., 2021).

The predominant source of risk for many exposure routes and geographic settings may not be obvious in the absence of site‐specific data. This study illustrates an example of human fecal sources being the predominant source of risk associated with drinking water from private wells, even in a rural region characterized by substantial agricultural activity. Based on land use, the study region is heavily influenced by livestock agriculture. The three Wisconsin counties considered here (Grant, Iowa, and Lafayette) contain more than 150,000 dairy and beef cows and approximately 18,000 households that rely on private septic systems (based on private well counts in the three counties, which likely occur approximately 1:1 with private septic systems; Table S9) (USEPA, 2023). The number of cows is twice the county average for Wisconsin per unit area (23.0 cows/km2 in the study region vs. 11.1 cows/km2 statewide; Table S9), and the number of households with private wells (and presumably private septic systems) is approximately half the Wisconsin county average (2.7 per km2 in the study region vs. 6.9 per km2 statewide; Table S9). However, human sewage, likely from private septic systems (Stokdyk et al., 2025), was the predominant source of identifiable risk and AGI cases associated with drinking contaminated private well water in Grant, Iowa, and Lafayette counties (Table 1).

The prevalence of human MST markers in wells and the type and number of co‐occurring pathogens contributed to the predominance of human sewage‐associated AGI cases and risk. Human MST markers occurred alone in more study wells (n = 40) than did livestock markers (n = 14), which corresponded to three times more persons in the “human only” exposure category than the “livestock only” category. With a larger population exposed, the total number of possible AGI cases associated with sewage was greater (Table 1).

Contamination of private wells by human MST markers was not only more prevalent; exposures to sewage contamination also posed a greater risk of AGI than exposures associated with manure or an absence of MST markers (Table 1). Pathogens co‐occurred with human MST markers at a higher rate than with livestock manure: there were 31 pathogen detections in the 40 “human only” samples (0.78 detections/sample) vs. nine pathogen detections in 14 livestock only samples (0.64 detections/sample); pathogen detections were even less frequent when no MST markers were present (Table S10). Furthermore, pathogen types differed by fecal source category. Eight different pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and protozoa, occurred in samples belonging to the human only category, compared to only three pathogens in samples belonging to the livestock only category (Table S7). Notably, C. hominis, which is human‐specific and highly infectious, accounted for more than half of estimated AGI cases associated with human sewage. Together with the higher number of persons exposed, the inherent riskiness of human exposures in this study leads to more overall AGI cases associated with human MST markers.

Comparison to Kewaunee County study

The predominance of sewage‐associated AGI for the current study is consistent with the notion that fecal contamination from humans poses a greater risk of AGI than contamination from animals (Soller et al., 2010), but findings contrast with a previous QMRA for private well water that found livestock sources were the predominate source of AGI risk in Kewaunee County, Wisconsin (Burch et al., 2021). Both studies included rural areas that were dominated by livestock agriculture and reliance on private septic systems and wells, and the contrasting findings can be explained by the intensity of livestock agriculture. While the density of cows in the current study's counties is relatively high, the density in Kewaunee County is higher still (58.8 cows/km2 compared to 23.0; Table S9). Furthermore, the nature and size of farms differ between the two study areas. Many cows in Kewaunee County are housed on concentrated animal feeding operations, which tend to concentrate livestock and manure on the landscape (Long et al., 2018), whereas farms in Grant, Iowa, and Lafayette counties have a smaller proportion of cattle on such large farms (Stokdyk et al., 2025). Despite general similarities, differences in these study settings were reflected in the contribution of pathogen sources to AGI risk, and the contrasting findings illustrate the value of recognizing how various pathogen sources influence waterborne AGI in specific settings (Schoen et al., 2020; Burch et al., 2024).

Some QMRA assumptions in the current study differ from those in Burch et al.’s (2021) Kewaunee County study. These differences include Cryptosporidium dose‐harmonization as well as dose‐response for ungenotyped Cryptosporidium spp. and C. parvum. Cryptosporidium dose‐harmonization was updated with site‐specific data from Stokdyk et al. (2025), since Cryptosporidium dose‐harmonization was previously based on only a single study (Stokdyk et al., 2019). The resulting difference in Cryptosporidium dose‐harmonization factor is small: a median of 14 genomic copies per oocyst in Burch et al. (2021) compared to a range of 8.5–14 genomic copies per oocyst in the current study (Table S3).

The differences in dose‐response for C. parvum and ungenotyped Cryptosporidium spp. are more substantial. Cryptosporidium parvum dose‐response was updated from M. J. Messner et al. (2001) in the Kewaunee County study to USEPA (2005) in the current study since the latter model is believed to be more representative of C. parvum infectivity. Similarly, dose‐response for ungenotyped Cryptosporidium spp. was updated from a hybrid model representing the midpoint of C. parvum and C. hominis (Burch et al., 2021) to a model that represents all 44 Cryptosporidium species known to infect humans (Burch et al., 2024). Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses on these dose‐response models demonstrate that using the Kewaunee County study assumptions in the current study would yield little impact on the relative distribution of AGI cases associated with human only fecal sources compared to livestock only fecal sources (Tables S11 and S12). The change in dose‐response for ungenotyped Cryptosporidium spp. does add a substantial number of cases to the fecal source categories both and none, but since the more recent dose‐response model represents a much broader range of Cryptosporidium species, estimates using the earlier model are not considered credible in the current study. Thus, methodological differences between these two studies do not account for the observed contrast in importance of human versus livestock fecal sources between the two study settings.

Influence of hydrogeology on AGI risk associated with drinking water

Hydrogeology could also influence AGI risk for waterborne pathogen exposures, as prior work demonstrates that vulnerable hydrogeologic features can lead to higher private well contamination rates by the fecal indicator bacterium E. coli (Borchardt et al., 2021; Latchmore et al., 2020). Borchardt et al. (2021) studied private wells constructed in fractured dolomite in a rural agricultural region of northeast Wisconsin. Fractured dolomite allows for rapid subsurface transport of fecal‐borne pathogens, and some areas of their study setting were characterized by thin overlying topsoil, providing little to no attenuation of contaminants originating at or near the land surface. Thus, Borchardt et al. (2021) found that E. coli contamination rates varied by depth of topsoil overlying bedrock, with 4%–7% of wells positive in <1.5 m depth‐to‐bedrock, ∼1% positive in 1.5‐ to 6.1‐m depth‐to‐bedrock, and <1% positive in >6.1‐m depth‐to‐bedrock.

However, higher E. coli contamination rates do not necessarily equate to higher risk, as data from Borchardt et al. (2021) used in a QMRA estimated that AGI risk in shallow versus deep depth‐to‐bedrock were equivalent. AGI risk for wells drilled in shallow depth‐to‐bedrock (≤6.1 m) was 0.08 cases/person‐year, whereas AGI risk for wells drilled in deep depth‐to‐bedrock (>6.1 m) was 0.11 cases/person‐year (Burch et al., 2021). Rather than geology, bovine fecal contamination of wells was a significant factor for AGI risk. Thus, while hydrogeology clearly influences contamination by fecal microbes, other factors can be equally or more important with respect to AGI risk.

In the current study, some wells drew water from the region's upper aquifer, some from the lower aquifer, and some from both (Stokdyk et al., 2025). The upper aquifer consists of fractured carbonate bedrock and is therefore considered vulnerable, whereas the lower aquifer is protected by a regional shale aquitard (Stokdyk et al., 2025). The upper aquifer's vulnerability was reflected by nitrate contamination, which was significantly greater than nitrate contamination in the lower aquifer (Stokdyk et al., 2025). However, microbial contamination was not found to vary by aquifer, which may owe to different transport characteristics of chemical versus microbial contaminants (Stokdyk et al., 2025). Accordingly, the CIs for AGI risk by aquifer overlapped, and variation in median AGI risk by aquifer was mild, with a maximum of 0.62 AGI cases/person‐year for wells open to the upper aquifer only and a minimum of 0.15 AGI cases/person‐year for wells open to the lower aquifer only (Table 2). For comparison, median AGI risk by fecal source detected varied by more than an order of magnitude, from 0.036 AGI cases/person‐year to 0.65 AGI cases/person‐year (Table 1). Thus, in both this study and prior work in Kewaunee County, Wisconsin, fecal source detected is a more important determinant of AGI risk than hydrogeology.

AGI risk associated with drinking water in context

AGI risk associated with drinking water from private wells in this study varied widely, with median estimates varying from 36 to 650 AGI cases per 1000 people per year (Table 1). These risk estimates are generally consistent with those for drinking water found in comparable studies. AGI risk for groundwater consumption in North American study settings has been estimated as 26–450 AGI cases per 1000 people per year (Borchardt et al., 2012; Macler & Merkle, 2000; Murphy, Thomas, Schmidt, et al., 2016; Reynolds et al., 2008; Burch et al., 2021, 2022). Similarly, AGI risk for North American public drinking water systems supplied by groundwater and/or surface water has been estimated as 7–330 AGI cases per 1000 people per year (Colford et al., 2006; M. Messner et al., 2006; Murphy, Thomas, Medeiros, et al., 2016; Burch et al., 2022). However, broad uncertainty inherent in nearly all assessments of drinking water risk limits detailed comparison across studies, which can vary with respect to study scope, sampling frequency, geographic scale, and assessment approach (e.g., QMRA vs. epidemiology).

While not directly applicable to private wells, risk estimates from the current study exceed the EPA risk benchmark for public drinking water systems of one infection per 10,000 people per year (Regli et al., 1988; USEPA, 1989). High estimated risk compared to the EPA benchmark is common for drinking water QMRAs. Owens et al. (2020) illustrated that risk for 29/64 (45%) scenarios in reviewed public drinking water QMRAs exceeded 1 infection per 10,000 people per year. Likewise, considering 24 studies based on QMRA and disease surveillance data, Lee et al. (2023) reported that estimates of disease risk from drinking water in high‐income countries commonly exceeded health benchmarks.

Risk estimates from the current study can also be compared to the rate of all‐cause AGI in the United States, which has been estimated at approximately 600 cases per 1000 people per year (Jones et al., 2007). Applying that rate to the current study's population and the median risk estimate across all fecal sources (330 AGI cases per 1000 people per year; Table 1), private well drinking water risk accounts for approximately 50% of all AGI cases in the study area's private wells. Previous studies have estimated the percent of all‐cause AGI due to drinking water between 2% and 22% in a variety of US settings (Borchardt et al., 2012; Colford et al., 2009; M. Messner et al., 2006).

Pathogens included in this study are monitored by the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System, including C. jejuni, EPEC, Salmonella, Cryptosporidium, and Giardia. The discrepancy between the median estimate and notifiable disease data for Salmonella could be explained by particularly poor under‐diagnosis of salmonellosis in the study area, acquired immunity to Salmonella among the study population, and/or overestimated infectivity in the QMRA, as Salmonella infectivity in humans is known to vary widely with serotype (Jones et al., 2008).

Sensitivity analyses

Most private well users in Wisconsin consume their well water (Malecki et al., 2017), but well users may also consume water from other sources, like schools, workplaces, or public water supplies. The proportion of daily water consumed from private wells versus other sources was unknown, so additional analyses evaluated a range (Supporting Information Section S1). Reducing the simulated volume of water consumed by individuals at home reduced overall risk and cases of AGI (Table S13); the decrease was linear with respect to water consumption. Similarly, reducing the exposed population resulted in linear decreases of AGI cases (Table S14). Reductions to the exposed population could correspond to residents who never consume their private well water (e.g., drink bottled water), install effective treatment systems, are immune to infection by the etiological agents considered, or any combination of these scenarios.

Sampling methods vary in their ability to recover target organisms and in the amount of water they concentrate for downstream analysis (Heffron et al., 2025). Large‐volume DEUF sampling outperforms small‐volume sampling for detection of groundwater‐borne pathogens (Heffron et al., 2025) and was essential to characterizing sources and etiological agents of risk in this study. Small‐volume grab sampling (which was paired with DEUF sampling for study wells) failed to detect 7/10 pathogens included in hazard identification (Table S15). These undetected hazards accounted for more than half of median annual cases of AGI (2489 of 4450 total) estimated using large‐volume samples. Furthermore, no pathogens detected in small‐volume samples co‐occurred with MST markers (Tables S15 and S16). Thus, QMRA based on small‐volume samples in this study would be unable to associate risk with detectable fecal sources and therefore fail to achieve a critical study objective.

The few pathogen detections in small‐volume samples (n = 6) generally exhibited higher concentrations than DEUF samples, which is consistent with that sampling method's superior recovery for some bacteria, protozoa, and viruses in laboratory experiments (Heffron et al., 2025). However, the resulting mean daily doses estimated from small‐volume samples (Table S16) were not always greater than doses estimated from DEUF (Table S8) because most small‐volume samples yielded non‐detections, which produced estimated doses of zero that moderated the higher measured concentrations. For QMRA, the higher pathogen concentrations may offset the fewer pathogen detections in some situations. Importantly, however, the conceptualization of AGI risk from private well water would differ significantly if based on small‐volume samples for the current study, with potential implications for risk perception and mitigation. Data from small‐volume samples would imply that AGI risk resulted from a few isolated contamination events by limited agents, whereas data from DEUF samples revealed broader contamination of wells by many human and zoonotic pathogens.

Limitations

The study is observational, and results characterize the time period of sample collection (April 2019 to March 2020). Changes in land use, rainfall, and/or unusual events (e.g., flooding) could alter pathogen occurrence and resulting risk estimates. The study considers daily drinking water as the route of exposure. Other routes are possible (cooking, dish washing, showering, and bathing) (Malecki et al., 2017), but these exposures involve smaller volumes of water compared to drinking, so the consequent risk would be lower. The study assumes that 100% of residents in households served by private wells consume all their daily drinking water at home, do not treat their water to remove microbial contaminants, and are fully susceptible to infection and illness by the pathogens included in hazard identification; data informing more specific assumptions with respect to these factors were not available for the study area. These assumptions were evaluated through additional analyses to illustrate their influence on risk estimates (Tables S13 and S14).

Pathogen‐negative samples translated to drinking water exposures with a risk of zero in the QMRA, but pathogens may have been present below method detection limits. Risk associated with those low concentrations would be less than the annual risk corresponding to the 95% detection limits for pathogens included in the study (Table S17). We lack data on the viability and infectivity of pathogens detected in our study by qPCR. However, our use of dose harmonization converts qPCR‐detectable genomic copies to infectious units (e.g., colony‐forming unit and oocysts) required for dose‐response assessment. Pathogens were assigned to detected fecal sources based on co‐occurrence with MST markers. MST markers may have been present in some samples below method detection limits, and some pathogens may have originated from these undetected fecal sources; diagnostic specificity and sensitivity of MST markers were not validated for the geographic location of the study area. Birds and other wildlife are present in the study area, but their impact is expected to be low. MST was included for the dominant fecal sources in the study area, including humans, cattle, and pigs. Previous estimates indicate that the volume of poultry and deer feces in the study area is approximately 100‐fold lower than cattle and sixfold lower than pigs (Stokdyk et al., 2023).

CONCLUSIONS

Overall estimated risk associated with consumption of private well water was moderate to high compared to previous private well studies, all‐cause AGI, and notifiable disease data. Fecal source was an important determinant of risk for drinking water from private wells. Fecal source produced more variation in risk than hydrogeological factors, and risk was predominantly associated with human fecal material in the current study rather than livestock fecal material, even though the study setting is a rural agricultural area. MST helped accurately characterize the relationship between risk and fecal source. Ten viral, bacterial, and protozoan pathogens were relevant waterborne hazards, and large‐volume DEUF sampling was essential to characterizing the sources and etiological agents of risk in this study. QMRA produces risk estimates consistent with established scientific literature, known community‐level illness rates, and notifiable disease data.
