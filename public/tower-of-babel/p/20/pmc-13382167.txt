Baicalein solid dispersion prepared by solvent evaporation enhances oral bioavailability and alleviates ulcerative colitis
Authors: Xiali Wei, Xinyu Ji, Ziyang Li, Zixuan Guo, Yuxian Li, Qingri Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBaicalein (BAC) is a flavonoid compound known for its effectiveness in treating ulcerative colitis (UC). However, its clinical application is limited by poor water solubility, low stability, and low oral bioavailability. In this study, a baicalein solid dispersion (BAC-SD) was prepared using Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) as carriers via the solvent evaporation method. Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM), Powder X-Ray Diffraction 6000 diffractometer (PXRD), Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC), and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) analyses confirmed the transition of BAC from a crystalline to an amorphous state. This transformation significantly improved the solubility, dissolution rate, and permeability of BAC. The in vitro dissolution results showed that the cumulative dissolution rate of BAC-SD reached 86.86% at 240 min, representing a 10.09-fold increase in solubility compared to BAC. In Caco-2 and MDCK cell models, the apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) of BAC-SD increased by 2.7- and 3.1-fold, respectively. Pharmacokinetic studies in rats demonstrated that the relative bioavailability of BAC-SD was 236.4% compared to BAC. In the DSS-induced UC mouse model, BAC-SD treatment alleviated UC by increasing colon length, improving pathological damage, and reducing both the Disease Activity Index (DAI) score and levels of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. In conclusion, we successfully prepared BAC-SD and demonstrated that its therapeutic effect on UC is achieved by enhancing solubility, permeability, and oral bioavailability.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Baicalein, Solid dispersion, Bioavailability enhancement, Anti-Inflammatory activity

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a complex, chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) primarily affecting the colorectal mucosa and submucosa. Its incidence is increasing globally, making it a widespread health concern(Du and Ha, 2020; Veltkamp and Voorneveld, 2025). The exact causes of UC remain unclear, but potential risk factors include genetics, environment, and immune dysfunction. UC is characterized by mucosal epithelial damage and disrupted intestinal homeostasis(Liu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024). Clinically, it presents with rectal to proximal colon inflammation, often causing bloody diarrhea. Current treatments like 5-aminosalicylic acid, immunosuppressants, corticosteroids, and monoclonal antibodies can have limited efficacy and cause adverse effects, with some being costly(Quansah et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). Thus, developing a safe, effective, and affordable new treatment is crucial.

Baicalein (BAC), also known as 5,6,7-trihydroxyflavone, is one of the most abundant flavonoids in Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi. (Dou et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2022). It exhibits a variety of pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory (Holanda et al., 2023), anti-tumor (Tuli et al., 2020), antioxidant (Bayram et al., 2023) and antibacterial properties (Song et al., 2024). These characteristics render it a promising candidate for the treatment of inflammation-related diseases, such as colitis, hepatitis, and cholecystitis (Jang et al., 2023). Numerous studies have demonstrated that BAC exerts therapeutic effects in inflammatory bowel disease by reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, protecting and repairing the intestinal mucosal barrier, modulating immune responses, combating oxidative stress, and regulating the intestinal microbiota(Li et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2025). However, as a Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) Class II drug, BAC has low solubility, poor stability, and low oral bioavailability, limiting its clinical use(Sun et al., 2022).

Despite these promising pharmacological properties, the clinical application of baicalein is severely constrained by its poor aqueous solubility, low oral bioavailability, and rapid metabolic clearance. To overcome these challenges, various formulation strategies have been explored in recent years. Baicalein-loaded aspasomal formulations have been developed using ascorbyl palmitate as a vesicle-forming component; the optimized formulation exhibited a particle size of (425 ± 1.00) nm, an encapsulation efficiency of 40 ± 1.02%, and sustained drug release of 85 ± 0.95%, with enhanced cellular permeation and maintained physicochemical stability over three months of storage, ultimately demonstrating significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (Hozan et al., 2025). In another study, natural deep eutectic solvents were explored for their ability to enhance baicalein solubility, stability, and antioxidant activity, with the structure-function relationship of the solvent systems being systematically elucidated(Chen et al., 2026). Additionally, nano drug delivery systems, including organic and inorganic nanoparticles, have been developed to improve the pharmacokinetics and targeted delivery of baicalein and its glycoside baicalin. Beyond these approaches, a wide range of formulation technologies have been investigated to enhance the solubility and oral bioavailability of baicalein, including liposomes, polymeric micelles, nanocrystals, microemulsions, inclusion complexes, and hydrogels. Among these, solid dispersion has emerged as one of the most promising approaches due to its simplicity, scalability, and effectiveness in achieving supersaturated drug levels (Gao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2023; Zang et al., 2024).

Solid dispersions (SDs) are systems where active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) are dispersed in a water-soluble carrier in the solid state. Specifically, amorphous solid dispersions can significantly improve the dissolution of insoluble drugs. In these systems, the drugs are uniformly dispersed in water-soluble carriers at the molecular level, forming solid molecular solutions. The water-soluble carriers enhance the wettability of the drugs, further facilitating their dissolution. Amorphous drugs possess high free energy and do not require additional energy to disrupt their crystalline structure (Bhujbal et al., 2021; Cho et al., 2020). This enhances drug wettability and eliminates the need for energy to disrupt crystalline structures, leading to supersaturation at the gastrointestinal absorption site and improved absorption, exposure, and therapeutic efficacy.

PVP and HPMC are key polymers in solid dispersions due to their non-toxicity, availability, and high drug-loading capacity (Jin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). PVP notably enhances drug solubility, bioavailability, and release profiles. For example, Hao et al. (2024) prepared a nimodipine/HPMC solid dispersion using supercritical antisolvent technology, which improved solubility and dissolution rate while retaining biocompatibility and antibacterial properties. Similarly, Jahangiri et al. (2022) used electrospray technology with PVP to create an erlotinib solid dispersion, resulting in higher dissolution rates and better bioavailability than bulk erlotinib.

In this study, baicalein solid dispersions were prepared via solvent evaporation using HPMC E15 and PVP K23–27 as co-carriers. Their microscopic morphology and phase characteristics were analyzed using structural techniques. The solubility, in vitro dissolution, cellular uptake, and in vivo bioavailability of the solid dispersions were comprehensively assessed. Additionally, their therapeutic efficacy was confirmed in a murine UC model. Compared to pure baicalein, the solid dispersions significantly enhanced in vivo absorption and pharmacological activity, demonstrating superior therapeutic potential for UC treatment.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Baicalein (98% purity), tetrahydropalmatine, formic acid, sodium ascorbate, dimethyl sulfoxide, PVP and HPMC series are all purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai China). Sodium dextran sulfate (molecular weight 36-50KDa) was purchased from Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai China). The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits were provided by Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai China), including Mouse TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, myeloperoxidase (MPO), malondialdehyde (MDA) and Diamine oxidase (DAO).

The Caco-2 and MDCK cell lines were sourced from the ATCC cell bank. Phosphate buffer (PBS), HBSS buffer, DMEM medium, MEM medium, 0.25% EDTA-trypsin, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin were obtained from Gibco. Fetal bovine serum was purchased from Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 0.4 μm Transwell plate and the 96-well cell culture plate were from Corning.

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (350 ± 20 g) and male Balb/c mice (20 ± 2 g) were housed in an animal facility. The animals had free access to food and water and were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. The room temperature and humidity were controlled. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Regulations on the Administration of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ministry of Science and Technology. The ethical approval number for the laboratory animal use is “202,502,012”.

Preparation of solid dispersions and physical mixtures

The solid dispersion of baicalein was prepared by the solvent evaporation method(Zhou et al., 2022). Accurately weighed Baicalein was dissolved in absolute ethanol and sonicated for 20 min Precisely weighed carrier was slowly mixed with an appropriate amount of anhydrous ethanol to form a clear solution, then mixed thoroughly with the drug solution with stirring for complete drug dispersion. The mixture was subjected to rotary evaporation at 55 °C and subsequently dried overnight in a blast drying oven at 50 °C. After drying, the product was ground through an 80-mesh screen to obtain the solid dispersion for further use.

According to the ratio of Baicalein: HPMC E15: PVP-K23 = 1:3:4, the drug and carriers were weighed accurately. They were then ground together in a mortar until a uniform mixture was obtained. The resulting mixture was screened through an 80-mesh screen to obtain a physical mixture for further use.

Optimization of carrier composition and preparation process for baicalein solid dispersion

A stepwise optimization strategy was employed to develop the BAC-SD. First, single-factor experiments were conducted to screen the optimal carrier type and drug-to-carrier ratio. Six carriers—HPMC E5, HPMC E15, HPMC E50, PVP K13–18, PVP K16–18, and PVP K23–27—were individually evaluated at drug-to-carrier ratios of 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4 using the solvent evaporation method, with equilibrium solubility as the primary evaluation index. On the basis of the single-carrier optimization results, a binary carrier system consisting of HPMC E15 and PVP K23–27 was finally adopted for further optimization. To determine the optimal proportion of the binary carriers, various mass ratios of BAC:HPMC E15: PVP K23–27 (1:3:1, 1:3:2, 1:3:3, 1:3:4, and 1:3:5) were prepared and assessed for both solubility and cumulative dissolution rate. Based on the optimization results, we figured out the best drug-to-carrier ratio. Detailed optimization data are provided in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S1–S3).

Subsequently, an orthogonal experimental design (L9(33)) was employed to optimize the preparation process parameters. The three factors investigated were stirring time (A), rotary evaporation temperature (B), and rotation speed (C), each set at three levels. The cumulative dissolution rate was used as the evaluation index to screen the optimal preparation conditions. The specific factors and levels are shown in Table 1.

Characterization of solid dispersion

The samples were prepared by placing a small amount on conductive tape–affixed bases using toothpicks, then gold-coating for 5 min. Morphology was examined using a TM 3030 SEM (Hitachi Hi-Tech, Japan) at 15.0 kV. Crystallinity was assessed with a Powder X-Ray Diffraction 6000 diffractometer (PXRD, Shimadzu, Japan) using Cu-Kα radiation (40 kV, 35 mA), scanning from 5° to 65° at 1°/min (wavelength 1.54184 Å). For FT-IR analysis, samples were mixed with KBr, ground, and pressed into pellets. Spectroscopy was performed using an IR Prestige-21 spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan) with a scanning range of 4000–400 cm−1 and resolution of 2 cm−1. TGA and DSC were conducted using a TG-DSC analyzer on baicalein, carrier, physical mixture, and solid dispersion. Samples (5.0–10.0 mg) were analyzed in aluminum crucibles under a nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min, heated at 10 °C/min from 35 °C to 650 °C.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of BAC

The HPLC analysis was conducted using an Agilent 1260 system with a Welch Ultimate C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase was methanol and 0.1% formic acid (60:40, v/v). The injection volume was 20 μL, flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and column temperature was 40 °C.

Determination of solubility

BAC, BAC-PM, and BAC-SD (each equivalent to 10 mg of BAC) were accurately weighed and transferred into centrifuge tubes containing 10 mL of distilled water. The mixtures were then shaken at 37 °C for 24 h in a constant temperature shaker. Subsequently, the resulting solutions were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The filtrates were appropriately diluted with the mobile phase, and the BAC content in distilled water was determined by HPLC to calculate its solubility.

Determination of cumulative dissolution

Following the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 edition), the in vitro release of baicalein was tested using an RC-806D dissolution apparatus (TDTF, China) with the paddle method. The dissolution vessel contained 500 mL of distilled water at 37 ± 0.5 °C, with a paddle speed of 50 rpm. BAC, BAC-SD, and BAC-PM (each containing 10 mg of BAC) were added to the medium. Samples (5 mL) were taken at 10, 20, 30, 50, 70, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210, and 240 min, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and analyzed by HPLC.

Cell test

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of BAC and BAC-SD towards Caco-2 and MDCK cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. The procedure was as follows: Accurate amounts of BA and BAC-SD, were weighed and dissolved in DMSO to prepare stock solutions at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 20, and 40 mg/mL. These stock solutions were then diluted with complete culture medium to obtain drug solutions at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 20, and 40 μg/mL.

Caco-2 and MDCK cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 5 × 103 cells/well with 100 μL of culture medium per well. They were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity for 24 h. After discarding the medium, fresh medium containing various concentrations of BAC or BAC-SD (100 μL/well) was added and incubated for another 24 h. The medium was then removed, and cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS. Fresh medium with 10% CCK-8 solution (100 μL/well) was added. Caco-2 cells were incubated for 2 h, and MDCK cells for 0.5 h. Cell monolayer transport test

Caco-2 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) and MDCK cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in a 12-well Transwell plate to test permeability. For Caco-2, 0.5 mL of cell suspension was added to the apical compartment and 1.5 mL of complete medium to the basal compartment. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and saturated humidity. Caco-2 cells were cultured for 21 days (medium changed every other day), while MDCK cells were cultured for 7 days (medium changed daily). Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured using an epithelial voltohmmeter (Beijing Metalworking Hongtai RE1600) to check monolayer integrity. When TEER values exceeded 500 Ω·cm2 for Caco-2 cells and 200 Ω·cm2 for MDCK cells, the transport test was conducted.

Based on cytotoxicity test results, a safe dose range was established. The drug was added to the AP of the Transwell insert, and 1.5 mL of preheated HBSS (pH 7.4) was added to the BL. After incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, 200 μL samples were collected from the BL at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min, with the same volume of blank HBSS added to replace the collected samples. For sample preparation, 100 μL of the collected sample was mixed with 20 μL of 0.5% (w/v) sodium ascorbate and 50 μL of methanol, vortexed for 5 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. A 20 μL aliquot of the supernatant was used for analysis. The apparent Permeability Coefficient (Papp) was calculated using the formula: Papp = (dQ/dt)/(AC0), where dQ/dt is the drug transport rate, A is the monolayer surface area (1.12 cm2), and C0 is the initial drug concentration in the AP (μg/mL).

Pharmacokinetic study

Experimental design

The 12 healthy Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into three groups (n = 4 per group): BAC, BAC-PM, and BAC-SD. The rats were fasted for 12 h before the experiment but allowed free access to water. After anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium, the rats were fixed on a rat board, and femoral artery and vein cannulation surgeries were performed. The three formulations were administered by oral gavage at a dose of 75 mg/kg. The oral dose was selected based on previous pharmacokinetic studies of BAC in rats (Tong et al., 2022).

Blood samples of approximately 300 μL were collected from the femoral artery at 0.167, 0.5, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 h. An equivalent volume of normal saline (approximately 0.35 mL per time) was administered through the femoral vein to maintain blood volume. The collected blood samples were immediately centrifuged, plasma was separated, and stored at −80 °C until analysis by UPLC-MS/MS.

All animals successfully completed the pharmacokinetic study without any loss of cannula patency or unexpected mortality. No outlier data points were excluded from the final statistical analysis, ensuring that the reported variability reflects the true inter-individual biological and experimental variance.

Plasma sample pretreatment and analysis

Plasma samples were prepared by transferring 60 μL of each sample to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, adding 20 μL of 0.5 mg/mL sodium ascorbate and 10 μL of 5 μg/mL luteolin (internal standard), and vortexing for 5 min. Then, 150 μL of acetonitrile containing 2% formic acid was added and vortexed for another 10 min. After centrifugation at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was collected for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. The analysis was performed using an Agilent 6495C system with an Agilent Poroshell SB-C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Phase A) and acetonitrile (Phase B) with the following gradient elution: 90% A and 10% B at 0–1.3 min, 10% A and 90% B at 1.3–3.5 min, and 90% A and 10% B at 3.5–4.0 min. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 1 μL.MS/MS analysis was conducted in positive ion mode with ESI parameters set at drying gas temperature 250 °C, sheath gas flow rate 12.0 L/min, nitrogen temperature 350 °C, and nitrogen flow rate 11.0 L/min. The monitoring ion pairs were m/z 276.1 → 123.0 for Baicalein and m/z 356.0 → 106.0 for the internal standard. Relative bioavailability (F) was calculated using the formula:F = (AUCT × D)/(AUCR × D) × 100%, where AUCT and AUCR are the areas under the concentration-time curves for the test (BAC-SD) and reference (BAC) formulations, respectively, and D is the dosage. Pharmacokinetic parameters were analyzed using the non-compartmental model in WinNonlin software.

Pharmacodynamic study

Experimental design

Male Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were divided into five groups (n = 6): healthy control, model, BAC, BAC-PM, and BAC-SD. Except for the healthy control group, all groups were given free access to a 3% DSS solution for 7 days to induce acute ulcerative colitis. The healthy control and model groups received a 0.5% CMC-Na solution via gavage. The BAC, BAC-PM, and BAC-SD groups were administered baicalein at 40 mg/kg via gavage for 7 days, starting from the first day of DSS treatment. On day 8, mice were fasted, and on day 9, they were sacrificed for blood collection.

The feeding behavior, activity, and hair condition of the mice were observed daily, while their weight, stool characteristics, and presence of hematochezia (bloody stool) were recorded. Additionally, the Disease Activity Index (DAI) score was calculated. After the experimental period, the mice were anesthetized via intramuscular injection with Zoletil (Virbac Company, France), and blood samples were collected. The blood was centrifuged to obtain serum, which was then stored at −80 °C. Serum samples from each group were collected, and the levels of Diamine Oxidase (DAO), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) were measured using ELISA kits.

After blood collection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The entire colon was removed and its length measured. A 1 cm colon segment was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology. The spleen was excised, weighed, and used to calculate the spleen index. Colonic tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) for microscopic examination. Additionally, colonic tissues were weighed and analyzed for myeloperoxidase (MPO) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content using ELISA kits.

Data analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. GraphPad Prism 10 was used for analysis and visualization. One-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc tests was used for multiple group comparisons, while t-tests were used for two-group comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The optimal combination was A1B1C1, achieving a cumulative dissolution rate of 84.84%. Taking all the above results into consideration, the optimal formulation was determined to be a drug-to-carrier ratio of 1:3:4, and the optimal preparation process was a stirring time of 2 h, a rotary evaporation temperature of 45 °C, and a rotation speed of 60 rpm.

Characterization of solid dispersions

BAC-SD were prepared via solvent evaporation, with a physical mixture serving as a control. Characterization using SEM, XRD, DSC, and FT-IR revealed distinct morphologies and properties. SEM results (Fig. 1a) show BAC as columnar crystals, HPMC E15 as cylindrical, and PVP-K23–27 as spherical/ellipsoidal. BAC-PM contains all three forms, indicating incomplete BAC encapsulation. BAC-SD presented an irregular flaky structure, suggesting a possible loss of crystallinity, which was subsequently confirmed by XRD and DSC.

PXRD results (Fig. 1b) show BAC crystalline peaks at 10.04°, 11.29°, 13.34°, 15.41°, 23.95°, and 26.49°(Gharari et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). PVP K23–27 and HPMC E15 display amorphous broad peaks. BAC-PM retains BAC's crystalline peaks but with reduced intensity, indicating partial encapsulation. BAC-SD lacks these peaks, suggesting complete encapsulation and an amorphous state. TGA analysis (Fig. 1c) showed Baicalein's weight loss in two stages: 2% loss below 100 °C and 70% loss between 260 °C and 650 °C. HPMC E15 and PVP K23–27 had 87% and 95% weight losses around 500 °C. BAC-PM and BAC-SD exhibited good thermal stability. FT-IR spectra (Fig. 1d) show BAC's -OH peaks at 3410.97 cm−1 and 3091.81 cm−1(Guo et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021), C=O peak at 1658.03 cm−1, and benzene ring peaks at 1618.94 cm−1, 1584.71 cm−1, and 1470.91 cm−1. In BAC-SD, the 3410.97 cm−1 peak weakens and shifts to 3449.54 cm−1, suggesting hydrogen bonding. DSC analysis (Fig. 1e) reveals a sharp endothermic peak for BAC at 271.2 °C(Aghajanzadeh et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2024),which is absent in BAC-SD. This confirms its amorphous nature.

In vitro cell test

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of BAC and BAC-SD in Caco-2 and MDCK cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 method. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). As shown in Fig. 3a and b, within the concentration range of 0–20 μg/mL, the cell viability of both formulations in the two cell lines remained above 80% for all tested concentrations. In MDCK cells, a marginally higher viability was noted at 1 μg/mL compared to the control. However, the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), and the slight increase fell within the range of normal experimental variability (e.g., biological variation in cell proliferation, minor differences in initial seeding density, or inherent fluctuations of the CCK-8 assay). Similar non-significant fluctuations have been commonly observed in CCK-8 assays at low concentrations of test compounds. Therefore, the 1 μg/mL concentration was considered safe and was selected for subsequent transmembrane transport studies, consistent with the general safety criterion of cell viability > 80%.

Cell transmembrane transport test

In this study, the TEER of the Caco-2 monolayer increased with culture time, reaching 772.05 ± 11.22 Ω·cm2 by day 21 (Fig. 3c). Similarly, the TEER of the MDCK monolayer increased with culture time, reaching 414.67 ± 10.1 Ω·cm2 by day 7 (Fig. 3d).

The cumulative permeation of BAC and BAC-SD in both cell types is shown in Fig. 3e-f. BAC-SD exhibited significantly higher cumulative permeation than BAC in both cell types. The apparent permeability coefficients (Papp) of 20 μg/mL BAC in Caco-2 and MDCK cells were 3.76 × 10−6 cm/s and 1.94 × 10−6 cm/s, respectively. In contrast, the Papp values for BAC-SD were 1.03 × 10−5 cm/s and 6.02 × 10−6 cm/s, respectively, representing 2.7-fold and 3.1-fold increases compared to BAC (Fig. 3g-h).

Pharmacodynamic study

BAC-SD alleviated DSS-induced UC symptoms

Discussion

In this study, baicalein solid dispersions (BAC-SD) were successfully prepared using PVP K23–27 and HPMC E15 as carriers via the solvent evaporation method. Solid dispersion technology has emerged as a promising strategy to improve the solubility and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs, particularly BCS Class II compounds like baicalein. The hydrophilic carriers used in this study, PVP and HPMC, are known for their ability to inhibit drug crystallization and enhance wettability, thereby promoting drug dissolution and absorption(Pandi et al., 2020).

Regarding residual organic solvent, although quantitative analysis was not conducted in the current study, the processing parameters—specifically, rotary evaporation at 45 °C under reduced pressure followed by a subsequent drying step—are theoretically sufficient to reduce residual ethanol to negligible levels. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that quantitative confirmation via Gas Chromatograph (GC) would further strengthen the safety profile of the formulation, and such analysis is planned in our future investigations.

The choice of these two polymers was based on their complementary properties: PVP exhibits strong crystallization-inhibiting effects due to its high glass transition temperature and ability to form hydrogen bonds with drug molecules, while HPMC further improves wettability and provides matrix stability. In addition, a recent review confirmed that PVP and HPMC form hydrogen bonds and yield a uniform, amorphous blend when combined(Homayouni et al., 2019). The characterization results (SEM, PXRD, DSC, FT-IR) collectively confirmed that BAC existed in an amorphous, molecularly dispersed state within the solid dispersion, without detectable crystalline domains. Notably, the shift and broadening of the –OH stretching band in FT-IR suggested hydrogen bonding between BAC and the carriers, which is a key mechanism for stabilizing the amorphous form and preventing recrystallization during storage(Rosiak et al., 2026).

In vitro dissolution is a crucial factor influencing the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs during oral absorption(Huang et al., 2022). Compared to previous reports on baicalein solid dispersions using single carriers, our dual-carrier system provided a more significant improvement in solubility (10.09-fold) and dissolution rate (86.86% at 240 min). For instance, Gao et al. used Soluplus as a carrier to prepare BAC-SD by the solvent evaporation method, achieving a cumulative dissolution rate of 52.67%, which was 7.28 times higher than that of BAC(Gao et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with previous reports on solid dispersions of other flavonoids, such as rutin and apigenin, where similar improvements in dissolution were observed(Nguyen et al., 2025; Rosiak et al., 2025).

The cellular transport studies using Caco-2 and MDCK monolayers provided further insight into the absorption-enhancing effect of BAC-SD. The significantly higher Papp values observed for BAC-SD compared to BAC indicate that the solid dispersion formulation improved the permeability of baicalein across intestinal epithelial barriers. Notably, the Caco-2 model yielded higher Papp values than the MDCK model for both formulations, which can be explained by the more complete tight junctions, higher polarization, and the presence of drug transporters such as PEPT1 and OATP in Caco-2 cells. These transporters are known to facilitate the absorption of flavonoids, making the Caco-2 model particularly suitable for predicting human intestinal absorption(Li and Liu, 2025; Wang et al., 2024). The 2.7-fold and 3.1-fold increases in Papp in Caco-2 and MDCK cells, respectively, strongly suggest that BAC-SD can enhance the oral absorption of baicalein in vivo.

It should be acknowledged that the cell transmembrane transport experiments in the present study were conducted using direct drug solutions without simulating gastrointestinal digestion. While this approach provides preliminary evidence of enhanced cellular permeability of BAC-SD, it does not fully recapitulate the complex gastrointestinal environment that orally administered formulations encounter in vivo, including gastric acid, digestive enzymes, and bile salts, which may significantly influence drug release and dissolution behavior. Previous studies have demonstrated that simulated gastrointestinal digestion combined with Caco-2 cell monolayer transport assays serves as a robust platform for evaluating the oral absorption potential of solid dispersions and nanoparticle formulations(Jia et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022). Therefore, while the current in vitro data support the improved permeability of BAC-SD, further studies incorporating simulated gastrointestinal digestion prior to cellular uptake assessment are warranted to better predict the in vivo performance of the formulation.

The pharmacokinetic results in Sprague-Dawley rats confirmed the improved oral bioavailability of BAC-SD. The 3.7-fold increase in Cmax and the relative bioavailability of 236.4% demonstrate that the solid dispersion formulation effectively enhanced the systemic exposure of baicalein. The reduction in Vd and CL indicates that BAC-SD may alter the distribution and elimination characteristics of baicalein, potentially due to improved solubility and altered plasma protein binding or tissue distribution. The decreased AUC%Extrap further validates the reliability of the absorption estimation. These findings align with the enhanced permeability observed in vitro and collectively confirm that solid dispersion technology is an effective approach to overcome the bioavailability limitations of BAC. Admittedly, the AUC₀₋ₜ of BAC-SD exhibited considerable inter-individual variability (CV ≈ 24%). This variation likely arises from (i) physiological differences in gastric emptying and intestinal motility affecting absorption of this BCS II drug, (ii) inter-animal variability in phase II metabolic enzymes (UGTs/SULTs) governing first-pass clearance, and (iii) minor surgical effects on vascular access during serial sampling. Importantly, despite this dispersion, the increases in C max (3.7-fold) and relative bioavailability (236.4%) remained statistically significant (p < 0.01), and the low AUC% Extrap (3.61%) confirms adequate sampling. Future studies with larger cohorts (n ≥ 6) and metabolic phenotyping may further clarify these sources.

The pharmacodynamic evaluation in the DSS-induced UC mouse model provided compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of BAC-SD. DSS-induced colitis is a well-established model that recapitulates key features of human UC, including weight loss, colon shortening, increased DAI scores, and histopathological damage(Gao et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2021; Sato et al., 2025). In this study, BAC-SD significantly ameliorated all these clinical and pathological parameters, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to BAC and the physical mixture. The marked improvement in colon length and spleen index further supports the protective effect of BAC-SD against DSS-induced inflammation(Gao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025).

Mechanistically, the anti-inflammatory effects of BAC-SD were reflected in the reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) and the decreased activity of MPO, a marker of neutrophil infiltration. These findings indicate that BAC-SD effectively suppresses the inflammatory response in the colon, which is a key therapeutic target in UC. Additionally, the reduction in MDA levels suggests that BAC-SD alleviates oxidative stress, another critical contributor to UC pathogenesis(Dong et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2022). The decreased serum DAO levels in BAC-SD-treated mice indicate improved intestinal barrier integrity, as DAO is a marker of mucosal damage and increased permeability(Li et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). Collectively, these results demonstrate that BAC-SD exerts its therapeutic effects through multiple mechanisms, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and intestinal barrier-protective activities.

The superior efficacy of BAC-SD compared to the physical mixture highlights the importance of achieving an amorphous state and molecular-level dispersion of baicalein within the hydrophilic carriers(Iyer et al., 2021). The physical mixture, despite containing the same components, failed to produce comparable improvements in solubility, permeability, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy, underscoring the critical role of the solid dispersion formulation strategy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that solid dispersion technology effectively addresses the pharmaceutical limitations of baicalein, enhancing its solubility, permeability, oral bioavailability, and anti-UC efficacy. The successful preparation of BAC-SD using PVP and HPMC as carriers offers a practical and scalable approach for developing an improved baicalein formulation. The comprehensive characterization, in vitro and in vivo evaluations provide a solid foundation for further clinical development of baicalein-based therapies for ulcerative colitis.
