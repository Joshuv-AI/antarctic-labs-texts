Barriers and facilitators in hospital-based physiotherapy services in Iran: a qualitative study using the theoretical domains framework
Authors: Shabnam ShahAli, Fahimeh Karshenas, Saeed Shahabi, Marzieh Mohamadi, Mehrnaz Kajbafvala, Aida Eghtesad, Masoud Behzadifar, Parisa Naserian Khiabani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-based physiotherapy can play an important role in the multidisciplinary treatment of hospitalised patients by optimising their functional mobility as part of the patient’s overall functional health condition. Considering that hospital-based physiotherapy services are structured differently among countries, providing hospital-based physiotherapy services may also face different barriers and facilitators.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the potential barriers and facilitators in providing hospital-based physiotherapy services in Iran.DesignBased on the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), individual semistructured interviews were conducted to identify barriers and facilitators in the delivery of physiotherapy services in hospital settings. Purposive and snowball sampling was conducted to gather information from clinical physiotherapists and physiotherapy professors.SettingThis study was conducted from September 2023 until October 2024 in Iran.ResultsInformation was obtained from 39 clinical physiotherapists and 4 physiotherapy professors. According to TDF, 64 barriers and 66 facilitators were determined in hospital-based physiotherapy in Iran which were categorised into 11 and 12 domains, respectively. The most frequent barriers were found in the ‘environment context and resources’ domain. Further, ‘Social/professional role and identity’, ‘reinforcement’ and ‘emotion’ were ranked, in order, as the next most frequent domains in which the constructs were categorised. Also, the most frequent codes, ‘lack of interdisciplinary collaboration’ and ‘lack of proper equipment’, belong to ‘Social/professional role and identity’ and ‘environment context and resources’ domains, respectively. Similarly, among facilitators, ‘environment context and resources’ is the most common domain that was addressed. After that, ‘Social/professional role and identity’, ‘beliefs about consequences’, ‘knowledge’, ‘reinforcement’ and ‘intentions’ were ranked respectively as frequent domains that were mentioned. Similar to barriers, the most frequent code, ‘attention to infrastructures’, belongs to the ‘environment context and resources’ domain. Then, with a negligible difference, ‘professional interactions’ in the ‘Social/professional role and identity’ domain were defined as the second most frequent code in this study.ConclusionThis study identified key facilitators and barriers to hospital-based physiotherapy in Iran across TDF domains. These findings highlight the need for strengthening professional identity, improving interprofessional collaboration, and addressing structural and motivational barriers. These insights offer a foundation for targeted strategies to enhance physiotherapy integration into hospital care and inform future approaches tailored to the Iranian healthcare context.

Background

Health promotion is universally recognised as essential to tackling lifestyle conditions.1 2 There is a concerted effort in the physiotherapy literature to focus the profession on health promotion.3 4 Due to its established commitment to health through non-invasive interventions and practice patterns that allow for frequent and prolonged patient contact, physiotherapy is in an ideal position to incorporate health promotion into practice.57

Hospital-based physiotherapy can play an important role in the multidisciplinary treatment of individuals requiring hospitalisation by optimising their functional mobility as part of the patient’s overall functional health condition.7 Hospital environments are dynamic, requiring physiotherapists to adapt to changing patient conditions, treatment priorities and psychosocial factors.8 9 For example, a physiotherapist needs an in-depth understanding of multiple body systems throughout a person’s lifespan to accommodate the variable mix of patients requiring acute care.8 10 11 It is also essential that physiotherapists adapt to health conditions, align with other healthcare disciplines and manage acute health conditions.8 10 12

Physiotherapy in acute care can be challenging and complex, with patients, staff (nurses, medical practitioners and other allied health professionals) and sometimes physiotherapists themselves having variable understanding of the physiotherapy role.13 14 Studies of hospital-based physiotherapy in Europe,15 Australia16 and Canada5 have revealed differences in practice, staffing and training between countries and regions. Considering that hospital-based physiotherapy services are structured differently among countries, providing hospital-based physiotherapy services may also face different barriers and facilitators. Recent qualitative and mixed-methods studies have highlighted the influence of professional beliefs, organisational structures and systemic barriers on physiotherapy practice and development. For example, Dinsdale et al identified how career motivations and barriers shape physiotherapists’ engagement with professional development,17 while Gleadhill et al emphasised the role of organisational and individual factors in implementing evidence-based care.18 Similarly, emerging evidence from low-income and middle-income countries highlights structural and resource-related challenges affecting physiotherapy service delivery (Shakya et al).19

A growing body of evidence supports the importance of hospital-based physiotherapy and physiotherapists are now recommended to play an integral role in the recovery of hospitalised patients.20 Despite the increasing demand for physiotherapy in hospital settings, the profession in Iran faces systemic constraints. National-level data on hospital-based physiotherapy policies remain scarce, and there is limited integration into interdisciplinary care pathways. This gap highlights the need for structured exploration using a comprehensive framework. The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF)21 offers a well-established approach to systematically investigate behavioural determinants across individual, organisational and contextual levels—making it suitable for exploring implementation barriers and facilitators in healthcare setting in Iran. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the potential barriers and facilitators in providing hospital-based physiotherapy services in Iran.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative method using semistructured interviews was chosen for this study to fully reveal subjective items from participants. This study was conducted from September 2023 until October 2024 in Iran. The ‘Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) Qualitative Checklist’22 and ‘Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)’23 guidelines were considered for methodological and reporting quality, respectively. The Institutional Review Board of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (No. 29306) granted approval for its protocol. Barriers and facilitators in hospital-based physiotherapy were detected by applying the TDF to the data. The TDF is a theoretical base originating in health psychology studies, and its purpose is to provide a wide range of explanations about behaviour variations. Therefore, the study aimed to explore a broad range of experiences related to the implementation context, without constraining responses to specific intervention outcomes.24 25

The research team acknowledged their professional backgrounds in physiotherapy and qualitative research as potential sources of bias. To mitigate this, reflexive journaling and regular debriefing sessions were employed during data collection and analysis, ensuring a balance between researcher insight and participant voice.

Sampling and recruiting approach

Purposive sampling was used initially for involving both physiotherapy clinicians who have been working in hospitals and physiotherapy professors who are employed to teach hospital-based theoretical and practical courses to physiotherapy students in hospitals. The Medical Council of the Islamic Republic of Iran (IRIMC) and the Iranian Scientometric Information Database (ISID) were explored to identify eligible physiotherapy clinicians and professors, respectively. Potential participants were initially contacted via email and professional social media platforms (eg, WhatsApp and Telegram), using contact information obtained from professional registries and academic databases. Inclusion criteria included: (1) holding a degree in physiotherapy, (2) having at least 1 year of clinical experience, and (3) current or prior work experience in hospital settings. No restrictions were placed on age, gender or type of hospital. Then, sampling was continued parallelly in the snowball method by asking participants to introduce other potential physiotherapists. In this study, an effort has been made to consider diversity in the demographic data of participants such as gender, age, membership type in hospital, city of employment, work duration, educational level and employment status. First, all participants were contacted to be informed about the study process, purposes and general information about the research team. Then, a voluntary consent form was sent to them via social media. Sampling continued to the point of data saturation when no new findings were gained in the last four interviews.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted either face-to-face or online, depending on participant preference and logistical considerations such as geographic location. Online interviews were conducted using either video calls or social media voice recordings. A group of authors were responsible for conducting individual, semistructured interviews. The meeting began with a summary of the study information, followed by another request for their voluntary consent. Before initiating the main conversation, demographic data were collected. Open-ended questions from the interview guide (box 1) were considered to direct the interview flow. The interview guide was developed based on the TDF domains and reviewed by the senior authors for content relevance and clarity. The guide was then piloted during the initial interviews to ensure comprehensibility and appropriateness, and minor wording refinements were made. These questions aimed to explore participants’ experiences and perceptions regarding interprofessional interactions in hospital settings. All interviews followed a semistructured guide and were conducted via secure online platforms (eg, Skype/Zoom), with standardised introductory scripts to ensure consistency across sessions. Regardless of the type of interview session (online or face-to-face), all answers were saved in recording files. The verbatim transcripts were written separately in an Office Word file at the end of each interview. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by members of the research team. To enhance credibility, verbatim transcripts were returned to participants for member checking to ensure accuracy and alignment with their experiences; analytical interpretations were not returned.

Data analysis

All transcripts were managed and analysed using MAXQDA 2018 (Berlin, Germany: VERBI Software GmbH, 2017). A directed content analysis was employed, informed by the TDF, to guide the coding and interpretation of data. The TDF comprises 14 theoretical domains derived from health psychology and behaviour change theory. The framework developed by Cane et al26 and further elaborated by Atkins et al27 was used to identify and map barriers and facilitators influencing physiotherapists’ behaviour. In this regard, data can be analysed both deductively (applying TDF to create a framework) and inductively (developing themes in relation to predefined themes).27 The TDF includes 84 constructs that are classified in 14 domains: ‘Knowledge’, ‘Skills’, ‘Social/Professional Role and Identity’, ‘Beliefs about Capabilities’, ‘Optimism’, ‘Beliefs about Consequences’, ‘Reinforcement’, ‘Intentions’, ‘Goals’, ‘Memory, Attention and Decision Processes’, ‘Environmental Context and Resources’, ‘Social Influences’, ‘Emotions’ and ‘Behavioral Regulation’.26 To analyse the collected data, two independent authors (SHSH and SSH) repeatedly read the transcripts to achieve familiarity with the data. Then, they separately coded each utterance inductively according to the TDF.

To enhance the transparency and rigour of the analytical process, each extracted code was systematically mapped to the relevant constructs within the TDF. Coding was performed iteratively, combining inductive identification of themes with deductive alignment to predefined TDF domains and constructs. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion among the research team. This approach enabled the research team to capture both context-specific insights and theoretically grounded behavioural determinants influencing hospital-based physiotherapy services. They resolved all contradictions by conferring with each other and, if necessary, involving the third author (FK). Same codes were tallied associated with the utterances of different participants. Direct quotes were inserted to support the codes.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To improve the rigour and trustworthiness of qualitative studies, several approaches have been depicted to make credibility, confirmability, authenticity, transferability and dependability of the findings better.28 To enhance transferability, a wide range of participant characteristics was considered. The research team has also made an effort to gather samples from various parts of Iran, particularly populated cities. Peer consultation was used to enhance credibility by resolving inconsistencies in data analysis. Researchers provided each participant with the written script and related analysis to ensure confirmability. The researchers considered quotes from nearly all participants to verify the authenticity of the findings. To ensure reliability, a research team comprising individuals with diverse backgrounds was assembled. Ultimately, to enhance transferability, a wide range of participant characteristics was considered.

Characteristics of participants

Barriers

Knowledge

‘Graduates lack of familiarity with hospital-related physiotherapy’ is the most frequent obstacle mentioned by physiotherapists in this domain. According to several participants, universities often fail to provide adequate opportunities for physiotherapy students to engage with hospital-specific issues, such as sufficient familiarity with medical information or the description of duties and the position of physiotherapists in hospitals for physiotherapy students, even though due to the high sensitivity of work in hospitals, mastery of such issues is necessary to establish effective communication with medical staff and providing minimal services to patients. On the other hand, during the internship periods, the education system focuses on services in clinics, and in the limited internships held in the hospital, course teachers are always the mediators between the medical staff and the students. Therefore, it takes time for newly graduated physiotherapists to become familiar with hospital workflows through practical experience.

Sometimes, the education system may not play its role properly. It leads to obstacles for our colleagues which relate to malpractice in preparing physiotherapists to enter the hospital environments.(28)

Lack of clinical knowledge by physiotherapists is identified as another important obstacle in this domain. According to physiotherapists, the lack of sufficient knowledge of physiotherapists about the various aspects of their profession such as complete information about indications and contraindications of physiotherapy interventions in hospital patients, the absence of elite professors in hospital-based physiotherapy and the lack of practical courses in accordance with the needs of physiotherapists in hospitals limit the effective presence of physiotherapists in hospitals.

“I think a gap is felt in hospital-based training. The hospitals sometimes organize courses, but their professors are not physiotherapists and are not familiar with our field. The information which is taught in these courses is very good, however, this is not exclusive enough to help our efficiency at work.”(23)

Skill

From the Iranian physiotherapists’ point of view, the most important barrier in this domain is ‘communication problems’. This problem has been surveyed from two perspectives. A group of physiotherapists believe that it is a difficult task to communicate with patients due to their sensitive conditions. This problem is more visible in patients in departments rather than in patients in intensive care unit (ICU), due to the alertness of patients in departments and the presence of their companions by their side. Another group of physiotherapists believes that communicating with the medical staff, especially physicians, is very difficult in hospitals, which is because of several factors, such as the lack of familiarity between the rehabilitation and the medical groups, the sensitive conditions of the patients in the hospital and presence of problems between different professions. Inadequate interaction with medical staff may give rise to unprofessional behaviour toward physiotherapists, undermine the provision of appropriate physiotherapy services and ultimately compromise patient outcomes. Physiotherapists point to the frequent prescription of physiotherapy for patients who do not need it or not prescribing MRI in cases where the physiotherapist needs it, as common examples of this case.

Unfortunately, as a physiotherapist, I could not communicate well with neurology patients or even with neurologists. Because these patients generally have very bad conditions and everyone, including the physicians, nurses, and the patient’s companion are very sensitive to them, it is difficult to convince the patient even for a simple walk.(22)

Social/professional role and identity

From the physiotherapists’ point of view, lack of interdisciplinary collaboration is one of the two most frequent obstacles in providing physiotherapy services in Iranian hospitals. This problem has two different aspects. First, there is a lack of teamwork in performing prescribed physiotherapy interventions among colleagues in different fields such as nurses in the hospital. Physiotherapists have admitted that sometimes nurses do not provide a patient’s history file or even a summary of the patient’s information, or that there is no proper coordination between nurses and physiotherapists in setting a suitable time for the presence of physiotherapists in the departments or the lack of permission to perform certain physiotherapy interventions, often due to the physician-led referral system and limited autonomy of physiotherapists within hospital setting.

There must be coordination with nurses to allocate the right time to provide physiotherapy services.(26)

The noteworthy point in this regard is the physiotherapists’ emphasis on the existence of teamwork with serious attention to professional boundaries. Physiotherapists have mentioned the interference of nurses in the specialised affairs of physiotherapists or the unreasonable expectations of physiotherapists in doing things that are not in their scope.

I would say that anyone would allow themselves to say that we can do this technique, once they observe its implementation.(27)

Second, there is a lack of consultation and consensus between medical experts and physiotherapists in prescribing different physiotherapy interventions. A large number of participants in this study have mentioned a similar experience in that they had patients who were prescribed physiotherapy services despite the clear presence of contraindications. They pointed out that due to the physician-based system in Iran’s medical system, physician imperative orders and medical practitioners’ distrust of physiotherapists and their clinical decisions, it is not usually beneficial to insist on convincing the medical staff.

The physician thinks that he has knowledge in all fields and can intervene in all situations, while he may not have much knowledge about the science of physiotherapy and does not know the indications and contraindications of these interventions. Some physicians do not heed their instructions at all. They do not want to accept the words of a physiotherapist. These cases are very annoying.(13)

From physiotherapists’ perspective, the cause of such an event, which ends up harming the patient, can be seen in two very frequent codes of this domain, ‘unawareness of medical staff about physiotherapy’ and ‘weak position of physiotherapy in hospital’. A large number of physiotherapists complain about the lack of physicians’ and nurses’ knowledge about indications and contraindications and even the scope of duties and interventions of physiotherapy. These findings suggest that awareness of physiotherapy roles among medical staff remains limited. The physiotherapy position in hospitals is considered very weak. In such a way, physiotherapists are mistakenly known as masseurs or patient transport staff. So, physiotherapists are not usually appreciated, the consultation and consensus with physiotherapists are considered unimportant by the medical staff, the advice of physiotherapists is considered an insult to the medical staff, and physiotherapists are not given enough facilities and access in hospitals. Finally, the patient will bear the consequences of these limitations.

Beliefs about capabilities

In this domain, the two-faceted problem is mentioned. On the one hand, physiotherapists do not have enough self-confidence to be among other specialists in the hospital. Of course, the hospital environment adds to this lack of self-confidence. On the other hand, some physiotherapists have a self-superiority vision and assume that they do not need to explain and communicate with some professionals in hospitals such as nurses.

Beliefs about consequences

‘Underestimating the value of physiotherapy interventions’ is the most important code in this domain. Usually, due to the simple implementation of physiotherapy techniques, the low cost of these interventions, and even the fact that they are free in some hospitals, the lack of sufficient knowledge of patients and medical staff physiotherapy, the value of this service is underestimated in hospitals and is considered as low-level intervention. Physiotherapists even point out that the medical staff do not understand the importance of these interventions and in some cases, they consider these interventions to be harmful for the patient. As a result, people usually refuse to receive physiotherapy services. However, in many cases, after the bad consequences of this decision, such as joint contracture or lung infection, they ask for physiotherapy for their patients again.

Although physiotherapy interventions seem to be low level, there are many patients who developed multiple contractures without physiotherapy.(6)

Reinforcement

These four frequently reported codes—‘Inadequate payment’, ‘mismatch between work pressure and reimbursement’, ‘discrimination in behaving with physiotherapists’ and ‘low hospital tariff’—collectively represent key barriers reported by participants within the reinforcement domain. The tariff for physiotherapy interventions in hospitals, especially public hospitals, is very low and disproportionate to their pressure and workload. This has unfortunate consequences. One of them is that physiotherapists do not try to improve themselves, because they know that several people, regardless of the quality of the services that are provided, only come to hospitals because of the low cost of these services. On the other hand, according to the importance of hospital departments based on their overall income, usually, management ignores the physiotherapy department for allocating facilities and credits to improve the department due to low overall income, following low tariffs. To compensate, more pressure is put on physiotherapists to visit more patients. Even weak insurance coverage worsens the existing situation, due to the low ceiling for daily physiotherapy sessions and the number of body areas. Discrimination in behaviour with physiotherapists adds to the previous problems. Unlike other medical staff in the hospital, physiotherapists are not considered a clinical workforce, despite their direct presence with patients. Moreover, despite high workload and exposure to physically and psychologically demanding conditions, physiotherapists are not formally recognised under occupational categories eligible for additional compensation or benefits associated with high-risk or demanding roles. For example, physiotherapists point to the period of the COVID-19 pandemic:

Especially during the time of COVID-19 pandemic, many of the colleagues who worked in the clinics didn’t go to work due to the quarantine. But we still had to see patients in the hospital and go to the ICU with the same salary as in the past. This was while nurses were receiving much better financial services.(8)

Intentions

‘Thirst to make money’ is the most frequent code in this domain. Physiotherapists claim that hospital finances are very important for hospital management. Therefore, they emphasise increasing the income of the departments regardless of the quality of their services. This wrong financial policy leads physiotherapists to visit more patients or to perform physiotherapy services for patients who do not need these interventions at all and this service is only ordered to receive the physiotherapy tariff from the patients. In a few cases, the physiotherapist’s opinion is to prioritise financial issues over the quality of services. Finally, a decrease in the quality of physiotherapy services, more use of electrotherapy instead of manual interventions and therapeutic exercises, and injustice to the patient’s rights are actualised at the cost of increasing the number of visited patients and the overall income of the hospital.

In Iran, high income is so important that they have put physiotherapy in people’s minds with electrotherapy devices and do not value manual techniques at all.(2)

Memory, attention, and decision processes

In some cases, the discrepancy between the physician’s orders and the physiotherapist’s knowledge leads to confusion in providing the best practice.

Also, the lack of importance of the physiotherapy department for hospital management due to the low profits compared with other departments leads to the lack of promotion in this department.

Because other parts of the hospital generate more income, they are promoted more.(30)

Environment

According to the physiotherapists’ opinion, this domain includes the many problems about physiotherapy service in Iranian hospitals. ‘Lack of proper equipment’ in both inpatient and outpatient departments of hospitals is one of the two problems that have been mentioned the most in this study. Physiotherapists point out that hospitals, especially public hospitals, often do not have modern equipment, also in many cases, there is little and obsolescent equipment. Sometimes hospitals do not even have minimal equipment to provide services.

Although we do not need special tools to work in inpatient departments, sometimes very simple equipment such as vibrators for percussion is not available there.(12)

The physiotherapy department in hospitals is usually under the pressure of management. Some aspects of this problem have been mentioned in other domains too. Also, the management of the hospital interferes in this department, usually, the management of this department is assigned to other specialists and unreasonable expectations are established without providing adequate facilities.

On the other hand, the management is focused on the physiotherapy department and they are sensitive that physiotherapy must be done, but they do not consider the proportion between patients and hospital staff number. Also, they do not provide facilities and proper space and other things to the physiotherapist and they only expect services to be provided.(20)

Physiotherapists in hospitals are faced with a huge number of patients. However, there are not enough physiotherapists in hospitals, nor is this department given enough assistance staff to perform interventions, especially in inpatient departments. Therefore, there is a high workload and low quality of physiotherapy services in hospitals. This interpretation is supported by participants’ comments, such as participant 3.

Social influences

‘Unawareness of people about physiotherapy’ leads to the fact that it becomes difficult to convince patients to perform physiotherapy interventions for individuals receiving care in hospital. This phenomenon is especially noticeable in specialised hospitals such as heart hospitals or in ICU departments. Patients usually think that this service is a joke, an excuse to get more money from patients, and a worthless and useless intervention.

They think we are playing a joke and our work has no effect. Especially in the respiratory cases, I encountered the issue that I give breathing exercises and perform a series of techniques, and then the patient says that he does not want physiotherapy services and thinks that the money he gave is not worth it. However, I was called later to visit the same patient again because the secretions in his lungs were too much.(23)

Emotion

Due to a set of reasons that were discussed in detail in the previous sections, as well as the condescending behaviours of medical staff and patients and the work process that is both mentally and physically exhausting for physiotherapists, physiotherapy in hospitals’ settings is not considered a preferred workplace choice by physiotherapists. After entering the hospital environment, the young and motivated physiotherapists are faced with inappropriate working conditions in the hospital and gradually become hopeless and demotivated, and if they are not forced to work in the hospital and can work in the private clinics, after a while they quit working in the hospital.

When I started working in the hospital, I felt great. I was trying to create a good relationship with patients, nurses, and physicians and I thought that we are all a team that is supposed to bring the patient to a good condition. But over time, due to issues related to the opinionated physician and their lack of knowledge about our field, the interference of nurses, financial issues, and even issues related to physiotherapist colleagues, I do not have the previous job satisfaction and motivation anymore.(23)

Facilitators

In addition to the barriers identified, several facilitators were consistently reported by physiotherapists. Within the knowledge and skill domains, hospitals were perceived as valuable environments for professional growth due to interdisciplinary exposure and access to diverse patient populations. In the professional identity and social roles domain, collaboration with physicians and visibility in multidisciplinary teams were noted as empowering. Facilitators under beliefs about capabilities and consequences included confidence in clinical abilities and the observation of tangible patient improvements, especially in critical care settings. The reinforcement domain highlighted the importance of equity in salary, managerial autonomy for physiotherapists and alignment of financial incentives with workload. Other facilitators included intentions to raise awareness about physiotherapy among patients and colleagues, organisational support from professional associations and motivation driven by patient satisfaction. Lastly, recommendations such as modernising departmental management and enhancing infrastructure were noted under behavioural regulation and environmental resources.

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to identify and explore potential barriers and facilitators in providing hospital-based physiotherapy services in Iran. This discussion reflects insights from 43 physiotherapists working in hospital settings across Iran. The analysis identified a total of 64 barriers and 66 facilitators in providing hospital-based physiotherapy services in Iran, which were categorised into 11 and 12 domains of the TDF, respectively. While individual experiences of physiotherapists were explored, the primary focus of this study was to identify system-level and behavioural determinants influencing the delivery of hospital-based physiotherapy services, rather than personal career trajectories.

Barriers to providing hospital-based physiotherapy services

In the current study, the interviews revealed multiple barriers to providing hospital-based physiotherapy services. Many participants identified social/professional role and identity, environmental context, and resources as considerable barriers to delivering hospital-based physiotherapy services followed by reinforcement, emotion, skills, knowledge, social influences, intentions, beliefs about consequences, memory/attention/decision-making processes, and beliefs about capabilities.

Although hospital-based physiotherapists are regarded as integral team members in many international contexts,2932 findings from this study suggest that in Iranian hospitals, the system as perceived by physiotherapists remains challenging, particularly in relation to interdisciplinary collaboration and continuity of care. Participants also highlighted barriers related to the awareness of other professions about physiotherapy, lack of interdisciplinary collaboration and unclear professional boundaries. To improve collaborative practice, it is essential to implement interprofessional education.33 Poor or inadequate knowledge of healthcare practitioners’ roles and scopes profoundly affects the health and well-being of communities and populations.34 35 The significance of physiotherapy and its functions within hospital settings should be integrated into the education of physicians and other medical professionals. Physicians and physiotherapists need to comprehend the scope and boundaries of emerging roles and establish practice standards and protocols that optimise benefits for patients, the practice and within the healthcare system.

Participants also raised concerns regarding insufficient payment, weak insurance coverage, low hospital tariffs, mismatch of work pressure, and reimbursement, lack of tariff for some interventions in the hospital, and inadequate compensation for extra patient visits. It is partly the result of a lack of federal regulation and recognition of physiotherapy competencies and unclear instructions regarding physiotherapy payment in Iran, which prevents well-qualified physiotherapy professionals from fully using their competencies and taking responsibility for their practice scope.36 In the Iranian context, prior research has also emphasised systemic challenges affecting the physiotherapy sector, including policy and financing constraints37 and broader inefficiencies in hospital payment systems.38 These findings align with the present study’s identification of reinforcement and environmental domains as critical determinants.

Several environmental contexts and resource factors pose barriers to hospital-based physiotherapy in Iran. Physiotherapists frequently cite high workloads, inadequate physiotherapy workforce, lack of proper equipment, overcrowding in hospital centres, unstable patients and time constraints as barriers to providing hospital-based physiotherapy. The findings of this study are consistent with previous research highlighting the complex interplay between individual, organisational and systemic factors influencing physiotherapy practice. For instance, Boyd et al demonstrated challenges in implementing patient-centred approaches within hospital settings, particularly due to time constraints and organisational barriers.39 Similarly, studies in comparable contexts have reported limitations in infrastructure, workforce capacity and policy support as key barriers to effective physiotherapy service delivery.19 A comprehensive and effective referral system is crucial for reducing hospital overcrowding and workload.40 Furthermore, compared with chronic care physiotherapy settings, where continuity of care, long-term patient engagement and structured rehabilitation pathways are more established, hospital-based (acute care) physiotherapy is often characterised by time constraints, high patient turnover and limited interdisciplinary coordination.12 39 41 These differences may explain the heightened emphasis on environmental constraints and professional role ambiguity observed in this study. In contrast, chronic care settings may allow for stronger therapeutic relationships and clearer role definition, potentially reducing some of the barriers identified in acute hospital environments.

Facilitators in providing hospital-based physiotherapy services

Unlike the barriers, facilitators in this study often reflected the presence of enabling systems or conditions rather than the absence of obstacles. For example, while the lack of interdisciplinary collaboration was a key barrier, the presence of collaborative communication with physicians and nurses emerged as a facilitator. Similarly, rather than merely lacking equipment, some participants identified departments with proper infrastructure and staffing as supportive environments for effective physiotherapy. These contrasts suggest that improvement strategies should not only remove barriers but also actively build on existing strengths and best practices within the system.

The present study’s findings indicated that many participants believed that ongoing education and research across various fields, adherence to established guidelines and high standards, the development of communication skills, and increased knowledge and clinical skills could contribute to facilitating hospital-based physiotherapy services. Enhancing the knowledge and skills of physiotherapists, as well as addressing their concerns, is therefore essential for facilitating the availability of hospital-based physiotherapy services. This aligns with existing literature that suggests enhanced communication practices, along with improved clinical knowledge and skills, can facilitate interpersonal interactions and augment the effectiveness of interventions.40 42

Enhancing the role of physiotherapists within the medical system, fostering professional interactions, facilitating interdisciplinary collaboration and increasing trust and satisfaction among medical staff were also identified as potential facilitating factors. A prior study advocates for enhanced collaboration and information sharing among service systems to promote evidence-based physiotherapy practice in Iran.36 By understanding each other’s roles and responsibilities, health practitioners from different fields are better able to provide comprehensive and coordinated client care, which may result in superior results.6 These findings highlighted the importance of establishing strong links with other health professionals, such as general practitioners, who often refer patients to physiotherapy services.

Beliefs about consequences were identified as a prominent facilitator factor. Participants commonly believed that physiotherapy had a positive impact on patient outcomes, which in turn appeared to influence the likelihood of its use in hospitals. While the study does not provide empirical validation of these outcomes, the perceived effectiveness by clinicians acted as a strong motivating factor and facilitator for service provision. A prior study indicates that physiotherapists’ observations of functional improvements in stroke patients serve to validate the value of the intervention, thereby facilitating the implementation of high-intensity gait training for inpatient stroke patients.43 These findings emphasise the importance of hospital-based physiotherapy.

This present study identified some reinforcement factors that facilitate the implementation of hospital-based physiotherapy. The enhancement of physiotherapists’ salaries, equitable treatment of physiotherapists and alignment of tariffs with workload were cited as facilitators for hospital-based physiotherapy services. The findings suggest that modifications to reimbursement mechanisms, coupled with the implementation of effective incentives, may considerably enhance the provision of hospital-based physiotherapy services.

Additional facilitating factors, referring to intentions and goals, were identified including informing medical staff and the public about physiotherapy, as well as the establishment of dedicated physiotherapy departments within hospitals. This aligns with previous research that has reported the general public and healthcare professionals do not have a comprehensive understanding of physiotherapy’s scope, and they need more information regarding physiotherapy services.4446 It is recommended to introduce physiotherapy in interprofessional undergraduate training or during clinical opportunities as practical steps to increase health professionals’ understanding of hospital-based physiotherapy services.

This study identified several environmental context and resource factors that facilitate the implementation of hospital-based physiotherapy, such as the provision of equipment, a reduced patient-to-physiotherapist ratio, the availability of assistants, the consideration of adequate spaces, an increase in the number of physiotherapists and decreasing the work pressure. These findings are consistent with the specified responsibilities of physiotherapists as outlined in the guidelines for the provision of intensive care services.47 These findings underscore the ambiguity and lack of consensus within Iranian upstream laws and policies. The absence of standardised definitions for rehabilitation services, including hospital-based physiotherapy, in national legislation gives rise to multiple challenges in their implementation.48 Currently, Iran does not possess a dedicated information infrastructure capable of supporting the collection of clinical and administrative data pertinent to physiotherapy.49 The establishment of a comprehensive system for the collection, analysis and reporting of critical information regarding the utilisation, access, quality and outcomes of rehabilitation services, following existing guidelines, is essential for informing clinical governance, quality improvement and decision-making processes in policy formulation.48

Participants emphasised the role of social influences, emotions and behavioural regulation as factors that may support or hinder physiotherapy services. These domains suggest a need for greater institutional recognition of physiotherapists’ contributions, as well as mechanisms for supporting their motivation and autonomy. These findings align with prior research that underscores the influence of patient progress on clinician motivation.42 The emotional and social burden experienced by physiotherapists highlights the necessity for improved support systems to assist them in managing these stresses.

The findings of this study have several important implications. At the policy level, there is a need to establish clear regulations and reimbursement structures that recognise physiotherapy as an integral component of hospital care. At the organisational level, improving staffing ratios, infrastructure and access to equipment can significantly enhance service delivery. From a professional perspective, integrating interprofessional education and promoting awareness of physiotherapy roles among healthcare providers may improve collaboration. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating targeted interventions based on the identified TDF domains to enhance the implementation of hospital-based physiotherapy services.

Limitations

The qualitative study is subject to several limitations. First, this study only investigated the experiences of physiotherapists employed part-time or full-time in hospital settings. So, future research could examine the potential barriers and facilitators in delivering hospital-based physiotherapy services from the perspectives of other team members and also service users. Second, the use of snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias by recruiting participants with shared professionals network or similar perspectives. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the transferability of findings. Third, the reliance on self-reported data limits the objectivity of some insights which can improve by future quantitative studies. Fourth, a further limitation is that the type of hospital was not recorded; therefore, we were unable to examine whether perceptions differed according to hospital characteristics or clinical setting.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identified key facilitators and barriers to hospital-based physiotherapy in Iran across TDF domains. Emphasis should be placed on strengthening professional identity, improving interprofessional collaboration and addressing structural and motivational barriers. These insights offer a foundation for targeted reforms to enhance physiotherapy integration into hospital care and inform future strategies tailored to the Iranian healthcare context.
