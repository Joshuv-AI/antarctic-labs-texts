Oral contraceptives and tissue factor expression. Comments on “Do combined oral contraceptives induce formation of tissue factor?”: reply
Authors: Søren Risom Kristensen, Jette Nybo, Jesper Strandberg
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We want to thank Østerud for his interest in our recent publication [1] and for his valuable comments.

Østerud [2] refers to some of his previous publications demonstrating that oral contraceptives (COCs) [3] or increased estrogen, due to pregnancy [4] or hormone replacement therapy [5], do not induce increased tissue factor (TF) activity in monocytes [3]. In a small case-control study including 13 women taking various types of COC, they found lower TF activity in monocytes among COC users compared with nonusers, although the difference was not significant due to substantial variation [2]. In endotoxin-stimulated monocytes, TF activity was significantly lower in COC users [3]. In 2 other studies, they found lower TF activity in monocytes from pregnant women [4] and women using hormone replacement therapy [5], ie, 2 groups also with increased estrogen levels, indicating an anti-inflammatory effect of estrogen.

We must admit that we did not find this 40-year-old paper [3] during our search for studies investigating the effects of COC on TF. We found the 1999 study by Hölschermann et al. [6], which reported increased TF activity in monocytes of COC users, as measured by coagulation activity and gene expression, ie, a finding contrary to Østerud’s findings. They used monocytes that had been incubated for a short period before investigation (in contrast to Østerud, who investigated monocytes immediately after isolation from blood), and Østerud suggests that the results from Hölschermann et al. [6] may have been due to preactivation during this incubation step. Østerud is an expert on these monocytes, and we cannot, of course, exclude this possibility, but Hölschermann et al. [6] actually found quite large differences, and the different groups in that study were probably treated in exactly the same way. Nevertheless, our results are in accordance with those of Omsjø et al. [3], and we think that it is a strength that this has now been shown in plasma by measuring TF in extracellular vesicles (EVs) [1]. This paired study [1] excludes between-subject variation, which substantially reduces the total variation of the measurements in the groups (there was large variation in the study by Østerud); therefore, in addition to being consistent with the previous study [3], it also strengthens this conclusion. We thank Dr Østerud for bringing this additional information to our attention.

Finally, regarding the comment on the possible interference of procoagulant phospholipids (PPLs) with the measured activity, we called it a sensitive test [7] because we eliminated, or at least considerably reduced, interferences (ie, we used tubes with low contact activation, added antibodies against TF pathway inhibitor, etc.), isolated EVs, and added a concentrated amount to the assay. PPLs from EVs are not likely to have any influence on the measured TF activity. First, we took measurements using reagents containing 4 μmol/L PPLs and showed that the addition of 1 or 2 μmol/L had only a small effect on activity [7], an amount probably much higher than the amount of PPLs added from the EVs. Second, and most importantly, we measured activation after the addition of antibodies against TF. This activity is small, much lower than that in the absence of antibodies, but it is subtracted from the latter. Thus, the results reflect TF-dependent activity and, consequently, the potential interference from PPLs will be minimal.
