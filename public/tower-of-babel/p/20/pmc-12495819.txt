Priority setting in critical care: a multicriteria approach to ranking access to Intensive Care Unit beds
Authors: Ana Flávia A dos Santos, Lucas A dos Santos, Talita D C Frazão, Amanda G de Assis, Maiko S C de Oliveira, João F da Costa Júnior, Patrícia Angélica T da S Ferro, Ricardo P de Souza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a situation in public health services where there is a high demand for services but not enough capacity to meet that demand. Within the critical care landscape in Brazil, there exists a significant disparity: of the total of 45,848 beds in intensive care units, only 49% are accessible to the Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde - SUS), while the remaining 51% are exclusively allocated to a mere 23% of the population. Therefore, multicriteria decision analysis has the potential to be utilized as a tool in the decision-making process since it has been stated as a tool that promotes transparency and brings rationality to the decision-making process. The objective of this study was to create a multi-criteria model that can assist in the allocation of beds in Adult Intensive Care Units in the state of Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil.MethodsA twelve-step framework was used to carry out the decision-aiding process. The PROMETHEE I and II methods were employed to build the multicriteria model. Three stages were followed: problem structuring, preference modeling and aggregation, and finalization. The decision problem was approached from the perspective of the P. ranking problem in which six clinical vignettes based on actual patients were ranked in order of preference, defined by the decision-maker’s preference system.ResultsTwo pre-orders were recommended: a partial one, in which four pairs of actions were incomparable with each other; and a complete one, in which relationships between each pair of alternatives were described. The sensitivity analysis revealed considerable robustness of the model because of the range of the stability interval.ConclusionThe partial ranking proposed by PROMETHEE I showed that Patient 6 outranks all the patients within the set of alternatives. When the complete ranking was generated, aided by PROMETHEE II, Patient 6 remained as the alternative that outranks all the others. Therefore, considering the resource-constrained scenario, Patient 6 was recommended to be admitted. The proposed model formalized the decision-making process of prioritizing access to Adult Intensive Care Unit beds, ensuring transparency and rationality by structuring all relevant factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-12647-3.Keywords: Healthcare resource allocation, Decision-making, Priority setting, Critical care, Multi-criteria decision analysis

Introduction

The concept of prioritization in healthcare is far-reaching and there is no consensus on its definition; thus, it can be used interchangeably with other terms, such as “resource allocation”. Hence, the distribution of goods and services across programs and people can be understood as a general conceptualization of priority setting in healthcare [1, 2]. The fact that health is a priceless and irreplaceable good might explain why healthcare resource allocation involves a considerable amount of stress for decision-makers [3].

Evidence suggests that rational and transparent approaches to setting healthcare priorities are needed to enable policymakers and managers to make decisions that maximize social welfare. Consequently, validated, transparent, and fair institutional policies can guide healthcare professionals in complex resource allocation decisions [4–6].

At a national level, poor distribution of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) beds has been observed in Brazil, as highlighted in reports by the Federal Medicine Council and the Brazilian Association of Intensive Care Medicine: of the 44,253 ICU beds distributed across 532 out of the 5,570 Brazilian counties, only 49% are available to the Brazilian national healthcare system, known as Sistema único de Saúde (SUS), whilst 51% is reserved for 23% of the Brazilian population, as it is exclusive to private or supplementary healthcare, the latter being provided by health insurance organizations. This scenario paints a bigger picture of Brazilian critical care reality, confirming a considerable scarcity of resources [7]. Thus, in the context of a scarce number of ICU beds, prioritization is an essential process for the provision of care, as it is capable of maximizing the use of available resources by directing bed occupancy to the patient with the greatest benefit [8, 9]. In this context, regulation is an essential process for the functioning of systems since it addresses the existing dysfunctions in the health system so that the promotion of social well-being is ensured, thus configuring itself as a mechanism capable of promoting the rationalization of health care with a potential impact on the optimization of available resources [10].

Decisions on whether to admit patients to the ICU involve a high degree of complexity due to required trade-offs between potential benefits and risks for each individual patient, including considerations of the impact of individual admissions on the overall health status of other patients [11, 12]. Therefore, from the physicians’ perspective, this type of decision can be emotionally charged and subject to high levels of pressure and uncertainty [11]. Critical care admission decisions are influenced not only by patient-related factors but also by physician and environmental aspects [13].

The use of multiple criteria decision analysis (MCDA) approaches in healthcare has been reported in the literature as a tool that can promote transparency and rationality in decision-making [14–16]. Regarding the critical care context, a fit-for-purpose use of MCDA could support the planning of appropriate protocols to regulate access to healthcare services, such as access to ICU beds, given the incidence of the multiple and conflicting aspects related to critical care admission decisions. In addition to the aforementioned advantages of the MCDA approach, it also has the ability to deal with subjectivity, a known inherent element of decision problems [17], which has already been reported in the admission routine even in the presence of established criteria [11]. Guided by the iterative and constructive approach that the problem structuring and model building activities that MCDA approaches typically require, MCDA was chosen as the methodological framework on which to base the present study. This is due to the learning and shared understanding that the process can involve in order to “elicit” the elements to build the model [18]. MCDA has been chosen elsewhere over alternative approaches due to its flexibility aspect and potential to accommodate diverging views and sources of evidence [19, 20].

The current study aims to develop a multicriteria model to aid decisions on prioritizing access to intensive care units in a resource-constrained scenario in which six patients with the same priority level demand critical care, but only one bed is available. The model is intended to address the reality of Rio Grande do Norte (RN), a state located in the northeast region of Brazil, subject to the National Regulation Policy established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. The secondary objective of this study is to illustrate how MCDA could aid further planning of appropriate protocols to help the accomplishment of the regulation of access to healthcare services, especially in the scenario of scarce resources. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: the Literature review section presents a literature review section covering topics such as related studies investigating ICU admission decisions and MCDA. The Methods section presents the methodology underlying the development of the multicriteria model, and the Results section presents the results from the development of the multicriteria model. The Discussions section discusses the outcomes of the MCDA approach. Finally, the Conclusions section summarizes some aspects and will lead to reflections on the main contributions of the study as well as its limitations.

Literature review

ICU admission decisions

It is assumed that patients should be admitted to the ICU if intensive care has the potential to result in lower mortality or morbidity than treatment offered in other wards. However, ICU admission does not always mean potential recovery or benefit, as life-support treatments can also increase the risk of aggressive and invasive procedures, exposure to errors and infections, and possible patient deterioration, among other factors [11, 21, 22]. Thus, professionals in the ICU usually have to weigh the pros and cons when deciding whether or not to admit a patient, even though specific criteria to determine the potential benefit of patients through ICU access are not always clearly defined, which makes the assessment of trade-offs a challenging task [11]. This complex decision process has motivated a series of investigations in the literature to enhance learning about the factors that influence the decision to admit a patient to critical care.

Escher et al. [12] conducted research in a Swiss hospital into the factors influencing physicians’ decisions to admit patients to intensive care. The triage determinants for intensive care were split into two groups: those related to the patient’s clinical status and those associated with the medical team. The patient’s (or family’s wishes, when suitable), family pressure, and the medical professional’s concerns about legal repercussions came out during interviews as factors related to the clinical situation. Still regarding this component, the doctors’ apprehension about disagreeing with the level of care recommended by the established procedures, the absence of information in the patient’s file, and the problems of defending their choice to everyone involved-the patient, family, nurses, and other doctors-were also noted. On the other side, regarding the factors related to the medical team, the authors identified the intensive care physicians’ experience, referring physicians’ experience and specialty, and the quality level of the interaction between these two specialties as either a facilitator or an obstacle to the triage process, depending on the level of professional tensions.

The decision-making scenario related to patient referrals for ICU admission during periods of restricted bed availability in a New York-based hospital was investigated by Orsini et al. [23]. They noticed a linear correlation between disease severity, measured via APACHE-II, and the probability of being admitted to the ICU. In the study, there was no influence of bed availability, age, or gender on triage decisions. Also, even though explicit admissions criteria did exist in the study environment, it emerged that most professionals did not strictly adhere to them when making daily decisions.

Exploring the perceptions of 166 intensive care physicians who are members of the Dutch Society of Intensive Care, Oerlemans et al. [24] found that the patient’s physical condition, which was seen as a predictor of treatment success, and the patient’s quality of life were usually regarded as the most significant factors, as were the patient’s wishes. The less significant factors were those related to the budgetary impact that the treatment would make on the patient, family, or society. When the gender, years of experience, and age of the interviewees were compared, no considerable differences were detected.

When evaluating the effects of implementing a tool to support admissions decisions based on a prioritization algorithm, Ramos et al. [25] noticed a reduction in potentially inappropriate admissions to ICU. The study’s main finding was that the isolated use of triage scores may not be sufficient to guide decision-making, which prompts consideration of the development of institutional policies that are open and comprehensive, capable of guiding decisions and minimizing the influence of bias on admissions.

According to the systematic review study conducted by James, Power, and Laha [26], the absence of guidelines in decision-making contexts marked by pressure and risk, which is the reality in ICU settings, contributes to the use of irrational or otherwise flawed heuristics that are not in line with the unit’s institutional reality.

On the other side, there is evidence that although guidelines and protocols on admission do exist at the institutional level, non-adherence situations also occur, especially in the case of full bed occupancy [13, 24].

The studies under discussion demonstrate the importance of a tool that not only facilitates a transparent, logical decision-making process but can also consider the subjective concerns of intensive care professionals. The current authors maintain that the MCDA approach may fit such purposes since one of its main characteristics is the capability of tackling multiple and conflicting objectives, including the underlying subjective determinants from the evaluators’ perspective. For instance, within the MCDA context, Frej et al. [27] proposed a decision model based on the portfolio approach considering the Expect Utility Theory and Bayesian decision analysis to act as a tool to support health system managers dealing with the allocation of ICU beds. When compared to other common approaches designed to aid ICU bed allocation, their proposed decision model was deemed more useful.

Multicriteria decision analysis

A decision problem occurs whenever there is a decision in which two or more options are possible and a choice is required. Thus, multiple criteria decision analysis (MCDA) is an umbrella term that refers to a set of formal approaches and methods that can be applied to decision problem scenarios in which multiple and conflicting objectives must be considered during the solutions appraisal [17, 18]. The usefulness of approaching decision problems from a multidimensional perspective is evident when there are multiple and conflicting objectives and stakeholders carrying out guess-based decisions, which are rarely appropriate [18].

Roy [28] presents three preceding bases that can be seen as fundamental features of multiple criteria decision-making (MCDM) framework, which comprise a well-defined set A of feasible alternatives a; a preference model shaped according to decision-makers mind, which is structured considering a set of attributes; and a well-formulated mathematical problem to find an alternative such that . Such features help envision the basic elements of MCDM approaches but are arguably not representative when referring to a methodological decision-aiding tool to help make “better” decisions. A stream of thought was born from limitations that derive from approaching a multiple criteria decision problem with the same “objectivity lens” as the ones that define the MCDM framework previously mentioned, which led to coining the term multiple criteria decision-aid as a way to avoid such limitations. As a result, the three preceding bases that define any multiple criteria decision-aid framework are defined as a not necessarily well-defined or stable set of potential actionsa, comparisons based on n criteria , and an ill-defined mathematical problem based on the family of the n criteria and on additional inter-criteria information (e.g., weights) [28]. Hereafter, whenever referring to multicriteria decision analysis, its meaning should refer to the decision-aiding perspective as discussed.

With the basic elements of a decision problem at hand, the MCDA method may model the decision maker’s preferences and aggregate the multiple criteria. The axiomatic and methodological basis defines the aggregation convention, which distinguishes the multicriteria methods from one another [17, 29, 30]. The various MCDA approaches can be grouped into three broad categories: value measurement models, outranking models, and goal, aspiration, or reference level models [18, 31]. However, there is no single way of classifying MCDA methods. Another classification is to group methods according to their underlying “rationality”, which refers to each method’s compensation logic for aggregating the criteria. This notion of compensation can help to define MCDA methods as compensatory and non-compensatory [17]. In a compensatory MCDA method, it is assumed that a good performance on one criterion can offset a poor performance on another criterion. On the other hand, non-compensatory methods do not assume trade-offs between criteria, as preference between elements (e.g., alternatives) depends only on the subset of criteria that favor each of them [17, 29].

Methods

The technical steps that were followed to apply the multi-criteria approach and develop the model were based on approaches from Roy [35], Belton and Stewart [18], and de Almeida et al. [17]. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the framework used for building the multicriteria model. Three general stages grouping eleven steps were followed: problem structuring; preference modelling and aggregation; and finalization. The Regulation Center of the State of Rio Grande do Norte, hereafter referred to as CE (from the Portuguese “Central Estadual”), was the object of the current study since one of its legal liabilities is to manage the bed occupancy and the agenda of the health units within the State. The staff of the CE manages all ICU public access requests formalized by the health units from 5 out of the 8 health districts of the State, namely I, III, IV, V, and VI. These 5 health districts account for around 139 cities and municipalities. Figure 2 depicts the map of the State of Rio Grande do Norte and all eight health districts.

Problem structuring

The decision problem was defined as prioritizing access to a single available ICU bed with 6 simultaneously requesting patients of equal priority. At the time of this study, priority levels were defined as Priority 1, Priority 2, Priority 3, Priority 4, and Priority 5 in decreasing order of priority (Table 1). These priorities were consistent with the Society of Critical Care Medicine’s guidelines (see [37]) and internally established along with other orientations that collectively defined the normative guidance on the request and regulation flows. The only tie-breaking factor made explicit in the regulation protocol was the chronological order, i.e., whenever requesting patients with the same priority levels were competing for an Adult ICU bed, preference would be given to the earliest request. Nevertheless, this same instrument also defines that one patient could be prioritized over another depending on the type of care required by each clinical case, although this was not referred to as a tie-breaking factor.

Characterizing the DM and other actors

After defining the decision problem, the next step in the problem structuring stage was to identify the decision-maker and other relevant actors. Although the regulation process is conducted by an entire group of regulating doctors, the multicriteria model was developed from the perspective of a single evaluator who was defined as the decision-maker (DM) - the coordinator medical regulator. The other regulatory physicians, nurses from the bed management nucleus, and the nursing coordinator were listed as experts because they were an essential source of information for the regulatory decision-making process.

Identifying objectives

Before delving into the criteria, it was important to understand the objectives that guide the context of the decision problem. As such, given the nature of the regulation process of access to Adult ICU beds, within the domain of the National Public Health System, the objective of the prioritization problem was derived from items I and II of the first paragraph under Article 8 of the Brazilian National Regulation Policy [38]: “to ensure proper access to health services”, and “to guarantee compliance with the principles of equity and completeness” (free translation). The conflicting nature of these objectives is highlighted by the fact that if viewed from an isolated perspective, a single request for access to an ICU bed may be appropriate from a clinical point of view, but in the face of the current regulatory status and other patients’ needs, it may not meet the principles of equity and completeness at that given moment.

Establishing criteria

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with the experts and the decision-maker to identify the set of criteria. After the systematization of the information obtained from the interviews, a questionnaire (see Additional file 1) was given to ten regulating doctors, including the decision-maker (medical coordinator). Following structuring the data extracted from the questionnaires, the DM was presented to a descriptive panel with a summary of the answers. This procedure was performed to validate the criteria to be included in the model. In the end, eight criteria were defined, namely: i) neurological dysfunction, ii) cardiovascular dysfunction, iii) respiratory dysfunction, iv) coagulation dysfunction, v) liver dysfunction, vi) kidney dysfunction, vii) long-term prognosis, and viii) assistance level. Establishing the set of actions and problematic

With regard to the fourth step in the problem structuring stage, the problematic that best suited the intended decision-aiding approach was ranking (problematic P.) [35] since it enables support for the decision-making process by recommending a ranking in decreasing order of preference that is compatible with the preference structure of the decision-maker.

The set of actions for the decision problem of prioritizing ICU admissions is discrete, and each element corresponds to a patient with a need for access to the ICU validated by a competent professional. Through consultation with historical data, information based on actual patients requesting an ICU bed was used to formulate clinical vignettes. The identity of the patients was preserved so that the set of actions of this problem is composed of six alternatives identified by Patients 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 (see Additional file 2).

Identifying the state of nature

The last step of the structuring phase aimed to identify factors that may change randomly and influence the outcomes of the decision process [17]. In this regard, it was important to acknowledge that the clinical status of the requesting patients may evolve while waiting for the admission decision. Therefore, in the context of the study, the requesting health units are required to update the referred patient’s clinical status data every 24 hours. Thus, the clinical judgment of the regulating physician may change in light of the new information. Although this aspect was identified, it was decided not to model this state of nature for the sake of simplicity.

Preference modeling and aggregation

Preference modeling

To model the decision maker’s preferences, a bottom-up approach was taken by asking him to state his preferences for a set of pilot options. Since the preference independence conditions were not met, the simple weighted sum could not be used as the aggregation function [39]. In interviewing the decision maker, it was found that the group of organic dysfunction criteria is evaluated to distinguish the nature of requests between those requiring immediate advanced life support intervention and those motivated by monitoring needs [40]. Then, the decision-maker demonstrated to be more inclined to prioritize referrals of patients requiring immediate access to life support over those motivated by monitoring needs. This aspect demonstrates that a non-compensatory rationality better describes the DM’s judgment process as the preference between two requests is shown to depend on the subset of criteria that favors one request over another. Therefore, the preference structure incorporated by this actor is that defined by the binary relations of strict preference (P), weak preference (Q), indifference (I), and incomparability (R), denoted by (P, Q, I, R).

Intra-criterion evaluation

As a result of the preference modeling step (see Preference modeling section), an outranking multicriteria method was deemed appropriate to build the model. In this study, both Preference Ranking Organization METHod for Enrichment Evaluation (PROMETHEE) I and II were used. Both methods focus on ranking problems: while PROMETHEE I suggests a partial pre-order, PROMETHEE II aims to suggest a complete pre-order.

The intra-criterion evaluation step aimed at identifying the generalized criterion (see Preference ranking organization method for enrichment evaluation section) in order to obtain the information within each criterion that is required to run PROMETHEE. This identification task also required eliciting the parameters in order to define the preference functions. In other words, the intra-criterion evaluation step consisted of eliciting the preference (p) and indifference (q) thresholds after having established the preference function (Eq. 3) that better represented the DM’s preferences for each criterion. It is relevant to note that these parameters were defined by the decision-maker with the assistance of analysts. Inter-criteria evaluation

The final step in the preference modeling and aggregation stage was to perform the inter-criteria evaluation by determining the criteria weights. In outranking methods, the weights reflect the degree of relative importance of the attribute from the perspective of the decision-maker [17]. The procedure used to elicit the degrees of importance was the Rank-Order Centroid (ROC) [41, 42] since ROC was acknowledged in [43] as consistent with the conceptual framework of PROMETHEE methods. To apply this elicitation procedure, the DM was asked to rank the criteria in descending order of relative importance. Finalization

With the outcomes from the problem structuring and preference modeling stages, the finalization stage was carried out with the support of the academic edition of the Visual PROMETHEE software. After defining the generalized criterion (see Preference ranking organization method for enrichment evaluation section) for each of the eight criteria identified in the problem structuring (Establishing criteria section), the alternatives were evaluated with the support of Visual PROMETHEE. After that, sensitivity analysis was carried out to explore the robustness of the recommendations proposed by the model. The outcomes of this step will be detailed next.

Visual stability intervals

Although PROMETHEE II provides a complete ranking and makes it easier for the decision-maker on what decision could be taken, it results in a loss of information. Therefore, it was important also to consider the partial pre-order provided by PROMETHEE I, as it contains more realistic information and is more prudent [34]. By using both rankings, the proposed recommendations provided a greater deal of information. For example, the central contribution of the cardiovascular and kidney dysfunction criteria was clear alongside the level of support criterion, as these were the criteria for which the decision maker’s preferences were modeled by the usual criterion preference function (16). As pointed out by Brans, Vincke, and Mareschal [34], this function states that once the evaluations are different, the DM strictly prefers the action with the greatest evaluation. This is somewhat consistent with the fact that two of these criteria had the highest weights. On the other hand, there is a range in the DM’s preference function for the remaining criteria for which the decision maker has a weak preference situation [34], so the preference system for these criteria was modeled by level criterion preference functions. As a result, these criteria do not do as much to discriminate between patients as the renal and cardiovascular criteria.

Discussions

The recommendations made by the multicriteria model were sufficient to support the prioritization decision of access to Adult ICU beds in a scarcity context wherein six eligible patients were competing for the occupancy of a single available ICU bed. The decision-making context in which the model was constructed defines that all patients were referred to a general ICU ward, being assigned to the same priority level. Given this hypothetical scenario, it was noted that the then-existing protocols are not sufficient to aid the regulatory staff’s decision. Instead of considering age as an evaluation criterion, the ‘long-term prognosis’ criterion was used so that the survival prediction could be evaluated based on the patient’s comorbidities or degree of frailty. By adopting this strategy, the ethical issue of focusing on the patient’s chronological age it is avoided.

Given that objective evaluation scores commonly occur in the intensive care routine, during the structuring phase, it was sought to determine the best way to account for an objective score that could be used to evaluate the patients whilst considering the multidimensional nature of each patient’s clinical status. During the interviews, it was noted that the regulatory medical team already had a good deal of expertise with the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [44]. Nevertheless, each organ dysfunction dimension that composes the SOFA was used as an evaluation criterion rather than the SOFA itself. For each of the six components of this score, value ranges grading the patient’s degree of organic impairment were taken into consideration based on the scores assigned on the original scale. Hence, in the current study, SOFA was split into six different evaluation criteria.

During the problem structuring phase, it came up that the operational capacity for delivering care of the requesting unit that accommodates the referring patient was one of the factors assessed in the admission decision. To incorporate this aspect the assistance level criterion was included in the model and defined as the degree of assistance of the requesting unit.

Furthermore, it was shown that the criterion of renal disorder had a significant impact on the suggestions made by the PROMETHEE II complete ranking, which may indicate a propensity to give dialysis patients priority. This is due to the fact that hemodialysis, a crucial technique for this class of patients, is not supported in all intensive care units in the study area. Thus, PROMETHEE I was deemed advantageous as the incomparability binary relation prompts a warning sign to the regulatory physician in an attempt to point out a potential conflict between the requesting patients. Consequently, the clinical judgment of the doctor is not substituted, and the doctor’s independence is assured.

Finally, the strategy proposed in [17, 32, 34] of considering both recommendations of PROMETHEE I and II was adequate to the problem addressed because it was actually observed that whilst the PROMETHEE II complete ranking gave the decision-maker a more intuitive view of the recommendation, the PROMETHEE I partial rankings led to a more appropriate conclusion of the recommendations made by having a higher level of information when allowing the binary incomparability relation.

Conclusions

With the support of the principles of the multicriteria decision support methodology [18, 35], and by following the step-by-step framework proposed by de Almeida et al. [17], the primary objective of the study was achieved as a multicriteria model was built and able to elaborate recommendations on how to prioritize six priority 1 referring patients to a single Adult ICU bed available. The secondary objective was also fulfilled as building the model required an in-depth immersion in the daily routine of the study’s field, gathering information, mainly in the problem structuring stage, that helped to frame the reality of the decision problem continuously faced by the Regulation Center of the State of Rio Grande do Norte.

From an ethical standpoint, this study received authorization to be conducted. The approval was granted by the department responsible for evaluating, on a case-by-case basis, whether a study meets the necessary requirements to be conducted within the jurisdiction of the state Health Secretary. The authorization is available upon request from the corresponding author. The set of alternatives, i.e., the patients that comprised the set of actions of the decision problem (see Establishing the set of actions and problematic section) consisted of clinical data from real patients who had been evaluated by the regulating staff. To ensure privacy, all data was anonymized.

From an academic perspective, this study contributes to the field by presenting the results of an empirical MCDA application carried out in a real context. It is important to highlight the decision-aiding perspective evidenced throughout the paper in which the model intends to aid the decision by proposing recommendations instead of replacing the autonomy of the regulating staff. The main contribution of the model within the professional scope of the team is that the model allows a high level of information by proposing both partial and complete rankings while leaving the decision-maker free to follow or not the recommendation.

The analysis of the recommendations proposed by the model highlighted the important contribution of the MCDA process in stimulating reflection on the process of prioritizing access to Adult ICU beds. Future updates of the access regulation protocol can be guided by the discussions carried out during the construction of the described multicriteria model. Furthermore, such reflections were able to elucidate the pre-orders generated by the PROMETHEE I and II algorithms by exploring outranking binary relations among the set of patients through interpreting positive, negative, and net outranking flows. As already mentioned, the proposed model was built under non-compensatory rationality as it was deemed appropriate to the aspects of the decision problem at the time the project was carried out. The reader is referred to Frej et al. [27] for an ICU allocation-related application under the compensatory rationality.

It is important to point out that once it was identified that the preferential independence condition was not met and that the additive aggregation model within the scope of multiattribute value theory would not be possible to be applied, the choice of a non-compensatory method was driven by simplicity in terms of parameters to be elicited. Therefore, because PROMETHEE I and II had such a direct interpretation of the indifference and preference thresholds, they were selected as the MCDA aggregation approaches to proceed with.

The current study is subject to some limitations. First, we advise a careful reproduction of the model, even in a context with similar regulations in other Brazilian states, given that this is a case study research. Consequently, the recommendations made were based on the parameters elicited by the decision-maker, which means that the model developed only applies to the explored reality of the State Regulatory Centre within the scope of the Regulatory Complex of the Rio Grande do Norte state when managing Adult ICU admission decisions. With regard to the issue of reproducibility, it should be noted that the results presented are not generalizable. This is due to the fact that they are dependent not only on the preference system of the decision-maker, but also on the set of alternatives that is under assessment. However, once the parameters have been set, the model can be run as many times as evaluations are required. The only change that needs to be made is to update the set of alternatives on the Visual PROMETHEE decision support system to reflect the patients currently under assessment. It is therefore considered that, despite these limitations, the decision model is deemed as reproducible. However, it is recommended that the decision model undergo continuous revision to ensure that the initial elicited parameters -i.e., value functions and thresholds- remain valid and accurately reflect the decision-makers’ preference system.

Another limitation arises from the discretization of scales whose measurement of the assessment parameters exists in continuous scales: the construction of the scale’s levels based on discrete values presented at continuous intervals may have resulted in a loss of information when evaluating the alternatives. Lastly, the assumptions that the six patients are equally eligible to a single available ICU bed limit the generalization degree of the findings to the set of considered alternatives.
