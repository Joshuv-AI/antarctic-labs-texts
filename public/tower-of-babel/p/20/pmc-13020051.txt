Introduction

The armed conflict in Ukraine started in 2022, putting millions of citizens at risk and triggering a severe humanitarian crisis. As a result, the situation brought renewed focus to the issue, prompting reflection on the consequences for their health status.

Humanitarian crises caused by mass migration are not new. In recent years, increased global attention has been given to migratory population movements, especially those involving refugees. According to the most recent data, over 80 million people worldwide have been forcibly displaced due to unsafe conditions in their countries of origin.

Mass migration has both positive and negative consequences for displaced individuals and host communities. While cultural exchange can be very beneficial, in conflict situations, negative consequences often take precedence.

Public health approach in refugee populations

Migration is not inherently a health risk factor; however, many migrants are exposed to both communicable and non-communicable diseases. Non-communicable diseases often have higher prevalence rates among migrants compared to the host country's population. Regarding communicable diseases, migrants may face exposure due to endemic conditions in their home countries and the disruption of health services.

Several studies have shown that vaccination coverage among migrants is often insufficient. Vaccination is essential not only to protect migrants but also to safeguard the health of host populations, turning a crisis into a public health opportunity.

Historical and geopolitical context of the conflict in Ukraine

Since the Russian Federation launched a military offensive against Ukraine on 24 February 2022, over 2.5 million people have been forced to flee the country and more than 185 million residents have been internally displaced.

The Ukrainian healthcare system was already under strain before the conflict, and the war worsened its condition. Key health risk factors among the Ukrainian population include high blood pressure, smoking, dyslipidaemia, obesity, alcoholism, diabetes, kidney disease and exposure to extreme temperatures.

Vaccination coverage in Ukraine is among the lowest in the WHO European Region and falls below target thresholds for BCG, DTP3, Pol3, MCV and HepB3. Measles remains a concern: in 2021, Ukraine reported 16 cases, the second-highest number in Europe. Tuberculosis caused 2927 deaths in 2020 [1].

Portugal, the National Health Service and medical assistance in the host country

The ongoing conflict in Ukraine has forced many citizens to seek refuge in other European countries, such as Portugal. In response, the Portuguese government issued the Resolution of the Council of Ministers No. 29-A/2022 on 1 March 2022, establishing criteria for granting temporary protection to people displaced by the war. Many of these refugees arrived by land routes, often relying on individual solidarity initiatives. Portugal's NHS guarantees the right to health for all citizens and includes all institutions and official services providing healthcare.

Portugal's health policies for migrants are outlined in integration plans that focus on accessibility and include two main objectives: ensuring the right to health for foreigners and improving diversity management in healthcare centres.

However, many initiatives are led by healthcare providers, social health insurance funds, non-governmental organisations, research centres and local authorities but without fully integration into national policies.

Goals

In response to the health needs of refugees arriving from Ukraine and other countries, and amid initial uncertainty regarding national strategy, Medical Refuge project was created in the Lisbon Metropolitan Area (Almada and Seixal). It was launched by a group of resident doctors from different specialties, and provided temporary primary health care services to refugees. The project served as a way to reduce pressure on existing health units and offer a faster, more flexible access point of care.

The main goals of this project were to:-Ensure health care for refugees arriving in Portugal;-Provide an alternative to the Portuguese healthcare system; and-Serve as a gateway for initial health care access.

Methods

This is an observational, cross-sectional study, covering the working period of the Medical Refuge project: April to September 2022. All refugees who requested a medical appointment (n = 136) were included, with no exclusion criteria.

The initial phase of the project consisted of bibliographical research on the concepts of migrant, refugee and refugee crisis, as well as epidemiological analysis of key health indicators in the Ukrainian population.

The second phase involved peer discussions about how to address the health needs of refugees who arrived in the Lisbon Metropolitan Area, and how to obtain the facilities and volunteers to launch the initiative. The project was presented to the executive management and clinical council of Primary Health Care in Almada and Seixal, two cities near Lisbon, in order to establish a partnership.

All volunteers were family medicine residents or practising family doctors. Before medical appointments began, volunteers received online training via a general meeting, where the project, its main goals, logistics, and available support materials were explained. Several documents with checklists were created to guide volunteers in the different types of appointments.

Medical appointments were scheduled by patients using a “walk-in clinic” model, through email or telephone. Before each appointment, patients completed a questionnaire on sociodemographic information and personal health history, translated into Portuguese, English and Ukrainian, to support communication. Google Translate was used when necessary. Gender was self-reported and sex assigned at birth was not collected.

Aware that this population was more susceptible to psychiatric conditions, we applied four screening tools to detect potential pathology: the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ9), General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD7), Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS) and Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL5), to screen depression, anxiety, hopelessness and post-traumatic stress, respectively. We translated to portuguese and ukranian, with help of an interpreter, to reduce misinterpretation. These non-validated versions constitute, therefore, a potential measurement error.

These are validated scales with clinical cut-off points (PHQ-9 ≥ 5; GAD-7 ≥ 10; BHS ≥ 9; PCL-5 ≥ 31); when patients had scored above, they were referred to secondary health care for a psychiatry appointment at the local hospital (Hospital Garcia de Orta). We scheduled a follow-up appointment to reevaluate patient's mental status and referral's response.

The study variables included: age, gender, nationality, type of appointment, reason for appointment, and vaccination status.

All data were collected without personal information (name, address, phone number), and only the authors of this study had access to it.

Patients that had an appointment in Medical Refuge project gave oral consent for the use of data, which was recorded in clinical notes; parents/guardians gave consent for their children. The study was approved, in these terms, by the ethics committee of the Regional Health Administration of Lisbon; patients didn't receive any compensation for participation.

As part of the Medical Refuge project, a total of 136 medical appointments were conducted, mostly for Ukrainian refugees (96.5%). Approximately 71% of patients were female. Patients range in age, with the 0–0 and 30–39 age groups comprising nearly half of the sample.

Various types of appointments were available: checkup/routine appointments, including for children and pregnant women, and “acute illness” (sick) appointments.

In each appointment, doctors recorded the main symptom, helping to track the type of care most frequently needed. The most common reasons for consultation included surveillance appointments, where patients showed the results of medical exams and monitored their chronic diseases (e.g., arterial hypertension, diabetes, hypothyroidism), underscoring the importance of primary health care for any population. The second most frequent reason was the presence of acute symptoms, often related to trauma from the armed conflict (e.g., anxiety, insomnia, headaches, musculoskeletal pain).

Thirteen patients requested prescription renewals for chronic medications, many of them which had been interrupted due to their sudden displacement and inability to bring supplies when fleeing from the war.

Regarding mental health, only seven patients reported psychiatric symptoms as the reason for their appointment, and two were referred to Psychiatry.

As for vaccination, a cause concern discussed earlier, 14% of patients had incomplete or unknown records. Among these, only three children and one adult agreed to complete vaccination.

Vaccination status was determined based on medical records only, acknowledging the fallibility of self-report. Patients without medical information were considered with incomplete status.

Discussion

For host countries, managing a large influx of refugees and migrants is complex and requires both political commitment and well-established policy framework. Providing housing, protection and, job opportunities is essential, but access to healthcare is also a fundamental human right.

Following the outbreak of armed conflict between Ukraine and Russia, thousands of people sought to leave Ukraine in order to secure their safety. This mass movement required host countries to reorganise their health systems to accommodate the additional demand [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].

In Portugal, while temporary protection is granted to all refugees, there was no unified national strategy to meet the specific health needs of Ukrainian refugees [9]. The Medical Refuge project was designed to fill this gap, providing primary health care to refugees in Almada and Seixal.

The findings of this study are discussed in relation to existing studies from other countries and how they responded to the influx of Ukrainian refugees, to help them and provide health care.

In Italy, the government developed structured procedures to support Ukrainian refugees, focusing on public health interventions such as COVID-19 vaccination [10].

A study by Aleksander Biesiada et al. examined Poland's response to providing essential medical care for Ukrainian refugees. The authors highlighted common challenges identified via and original questionnaire, including the lack of previous medical records and the language barrier, which limited care delivery [11].

Medical Refuge faced similar obstacles, beginning with difficulties in publicising the project to reach the refugee population. This was addressed through meetings with volunteer associations, local government institutions, and outreach via all nearby healthcare units. Language barriers during appointments were the main limitation, along with the absence of health records. To mitigate this, patients completed a health questionnaire to provide key background information.

War, along with its associated traumatic, economic and social stressors, is known risk factors for mental health problems, particularly post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression [12].

In Czechia, researchers used PHQ-8 and GAD-7 scales, the SELF-I instrument, and help-seeking questionnaires to assess mental health in Ukrainian refugees. Over 41.5% screened positively for moderate or severe depression, and nearly 25% for moderate or severe anxiety. A significant number of refugees failed to recognise or report their mental health issues [13].

In Medical Refuge, patients completed validated psychological scales symptoms (PHQ-9, GAD-7, BHS and PCL-5) prior to appointments. Those scoring above the cut-off thresholds were referred to Psychiatry care. Only seven patients self-reported psychological symptoms, which may reflect the same lack of recognition of mental health issues identified in other studies. Volunteers reported that mental health was a difficult topic to address, because of the language barrier, but also due to the patients` reluctance to open up. This could be indicative of a cultural barrier to mental health disclosure, making difficult to evaluate and treat these problems.

The Medical Refuge project is one of many examples of how Europe responded to the mass influx of Ukrainian refugees and the resulting strain on the host nations` health systems.

Given the long-standing challenges within Ukraine's healthcare system, particularly in areas like vaccination, it was essential to ensure a strong primary care response. Although local responses varied, the Medical Refuge project in Almada and Seixal demonstrated the feasibility of providing accessible care to refugees, while remaining attentive to their specific needs.

Limitations and future research

This study provided an opportunity to study and characterise the response of a region of Portugal to the arrival of refugees and their health needs, just weeks after the beginning of war. However, given the limited time for planning, only assessed immediate outcomes. We understand that a prospective study would be more appropriate, as it would allow us to evaluate the impact of variables over time and enhance the transferability and generalizability of our findings.

Also, this study only included quantitative variables, lacking qualitative information about patient experience and volunteer perspectives, that could be useful to understand the strengths and weaknesses of the project.

The failure to collect sex assigned at birth is other limitation of the study, within the context of gender/sex construct.

Conclusion

The war in Ukraine brought the issue of refugees and migrants back to the forefront as thousands were forced to leave their homes in search of safety and shelter abroad.

Portugal's social and healthcare response to incoming ukrainian refugees was heterogeneous and largely driven by local authorities. In Almada and Seixal, through the solidarity of local residents and family medicine specialists, the Medical Refuge project emerged. This initiative made it possible to provide health care to refugees arriving in Portugal, offering an initial assessment and monitoring of their health status.

This study describes the creation project from its inception, when a group of residents expressed concern about access to healthcare for Ukrainian refugees, through its implementation, in which medical appointments were delivered by volunteer doctors.

It is essential to pay special attention to vulnerable populations, but this must aim at their integration, not their marginalisation.
