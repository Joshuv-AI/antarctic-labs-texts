Synthesis of a metal-organic framework with red mud for organic pollutant adsorption using response surface methodology for sustainable chemistry
Authors: Mahdieh Chegeni, Mohanna Chegeni, Ghazal Ebrahim Zade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study revealed the synthesis of a novel metal-organic framework (MOF) through the reaction between red mud as an industrial waste material, and trimesic acid (TCA) for the adsorption of methyl orange (MO) through Response Surface Methodology (RSM) from aqueous solutions. The synthesis process utilized red mud as a sustainable source of metal ions and TCA as the organic linker to obtain Red Mud-Trimesic Acid MOF (RM/TCA-MOF) under hydrothermal conditions. The synthesized MOF was characterized using various techniques such as Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis, Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA). The as-synthesized MOF was applied to remove methyl orange (MO) as an anionic dye. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was used to optimize adsorption parameters, including pH, adsorbent dosage, contact time, temperature, and initial dye concentration. Further, the optimum conditions for maximum adsorption efficiency were evaluated at pH = 2.33, 0.07 g RM/TCA dose, 6 ppm of MO concentration, 61 min, and 26 °C using the Quadratic model. Based on the results, RM/TCA-MOF can be adsorbed MO by active functional groups derived from the red mud and TCA. The study of adsorption isotherms and kinetics studies shows the Langmuir isotherm and pseudo-second-order kinetics, respectively. Introduction

The increase of industrialization has resulted in the release of hazardous organic pollutants, and the risk for health and the environment is increased in environment. Therefore, the removal of containment is an essential challenge in the world, and the search for innovative and sustainable remediation strategies has become a necessary subject. Dyes and heavy metals are considered among the most hazardous pollutants in water sources, due to their long-term consequences for human health as well as detrimental impacts on aquatic organisms, plant life, and wildlife1.

Various methods are used to solve this problem such as filtration, ultraviolet, biological methods, separation, and reverse osmosis2. Among the used strategies, adsorption using advanced materials has emerged as a promising performance, because of its simplicity, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness, which make adsorption as a good choice3. Several adsorbent was reported to remove organic dye such as graphene oxide4, magnetic magnetic polymeric composite5, metal-organic frameworks6, carbon-based composite7, and clay-based adsorbent8.

The synthesis of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) has attracted significant interest over the past two decades, due to their potential to produce several structures with applications in various areas of porous materials. These include many fields such as storage, separation, and catalysis, which rely on pore size, shape, and host-guest interactions3. Additionally, there is a rising interest in exploring their applications in biomedicine and as materials for sensors9. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have been used as good adsorbents for water purification applications, due to their porous structure. These materials consist of three-dimensional crystalline networks, that formed by the coordination of metal ions or clusters with multifunctional organic linkers. MOFs exhibit exceptional physicochemical properties including high surface area, large pore volume, tunable host–guest interactions, and notable chemical stability. The choice of metal centers and organic ligands can be resulted to suit specific applications10. Today, several MOFs have been synthesized such as iron based metal organic framework11, Ni-based MOFs12, Cu-Fe-BDC MOF13, polyoxopalladate-loaded metal-organic frameworks14, rare-earth-based MOFs for photo/electrocatalysis15, Co-based MOFs16, palygorskite/UiO-6617, and Fe3O4@Ag-MOF1. Further, conventional MOF synthesis often involves starting material precursors and environmentally taxing methods with the limitation of their practical applications. To overcome this challenge, the use of industrial waste as a precursor for MOF synthesis can be considered as a sustainable and cost-effective alternative strategy.

In recent years, some linker waste sources were used for the sustainable synthesis of MOF such as polyethylene terephthalate and polylactic acid to produce terephthalic acid and lactate, and the metal waste sources were included in spent alkaline batteries, carbon black waste, industrial wastewater, and recycled beverage cans and aluminum foil18. Red mud is a by-product of bauxite from aluminum production, which is contained in Al2O3, Fe2O3, TiO2, CaO, and Na2O. Also, a large amount of red mud is generated from the Byer procedure to produce aluminum. Red mud was applied for the removal of Cr(VI)19 phosphate20 methylene blue, malachite green21 and rhodamine B22. Red mud is known as an abundant and underutilized material rich in metal oxides. The combination of red mud (a metal source) with trimesic acid (TCA, an organic linker) can be proposed as a green and sustainable pathway for MOF synthesis.

The optimization of the adsorption process is an important procedure to maximize efficiency and to find interactions between variables. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is a statistical and mathematical tool to optimize multiple parameters while minimizing experimental runs. Quadratic is a type of (RSM) for evaluating the effects of parameters and their interaction on the adsorption process. Many literatures reported the use of the CCD method to study the removal of pollutants such as black-T dye23, Pb(II)24, tetracycline25, and methyl orange26.

This research focuses on the synthesis of MOF by red mud and trimesic acid to investigate the adsorption of methyl orange by the Quadratic-RSM method to optimize the adsorption conditions. The aim of this study is the development of cost-effective and environmentally friendly solutions for water pollution mitigation. To achieve this purpose, the red mud/trimesic acid (RM/TCA) MOF was synthesized by using sustainable starting materials corresponding to sustainable chemistry. The as-synthesized MOF was characterized by FTIR, XRD, FESEM, TEM, EDX, BET, and TGA techniques. Then, the application of RM/TCA MOF was evaluated by methyl orange (MO) dye as an organic pollutant through the Quadratic-RSM method, and the best condition was determined. Based on the findings, RM/TCA can be employed as a novel MOF for water treatment, due to its porosity and functional surface groups, although access to the best conditions needs more study.

Results and discussion

The FTIR technique was used to determine the functional groups of materials, which is shown in Fig. 1. FTIR of TCA contains O–H stretching vibration (2800–3300 cm−1), carbonyl stretching vibration (1690–1730 cm−1), C–O stretching vibration (1200–1300 cm−1), and C=C (1400–1600 cm−1). The red mud FTIR is shown as a complex mixture of metal oxides composed of Al–O and Fe–O vibrations (450–900 cm⁻¹), Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching vibration 1117 cm⁻¹, H–O–H bending vibration 1636 cm⁻¹ (water molecules adsorbed on the surface of the red mud particles). In RM/TCA, the carbonyl peak shifts to a lower wavenumber, indicating coordination of the carboxyl group with metal ions (Fe³⁺, Al³⁺) in red mud. The intensity of broad O–H peak is decreased, due to the deprotonation of the carboxyl groups during metal-ligand coordination. The peaks at 450–900 cm⁻¹ indicate the formation of new metal-oxygen bonds between the metal ions from red mud and the carboxyl groups of TCA.

FTIR spectra of RM and RM/TCA MOF.

The XRD analysis was used to determine the crystalline structure and phase composition of materials (Fig. 2). The TCA XRD patterns show sharp peaks, which indicate the crystalline nature, and the ordered packing of TCA serves at 2θ = 11°–25°. Red mud contains multi-phase materials such as Hematite (2θ = 33°, 36°, and 46°), Gibbsite (2θ = 16° and 21°), and Quartz (2θ = 26° and 49°). The synthesized MOF is exhibited the XRD patterns of a new crystalline framework. According to the XRD pattern of RM/TCA, the intensity of some peaks from red mud is decreased, because of the coordination of metal ions with TCA. Further, the sharp peaks of TCA become less intense, which indicated the reaction of TCA and metal ions compared to remaining in its free crystalline form.

XRD patterns of RM, TCA, and RM/TCA MOF.

FFSEM was used to show the morphology of materials, as seen in Fig. 3. According to Fig. 3a, the RM image appears an irregular and heterogeneous shape, because of a mixture of metal oxides, and the surface of the particles is seen as rough and porous. According to Fig. 3b, c, RM/TCA indicates uniform morphology and plate-like structure and nanoscale pore.

FESEM images of a red mud, b, c RM/TCA, d EDX curve of RM/TCA, and e TEM image of RM/TCA.

The EDX analysis confirms that the formation of red mud/TCA MOF contains key elements from both red mud and TCA. This elemental composition indicates the successful formation of a hybrid material, where the red mud provides a metal-rich framework. TCA contributes organic functional groups, and peaks of Fe, Al, Si, Ti, and C can be evidenced the presence of red mud as metal-rich framework and TCA as organic functional groups (Fig. 3d).

TEM image of RM/TCA is depicted in Fig. 3e, that transparency shows a porous structure. The uniform formation of the MOF can be demonstrated by its sheet-like and crystalline shape, and relatively sharp edges and minimal defect dispersion indicate the high quality of the synthesized MOF. The relatively large, thin, and transparent region is explained as plate-shaped with planar ligands (TCA). This plate morphology can be resulted from the coordination environment of metal ions from red mud with the multi-dentate carboxylate ligands, which leads to thin, extended sheets.

The TGA curve of RM/TCA MOF is seen in Fig. 4, and the weight remains relatively stable at 25–200 °C, which indicates low moisture and volatile impurities. A gradual weight loss is observed at 200–400 °C, and corresponds to the decomposition of organic linkers and functional groups. The second weight loss (400–800 °C) represents the decomposition of the MOF framework, such as the breakdown of organic linkers or structural collapse of the MOF (55.13% weight change). Finally, the data can be determined the stability of as-synthesized MOF at high temperatures.

TGA curve of RM/TCA MOF.

The BET analysis is provided information about the surface properties, pore structure, and adsorption capacity of RM/TCA MOF. Based on the data, RM/TCA MOF revealed a moderate surface area (81.385 cm² g⁻¹), total pore volume of 0.039633 cm³ g⁻¹, and an average pore diameter of 19.479 nm, which determined a mesoporous material with potential for adsorbing large organic molecules through surface interaction and pore diffusion. The moderate BET constant (C = 7.7703) is indicated that the adsorbent–adsorbate interaction strength is sufficient for effective adsorption. The adsorption/desorption isotherm is shown as a type IV isotherm, according to the IUPAC classification (Fig. 5). Type IV isotherms are characteristic of mesoporous materials, which have pore diameters, and a low-cost adsorbent for wastewater treatment applications for organic molecules.

BET curve of RM/TCA MOF.

Adsorption study

Quadratic-RSM was employed to study the effect of four independent variables including initial MO concentration (ppm, C), MOF amount (g/L, B). pH of the solution (A), temperature (°C, E), and contact time (min, D) with 31 runs. The quadratic model was shown as an excellent fit to the experimental data with an R² value of 0.98 and 98% of the variability in MO adsorption. The lack-of-fit test was insignificant (p > 0.05), which can be confirmed the adequacy of the prediction model. The high R² (0.98) and adjusted R² (0.97) values can be presented the adequacy of the model in the adsorption process.

The main effects of process variables were studied in the adsorption process. The pHpzc of RM/TCA was determined 2.9, that the surface of the composite was positively charged at pH values below 2.9 and negatively charged at pH values above 2.9, which can be facilitated by electrostatic attraction with the negatively charged dye molecules. The best condition of MO removal was achieved at pH = 2.33, 0.07 g, RM/TCA, 6.40 ppm of MO concentration, 61 min, and 26 °C (yield = 97%). Based on the software results, Eq. (1) is provided for the effect of parameters for MO adsorption on RM/TCA.

Based on the ANOVA data, pH, adsorbent dosage, and initial concentration are the most significant factors for removing MO. There are important interactions between pH, adsorbent dosage, and concentration, which express the importance of optimizing these parameters simultaneously. The 3D surface plots represent the effect of two factors on the response (Fig. 6). Based on Fig. 6a, the optimum adsorption was done at a low pH and dose, and the surface charge of the red mud/TCA material and the ionization of MO dye were influenced by pH. Figure 6b is illustrated maximum adsorption at lower pH and moderate concentrations. To maximize removal efficiency, a balance between MO concentration and RM/TCA dose can be achieved (Fig. 6c).

Response level diagram of the MO removal by RM/TCA MOF.

The effect of pH was evaluated experimentally and compared to the theoretical prediction obtained from the fitted response surface model under optimum conditions. As shown in Fig. 7a, the removal is decreased in theoretical model from acidic to neutral pH with a slight increase toward alkaline conditions, that it is like to experimental data (Fig. 7b). Based on the data, the best yield was achieved in acidic media, due to electrostatic attraction between the protonated adsorbent surface and the anionic dye. Minor deviations between the curves could be attributed to practical limitations such as surface heterogeneity or slight changes in adsorbent morphology during the experiment. The good agreement between the theoretical and experimental results can be confirmed the reliability of the response surface methodology in modeling the adsorption process.

Cost estimation

RM/TCA was synthesized using red mud as an available industrial byproduct, which considered to have negligible or no cost. TCA is priced at approximately USD 100 per 100 g, and aluminum nitrate nonahydrate costs about USD 220 per 50 g, that were used in limited quantities during synthesis. This cost is expected to decline substantially under industrial-scale conditions due to bulk procurement of reagents and more efficient processing. The use of red mud as a no-cost component contributes to reducing the overall expenses. Moreover, the potential reusability and regeneration of the adsorbent can help minimize long-term operational costs in wastewater treatment applications4,27.

The resulting MOF is similar to MIL-96(Al), due to the high content of Al metal. The mechanism can be considered, that the removal of MO by RM/TCA is primarily driven by electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions. Under acidic conditions, the functional groups (–OH and –COOH) on the RM/TCA surface were protonated, and facilitated electrostatic attraction between the adsorbent and the negatively charged sulfonate groups of MO. In addition, the aromatic structures in both MO and trimesic acid can be resulted in π–π stacking interactions. Hydrogen bonds can be formed between the functional groups on the RM/TCA surface and the azo or sulfonate moieties of MO. Furthermore, these interactions enhance the adsorption performance of RM/TCA toward MO.

Conclusion

The RM/TCA as a novel MOF was synthesized successfully and showed excellent adsorption performance for methyl orange (MO) removal. Maximum removal efficiency of 95.2% was achieved under optimal conditions (pH 2.33, dose 0.07 g, and MO concentration 6 ppm). The adsorption mechanism was done by electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking, and physical adsorption, which its plate-like morphology can be accelerated adsorption. Statistical analysis validated the model with a high regression coefficient (R² > 0.95) and significant p-values (< 0.05). Compared to other adsorbents, RM/TCA offers competitive performance, lower cost, and moderate synthesis complexity, which can be used as an ideal choice for wastewater treatment applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

TCA, MO, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Merck Company. The origin of RM was Jajarm, North Khorasan, Iran.

Instruments

The characters of materials were obtained by FTIR (Unicam-Galaxy 5000), XRD (Panalytical XpertPro diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å)), FESEM (Tescan Mira3-LMU Microscope), BET (nitrogen adsorption in a Belsorp Mini II apparatus), and TGA(Q600-TA (Unit State, 25 °C to 600 °C, N2 atmosphere, a constant heating rate of 20 °C/min). Ultraviolet-visible (UV) spectroscopy (Dynamica–HALO- DB–20, Germany) was used to determine the MO amount at λmax = 461 nm.

Synthesis of RM/TCA MOF

In a typical procedure, trimesic acid (TCA) (0.1 g) was stirred in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 30 mL), and RM (0.8 g) was added to first solution and stirred for 10 min, then ethanol (30 ml) was added. The mixture was transformed into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave at 80 °C for 24 h. The resulting mixture was centrifuged and washed with DMF and alcohol several times. The obtained brown crystals were achieved by drying at air.

Adsorption process

The adsorption performance of RM/TCA was studied to remove MO dye from aqueous solution. In a typical experiment, adsorbent dose (0.01–0.08 g) was dispersed into 10 mL of MO solution with an initial concentration (6–22 mg/L). The pH of the solution was adjusted in the range (2–9) using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. The mixture was stirred at a temperature (15–45 °C) for a fixed contact time (15–120) to reach adsorption equilibrium. After the adsorption time, the suspension was centrifuged to separate the solid and liquid phases. The residual concentration of MO in the supernatant was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at the λ_max = 465 nm, and calculated according to Eq. 1S. Quadratic-RSM design is a factorial design for the removal of organic pollutants, the MO removal efficiency (%) was carried out by Design-Expert 13 with 31 runs (Tables 1S, 2S). The important parameters were studied for the removal of MO including the amount of MOF, concentration of MO, pH, time, and temperature. A critical parameter in the adsorption process is the point of zero charge (pHpzc) for water treatment. In this work, the pHpzc of a RM/TCA was determined 2.9, that the surface of the composite is positively charged at pH values below 2.9 and negatively charged at pH values above. Based on Table 2S, the amounts of MO and RM/TCA MOF were mixed under determined pH, time, and temperature, then the solution was centrifuged and filtered.

The two important isotherms were considered to reveal the multilayer and monolayer adsorptions process including Freundlich and Langmuir isotherms Equations (1S,2 S). Further, the pseudo-first-order and the pseudo-second-order kinetic models were studied to demonstrate the adsorption performance, as shown in Equations (3 S,4 S).

Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to investigate the equilibrium behavior of MO adsorption onto RM/TCA under optimized conditions. A series of MO solutions were prepared, and 0.07 g of the adsorbent was added to 10 mL solution. The suspensions were agitated in a thermostatic shaker at 4000 rpm for a contact time determined from kinetic studies to ensure equilibrium. After filtration, the residual MO concentration was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

The reusability of TCA/RM was evaluated using MO as the model organic pollutant. After each adsorption run, the saturated adsorbent was separated by centrifugation and washed, and then the used adsorbent was stirred in a 0.01 M NaOH solution for 30 min at room temperature. The regenerated TCA/RM was washed and dried.
