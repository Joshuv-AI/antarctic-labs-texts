Engineering multifunctional chitosan-based hydrogels for integrated antimicrobial therapy and endogenous stem cell-driven tissue repair in infectious keratitis
Authors: Wen Geng, Yiwen Lu, Hanpeng Xu, Yu Song, Shuting Liu, Xin Liu, Jie Cai, Mingchang Zhang, Huatao Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial keratitis is a major contributor to preventable blindness worldwide, with infections caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa posing significant challenges due to the formation of biofilms and insufficient tissue regeneration following antimicrobial therapy. Conventional treatments predominantly target the infection while neglecting the promotion of healing processes, often resulting in corneal scarring and subsequent vision impairment. Therefore, therapeutic approaches for condition should extend beyond antibacterial measures to include strategies that facilitate early limbal stem cell participation in tissue regeneration. In this study, we present a dual-functional hydrogel system that integrates quaternized ultra-highly deacetylated chitosan (QUDCS) with oxidized dextran (OD) and stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1). This system effectively eradicates bacterial biofilms while simultaneously promoting the recruitment of endogenous stem cells for corneal regeneration. The hydrogel exhibits rapid gelation and mechanical properties similar to corneal tissue, along with significant antimicrobial activity mediated by electrostatic membrane disruption. The sustained release of SDF-1 establishes chemotactic gradients that attract limbal stem cells, thereby activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways and enhancing cellular proliferation and regenerative potential. This integrated therapeutic platform represents a paradigm shift from traditional monotherapy to advanced biomaterial systems that simultaneously address infection and facilitate molecular-level tissue regeneration. It offers transformative potential for the treatment of infectious ocular diseases and other biofilm-associated infections that necessitate concurrent antimicrobial intervention and tissue repair.Keywords: Antimicrobial hydrogel, SDF-1, Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratitis, Endogenous stem cell recruitment, Wnt/β-catenin signaling

Introduction

Bacterial keratitis constitutes a devastating ocular emergency that threatens vision for millions worldwide, accounting for up to 90% of microbial corneal infections and representing a leading cause of preventable blindness [1]. Among causative pathogens, Pseudomonas aeruginosa poses exceptional therapeutic challenges because of its rapid progression and sophisticated virulence mechanisms [2]. This Gram-negative organism establishes robust biofilms within hours of corneal colonization—three-dimensional bacterial communities encased in extracellular polymeric substances that exhibit up to a 1000-fold increase in antimicrobial resistance compared with planktonic cells [3,4]. Concurrently, secreted proteolytic enzymes, particularly elastase and alkaline protease, systematically degrade corneal architecture, potentially causing stromal melting and perforation within 24–48 h [2]. Current fluoroquinolone-based therapies fail in more than 10% of cases due to biofilm-mediated resistance, leaving survivors with permanent scarring and visual impairment [5]. This clinical reality underscores an urgent need for innovative therapeutics that simultaneously overcome biofilm resistance and promote tissue regeneration.

Recent advances in biomaterial engineering, including antimicrobial peptides, nanoparticles, and chitosan, offer transformative opportunities to address these dual challenges through integrated antimicrobial and regenerative platforms [[6], [7], [8]]. Among emerging materials, chitosan—a cationic polysaccharide obtained through chitin deacetylation—demonstrates inherent antibacterial activity via electrostatic disruption of bacterial membranes [9]. When formulated as hydrogels through polymer crosslinking, chitosan-based systems provide additional therapeutic advantages, including maintaining optimal wound moisture, enabling sustained drug release, and exhibiting mechanical properties compatible with ocular tissues [10,11]. However, conventional chitosan formulations often lack sufficient antimicrobial potency against biofilm-embedded bacteria and fail to actively promote tissue regeneration beyond passive wound coverage.

Limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs) are adult stem cells located in the annular region between the transparent cornea and the opaque sclera and serve as the primary source for proliferation and differentiation of the normal corneal epithelium. These cells are essential for maintaining corneal transparency, facilitating injury repair, and preserving corneal integrity. The surrounding limbal microenvironment cells, also referred to as limbal niche cells (LNCs), represent a type of mesenchymal stem cell capable of extensive in vitro proliferation and differentiation into corneal stromal cells, thereby playing a significant role in corneal stromal regeneration and remodeling [12,13]. The intricate network of intracellular and extracellular signaling pathways between LESCs and LNCs collaboratively regulates and sustains the stemness characteristics of limbal stem cells. Strategic mobilization of these endogenous stem cell populations represents an untapped therapeutic opportunity for accelerating healing while preventing fibrotic scarring. Stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) is a continuously synthesized CXC-chemokine that influences a variety of physiological processes, including embryonic development and organ homeostasis. It is recognized as one of the most potent chemotactic agents for stem cell mobilization [14,15]. Our previous research demonstrated that the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis plays a critical role in maintaining the stemness of LESCs [16]. Under normal physiological conditions, the concentration of SDF-1 in the limbal region is relatively elevated, facilitating stem cell homing and retention in this area. The canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a significant downstream signaling cascade of the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis and is an evolutionarily conserved mechanism essential for embryonic development, stem cell self-renewal, proliferation, and maintenance of adult tissue homeostasis. Activation of Wnt signaling leads to stabilization of β-catenin protein levels, which then translocate to the nucleus and promote cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, and migration [17,18]. This mechanistic understanding provides a molecular framework for therapeutic intervention; exogenous SDF-1 delivery could reconstitute disrupted chemotactic gradients while amplifying endogenous regenerative responses.

Building upon mechanistic insights into LESCs or LNCs dynamics and antimicrobial biomaterial design, we developed a multifunctional therapeutic platform that addresses the dual challenges of bacterial eradication and tissue regeneration in keratitis treatment. Through precise control of deacetylation to achieve 99%—a critical threshold that maximizes both cationic charge density and chain flexibility—we synthesized quaternized ultra-highly deacetylated chitosan (QUDCS) with enhanced antimicrobial efficacy. When crosslinked with oxidized dextran (OD) via dynamic Schiff base chemistry, this polymer forms an injectable hydrogel that achieves rapid gelation within 60 s, demonstrates mechanical compliance comparable to native corneal anterior corneal stroma (storage modulus ∼24–39 kPa), and exhibits autonomous self-healing essential for maintaining structural integrity during physiological eye movements [19]. The incorporation of SDF-1 transforms this antimicrobial platform into a dual-action therapeutic system (Fig. 1). We hypothesize that the hydrogel operates through synergistic mechanisms: the high cationic charge density of QUDCS electrostatically disrupts P. aeruginosa membranes and penetrates biofilm matrices, while sustained SDF-1 release establishes chemotactic gradients that recruit LESCs and limbal LNCs to injury sites. Our findings demonstrate that SDF-1/CXCR4 engagement activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, thereby enhancing proliferation and regenerative capacity of recruited stem cells. This integrated approach represents a transition from conventional antimicrobial monotherapy toward sophisticated biomaterial platforms that simultaneously resolve infection and promote molecular-level tissue regeneration, thereby addressing the limitations of current treatments, which often fail against biofilm-associated infections while neglecting regenerative requirements.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of QUDCS/OD hydrogel

The multifunctional hydrogel system was developed through Schiff base chemistry between QUDCS and OD. QUDCS was synthesized via an environmentally benign quaternization process [20], yielding a water-soluble derivative with enhanced bioactivity. Starting from chitosan with a 99% deacetylation degree, the quaternization reaction achieved a substitution degree of 0.45 while maintaining a weight-average molecular weight of 1.97 × 105 g/mol, as confirmed by SEC-MALS analysis. The ζ potential of 1 mg/mL QUDCS solution was 43.5 ± 2.7 mV. Oxidation of dextran introduced 1.03 mmol/g aldehyde functionalities, as verified through hydroxylamine titration. 1H NMR spectroscopy confirmed the successful chemical modifications, with characteristic peaks at δ 3.16 ppm corresponding to protons of quaternary ammonium groups in QUDCS and aldehyde protons at δ 9.6 ppm in OD (Fig. 2A) [21,22].

Hydrogel formation occurred spontaneously upon mixing aqueous solutions of QUDCS and OD under physiological conditions (Fig. 2B). The crosslinking mechanism involves dynamic imine bond formation between primary amines of QUDCS and aldehyde groups of OD, enabling rapid gelation without external stimuli [23]. The gelation times of hydrogels containing different concentrations of QUDCS were 29, 24, 22, and 18 s, respectively (Supplementary Videos S1-S4). Rheological characterization revealed concentration-dependent mechanical properties critical for ocular applications. Time-sweep measurements demonstrated rapid gelation kinetics, with the storage modulus (G′) consistently exceeding the loss modulus (G″) within minutes of mixing, confirming stable viscoelastic network formation (Fig. 2C). Frequency sweep analysis showed minimal frequency dependence, characteristic of covalently crosslinked networks [24]. The mechanical properties were systematically tuned by varying the QUDCS concentration from 0.5% to 3% (w/v), yielding storage moduli ranging from 4.9 to 34.1 kPa (Fig. 2D). Notably, the 2% formulation exhibited G′ = 26.6 kPa, closely matching the elastic modulus of human corneal stroma (24–39 kPa) measured by atomic force microscopy [19]. This biomechanical compatibility is crucial for maintaining corneal integrity during regeneration while preventing perforation in compromised tissues [25]. Strain amplitude sweeps revealed exceptional strain tolerance, with the gel–sol transition occurring at 820% strain (Fig. 2E), significantly exceeding typical physiological strains experienced during blinking and eye movements [26,27]. The dynamic nature of imine bonds conferred remarkable self-healing properties to the hydrogel. Alternating strain tests (1%/1200%) demonstrated complete recovery of mechanical properties within seconds after network disruption (Fig. 2F). Under high strain (1200%), G′ decreased by three orders of magnitude because of imine bond dissociation. Upon strain relaxation, both moduli rapidly recovered to their initial values, indicating efficient network reformation through dynamic bond exchange [24]. This self-healing capability was macroscopically verified by spontaneous fusion of severed hydrogel pieces within minutes and by maintained injectability through 26-gauge needles (Fig. 2G), enabling minimally invasive administration. Additionally, the incorporation of SDF-1 preserved the original mechanical strength and rheological properties of QUDCS/OD hydrogel (Fig. 2C–F). Thus, successful loading of SDF-1 into the QUDCS/OD matrix forms a multifunctional platform that synergistically integrates antibacterial effects and regenerative signaling while maintaining excellent material performance. QUDCS was synthesized from chitosan through a simple, environmentally friendly, and scalable method. QUDCS serves not only as a potent antibacterial agent but also as the structural foundation for hydrogel formation and as an essential drug carrier. Primary amino groups in the QUDCS backbone and aldehyde groups generated during the OD oxidation were utilized to synthesize hydrogels via Schiff base reactions. Notably, this method does not require additional toxic crosslinking agents, thereby effectively preserving the biological activity of the encapsulated drug, SDF-1. By modulating the concentrations of these two components, the hydrogel exhibits tunable mechanical properties, allowing for the identification of an optimal hydrogel that closely resembles native anterior corneal stromal tissue. A distinguishing feature of this hydrogel preparation, compared with other methods, is its suitability for ocular surface applications. This suitability includes considerations such as the small lesion area, physiological effects of eyelid closure, and the need to prevent damage to intraocular structures, such as the retina, from infrared radiation [28]. Moreover, the rapid gelation and strong adhesive properties of the QUDCS/OD hydrogel mitigate clearance caused by blinking, thereby enhancing patient compliance.

Controlled drug delivery capabilities were evaluated using SDF-1 as a model therapeutic protein. The hydrogel exhibited sustained release kinetics over 7 days, with an initial burst release of 35% within 24 h, followed by steady zero-order release (Fig. 2H). This biphasic profile provides an immediate therapeutic effect while maintaining sustained chemokine levels necessary for continuous stem cell recruitment [29]. The release mechanism involves both diffusion through the porous network and gradual hydrogel degradation. In vitro degradation studies using physiological lysozyme concentration (2 mg/mL) showed progressive mass loss reaching 60% after 42 days (Fig. 2I), thereby ensuring temporary mechanical support without inducing a long-term foreign body response. Microstructural analysis by scanning electron microscopy revealed an interconnected porous architecture essential for nutrient transport and cellular infiltration (Fig. 2J). Increasing QUDCS concentration from 0.5% to 3% resulted in pore diameters of 20.8 ± 11.4, 13.7 ± 5.9, 10.5 ± 5.2, and 8.1 ± 3.8 μm. The decreased pore size correlated with enhanced crosslinking density and mechanical strengths, enabling protein encapsulation while maintaining sufficient permeability to meet corneal metabolic requirements.

Based on preliminary experiments, we established that the effective in vitro concentration of SDF-1 for LESCs + LNCs coculture is approximately 500 ng/mL. By integrating the treatment regimen from subsequent animal studies with concentration guidelines from existing literature, we refined the SDF-1 loading concentration in the hydrogel [24,30]. Ultimately, we determined that a loading concentration of 100 μg/mL allows the hydrogel to sustain a release concentration exceeding 500 ng/mL in the conjunctival sac. This configuration ensures that, during the subsequent 7-day treatment period in the keratitis animal model, a single administration maintains an effective therapeutic concentration of SDF-1 on the ocular surface of mice.

In vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy of QUDCS/OD hydrogel

The antimicrobial properties of the QUDCS/OD hydrogel were systematically evaluated against clinically relevant pathogens associated with bacterial keratitis. Quaternary ammonium chitosan derivatives exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity through membrane-disrupting mechanisms that are distinct from those of conventional antibiotics [20,31]. The hydrogel demonstrated potent bactericidal activity against both Gram-positive (S. aureus and MRSA) and Gram-negative (P. aeruginosa and E. coli) bacteria, with efficacy directly correlated with QUDCS concentration (Fig. 3A and B). Quantitative bactericidal assessment revealed concentration-dependent killing kinetics. At 0.5% QUDCS, bacterial viability decreased to 43–81% across all tested strains. Increasing the concentration to 2% achieved >99% bacterial reduction (>2 log10 reduction) against an initial inoculum of 109 CFU/mL. Complete bacterial eradication (>99.9% killing) was observed at a 3% QUDCS concentration. Notably, the hydrogel maintained comparable efficacy against antibiotic-resistant MRSA, highlighting its potential for treating recalcitrant infections in which conventional antibiotics fail [32]. Time-kill kinetics showed rapid bactericidal activity following treatment with 2% QUDCS/OD hydrogel, with a prolonged effect that reached 99% bacterial reduction within 6–8 h (Fig. 3C). Mechanistic insights into the antibacterial action were obtained using high-resolution scanning electron microscopy. Control bacteria exhibited characteristic smooth cell surfaces with intact morphology—spherical cocci for S. aureus and rod-shaped bacilli for P. aeruginosa and E. coli (Fig. 3D). Following QUDCS/OD treatment, dramatic morphological alterations were evident, including cell surface roughening, membrane wrinkling, and cytoplasmic leakage, consistent with membrane permeabilization. The bactericidal mechanism involves initial electrostatic attraction between cationic quaternary ammonium groups and anionic bacterial phospholipids, followed by membrane insertion of hydrophobic chitosan segments, resulting in lipid bilayer destabilization and cell lysis [33,34]. This physical disruption mechanism reduces the likelihood of resistance development compared with target-specific antibiotics.

Given that biofilm formation represents the predominant virulence factor in chronic bacterial keratitis, conferring 100–1000-fold increased antibiotic resistance [35,36], we evaluated the hydrogel's antibiofilm efficacy using P. aeruginosa as a model organism. The hydrogel demonstrated dual antibiofilm activity: prevention of initial biofilm formation and disruption of established biofilms (Fig. 3E and F). In biofilm prevention assays, 2% QUDCS/OD hydrogel inhibited >99% of biofilm formation compared with untreated controls and exhibited efficacy comparable to that of tobramycin, the current gold-standard aminoglycoside for P. aeruginosa keratitis [37]. More significantly, the hydrogel exhibited superior activity against mature 48 h biofilms, which represent a clinically relevant challenge. While tobramycin reduced biofilm viability to 26%, consistent with known biofilm recalcitrance to antibiotics [38], QUDCS/OD treatment achieved 94% biofilm eradication (Fig. 3F). Three-dimensional confocal microscopy with live/dead staining revealed the structural basis for this enhanced efficacy (Fig. 3G). Untreated biofilms displayed characteristic dense three-dimensional architecture with predominantly viable cells (green fluorescence) embedded in an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix. Tobramycin treatment resulted in partial biofilm disruption with residual viable microcolonies, which may explain the high recurrence rates observed clinically [39]. In contrast, QUDCS/OD treatment caused extensive biofilm dismantling and reduction of biofilm thickness, with predominantly dead cells (red fluorescence). Quantification experiments further demonstrated a significant decrease in biofilm biomass and EPS content, including protein and polysaccharide components (Fig. 3H–J). The superior antibiofilm activity stems from the hydrogel's multifaceted mechanism: (i) electrostatic binding to anionic EPS components (extracellular DNA, proteins, and polysaccharides) disrupts matrix integrity [40]; (ii) the cationic polymer penetrates biofilm channels, thereby accessing embedded bacteria that are inaccessible to antibiotics [41]; and (iii) the membrane-disrupting mechanism remains effective against metabolically dormant persister cells that survive antibiotic treatment [42]. This comprehensive antibiofilm activity addresses a critical limitation of current therapies, in which biofilm-associated infections exhibit high treatment failure rates [43]. Therefore, the combination of potent bactericidal activity, effectiveness against antibiotic-resistant strains, and superior biofilm eradication positions the QUDCS/OD hydrogel as a promising alternative to conventional antimicrobial therapy for bacterial keratitis, particularly in cases complicated by biofilm formation or antibiotic resistance.

Therapeutic efficacy of SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel in bacterial keratitis

Aminoglycoside antibiotics, exemplified by tobramycin, are traditional and classic antibacterial agents that have played a crucial role in the treatment of infectious bacterial keratitis [44]. Tobramycin exhibits potent antibacterial activity and has become a preferred choice for clinical treatment, particularly for infections caused by P. aeruginosa [45]. Nevertheless, several limitations associated with the clinical use of tobramycin eye drops warrant careful consideration. For example, extended, prolonged, and widespread use contributes to the development of bacterial resistance. Tobramycin demonstrates limited efficacy in penetrating and eradicating bacteria within biofilms associated with chronic and contact lens-related infections. Furthermore, its limited tissue permeability poses challenges in achieving effective bactericidal concentrations in deeper tissues through topical administration. The transient retention of eye drops on the ocular surface, combined with tear dilution and rapid clearance through the nasolacrimal duct, necessitates frequent administration to maintain therapeutic drug concentrations. This requirement imposes a significant burden on patient compliance [2,46,47]. These challenges underscore a critical and innovative area of research; the development of novel antibiotic eye drop formulations is transitioning from traditional solution-based approaches toward precise drug-delivery strategies that incorporate advances in materials science, nanotechnology, and pharmaceutics.

The translational potential of the SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel system was evaluated using a clinically relevant murine model of P. aeruginosa keratitis, a leading cause of vision-threatening corneal infections worldwide [5]. Following corneal scarification and bacterial inoculation, characteristic clinical manifestations including stromal edema, epithelial defects, hypopyon formation, and purulent discharge confirmed successful establishment of infection within 24 h. This model recapitulates the aggressive tissue destruction characteristic of P. aeruginosa keratitis, providing a stringent test for therapeutic efficacy [2]. Clinical assessment revealed a biphasic therapeutic response pattern across treatment groups (Fig. 4A and B). During the initial 48-h period, all groups exhibited comparable disease severity, reflecting the acute inflammatory phase dominated by bacterial proliferation and host immune activation. However, divergent therapeutic trajectories emerged from day 3 onward. The QUDCS, SDF-1/QUDCS/OD, TOB, and TOB + SDF-1 groups demonstrated progressive clinical improvement relative to PBS controls (p < 0.05), with the SDF-1-loaded hydrogel exhibiting superior performance from day 5 onward. This temporal profile suggests that, while immediate antibacterial activity provides initial disease control, sustained release of regenerative factors becomes increasingly important during the resolution phase. Quantitative bacteriological analysis on day 7 revealed substantial antimicrobial efficacy across active treatment groups (Fig. 4C). Both QUDCS-containing formulations achieved >2-log reduction in bacterial burden compared with PBS controls (16.6 vs. 5.0 vs. 4.5 × 102 CFU/cornea, p < 0.05), matching the efficacy of clinical standard tobramycin. This equivalence is particularly significant given tobramycin's established role as a first-line therapy for P. aeruginosa keratitis [44]. The comparable bacterial clearance observed between the QUDCS and SDF-1/QUDCS/OD groups indicates that SDF-1 incorporation does not compromise the hydrogel's antimicrobial properties, whereas the superior clinical scores in the latter group suggest additional therapeutic mechanisms beyond bacterial eradication.

The multifaceted healing response observed with SDF-1/QUDCS/OD treatment extends beyond antimicrobial activity to encompass active tissue regeneration and fibrosis prevention. Immunofluorescence analysis of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a canonical marker of myofibroblast transdifferentiation, revealed striking differences in fibrotic responses (Fig. 4D). While PBS, QUDCS and TOB groups exhibited robust α-SMA expression throughout the corneal stroma (mean fluorescence intensity: 650–700 AU), the SDF-1/QUDCS/OD group showed negligible signal (127 ± 36 AU, p < 0.05). This profound antifibrotic effect likely stems from the documented ability of SDF-1 to modulate TGF-β signaling pathways and prevent epithelial-mesenchymal transition, mechanisms previously demonstrated in pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis models [48]. The accelerated re-epithelialization observed with SDF-1/QUDCS/OD treatment (median: 4 days vs. 7 days for QUDCS and TOB groups, p < 0.05) represents a critical therapeutic advantage (Fig. 4E). Rapid restoration of epithelial barrier function not only prevents secondary infection but also reduces exposure of stromal keratocytes to profibrotic stimuli from the tear film [49]. This finding confirms that SDF-1 accelerates the migration of corneal epithelial cells and promotes healing; we speculate that this effect is related to the established role of SDF-1 in mobilizing LESCs through CXCR4-mediated chemotaxis. The temporal correlation between epithelial closure and reduced fibrosis suggests a mechanistic link wherein early barrier restoration interrupts the inflammatory-fibrotic cascade. In an additional experimental group, a tobramycin aqueous solution supplemented with active SDF-1 (TOB + SDF-1) was established to assess the impact of SDF-1. Compared with tobramycin alone, the inclusion of SDF-1 in solution form did not enhance the clinical score of keratitis (Fig. 4A and B), inhibit the expression of the fibrotic factor α-SMA (Fig. 4D), or accelerate corneal epithelial healing (Fig. 4E). We hypothesize that this outcome is attributable to the rapid and uniform distribution of the SDF-1 solution across the ocular surface in mice, followed by rapid clearance from the conjunctival sac through the nasolacrimal duct and related pathways. Consequently, an effective concentration gradient between the lesion and the limbal area was not established, thereby failing to recruit LESCs and LNCs effectively for tissue repair. These findings underscore that although SDF-1 possesses a robust capacity for stem cell recruitment, selection of an appropriate formulation, particularly hydrogel loading, is crucial for its application on the ocular surface.

Histopathological evaluation provided compelling evidence of the tissue-protective effects of SDF-1/QUDCS/OD treatment (Fig. 4F). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed preservation of normal corneal architecture, with intact epithelial stratification, organized stromal lamellae, and minimal inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, the control and comparator groups displayed extensive structural disruption, including epithelial hyperplasia, stromal edema, polymorphonuclear cell infiltration, and neovascularization. These pathological changes, characteristic of severe keratitis, correlate with permanent vision loss in clinical settings [44]. Masson's trichrome staining further elucidated the superior tissue remodeling achieved with SDF-1/QUDCS/OD treatment. The maintenance of parallel collagen fibril organization, essential for corneal transparency, contrasted sharply with the chaotic fibrillar arrangement observed in other groups. This preservation of ultrastructural organization likely results from the synergistic effects of (i) reduced inflammatory cytokine expression, (ii) prevention of keratocyte-to-myofibroblast transformation, and (iii) promotion of physiological wound healing through stem cell recruitment [50,51]. The dense, regularly spaced collagen lamellae observed exclusively in the SDF-1/QUDCS/OD group provide the structural basis for maintained optical clarity, thereby addressing a critical limitation of current antimicrobial therapies which often eliminate infection at the expense of corneal scarring [44].

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) images in mice effectively illustrated the coverage and retention of the hydrogel on the corneal surface. The hydrogel exhibited a high-reflectivity signal (Fig. 4G, indicated by the arrow) on the anterior surface of the mouse cornea. During the initial 3 days of treatment, uneven corneal edema was still observable in the central corneal lesion area. However, as treatment progressed, by day 7, the cornea exhibited a smooth, uniform semiarc shape covered with a highly reflective hydrogel. An additional 2 days of observation were conducted, and by day 9, the hydrogel was still observed covering the fully epithelialized corneal surface. These findings suggest excellent adhesion properties and slow degradation characteristics of SDF-1/QUDCS/OD on the mouse cornea. A single administration was sufficient for the entire treatment cycle of murine keratitis, providing adequate mechanical support and an optimal microenvironment for anti-inflammatory action, tissue repair, and scar reduction in infectious keratitis.

In this study, we successfully developed a murine model of infectious bacterial keratitis by inducing corneal epithelial injury followed by inoculation with P. aeruginosa. This model further substantiates the critical role of corneal epithelial integrity as a primary defense mechanism against external pathogens and underscores its importance as a common pathological foundation for various corneal diseases. Disruption of corneal epithelial integrity can lead to or exacerbate infection, ulceration, scarring, and vision impairment. Consequently, promoting epithelial repair is central to the therapeutic management of numerous corneal conditions [52,53]. Notably, corneal epithelial regeneration is a highly regulated process orchestrated by LESCs, rather than random cellular proliferation. These stem cells reside at the corneoscleral junction and serve as progenitors for the entire corneal epithelium. Understanding the mechanisms that trigger stem cell proliferation, guide migration, and direct differentiation remains a focal area of research [54]. Previous studies have incorporated SDF-1 into hydrogels to address LESCs deficiency resulting from alkali burns, achieving notable therapeutic outcomes [30]. In recent years, the convergence of medicine and engineering, together with advances in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering, has significantly expanded the role of hydrogels beyond physical support and conventional drug delivery. Through precise molecular design and multiscale structural regulation, hydrogels have evolved into complex, multifunctional systems that are responsive and functional, enabling adaptation to intricate and dynamic biological or industrial environments [55]. The hydrogel developed in this study exemplifies these advancements, exhibiting mechanical strength comparable to that of native corneal tissue, thereby contributing to intraocular pressure stability while enhancing stem cell colonization and differentiation in the affected area. Furthermore, its anti-infection capabilities extend beyond bacterial eradication to include prevention of secondary microbial colonization during the critical epithelial repair phase. Moreover, its capacity to recruit stem cells extends beyond tissue repair following P. aeruginosa infection and holds substantial therapeutic potential for a range of corneal diseases, including LESCs deficiency resulting from chemical or thermal burns, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, repeated surgical operation, diabetic keratopathy, and delayed epithelial healing associated with neurotrophic keratitis [56,57].

Biocompatibility assessment

The clinical translation of biomaterial-based therapeutics necessitates comprehensive biocompatibility evaluation. Here, we systematically assessed the safety profile of SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogels through integrated in vitro and in vivo approaches. In vitro cytocompatibility was initially evaluated using immortalized human corneal epithelial cells (iHCECs) and LESCs + LNCs exposed to varying hydrogel concentrations (0.5–3% w/w). Live/dead fluorescence imaging revealed predominantly viable cells exhibiting green fluorescence across all treatment groups over 48 h, with minimal detection of dead cells (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis demonstrated cell viability consistently exceeding 90% across all concentrations, confirming the non-cytotoxic nature of the hydrogel formulation (Fig. 5B). Enhanced cell proliferation was observed at 2% and 3% hydrogel concentrations compared with lower concentrations. This concentration-dependent effect likely correlates with the mechanical properties of the hydrogel matrix [19]. The 2–3% hydrogel formulations likely achieve improved biomechanical compatibility, thereby promoting enhanced cellular responses. Hemocompatibility represents a critical safety parameter for ophthalmic applied biomaterials, as corneal neovascularization or hemorrhage during inflammatory conditions may expose the material to blood components. Hemolysis assays demonstrated that all hydrogel concentrations maintained hemolysis rates below the clinically acceptable threshold of 5%, comparable with PBS controls (Fig. 5C). This excellent hemocompatibility indicates minimal interaction with erythrocyte membranes, supporting the material's safety for potential application in vascularized tissues.

Long-term ocular tolerability was evaluated through a 30-day continuous application study in a murine model. Histological analysis using H&E staining demonstrated preservation of normal corneal architecture with intact epithelial stratification and organized stromal lamellae. In the untreated positive control group with keratitis, pronounced ulcers, edema, fibrous tissue hyperplasia, and inflammatory cell infiltration were evident. TUNEL assays detected no significant apoptosis in corneal tissues, contrasting sharply with the positive control samples (Fig. 5D). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis of corneal tissue homogenates indicated that the expression levels of interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were significantly reduced compared with those in the positive control group. Conversely, in the PBS and SDF-1/QUDCS/OD groups, no significant differences were observed in the expression levels of these cytokines, which remained at low baseline levels (Fig. 5E). Post-treatment examination revealed maintenance of corneal transparency and surface integrity, and negative fluorescein staining confirmed the absence of epithelial defects (Fig. 5F). These findings collectively indicate excellent ocular surface biocompatibility without inducing cytotoxicity or structural damage. Systemic safety evaluation was conducted to exclude potential off-target effects following topical ocular administration. Histopathological examination of major organs—heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys—revealed no pathological alterations compared with PBS-treated controls (Fig. 5G). Tissue architecture remained intact, with preserved cellular morphology, appropriate organ-specific structural organization, and absence of inflammatory infiltrates or degenerative changes. Furthermore, physiological monitoring further showed stable body weight progression within normal parameters (Fig. 5H), indicating no systemic toxicity or metabolic disturbance. Comprehensive biochemical profiling provided additional evidence supporting systemic safety. Hematological parameters, including complete blood counts, remained within physiological ranges without significant intergroup variations. Hepatic function markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) and renal function indicators (blood urea nitrogen [BUN], creatinine) showed no elevation, confirming the absence of organ-specific toxicity (Fig. 5I). Maintenance of these homeostatic parameters throughout the treatment period substantiates the favorable biocompatibility profile of the SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel system. Collectively, these multi-tiered biocompatibility assessments establish the SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel as a safe therapeutic platform for ocular surface applications. The combination of favorable cytocompatibility, hemocompatibility, ocular tolerability, and systemic safety profiles positions this hydrogel system as a promising candidate for clinical translation in the treatment of inflammatory keratitis through its dual anti-inflammatory and regenerative mechanisms.

Spatiotemporal dynamics of the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis in P. aeruginosa keratitis

The directed migration of stem cells to injury sites, termed “homing”, represents a fundamental mechanism in tissue regeneration mediated through chemokine-receptor interactions [58]. Among the diverse chemokine networks, the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis has emerged as a principal biological pathway governing mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) recruitment to damaged tissues [59,60]. CXCR4, a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor, exhibits high-affinity binding to its cognate ligand SDF-1, thereby initiating intracellular signaling cascades that regulate cell migration, proliferation, and survival [14,59]. Although this axis has been extensively characterized in multiple tissue repair contexts, its spatiotemporal dynamics during corneal infectious inflammation remain incompletely defined.

The biological function of the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis is closely associated with its spatiotemporal distribution characteristics. This distribution is dynamic rather than static or uniform and demonstrates both temporal variability and spatial polarity [14,59,61]. In normal adult tissues, SDF-1 is expressed at relatively high levels in a tissue-specific manner, establishing stable spatial anchor points. This macroscopic gradient remains relatively stable and functions as a positional cue for cellular localization [14]. The baseline distribution pattern in healthy corneal tissue confirms the presence of regional heterogeneity (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated significantly higher expression of both SDF-1 and CXCR4 in the limbal region compared to the central cornea in wild-type mice. This distribution pattern corresponds to the established localization of limbal stem cells, suggesting a constitutive role for the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis in maintaining the limbal stem cell niche [16]. Such spatial stability supports preservation of stem cells and contributes to tissue homeostasis and developmental processes. Conversely, P. aeruginosa infection induced marked upregulation of both molecules at the central corneal injury site, indicating activation of endogenous repair mechanisms through chemokine signaling.

Under pathological conditions, treatment-induced modulation of SDF-1 expression demonstrated distinct kinetics between endogenous and exogenous chemokine delivery systems (Fig. 6B and C). The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel group demonstrated rapid and sustained elevation of SDF-1 levels in the central cornea from day 3 onward, exceeding levels observed in both the QUDCS control group and the limbal region. This accelerated accumulation reflects successful exogenous SDF-1 delivery and retention at the injury site through the hydrogel matrix. In contrast, the QUDCS group exhibited delayed endogenous SDF-1 upregulation, with central corneal levels approaching limbal expression only by day 9. This temporal delay suggests that antibacterial treatment alone is insufficient to induce rapid chemokine production required for optimal tissue repair, particularly in P. aeruginosa keratitis, which is highly susceptible to stromal degradation. Delayed tissue repair may result in corneal perforation, leakage of intraocular contents, and potentially irreversible vision loss, leading to severe clinical outcomes.

CXCR4-expressing cell recruitment displayed distinct temporal dynamics contingent upon treatment modality (Fig. 6D and E). Despite rapid SDF-1 accumulation, CXCR4+ cell migration exhibited delayed kinetics, consistent with the time required for chemotactic gradient establishment and cellular response [62,63]. By day 3, all groups exhibited higher CXCR4 expression in the limbus than in the central cornea, indicating initial preservation of the stem cell niche. However, divergent patterns emerged during subsequent time points. The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel group demonstrated substantial CXCR4+ cell accumulation in the central cornea by day 6, surpassing limbal levels and preceding the QUDCS group by approximately 3 days. This accelerated recruitment correlates with enhanced SDF-1 gradient formation through exogenous delivery.

The mechanistic implications of these findings extend beyond simple chemokine supplementation. Establishment of an effective chemotactic gradient requires not only adequate chemokine concentration but also appropriate spatial distribution and temporal stability [64,65]. CXCR4 responses to SDF-1 are transient and adaptive in nature, following a cyclical sequence of response–inactivation–recovery [66,67]. This temporal dynamic pattern at the single-cell level enables cells to continuously detect relative changes in concentration gradients rather than absolute concentrations, thereby facilitating directional movement. Exogenous SDF-1 delivery generates sustained concentration gradients that resist rapid diffusion. Spatial polarity distribution of the receptor CXCR4 modulates directional perception at the single-cell level, whereas delayed receptor responsiveness contributes to coordinated tissue development [59]. This coupling of temporal and spatial factors underscores the biological importance of central retention, marginal recruitment, and peripheral activation of stem cells [15]. The accuracy of this spatiotemporal coding system surpasses the simplistic notion of chemokines merely as chemical messengers. Rather, it resembles an integrated regulatory framework incorporating spatial positioning, temporal modulation, local matrix content, and cellular state [68]. The hydrogel delivery system addresses these requirements through (i) sustained release kinetics maintaining therapeutic SDF-1 levels, (ii) localized retention preventing rapid clearance, and (iii) preservation of chemokine bioactivity through protective encapsulation. The observed 3-day advancement in CXCR4+ cell recruitment in the SDF-1/QUDCS/OD group represents a clinically significant acceleration of the repair cascade, potentially reducing infection duration and minimizing tissue damage. PBS control group dynamics provided crucial insights into unassisted repair processes. Although gradual CXCR4 upregulation occurred in the central cornea, expression remained below limbal levels throughout the observation period. This persistent imbalance suggests insufficient endogenous chemokine production to overcome infection-induced disruption of stem cell trafficking. Failure to establish an effective chemotactic gradient likely contributes to delayed healing and increased susceptibility to complications observed in untreated keratitis. Collectively, these spatiotemporal analyses collectively demonstrate that although the endogenous SDF-1/CXCR4 axis participates in P. aeruginosa keratitis repair, its activation kinetics are insufficient for rapid tissue restoration. The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel system augments this natural repair mechanism through controlled exogenous chemokine delivery, establishing robust chemotactic gradients that accelerate CXCR4+ stem cell homing to injury sites. Targeted enhancement of the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for promoting corneal regeneration while preserving the antimicrobial efficacy necessary for infection resolution.

SDF-1-mediated Activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in limbal stem cells

Building upon our in vivo findings demonstrating SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel-mediated corneal repair through stem cell homing, we further investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying this regenerative response. The SDF-1/CXCR4 axis activates multiple downstream signaling cascades, including ERK/MAPK, PLC, PI3K-Akt, and notably the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [61,69,70]. Given the established role of Wnt signaling in maintaining limbal stem cell homeostasis and its correlation with stem cell markers in the corneal epithelium [51,71], we hypothesized that exogenous SDF-1 promotes corneal regeneration through CXCR4-mediated activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Cellular characterization and CXCR4 expression analysis confirmed the phenotypic identity of primary cultured limbal cells (Fig. 7A). LESCs exhibited characteristic expression of the epithelial progenitor marker ΔNp63 and stem cell marker ABCG2, whereas LNCs expressed the mesenchymal marker vimentin and pluripotency-associated factor SOX2 [72,73]. Importantly, both cell populations expressed CXCR4, confirming their responsiveness to SDF-1-mediated chemotactic signals. This dual expression pattern suggests coordinated stem and niche cell responses during corneal repair processes. Dose-dependent chemotactic responses were evaluated using transwell migration assays (Fig. 7B and C). SDF-1 induced robust chemotaxis in both LESCs and LNCs, with maximal response occurring at 500 ng/mL, beyond which no additional migration was observed, indicating receptor saturation. Immunofluorescence staining of cells that migrated to the lower chamber (Fig. 7D) confirmed expression of ΔNp63, ABCG2, vimentin, and SOX2, indicating that SDF-1 recruited a mixed population of LESCs and LNCs. Following treatment with the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100, SDF-1-induced chemotaxis was abolished. Notably, the Wnt pathway inhibitor DKK1 significantly attenuated SDF-1-induced migration without affecting baseline motility, providing initial evidence for Wnt signaling involvement in SDF-1-mediated chemotaxis. The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel similarly promoted cell migration, validating its bioactivity in delivering functional SDF-1.

Functional migration capacity was further validated using wound healing assays (Fig. 7E and F). Both 500 ng/mL SDF-1 and SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel treatments accelerated scratch closure over 48 h, with healing rates significantly exceeding those of controls. The inhibitory effect of DKK1 on SDF-1-induced wound healing further substantiated the involvement of the Wnt pathway in this reparative process. AMD3100 competitively binds to CXCR4, thereby inhibiting the chemotactic influence of SDF-1 on stem cells and confirming the specificity of SDF-1 as the natural ligand for CXCR4. In addition to recruiting CXCR4-positive limbal stem cells through chemotactic gradients, SDF-1 enhances their directional motility, which is a critical factor for effective wound coverage. Proliferative responses exhibited temporal specificity, with significant effects observed only after 72 h of treatment (Fig. 7G and H). This delayed proliferative response, in contrast to the immediate migratory effects, suggests distinct temporal regulation of migration and proliferation downstream of CXCR4 activation. Higher SDF-1 concentrations (1000–2000 ng/mL) induced greater proliferation, whereas DKK1 co-treatment abolished these effects, confirming Wnt pathway dependency. The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel achieved proliferative efficacy of soluble SDF-1, demonstrating sustained bioactivity through controlled release.

Molecular mechanism validation using Western blot analysis revealed activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Fig. 7I). SDF-1 treatment induced significant nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, a hallmark of Wnt pathway activation. Mechanistically, this occurred through enhanced GSK3β phosphorylation at Ser9, which inhibits GSK3β catalytic activity and prevents β-catenin degradation [74]. The phosphorylated β-catenin remained cytoplasmic, whereas non-phosphorylated β-catenin translocated to the nucleus. DKK1 co-treatment effectively blocked nuclear β-catenin accumulation despite SDF-1 presence, confirming pathway specificity. Subcellular localization dynamics were visualized using immunofluorescence microscopy (Fig. 7J). Under basal conditions, β-catenin localized predominantly to cell membranes and cytoplasm, consistent with its structural role in adherens junctions. SDF-1 or SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel treatment induced pronounced nuclear translocation, indicative of transcriptional activation. DKK1 prevented this nuclear accumulation, maintaining cytoplasmic sequestration despite SDF-1 stimulation.

In prior in vivo studies, we demonstrated that the QUDCS/OD hydrogel enriched with SDF-1 facilitates the recruitment of marginal stem cells through the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis, thereby contributing to corneal repair in murine models of keratitis. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear. Existing evidence indicates that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is active in corneal epithelial cells. β-catenin is predominantly localized in the perinuclear cytoplasm of LESCs and positively correlates with the expression of Wnt7A and its receptor FZD5 in these cells. Inhibition of the Wnt signaling results in downregulation of LESC-associated markers [50,51,71]. The Wnt signaling pathway exhibits a dual role, functioning as a morphogenetic factor during the embryonic stage, a maintainer of tissue regeneration in adulthood, and a driver of tumorigenesis under pathological conditions [75]. Accordingly, aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a critical driver in colorectal cancer and is closely associated with therapeutic targeting strategies and tumor drug resistance [75,76]. In the in vitro migration experiment, the chemotactic effect of SDF-1 on limbal stem cells preceded its proliferative effect, thereby mitigating the risk of adverse outcomes such as uncontrolled proliferation. This precise multi-level control is likely attributed to the core negative regulatory kinase, GSK-3β, which serves as a convergence point for multiple signaling pathways [77]. Dual regulation of migration and proliferation through the common Wnt/β-catenin axis offers a coordinated mechanism for tissue repair responses. The temporal dissociation between immediate migratory and delayed proliferative responses indicates a hierarchical regulation, in which migration is prioritized to rapidly populate injury sites prior to proliferative expansion. This sequence optimizes repair efficiency while potentially minimizing the risk of aberrant proliferation. The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel effectively replicates these physiological responses, supporting its therapeutic potential in enhancing endogenous repair mechanisms through sustained chemokine delivery and activation of the Wnt pathway.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a transformative approach to bacterial keratitis treatment through the development of a multifunctional hydrogel platform that integrates antimicrobial action with endogenous tissue regeneration. The synergistic combination of potent antibacterial properties of QUDCS with SDF-1 regenerative signaling represents a fundamental advance in addressing the dual challenges of infection control and tissue repair. The rapid gelation kinetics, mechanical compliance, and self-healing properties of this hydrogel underscore its translational potential for clinical application. In the realm of fundamental research, we demonstrate that the chemotactic response and proliferation of limbal stem cells mediated by the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis occur through the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Furthermore, we provide a detailed analysis of the biological basis underlying the temporal and spatial distribution patterns of SDF-1 and CXCR4. These findings offer insight into stem cell regulation and contribute to the advancement of regenerative medicine strategies. Future studies should investigate pathway crosstalk mechanisms, optimize controlled-release kinetics across distinct therapeutic windows, and evaluate efficacy against polymicrobial infections, thereby advancing toward next-generation therapeutics that harness intrinsic regenerative capacity while combating resistant pathogens.

Methods

Materials

β-Chitin was isolated from squid pens (Zhejiang Golden-Shell Biochemical Co. Ltd., China) following established protocols [78]. Ultra-highly deacetylated chitosan (UDCS) was prepared through repeated alkaline treatment of β-chitin with 60% KOH at 80 °C for 1 h, followed by neutralization with deionized water. This process, repeated three times, yielded UDCS with 99% degree of deacetylation and weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 1.93 × 105 g/mol. Dextran (Mw ∼500 kDa) was purchased from Shanghai Ruiyong Biotechnology (China). 2,3-Epoxypropyltrimethylammonium chloride (EPTMAC), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT), and analytical-grade reagents were obtained from Shanghai Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (China). The calcein-AM/PI double stain kit was acquired from Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Bacterial strains including Staphylococcus aureus ATCC6538 (S. aureus), methicillin-resistant S. aureus USA300 LAC (MRSA), and Escherichia coli ATCC25922 (E. coli) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. A P. aeruginosa clinical isolate was provided by Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College.

Synthesis and preparation of SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel

QUDCS was synthesized via nucleophilic substitution following our previous work [20]. Briefly, UDCS (1 g) was dissolved using an acid-base dissolution method, followed by the addition of EPTMAC (3.77 g). The molar ratio of EPTMAC to D-glucosamine units of UDCS was 4:1. The reaction proceeded at 0 °C for 24 h under continuous stirring. After neutralization with HCl, the product was dialyzed against distilled water and lyophilized to obtain QUDCS. Oxidized dextran (OD) was prepared through periodate oxidation [24]. Dextran (2 g) was dissolved in distilled water (200 mL) and oxidized with NaIO4 (1 g) for 2.5 h. The reaction was quenched with ethylene glycol (0.5 mL) for 1 h, followed by dialysis and lyophilization.

For hydrogel preparation, QUDCS was dissolved in sterile PBS at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, or 3.0% (w/v), while OD was prepared at 2.0% (w/v). SDF-1 recombinant protein (460-SD, R&D Systems, USA) was incorporated into the OD solution to achieve a final concentration of 100 μg/mL. Hydrogels were formed by mixing equal volumes of QUDCS and SDF-1/OD solutions, resulting in rapid gelation through Schiff base formation.

SDF-1 release kinetics and enzymatic degradation

Release kinetics were evaluated using a mouse SDF-1 ELISA kit (MCX120, R&D Systems, USA). SDF-1 solution (2 μg in 100 μL) was mixed with OD solution (20 μL, 20 mg/mL), followed by addition of QUDCS solution (20 μL, 30 mg/mL) to form the hydrogel. The gel was immersed in PBS (10 mL) as a release medium. At predetermined intervals (1, 6, 12, 24 h, and 2, 3, 5, 7 days), aliquots (100 μL) were collected and stored at −80 °C, with fresh buffer replacing the withdrawn volume. Cumulative SDF-1 release was quantified according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Enzymatic degradation was assessed by incubating hydrogel samples (1 mL) in lysozyme solution (10 mL, 2 mg/mL in PBS) at 37 °C. The enzyme solution was refreshed weekly to maintain activity. Characterization

The degree of quaternization (DQ) was determined through argentometric titration of chloride ions [79], while the oxidation degree of OD was measured via hydroxylamine hydrochloride titration [80]. Molecular weights of QUDCS and OD were characterized using size exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS, DAWN HELEOS-II, Wyatt Technology, USA) equipped with a He-Ne laser (λ = 663.4 nm), using 0.3 M HAc/0.2 M NH4Ac as eluent. 1H NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III HD 400 MHz spectrometer in proton noise-decoupling mode.

Rheological properties were characterized using a Discovery HR-2 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) with parallel-plate geometry (40 mm diameter). All measurements were performed within the linear viscoelastic region at 1% strain amplitude. Time-sweep experiments monitored gelation kinetics at 37 °C and 10 rad/s angular frequency. Frequency-sweep measurements (0.01–100 rad/s) were conducted at 37 °C following 1 h pre-gelation.

Antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity

Bacterial cultures were grown in LB broth at 37 °C for 10 h with agitation, harvested by centrifugation (3500 rpm), and resuspended in PBS to 2 × 109 CFU/mL. Equal volumes of bacterial suspension and QUDCS/OD hydrogel were combined and incubated at 37 °C for 8 h. Viable bacteria were enumerated through serial dilution and plate counting on LB agar. PBS-treated bacteria served as controls. Bacteria survival was calculated relative to the control.

For morphological analysis, treated bacteria were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight, dehydrated through graded ethanol series (30-100%), deposited on silicon wafers, and imaged using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Zeiss SIGMA, Germany).

Biofilm inhibition was assessed in 96-well plates by combining QUDCS/OD hydrogels (50 μL) or tobramycin (6 mg/mL) with P. aeruginosa suspension (50 μL, 2 × 106 CFU/mL in MH broth). After 48 h incubation at 37 °C, biofilm viability was quantified using the MTT assay. Optical density at 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Spark 10M, Switzerland). Biofilm formation percentage was calculated relative to untreated controls.

For mature biofilm eradication, P. aeruginosa (100 μL, 106 CFU/mL) was cultured for 4 days to establish biofilms. Treatment with QUDCS/OD hydrogels or tobramycin (3 mg/mL) was applied for 12 h at 37 °C. Biofilm viability was assessed via MTT assay, with survival percentage calculated relative to untreated mature biofilms.

For biofilm biomass quantification, treated mature biofilms were stained with crystal violet solution (0.1% w/v, 60 μL) and incubated for 30 min. Excess dye was removed by gentle washing with deionized water, and 200 μL 95% ethanol was added to solubilize bound dye, followed by measurement of optical density at 610 nm.

For biofilm protein content quantification, treated biofilms were incubated with 20 μg/mL FITC solution for 30 min in darkness. Following three washes with PBS, biofilms were resuspended in 200 μL deionized water. Fluorescence intensity was measured at λex = 485 nm and λem = 528 nm.

Biofilm polysaccharide content was quantified using the phenol-sulfuric acid method: 40 μL of 3% phenol solution and 100 μL of concentrated H2SO4 was added to treated mature biofilms. Following 30 min incubation at room temperature, the optical density was measured at 490 nm.

Mouse model of P. aeruginosa keratitis

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained and acclimated for one week under specific pathogen-free conditions. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC-3467). Following anesthesia with 1% pentobarbital sodium, central corneal epithelium was mechanically debrided and inoculated with 10 μL of P. aeruginosa suspension (108 CFU/mL). Successful infection was confirmed 24 h post-inoculation by a keratitis grade ≥2 [81]. Mice were randomly allocated to five treatment groups (n = 11 per group): PBS control, QUDCS alone, SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel, tobramycin (TOB), and TOB + SDF-1. The SDF-1/QUDCS/OD group received a single treatment on day 1, whereas other groups received daily treatments. Anterior segment photography and fluorescein sodium staining were performed daily under anesthesia. AS-OCT images were obtained using a multimodal small-animal imaging system (Saris, Robotrak Technologies, China). Clinical scores were independently evaluated by three masked investigators according to established criteria [82]. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation on days 7 or 9 for tissue collection and analysis.

Bacterial load quantification

Harvested corneas were homogenized in 1 mL sterile saline and serially diluted tenfold. Aliquots (100 μL) were plated on nutrient agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Colony-forming units were enumerated, and bacterial load was calculated as CFU per cornea.

Histopathological analysis

Enucleated eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) or Masson's trichrome staining for morphological assessment. For immunohistochemistry, deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and overnight incubation at 4 °C with anti-SDF-1 antibody (1:50, 17402-1-AP, Proteintech, China). Sections were then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were acquired using a light microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Paraffin sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized through graded alcohols, subjected to microwave-mediated antigen retrieval, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Following blocking with 5% BSA for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-α-SMA (1:500, GB111364, Servicebio, China), anti-SDF-1 (1:200, 17402-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-CXCR4 (1:100, 11073-2-AP, Proteintech). After Phosphate Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (PBST) washing, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at 37 °C in darkness. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (G1407, Servicebio) and mounted with anti-fade medium. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal microscope.

Isolation and culture of primary murine LESCs and LNCs

LESCs and limbal niche cells LNCs were isolated from 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice following established protocols [83,84]. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and enucleated eyeballs were thoroughly washed with PBS to remove blood contamination. Central corneal tissue was excised using a 2-mm trephine, preserving the peripheral limbal region. The tissue underwent enzymatic digestion in 2 mg/mL dispase II (D6430, Solarbio, China) for 25 min at 37 °C. Following PBS washing, the corneal epithelium was mechanically separated from the underlying stroma. The epithelial layer was further digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 15 min, neutralized with culture medium, and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. The remaining stromal tissue containing LNCs was digested in 2.5 mg/mL collagenase type I (1940MG100, Biofroxx, Germany) for 3 h at 37 °C, followed by trypsinization for 15 min. After neutralization and filtration, cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured in supplemental hormonal epithelial medium (SHEM) comprising DMEM/F-12 (1:1) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum, 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide, 2 ng/mL recombinant human EGF, 5 μg/mL insulin, 5 μg/mL transferrin, 5 ng/mL sodium selenite, 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone, 1 nM cholera toxin, 50 μg/mL gentamicin, and 1.25 μg/mL amphotericin B. Following two passages, enriched populations of LESCs and LNCs were obtained and co-cultured for subsequent experiments.

Functional assays for cell behavior

Cell Proliferation Assessment. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; BS350B, Biosharp, China). Briefly, 1 × 104 cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates and treated with various concentrations of SDF-1, DKK1 (200 ng/mL; 5897-DK, R&D Systems, USA), AMD3100 (50 ng/mL; HY-10046, MedChemExpress, USA) or SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel. At designated time points, the medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Chemotaxis Analysis. Cell migration was assessed using 24-well Transwell chambers with 8-μm pore polycarbonate membranes. LESCs and LNCs (5 × 104 cells) suspended in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, whereas the lower chamber contained complete medium supplemented with various concentrations of SDF-1, DKK1, AMD3100, or SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel. After incubation for 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed using cotton swabs. Migrated cells were imaged under a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ software. Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate.

Wound Healing Assay. Cells were cultured to 90% confluence in 6-well plates. Linear scratches were created using a sterile 200-μL pipette tip, followed by thorough washing with PBS to remove cellular debris. Cells were subsequently cultured in serum-free medium containing various treatments. Wound closure was monitored at regular intervals using phase-contrast microscopy, and the healing rate was quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunocytochemistry and Western Blot Analysis

Immunofluorescence Staining. LESCs and LNCs cultured on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ABCG2 (1:100; 27286-1-AP, Proteintech, China), ΔNp63 (1:1000; ab203826, Abcam, USA), SOX2 (1:1000; GB15249, Servicebio, China), vimentin (1:500; GB11192, Servicebio, China), CXCR4 (1:100; 11073-2-AP, Proteintech, China), or β-catenin (1:1000; 80488-1-RR, Proteintech, China). Following incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using fluorescence or confocal microscopy for nuclear-cytoplasmic β-catenin localization analysis.

Western Blot Analysis. After 72 h of treatment with PBS (control), SDF-1, SDF-1+DKK1, or SDF-1/QUDCS/OD hydrogel, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using a commercial extraction kit. Total cellular protein lysates were prepared for GSK3β analysis. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against β-catenin (1:8000; 80488-1-RR, Proteintech, China), phospho-β-catenin (1:8000; 80035-2-RR, Proteintech, China), GSK3β (1:1000; TA5016, Abmart, China), or phospho-GSK3βSer9 (1:1000; T40070, Abmart, China). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000; SA00001-2, Proteintech, China) for 1 h at room temperature, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance between two groups was determined by an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test, whereas multiple comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
