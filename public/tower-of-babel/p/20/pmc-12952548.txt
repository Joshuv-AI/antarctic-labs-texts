Artificial Intelligence–enabled Automatic Computed Tomography Segmentation for Craniomaxillofacial Surgery Using a Real Surgical Database
Authors: Songying Wu, Pui Hang Leung, Andy Wai Kan Yeung, May Chun Mei Wong, Kar Yan Li, Taku Komura, Wei-fa Yang, Yu-xiong Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Skull computed tomography (CT) segmentation is the cornerstone of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery. Clinical routine threshold-based segmentation is time-consuming and yields suboptimal results in critical anatomical regions. This study evaluated the performance of an artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled automatic segmentation of skull CT scans and investigated clinical factors affecting its performance.Methods:The segmentation outcomes of 44 preoperative skull CT scans from a surgery database by AI-enabled and clinical routine methods were evaluated using quantitative metrics and qualitative assessment. The impact of occlusal contact, metallic artifact, bone involvement, slice increment, and pixel size of the CT scan was analyzed.Results:The mean Dice coefficient (DICE) of AI-enabled segmentation of the upper skull was 92.19% ± 2.59%, comparable to the clinical routine segmentation at 91.72% ± 3.77% (P = 0.228). The mean DICE of AI-enabled segmentation of the mandible was 94.81% ± 3.24%, outperforming clinical routine segmentation at 91.77% ± 5.21% (P < 0.001). AI yielded superior segmentation at the anterior maxillary wall and the temporomandibular joint. The presence of occlusal contact adversely affected AI segmentation of the mandible. Smaller slice increments and pixel sizes were associated with improved AI accuracy, whereas metallic artifacts and bone involvement had no significant effect.Conclusions:AI yielded comparable accuracy to the clinical routine method for skull CT segmentation, with better performance in critical anatomical regions and elimination of metallic artifacts. This study served as an external validation cohort to support future application of this AI-enabled segmentation model in the workflow of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery.

Takeaways

Question: How does automatic artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled segmentation perform compared with clinical routine threshold-based segmentation for computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery?

Findings: AI yielded at least equivalent segmentation accuracy to the clinical routine method for skull computed tomography segmentation, with better performance in critical anatomical regions and the elimination of metallic artifacts.

Meaning: The result of this study served as an external validation cohort to support future application of this cloud-based AI-enabled segmentation model in the workflow of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Computer-assisted surgery (CAS) applies computer technology in surgery by dedicating virtual surgical planning (VSP) and accurate execution of the plan in surgery. In recent decades, CAS has been revolutionizing the field of craniomaxillofacial surgery, yielding favorable clinical outcomes, especially with the application of 3D-printed patient-specific surgical plates and simultaneous dental implants.1,2 Computed tomography (CT) segmentation, which extracts bone structures from CT images, is the cornerstone for constructing 3D virtual models and enhancing the streamlined VSP workflow.3

The clinical routine CT segmentation method in the field of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery adopts a threshold-based strategy based on Hounsfield units (HUs), which is a quantitative scale proportional to x-ray attenuation and radiodensity of different tissues.4,5 The single HU threshold-defined segmentation of the skull is subject to intrinsic flaws due to the inhomogeneous intensity of bony structures and the presence of metallic artifacts. It has been shown that the selection of different thresholds could lead to a surface deviation of up to 0.6 mm.6 The segmentation process is also highly labor-intensive and time-consuming.5,7

The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled imaging segmentation in recent years has demonstrated great potential for skull CT segmentation and for streamlining the CAS workflow (Fig. 1).8,9 One of the most commonly used techniques for AI-enabled segmentation is a convolutional neural network, which uses a structured multilayer neural network, independently of prior knowledge and human intervention, to solve computer vision tasks.10–13 The prevalent 3D U-Net uses a U-shaped convolutional neural network architecture for medical image segmentation, which has been exploited in the segmentation of the mandible and teeth in research.14–17

A brief workflow for computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery with AI-enabled segmentation. AI-enabled segmentation serves as the cornerstone for computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery.

From the clinical perspective, AI-enabled segmentation is expected to boost the VSP workflow, reduce interoperator deviation, and potentially improve surgical accuracy. Nevertheless, despite the burgeoning role of AI-related research, the evaluation of its clinical performance and applications has significantly lagged behind. This study aimed to conduct a clinical evaluation of AI-enabled segmentation of preoperative CT scans in computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery, which laid the basis for large-scale clinical applications. The secondary objective of this study was to identify clinical factors that could potentially affect the accuracy of the current AI model.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Surgical Database

Preoperative CT scans from the surgical database of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery were selected. The inclusion criteria were (1) patients with signed informed consent; (2) availability of preoperative spiral CT of the skull; and (3) a diagnosis of head and neck tumor or pathology. The exclusion criteria were (1) prior head and neck operation, and (2) congenital deformities affecting the skull bones. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster with a reference number UW 21-792.

Ground Truth and Segmentation Workflow

The preoperative spiral CT digital imaging and communications in medicine dataset was imported into ProPlan CMF (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) for the construction of ground truth volume and clinical routine segmentation. The ground truth segmentation was conducted by 2 calibrated assessors (S.W. and P.H.L.) in a slice-by-slice fashion of the axial plane. Each case was cross-checked by both assessors until agreement was reached to ensure the accuracy of the ground truth segmentation. Each CT scan was segmented into the upper skull and the mandible.

The clinical routine segmentation, established as the method for daily clinical practice in CAS, was conducted using the global threshold-based approach (S.W. and P.H.L.). The default threshold for bone structure in ProPlan software was 226–3071 HU, which was adjusted for each case to achieve the best segmentation outcomes. Manual delineations of the upper and lower dentitions, the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), and the atlanto-occipital joint were performed on the threshold-defined model to segment the upper skull and the mandible.

The AI-enabled segmentation was performed in a cloud-based platform launched by Mimics Viewer (Materialise), the algorithm of which uses AI, including thresholding, machine learning, deep learning, graph-based and model-based segmentation algorithms, and combinations thereof that have been locked with proprietary limitations. Raw CT data were packed in .mcs format and uploaded to the Materialise Mimic Viewer CMF platform, which automatically segmented the CT scan to the upper skull, mandible, maxillary teeth, mandibular teeth, hyoid, and metal (Fig. 2). The upper skull and maxillary teeth, as well as the mandible and mandibular teeth, were merged with a gap-closing distance equal to the pixel size, forming the AI-segmented upper skull and mandible for evaluation.

Quantitative Assessment

Four commonly used quantitative metrics were computed to evaluate the accuracy of different segmentation methods by comparison with the ground truth18: (1) Dice similarity coefficient (DICE); (2) intersection over union (IoU); (3) 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95); and (4) average HD (AVD). These metrics measure 2 things in essence: the overall volumetric agreement between 2 segments in percentage (%), and the surface outline discrepancy in millimeters.

DICE and IoU coefficients are volumetric-based metrics that indicate the degree of volumetric agreement between the ground truth and the proposed segmentations. The DICE and IoU coefficients are between 0% and 100%. A higher value indicates a higher degree of agreement between the ground truth segmentation and the proposed segmentation.

HD95 and AVD are spatial distance–based metrics for evaluating the agreement of surface contours, which are measured in distances equal to or larger than 0 mm. A smaller value indicates a higher degree of agreement of the surface contours between the ground truth segmentation and the proposed segmentation. Dice Similarity Coefficient

DICE is the most commonly used volumetric metric for the evaluation of medical segmentation. It is defined as DICE=2TP/(2TP+FP+FN). TP, FP, and FN stand for true positive, false positive, and false negative, respectively.

Intersection Over Union

IoU is another commonly used volumetric metric for the evaluation of medical segmentation. It is defined as IoU=TP/(TP+FP+FN). DICE and IoU are mathematically related by the following formula: DICE=(2×IoU)/(1+IoU). IoU was included in addition to DICE primarily for future review reference considerations, given that DICE is more commonly used in AI model optimization, whereas IoU is more intuitive in performance evaluation.

Ninety-fifth Percentile HD95

The directed HD from point set A on the surface of 1 segmentation object to point set B on the surface of the other segmentation object is defined as h(A,B)=maxa∈A(minb∈B(d(a,b))), where d(a,b) is defined as the Euclidean distance from point a to point b, given that point sets A and B represent ground truth and proposed segmentation results, respectively. The HD between A and B is H(A,B)=max{h(A,B), h(B,A)}. HD is sensitive to outliers and noise generally. To exclude the possible outliers, the 95th quantile of distances is commonly used.18

Average HD

AVD between point sets A and B is defined as AVD(A,B)={1|A|∑a∈Aminb∈B[d(a,b)]+ 1|B|∑b∈Bmina∈A[d(b,a)]}/2, which calculates the average HD. AVD is relatively stable and less sensitive to noise and outliers, while still being able to detect the difference in segmentation results.

Qualitative Assessment

To better evaluate the CT segmentation in the context of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery, qualitative assessments were performed by assessors with VSP experience to evaluate the completeness of anatomical regions of interest for different segmentation methods, including the anterior maxillary sinus wall, piriform rim, orbital rim, zygoma, mandibular condyle, mandibular fossa, and teeth, which were critical to ensuring smooth designing and fitting of patient-specific surgical plates in computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery.

Clinical Factors

Three patient factors were proposed based on the challenges they posed in the clinical routine segmentation. Specifically, CT scans taken with patients’ teeth in occlusion often require much more time in segmentation than those taken with patients’ teeth apart due to the difficulty in delineating the upper and lower dentition of similar HU. Similarly, the presence of metallic artifacts poses challenges in distinguishing the real dentition from the artifacts, and intraosseous lesions cause variations in bone morphology and density. Therefore, these factors were selected for clinical factor analysis.

Additionally, both the slice increment and the pixel size, which affect the spatial resolution of CT imaging, have been shown to influence automatic contouring algorithms. Therefore, they were also selected as independent variables of interest in this study.3

Statistical Analysis

Data in this study did not follow normal distributions as indicated by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Nevertheless, with reference to the current literature on AI segmentation, which commonly reports mean and SD, both parametric tests and nonparametric tests were conducted, with the results of nonparametric tests listed in the supplementary material. Specifically, the paired sample t test was applied to evaluate the accuracy of different segmentation methods; the independent t test and Pearson correlation analysis were applied to evaluate the potential patient and imaging factors that might affect the segmentation accuracy. Corresponding nonparametric tests were the related-samples Wilcoxon signed rank test, the Mann-Whitney test, and Spearman correlation. In general, nonparametric tests were more robust to outliers in the dataset. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed on IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29.

RESULTS

AI-enabled Segmentation Versus Clinical Routine Segmentation

A total of 44 preoperative spiral CT scans of 44 patients who underwent computer-assisted jaw reconstructive surgery were included. The average age of the patients was 64 ± 17 years, including 24 women (58.5%) and 20 men (41.5%). Diagnosis included oral squamous cell carcinoma (79.5%), ameloblastoma (9.1%), osteosarcoma (2.3%), ossifying fibroma (2.3%), osteoradionecrosis (2.3%), epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (2.3%), and carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma (2.3%). The imaging slice thickness was between 0.625 and 2.5 mm, the pixel size was between 0.412 and 0.625 mm, the x-ray tube voltage was 120 or 140 kV, and the gantry tilt was 0 degrees.

DISCUSSION

Overall, AI segmentation yielded at least equivalent results compared with clinical routine segmentation. Advantages of AI-enabled segmentation included higher segmentation accuracy in the mandible, better capture of anatomical structures critical to computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery, and more thorough elimination of metallic artifacts.

Different commercial and in-house AI-enabled or AI-assisted segmentation platforms reported variable accuracy of head and neck spiral CT or cone beam CT segmentation, varying from 88.1% to 97.2% of craniomaxillofacial structures.8,13,15,16,19–21 AI segmentation of cone beam CT showed a pooled accuracy of AI segmentation of 92% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 91%–0.94%).22 The more recently published systematic view by Alahmari et al23 yielded a pooled DICE of 90.7% (95% CI: 86.7%–94.8%) of the upper skull and 94% (95% CI: 91%–98%) of the mandible. The mean DICE coefficients of AI-enabled segmentation of this study were comparable to our pilot study, where the median DICE coefficients were 92.4% for the upper skull and 94.9% for the mandible.9

This study focused on the clinical performance evaluation of a cloud-based AI-enabled segmentation algorithm. The advantages of the cloud-based platform include minimal requirements for computer hardware; data access on different devices; and, arguably, the ease of data transfer to other software for downstream VSP. The Mimics Innovation Suite (Materialise), which entails clinical threshold-based segmentation in the workflow, has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration. As of May 2025, the product of Mimics Enlighten AI, as a standalone AI-enabled segmentation tool, is Conformité Européenne–marked for research and clinical use in Europe. Based on public records, the AI segmentation tool has not yet received approval from the Food and Drug Administration.

It is important to note that the entire upper skull, including the cranium, was considered as 1 entity during the evaluation, which may lead to a slight overestimation of the segmentation accuracy by HD95, given the relatively large, uncomplicated area of the cranium in comparison to the small yet complex midface region. To compensate for this, AVD was reported, which considered all outliers and small segmentation differences, such as teeth and the anterior maxillary sinus wall.

In the clinical routine segmentation, the anterior wall of the maxillary sinus has always been a challenging region of interest. The thin anterior maxillary sinus wall often presents as hollow surfaces in threshold-based segmentation, which must either be fixed by slice-by-slice mapping of bone margins at the segmentation stage or by the semiautomatic fill-hole function of the VSP software at a later stage. Both approaches induce sources of errors that can cause ill-fitting patient-specific surgical plates in the maxilla. The observation that the AI-enabled segmentation model preserved an intact anterior maxillary sinus wall despite varied bone thickness and density is particularly beneficial in the workflow of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery involving the maxilla. Notably, the AI model performed impressively in the TMJ area compared with the clinical routine segmentation method, which is another key benefit underscoring its clinical application.

Interestingly, the AI-enabled segmentation of the mandible seemed to be more sensitive to the presence of occlusal contact, suggesting it may be wise to instruct patients to keep their teeth slightly apart during CT exposure if segmentation is the priority, as with the clinical routine segmentation method. Nonetheless, despite the adverse effect of occlusal contact on the segmentation of the mandible, AI overall outperformed the clinical routine method in the segmentation of the dentitions.

The results of this study showed that AI-enabled segmentation was more sensitive to CT scan resolution than clinical routine segmentation. Nevertheless, the clinical implications of the correlation between slice increment and AI segmentation outcomes must be inferred cautiously. It is acknowledged that some of the cases included in this study had a slice increment of 2.5 mm, which was higher than what is typically required of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery at 1 mm.24 Although a smaller slice increment is associated with a higher AI-enabled segmentation accuracy, CT exposure should abide by the “as low as reasonably achievable” protocol to minimize the radiation dose to the patient while satisfying the requirements for VSP.25

In terms of financial implications, the average cost for AI segmentation was approximately US $25 per case at the time of this study. Although quantitative comparison with the labor cost of the clinical routine method was not feasible in this study, a study on the cost-effectiveness of CT lymph node segmentation showed significant time and cost savings by AI segmentation compared with manual segmentation.26 Given the repetitive nature of the skull segmentation task and high case load, the long-term cost-effectiveness is expected to lean favorably toward AI segmentation.

The clinical application of AI, as an emerging and rapidly evolving technology, remains at an investigational stage. Admittedly, this study did not entail further downstream clinical application of AI-enabled segmentation in computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery. This study was also limited by the single-center design and the proprietary nature of the AI algorithm. Additionally, the CT scan selection was limited to adult tumor cases without prior head and neck operation; AI segmentation of postoperative CT scans after tumor resection and reconstruction constitutes another important part of the CAS workflow, which is beyond the scope of this study. Future research on AI segmentation in more diverse patient populations and evaluation of the impact on surgical outcomes is required to further explore the potential of learning AI in computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery and beyond. Meanwhile, socioeconomical considerations, including ethical considerations and regulatory convergence, must be addressed before large-scale clinical application of fully automatic AI segmentation of skull CT scans in the workflow of computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery.27

CONCLUSIONS

The results of this study serve as an independent external cohort to confirm the performance of this cloud-based AI segmentation model, which endorses future clinical application of AI-enabled segmentation in the VSP workflow for computer-assisted craniomaxillofacial surgery. AI-enabled segmentation yielded comparable accuracy to the clinical routine segmentation method in the upper skull and better overall results in the mandible, with better performance in clinically critical regions, including the anterior maxillary wall, TMJ, and delineation of upper and lower teeth. In general, smaller slice increments and pixel sizes were associated with increased accuracy of AI-enabled segmentation.
