Two-year outcome of 3D-printed acetabular cup for total hip arthroplasty in Japanese patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Tomonori Tetsunaga, Takeshi Miyasaka, Nobuhiko Sugano, Shigeru Mitani, Takashi Sakai, Kazuo Fujiwara, Daisuke Takahashi, Wataru Ando, Yoshitada Harada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThree-dimensional (3D)-printed acetabular cups may enhance initial fixation and osseointegration; however, early radiographic findings warrant careful monitoring. This study evaluated short-term radiographic outcomes of an electron beam melting (EBM)–fabricated 3D-printed titanium acetabular component (GS cup) used in primary total hip arthroplasty (THA).MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study included 236 consecutive primary THAs performed at seven hospitals. Postoperative radiographs at approximately 2 years were analyzed (mean follow-up, 24.6 months; range, 23–31 months). The primary endpoint was the presence of a radiolucent line (RLL) in DeLee and Charnley Zone 2 at 2 years. Secondary endpoints included initial polar gaps (≥ 1 mm), gap filling, RLLs in other zones, osseointegration assessed by Moore criteria, and survivorship with acetabular component revision for any reason as the endpoint.ResultsInitial polar gaps occurred in 19/236 hips (8.1%); 14/19 (73.7%) demonstrated gap filling at follow-up. RLLs were present in 48/236 hips (20.3%) (Zone 1, 13.6%; Zone 2, 9.7%; Zone 3, 11.4%). In exploratory univariate analyses, same-diameter reaming showed lower proportions of polar gaps and Zone 2 RLLs compared with 1-mm underreaming; however, no factors remained independently associated with Zone 2 RLLs after multivariable adjustment. At follow-up, 188 cups (79.7%) demonstrated ≥ 3 Moore signs, and no acetabular component revisions occurred by the standardized 2-year assessment.ConclusionsIn this multicenter retrospective cohort, the EBM-fabricated 3D-printed titanium acetabular component demonstrated generally favorable early radiographic findings and no acetabular component revisions at approximately 2 years. Given the retrospective design and the limited number of Zone 2 RLL events, the observed associations between surgical factors and radiographic findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. Longer-term follow-up, ideally with comparative designs and clinical outcomes, is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09708-x.

Background

Aseptic loosening is one of the leading causes of revision hip arthroplasty [1, 2]. To prevent aseptic loosening, it is necessary to improve the initial fixation and bone anchoring. A porous material with a high friction coefficient is necessary to obtain strong initial fixation [3] and to achieve bone ingrowth and biological fixation after cementless total hip arthroplasty (THA) [4]. Although titanium (Ti) mesh, plasma spraying, and metal foam have traditionally been used for porous processing, it is difficult to arbitrarily change the friction coefficient. Highly porous three-dimensional (3D) acetabular components have recently been developed to improve initial fixation, and good short- and medium-term outcomes have been reported [5, 6]. However, even with 3D porous acetabular cups, some implants require revision surgery in the early postoperative period due to aseptic loosening [7, 8]. Thus, the use of 3D porous acetabular cups must be monitored with caution.

3D-printing technology has facilitated significant advancements in the field of orthopedic surgery, including the application of 3D-printed implants for THA [9]. Bone models with the 3D-printing technique enable hip surgeons to plan the surgical procedure more precisely [10]. Custom-made 3D-printed implants tailored to the patient’s specific anatomy have been applied to offer stable initial fixation of large bone defects [11] and to restore the anatomical center of the hip joint in dysplastic hips [12]. Satisfactory midterm outcomes obtained with a 3D-printed titanium augment combined with a tantalum cup for the treatment of acetabular defects in revision THA were reported [5]. The 3D-printing technique enables optimization of the friction coefficient and free change in the porous structure, which mimics cancellous bone and promotes osseointegration. Electron beam melting (EBM) produces acetabular cups with a specific design and controlled 3D porous structures that are impossible to fabricate via conventional cutting techniques. However, it is still unknown what kinds of 3D porous acetabular cups have good radiographic and clinical outcomes. In this study, we aimed to investigate the radiographic outcome of 3D-printed cementless titanium acetabular cups manufactured by EBM.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This multicenter observational cohort study was approved by the institutional review board/ethics committee of the coordinating institution and by the appropriate ethics committees at each participating center (approval number: K1908-004). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committees/institutional review boards because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients’ background characteristics

This study was a retrospective review of 236 consecutive hips (194 females, 42 males) planned for THA using hemispherical, uncemented 3D-printed acetabular cups (GS cup, Teijin Nakashima Medical Co., Ltd., Okayama Japan) from April 2015 to March 2018 at seven hospitals in Japan. Patients who were suffering from either osteoarthritis of the hip, osteonecrosis of the femoral head, rheumatoid arthritis, subchondral insufficiency fracture, or femoral neck fracture were included. Exclusion criteria of this study was major deformity of the acetabulum due to pelvic fracture or surgery. The severity of developmental dysplasia of the hip was determined according to the Crowe’s classification [13]. We collected baseline demographic and anthropometric data (including age, sex, height, weight, and BMI) from medical records. Comorbidities and socioeconomic status/employment were not systematically recorded across participating centers during the study period and were not available for analysis.

Cup design

The GS cup (Fig. 1) was manufactured with the EBM process (GE Additive, West Chester, UK). The GS cup is an EBM-fabricated implant consisting of a cup component and a porous component integrated in a single monolithic structure, with the porous component designed as a 3D geodesic structure (GS). The porous component of the GS cup consists of two layers, which are superposed with a half-cycle phase shift between layers, in a tightly correlated configuration to promote bone ingrowth. The porous component at the outer surface is designed to include microspikes (height, 250 μm) at intersection regions. Optimizing the porous component of the GS cup based on various mechanical tests yielded the following optimal design parameters: degree of porosity, 38%; equivalent circle diameter, 650 μm. The liners used with the GS cup were made from a vitamin-E-blended crosslinked polyethylene material (BLEND-E®XL; Teijin Nakashima Medical Co., Ltd.). The cup/liner locking mechanism is a snap-in structure that does not use metal rings or similar components. This facilitates verification of the junction between the cup and liner and reduces the risk of damage to the liner.

Surgical procedures

A total of 32 surgeons across seven institutions contributed to the procedures in this cohort, and each surgeon had performed more than 100 primary THAs. All surgeons received standardized pre-study training on the implant system, including the manufacturer’s surgical technique and intraoperative instrumentation, prior to using the system in this study. The surgical approaches were performed by senior orthopedic surgeons according to their preference (Table 1). The acetabulum was sequentially reamed using dedicated hemispherical reamers until blood oozed evenly from the bone surface. The final reaming was either 1-mm-undersized reaming or same-diameter reaming, depending on the surgeon’s preference. Same-diameter reaming was defined as reaming with the indicated size. The GS cup has an effective outer diameter 0.9 mm larger than its labeled size due to the porous titanium layer; therefore, the manufacturer recommends final reaming line-to-line to the labeled diameter (same-diameter reaming). In this study, the final reaming (same-diameter or 1-mm under-reaming) was selected by the operating surgeon based on intraoperative bone quality and press-fit. In this cohort, femoral head materials comprised metal, ceramic, and Oxinium heads. The choice of head material was determined by the operating surgeon based on clinical judgment and patient characteristics; all hips used a polyethylene liner. The acetabular cups were fixed by press-fit technology with additional screw fixation, if necessary. In this study, an “ischial screw” was defined as at least one supplementary acetabular screw directed into the ischium (posterior–inferior quadrant) on postoperative radiographs (Fig. 2). The posterior–inferior quadrant has historically been described as a caution zone because of nearby neurovascular structures [14]; therefore, we evaluated the presence of an ischial screw as a separate variable from the total number of screws. Structural bone grafting was performed to fill the gap between the cup and the host bone when the cup center edge angle was 0 degrees or less. Depending on the case, bone grafting with reaming bone was added to the bone deficiency in acetabular dysplasia. Cement or cementless stems were effectively used, depending on the shape and bone quality of the femur. Cementless stems were used in 222 patients (PrimeN stem, GS Taper stem, Teijin Nakashima Medical Co., Ltd.), and cement stems were used in 14 patients (BLIAN, Teijin Nakashima Medical Co., Ltd.). All patients began full weight-bearing exercises the day after surgery.

Radiographic assessment

Radiographs after the surgery and at the time of the final examination were evaluated by five orthopedic surgeons (TT, TM, SM, DT, and WA). Each observer performed one-time radiographic measurement. The horizontal position of the acetabular cup, defined as the horizontal distance from the medial corner of the acetabular cup to Kohler’s line, was measured on the postoperative radiographs. We examined the presence or absence of an initial gap (≥ 1 mm) at the time of surgery and of radiolucent lines at 2 years. The radiolucent line at 2 years in patients without an initial gap was considered to indicate bone resorption. Gap filling at 2 years in patients with an initial gap was also considered. Cup osseointegration was assessed according to the criteria of Moore et al. [15], which includes the absence of radiolucent lines, the presence of superolateral buttresses, medial stress shielding, radial trabeculae, and inferomedial buttress. The three zones of DeLee and Charnley [16] were inspected for bone reactions around the cup. Definite cup loosening was defined as acetabular migration of ≥ 2 mm with implant rotation or screw breakage [5]. Probable cup loosening was defined as a radiolucent line > 1 mm through all three acetabular zones without any signs of migration, rotation, or screw breakage [5].

In addition to the standardized radiographic assessment at 2 years postoperatively, we assessed longer-term survivorship based on revision surgery records up to the latest available follow-up at each participating center. Revision events (including revision for aseptic loosening and periprosthetic joint infection) were identified from institutional surgical databases and medical records. Because follow-up beyond 2 years was not uniform across centers and complete ascertainment was not available for all patients, longer-term survivorship was summarized descriptively.

Study size

This was a retrospective multicenter cohort study; therefore, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The study size was determined by the number of consecutive eligible primary THAs performed during the study period across the seven participating institutions, according to prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Because the number of Zone 2 radiolucent line events was limited, analyses of factors associated with the primary outcome were considered exploratory.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the presence of a RLL in DeLee and Charnley Zone 2 on standardized radiographs obtained at 2 years postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included (1) the presence of an initial polar gap (≥ 1 mm) and gap filling at follow-up (2), RLLs in Zones 1 and 3 (3), osseointegration assessed by the Moore criteria (4), postoperative hip pain at the 2-year follow-up visit, and (5) revision/survivorship outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were expressed as the means and range and compared by t test if they were normally distributed. Comparisons of categorical variables used the appropriate chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate analyses were performed for the factors involved in the polar gap and radiolucent lines at 2 years after surgery. In addition, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed with the presence of a Zone 2 RLL at 2 years as the dependent variable; age, BMI, Crowe classification, reaming strategy (1-mm underreaming vs. same-diameter reaming), and ischial screw use (yes/no) were entered as covariates, and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Crowe II and III were combined due to sparse data. Screw use (0 vs. ≥ 1) was not included because no Zone 2 RLL events occurred in the screw-free group, which may cause separation. The acetabular component survival rate was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. The endpoint was acetabular component revision for any reason, defined as removal or exchange of the acetabular cup and/or liner. Revisions in which only the femoral component was revised with the acetabular component retained were not counted as events and were censored at the time of reoperation. Interobserver reliability for the two key binary radiographic variables (IG and Zone 2 RLL at 2 years) was assessed using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement for the mean of k raters (ICC [2,k]); radiographic evaluations were independently performed by five observers (k = 5), and 95% CIs were reported. All differences were considered significant at a P value < 0.05.

At the standardized 2-year radiographic assessment, the final radiographs were obtained at a mean of 24.6 months postoperatively (range, 23–31 months). Radiolucent lines at any area within 2 mm appeared in 48 hips (20.3%). These radiolucent lines were distributed in DeLee and Charnley Zone 1 in 32 hips (13.6%), Zone 2 in 23 hips (9.7%), and Zone 3 in 27 hips (11.4%). Of the 19 patients with initial gaps, 14 (73.7%) had gap filling at the final survey, and 5 (26.7%) had radiolucent lines in DeLee and Charnley Zone 2. Of the 217 hips without an initial gap, 18 (8.3%) showed a radiolucent line in DeLee and Charnley Zone 2 (bone resorption) at the final survey. Because the absolute number of Zone 2 radiolucent line events was limited (n = 23), comparisons of potential associated factors were considered exploratory and are presented descriptively in Table 4; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. At the 2-year radiographic evaluation (mean, 24.6 months; range, 23–31 months), no definite loosening of the acetabular cup was observed. Probable cup loosening was observed in 10 hips (4.2%). At this 2-year assessment, 188 cups (79.7%) showed excellent osseointegration according to the Moore criteria. One patient underwent revision surgery due to aseptic loosening of the femoral stem alone, with the acetabular component retained. There was one case (0.4%) of postoperative hip pain reported at the 2-year follow-up visit. The Kaplan–Meier cumulative survivorship with acetabular component revision as the endpoint was 100% at 2 years (Fig. 3).

Beyond the standardized 2-year radiographic evaluation, longer-term revision events were assessed using the latest available clinical records; during this additional follow-up, revision surgery was performed in four hips (three for aseptic loosening and one for periprosthetic joint infection). Because complete long-term follow-up was not available for all patients across centers, these revision numbers may underestimate the true event rate.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for age, BMI, Crowe classification, reaming strategy, and ischial screw use, none of the prespecified covariates showed a statistically significant independent association with Zone 2 RLLs at 2 years, and confidence intervals were wide (Supplemental Table 1).

Interobserver reliability was high for both key binary radiographic variables: the ICC for the presence of an initial gap was 0.93 (95% CI, 0.90–0.95), and the ICC for the presence of a Zone 2 RLL at 2 years was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.87–0.94).

Discussion

The 3D-printing technique enables porous structures and optimized surface textures that may enhance initial stability and osseointegration of modern uncemented acetabular components. In this multicenter retrospective cohort, the GS cup demonstrated generally favorable short-term radiographic outcomes at approximately 2 years, including no definite acetabular loosening and a high proportion of cups meeting the Moore criteria for osseointegration, although radiolucent lines were observed in 20.3% of hips. Because the number of Zone 2 radiolucent-line events was limited, analyses exploring associations with surgical factors (e.g., reaming strategy and screw-related variables) are presented as hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as demonstrating factors that “prevent” radiolucent lines. The clinical role of supplementary screws in cementless acetabular fixation remains debated: meta-analyses have reported no clear differences in key outcomes between cups implanted with versus without screws, while emphasizing that the certainty of evidence is limited and that screwless fixation should be applied prudently [17, 18]. Accordingly, we interpret the present findings primarily in terms of the principal radiographic and survivorship outcomes, and we consider exploratory associations in the context of the retrospective design and potential confounding due to intraoperative decision-making.

In clinical settings, achieving initial stability of a cementless acetabular cup is based on rim press-fit and, when necessary, supplementary screw fixation. When porous surfaces have a high friction coefficient, seating the cup may require higher impaction forces; therefore, the implant surface should balance strong initial fixation with intraoperative maneuverability. Shirazi-Adl et al. reported that optimal resistance to friction can be achieved by controlling the surface textures of porous structures [4], and additive manufacturing provides design freedom that conventional techniques cannot offer [19].

The GS cup was developed as a 3D porous cup manufactured by EBM, and the porous outer surface incorporates microspikes to enhance initial fixation. With respect to early postoperative radiographic findings, an initial polar gap is often not clinically problematic when secure peripheral rim fit and initial stability are achieved, and the gap may progressively fill during follow-up [20]. Similarly, screwless press-fit fixation has been reported to maintain stability without measurable migration in selected cases [21]; however, in retrospective cohorts, screw use is commonly determined intraoperatively (e.g., bone quality and perceived press-fit), and comparisons may therefore be influenced by confounding by indication. For this implant, the actual cup size is 0.9 mm larger than the labeled size due to the 3D porous thickness; consequently, polar gaps may occur, and undersized reaming should be performed carefully. In patients with good bone quality, same-diameter reaming may be preferable.

Tantalum is an ideal implant material with excellent histocompatibility and an elastic modulus similar to that of subchondral bone, which is conducive to the adhesion and growth of osteoblasts and promotes long-term osteogenesis [22, 23]. The extent of filling of the pores of the tantalum material with new bone was reported to reach approximately 80%, with evidence of Haversian remodeling within the pores at 1 year after surgery [22]. The porous tantalum monobloc cup in primary THA has demonstrated excellent mid-term clinical and radiographic outcomes with no failures because of osteolysis or loosening [24]. Similarly, satisfactory short-term outcomes of 3D porous titanium cups in primary THA have also been reported [25].

Cementless cups typically implement one of two common strategies for increasing surface roughness: (1) joining a porous metal to the cup or (2) thermally spraying molten metal onto the cup. However, these approaches may not fully eliminate the risk of separation or detachment at the porous–substrate interface. In contrast, the EBM approach yields a monolithic structure without an interface between the cup and porous component, which may improve implant safety. The mid-term outcomes of EBM-produced porous-coated titanium cups have shown good clinical function, excellent survivorship, and adequate biological fixation [6]. Encouraging short- to mid-term outcomes have also been reported for off-the-shelf 3D-printed trabecular titanium acetabular cups manufactured using EBM in revision THA [26]. Perticarini et al. reported that 99.3% of EBM-produced trabecular titanium cups were radiographically stable at a minimum follow-up of 5 years, without radiolucent lines, sclerotic areas, or periprosthetic osteolysis [27]. Conversely, Miyagawa et al. reported that radiolucent lines frequently appeared in association with micromotion despite excellent mid-term clinical outcomes using an EBM-produced 3D porous titanium cup, and Imai et al. reported radiolucent lines accompanied by sclerotic lines at 1 year after THA [28, 29].

In the present study, radiolucent lines were observed in Zone 2 in 9.7% of hips and in Zone 3 in 11.5% of hips, which was less frequent than that reported by Imai et al. and comparable to the findings reported by Miyagawa et al. [28, 29]. Radiolucent lines are considered to reflect fibrous fixation at the bone–implant interface and may occur when micromotion is excessive (e.g., ≥ 150 μm) [30]. In our cohort, approximately 80% of patients had secondary osteoarthritis due to developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). In such cases, screw fixation can influence acetabular component alignment [31]. Therefore, any interpretation regarding screw trajectory (including ischial screw placement) should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and should be interpreted cautiously in the context of intraoperative decision-making and potential confounding by indication.

Carli et al. reported that despite adequate implant survivorship, over one-third of Tritanium primary cups (72% porosity, pore size: 546 μm) had 2 or more zone radiolucency after a minimum 5-year follow-up period with associated lower Harris hip scores [7]. Radiolucency around the cup was significantly higher for the highly porous titanium cup than for the hydroxyapatite (HA)-coated porous titanium cup. The HA-coated porous titanium cup may be more advantageous than the 3D porous titanium cup for the acquisition of bone ingrowth in the early postoperative period. In this study, 2-year radiographs revealed radiolucent lines in 2 or more DeLee zones in 10.6% of cups, including those in which 3 all zones were involved, which was a total of 4.3%. The current study and the report by Carli et al. cannot be simply compared due to the different follow-up periods. Although no acetabular component revisions were observed by the standardized 2-year radiographic assessment, longer-term revision events were identified in the available clinical records (three revisions for aseptic loosening and one for periprosthetic joint infection). Because follow-up beyond 2 years was not uniform across centers and complete ascertainment was not available for all patients, these data should be interpreted cautiously and may underestimate the true event rate. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the importance of continued surveillance to confirm mid- to long-term durability of this implant.

This study had some limitations. First, because the purpose of this study was to examine the presence or absence of radiolucent lines in the early postoperative period, medium- and long-term outcomes need to be considered. Second, comorbidities and socioeconomic status/employment were not systematically available in this retrospective multicenter dataset. Therefore, residual (unmeasured) confounding may have influenced the observed associations. Third, a serious limitation of this study is that this study conducted only radiographic evaluations without clinical evaluations using patient-reported outcomes. Even if there is an initial gap or a radiolucent line on the radiographs, it is rarely a problem if there are no symptoms. Follow-up on radiographs is necessary, but clinical outcomes must also be followed over the medium to long term. The fourth limitation is selection bias. The patient selection process may influence the results due to selection bias. The patients included in this retrospective study were selected in accordance with surgeon’s preference based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Selecting patients with biased characteristics may lead to overestimation of whether those characteristics influence the outcome [32]. However, we believe that the patient background of this study is not particularly biased in terms of age, gender, or radiographic characteristics compared to other studies. Finally, because the absolute number of Zone 2 radiolucent line events was limited (n = 23), comparisons of potential associated factors were considered exploratory and are presented descriptively in Table 4; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. At the 2-year radiographic evaluation (mean, 24.6 months; range, 23–31 months), no definite loosening of the acetabular cup was observed. Probable cup loosening was observed in 10 hips (4.2%).

The short-term outcomes of cementless titanium acetabular cups using 3D-printing technology were within a satisfactory range, and we consider that the GS cup has excellent mechanical properties, including a high friction coefficient. However, there was a possibility that sufficient osseointegration was not achieved in some patients. The clinical advantages of HA coating over more conventional implant surfaces are evident in primary and revision hip arthroplasties [33]. Therefore, in developing a new acetabular component, it might be desirable to apply a coating, such as HA, in addition to 3D printing technology.

Conclusions

In this multicenter retrospective cohort, the 3D-printed titanium acetabular cup showed generally favorable radiographic outcomes at approximately 2 years. Given the retrospective design, limited events, and potential confounding, exploratory associations should be interpreted cautiously, and longer-term, preferably comparative studies are needed.
