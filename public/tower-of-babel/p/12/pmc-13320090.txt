Clonal Metamorphosis: Deconstructing MPN Evolution with Single-Cell and Spatial Multi-Omics
Authors: Muhammad Shahid Iqbal, Abdullah K Alahmari, Mohd Faiyaz Khan, Sadaf Farooqui, Muhammad Zahid Iqbal, Salah-Ud-Din Khan, Nazia Khan, Vidya Devanathadesikan Seshadri, Amr Ali Mohamed Abdelgawwad El-Sehrawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) present a fundamental paradox: despite sharing a small set of canonical driver mutations in JAK2, CALR, or MPL, patients exhibit striking heterogeneity in disease latency, clinical presentation, and evolutionary trajectories to myelofibrosis or secondary acute myeloid leukemia. This review synthesizes recent advances in single-cell and spatial multi-omic technologies that are resolving this paradox by moving analysis from bulk averages to individual cells and their microenvironmental ecosystems. We examine how targeted single-cell DNA sequencing reconstructs clonal architectures and phylogenies, revealing that driver mutations arise within complex mosaics where mutation order, co-mutation context, and cellular ancestry determine phenotypic outcomes. Integrated single-cell transcriptomic and epigenomic profiling exposes within-clone heterogeneity, lineage biases, and functional states that explain variable penetrance and therapy responses. Spatial transcriptomics, especially when integrated with single-cell transcriptomics, histopathology, and multiplex proteomics, further demonstrates that malignant hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells actively remodel bone marrow niches, creating localized inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironments that select for aggressive subclones. Together, these approaches support a new ecological model of MPN pathogenesis in which early epigenetic hits create permissive stem cell reservoirs, clonal competition and cooperation shape disease progression, and non-cell-autonomous niche and immune signals drive malignant metamorphosis. We discuss how this framework refines prognostication, informs rational combination therapies targeting both malignant cells and their ecosystem, and enables real-time monitoring of clonal dynamics, ultimately charting a course from descriptive atlases to actionable clinical strategies.Keywords: Myeloproliferative neoplasms, Single-cell sequencing, Spatial transcriptomics, Clonal evolution, Bone marrow microenvironment

Introduction: The paradox of a simple genetic basis

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), principally polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, and primary myelofibrosis, are clonal disorders of hematopoietic stem cells characterized clinically by excess production of one or more mature myeloid lineages and by risks of thrombosis, marrow failure, and transformation to acute leukemia [1]. Historically, the field celebrated a deceptively tidy genetic explanation: recurrent, mutually exclusive driver lesions in JAK2, CALR, or MPL that activate JAK–STAT signalling and provide an elegant mechanistic link to cytokine hypersensitivity and proliferative phenotypes [2–4]. Yet this tidy picture is immediately complicated by a striking clinical truth: patients who share the same canonical driver mutation can present decades apart, with divergent blood counts, tissue pathology, symptom burden, and long term outcomes [5].

Classical models of MPN evolution adopt a multi-step genetic narrative in which a founder driver appears in a long term hematopoietic stem cell and subsequent hits in epigenetic regulators, splicing factors, or tumour suppressors (for example TET2, ASXL1, TP53) accumulate to produce advanced disease or leukemic transformation [6, 7]. This model is attractive because it maps naturally onto well known examples of clonal progression and on the association between particular secondary mutations and adverse prognosis [8]. Nonetheless the model leaves several central observations unresolved. First, the long latency that frequently separates driver acquisition and clinical disease implies that mutation alone is insufficient to set the evolutionary clock [9]. Second, penetrance is highly variable: many individuals harbour low burden JAK2 or other driver clones without ever developing fulsome MPN, while others progress rapidly [10, 11]. Third, the order and cellular context of additional mutations materially alter phenotype and therapeutic response, a nuance not captured by a purely linear steps model [9]. Collectively these mismatches constitute a genetic paradox: a small set of recurrent drivers explain diagnosis but not the broad distribution of disease behaviour or the stochasticity of progression.

The revolution of single-cell and spatial multi-omic technologies provides a new lens capable of resolving this paradox by moving the unit of analysis away from bulk averages and toward individual cells and their local ecosystems. Single-cell genotyping coupled to transcriptome and surface phenotype has revealed that driver mutations arise in complex clonal mosaics with frequent coexisting wild-type stem cells, lineage biases established at early progenitor stages, and mutation-specific transcriptional programs that are masked in bulk assays [12, 13]. Spatially resolved approaches further expose how malignant HSPCs interact with a remodeled marrow niche and inflammatory circuits that amplify fibrosis and selection for aggressive subclones [14]. Importantly, single-cell studies have shown that mutation order, clonal co-existence, microenvironmental inflammation, and structural genomic events combine to determine which clones expand, which remain quiescent, and which acquire leukemogenic properties [9, 11].

Given these convergent advances, the aim of this review is to synthesize the most recent single-cell and spatial multi-omic studies and to use them as the basis for a new, holistic model of MPN pathogenesis and evolution. We will critically examine how single-cell resolved clonal architecture, lineage skewing, niche remodelling, and inflammatory signalling together explain latency, variable penetrance, and the apparently unpredictable leaps to myelofibrosis or secondary acute myeloid leukaemia. By centring individual cellular behaviours and ecological interactions, we argue that a mechanistic framework emerges in which a simple genetic basis produces complex and contingent disease trajectories [5, 11]. Importantly, this review builds on prior MPN single-cell syntheses, including O’Sullivan et al., who summarized scDNA- and scRNA-based advances in MPNs and explicitly identified spatial transcriptomics as an emerging direction, but extends that framework by placing spatial multi-omics at the center of clonal evolution and niche remodelling [15, 16].

The technological toolkit for dissecting MPN clonal architecture

Genotyping the unseen: Single-cell DNA sequencing (scDNA-seq)

Targeted single-cell DNA sequencing has transformed how we define clonal architecture by placing somatic genotypes into the cellular units that actually execute hematopoiesis [17]. This advancement was built upon earlier, lower-throughput techniques such as whole-genome amplification (WGA)-based single-cell genotyping, which first enabled the interrogation of somatic mutations in individually isolated hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. In MPNs, Guo et al. used WGA-enabled targeted sequencing to show that megakaryocytes can acquire additional somatic mutations associated with marrow fibrosis, while Parenti et al. applied single-cell WGA to CD34-positive cells and reconstructed clonal hierarchy in primary myelofibrosis before AML transformation [18, 19]. These pioneering approaches provided critical early insights into clonal architecture and mutation order in myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) and other myeloid malignancies. Modern methods like TARGET-seq were explicitly developed to build on this foundation by pairing high-sensitivity mutation detection with parallel single-cell transcriptomes and surface phenotyping, thereby reducing allelic dropout and enabling confident assignation of hotspot mutations to individual HSCs and progenitors [17]. Droplet microfluidic platforms and commercial solutions have further scaled this approach to tens of thousands of cells so that rare subclones and co-occurrence patterns become observable in clinical samples [20]. For example, a custom panel run on the Mission Bio Tapestri platform resolved biclonal JAK2 and CALR or JAK2 and MPL driver architectures within individual patients, proving that canonical drivers can exist in independent clones and therefore challenging assumptions about single-clone dominance [20, 21]. Single-cell DNA data have also exposed long-lived DNMT3A mutant clones that predate clinical transformation by years, implying that the mere presence of an early epigenetic hit is insufficient to predict phenotype without cellular context [22]. Beyond presence or absence, single-cell genotyping reveals zygosity, subclonal copy number alterations, and phylogenetic branching that together determine which subclones carry transformation-associated lesions such as TP53 or RAS pathway hits [20, 21]. These capabilities make scDNA-seq indispensable for defining clonal topology, mutation order, zygosity, and rare subclone structure. In MPN, this approach has shown that canonical drivers can occur in independent clones rather than a single linear hierarchy, as demonstrated by TARGET-seq and single-cell DNA profiling studies [17, 20, 21, 23]. Key single-cell and spatial multi-omic technologies used in MPN studies

The functional phenotype: Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Genotype is necessary but not sufficient; scRNA-seq provides the functional readout that links mutations to lineage bias, stress responses, and differentiation blocks [12, 17]. High-resolution transcriptional atlases of MPNs have shown that JAK2 and CALR driver clones imprint distinct expression programs across HSCs and early progenitors, with JAK2 mutant cells demonstrating erythroid and megakaryocyte skewing and CALR clones often producing alternative cytokine signalling signatures [12, 31]. Single-cell transcriptomes also reveal disease-specific aberrant progenitor populations that are not readily recognized in bulk assays, such as pre-leukemic HSCs and megakaryocyte-biased progenitors that expand in myelofibrosis and express fibrosis-associated secretomes [12]. Importantly, integrated single-cell genotype–phenotype studies demonstrate that the same canonical driver can yield different transcriptional consequences depending on co-mutational context and cellular identity, explaining phenotype heterogeneity across patients who carry identical driver lesions [17, 20]. Finally, scRNA-seq enables trajectory and pseudotime analyses that map how mutant clones reshape differentiation states over time and under therapeutic pressure. In myelofibrosis, these analyses reveal megakaryocyte-biased hematopoiesis and disease-specific progenitor programs that are best presented here as functional readouts of clonal state rather than as the main biological thesis of the review [12, 16, 24, 32].

The regulatory layer: Single-cell Epigenomics (scATAC-seq, scMethylation)

Chromatin accessibility and methylation profiling at single-cell resolution reveal how mutations reconfigure the regulatory substrate on which transcriptional programs are executed [25, 32]. In addition, 10x Genomics’ Epi Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression assay profiles chromatin accessibility and 3’ gene expression from the same nucleus, providing a direct regulatory-transcriptional readout. In myeloproliferative neoplasms, Calabresi et al. analyzed paired chronic-phase and blast-phase samples from 10 patients to map clonal trajectories and copy-number variants, illustrating how single-cell approaches can resolve leukemic evolution beyond bulk profiling [33–35]. Integrated approaches that co-map genotype to scATAC profiles show that JAK2 V617F, for instance, produces cell-type specific shifts in transcription factor motif accessibility and enhancer co-accessibility that promote erythroid and megakaryocytic programs in mutated progenitors [32]. Likewise, DNMT3A and TET2 mutant clones carry distinct enhancer dysregulation and DNA methylation footprints that bias lineage output and inflammatory responsiveness, providing a mechanistic explanation for why identical driver mutations can yield divergent clinical phenotypes [25, 36]. New multimodal assays that capture chromatin, methylation, and genotype in the same nucleus allow direct inference of causality: epigenomic rewiring frequently precedes, and in some cases predicts, transcriptional shifts that enable clonal expansion [37, 38]. Thus, epigenomics is best presented here as the regulatory layer that complements genotype and transcriptome, revealing how lineage-specific chromatin states and DNA methylation help interpret the same driver mutation differently across cell types. In MPN, this is now directly measurable with genotype-linked chromatin accessibility assays such as GoT-ChA and related single-cell epigenomic platforms [25–27, 32, 39].

The spatial context, a new frontier: Spatial transcriptomics and proteomics

Dissociation destroys microanatomical relationships that are essential for niche-driven selection, so spatially resolved methods reconnect genotype and phenotype to real tissue neighborhoods. In practice, the highest value comes from integrating ST with scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, histopathology, and multiplex protein imaging to annotate cell identities, infer ligand-receptor interactions, and localize clone-specific inflammatory or fibrotic niches within intact marrow architecture [15, 16, 28, 40]. Pioneering work combining single-cell and spatial transcriptomics mapped bone marrow niches and identified peri-vascular and endosteal microenvironments that supply cytokines and chemokines relevant to HSC maintenance and clonal selection [40]. Commercial spatial platforms such as the Visium family and in-situ imaging products including 10x Genomics Xenium have matured to provide transcriptome- or targeted gene-level maps at near single-cell resolution, while highly multiplexed protein imaging methods such as CODEX resolve cell phenotypes and their adjacencies in intact marrow architecture [28, 30, 41]. Applied to MPN, these tools expose how mutant HSCs and megakaryocytes remodel stromal niches, attract pro-fibrotic macrophages, and create localized inflammatory milieus that select for specific subclones; such spatial ecology is likely a major determinant of latency and sudden fibrotic progression [14, 42]. In summary, these platforms provide complementary readouts of genotype, transcriptional state, regulatory potential, tissue context, and protein phenotype. Together, they form the measurement layer that Sect. 3 uses to build the biological model of clonal evolution.

The proteomic layer: Single-cell proteomic approaches

Single-cell proteomic approaches add a protein-level layer that captures cell-state information missed by nucleic-acid assays alone. CITE-seq uses oligonucleotide-labeled antibodies to measure surface epitopes and transcriptomes in the same cell, providing a scalable readout of immunophenotype together with gene expression. In hematologic malignancies, Tapestri-based proteogenomic workflows such as DAb-seq combine targeted single-cell DNA sequencing with antibody-oligonucleotide protein readouts, directly linking clonal genotype to immunophenotype and revealing genotype–phenotype decoupling in leukemia. Together, these methods strengthen clonal mapping by showing which genetic clones are also functionally distinct at the protein level [20, 29]. Taken together, scDNA-seq defines the clone, scRNA-seq defines its functional state, scATAC/scMethylation define its regulatory wiring, and spatial/proteomic assays place that biology inside the marrow niche. The biological interpretation of how these layers jointly produce clonal metamorphosis is developed in Sect. 3, after the technologies themselves have been presented in a strict method-first order. Reconstructing MPN evolution across time, lineage, and tissue context: From a single cell to a malignant ecosystem

Here, we use phylogeography to mean the mapping of clonal genealogies onto anatomical and microenvironmental space. In practice, this framework integrates mutation order, lineage ancestry, and marrow niche context.

The origin story: The pre-leukemic HSC and the first hit

A large body of genetic and single-cell evidence supports a model in which the earliest recurrent lesions in myeloid malignancy pathogenesis are epigenetic governor mutations such as DNMT3A or TET2 that arise in long-lived hematopoietic stem cells and create a persistent, pre-leukemic reservoir on which later driver events act [43–45]. This concept is formally recognized as clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). CHIP is typically defined by the presence of a cancer-associated somatic mutation at a variant allele fraction of ≥ 2% in blood, in an individual without evidence of a hematologic malignancy [44, 46]. CHIP, most commonly driven by DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 mutations, represents a precursor state that confers an increased risk for developing overt myeloid neoplasms, including MPNs [44, 47]. Population sequencing studies first established that DNMT3A and TET2 clones, consistent with CHIP, are common in aging individuals and are detectable years prior to clinical disease, establishing the epidemiologic substrate for the first-hit model [44, 48]. Functional and lineage tracing experiments confirm that DNMT3A loss or mutation confers stem cell fitness advantages that permit clonal expansion while preserving multipotency, consistent with the notion of a permissive HSC state [49]. Single-cell lineage and genotyping approaches have then provided decisive resolution by showing that these epigenetic-mutant HSCs frequently precede acquisition of canonical MPN drivers and occupy distinct branches of the clonal phylogeny [50, 51]. Importantly, this section emphasizes not only mutation order but also the cell state and marrow niche in which later drivers are acquired and selected [50, 51]. Critically, the single-cell phylogenies reveal that the timing and cellular context of a subsequent JAK2, CALR, or MPL mutation determine whether the clone remains indolent or gains proliferative dominance; this is best understood as a lineage-plus-context model in which ancestry, timing, and tissue environment jointly shape phenotype [9, 52].

Clonal competition and cooperation: A darwinian struggle in the marrow

Clonal competition in the marrow is empirically measurable with single-cell genotyping. Longitudinal single-cell studies of patients receiving targeted therapies show that treatment imposes selection pressures that can suppress some subclones while enabling the expansion of others, thereby reshaping the clonal landscape in ways not apparent from bulk variant allele frequencies alone [53, 54]. Examples include the consistent molecular responses to pegylated interferon in which JAK2 allele burdens fall substantially in many patients, an effect that can be resolved at single-cell level into contraction of the JAK2-carrying HSC compartment in responders [55]. By contrast, longitudinal single-cell analyses during JAK inhibitor therapy reveal increased clonal complexity in some patients and the selection of subclones with co-occurring high-risk lesions, indicating that suppression of one proliferative axis may unmask or select for alternative fitness programs [54, 56]. Beyond competition, emerging spatial and single-cell data raise a complementary possibility that distinct subclones can cooperate through niche modification or paracrine signalling, such that one genetically defined population alters the tissue ecology in a way that secondarily benefits a different clone. Support for cooperativity is presently indirect but biologically plausible: single-cell profiling of myelofibrosis shows that mutant megakaryocyte progenitors secrete pro-fibrotic and inflammatory mediators that remodel stroma and immune composition, and spatial maps localize these changes to discrete niches where neighbouring HSCs and progenitors reside, establishing an architecture where ecological engineering by one clone could increase the fitness of others [12, 40].

The leopard’s spots: Genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity within a single patient

Single-cell multi-omic datasets make it clear that the genotype label alone is insufficient. Cells that share an identical driver mutation often display heterogeneous transcriptional programs, surface proteomes, and functional behaviours. These within-clone phenotypic mosaics extend the argument beyond mutation order by showing that identical genotypes can occupy distinct functional niches and transcriptional states within the same patient [12, 51]. Techniques that couple mutation detection to transcriptomes and surface proteins, such as TARGET-seq and CITE-seq, have revealed within-clone diversity in signaling states, adhesion molecules, and cytokine response genes that correlate with distinct lineage biases or drug sensitivities [17, 51, 57]. CITE-seq uses oligonucleotide-labeled antibodies to integrate protein and transcriptome measurements, whereas DAb-seq directly profiles DNA genotype and surface protein phenotype in the same single cell [20, 29]. A striking example from myelofibrosis studies is the identification of heterogeneous expression of the platelet-associated receptor G6B across JAK2-mutant progenitors, where a subpopulation bearing a specific surface signature preferentially exhibits megakaryocyte differentiation programs and profibrotic secretomes [12]. These within-clone phenotypic mosaics provide a mechanistic explanation for variable clinical features such as thrombocytosis or erythrocytosis in patients with the same driver, and they identify surface markers that can be exploited for targeted depletion of pathogenic subpopulations [12].

The evolutionary trajectory to myelofibrosis and sAML

The transition to myelofibrosis or secondary acute myeloid leukemia follows convergent but heterogeneous evolutionary paths that can be parsed into genetic and phenotypic routes [11]. Genetically, single-cell DNA sequencing identifies the aggressive subclones that acquire additional high-risk lesions such as TP53 multihit alterations, IDH1/2 mutations, and RAS-pathway activating mutations; these events frequently coincide with copy-number alterations and complex/monosomal karyotypes, including del(20q), del(17p)/TP53 loss, and del(12p), which accumulate during progression from myelofibrosis to sAML and mark rapid clonal expansion at transformation [11, 58–61]. Phenotypically, single-cell transcriptomes of transforming clones demonstrate loss of normal differentiation trajectories, emergence of leukemia stem cell transcriptional programs, and reactivation of stemness and stress response circuits that underpin chemoresistance and fitness in hostile, inflamed microenvironments [11, 62]. A further layer is metabolic reprogramming. Single-cell and metabolic studies in AML show that LSCs and CD34 + CD38low/- cells are metabolically plastic, often relying on OXPHOS with elevated mitochondrial fitness and antioxidant buffering, yet also occupying mixed oxidative-glycolytic states associated with poor outcome. Spatial metabolomics and single-cell metabolic readouts could therefore be added to MPN-to-sAML studies to determine whether hypoxic, fibrotic niches favor an OXPHOS-leaning stem-like state or a hybrid program that supports quiescence, survival, and relapse [63–66]. Importantly, temporally resolved single-cell multi-omic studies show that epigenomic rewiring and niche-mediated inflammation often precede or accompany these genetic hits, suggesting that phenotypic destabilization of the hematopoietic hierarchy is both a driver and a permissive context for malignant escalation [11, 40]. Together, these observations argue for a reconceptualization of MPN evolution as a multiscale process in which early epigenetic hits create a permissive HSC field, therapy and microenvironmental forces select among competing clones, within-clone phenotypic heterogeneity supplies the raw material for adaptation, and both genetic and non-genetic changes collaborate to produce fulminant myelofibrosis or leukemic transformation [11, 44, 54]. The non-cell-autonomous drivers: The niche and the immune system

The malignant niche: How MPN cells remodel their own home

Single-cell and spatial studies have converged to show that MPN is not solely a hematopoietic stem cell disorder but a disease of a remodeled bone marrow ecosystem in which malignant hematopoietic clones actively reprogram stromal, mesenchymal, and vascular elements to their own advantage [40]. Spatial transcriptomics combined with single-cell atlases mapped discrete peri-vascular and endosteal niches and demonstrated that disease-specific megakaryocyte progenitors and inflammatory monocytes localize to, and transcriptionally remodel, these microenvironments [12, 40]. Mechanistically, aberrant megakaryocytes in myelofibrosis upregulate and secrete profibrotic mediators including transforming growth factor beta and interleukin-13 that activate marrow fibroblasts and mesenchymal stromal cells to deposit collagen and extracellular matrix, a process documented both by single-cell secretome signatures and by spatial colocalization of ligand and responding stromal cell types [12, 68]. Functional perturbation in murine models and genetic ablation experiments indicate that TGF beta signalling in mesenchymal stromal cells is necessary for the fibrotic phenotype, implying that malignant hematopoietic cells are sufficient to create a fibrotic niche through paracrine enforcement of a stromal program [68]. Beyond fibrosis, malignant clones also rewire the vascular niche; single-cell atlases reveal endothelial heterogeneity and transcriptional shifts in peri-vascular mesenchymal cells in disease that create local cytokine gradients, altered adhesion molecule expression, and niche cues that preferentially support malignant HSC survival and retention [40, 69]. This point deserves explicit emphasis because endothelial cells themselves can be mutated and functionally co-opted. JAK2V617F-bearing endothelial cells have been shown to protect mutant HSPCs and promote clonal expansion in experimental models, and recent spatial profiling of MPN bone marrow has begun to map vascular-niche programs in situ. Taken together, these data argue that endothelial injury, angiocrine signaling, and sinusoidal remodeling are active determinants of progression, thrombosis, and therapeutic resistance rather than passive bystanders [69–72].

The immune microenvironment: From surveillance to evasion

Single-cell immunophenotyping of MPN marrow and blood reveals broad immune dysfunction in chronic disease, characterized by evidence of T cell exhaustion, altered natural killer cell states, and expansions of monocyte and macrophage populations with pro-fibrotic or tolerogenic phenotypes [24, 73]. In multiple studies JAK2V617F activity associates with increased PD-L1 expression on monocytes, megakaryocytes and platelets and with STAT3/STAT5 driven promoter activation of PD-L1, providing a mechanistic route from oncogene to immune checkpoint upregulation and potential T cell suppression [74]. Immunohistochemical and single-cell surveys detect macrophage polarization skewed toward M2-like phenotypes, and increased frequencies of SLAMF7-high monocytes in myelofibrosis, which correlate with fibrotic progression and may act as precursors to fibrocytes that deposit matrix components [75–78]. In parallel, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have been reported in MPN marrow and are increasingly viewed as a cellular bridge between chronic inflammation and immune suppression [79–81]. Although direct spatial quantification of CD8 + T-cell exclusion from fibrotic islands in large MPN cohorts remains limited, the available single-cell and spatial data are consistent with a segregated marrow architecture in which fibrotic, megakaryocyte-rich territories are associated with MDSC-like monocytes, whereas T/NK cells show dysfunctional or exhausted programs rather than effective antitumor activity [79, 80, 82, 83].

At transformation, high-resolution single-cell multi-omic profiling demonstrated that leukemic and pre-leukemic clones evolve immune-evasive phenotypes, including PD-L1 upregulation and suppression of antigen presentation pathways, an evolution that parallels loss of immune surveillance and that is detectable before frank clinical transformation [11, 74, 84].

Inflammation as a double-edged sword

Chronic inflammation is both a driver and a consequence of clonal selection in MPN: elevated circulating cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha are reproducible features of MPN cohorts and animal models, correlate with symptom burden and adverse outcome, and alter HSC function in ways that favour expansion of mutant clones [85, 86]. Experimentally, inflammatory signalling creates selective landscapes in which wild-type HSCs are disadvantaged while clones bearing mutations in epigenetic regulators or TP53 acquire a fitness advantage, thereby accelerating genetic evolution and promoting the emergence of high-risk subclones [11, 87, 88]. Single-cell longitudinal studies have shown that inflammation-associated transcriptional and epigenetic programs frequently precede detectable genetic escalation, implying that targeting inflammation could interrupt the ecological dynamics that permit malignant evolution [11, 67]. At the same time, inflammatory mediators are part of the anti-tumour response and can reinforce immune surveillance; therefore therapeutic attenuation of inflammation requires a precision approach because indiscriminate suppression risks reducing anti-clone immunity while selective modulation of pathogenic axes such as transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ), galectin-1, or IL-13 may collapse the pro-fibrotic niche without abolishing beneficial immune functions [67, 68]. Taken together, these niche and immune alterations appear to evolve as a spatially patterned continuum rather than a late, binary switch. Single-cell data show that inflammatory, fibrotic, and immunologic dysfunction signatures are already detectable in pre-PMF and increase further in overt PMF, while spatial studies show that diseased marrow and splenic compartments are organized into distinct peri-vascular, endosteal, and fibrotic territories. This suggests that canonical MPN drivers can initiate the inflammatory loop, but sustained niche remodeling and immune dysfunction become progressively more prominent during disease progression and transformation [14, 24, 67].

Clinical and translational horizons: From atlas to action

The ecological and immune remodeling described above has direct translational implications: disease modification in MPN will likely require therapies that act on both malignant cells and their niche or immune context, and single-cell readouts can serve as early pharmacodynamic markers of such dual targeting [12, 40]. Clinical and preclinical data illustrate this principle: JAK inhibitors modulate cytokine milieus but can select for alternative resistant clones, interferon reduces JAK2 allele burden in some patients, and checkpoint blockade shows preclinical activity but will likely need combination with niche-modifying agents for robust efficacy [74, 89].

Refining prognostication: Beyond clinical scores

Building on the phylogeographic framework developed in the previous sections, the translational value of single-cell and spatial biomarkers is that they preserve the three dimensions that bulk scores flatten: clonal composition, temporal evolution, and marrow location [6, 14, 54]. Current clinicogenomic risk tools such as MIPSS-70 and its refinements remain useful, but they are static snapshots that do not capture the clonal architecture, mutation order, cell-state heterogeneity, or niche remodeling that the earlier phylogeographic sections show to be central to progression [6, 14, 54, 90]. Single-cell and spatial multi-omic data offer orthogonal, dynamic features that are conceptually well suited to improve prognostication, because they measure the cellular composition, subclone topology, and microenvironmental programs that actually drive progression [12, 40]. We propose three tractable single-cell derived biomarkers that could be validated prospectively. The first is a clonal diversity index, computed similarly to ecological diversity metrics such as the Shannon index applied to single-cell genotypes, which quantifies the number of subclones and their relative abundances and has prognostic signal in other cancers and in phylogenetic studies of hematologic malignancies [91, 92]. The second is a leukemia stem cell-like score, derived from single-cell transcriptomes of HSC and progenitor compartments and analogous to the LSC17 stemness signature in acute myeloid leukemia. Recent work by Medeiros et al. used scRNA-seq data from Lin-CD34 + myelofibrosis HSPCs to guide the development of MPN24, a 24-gene stem/progenitor-cell expression signature that was prognostic for overall survival independently of clinical and genomic variables and complements existing risk models. A compact MPN-adapted LSC-like score could therefore identify quiescent, therapy-resistant stem programs that predict poor outcome independent of bulk mutation profile [11, 93, 94]. Extending this framework to secondary AML, liquid biopsy coupled with single-cell profiling can track measurable residual disease and detect LSC-like populations that survive therapy. Recent AML studies show that single-cell RNA sequencing can resolve residual stem-like compartments and relapse-prone blast subclones, while cfDNA/ctDNA-based liquid biopsy provides a minimally invasive way to follow disease burden over time. This makes an LSC-informed liquid-biopsy strategy a natural bridge between MPN prognostication and early detection of leukemic transformation [95–99].

The third is a spatial niche metric defined by the abundance and proximity of pro-fibrotic niche cells such as aberrant megakaryocyte subsets, activated mesenchymal stromal cells, and galectin-1 expressing stromal/immune elements, which single-cell and spatial atlases now link to progression and survival in myelofibrosis [12, 67]. Together, these measures could be integrated with existing clinical scores to produce a mechanistically interpretable composite that better discriminates patients who will progress rapidly from those who will remain indolent, and serial reassessment of the same readouts could also track treatment-induced shifts in clone burden, stemness, and niche activation over time [6, 14, 54].

A blueprint for rational combination therapies

Single-cell resolved phylogenies and niche maps argue that durable disease modification will require simultaneous targeting of malignant clones, their adaptive survival programs, and the niche signals that sustain them [40, 100]. One rational triplet, suggested by mechanistic data, is JAK inhibition to blunt inflammatory cytokine-driven selection, TGF-beta pathway blockade to prevent or reverse stromal activation and fibrosis, and immune checkpoint modulation to restore anti-clone T cell activity; early phase trials of TGF-beta traps and PD-1 blockade in MPN provide proof of principle that each axis is targetable but monotherapy is insufficient [101]. A second, clonally informed strategy is synthetic lethality targeted to subclonal dependencies revealed by single-cell functional profiling; for example, JAK inhibition increases apoptotic dependence on BCL-2 family members in preclinical models and fedratinib or ruxolitinib combined with venetoclax or with the BCL-XL/BCL-2 pathway inhibitor navitoclax has demonstrated preclinical and early clinical activity, arguing that profiling BCL-2 family expression at single-cell resolution could nominate combination partners for dominant and resistant minor clones [102–104]. Finally, spatial atlases point to niche modifiers such as galectin-1 or select stromal signalling pathways as attractive adjuncts because their inhibition can collapse the local pro-fibrotic ecology and thereby reduce the fitness advantage of malignant subclones, potentially lowering the probability of resistance emerging from the niche [67, 105].

Monitoring clonal dynamics in real time: The future of liquid biopsy

To deploy adaptive, combination regimens we need minimally invasive, frequent readouts of clonal dynamics; circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assays already detect myeloid mutations and capture temporal changes in variant allele fraction, and clinical ctDNA workflows are increasingly mature for monitoring hematologic disease [106, 107]. Augmenting ctDNA with single-cell profiling of circulating peripheral blood mononuclear cells, circulating CD34 positive progenitors, or circulating cell-free RNA could resolve which clones are transcriptionally active, which cell states carry therapeutic liabilities, and whether emerging resistance is genetic, epigenetic, or niche-driven [12, 108]. Proof-of-concept studies show that peripheral blood single-cell assays can recover HSPC states and clonal signals that reflect marrow biology, and paired ctDNA plus peripheral single-cell assays provide complementary sensitivity because ctDNA more faithfully reports DNA-level events while single-cell RNA or CITE-seq reveals phenotype and target expression at cell resolution [106, 108]. Operationally, a clinic-ready surveillance platform would combine personalized, panel-based ctDNA for rapid mutation tracking with periodic single-cell HSPC profiling when ctDNA trajectories indicate expansion or therapy escape, enabling adaptive switching or addition of agents before clinical transformation [107, 109]. Validation of such a paradigm will require prospective trials that test whether single-cell informed interventions reduce progression and improve survival beyond current standard approaches and whether the additional complexity and cost of single-cell surveillance deliver clinically meaningful benefit [110, 111]. However, routine implementation will also depend on practical turnaround constraints: published NGS-based liquid-biopsy workflows commonly report results in about 7–14 days, and real-world ctDNA workflows can return molecular results earlier than tissue testing, but they still require batching, centralized processing, and nontrivial analytical time [96, 112]. Single-cell add-on assays further increase cost and computational burden because they add cell isolation/sorting, library preparation, and downstream data processing, and recent reviews still describe costs as prohibitive for routine use in many laboratories [96, 113]. Before this strategy can be incorporated into adaptive therapy protocols, laboratories will need harmonized pre-analytical handling, validated quality control, and standardized bioinformatic pipelines; consensus liquid-biopsy guidance and single-cell benchmarking studies both emphasize standardization and reproducible analysis as prerequisites for clinical deployment [114–116]. Until these conditions are met, liquid biopsy plus single-cell profiling should be viewed as a research-grade surveillance platform rather than a routine clinical monitoring tool.

Challenges, limitations and future directions

Single-cell and spatial multi-omic approaches have opened transformative windows onto myeloproliferative neoplasm biology, but the methods carry intrinsic technical limitations that demand cautious interpretation and active methodological development [117]. A central practical problem is measurement sparsity and dropout in single-cell RNA sequencing, where low per-cell capture of transcripts produces many false zeros that complicate quantification of gene expression and downstream inference of cell states [118, 119]. Analogously, single-cell DNA sequencing workflows suffer from high allelic dropout, misalignment artefacts, and coverage variability that reduce sensitivity for low-frequency variants and impair confident zygosity calls, which limits the resolution of clonal phylogenies unless carefully controlled by orthogonal validation [120, 121].

Spatial transcriptomics platforms trade breadth for resolution in distinct ways, because sequencing-based spot arrays capture mixed transcripts from multiple cells per spot while imaging-based methods deliver single-cell resolution at the cost of gene panel size or throughput, so platform choice imposes unavoidable biases on the biological conclusions that can be drawn [122, 123]. The cost and sample throughput of high-quality multi-omic profiling remain substantial barriers to assembling large, longitudinal cohorts that are necessary to infer causal trajectories and treatment effects at population scale [124]. Computational integration of multimodal data is itself an active frontier because modalities often have weakly linked features, imbalanced cell populations, and platform specific batch effects, and no single algorithm yet universally outperforms others across the key tasks of clustering, batch correction, and cross-modal mapping [125–128].

Noise reduction, imputation, and contrastive learning strategies can mitigate some technical artefacts, but these approaches can also introduce false structure or obscure rare true biology when applied without rigorous benchmarking and simulated controls [129, 130].

Biological sampling introduces additional constraints: bone marrow is spatially heterogeneous and focal lesions may escape detection when only limited biopsy cores are profiled, while peripheral blood surrogates incompletely capture marrow niche interactions that are essential to MPN evolution [131, 132].

Single-cell datasets are inevitably observational and correlative, so translating atlas-scale associations into causal mechanisms requires orthogonal functional perturbation in tractable models and careful temporal sampling to discriminate drivers from passengers [133]. At the level of biological interpretation, the proposed phylogeography model is already strongly supported by converging single-cell and spatial evidence: MPN evolution is branching rather than strictly linear, driver and cooperating lesions can reside in independent clones, mutation order materially alters phenotype, and pre-leukemic epigenetic reservoirs together with niche remodeling can shape later selection [9, 11, 21, 40, 50, 54, 56, 67, 100, 133]. However, several elements remain inferential rather than proven, including the exact causal order of niche remodeling versus clonal expansion, the frequency and mechanistic importance of true clone–clone cooperation, and the extent to which one evolutionary route can be generalized across all MPN subtypes and treatment settings. These open questions define an important future direction: longitudinal, spatially resolved, perturbation-based studies that can separate selection from adaptation and distinguish drivers from passengers [133].

Regulatory, logistical, and ethical considerations also constrain rapid clinical adoption: standardization of protocols, harmonized quality control metrics, secure data sharing, and cost-effective clinical assays are prerequisites for deploying single-cell derived biomarkers in routine practice [125, 133]. Despite these limitations, clear technological progress points to concrete near-term directions that can materially strengthen the field: improvements in chemistry and capture to reduce dropout, hybrid spatial approaches that combine whole-transcriptome spots with targeted high-resolution imaging, and better single-cell DNA methods that lower allelic dropout will together raise inferential confidence [131, 134]. On the analysis side, community benchmarks and open challenges are already clarifying which integration strategies work best for particular tasks and motivating methods that explicitly model modality-specific noise and cell sampling bias [125, 133]. Emerging AI architectures are likely to provide the next layer of leverage here. Foundation models such as scGPT and standardized benchmarking frameworks such as BioLLM can learn reusable representations from very large single-cell compendia and transfer them to smaller disease cohorts, while agentic systems such as CellAtria can orchestrate literature parsing, metadata extraction, QC, integration, and downstream analysis in a reproducible manner [135–137]. In hematology, similar workflows could be deployed locally on hospital-controlled data or trained via federated learning so that sensitive marrow and blood datasets remain on site while model updates are shared across institutions; cloud-based implementations, exemplified by CloudATAC, can provide the GPU-backed throughput needed for large scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics atlases [138, 139]. A hematology-specific AI agent could then be fine-tuned on marrow atlases, mutation panels, and outcome labels to turn these embeddings into calibrated clinical risk scores for progression, transformation, or treatment response [135–139].

Critically, the highest-value future investments are those that couple dense multi-omic measurement with careful study design: longitudinal sampling, spatially resolved paired marrow and blood profiling, and randomized interventional studies in which molecular readouts are prespecified pharmacodynamic endpoints [124, 132]. In parallel, translational success will require iterative cycles of atlas generation, hypothesis testing in experimental systems, and pragmatic clinical validation that demonstrates that single-cell informed interventions actually change patient outcomes rather than merely refining mechanistic description [133]. In sum, single-cell and spatial multi-omics provide an unparalleled toolkit for dissecting clonal metamorphosis in MPN, but realizing their promise depends on solving linked technical, computational, sampling, and translational challenges through methodological innovation, rigorous benchmarking, and tightly integrated experimental-clinical pipelines [117, 125]. Conclusion

The integration of single-cell and spatial multi-omic technologies has fundamentally reshaped our understanding of myeloproliferative neoplasm pathogenesis, moving the field beyond a simplistic linear genetic model toward a dynamic, ecological framework. The evidence synthesized in this review demonstrates that MPN evolution cannot be reduced to the sequential acquisition of driver mutations alone; rather, it emerges from the interplay of clonal architecture, cell-state heterogeneity, microenvironmental remodeling, and immune dysfunction. Single-cell resolved phylogenies reveal that the same canonical driver can produce profoundly different outcomes depending on its cellular context, mutation order, and co-existing genetic lesions, explaining the long-standing clinical paradox of variable penetrance and unpredictable progression. Spatial transcriptomic and proteomic maps further establish that malignant clones are not passive passengers but active architects of their own niche, remodeling stromal, vascular, and immune compartments to create permissive ecosystems that select for aggressive subclones and drive fibrotic or leukemic transformation.

This reconceptualization carries direct translational implications. Prognostication can now incorporate clonal diversity indices, stemness signatures, and spatial niche metrics that capture the functional and ecological dimensions of disease biology. Rational combination therapies must simultaneously target malignant clones, their adaptive survival programs, and the niche signals that sustain them, with emerging evidence supporting strategies that pair JAK inhibition with TGF-beta blockade, immune checkpoint modulation, or BCL-2 family inhibition. Real-time monitoring of clonal dynamics using liquid biopsy approaches, augmented by single-cell profiling of circulating cells, offers the prospect of adaptive therapy guided by early detection of resistance or impending transformation.

Nevertheless, significant challenges remain. Technical limitations including allelic dropout, measurement sparsity, and platform-specific biases demand continued methodological innovation and rigorous benchmarking. The observational nature of current datasets necessitates orthogonal functional validation and carefully designed longitudinal studies to establish causality. Clinical adoption requires standardized protocols, cost-effective assays, and prospective trials demonstrating that single-cell informed interventions improve patient outcomes beyond current standards.(Fig. 4).

Contrasting models of MPN evolution: from linear progression to spatial‑phylogenetic complexity. (A) Linear mutation‑order model – a traditional view where a founder driver mutation is followed by sequential acquisition of secondary and tertiary hits, leading linearly to disease progression. This model inadequately explains variable latency, penetrance, and clonal heterogeneity. (B) Spatial‑phylogenetic (ecological) model, proposed from single‑cell and spatial multi‑omic data. A founder clone arises within a specific bone marrow niche. Branching evolution generates diverse subclones that coexist, compete, or cooperate. The local microenvironment (e.g., perivascular or endosteal niches) selectively pressures subclones, leading to non‑linear clonal expansions, spatial segregation, and ultimately divergent clinical outcomes. This framework integrates mutation order, clonal ancestry, and niche‑dependent selection to explain the full complexity of MPN evolution

Looking forward, the highest-impact investments will couple dense multi-omic measurement with thoughtful study design: paired marrow and blood profiling, spatially resolved sampling, and randomized interventional trials with prespecified molecular endpoints. As these technologies mature from discovery tools to clinical platforms, they hold the promise of delivering personalized, ecologically informed management strategies that intercept progression before it occurs, ultimately transforming the therapeutic landscape for patients with MPN. The era of viewing MPN solely through the lens of bulk genetics has passed. A more complete framework is phylogeographic: mutation order remains important, but it explains disease behavior only when interpreted together with the spatial location of the clone, its occupancy of marrow niches, and its interactions with stromal and immune ecosystems.
