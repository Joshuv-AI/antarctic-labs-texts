Multivariate analysis of treatment choices, disease activity, comorbidities, and oral impairments in rheumatoid arthritis patients
Authors: Anitha Krishnan Pandarathodiyil, Ramya Ramadoss, K. Hema Shree, Saranya Chinnadurai, Pratibha Ramani, B. Sivapathasundharam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease that affects the synovial joints and is often associated with multiple comorbidities. Emerging evidence suggests a bidirectional link between RA and periodontal inflammation, wherein shared inflammatory mediators contribute to disease severity and therapeutic outcomes.Objective:The objective of the study was to examine the relationships among demographic and clinical characteristics, comorbidities, periodontal impairment assessed using Russell’s Periodontal Index, systemic disease activity measured by Disease Activity Score (DAS28) and C-reactive protein (CRP), and treatment regimens in patients with RA.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional cohort of 33 patients clinically diagnosed with RA (21 seropositive and 12 seronegative) was analyzed. Demographic, serological, and clinical data were retrieved from medical records and verified during follow-up consultations. Russell’s Index was used to quantify periodontal involvement, whereas disease activity was assessed using the DAS28 score, which incorporates CRP levels. Details of comorbidities and prescribed treatments, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), corticosteroids, or combination therapy, were documented. Descriptive and correlational analyses were performed to evaluate associations between disease activity, oral impairment, and therapeutic approach.Results:The cohort comprised 91% females, with a mean age of 46.5 ± 9.8 years. Seropositive RA was identified in 64% of patients. The mean Russell’s Index was 2.42 in the RA+ and 1.92 in the RA- groups, showing greater periodontal impairment in seropositive cases. Hypertension (12%), diabetes (6%), and hypothyroidism (6%) were the most frequent comorbidities. Combination therapy (HCQ + steroids) was most commonly prescribed (36%) and associated with higher DAS28 and Russell’s scores. A positive correlation was noted between disease activity and periodontal index values.Conclusion:Both systemic comorbidities and oral inflammatory burden influence RA severity. Integrating periodontal assessment with systemic disease monitoring enables more precise, personalized therapy selection. Russell’s Index, in conjunction with DAS28 and CRP, may serve as a useful adjunctive marker for therapeutic stratification in RA.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disorder that primarily affects the joints, resulting in pain, swelling, stiffness, and potential loss of function.[1] RA is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body’s tissues.[2] RA is estimated to affect approximately 1% of the global population, with a higher prevalence observed in women.[3] Timely diagnosis and intervention are crucial for managing symptoms and preventing joint damage. Understanding the various factors influencing the disease’s progression is essential for improving patient outcomes.

The coexistence of other health conditions significantly impacts the health outcomes and progression of RA in affected individuals. Studies have shown that comorbidities such as hypertension, osteoporosis, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease are frequently observed in patients with RA, thereby influencing treatment choices and overall disease outcomes.[4] In addition, the presence of multiple health conditions, including mental health disorders, substance use disorders, cardiovascular issues, and chronic pain, has been associated with increased RA disease activity and diminished functional status. This underscores the importance of identifying and managing these comorbidities to achieve better treatment results.[5]

Moreover, the decision to initiate disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) for early RA is often shaped by the presence of other health conditions, with most comorbidities not restricting the initiation or continuation of DMARD therapy.[6] However, certain health conditions and advancing age may lead to a more conservative approach to treatment. A comprehensive understanding of the interactions between multiple health conditions, Russell’s Index Scores, and treatment decisions is critical for the effective management of RA and for enhancing patient health outcomes.[7]

The development of RA is significantly influenced by the presence of comorbid health conditions, which can affect treatment efficacy, disease activity levels, and long-term prognosis. Research indicates that comorbidities are prevalent among RA patients, with conditions such as hypertension, pulmonary disease, and diabetes frequently reported.[78] The coexistence of multiple health issues in RA patients is associated with a diminished likelihood of achieving treatment goals and effectively managing disease activity, underscoring the necessity for a comprehensive management strategy.[9] Furthermore, a greater burden of comorbidities correlates with elevated Disease Activity Scores (DASs) following the initiation of biological treatments, suggesting a reduced probability of effective disease control in these individuals.[10] The increased mortality rates further highlight the detrimental effects of comorbidities on RA prognosis, poorer treatment responses, and diminished quality of life experienced by RA patients with these additional health challenges.[11]

The treatment options available for RA significantly influence patients’ health outcomes. Research indicates that early implementation of additional steroid therapy is linked to improved short-term treatment results.[12131415] Patient preferences are essential in the selection of treatment, with individuals placing a high priority on factors such as enhanced physical functionality and minimized side effects.[16] It is imperative to comprehend patients’ values to facilitate shared decision-making in RA management, as patients weigh the effectiveness of treatments, potential side effects, and the importance of physical well-being when making healthcare choices.[17]

Furthermore, patient preferences regarding RA treatments exhibit considerable variation, with some individuals demonstrating a willingness to accept higher risks of side effects in exchange for improvements in pain management, daily activities, or treatment convenience.[18] Therefore, the incorporation of patient preferences into clinical practice recommendations is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and ensuring patient-centered care in RA management.[19]

In addition, the prognosis of RA can be significantly affected by concurrent medical conditions, complicating disease management and potentially leading to unfavorable overall outcomes. Comorbidities such as heart disease, diabetes, and respiratory issues can exacerbate the severity of RA, necessitating more intricate treatment approaches. These concomitant conditions often limit medication options and elevate the risk of adverse effects, consequently diminishing physical capabilities and quality of life. The emotional burden of managing multiple chronic illnesses, including depression and anxiety, further complicates the challenges faced by RA patients. Therefore, it is essential to adopt a comprehensive approach that addresses both RA and its associated medical conditions to enhance patient outcomes and improve quality of life.

This study investigates the complex interrelationship between comorbidities, Russell’s Index scores, and treatment decisions in patients with RA. By analyzing these factors, the research seeks to identify patterns and correlations that could greatly improve clinical decision-making and contribute to the development of personalized treatment strategies. Personalized treatment in this context refers to adapting therapy choices (e.g., monotherapy, steroids, and combination DMARDs) based on individual profiles, including age, serostatus, comorbidity burden, and oral health scores, thereby maximizing therapeutic response and minimizing adverse effects. The relevance of this study is underscored by its focus on the multifaceted nature of RA, a significant and critical area of interest in the field of rheumatology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cohort study presents a comprehensive analysis involving 33 patients diagnosed with RA between June 2023 and June 2024. Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the SRB. The primary focus of the study is to assess various clinical and demographic characteristics of seropositive and seronegative RA patients, considering their diverse comorbidities and treatment regimens. The inclusion criteria comprised Rheumatoid Arthritis patients with a confirmed clinical diagnosis and complete medical records, including relevant demographic details, disease characteristics, periodontal assessment findings, laboratory investigations, and treatment history. RA patients were excluded if they had incomplete clinical data or a documented follow-up period of less than 6 months.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) for a two-group comparison between seropositive (RA+) and seronegative (RA+) patients on continuous outcomes such as Russell’s Periodontal Index and DAS28 scores. Parameters were set at a medium effect size (f = 0.30; Cohen’s d ≈ 0.60), α = 0.05, and power (1– β) = 0.80. This generated a minimum required sample of 28 participants. To compensate for potential attrition or incomplete data (~15%), the target size was increased to 33 patients, which was achieved.

The assumed effect size was guided by the randomized controlled trial by Thilagar et al. (2022), which evaluated periodontal therapy in RA patients with chronic periodontitis. That study – comprising 28 rheumatoid arthritis–chronic periodontitis patients – demonstrated moderate improvements in DAS28 and C-reactive protein (CRP) following intervention, supporting the selection of a medium-magnitude effect for power estimation.[19]

The data employed in this study were procured through a thorough collection process involving multiple sources, including patient medical records, surveys, and follow-up consultations. The primary objective was to ensure both the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the data collected. The medical records yielded extensive demographic information, encompassing patient age, gender, and serological status (either seropositive or seronegative for RA), which are critical for differentiating among the various subtypes of the disease. The determination of serological status was conducted using the American College of Rheumatology-European League Against Rheumatism Classification Criteria for RA, which classifies patients as seropositive if they present with elevated levels of rheumatoid factor (RF) or anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs).[2021] The ability to differentiate between seropositive and seronegative RA is of significant importance, as these subtypes may exhibit variations in severity and progression, which could influence treatment strategies and the management of patient care.

Detailed data on comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hypothyroidism, and Vitamin D deficiency were extracted from the medical records, as these conditions can complicate treatment strategies and significantly impact the overall health of RA patients. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, measured on at least two separate occasions using an automated blood pressure cuff. Diabetes was diagnosed based on established criteria, including a fasting blood glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥6.5%. The management approach, including the use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin therapy, was recorded for each patient. Hypothyroidism was identified through biochemical analysis, with elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone levels >4.0 mU/L and reduced free thyroxine (T4) levels serving as diagnostic markers. Vitamin D deficiency was determined by a serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25[OH] D) concentration of <20 ng/mL. Documentation included any supplementation or therapeutic measures undertaken to address the deficiency.

DASs28 were recorded to assess the activity and severity of RA in each patient. The DAS28 incorporates joint counts, patient-reported assessments, and inflammatory markers like CRP levels to provide a comprehensive measure of disease activity.

Furthermore, data on treatment regimens, including monotherapy with hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), steroid treatments, and combination therapies, were collected. The effectiveness of these treatments was assessed by tracking changes in DAS28 scores, CRP levels, and Russell’s Index scores. CRP levels, a marker of systemic inflammation, were measured to monitor inflammation levels, as elevated CRP indicates active disease. In addition to the objective data from medical records, patient surveys, and follow-up consultations provided subjective insights into each patient’s health status, discomfort levels, and functional capacity. These consultations allowed for ongoing monitoring of disease progression and treatment efficacy, ensuring that any changes in symptoms or treatment plans were accurately recorded.

Disease activity was evaluated using the DAS28 joints erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), which incorporates tender and swollen joint counts, the ESR, and the patient’s global health assessment. In parallel, CRP levels were quantified independently to provide a biochemical correlate of systemic inflammation and to compare with periodontal severity (Russell’s Index) and treatment subgroups. CRP estimation was performed using a standard immunoturbidimetric assay in the institutional biochemistry laboratory, with a reference range of <6 mg/L. This approach was adopted because the DAS28 in this study used ESR as the inflammatory parameter, and therefore, CRP was analyzed separately to strengthen the comparative analysis between systemic and oral inflammatory markers.

The DAS28 scores were used to assess the disease activity of RA, with a score of <2.6 indicating low disease activity, 2.6–3.2 indicating moderate activity, and >3.2 indicating high disease activity. Periodontal status was assessed using the original Russell’s Periodontal Index as described by Russell (1956). Each tooth was assigned a score based on clinical findings: 0 (healthy), 1 (mild gingivitis), 2 (gingivitis), 6 (gingivitis with pocket formation), and 8 (advanced periodontal destruction). The patient-level score was calculated by dividing the total of individual tooth scores by the number of teeth examined, yielding a final index ranging from 0 to 8. Higher values indicate greater periodontal inflammatory burden and tissue destruction. The index was used solely to quantify periodontal disease severity and was analyzed in relation to systemic disease activity (DAS28) and inflammatory markers (CRP).[12]

CRP levels were measured to assess the degree of inflammation, with elevated levels correlating with increased disease activity. The presence of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypothyroidism was also recorded, as these conditions may influence disease progression and treatment outcomes. Finally, the study categorized patients based on their treatment regimens, which included monotherapy and combination therapy approaches. The impact of these treatments on disease activity and functional status was carefully monitored throughout the study.

RESULTS

The study cohort consisted of 33 patients clinically diagnosed with RA, exhibiting a pronounced female predominance. A total of 30 patients were female (91%), whereas only three were male (9%) [Table 1 and Figure 1]. The age of participants ranged from 24 to 67 years, with a mean of 46.5 years. This distribution is consistent with established epidemiological patterns of RA, which disproportionately affect women, particularly during the middle decades of life when hormonal shifts, occupational stressors, and autoimmune triggers may intersect. The wide age span ensured inclusion of early, active, and advanced RA stages, offering a holistic understanding of disease burden and treatment variability across different life phases.

Age distribution of the rheumatoid arthritis patient cohort

The predominance of females and middle-aged individuals underscores the intersection between immunological susceptibility and hormonal status in RA pathogenesis. Female hormonal transitions – such as perimenopause and menopause – may exacerbate inflammatory pathways, contributing to the heightened prevalence and severity of RA in women. Furthermore, many female patients in this age group are caregivers or professionals, intensifying the physical and emotional stress associated with chronic illness.

Age-wise stratification revealed distinct comorbidity patterns. Younger patients (below 30 years) exhibited relatively preserved systemic health, with only one reported case of Vitamin D deficiency. This suggests early-stage RA in this group, possibly with lesser cumulative inflammation or fewer treatment-related side effects. In contrast, patients between 30 and 70 years exhibited higher frequencies of comorbidities such as hypertension, hypothyroidism, and diabetes – conditions typically associated with chronic inflammation, endocrine dysfunction, and prolonged pharmacotherapy.

Notably, no comorbidities were recorded among patients older than 70 years. This absence likely reflects the underrepresentation of elderly patients in the cohort rather than a true lack of systemic disease. Given the increased prevalence of comorbidities in aged RA populations, this gap highlights a sampling limitation. Nevertheless, the observed trend supports the hypothesis that comorbid burden intensifies during the middle-aged decades when immune dysregulation and metabolic syndrome co-occur.

Among the 33 patients, 21 (64%) were seropositive for RA (RA+), and 12 (36%) were seronegative (RA-), based on RF and/or anticyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibody status [Table 2]. This classification is pivotal as RA+ patients typically manifest more aggressive disease, systemic involvement, and poorer long-term prognosis.

Periodontal health, quantified via Russell’s Periodontal Index, showed significant differences across serotypes. The mean Russell’s Index among RA+ patients was 2.42 (range: 1.4–3.8), compared to 1.92 (range: 0.4–2.8) in RA+ patients. This suggests that periodontal destruction was more severe in RA- individuals, aligning with previous literature that emphasizes the immuno-inflammatory overlap between oral and systemic RA manifestations.

Furthermore, a robust positive correlation was observed between Russell’s Index scores and DAS28, as illustrated in Figure 2. Patients with higher periodontal burden consistently exhibited elevated systemic disease activity. This reinforces the concept of a bidirectional RA–periodontitis link, wherein chronic oral inflammation may contribute to systemic immune priming and disease exacerbation.

Comorbidities were prevalent across the cohort, with a notably higher concentration among seropositive (RA+) patients compared to seronegative (RA-) individuals [Table 3 and Figure 3]. Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, affecting four patients (12%), of whom three were RA+ and one RA-. Diabetes mellitus was observed in two patients (6%), equally distributed between the serological subgroups, while hypothyroidism occurred in two patients (6%), both belonging to the RA+ category. Less frequent but clinically relevant conditions included a seizure disorder in one RA+ patient (3%) and methotrexate (MTX)-related oral mucositis in another RA+ patient (3%). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that seropositive individuals tend to exhibit a greater systemic disease burden and multisystem involvement, reflecting the broader inflammatory and metabolic interplay characteristic of RA+ pathology.

RA+ individuals bore a higher overall comorbidity burden, suggesting an additive inflammatory and endocrine impact in this group. Although statistical significance could not be robustly established due to small subgroups, the trend is consistent with known clinical trajectories of seropositive RA. Notably, both thyroid dysfunction and hypertension may contribute to heightened systemic inflammation and fatigue, amplifying the subjective and objective disease burden.

Importantly, even relatively uncommon comorbidities, such as oral mucositis or seizure disorders, can dramatically impact treatment tolerability, especially when overlapping with RA flares or drug toxicity.

The treatment modalities employed in the cohort encompassed HCQ, steroids, and their combinations, reflecting a stratified therapeutic approach based on disease severity and comorbidity profiles [Table 4 and Figure 4]. Combination therapy with HCQ and steroids was the most frequently utilized regimen, administered to 12 patients (36%). This approach was generally reserved for those exhibiting higher disease activity or multiple comorbid conditions, aligning with the need for intensified immunomodulatory control. HCQ monotherapy was prescribed in six patients (18%), typically those with milder or stable disease, particularly within the seronegative subgroup. Steroid monotherapy, used in four patients (12%), was often employed for acute symptom control or in patients with specific complications such as MTX-related oral mucositis or musculoskeletal overlap disorders like spondylosis. This distribution underscores the individualized nature of treatment selection in RA, where clinical decisions are guided by a combination of systemic activity, periodontal involvement, and patient-specific risk factors.

Treatment patterns and estimated effectiveness metrics

The remaining patients were either on supportive medications or alternative DMARDs not included in the above categories. Combination therapy (HCQ + steroids) was most frequently prescribed, particularly among patients with higher Russell’s Index scores and multiple comorbidities. This suggests that clinicians favored a multidrug approach for patients presenting with both high systemic inflammation and periodontal destruction. HCQ monotherapy was preferred in milder cases, often among RA- individuals or those with stable disease markers. Steroid-only regimens were typically reserved for patients requiring rapid symptom control, including those with oral mucositis or concurrent spondylotic changes.

Treatment allocation also varied based on the comorbidity profile. For instance, HCQ combined with steroids was often administered to patients with hypertension and hypothyroidism, likely due to their synergistic anti-inflammatory effect. In contrast, steroid-only therapy was noted among patients with rarer conditions (e.g., seizure disorders), possibly to avoid interactions with other systemic medications.

The average Russell’s Index across the entire cohort was 2.1, indicating a moderate level of periodontal disease severity. Among those with index scores >3.0, disease severity (DAS28) was also high (>6), reinforcing the clinical correlation between oral and systemic inflammation. These patients often required aggressive therapy, including steroid combination regimens, and frequently reported overlapping comorbidities. Conversely, patients with Russell’s Index <1.0 exhibited lower DAS28 scores and generally responded well to HCQ monotherapy. This subgroup maintained functional independence and experienced fewer flares, indicating that early periodontal control may act as a prognostic marker for milder RA progression. Importantly, these correlations strengthen the rationale for integrating periodontal assessment into routine RA evaluation. Russell’s Index may serve as a surrogate indicator for systemic inflammatory load and guide escalation or de-escalation of therapy.

Patients with metabolic comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes consistently demonstrated higher DAS28 scores. Hypertension contributes to vascular endothelial dysfunction, amplifying inflammatory cascades, while diabetes impairs immune regulation and increases susceptibility to joint damage. Moreover, these conditions complicate pharmacologic management; for example, corticosteroids can worsen glycemic control, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may elevate blood pressure.

Hypothyroidism, though present in only two patients, was associated with increased fatigue, pain, and stiffness, symptoms that often mimic RA flares and complicate disease assessment. In addition, hypothyroidism may alter hepatic metabolism of DMARDs, influencing drug efficacy. Vitamin D deficiency, while rare (3%), remains clinically relevant due to its known role in bone metabolism and immune tolerance. Patients with deficiency may experience worsened joint symptoms, increased risk of osteoporosis, and reduced response to immunomodulatory therapy. Overall, the presence of ≥2 comorbidities significantly increased clinical complexity, often necessitating individualized therapeutic plans, closer monitoring, and multidisciplinary care.

Although patient-wise CRP values were not uniformly available, clinical records indicated that patients receiving combination therapy (HCQ + steroids) reported more consistent symptom control and fewer flares than those on monotherapy. This suggests superior inflammation modulation when dual-mechanism agents are used in tandem.

Elevated CRP levels correlated with higher DAS and Russell’s Index scores, reinforcing their utility as noninvasive inflammatory biomarkers. Regular CRP monitoring, especially during medication transitions, remains essential to gauge treatment effectiveness and guide future adjustments.

Clinical correlations and case trajectories

Individual case analyses yielded valuable clinical insights. Patients with Russell’s Index >3.0 frequently presented with aggressive disease, elevated DAS, and multiple comorbidities. These patients required intensified treatment regimens and often exhibited poor response to monotherapy. In contrast, patients with an index <1.0 maintained functional stability and responded favorably to HCQ alone, requiring fewer medication adjustments over time.

These case patterns underscore the utility of integrated clinical scoring systems, including DAS, Russell’s Index, and comorbidity profiles, in personalizing care, predicting disease course, and preemptively identifying patients at risk for rapid deterioration.

DISCUSSION

RA is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by inflammation of the joints, leading to pain, stiffness, and progressive disability.[22] Despite advancements in treatment, RA remains a complex condition with varied clinical presentations and outcomes. Understanding the factors influencing disease progression and treatment response is crucial for optimizing patient care. The study has revealed significant correlations between Russell’s Index scores, comorbidities, and treatment outcomes in patients with RA. Elevated Russell’s Index scores and comorbidities have been associated with heightened systemic inflammatory activity, necessitating more aggressive treatment approaches.[1223]

RA is significantly more common in women than in men, with a reported female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1. The typical onset of RA occurs between 30 and 60 years of age, although it can develop at any stage of life. Hormonal factors are believed to contribute to the observed gender disparity, whereas age influences both disease severity and the likelihood of comorbidities.[2425] In our cohort, most patients were female (85%), with a median age of 46 years. This aligns with established literature identifying middle-aged women as the demographic most affected by RA.[1124]

The present findings emphasize the need for tailored therapeutic regimens that integrate comorbidity profiles, Russell’s Index scores, and CRP/DAS trends. For instance, patients with high Russell’s Index and multiple comorbidities responded better to combination therapies, whereas those with lower disease activity and minimal systemic involvement could be effectively managed with HCQ monotherapy. These trends suggest that risk stratification using simple indices such as Russell’s Score, alongside seropositivity and inflammatory markers, could guide early therapy intensification or de-escalation. Incorporating such variables into treatment algorithms can optimize outcomes and reduce the burden of overtreatment in mild cases or undertreatment in aggressive disease.

RA is categorized as RA-positive (seropositive) or RA-negative (seronegative) based on the presence of RF and anticyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies. Seropositive RA is often associated with more severe disease progression and heightened inflammatory activity.[26] In our cohort, 70% of patients were seropositive (RA+), which is consistent with findings from previous studies, which state that RA is frequently accompanied by comorbidities such as cardiovascular diseases, interstitial lung disease, diabetes, and osteoporosis, all of which can exacerbate disease outcomes. These associations highlight the need for tailored therapeutic strategies to manage RA and its complications effectively.[23]

The high prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hypothyroidism, and Vitamin D deficiency in our cohort significantly impacts the management of RA and patient quality of life. These findings align with previous research, which emphasizes the substantial effect of comorbidities on RA outcomes and the added complexity they introduce to treatment.[10] Comorbid conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, hypothyroidism, and Vitamin D deficiency contribute to RA progression by increasing systemic inflammation and reducing treatment efficacy. Notably, Vitamin D deficiency is associated with increased disease activity due to its critical role in immune modulation. Together, these comorbidities exacerbate the inflammatory processes in RA, complicating disease management and potentially leading to poorer clinical outcomes. Addressing these comorbidities is crucial for improving treatment response and controlling RA progression.[1024]

Our analysis suggests that combination therapies are generally more effective in reducing disease activity, especially for patients with higher Russell’s Index scores. This demonstrated the superior efficacy of combination therapy in controlling RA symptoms compared to monotherapy. The effectiveness of HCQ as a first-line treatment and the role of steroids in managing inflammation are well documented, further validating our treatment insights.[27]

The Russell’s Periodontal Index is a clinical measure of periodontal inflammation and destruction. In this study, higher periodontal scores correlated with increased systemic inflammatory activity (DAS28 and CRP), supporting the bidirectional relationship between periodontal disease and RA.[28] This index plays a critical role in monitoring long-term outcomes, particularly in patients with multiple risk factors. In our cohort, the Russell’s Index scores ranged from 0.4 to 3.8, with an average score of 2.1. Notably, the Russell’s Index correlated positively with DAS28, underscoring its value as a marker of oral inflammatory burden that parallels systemic disease activity.

The DAS score, a widely utilized measure of disease activity, combines swollen and tender joint counts, patient-reported outcomes, and inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP. It is influenced by comorbid conditions and disease stages, reflecting the multifaceted nature of RA. Studies have shown that individuals with RA are at an increased risk of periodontitis, suggesting a bidirectional association between these two conditions.[11] Both periodontitis and RA share common inflammatory pathways, with oral bacteria and systemic inflammation playing significant roles.[29]

For individuals with both periodontitis and RA, the disease outcomes tend to be more severe, including heightened disease activity and joint damage progression.[30] Managing RA effectively involves a multifaceted approach that addresses both systemic inflammation and associated comorbidities, including periodontal health. RA treatment typically begins with DMARD monotherapy, such as HCQ for mild cases, or MTX, the gold standard for RA management. For patients with moderate-to-severe disease or those unresponsive to monotherapy, combination therapies like MTX with biologics (e.g. tumor necrosis factor [TNF] inhibitors such as adalimumab) are employed. Steroids, such as prednisone, are often used adjunctively to control acute flares and severe inflammation. The choice of treatment is tailored to the patient’s disease severity, comorbidities, and preferences, with efficacy assessed through reductions in DAS scores, improved functional outcomes, and lower CRP levels. Biological therapies, including abatacept and adalimumab, have shown exceptional efficacy in achieving remission, particularly in seropositive RA cases.[31]

Beyond inflammation, converging microbiological and genetic mechanisms underpin the RA-periodontitis connection. Porphyromonas gingivalis expresses a unique peptidylarginine deiminase enzyme that citrullinates host peptides, generating neoantigens that provoke ACPAs, a serological hallmark of RA. Similarly, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans promotes hypercitrullination in neutrophils through leukotoxin A, intensifying autoantigen exposure and systemic immune activation.[29]

Genetically, carriers of HLADRB1 “shared epitope” alleles exhibit heightened susceptibility to both RA and severe periodontitis, highlighting shared immunogenetic pathways influencing T-cell reactivity and cytokine regulation. Variants in PADI4, TNF-α, and interleukin-1 β further modulate inflammatory and autoimmune responses common to both disorders. Together, these overlapping microbial and genetic drivers strengthen the concept of a bidirectional axis wherein dysbiotic oral microbiota and host genotype jointly sustain chronic autoimmunity.[323334]

Addressing periodontal health as part of RA management has been associated with clinical benefits, as treating periodontitis may improve RA symptoms and enhance treatment responses. This underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration between rheumatologists and dentists to provide comprehensive care. Integrating routine dental examinations and targeted periodontal interventions into RA management could improve overall patient outcomes. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanistic links between periodontitis and RA and to explore the potential of periodontal treatments in enhancing RA management.

The study involved 33 patients, providing valuable insights into the research topic. However, the relatively small sample size represents a limitation, as it may affect the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger cohorts are recommended to validate the conclusions and strengthen the evidence base. The study also acknowledges the potential limitations of the retrospective nature of data collection. In addition, the study relies on self-reported data for some parameters, which may introduce bias. Future research with larger cohorts and prospective designs could address these limitations. The study underscores the importance of considering comorbidities, Russell’s Index scores, and individualized treatment strategies in RA management. Effective treatment plans should address the full spectrum of patient health issues to optimize outcomes. Clinicians should assess Russell’s Index and comorbidities when developing treatment plans for RA patients. Personalized approaches that consider these factors can improve disease control and patient quality of life.

CONCLUSION

This study underscores the value of integrating a personalized treatment approach into the routine clinical management of RA. By evaluating periodontal inflammatory burden through Russell’s Index, systemic inflammation via CRP and DAS28 scores, and the presence of key comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hypothyroidism, and Vitamin D deficiency, clinicians can better stratify patients and individualize treatment strategies. Our findings suggest that patients with higher Russell’s Index scores and multiple comorbidities benefit from more intensive, combination-based regimens, while those with milder disease may be effectively managed with monotherapy.

This tailored framework not only enhances disease control but also minimizes unnecessary exposure to aggressive treatments in lower-risk individuals. The correlation between oral inflammatory burden, systemic inflammation, and therapeutic response highlights the importance of multidisciplinary care that includes both rheumatologic and oral health perspectives. Although limited by a modest sample size, this study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility and value of a personalized, stratified treatment model for RA. Future studies involving larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and to establish robust clinical algorithms for individualized RA management.
