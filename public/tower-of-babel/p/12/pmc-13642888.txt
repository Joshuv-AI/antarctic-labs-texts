Causal Relationship Between Circulating Leukocyte Characteristics and Immune Cell Traits With Multiple Sclerosis Risk: A Two-Sample Bidirectional Mendelian Randomisation Study
Authors: Jia-Jia Yun, Ya-Lei Li, Jing-Man Qiu, Yu Yang, Dan-Tong Han, Xiao-Han Yang, Chao Lv, Chao Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study aimed to investigate MR-based evidence for potential causal relationships between circulating leukocytes, immune cell traits, and multiple sclerosis (MS) using a two-sample, bidirectional Mendelian randomisation (MR) approach. We analysed summary-level data from large-scale genome-wide association studies conducted by the International Multiple Sclerosis Genetics Consortium, the Blood Cell Consortium, and Immune Cell traits. Using inverse variance weighted (IVW), weighted mode, weighted median, simple mode, and MR-Egger methods, we estimated genetically predicted associations. The MR-Egger intercept assessed horizontal pleiotropy, Cochran’s Q test examined genetic heterogeneity, and leave-one-out procedures evaluated the sensitivity of the results. We adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR). Forward MR analysis using IVW as the primary method revealed associations between increased white blood cell count (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.05–1.29, P = 0.004, PFDR = 0.031) and lymphocyte count (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.06–1.31, P = 0.003, PFDR = 0.035) with a higher risk of developing MS. Conversely, the CD25hi CD45RA-CD4 not Treg %CD4+ (OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.808–0.942, P = 0.0005, PFDR = 0.075) and the CD16-CD56 on HLA DR+ NK (OR = 0.85, 95% CI = 0.780–0.935, P = 0.0007, PFDR = 0.093) were associated with a reduced risk of MS. In reverse MR analyses, MS showed no robust evidence of a causal effect on white blood cell or lymphocyte counts. Although an FDR-significant reverse association was observed for the IgD+ CD38 + B-cell absolute count subtype, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated instability. Therefore, this finding was not considered reliable evidence of reverse causality. Genetic predisposition to increased white blood cell and lymphocyte counts elevates the risk of MS, while certain immune cell traits may reduce this risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10571-026-01777-9.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an inflammatory condition characterised pathologically by persistent inflammation, demyelination, and axonal injury within the central nervous system. It is the leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young and middle-aged individuals, with a notably higher prevalence in women (Global, regional, and national burden of multiple sclerosis 1990-2016: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2016 2019). The clinical course of MS is highly variable, consisting of relapsing-remitting, secondary progressive, and primary progressive forms. The unpredictable progression of MS often complicates diagnosis, treatment, and long-term management (Lucchinetti et al. 2000). By the end of 2020, over 2.8 million people worldwide were affected by MS. Since 2013, prevalence rates have steadily increased across all regions, with the average age of diagnosis being 32 years old (Walton et al. 2020). The fundamental pathogenic process of MS involves immune cells crossing the blood-brain barrier to attack myelin and oligodendrocytes, impairing neuronal signal transmission and ultimately leading to a gradual decline in motor, sensory, and cognitive functions (Lassmann 2018). Over the past thirty years, disease-modifying therapies for MS have advanced significantly, primarily by managing disease activity through the inhibition or modulation of aberrant inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, these treatments are not curative, and their effectiveness in delaying neurodegeneration and progression of disability varies widely among individuals. As a result, many patients continue to experience progressive neurological decline (Oh et al. 2024). Thus, future research must explore the aetiology and underlying molecular processes of MS, with a focus on developing innovative therapeutics that target chronic inflammation, myelin repair, and axonal protection. This approach should extend beyond simple inflammation suppression to include multi-targeted combination therapies that enable precise, personalized treatment strategies, thereby significantly improving patients’ long-term quality of life.

White blood cell (WBC) count is a crucial biomarker for evaluating overall immune system function and plays a significant role in MS. The substantial increase in myeloid cells reflects the active presence of chronic inflammation in MS patients. This process exacerbates pathological damage, including neuroinflammation and demyelination, by stimulating the release of various inflammatory mediators (Haschka et al. 2020). Moreover, several immune cell subsets, such as CD4 + T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and monocytes, are central to the immunoregulatory processes in MS. For instance, impairments in regulatory T cells (Tregs) may trigger the breakdown of autoimmune tolerance, while certain NK cell subtypes may offer protective benefits (Aghaee et al. 2025). However, the causal direction and genetic relevance of leukocyte and immune cell traits in MS remain incompletely understood. Traditional observational studies suggest that immunological features, such as peripheral blood leukocytosis, are associated with MS, but these associations may be influenced by confounding and reverse causality. Therefore, MR-based evaluation of genetically predicted immune traits may help prioritise immune phenotypes for further biological validation.

Mendelian randomisation (MR), an analytical approach that uses genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV), effectively addresses the aforementioned limitations and provides strong evidence for causal inference (Smith and Ebrahim 2003; Davey Smith and Hemani 2014; Sekula et al. 2016). Importantly, previous MR studies have established a correlation between certain downstream inflammatory markers and the likelihood of MS (Li et al. 2024). However, comprehensive MR evidence for peripheral leukocyte indices and immune cell traits remains limited. To address this gap, we used a two-sample bidirectional MR framework based on large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to examine whether genetically predicted WBC characteristics and immune cell traits are associated with MS risk and whether genetic liability to MS may influence these immune phenotypes. By focusing on MR-based genetic evidence rather than direct functional mechanisms, this study aims to prioritise peripheral immune profiles for subsequent validation in experimental, longitudinal, and clinical studies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study rigorously adheres to the STROBE-MR reporting guidelines (Skrivankova et al. 2021). Bidirectional MR is an epidemiological technique that uses genetic variation as IVs to assess causal relationships between exposure and outcome. We employed MR analysis to evaluate the causal link between WBC count, immune cell subtypes, and the risk of MS. For valid causal inference, IVs must satisfy three essential assumptions: Assumption 1: Genetic variation is directly associated with the exposure; Assumption 2: Genetic variation is independent of confounding factors that may influence the relationship between exposure and outcome; Assumption 3: Genetic variation affects the outcome solely through the exposure, without any alternative pathways (Didelez and Sheehan 2007) (Fig. 1).

Data Sources for MS

Based on publicly available genetic data, the International Multiple Sclerosis Genetics Consortium (IMSGC) study included 115,803 individuals of European ancestry, comprising 47,429 cases and 68,374 controls (Multiple sclerosis genomic map implicates peripheral immune cells and microglia in susceptibility 2019) (Supplementary Table 1).

Data Sources for Peripheral Blood Immune Cells

Using publicly available genetic data, we performed a phenotypic meta-analysis involving 562,243 individuals of European descent from the Blood Cell Consortium. This GWAS analysis identified genetic variations associated with total WBC count, as well as the counts of lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils. Aggregate statistics for total WBC count can be accessed at http://www.mhi-humangenetics.org/en/resources/. The most recent aggregation findings for lymphocyte subtypes are derived from the GWAS Public Catalogue (access codes GCST90001391 to GCST90002121). This study included 3,757 Sardinian participants, with 731 immune cell subtypes classified into four categories: absolute cell counts (AC, 118 features), median fluorescence intensity of surface markers (MFI, 389 features), morphological parameters (MP, 32 features), and relative cell proportions (RC, 192 features). The dataset comprehensively covered several cell types, including B cells, dendritic cells, T cells, regulatory T cells, monocytes, granulocytes, myeloid cells, and natural killer cells (Orrù et al. 2020) (Supplementary material 1).

Selection of IVs

The MR analysis of exposure factors was conducted using the ‘TwoSampleMR’ toolkit. For WBC counts, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) exhibiting GWAS significant associations (P < 5 × 10− 8) were designated as IVs. To ensure IV independence, clustering was performed using the Genomes Project linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference panel (kb = 10,000, r2 < 0.001) (Auton et al. 2015). Lymphocyte subtypes were defined using the same methodology as for WBC counts. IVs were extracted from outcome summary statistics, 1000 ensuring SNP coordination between exposure and outcome to maintain consistent effect estimates for identical effect alleles. To assess IV strength and avoid weak instrument bias, an F-statistic was calculated for each IV, with an F > 10 indicating the absence of weak instrument bias.

Statistical Analysis

MR analysis was conducted using inverse variance weighted (IVW)(Burgess et al. 2019), weighted mode (Hartwig et al. 2017), weighted median(Bowden et al. 2016), and simple mode. All analyses were conducted in R software version 4.3.1 (http://www.Rproject.org), utilising packages including TwoSampleMR (version 0.6.8), forestplot (version 1.1.2), grid (version 4.4.1), ggplot2 (version 3.5.1), readxl (version 1.4.3), dplyr (version 1.1.4), and openxlsx (version 4.2.8). When IVW analysis indicated a causal effect of exposure on the outcome, Cochran’s Q test (Greco et al. 2015) was used to assess heterogeneity in the IVW model, and the MR-Egger intercept test(Arbelaez et al. 2022) was employed to examine horizontal pleiotropy in the MR results. Furthermore, the MR-PRESSO method was used to identify and exclude potential outliers due to horizontal pleiotropy that could affect the estimates. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the results (Verbanck et al. 2018).

Results

Causal Estimates Between WBC Count and MS

Following IV screening and harmonisation, we employed 588 SNPs for monocyte count, 601 for total WBC count, 560 for lymphocyte count, 466 for neutrophil count, 472 for eosinophil count, and 171 for basophil count in MR assessments. All these SNPs yielded F-values exceeding 10, confirming their suitability as robust IVs. Analyses revealed a potential causal association between MS and elevated WBC counts using IVW as the core method, adjusted for FDR (OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.05–1.29, P = 0.004, PFDR = 0.031). Elevated lymphocyte counts were also associated with increased MS risk (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.06–1.31, P = 0.003, PFDR = 0.035) (Fig. 2). However, no causal effect was observed between neutrophil, eosinophil, or basophil counts and MS susceptibility. The MR-Egger intercept test indicated no level-specific multi-effect between WBC, lymphocyte counts and MS. Cochran’s Q test suggested significant heterogeneity, prompting the use of the MR-PRESSO method to identify and remove multi-effect SNPs and correct for bias. After outlier removal, the distortion test yielded a P-value > 0.05, indicating no substantial difference in estimates before and after adjustment. This confirms that causal inference via IVs was not significantly affected by outliers, demonstrating robust and reliable results with high consistency between corrected and uncorrected analyses, thereby supporting the reliability of the findings (Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis further validated the robustness of our results (Fig. 3). Consequently, a significant causal association exists between WBC, lymphocyte counts and MS. Conversely, reverse MR analysis with MS as the exposure revealed no causal effect of MS on elevated WBC and lymphocyte counts (P > 0.05).

Causal Estimates Between Circulating Immune Cell Subtypes and MS

Based on the IV screening criteria, 7,709 SNPs were utilised as IVs for 731 immune cell traits. To investigate the causal effects of MS on immune traits, a two-sample MR analysis was conducted, using the IVW method as the primary approach alongside supplementary methods, including MR-Egger, weighted mode, weighted median, and simple mode. Using IVW as the primary method, and after FDR adjustment (PFDR < 0.1), we identified two immune traits with significant causal associations with MS (Supplementary Table 4). One significant trait was within the Treg group, and the other was within the TBNK group. Using IVW analysis, we observed that the CD25hi CD45RA- CD4 not Treg %CD4+ immune trait (OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.81–0.94, P = 0.0005, PFDR = 0.075) was associated with a reduced risk of developing MS, while the CD16-CD56 on HLA DR+ NK immune trait (OR = 0.85, 95% CI = 0.78–0.94, P = 0.0007, PFDR = 0.093) was also linked to a reduced risk of MS. Similar findings were observed using four alternative methods: MR-Egger, weighted-median, weighted mode, and simple mode (Fig. 4). The MR-Egger intercept test indicated no level-specific pleiotropy between the aforementioned immune cell types and MS (Supplementary Table 3). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis further confirmed the stability of the results (Fig. 5). Conversely, reverse MR analysis with MS as the exposure revealed a significant causal relationship between MS occurrence and the IgD+ CD38 + B cell absolute count subtype (OR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.89–1.02, P = 0.000006, PFDR = 0.009). However, this reverse association was not considered robust because the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated instability, suggesting that the estimate may have been influenced by one or a small number of genetic instruments. Therefore, the reverse MR analysis did not provide reliable evidence supporting a causal effect of MS on this B-cell subtype. (Supplementary material 4)

Discussion

In this bidirectional two-sample MR study, we investigated the causal associations of circulating leukocyte characteristics and immune cell traits with the risk of MS. The main findings were that genetically predicted higher WBC count and lymphocyte count were associated with an increased risk of MS, whereas two immune cell traits, CD25hi CD45RA− CD4 not Treg %CD4+ and CD16 − CD56 on HLA DR+ NK, were associated with a reduced risk of MS. In reverse MR analyses, MS showed no robust evidence of a causal effect on WBC count or lymphocyte count. Although an FDR-significant reverse association was observed for IgD+ CD38 + B cell absolute count, this result was unstable in leave-one-out analysis and was therefore not interpreted as reliable evidence of reverse causality. Overall, these results suggest that genetically influenced peripheral immune profiles may contribute to MS susceptibility, rather than simply representing secondary immune alterations after disease onset.

Among the immune cell traits analysed, CD25hi CD45RA− CD4 not Treg %CD4+ showed an inverse association with MS risk. CD25, the α-chain of the interleukin-2 receptor, is expressed on activated T cells and regulatory T cells and is closely related to IL-2 signalling, T-cell activation, and immune tolerance (Peng et al. 2023). While our MR analysis cannot directly establish cellular mechanisms, prior functional studies suggest that the efficiency of CD25 signaling may be linked to the stability and suppressive activity of Tregs. In experimental settings, robust signaling has been observed to support Foxp3 expression and mitigate phenotypic conversion, which hypothetically contributes to the regulation of autoimmune inflammation (Permanyer et al. 2021). Low-dose IL-2 has also been investigated as a potential strategy to expand regulatory T cells in relapsing-remitting MS (Louapre et al. 2023). However, the CD25hi CD45RA− CD4 not Treg phenotype is complex and should not be simplistically interpreted as a classical regulatory T-cell marker. Some activated memory CD4+ T-cell subsets may display pro-inflammatory properties under specific inflammatory conditions, including involvement in Th17- or GM-CSF-related responses (Cho et al. 2023; Langrish et al. 2005). Therefore, the inverse MR association observed in our study may indicate that genetic determinants of this phenotype capture an immunoregulatory or context-dependent CD4+ T-cell state, rather than proving a direct protective cellular mechanism. Further functional studies are needed to clarify the biological meaning of this association.

We also observed an inverse association between CD16 − CD56 on HLA DR+ NK and MS risk. CD16 − CD56 NK cells largely overlap with the CD56brightCD16 − NK-cell population, which has been regarded as an immunoregulatory subset with relatively limited direct cytotoxicity but strong cytokine-producing capacity(Cooper et al. 2001; Fehniger et al. 2003) HLA-DR expression on NK cells is considered a marker of activation and may identify functionally distinct NK-cell states(Evans et al. 2011; Kust et al. 2024) Although the identified CD4+ T-cell and NK-cell traits were associated with a lower risk of MS, the present MR analysis cannot determine whether these immune phenotypes directly promote remyelination or tissue repair. A more cautious interpretation is that they may reflect immunoregulatory states that limit inflammatory injury and thereby create a more favourable environment for neuroprotection and repair. This hypothesis requires validation in experimental models and longitudinal studies across different MS phenotypes. Similarly, immune reconstitution after alemtuzumab therapy has been linked to changes in NK-cell populations(Rolla et al. 2020) Our MR finding is consistent with the possibility that genetically predicted regulatory NK-cell traits are associated with lower MS risk. However, this result should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and does not establish that this NK-cell subset directly prevents MS.

Compared with previous MR studies that mainly focused on circulating inflammatory proteins or selected immune biomarkers, our study extends the genetic investigation of MS susceptibility to a broad panel of leukocyte indices and 731 immune cell traits. For example, Li et al. reported genetically predicted associations between circulating inflammatory proteins and MS risk, supporting the relevance of systemic immune regulation in MS (Li et al. 2024). Our study complements this evidence by focusing on cellular immune phenotypes, including leukocyte counts and flow-cytometry-derived immune cell traits. In addition, the use of bidirectional MR allowed us to examine whether MS might causally alter peripheral immune profiles. The absence of stable reverse MR evidence suggests that the identified associations are less likely to be fully explained by reverse causation. However, this conclusion remains dependent on the validity and strength of the genetic instruments used.

The clinical implications of this study should be interpreted cautiously. The positive associations of white blood cell and lymphocyte counts with MS risk support the relevance of peripheral immune activation in disease susceptibility. Meanwhile, the inverse associations observed for selected CD4 + T-cell and NK-cell traits suggest that not all immune activation is harmful and that some immune phenotypes may reflect regulatory or protective immune states. This distinction may be important for future biomarker discovery and therapeutic research, as current MS therapies increasingly aim not only to suppress inflammation but also to restore immune balance. Nevertheless, the present MR analysis does not provide direct evidence for treatment efficacy, disease activity prediction, or clinical decision-making. The identified immune traits should therefore be prioritised for validation in independent genetic datasets, longitudinal immune-profiling cohorts, and experimental models.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we performed multiple sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger intercept testing, Cochran’s Q test, MR-PRESSO, and leave-one-out analysis, residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be completely excluded. Some genetic variants may influence MS through pathways other than the measured leukocyte or immune cell traits, which could bias causal estimates. Second, potential sample overlap between exposure and outcome GWAS datasets cannot be fully ruled out. Such overlap may bias MR estimates toward observational associations, particularly when genetic instruments are weak. Third, most GWAS datasets used in this study were derived from individuals of European ancestry, and the immune cell traits GWAS was based on a Sardinian population. Therefore, population stratification and ancestry-specific genetic architecture may limit the generalisability of our findings to non-European populations. Fourth, although F-statistics were used to assess instrument strength, weak instrument bias remains a concern for some immune cell traits because the GWAS sample size for immune phenotypes was relatively modest. Moreover, the selected SNPs may explain only a limited proportion of variance in complex immune cell traits, reducing statistical power and precision. Fifth, immune cell traits were measured in peripheral blood and may not fully reflect immune activity within the central nervous system or during different clinical stages of MS. Finally, MR estimates reflect lifelong genetic predisposition and cannot directly establish biological mechanisms, dynamic immune-cell functions, or therapeutic effects.

In conclusion, this study provides MR-based genetic evidence that higher white blood cell and lymphocyte counts are associated with increased MS risk, whereas selected CD4+ T-cell and NK-cell traits are associated with reduced risk. These findings support a potential role of genetically influenced peripheral immune profiles in MS susceptibility. However, they should be interpreted as MR-based causal evidence related to genetic predisposition rather than direct proof of cellular mechanisms. Further studies integrating larger immune-cell GWAS datasets, multi-ancestry populations, longitudinal immune profiling, and functional experiments are required to clarify the biological and clinical relevance of these associations.

Conclusion

This bidirectional two-sample MR study provides genetic evidence that higher WBC and lymphocyte counts are associated with increased susceptibility to MS, whereas CD25hi CD45RA− CD4 not Treg %CD4+ and CD16 − CD56 on HLA DR+ NK are associated with a lower risk. Reverse MR analyses did not provide robust evidence supporting a causal effect of genetic liability to MS on leukocyte or immune cell traits. These findings highlight the potential contribution of genetically predicted peripheral immune profiles to MS susceptibility. Further validation in independent cohorts and functional studies is warranted to clarify the biological relevance of these associations.
