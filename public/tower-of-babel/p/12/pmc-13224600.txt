How much do lower respiratory tract infections in adults cost? A hospital-based cost analysis in Colombia
Authors: Paula Andrea Castro García, Luisa Fernanda Moyano Ariza, Esteban Orlando Vanegas Duarte, Juliana Quintero Espinosa, Angela Patricia Aguirre Rodríguez, Darío Londoño Trujillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Colombia, lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) are one of the main events of interest to public health. This study aims to describe and quantify the direct medical costs associated with hospital care for LRTI in the Colombian health system.MethodsA retrospective observational study using hospital and national databases was conducted to analyze the direct hospital costs of patients with LRTI from the perspective of the Colombian healthcare system. Data was obtained from hospital records and the Individual Registry of Health Services Provision (RIPS). Cost assessment was performed using national databases (Sufficiency for Health Benefits and the Drug Price Information System - SISMED). Costs were classified as follows: consultations, laboratory and imaging costs, procedures, medications, supplies, and hospital stay, segmented by age groups and diagnostic subgroups. The analysis used measures of central tendency and dispersion.ResultsThe services of 601 patients from a university hospital and 14,979 hospitalization cases registered in RIPS were analyzed. The findings revealed that costs are concentrated primarily in the population over 60 years of age, especially in treating influenza and pneumonia (J09-J18), accounting for more than 70% of the total cost. According to RIPS, the mean cost per hospitalized patient with LRTI was Int$5,657. At the university hospital, the median cost per patient was Int$7,525 (IQR Int$14,346). Medications and hospital stays were the largest components of the total cost. Additionally, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) cases had mean hospital costs per patient of Int$7,340, with medications and hospital stays being the main cost drivers.ConclusionThe significant economic burden of LRTI on the Colombian health system, especially among adults over 60 years of age, highlights the need to strengthen public health policies aimed at their prevention and control. It is recommended to emphasize vaccination strategies, optimization of hospital management, and strengthening epidemiological surveillance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Cost analysis, Costs of illness, Lower acute respiratory infection, Respiratory syncytial virus

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) represent a significant burden for health systems globally, not only in the pediatric population but also in adults over 18 years of age, particularly those with comorbidities or immunosuppression [1, 2]. These infections include clinical conditions such as bronchitis, pneumonia, and exacerbations of chronic respiratory diseases, the care of which generates a significant burden on health systems [1, 3, 4]. Among the most relevant etiological agents is the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), a pathogen that, although traditionally associated with the child population, has gained recognition for its economic impact and for being an important cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in older adults and patients with risk factors [4–6].

Recent studies have shown that RSV hospitalizations in children can be significantly more costly than those caused by other respiratory pathogens due to the need for intensive care, prolonged hospital stays, and management of complications in patients with chronic illnesses [7]. However, this information is not documented for adults. In the Colombian context, some sectoral studies have shown that Acute Respiratory Infection (ARI) in adults represent a growing challenge for the health system, generating high direct and indirect costs associated with hospitalizations, treatments, and years of life lost [8, 9], but to date, there are no known cost studies specific to LRTI with a national scope, which may limit decision-makers ability to implement efficient prevention and control strategies.

A comprehensive approach that analyzes the economic impact of these infections can provide key information for optimizing resource allocation and guiding the implementation of more efficient interventions. The emphasis on RSV as one of the primary causative agents allows for a more specific analysis of how this virus contributes to the overall economic burden of respiratory infections in the adult population. This information is essential for health decision-makers and the development of public policies to prevent and timely manage these diseases [10].

Colombia has a mixed healthcare system that provides universal coverage through two main schemes: the contributory regime, which covers formally employed individuals and their families, and the subsidized regime, which serves those without the ability to pay. Both schemes guarantee access to a comprehensive benefits package, including hospital care, financed primarily with public funds. While the benefits are standardized, actual payment arrangements and negotiated tariffs between insurers and providers may vary across schemes. For this study, cost estimates were derived from national references to reflect average values from the perspective of the Colombian healthcare system.

This article seeks to contribute to the existing literature by describing and estimating the direct medical costs of hospital care associated with treating LRTI in adults aged 18 years and older in Colombia, focusing on RSV infections. Understanding these costs are essential not only for improving the efficiency of the health system but also for informing the economic burden associated with these infections and tailoring public policies that prioritize prevention and appropriate management in the adult population.

Methodology

Design

A retrospective observational study based on hospital and national database records was conducted to estimate the medical costs associated with LRTI in Colombia. No inferential modeling or adjustment for covariates was planned, as the study was not designed to identify causal determinants of cost. The analysis was performed from the perspective of the Colombian health system, considering only direct medical costs derived from inpatient care. These include medications, laboratory tests, hospital stays, consultations, procedures, diagnostic imaging, and therapies, according to the Single Classification of Health Procedures (CUPS) and the Anatomical, Therapeutic, and Chemical Classification System (ATC) for medications. The time horizon corresponded to the hospitalization episode, and no discounting was applied given the short-time frame. This study followed key reporting elements from the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) [11].

Patients and sample

Two data sources were used to construct the study sample. First, all patients over 18 years of age with a diagnosis of LRTI (ICD-10 J09 to J22) admitted to a high-complexity university hospital in Bogotá between January 2018 and December 2024 were included for the micro-costing analysis. Second, national estimates of service utilization were obtained from the Individual Health Service Provision Records (RIPS) database for the year 2022, which was the most recent year with complete national reporting at the time of analysis. RSV cases were identified based on clinical diagnoses recorded in both the national Integrated Social Protection Information System (SISPRO) database and the institutional hospital database, using ICD-10 codes for acute bronchitis due to RSV (J205) and pneumonia due to RSV (J121). These national data were used to contextualize LRTI hospitalizations in Colombia and were not merged with the hospital dataset. The cost analysis included only the resources directly associated with the LRTI hospital episode and excluded outpatient care or post-discharge complications.

Data sources – National databases

This study used information from two national administrative health databases in Colombia, accessed through SISPRO: the RIPS and the Capitation Sufficiency database. Both databases are part of the national health information platform and are populated through mandatory reporting by healthcare providers across the country. These datasets are standardized and coded using ICD-10 for diagnoses, ATC for medications, and CUPS for clinical procedures. For the RIPS database, we accessed individual-level anonymized records of inpatient care, while for the Capitation Sufficiency database, we used publicly available aggregated information (“data cubes”) on hospitalizations due to LRTI.

Sources of cost information

Direct medical costs were identified and measured by analyzing individual patient records from a high-complexity hospital. These records detailed the various health services with their respective CUPS or ATC in the case of medications, as well as the number of services provided and the dispensing units for the medicines. In the case of data from RIPS, information was available on the CUPS and ATC, as well as the quantities demanded and the number of users with the diagnosis of interest in the hospital setting during 2022. No patient-disaggregated information was available in this database, and it was necessary to perform the analyses with aggregated information. Additionally, each database included information on age and diagnosis according to ICD-10.

In Colombia, hospitals are required to report all service encounters to the national RIPS-SISPRO system. Therefore, patients treated in the study hospital may also be partially represented in the national database. However, RIPS applies validity rules (structure, consistency, and completeness checks), and not all hospital-reported encounters pass these filters. Furthermore, once incorporated into RIPS, records are anonymized and aggregated by diagnosis and type of service, without personal identifiers. For this reason, although some overlap is expected, it is not possible to identify or link individual hospital patients within the national dataset.

To ensure consistency across costing sources, a hierarchical approach was applied. When available, unit costs were derived from the technical note of the sufficiency study of the Capitation Payment Unit (UPC) for 2023 [12], which contains the national mean costs for each benefit reported during the immediately preceding year. The UPC acts as the national capitation reference applicable to both Contributory and Subsidized schemes; our costing uses national average references and does not stratify by insurance regime. For services not listed in these database, the National institute of Social Security 2001 tariff manual was used, with an average adjustment of 142% [13], which, despite its age, continues to be used in the country as a reference for evaluating health services. Unit prices for medicines were calculated based on information reported through the institutional channel of the Drug Pricing Information System (SISMED) for 2023. Given the lack of an official database reporting the prices of medical and surgical supplies in Colombia, these were priced based on market value.

Cost estimation

For both databases, the total costs of care were analyzed by age group and type of diagnosis, the distribution of total costs by type of resource, the distribution of total costs among different diagnoses, and the mean cost per patient, age group, and diagnosis. Given the right-skewed distribution of cost data, median and interquartile ranges (IQR) were used as primary measures of central tendency and dispersion. Means and standard deviations (SD) were also reported for comparability. Cost, quartile 1 (Q1) and quartile 3 (Q3), minimum and maximum values were also calculated when data allowed.

Additionally, the total cost was segmented by care category into medications, consultations, hospital stays, laboratory costs, imaging, procedures, materials and supplies, or fees. Diagnoses were grouped into “J09-J18 Influenza and Pneumonia” and “J20-J22 Other acute lower respiratory tract infections” to facilitate analysis. The ages of the patients were also categorized into four large groups: “18 to 26 years old”, “27 to 44 years old”, “45 to 59 years old” and “60 years old or older”.

In the analysis of the hospital’s service provision database, outliers were identified, defined as those total cost values below Q1–1.5 * IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 * IQR. These cases were reviewed to determine whether they represented data inconsistencies or clinically plausible high-resource episodes. No observations were excluded unless a clear recording error was identified. This approach aimed to preserve true high-cost hospitalizations while maintaining transparency in data quality assessment.

Costs were adjusted to 2023 values using inflation adjustment indices [14]. All costs were initially calculated in Colombian pesos (COP) for the price year 2023. For international comparability, costs were converted into international dollars (Int$) using the 2023 purchasing power parity (PPP) conversion factor for Colombia (COP 1,445.98 per Int$) as reported by the World Bank [15]. Therefore, all cost values are presented in 2023 international dollars (Int$).

Results

Data from 601 patients treated at the hospital with a diagnosis of LRTI were analyzed, of whom 76% were over 60 years of age. The total cost of care was Int$11,011,165, with 96.5% of the costs concentrated in cases of influenza and pneumonia (group J09-J18). Cost distribution showed a median of Int$7,525 (IQR Int$14,346) per case, ranging between Int$406 and Int$449,334. Analysis of outlier data revealed that 12% (n = 63) of patients with influenza and pneumonia (J09-J18) had costs exceeding Int$44,290, while in other LRTI (J20-J22), 9% (n = 7) exceeded Int$10,850. Together, the 70 outlier hospitalizations accounted for Int$6,176,170 (56% of the total cost of care). These elevated costs were primarily associated with prolonged hospital stays (> 30 days), intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and complex surgical interventions. All outlier cases were retained in the overall cost analyses to ensure a comprehensive estimate of the economic burden; however, they are described separately here to highlight their disproportionate impact on total expenditures. For the remaining 531 hospitalizations with typical cost profiles, total expenditures amounted to Int$4,834,996 (44% of the total), with a mean cost per patient of Int$9,088 (SD Int$8,734) and a median of Int$5,777 (IQR Int$8,758).

On the other hand, SISPRO data identified 14,979 hospitalized cases nationwide during 2022, of which 66% were in patients over 60 years of age. The total cost was Int$84,743,378, with a mean cost of Int$5,657 per patient. 84% of the costs corresponded to the care of the J09-J18 group of influenza and pneumonia, while the remaining 16% was allocated to the care of the J20-J22 group of the other LRTI. When analyzing costs by age group and diagnostic group in hospital data, it was observed that pneumonia and influenza account for the largest proportion of total spending on LRTI care. This cost is concentrated mainly in the population over 60 years of age, representing 73.5% of the total cost of care. Consistently, in the information from SISPRO, the analysis by age group and diagnosis showed that 70% of the total cost was concentrated in the population over 60 years of age. This is followed by the 45–59 age group and the 27–44 age group, each with a 13% share of the total cost. In contrast, young people aged 19 to 26 accounted for only 6% of spending.

In hospital data, medications represent the largest expense (28%), followed by hospital stays (25%). This distribution is maintained for influenza and pneumonia (J09-J18), but in other LRTI (J20-J22), hospital stay increases to 31%, while medications and laboratory tests represent 20% and 15%, respectively. In SISPRO, medications account for 50% of the total cost, followed by hospital stays (30%). This distribution is maintained for influenza and pneumonia, but in other LRTI, the hospital stay decreases to 26%. In Fig. 1 the participation of each item in the total cost is shown.

Percentage distribution of the total cost by type of resource

According to hospital data, 35% of medication costs were for antibiotics and 6% for antifungals. This distribution was maintained in influenza and pneumonia (J09-J18), but in other LRTI (J20-J22), the cost of antibiotics increased to 45%. In addition, 85% of patients received antibiotics, 12% antiparasitics, 9% antifungals, 6% antivirals, and 4% antimalarials. In SISPRO, the cost of these medications was minimal: antibiotics represented 3.8% of the total cost, antifungals 0.14%, and antivirals 0.01%. However, in other LRTI (J20-J22), antibiotics increased to 5.4%, antifungals to 0.45%, and antivirals to 0.02%.

Analysis of RSV cases

Four cases of RSV were identified at the hospital, with a total cost of Int$84,779 and a mean of Int$21,195 per patient. Hospital stays accounted for 39% of the total cost, medication 22.7%, and laboratory tests 13.5%. In RSV pneumonia, hospitalization accounted for 44% of the total cost and medications 20%, while in RSV bronchitis, these items were 22% and 32%, respectively.

In SISPRO, 31 patients diagnosed with RSV were identified, with an average hospital stay of 18 days. The highest costs were concentrated in the 45 to 59 age groups (46.5%) and those over 60 years old (45.8%). The average cost of treatment was Int$7,340, with a median cost of Int$15,978 for RSV pneumonia and Int$3,227 for RSV bronchitis. Medications accounted for 50% of the total cost, and hospital stay 35%. 6.5% of the cost of medications corresponded to antibiotics, with no identified use of antivirals. In RSV pneumonia, hospital stay increased to 41% of the total cost, while in RSV bronchitis, it decreased to 21%. Given the very small number of hospitalizations coded as RSV, analyses for this subgroup were considered exploratory and descriptive.

Discussion

Hospital costs associated with LRTI vary considerably depending on the diagnostic group and the age of the population served. In this study, the average cost of hospitalization for LRTI in adults was approximately Int$6,000 per patient, with marked differences between diagnostic groups. Patients diagnosed with influenza and pneumonia (J09-J18) had the highest costs, reaching an average of Int$6,900, while those diagnosed with other LRTI (J20-J22) showed a lower average cost of approximately Int$3,000. Furthermore, a significant concentration of costs was identified in patients over 60 years of age, who mostly presented with diagnoses related to influenza or pneumonia, reflecting a greater economic burden in this age group. In both sources analyzed, costs were distributed primarily between hospital stays and medications, demonstrating the impact of these variables on the management of these diagnoses. On the other hand, the specific costs associated with respiratory syncytial virus were similar to those of the influenza and pneumonia group, averaging Int$6,900; however, the small sample size limits the precision of this estimate and suggests further research to better understand its economic impact.

The presence of a small subset of patients with very high hospitalization costs is consistent with the heavy-tailed distribution typically observed in cost-of-illness studies. In our sample, these cases were clinically plausible and mainly related to prolonged hospital stays, intensive care unit (ICU) management, and surgical procedures. Rather than excluding these observations, we retained them in all analyses to ensure that the full economic burden was represented. Their disproportionate contribution—over half of the total cost despite accounting for only 12% of cases—underscores the substantial resource use associated with severe LRTI episodes. These findings highlight the importance of early identification of high-severity cases and suggest that interventions that reduce progression to severe disease may yield significant economic benefits.

While the absolute cost estimates in our study are higher than those reported in previous research, the overall patterns are aligned with international evidence. Similar to Zhang et al. [16], we observed that costs increase substantially with age, and a small proportion of severe cases accounts for a disproportionate share of total expenditures. Likewise, Trucchi et al. [17] documented higher costs among older adults and those with comorbidities, which is consistent with our finding that patients over 60 years represent the largest share of total costs. These parallels suggest that, despite contextual differences in unit prices and costing methodologies, the economic burden of LRTI in adults follows comparable trends across health systems.

In this study, 85% of patients received antibiotics during hospitalization. Although a substantial proportion of LRTIs—especially bronchiolitis and other viral infections—do not require antibacterial therapy, the ICD-10 categories included in our analysis encompass both viral and bacterial conditions. For example, pneumonia (J09–J18) frequently warrants empiric or targeted antibiotic treatment according to clinical guidelines [18]. Therefore, the high proportion of antibiotic use observed cannot be interpreted solely as inappropriate prescribing. Instead, it likely reflects a combination of (i) clinically indicated treatment in bacterial or suspected bacterial infections, (ii) uncertainty in distinguishing viral from bacterial etiologies in routine clinical practice, and (iii) potential overuse in cases where antibiotics offer no benefit. Evidence from Colombia shows that adherence to LRTI and pneumonia management guidelines is suboptimal, with unnecessary or non-recommended antibiotic regimens frequently reported [19]. This context suggests that a portion of antibiotic use may still represent inappropriate prescribing, which has implications for antimicrobial resistance, prolonged length of stay, and increased healthcare costs [20]. Future studies should include clinical or microbiological data to more accurately differentiate justified from unjustified antibiotic use.”

Hospital costs associated with RSV treatment vary widely across countries, reflecting differences in health system structure, pricing policies, and clinical practice. Studies from Asia and Oceania report average hospitalization costs substantially lower than those observed in the United States: China (US$3,123), South Korea (US$2,600), and New Zealand (US$3,450) [21–23]. In contrast, United States estimates often exceed US$20,000 per episode, particularly in severe cases requiring intensive care [24, 25]. Our findings for Colombia fall between these extremes, with costs higher than those reported in Asia and Oceania but markedly lower than U.S. figures. While our RSV results are purely descriptive and exploratory due to the very small sample size, they suggest that universal health coverage may help contain costs compared to predominantly private systems; however, other factors—such as case severity, resource intensity, and hospital complexity—also play a critical role in shaping RSV-related expenditures.

The main strength of this study lies in its methodology, which combines data from national and institutional sources to provide a comprehensive view of the costs associated with hospital care for acute lower respiratory infections. On the one hand, using the national SISPRO database allowed for a broad and representative perspective of costs at the country level, including different regions and providers. On the other hand, hospital records provided a more specific level of detail, including individual clinical characteristics, resource use patterns, and variations in medical practice. This combination of sources not only strengthens the validity of the findings by allowing for comparison across different contexts but also facilitates the identification of cost patterns that might otherwise go unnoticed when analyzing the databases separately. Overall, this methodological approach improves understanding of the economic burden of LRTI and provides a more solid basis for designing health policies and resource allocation strategies.

This study has methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The main limitation is related to the quality and representativeness of the two sources of information used. First, although the -RIPS database has national coverage, it may present inconsistencies resulting from errors in reporting by providers and insurers. Additionally, public access to these databases is limited to aggregated data at the national level on the amounts and values of benefits, without the possibility of analyzing individual details. However, it’s important to mention that this data goes through a validation process that selects only records from institutions with the highest quality reports.

Second, the data obtained from the hospital may not fully represent the national context, considering that they come from a highly complex institution located in the nation’s capital, which has high-quality standards and health accreditations. These characteristics could lead to differences in care patterns and resource utilization compared to other institutions in the country. However, using the Sufficiency Base and SISMED for cost estimation allowed for harmonizing the obtained values, reducing potential disparities, and improving comparability between sources. This methodological approach contributed to maintaining consistency in the costs derived from both sources of information, which supports the validity of the findings presented in this study.

Another significant limitation of this study is the small sample size obtained for the cost estimation in patients with RSV, which could affect the estimates’ accuracy. This limitation is mainly derived from the probable underdiagnosis of RSV infection in the adult population [6] since PCR confirmation is not routinely performed in all patients with respiratory symptoms. The high costs associated with the diagnostic test, which can range between Int$ 523–607 per patient, create an economic barrier that leads to its implementation being restricted to specific cases or epidemiological surveillance protocols. This situation could result in an underestimation of the true burden of the disease and its associated costs, particularly in resource-limited care facilities or regions where access to specialized diagnostic tests is more limited. The limited number of RSV-coded hospitalizations restricts statistical precision and generalizability; therefore, RSV estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Another limitation of this study is the failure to include indirect costs associated with LRTI and RSV, which could be substantial considering the population studied. Our estimates did not include the costs of lost work productivity for working-age adults and the opportunity costs borne by caregivers of older adult patients. In an analysis conducted in South America, acute respiratory infections were estimated to have caused the loss of approximately 465,211 years of productive life in 2019, with a total estimated cost of US$835 million [10]. Zhang et al. [16] They found that indirect costs associated with LRTI in adults can represent between 30% and 40% of the total cost of the disease, mainly due to lost work days and reduced productivity during the recovery period. In a study conducted in India with a cohort of 1,766 patients, Peasah et al. [26] analyzed the economic impact of LRTI, finding that indirect costs associated with the loss of work productivity represented between 16% and 25% of the total cost of the disease. Additionally, they documented that out-of-pocket expenses for hospitalized patients reached 34% of annual per capita income, highlighting the considerable financial burden these infections impose on households. Given the above, excluding these cost components suggests that our estimates may be underestimating the actual economic impact of these pathologies.

This study is subject to several limitations that introduce uncertainty into the estimated costs. As with any cost analysis, variability in clinical practice, tariffs, and reporting quality may lead to under- or overestimation of the true economic burden. The use of hospital billing data and RIPS records carries potential issues such as incomplete reporting, differences in validation filters across institutions, and duplication of encounters at the national level. Additionally, the analysis relied on descriptive statistics without multivariable adjustment and lacked key severity indicators (e.g., ICU stay, mechanical ventilation, length of stay), which limits comparability across patient groups. Future studies should incorporate GLM or other multivariable models, perform sensitivity analyses, and include standardized severity metrics to better account for uncertainty and improve the robustness of the estimates.

The findings of this study have significant implications for health policy and health resource management. Evidence of high hospital costs associated with LRTI and RSV, particularly in vulnerable populations and older adults, suggests the need to strengthen prevention and management strategies, as well as immunization programs. Decision-makers should consider implementing standardized early diagnosis protocols and optimizing care pathways to reduce complications and prolonged hospital stays. These implications are especially relevant in Colombia, where the population is rapidly aging: in 2023, approximately 7.7 million people were aged 60 years or older (15.1% of the national population), according to the National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE) [27]. As the size of this high-risk group continues to expand, the demand for hospital services for LRTI and RSV is expected to increase, suggesting that the economic burden estimated in this study may underestimate future needs and reinforcing the urgency of implementing effective prevention and care strategies.

Additional studies are needed to address important gaps in current literature. It is a priority to develop comprehensive economic evaluations that include cost-effectiveness analyses of the new RSV vaccines in different population groups, considering the direct and indirect costs of the disease. Systematic measurements of indirect costs, including lost work productivity and costs borne by caregivers, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the true economic impact of these conditions. Additionally, longitudinal studies are recommended to evaluate the long-term economic impact of post-infection sequelae and their effect on patients’ quality of life. These studies should incorporate standardized costing methodologies that facilitate comparisons across different contexts and health systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, LRTI represent a significant economic burden for the Colombian healthcare system, especially in adults over 60 years of age. This finding highlights the need to strengthen public health policies focused on prevention and control, emphasizing strategies such as influenza and pneumococcal vaccination in high-risk populations. The evidence presented supports prioritizing this group in immunization programs and adopting cost-effective interventions that contribute to reducing the impact of the disease.
