Design and rationale of the community-led initiative on cardiovascular disease risk reduction among adults in Baltimore: The EngAGE with heart program
Authors: Thomas Hinneh, Faith Elise Metlock, Ebenezer Kobbie Aryee, Patricia Akyeampong, Janice A Dugbartey, Katie Shapcott, Paramroup Kaur Thind, Melissa Gong Mitchell, Stephanie D Adams, Terris King, Yvonne Commodore-Mensah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death in the United States, and Black adults are disproportionately affected. Community-engaged strategies that incorporate cultural, environmental, and social contexts offer a promising opportunity for promoting cardiovascular health equity. This study aims to evaluate the impact of a community-led initiative in reducing CVD risk and improving heart health knowledge and behaviors.MethodsThe EngAGE with Heart Program is a community-led, quasi-experimental intervention co-developed with multisectoral partners to improve the cardiovascular health of 200 adults, 25 years or older, who will be recruited from two senior centers and four faith-based organizations in Baltimore. Participants in the program receive structured health education sessions, CVD risk screening, community outreach with follow-up visits for education and counseling on lifestyle modification from community health ambassadors over a 6-month period, and healthy food and nutrition initiatives over the same 6-month period. Primary outcomes include changes in CVD risk, heart disease risk knowledge, and health behaviors after 3 and 6 months.ConclusionThe EngAGE with Heart Program presents a scalable model for community-centered cardiovascular health promotion. It is designed to generate preliminary findings on the impact of community-led initiatives to reduce disparities, improve health behaviors, and foster sustainable partnerships to address CVD risk in underserved communities.Keywords: Cardiovascular health, Community-based participatory research, Health disparities, Underserved population, Social determinants of health

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, contributing to about 32 % of all deaths. [1] In the US, CVD claims a life approximately every 33 s. [2] National estimates show that over 695,000 people died from CVD in 2021 alone, with about 127 million Americans having one form of CVD. [2] Equally concerning is the disproportionate burden of CVD borne by racial and ethnic minorities, particularly non-Hispanic Black adults, who are at higher risk of CVD mortality compared to non-Hispanic White adults. [3,4] Beyond disease burden, Black and Hispanic adults have lower statin use compared with White adults (Black adults, 20.0 %; Hispanic, 15.4 %; White, 27.9 %) – one example of healthcare disparities among underserved communities in the US. [5,6] Despite the improvement in diagnosis and treatment of traditional CVD risk factors, a substantial number of underserved communities in the US continue to suffer adverse CVD outcomes and have undiagnosed CVD risk factors. [7]

CVD is a major contributor to disparity in life expectancy among Black populations who live in poorer neighborhoods characterized by low household income [8], yet Black patients are less likely to be included in clinical trials and other community-based programs. [9] Persistent inequities in social determinants of health, such as high crime rates, reduced access to high-earning jobs, and limited access to healthy foods, transportation, and housing, contribute to the disparities in poor CVD outcomes in Baltimore City. These factors operate together to create and maintain an environment that engenders adverse cardiovascular outcomes among underserved communities. [10] There is ample evidence highlighting the profound association of unhealthy aging with increased risk of atherosclerosis and CVD. [[11], [12], [13]] Future projections show that CVD will account for almost 40 % of deaths among people older than 65 years by 2030. [11,14] At the same period, about 20 % of the world's population will be older than 65. Without effective community-level intervention, CVD will derail improvement in life expectancy, particularly in communities with significant inequities in access to health care, food insecurity, and structural limitations that impact healthy aging and quality of life.

Engaging multiple stakeholders and community leaders in program design can improve both the environment and the sustainability of interventions targeting populations disproportionately affected by CVD. While community-engaged strategies show promise in addressing social determinants of health, gaps remain in how these interventions leverage resources to tackle systemic disparities. Such programs prioritize community needs and foster authentic collaboration among diverse partners, a critical factor demonstrated in other disease interventions for minoritized groups. However, few community-engaged initiatives in Baltimore have involved multisectoral stakeholders throughout all phases of cardiovascular health interventions. The EngAGE with Heart program fills this gap by partnering with local and national leaders, healthcare providers, and community health ambassadors (CHAs) to improve cardiovascular health among underserved Baltimore communities. This manuscript presents the program's development and evaluates its feasibility and preliminary impact on heart disease risk knowledge and health behaviors.

Methods

Overview of the design

This community-led initiative to reduce CVD risk among older Black adults in underserved areas of Baltimore uses a quasi-experimental design guided by community-based participatory research (CBPR). This approach emphasizes collaboration with community members in developing culturally relevant interventions to address health disparities. Guided by the ethos of community-engaged research, this program exemplifies principles such as working with the community to identify problems that impact cardiovascular health. We exemplify and advance health equity through a community-engaged approach by working with community leaders, rethinking, reimaging, and reigniting to co-create and implement an age-friendly cities pilot program in Baltimore (see Fig. 1). With institutional review board approval, 200 adults are being recruited from four churches and two senior centers. All participants receiving the structured health education component will also complete health screenings and surveys. Each participant will be assigned a unique study identification number to link education attendance with individual-level screening and follow-up data. The primary objective is to assess the program's impact on hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, 3 and 6 months post-baseline. Secondary outcomes include changes in cardiovascular health knowledge, health-related behaviors (such as smoking, physical activity, and dietary habits), and access to care. Focus group discussions and interviews will further explore the acceptability, adoption, and sustainability of the program among stakeholders and participants.

Intervention

Community outreach and structured curriculum-based health education

Population-level CVD risk is amenable to health education strategies targeted at addressing social determinants of health that influence behavior change. [15] The educational aspect of the engAGE with Heart program seeks to integrate educational campaigns that prioritize community-centered health promotion initiatives, aiming to fill knowledge gaps in CVD risk awareness. The health education component includes frequent community outreach activities to promote awareness and distribute resources about CVD prevention and self-management in the community. The engAGE with Heart program will implement its structured, outcome-based, educational curriculum through diverse methods to maximize engagement and impact (Fig. 2). These methods will range from innovative approaches, such as seated cardio classes for seniors and heart health bingo, to more traditional methods, including presentations by specialist speakers at events and door-to-door canvassing with informational flyers.

Educational curriculum for community health ambassadors.

CHA training and intervention

The CHA management was led by the High Impact Network of Responsible Innovators (HINRI), who leveraged a hyperlocal model to engage community leaders and develop effective outreach strategies. With 200 h of heart health-specific training, 380 hours of program collaboration, and resource toolkits, the 20 CHAs will be prepared to engage and educate the community confidently. The training program will begin with the development of essential soft skills based on the Be REAL framework (26), digital skills, and an introduction to the program's background, objectives, and various components. This foundational training is crucial for building programmatic knowledge and developing the interpersonal skills necessary for effective community engagement. Following the initial training, CHAs will participate in specialized engAGE with Heart curriculum sessions focused on CVD and its risk factors. This phase of training will include scenario-based sessions, allowing CHAs to practice real-life situations they may encounter while working in the community. Additionally, group meetings will be held regularly to provide ongoing support for CHAs.

Healthy food initiatives, food lessons, and nutrition education

The EngAGE with Heart program will offer a comprehensive healthy food program featuring fresh food markets, cooking demonstrations, and heart-healthy recipes. To provide healthy and culturally appropriate food options, the program will work with local nutritionists, chefs, and church culinary teams. The program will also partner with the Black Church Food Security Network and Hungry Harvest to bring fresh fruits and vegetables to community sites. The Black Church Food Security Network connects local Black farmers with communities to offer fresh produce at fair prices. Chefs, church cooks, and CHAs will help create healthy, culturally familiar recipes, which will be shared in a community recipe booklet. The program will also include cooking demonstrations and nutrition education to give participants practical skills and support healthy eating habits in the long term. Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants will be residents of the Baltimore area identified at one of the senior centers or church sites and at least 25 years of age as of the date of first contact. People with serious cognitive impairment or other conditions (e.g., severe hearing or vision impairments, acute psychiatric illness, or terminal illness) and those who are not available at the senior center or their respective church site or who are unwilling to provide informed consent will be excluded from the study.

Study setting

This community-based intervention will be conducted in the Baltimore metropolitan area, a racially diverse city where approximately 64 % of the population identifies as Black, 29 % as non-Hispanic White, and about 7 % as other races. The two senior centers and four faith-based organizations where the intervention will be implemented were selected based on established relationships with the research team and their trusted role within the community, which facilitates strong engagement and effective program delivery.

Recruitment

Program announcements will be made during community gatherings, and flyers will be placed at frequented locations to promote awareness. Screening will be open to everyone; however, only those who meet the eligibility criteria will be enrolled and followed throughout the study. Eligible participants will provide informed consent and complete baseline surveys. Triaging lists will be generated from the screening data, and participants with elevated health risks will be followed up by CHAs. Participants will be referred to their primary care providers or if uninsured, referred to community health services if any of the clinical measures are elevated( BP, lipids, HBA1c) The overall screening process is shown in Fig. 3.

Overall flow of the intervention

The intervention will consist of monthly education and support sessions delivered in community settings and facilitated by community health assistants (CHAs) over a six-month period. Each session will last at least one hour and include cardiovascular disease (CVD) counseling, food demonstrations by local farmers on preparing healthy meals, and periodic counseling by dietitians. To promote retention, CHAs send reminders to participants prior to each session. In addition, participants without a primary care provider will be connected to CHAs, who will ensure that they are appropriately linked to care.

Data collection

Data will be collected at three time points: baseline, 3- and 6-months post-baseline. We will measure biomedical indices (blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c, random plasma glucose (RPG)) and anthropometric measures (weight, self-reported height) that have been shown to predict CVD risk (Table 2). We are use validated A1CNow®+ multi test system to measure HBA1c, and CLIA-waived Cholestech LDX™ to measure RPG and blood lipids. The accuracy and precision of the Cholestech LDX™ have been shown in earlier studies, and it has been approved for use in non-clinical settings [16,17]. For blood pressure, we clarified that we will take three measurements per participant using validated devices and proper cuff size, following the American Heart Association guidelines (resting quietly, correct positioning, and repeated readings 1 minute apart). Screening results will be recorded on a report card and shared with participants to establish follow-up and referral system. The Heart Disease Facts Questionnaire will be administered at baseline and at six months post-baseline to evaluate changes in participants’ knowledge of CVD [18]. We will use the Accountable Health Communities Health-Related Social Needs tool developed by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services to assess social determinants of health. [19]This 10-item screening tool identifies needs in five key areas: housing instability, food insecurity, transportation challenges, utility assistance, interpersonal safety, and financial strain.

Participant follow-up and retention

Participants will be contacted for follow-up data collection using multiple methods, including phone calls, text message reminders, and in-person visits by CHAs. These follow-up visits will take place at the same community churches and senior centers where the baseline data were collected to make participation more convenient. The CHAs will maintain regular communication with participants to support continued engagement and reduce the likelihood of missing follow-up data.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of the study is the measurable reduction in CVD risk among participants at 6 months (clinical indicators such as blood pressure, HbA1c, and lipid profiles). Secondary endpoints include increased cardiovascular health knowledge, improved health behaviors (diet, physical activity, smoking cessation, body mass index), and linkage to care at 3 and 6 months.

Program acceptability will be evaluated using both quantitative and qualitative indicators. Quantitative measures will include retention, loss to follow-up, number of participants linked to care, and the number of community health workers engaged in the intervention. Qualitative data from participant interviews and focus group discussions will complement these analyses by exploring perceived barriers, facilitators, and recommendations for program improvement.

Qualitative component

To complement the quantitative analysis, we will conduct focus group discussions with participants (n ≈ 12) and in-depth interviews with key stakeholders (n ≈ 4) who express interest in participating in the qualitative portion of the study. These discussions and interviews will explore perceptions of program acceptability, adoption, and opportunities for improvement using qualitative guides developed along these constructs. The sessions will be moderated by trained personnel, and each section will run for a maximum of 60 min.

Statistical analysis plan

We will describe the participant characteristics, including demographic, clinical, and behavioral variables, as well as measures related to cardiovascular health (blood pressure, blood sugar, lipid profiles, and heart disease knowledge). Categorical variables will be summarized using percentages, while continuous variables will be reported using means and standard deviations when normally distributed, or medians and interquartile ranges for skewed data. Initial bivariate analyses will explore associations between participant characteristics and outcome measures to identify variables for possible adjustment in further analyses. For continuous outcomes, bivariate analysis will use t-tests, ANOVA, or nonparametric equivalents and simple linear regressions; for categorical outcomes, chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests will be used as appropriate. Variables with a bivariate association at p < 0.20 will be considered candidate covariates for adjusted models. For acceptability measures, the outcomes will be assessed at baseline and at the end of the intervention and analyzed descriptively, with comparisons over time using chi-square or t-tests as appropriate.

To analyze changes over time and account for repeated measurements within participants, we will apply mixed-effects regression models suitable for the outcome type. These models will include fixed effects for time, intervention exposure, and other covariates identified from initial analyses. Random effects will capture participant-specific variability.

All interviews and focus groups will be audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic content analysis. Two independent coders will review transcripts, develop a coding framework, and identify recurring themes. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, and findings will be triangulated across participants and stakeholders to capture diverse perspectives on program feasibility and acceptability.

Discussion

The EngAGE with Heart Program is a community-led initiative in underserved Black communities that comprises community outreach and structured curriculum-based health education, healthy food initiatives, and community–based CVD risk screening at churches and senior centers in Baltimore. This program is a pilot implementation to address disparities in cardiovascular health through CBPR to generate preliminary evidence to inform the extension of the program to other underserved communities in the US. [21]. The EngAGE with Heart Program is designed to address community-identified problems and barriers and implement evidence-based context-appropriate approaches aimed at increasing awareness of CVD risk factors, promoting access to healthy foods and health services, and providing resources that are beneficial to improving cardiovascular health. The end goal is to measure the effect of the program on physical and biomedical measures, patient behaviors, and improvement in heart disease knowledge about cardiovascular health during the period.

We are leaning on our earlier lessons of building bidirectional community trust and shared leadership with the community and religious leaders to implement a sustainable and context-acceptable program in predominantly underserved communities. [22,23] A hallmark of the program is its commitment to mutual benefit, ensuring that interventions serve the needs and interests of both the community and stakeholders. The EngAGE with Heart program is conceived with a vision of sustainability, aiming to establish a replicable model for cardiovascular health promotion across Maryland and beyond. Through partnerships with churches and senior centers, the program aims to embed its initiatives into existing community structures, ensuring long-term sustainability beyond the initial implementation phase.

Our community-engaged program is innovative in many ways. First, we have developed a community-academic-government-industry partnership to co-design and pilot this intervention among underserved Black populations to address social determinants of health that influence cardiovascular health. We are working with CHAs who are trusted by the communities in all components of the program to drive community engagement activities and support follow-up activities. The success of the EngAGE with Heart program hinges on continued partnerships spanning various sectors, including academia, industry, and government. Through the integration of diverse perspectives and expertise, the program adopts a comprehensive approach to cardiovascular health promotion. These partnerships foster collaboration, innovation, and resource-sharing, amplifying the program's impact and reach within the community. We are leveraging and mobilizing local resources effectively through partnering with organizations like the Black Church Food Security Network, and existing community networks to promote healthy behaviors. Hosting events at familiar community sites further maximizes resource utilization, demonstrating the potential for grassroots initiatives to drive positive health outcomes through collaborative efforts.

To assess the effectiveness and sustainability of the EngAGE with Heart program, we will conduct a comprehensive program evaluation focusing on both health outcomes and implementation processes. We will examine changes in biomedical and behavioral outcomes, including changes in blood pressure, blood glucose, lipid levels, and lifestyle behaviors, from baseline, three months, and six months. Additionally, we will assess improvement in participants’ cardiovascular health knowledge and awareness at the different time points specified. Qualitative insights from focus group discussions and stakeholder interviews will help evaluate program acceptability, adoption, and areas for improvement.

However, we envision some challenges in the execution of the program. First, recruitment of eligible participants into the program may be challenging given that these activities may occur mostly on Sundays for the church sites. Potentially eligible participants may be missed if they are unable to attend church services on designated Sundays where recruitment activities may occur. To address this, we are working with church leaders and CHAs to disseminate information about events to potential participants in advance, to address low recruitment turnout as well as loss to follow-up. Second, there is a possibility of higher turnout for screening at churches with larger congregations, which may override the capacity of the health team to conduct timely physical and biomedical measurements of all eligible participants. To mitigate this challenge, additional trained student nurses will be recruited to perform some of the screening tasks to maximize time for counseling and health education.

At the core of the program lies a commitment to building the capacity of community members to become active agents of change. Through initiatives such as the CHAs, individuals are empowered to lead health promotion activities within their communities. Providing training and support will foster a sense of ownership and responsibility among participants, facilitating sustainable behavior change and health outcomes. Overall, the EngAGE with Heart program aims to improve cardiovascular health by piloting community-engaged sustainable strategies to address disparities and prevent CVD among underserved Black populations in Baltimore. Transparency and community engagement are central to the program's approach to dissemination. Findings and outcomes are shared directly with community members, empowering them with knowledge and insights. Through channels such as the CHAs, residents are actively involved in all the processes, shaping the narrative and outcomes of the program.

Strengths and limitations

The study benefits from recruiting participants through churches and senior centers, trusted community venues that facilitate engagement with underserved Black adults and enhance cultural relevance. However, a limitation is that this sample may not fully represent the entire target population, as individuals who do not attend these community settings are less likely to be included. This may introduce selection bias, given that participants who attend such programs might be more motivated or resourceful than those who do not, which could affect the generalizability of the findings. To mitigate this, the study leverages CHAs who have strong ties to the community, helping to broaden outreach and encourage participation across diverse groups. Another limitation lies in the quasi-experimental, observational design, which limits causal inference due to potential confounding and lack of randomization. Despite these limitations, the current study provides important preliminary insights into community-engaged cardiovascular health interventions in underserved populations.
