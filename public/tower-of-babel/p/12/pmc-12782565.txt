Transfer RNA-derived small RNAs as novel players and biomarkers in cardiovascular disease
Authors: Qiuwang Zhang, Weifang Li, Rachel X. Chang, Michael J.B. Kutryk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An emerging field in cardiovascular research is the translational investigation of transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs). TsRNAs, a class of small non-coding RNA molecules, have been shown to modulate cellular functions by regulating gene expression post-transcriptionally. They are implicated in diverse pathological conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), infectious disease, diabetes, neurological disease, and metabolic disorder. Accumulating evidence suggests tsRNAs as important players and biomarkers in CVD. Dysregulated tsRNAs are identified in atherosclerosis, heart failure, hypertension and other types of CVD. Bioinformatics and in vitro experimental analyses reveal that tsRNAs may participate in the regulation of endothelial and inflammatory cell interactions, endothelial cell and vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, and cardiac metabolism, mitophagy and remodeling, contributing to the pathogenesis of CVD. In addition, altered tsRNAs possess great diagnostic and prognostic potential in CVD. Nevertheless, there are currently no in vivo mechanistic studies using animal models, and the small sizes of reported clinical studies that examined tsRNAs limit their diagnostic and prognostic value. Although of promise, further research is needed to address the utility of tsRNAs in cardiovascular care.

Introduction

Transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs), also termed tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) or tRNA halves, are small non-coding RNA molecules generated by ribonuclease cleavage of transfer RNAs (tRNAs) [1, 2]. According to the parental tRNA, tsRNAs are classified into type I and type II tsRNAs derived from mature tRNAs and pre-tRNAs, respectively; based on the cleavage site, tsRNAs are categorized into 5′tsRNAs, 3′tsRNAs, and i-tRFs (internal tRFs) [3, 4]. First discovered over four decades ago in cancer [5, 6], tsRNAs were not recognized as significant gene regulators until the early 2000s [7–9]. Since then, tsRNAs have emerged as critical modulators of cellular functions [10]. They can inhibit gene expression post-transcriptionally and play an important role in a wide range of biological processes, including cell proliferation [11, 12], apoptosis and differentiation [13–16], development [17, 18], and metabolism [19, 20].

Transfer RNA-derived small RNAs are implicated in diverse pathological conditions, including cancer [21, 22], infectious disease [23, 24], neurological disease [25, 26], cardiovascular disease (CVD) [27, 28], diabetes [29, 30], and metabolic disorder [31]. Although significant progress has been made in cardiovascular care in the past decades, CVD remains the leading cause of death worldwide. Current challenges in CVD diagnosis and management, such as failure to diagnose or diagnostic errors and limited cardiovascular drug development, necessitate the search for new diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets [32]. An emerging field in cardiovascular medicine is the study of tsRNAs. In this article, we aim to summarize current knowledge of tsRNAs as novel players and biomarkers in CVD, highlight the existing issues in the research field, and provide suggestions for future studies. There is no current consensus on nomenclature and tsRNAs, tRFs and tRNA halves are interchangeably used in the literature. Therefore, we include here the naming convention applied in each study we referenced.

Transfer RNA-derived small RNA biogenesis

Transfer RNA-derived small RNA biogenesis is heterogeneous across organisms. In humans, angiogenin and Dicer are two primary ribonucleases recognized to be responsible for tsRNA production [7, 18, 33]. Angiogenin, a ribonuclease induced by cellular stress, is involved in generating tRNA halves that are half the size of mature tRNAs as a result of cleavage at or within the anticodon of tRNAs [7, 33]. Therefore, tRNA halves are also termed tRNA-derived, stress-induced RNAs (tiRNAs). Yamasaki et al. [7] detected small RNA molecules of approximately 30–50 nucleotides in length originating from tRNAs in human osteosarcoma epithelial cells exposed to arsenite, heat shock or UV irradiation. Arsenite-induced tiRNA production was repressed by the knockdown of angiogenin, while enhanced by the knockdown of RNH1, an inhibitor of angiogenin; in addition, the culture of cells with wild-type angiogenin protein, but not with an inactive mutant, led to the production of tiRNAs [7]. Another study demonstrated that stress factors, i.e., serum starvation, heat shock, hypothermia, or hypoxia promoted tiRNA production in human HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells. Moreover, total RNA isolated from HepG2 cells incubated with angiogenin gave rise to tRNA halves [33]. In contrast, Dicer cleaves tRNAs to yield tRFs that are shorter than 30 nucleotides. It was shown that suppression of Dicer with siRNA in Hela and HEK293 cells led to a significant decrease in tRFs derived from a pre-tRNA (tRNAGln), and incubation of tRNAGln with Dicer yielded tRFs [34]. Dicer-dependent tRF production in other human cell types was also described [18, 35].

The enzymatic cleavage of tRNAs can occur at various sites, resulting in different tRFs [4]. tRF-5s or 5′tRFs are generated when a cleavage at the D-loop or in the stem region between the D-loop and the anti-codon loop occurs. Thereby, 5′tRFs contain the 5′ termini of tRNAs. In contrast, a cleavage at the T-loop produces tRF-3s or 3′tRFs that carry the 3′ termini of tRNAs [4]. tRF-5s and tRF-3s are also classified as tRF1. When two cleavages occur with one at the T loop and the other at the D loop, a fragment termed tRF2 or i-tRF that lacks both the 5′ and 3′ termini are generated [4]. Details of tsRNA biogenesis have been reviewed in several recently published articles [1, 2, 4].

Transfer RNA-derived small RNA regulation of gene expression

Although the regulatory mechanisms of tsRNAs are not fully understood, three pathways by which tsRNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally have been described (Fig. 1).

tsRNAs silence genes by destabilizing mRNAs. Several groups show that tsRNAs directly bind to target mRNA sequences complementarily [3, 36, 37]. The tsRNA-mRNA duplexes are then loaded into argonaute proteins, leading to mRNA degradation, a mechanism of action similar to that of miroRNAs (Fig. 1). This mechanism is further supported by computational analysis of the high-throughput sequencing datasets generated from HEK293 cells, this demonstrated that tsRNAs contained canonical seed sequences essential for mRNA binding [38]. A procedure termed cross-linking ligation and sequencing of hybrids (CLASH) was developed to study RNA-RNA interactions [39]. By analyzing CLASH datasets of HEK293 cells, Kumar et al. [38] identified approximately 40,000 tsRNA-mRNA chimeras, suggesting abundant direct binding between tsRNAs and mRNAs. tsRNAs can also induce mRNA degradation by disengaging stabilizing factors from mRNAs (Fig. 1). Goodarzi et al. [40] showed that tRFs displaced YBX1, an RNA-binding protein and stabilizer, from the 3′-untranslated region of mRNAs in breast cancer cells, resulting in mRNA decay.

In contrast to the inhibitory effects described, tsRNAs can also activate the translation of ribosomal proteins to enhance ribosome biogenesis, upregulating gene expression [44, 45]. With the exploration of the role of tsRNAs in cancer cell growth, Kim et al. [44] discovered that the tsRNA LeuCAG3′tsRNA upregulated the viability of HeLa and human colorectal carcinoma cells. Further examination with in vitro translation and the luciferase reporter assay revealed that LeuCAG3′tsRNA maintains the translation of ribosomal protein S28 (RPS28), a component of the 40S ribosome that is essential for the biogenesis of 18s rRNA. Inhibition of LeuCAG3′tsRNA reduced RPS28 expression and resulted in reduced 18s rRNA processing and lower numbers of 40S ribosomal subunits. Finally, RNA-RNA hybridization analysis unveiled two potential LeuCAG3′tsRNA binding sites in the RPS28 mRNA [44]. These findings indicate LeuCAG3′tsRNA may directly bind to RPS28 mRNA to promote translation. In addition, tsRNAs are shown to activate translation independent of mRNA binding (Fig. 1). Keam et al. [45] demonstrated that tsRNA 5′tRF Gln19 did not bind to target mRNAs but interacted with the mammalian multisynthetase complex to enhance ribosomal and poly(A)-binding protein translation in HeLa cells.

Literature search

An emerging field in cardiovascular research is the translational investigation of transfer RNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs). To conduct this review study, the PubMed and Scopus databases were searched using tRNA-derived small RNAs, tRNA fragments, or tRNA halves in combination with one of the following terms: cardiovascular disease, atherosclerosis, heart failure, hypertension, myocarditis, pathogenesis, and biomarkers. The search included studies published between January 2010 and May 2024. Original research articles and review papers focusing on tsRNAs in CVDs were included in this narrative review. Articles not written in English, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, and studies lacking relevance to tsRNA-CVD associations were excluded. A total of 13 clinical studies are included (Tab. 1 and 2).

Transfer RNA-derived small RNA as players in CVD

Accumulating evidence, mainly from clinical studies (Tab. 1), suggests a role for tsRNAs in the pathophysiology of CVD, which is presented in the central illustration. RNA sequencing and microarray assays are currently the most common methods used to screen for dysregulated tsRNAs that are further validated by RT-qPCR. To evaluate tsRNA functions in CVD, most studies rely on bioinformatic analyses to uncover the potential target genes and pathways affected by altered tsRNAs.

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ACVD)

tsRNA involvement in ACVD has been investigated in animal models and patients. Using PANDORA-sequencing technology, Hernandez et al. [46] identified 195 dysregulated tsRNAs in the atherosclerotic intima of LDL-receptor–deficient (Ldlr−/−) mice fed with high-cholesterol diet (HCD). Overexpression of tsRNA-Arg-CCG, one of the increased tsRNAs in HCD mice, upregulated proatherogenic genes, i.e., IL-6, IL-1α, ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and MCP-1 in human microvascular endothelial cells (HMECs). Therefore, tsRNA-Arg-CCG may contribute to ACVD by dysregulating EC gene expression, causing endothelial dysfunction [46]. Vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) migration and proliferation and resultant intimal hyperplasia is a hallmark of vascular injury including ACVD. tRFGlnCTG, a tsRNA selectively produced in VSMCs, promotes rat VSMC migration and proliferation [47]. Mechanistically, tRFGlnCTG inhibits the expression of the FAS receptor. In another study, tRF-Glu-CTC was shown to increase rat VSMC proliferation and migration via downregulating fibromodulin, a negative regulator of TGF-β1 signaling [48]. Therefore, tRFs may participate in atherogenesis by modulating VSMC activities, however this hypothesis remains to be explored.

There have been reports of tsRNA dysregulation in patients with ACVD [28, 49]. Wang et al. [28] comparatively profiled tsRNAs in patients and control subjects by RNA sequencing. They discovered two upregulated and 11 downregulated tsRNAs in the atherosclerotic carotid artery; the upregulation of tRF-Gly-GCC-009 and tRF-Gly-GCC-008, and the downregulation of tRF-Pro-AGG-006, tRF-Pro-AGG-005, tRF-Val-AAC-014 and tRF-Lys-CTT-006 were confirmed by RT-qPCR, with tRF-Gly-GCC-009 being further assessed with bioinformatics analysis. The target genes of tRF-Gly-GCC-009 were primarily involved in cell adhesion, calcium ion binding and regulation of cellular components according to gene ontology (GO) annotations. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment showed that tRF-Gly-GCC-009 targets were enriched in the Apelin/APJ, calcium, and Notch signaling pathways [28].

In another study, He et al. [49] identified 131 upregulated and 184 downregulated tsRNAs in patients’ atherosclerotic carotid arteries by RNA sequencing. The increase of tRF-1:28-Gly-GCC-4, tRF-1:31-Glu-TTC-2, tRF-1:16-Gly-GCC-1, tRF-+1:T23-Arg-TCG-1, tRF-51:71-chrM.Pro-TGG, and tRF-53:71-chrM.Gly-TCC was further validated by RT-qPCR. These tsRNAs regulate a total of 378 genes as identified by the target prediction algorithms, i.e., TargetScan and miRanda. Most notably, target genes MYC and KIF20A of tRF-1:28-Gly-GCC-4, and CDK1 and ORL1 of tRF-51:71-chrM.Pro-TGG are strongly associated with cell proliferation in ACVD [49]. Gain- and loss-of-function assays showed that overexpression of tRF-1:28-Gly-GCC-4 in human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) enhanced adhesion between HUVECs and THP-1 monocytic cells. Conversely, inhibition of tRF-1:28-Gly-GCC-4 in HUVECs decreased cell adhesion. In addition, tRF-1:28-Gly-GCC-4 augmented HUVEC migration and proliferation and upregulated ICAM-1 expression. Notably, tRF-1:28-Gly-GCC-4 also promoted human VSMC proliferation and migration [49]. Taken together, tsRNAs may participate in atherogenesis by regulating adhesion between ECs and monocytes and modulating EC and VSMC activities.

Heart failure

In a rat model of advanced heart failure (HF) induced by isoproterenol, tsRNA dysregulation was observed in the hypertrophied heart, with tRNA-derived fragments1 (tRFs1) and tRFs2 being the two most upregulated [50]. It was further demonstrated that overexpression of tRFs1 or tRFs2 in rat H9c2 cardiomyocytes increased cell surface area and enhanced the production of hypertrophic markers, i.e., ANF, BNP, and β-MHC. Moreover, sequence alignment identified two potential tRFs1 binding sites in the 3′-untranslated region of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 3 (Timp3), a critical regulator of extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. These findings were further confirmed by the luciferase reporter assay showing that tRF1 repressed Timp3 expression by directly targeting the 3′-untranslated region of Timp3 [50]. Timp3 protects against HF by inhibiting TNF-α activity and matrix degradation induced by matrix metalloproteinases. Mice with Timp3 knockout had increased myocardial TNF-α and ECM degradation associated with higher gelatinase and collagenase activities in an aortic banding, cardiac pressure overload induced cardiac hypertrophy HF model [51]. Genetic ablation of TNF-α combined with matrix metalloproteinase inhibition completely rescued HF induced by aortic banding in Timp3(−/−) mice [51].

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) has been implicated in the pathogenesis of HF [52]. A recent study identified a total of 24 differentially expressed EAT tsRNAs, with 17 being upregulated and 7 downregulated by RNA sequencing in HF patients compared to controls [53]. A significant increase in tiRNA-Pro-TGG-001 and tRF-Met-CAT-002, and a decrease in tRF-Tyr-GTA-010 and tRF-Tyr-GTA-011 in HF were confirmed by RT-qPCR. tRF-Tyr-GTA-010 and tRF-Tyr-GTA-011 were further studied with bioinformatics analysis. These two tsRNAs targeted genes associated with Ca2+ transport, nitric-oxide synthase binding, and cellular components of the somatodendritic compartment [53]. Ca2+ regulates cardiac muscle contraction and electrical signals. Disrupted Ca2+ homeostasis in cardiomyocytes serves as a major contributor to HF [54], while eNOS activity in both the coronary and systemic vasculature is impaired in HF [55, 56]. The top three pathways impacted by tRF-Tyr-GTA-010 and tRF-Tyr-GTA-011 were involved in adrenergic signaling, sphingolipid metabolism, and mRNA surveillance. The role of adrenergic signaling in HF is well established [57]. Sphingolipids and their metabolites are also implicated in HF. Law et al. [58] showed that ceramide, a metabolite of sphingolipids, caused mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cell death in cultured human cardiomyocytes. Accordingly, in a mouse model of HF, reduced de novo ceramide biosynthesis protected against HF [59]. Yu et al. [60] showed that patients with chronic HF had a higher level of plasma ceramide that was positively associated with mortality.

Substantially lower levels of plasma tRF-30-3JVIJMRPFQ5D, tRF-16-R29P4PE, tRF-21-NB8PLML3E, and tRF-21-SWRYVMMV0 in HF patients were detected in another study [61]. GO annotations demonstrated that target genes of tRF-21-NB8PLML3E were responsible for the biogenesis of the ribosome and Golgi body. An analysis of the Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes enrichment found that the top three pathways influenced by tRF-21-NB8PLML3E were associated with sphingolipid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, and mitophagy. Strikingly, the sphingolipid metabolism pathway was also affected by tRF-Tyr-GTA-010 and tRF-Tyr-GTA-011, two tsRNAs downregulated in HF as described above [53]. Therefore, tRF-21-NB8PLML3E, tRF-Tyr-GTA-010, and tRF-Tyr-GTA-011 may contribute to HF pathogenesis by targeting a common pathway related to sphingolipid metabolism. Mitophagy, a process to remove damaged mitochondria, is essential for maintaining normal cellular function. In the heart, abnormal mitophagy can lead to cardiomyocyte dysfunction and eventually the development of HF [62]. Experimental analysis demonstrated that overexpression of tRF-21-NB8PLML3E attenuated Ang II-induced hypertrophy of rat H9c2 cardiomyocytes [61]. Collectively, tsRNAs may play a role in the pathophysiology of HF by regulating cardiac metabolism, mitophagy, and remodeling.

Hypertension

The kidney plays a central role in the regulation of arterial blood pressure [63, 64]. Pan et al. [65] revealed different renal tsRNA spectrums in Dahl salt-sensitive (SS) rats and their salt-insensitive congenic counterparts (L26). High dietary salt significantly upregulated 3′-S-mtdbD00000840-Trp-TCA_L17 but downregulated 3′-S-mtdbD00000854-Thr-TGT_L16, 3′-M-trna719-Lys-CTT_L14, 3′-S-trna2250-Gly-TCC_L15, 3′-M-trna917-Pro-TGG_L19, 3′-M-trna6010-Ile-AAT_L15, 5′-M-trna8791-His-GTG_L28, 5′-M-trna8791-His-GTG_L29, and 5′-M-trna6038-Glu-TTC_L29 in SS rats. Instead, high salt downregulated 5′-M-trna3490-Gly-GCC_L18 in L26 rats [65]. Of interest, there were two common pathways, i.e., lymphocyte activation and immune response pathways among the top three pathways enriched in 3′-S-mtdbD00000840-Trp-TCA_L17 and 5′-M-trna3490-Gly-GCC_L18 [65].

Both B and T lymphocytes are involved in the development of hypertension and renal disease [66, 67]. Mice lacking B lymphocytes have lower systolic and diastolic blood pressure associated with decreased vasopressin receptor 2 in kidney medulla compared with wild type littermates [66]. High dietary salt increases renal infiltration of T lymphocytes that produce ANG II resulting in blood pressure elevation in SS rats. Accordingly, administration of the immunosuppressive agent mycophenolate mofetil prevented the infiltration of T lymphocytes and attenuated hypertension and renal disease [67].

Human kidney biopsy specimens from patients with hypertensive nephrosclerosis and control patients with minimal or no renal injury exhibit distinct tsRNA profiles [65]. Patients with hypertensive nephrosclerosis have markedly higher levels of 3′-M-tRNA-Pro-TGG-3-5_L15, 3′-M-tRNA-Pro-TGG-3-5_L19, 3′-M-tRNA-Pro-TGG-3-5_L20, 3′-M-tRNA-Val-CAC-5-1_L20, and 5′-M-tRNA-Ser-TGA-4-1_L16 but lower 3′-S-mtdbD00000528-Val-TAC_L15, 3′-S-mtdbD00000528-Val-TAC_L17, 3′-S-mtdbD00000528-Val-TAC_L23, 3′-S-mtdbD00000528-Val-TAC_L25, and 3′-S-mtdbD00000547-Thr-TGT_L15 [65]. Interestingly, 3′-M-tRNA-Pro-TGG-3-5_L19 was found to be dysregulated in both hypertensive rats and humans. Sequence pairing identified 10 potential target genes of 3′-M-tRNA-Pro-TGG-3-5_L19, including Scn5a, Kcnma1, Brd2, Kctd17, Arf5, Extl1, Cabin1, Unkl, Lrtm2, and Csdc2. Overexpression of 3′-M-tRNA-Pro-TGG-3-5_L19 in human kidney epithelial HK-2 cells repressed expression of Kcnma1, Brd2, Arf5, Cabin1, Unkl, and Csdc2 [65]. However, functions of these genes in hypertension are scarcely described. Kcnma1 is a subunit of the calcium–activated potassium channel; its genetic variations can cause defective channel activity in humans associated with hypertension that might be a result of renal potassium retention [68]. Additionally, Kcnma1 reduction was observed in kidney of spontaneously hypertensive rats and renal Kcnma1 levels were found inversely correlated with systolic blood pressure in humans [69]. In summary, renal tsRNA dysregulation may result in increased lymphocyte infiltration in kidney, disturbed immune response and defective renal ion channel activity, contributing to the pathophysiology of hypertension.

Idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension

Idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) is a rare and devastating disease characterized by hyperproliferation of ECs and VSMCs in small pulmonary arteries resulting in lumen occlusion. The pathogenesis of IPAH involves complex interactions of resident vascular cells with infiltrating inflammatory and immune cells in the lungs that have yet to be fully explored [70, 71].

tsRNAs may play a role in IPAH. Microarray analysis has identified 243 upregulated and 573 downregulated plasma tsRNAs in patients with IPAH. tRF3a-AspGTC-9, 5′tiRNA-31-GluCTC-16, i-tRF-31:54-Val-CAC-1, and tRF3b-TyrGTA-4 are upregulated, while 5′tiRNA-33-LysTTT-4, i-tRF-8:32-Val-AAC-2, i-tRF-2:30-His-GTG-1, and i-tRF-15:31-Lys-CTT-1 are downregulated as assayed by RT-qPCR [72]. The validated tsRNAs contain seed sequences for the binding of transcripts of their respective target genes, including BMPR2, SMAD9, KCNK3, AQP1, SOX17, STK40, PPP4R3A, and OR6C76 [72]. The target genes of the dysregulated tsRNAs are enriched in an array of pathways, including the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), BMPR2, and HIF-1 signaling pathways implicated in IPAH pathogenesis [73–75].

Aortic dissection

Aortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening condition that results from a tear in the intimal layer of the aorta, followed by the formation and propagation of a subintimal hematoma [76]. The pathogenesis of AD is multifaceted, involving genetic factors, chronic inflammation, ACVD, and elevated arterial blood pressure. It was recently recognized that tsRNAs might be involved in the pathophysiology of AD [77, 78]. Fu et al. [77] identified a total of 41 differentially expressed tsRNAs in human dissected aortic tissue by RNA sequencing, of which, tRF-1:30-chrM.Met-CAT was upregulated while tRF-54:71-chrM.Trp-TCA and tRF-1:32-chrM.Cys-GCA were downregulated as validated by RT-qPCR. In vitro analysis revealed that the introduction of a tRF-1:30-chrM.Met-CAT mimic in human aortic VSMCs increased cell proliferation and migration. Moreover, the contractile marker α-SMA was repressed by the mimic, suggesting tRF-1:30-chrM.Met-CAT promoted VSMC phenotypic switching from a quiescent/contractile phenotype to a synthetic one [77]. Synthetic phenotype VSMCs are known to be a key contributor to AD progression [76].

Ischemia–reperfusion injury induces the production of tsRNAs including 5′-tiRNA-Cys-GCA [78]. Zong et al. [78] showed that 5′-tiRNA-Cys-GCA was co-localized with α-SMA in human and mouse aortas, and that aortic tissues from AD patients or mice had remarkably lower levels of 5′-tiRNA-Cys-GCA than the control samples. Furthermore, overexpression of 5′-tiRNA-Cys-GCA in human aortic VSMCs not only inhibited cell proliferation and migration but also suppressed the phenotypic conversion from a quiescent/contractile phenotype to a synthetic one. Knockdown of 5′-tiRNA-Cys-GCA in human aortic VSMCs resulted in opposite effects [78]. These findings suggest a protective role for 5′-tiRNA-Cys-GCA in AD.

tsRNAs as biomarkers for CVD diagnosis and prognosis

tsRNAs as CVD biomarkers are presented in the central illustration. Studies exploring biofluid tsRNAs as potential biomarkers for CVD diagnosis and prognosis are listed in Table 2.

Heart failure

Lower concentrations of plasma tRF-30-3JVIJMRPFQ5D, tRF-16-R29P4PE, tRF-21-NB8PLML3E, and tRF-21-SWRYVMMV0tsRNA were detected in HF patients by RT-qPCR, compared to healthy controls. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that these tsRNAs had better diagnostic performance for concentric hypertrophy (pressure overload) than for eccentric hypertrophy (volume overload). With respect to the diagnostic potential for concentric hypertrophy, tRF-21-NB8PLML3E possessed the greatest diagnostic power with an area under curve (AUC) value of 0.848 [61]. As concentric hypertrophy occurs in the early stage of HF, measurement of plasma tRF-21-NB8PLML3E may be performed to aid in the early diagnosis of HF [61].

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is often complicated by HF [79, 80]. Treatment with sacubitril (angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor) combined with valsartan (angiotensin receptor blocker) improves cardiac function in AMI patients with HF [81]. However, the desired therapeutic effect cannot be achieved in some patients due to sacubitril/valsartan resistance (SVR). A recent study examined the potential of tsRNAs from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to predict SVR. The results showed that SVR patients had significantly higher levels of tRF-59:76-Tyr-GTA-2-M3 and tRF-60:76-Val-AAC-1-M5 but lower tRF-1:29-Gly-GCC-1 than non-SVR patients [82]. ROC curve analysis showed an AUC value of 0.875, 0.847 and 0.819 for tRF-59:76-Tyr-GTA-2-M3, tRF-60:76-Val-AAC-1-M5 and tRF-1:29-Gly-GCC-1, respectively in the prediction of SVR. These findings suggest PBMC tsRNAs are promising biomarkers that may be used for the prediction of sacubitril/valsartan therapeutic effects in AMI with HF [82].

Acute stroke

Acute stroke requires immediate diagnosis that relies on clinical assessment and neuroimaging. Initial clinical examination can confirm the severity of symptoms, and often the anatomical localization and the vascular distribution but clinical findings alone are often not able to differentiate an acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) or an acute ischemic stroke (IS) from other diseases that mimic strokes [stroke mimic (SM)]. Although computed tomography (CT) imaging is often sufficient to distinguish an acute stroke from an SM, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is regarded as the best imaging technique to detect the cause of a new sudden neurologic deficit (ICH vs. IS vs. SM) [83]. That MRI imaging is only available in advanced healthcare centers and patient-related factors can make MRI impractical in an emergency setting. For these reasons, a recent study explored circulating tsRNAs as early biomarkers to differentiate acute IS, ICH and SM. Different abundance patterns of plasma small RNAs, namely microRNAs, ribosomal RNAs and tRNA fragments among IS, ICH and SM patients were discovered by RNA sequencing [84]. Interestingly, plasma of ICH patients contained the highest abundance of ribosomal RNAs and tRNA fragments, while microRNAs were most abundant in plasma of those suffering IS. Most notably, tRNA fragments had the largest differences in abundance between the groups, making them the most favorable biomarkers for differential diagnosis [84]. Based on these findings, plasma tRNA fragments as biomarkers to distinguish IS, SM and ICH were further assessed in a discovery cohort and a validation cohort. The combination of different tRNA fragments was found to yield the greatest diagnostic accuracy in a discovery cohort. In the validation cohort, the combination of 3 tRNA fragments, i.e., tRNA-TyrGTA, tRNA-ThrCGT and tRNA-ValCAC led to an AUC of 0.875 for distinguishing IS from healthy controls and an AUC of 1.000 between IS and SM [84]. Therefore, circulating tsRNAs may be a valuable diagnostic tool for early differentiation of acute IS from SM.

Another study showed the superiority of circulating exosomal tsRNAs compared with plasma tsRNAs in the diagnosis of strokes caused by atherosclerosis of the large arteries of the head and neck [large artery atherosclerotic (LAA) stroke] [85]. Exosomes from patients with LAA stroke contained markedly higher levels of tRF-19-INVDRIFU and tRF-31-FN8DYDZDL9X1B but lower tRF-34-79MP9PMNH5IS15 and tRF-38-Q99P9P9NH57S36D1 than exosomes from healthy controls, as assayed by RT-qPCR. Regression analyses found that changes of exosomal tRF-19-INVDRIFU and tRF-38-Q99P9P9NH57S36D1 were associated with LAA stroke. ROC curve plotting showed an AUC value of 0.777 and 0.836 for tRF-19-INVDRIFU and tRF-38-Q99P9P9NH57S36D1 respectively for the diagnosis of LAA stroke. The combination of tRF-19-INVDRIFU and tRF-38-Q99P9P9NH57S36D1 resulted in greater diagnostic power with an AUC value of 0.847 [85]. Only two plasma tRFs were found to be dysregulated, i.e., tRF-31-FN8DYDZDL9X1B was upregulated and tRF-38-Q99P9P9NH57S36D1 downregulated. The performance of these two plasma tRFs was inferior to exosomal tRFs in the diagnosis of LAA stroke. Furthermore, exosomal tRFs were associated with short term prognosis. Higher baseline levels of exo-tRF-19-INVDRIFU and lower exo-tRF-38-Q99P9P9NH57S36D were detected in patients with better outcomes one month after discharge, although the predictive power was not described [85].

Oxidative stress is involved in the pathological processes of both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke [86, 87]. Although it was found over a decade ago that oxidative stress induced tRNA fragmentation [88], tsRNAs as predictors of functional outcomes in patients with acute stroke were only recently explored. Ishida et al. [89] reported that the plasma concentration of tRNA derivatives including tsRNAs on admission was positively correlated with the infarction size in patients with acute large vessel occlusion. Follow-up of patients who underwent endovascular thrombectomy revealed that a lower concentration of plasma tRNA derivatives on admission predicted better clinical outcomes at 90 days. A tRNA derivative level ≤ 142.0 mg/mL had an AUC value of 0.952 with a sensitivity of 85.7% and a specificity of 100% for the prognosis of acute stroke as determined by ROC curve analysis [89].

Congenital heart disease

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is an umbrella term that covers all heart defects present at birth that result from defective embryonic organogenesis and is the leading cause of death in children with birth defect [90, 91]. Early treatment of CHD depends on timely diagnosis. Maternal serum tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) have emerged as promising biomarkers for early CHD diagnosis [92]. Pregnant women with fetal cardiac malformations had increased serum tRF-58:74-Gly-GCC-1 but decreased tiRNA-1:35-Leu-CAG-1-M2 compared to those without fetal heart structural defect. ROC curve analysis showed an AUC value of 0.772 and 0.643 for tRF-58:74-Gly-GCC-1 and tiRNA-1:35-Leu-CAG-1-M2, respectively, for prenatal diagnosis of CHD [92].

Outstanding questions and future research

There is no current consensus on tsRNA nomenclature, that results in difficulties in data comparability, experimental protocol standardization, and data base integration. Bioinformatic tools are widely used to uncover cell signaling pathways regulated by tsRNAs in CVD. Although a limited number of studies examining tsRNA roles in CVD have used cell models, none have employed animal models. The mechanistic link between aberrant cell signaling pathways and dysregulated tsRNAs in CAD remains unclear. While tsRNAs have been evaluated as CVD biomarkers in exploratory cohorts, few studies have included validation cohorts to confirm the findings. Moreover, all clinical studies to date have involved small sample sizes, limiting generalizability.

Although tsRNAs hold promise as diagnostic biomarkers, several practical limitations hinder clinical translation. First, sample variability, including differences in RNA quality, storage conditions, and patient heterogeneity, can significantly affect tsRNA detection and quantification. Second, current detection techniques, such as RNA sequencing and qRT-PCR, require further standardization to ensure sensitivity and specificity across platforms and laboratories. Third, the reproducibility of tsRNA profiles across cohorts and disease states remain a challenge due to inconsistent nomenclature, annotation, and reference databases. Addressing these limitations through large-scale validation studies and the development of robust detection protocols will be essential for advancing tsRNAs toward clinical application.

To address the current lack of in vivo validation, future studies could utilize tsRNA mimics or inhibitors in preclinical models to investigate the causal role of specific tsRNAs in CVDs. For instance, tsRNA mimics or antisense oligonucleotides could be delivered via adeno-associated viral vectors or nanoparticles in mouse models of myocardial infarction or atherosclerosis to assess their functional effects on tissue remodeling, inflammation, or vascular integrity. Additionally, cardiac- or endothelial-specific transgenic or knockout animal models targeting tsRNA biogenesis enzymes (e.g., angiogenin or Dicer) may help elucidate the in vivo pathophysiological relevance of tsRNA pathways in CVDs. These approaches could provide mechanistic insights and support the translational potential of tsRNAs as therapeutic targets or diagnostic biomarkers in CVDs.

Conclusions

tsRNAs may contribute to the pathogenesis of CVD by regulating interactions between endothelial cells and inflammatory cells, endothelial cell and vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, and cardiac metabolism, mitophagy and remodeling. They have emerged as promising biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of CVD. More experimental assessments, especially using animal models, need to be performed to better define the mechanism underlining tsRNA roles in CVD. As knockout of a specific tsRNA in animals is impossible as tsRNAs are derived from cleavage of tRNAs and that a tRNA can generate multiple tsRNAs, oligonucleotides mimicking or inhibiting tsRNAs may be useful tools to decipher tsRNA functions in vivo. Additionally, large validation cohorts from multiple centers are required to verify tsRNAs as biomarkers and the additive effects of tsRNAs in combination with currently available diagnostic and prognostic parameters need to be explored in the future.
