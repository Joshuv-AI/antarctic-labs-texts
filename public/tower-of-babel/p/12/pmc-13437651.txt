Nanostructured electrode materials and flexible-substrate engineering for wearable multi-analyte biosensors in diabetes monitoring and personalized care: a comprehensive review
Authors: Roghieh Sodeify, Mir Amirhossein Seyednazari, Amir Mohammad Dorosti, Alireza Nourazarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review examines the convergence of wearable biosensors and artificial intelligence (AI) in personalized diabetes care. It addresses the limitations of traditional glucose monitoring and underscores the need for continuous, multi-analyte physiological surveillance. The manuscript evaluates multi-biofluid sensing platforms, specifically those utilizing interstitial fluid (ISF), sweat, saliva, tears, and urine. ISF, an extracellular medium and a plasma ultrafiltrate, exhibits low protein content, a property that reduces sensor biofouling. ISF glucose demonstrates a strong correlation with blood glucose (R² > 0.95) and can achieve high analytical sensitivity and specificity in clinically validated systems; however, diffusion-based time lags of 5–10 min present a kinetic challenge. Consequently, AI correction is necessary to ensure real-time accuracy, which is often achieved through minimally invasive microneedle arrays. Sweat analysis allows for non-invasive, multi-parameter measurements. Nevertheless, challenges such as pH instability and analyte loss due to evaporation complicate this sensing approach. Therefore, microfluidic techniques are essential for maintaining sample stability. A primary finding indicates that clinically validated Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) systems yield substantial improvements in glycemic control, increasing Time in Range (TIR) by 10–15% and reducing the incidence of hypoglycemic events by 30–40%. AI-based predictive algorithms can forecast glucose excursions 30–60 min in advance, exhibiting an accuracy exceeding 94%. Key barriers to implementation include sensor calibration challenges, algorithmic bias, and significant healthcare equity issues. Future research should prioritize the development of multi-analyte implantable devices, leverage federated learning frameworks, and incorporate additional biomarkers to deliver continuous, multi-analyte, skin-conformal monitoring.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Global burden of diabetes

Globally, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) accounts for 90–95% of the 537 million total diabetes cases; its prevalence continues to rise, driven by urbanization and increasing life expectancy [1]. Between 1990 and 2021, the years lost due to T2DM increased by 1.6% annually. Notably, this increase was even more pronounced in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where rates climbed from 2.72% to 3.02%. As a result, these regions now bear 75% of the global case burden and the associated financial strain [2]. In addition, by 2021, 14.6 million adolescents and young adults had been diagnosed with T2DM—a number that continues to grow [3]. Although therapeutic options have improved, persistent challenges such as suboptimal patient adherence [4] and food insecurity [5]. remain, highlighting the need for greater focus on personalized patient management [6, 7]. Evolution of diabetes monitoring technologies

The methodologies employed in health monitoring have advanced significantly, evolving from fundamental urine analysis to highly sophisticated, integrated systems. For example, while the self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) introduced in the 1980s enabled individuals to manually adjust insulin levels, the technique exhibited several intrinsic limitations. Specifically, the readings were prone to inaccuracy, required invasive skin pricking, and failed to capture the dynamic fluctuations in blood glucose levels [8, 9]. The subsequent introduction of Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) in the 2000s revolutionized this process by employing subcutaneously placed sensors that measure interstitial fluid (ISF) at intervals of 1–5 min [10, 11]. This technology enables frequent, high-resolution data collection, resulting in a 10–20% reduction in Time Outside Range (TOR) [12]. Furthermore, factory-calibrated devices that support extended wear periods of up to 14 days further facilitate longitudinal monitoring and enhance patient adherence [13]. Contemporary platforms co-monitor glucose, lactate, and ketone bodies on a single skin-worn patch, yielding minute-by-minute fuel-substrate ratios that reflect insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial workload. thereby offering a comprehensive perspective on systemic energy homeostasis [14, 15].

The convergence of biosensing and artificial intelligence

Multi-analyte biosensors, which quantify glucose alongside lactate, cortisol, ketones, and electrolytes within a single device, combined with AI-driven analysis, simultaneously map glycemic, inflammatory, and stress-related metabolic trajectories and stratify patients by hypoglycemia and ketoacidosis risk. This approach allows clinicians to titrate insulin dosing and adjunctive therapy based on each patient’s longitudinal sensor record, genotype, comorbidities, social determinants of health, and lifestyle data [16–18]. This review has three main objectives: (1) to evaluate recent technological advancements in biosensors and AI-enhanced diabetes care management; (2) to examine current clinical evidence and ongoing challenges; and (3) to highlight future research directions. The methodology synthesizes evidence from technology development, clinical trials, implementation studies, and health equity initiatives. The aim is to inform researchers, healthcare providers, policymakers, and technology developers about effective practices. Figure 1 presents the conceptual design of the integrated system.

Fundamentals of multi-analyte biosensing for diabetes

Simultaneous quantification of glucose, lactate, ketones, and cortisol resolves three pathological axes that single-analyte glucose monitoring cannot distinguish —substrate availability, anaerobic stress, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal activation—and links each to specific diabetes complications. These sensors integrate electrochemical and optical channels on one substrate to co-register metabolic, inflammatory, and stress biomarkers, allowing clinicians to detect impending hypoglycemia 20–40 min earlier than CGM alone and to adjust basal insulin, carbohydrate intake, or stress-management interventions on a per-patient basis [19]. This subsequent section is structured to outline the fundamental components of this methodology: biomarkers, types of biofluids, and mechanisms for signal conversion.

Biomarker landscape in diabetes management

Biosensors that track lactate, ketones, cortisol, and inflammatory cytokines in addition to glucose capture the metabolic, endocrine, and immune dimensions of diabetes that single-analyte glucose readings miss [20]. The choice of tests is influenced by factors related to metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation.

Metabolic basis

While glucose remains the standard clinical metric, reliance exclusively on this parameter overlooks other significant changes in systemic energy processing [21]. Lactate accumulation is observed during both physical exertion and insulin resistance, serving as an early-stage indicator for Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) risk and potential ketoacidosis [20]. Ketones, specifically β- hydroxybutyrate, elevate in concentration in response to insulin insufficiency, facilitating the early diagnosis of Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) [22]. Recent advancements in miniaturized sensor technology, often employing microneedle arrays, now enable the simultaneous measurement of lactate and ketones concurrent with glucose [20].

Homeostatic indicators

During physiological stress, cortisol levels rise, increasing insulin resistance and affecting glycemic stability. This hormone is also linked directly to adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) [23]. Changes in pH levels indicate acidosis during Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA). Electrolyte levels, especially sodium and potassium, give important information about hydration status and renal function. This is crucial for reducing risks of neuropathy and nephropathy [24]. Wearable skin patches now enable simultaneous monitoring of cortisol and electrolyte levels in sweat. These devices can reveal daily patterns that may predict nocturnal hypoglycemia [25, 26].

Inflammatory monitoring

Cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α disrupt insulin production and damage blood vessels. Measuring these metabolites in sweat can help predict complications [27]. MicroRNAs such as miR-126 may indicate a person’s potential to develop retinopathy, but their practical application is challenging due to their instability [28].

Biofluids as sensing matrices

Biofluid selection balances accessibility, biomarker fidelity, and sensor compatibility. Each matrix exhibits unique compositional profiles influencing analyte diffusion, stability, and correlation with blood values.

Time lag: Delay between blood glucose changes and biofluid detection

While correlation coefficients are often reported to demonstrate the agreement between sensor readings and reference methods, a comprehensive evaluation of multi-analyte biosensing platforms requires a deeper consideration of other clinically relevant metrics, particularly sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity, which is the assay’s ability to correctly identify subjects with the target analyte (true positives), is crucial for detecting low concentrations of biomarkers indicative of early disease states or subtle physiological changes [29, 30]. Conversely, specificity measures the assay’s ability to correctly identify subjects without the target analyte (true negatives), minimizing false positives that could lead to unnecessary interventions or patient anxiety[31, 32]. These issues become even more critical in multi-analyte sensing systems, where the simultaneous detection of several biomarkers increases the likelihood of signal interference, cross-reactivity, and interference from co-existing electroactive compounds present in complex biofluids such as sweat, interstitial fluid, saliva, or tears [30, 33]. Therefore, metrics such as the limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for determining optimal cut-off values, and explicit measures of cross-reactivity are as vital as correlation coefficients for establishing the true clinical utility and diagnostic power of wearable biosensing technologies [34, 35]. Future research should focus on consistently developing and reporting these parameters across different sensing modalities to enable robust comparisons and facilitate clinical translation.

Interstitial fluid (ISF)

Interstitial fluid (ISF), the extracellular medium surrounding dermal cells, is a plasma ultrafiltrate primarily composed of approximately 99% water, electrolytes, and small metabolites, including glucose and lactate. Its low protein content, about 50% lower than that of blood plasma, effectively reduces sensor biofouling [36]. Although there is a strong correlation with glucose levels (R² > 0.95), clinically validated ISF-based systems also demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity within clinically acceptable error bands; nevertheless, diffusion-based time lags of 5 to 10 min require AI correction to ensure real-time accuracy [37]. Microneedle arrays provide minimally invasive access and support extended wear periods of 6 to 14 days [38].

Sweat

Sweat, produced by glands during activity, enables continuous and non-invasive monitoring of specific bodily substances. It contains trace amounts of analytes such as glucose, lactate, and cortisol. These analytes exhibit moderate correlation with corresponding blood levels; however, their concentrations often demonstrate a time lag of 5–30 min due to the mechanisms of sweat formation and transport through the skin [39, 40]. Sweat analysis offers the advantage of simultaneous multi-parameter measurement and enhanced user comfort. Nevertheless, several challenges persist, including fluctuations in sweat rate, pH instability (4.5–7.5), and the loss of analytes through evaporation [41]. Microfluidic techniques present a promising solution for maintaining sample stability [42].

Alternative biofluids

Saliva shows a moderate correlation with blood glucose levels (R2 = 0.5–0.8, where R2 represents the proportion of variance explained by the association) and exhibits a time lag (delay in concentration changes) of 5–20 min; however, its application is hindered by inherent oral contamination [43]. In contrast, tears show a high correlation (rho = 0.95–0.97), indicating a strong relationship between tear and blood glucose levels [44]; but their extremely limited sampling volume (approximately 1 μl) significantly limits the monitoring frequency. Similarly, while urine can detect hyperglycemia (levels exceeding 0.8 mmol/L), its usefulness for acute monitoring is limited due to substantial renal processing delays (the time required for glucose to appear in urine after blood glucose levels rise) that can last for h [45]. Synthesizing the comparative evidence in Table 2 into actionable design guidance, three practical recommendations emerge for biofluid selection in wearable multi-analyte diabetes platforms. First, eccrine sweat is recommended as the primary biofluid for ambulatory daytime monitoring of glucose, lactate, and electrolytes because it offers the most favorable balance of accessibility, refresh rate, and analyte diversity, despite requiring iontophoretic stimulation in sedentary users [46]. Second, interstitial fluid (ISF) sampled via minimally invasive microneedle arrays remains the modality of choice for clinical-grade glycemic accuracy (MARD < 10%), particularly for insulin-dosing decisions, while sweat-based readings are best deployed as complementary trend indicators rather than stand-alone titration inputs [47]. Third, saliva and tears should be reserved for adjunct biomarkers (e.g., cortisol, inflammatory markers) where their lower glucose correlation (R² = 0.5–0.8) is acceptable [48, 49]. Transduction mechanisms in wearable biosensors

Transduction mechanisms convert the interaction between the target analyte and the biorecognition element into a measurable electrical signal. The selection of an appropriate method is contingent upon the physicochemical properties of the target (e.g., size, charge, reactivity) [50, 51].

Electrochemical detection remains highly sought after due to its inherent portability and high sensitivity [52, 53]. These methods can be categorized into three primary variants: amperometric, potentiometric, and impedimetric [50]. Amperometry, the first variant, measures the faradaic redox current, allowing for the detection of analytes such as glucose with a limit of detection (LOD) below 0.1 mM. The second variant, potentiometry, measures ion-selective potential shifts and is commonly applied to monovalent cations such as K⁺ and Na⁺ [54, 55]. The final variant, impedimetry, monitors resistance changes and is often used to assess sensor biofouling. Recent studies (e.g., Wang et al. 2025b; Jung and Jang 2024) demonstrate that these variants have the necessary accuracy. Incorporating non-enzymatic nanomaterials, such as NiCo₂O₄, enhances stability and reduces signal interference from uric acid and other electroactive species [56–58]. Optical sensing utilizes techniques such as fluorescence, colorimetric assays, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to enable label-free analyte detection in matrices like tears. However, its performance can be compromised by issues such as photobleaching and optical interference [59]. Piezoelectric techniques, including the Quartz Crystal Microbalance (QCM) integrated with ZnO nanowires, convert minute mass changes into measurable electrical signals. These methods are valuable for detecting large analytes, such as proteins and cytokines, and can operate autonomously. However, they require signal amplification to identify trace-level analytes [60, 61]. Thermal transduction leverages enzyme-catalyzed heat generation for precise lactate measurement, but widespread adoption remains limited due to susceptibility to thermal noise inherent in wearable device platforms [62–64]. A direct, quantitative comparison of the four dominant transduction modalities is therefore warranted rather than a single “optimal” designation. Electrochemical (amperometric) platforms typically deliver glucose LODs of 1–10 µM with response times of 5–30 s, linear ranges spanning 0.05–30 mM, and per-sensor material costs below ~US$1, but suffer baseline drift of 5–15% over 7–14 days driven by enzyme leaching, biofouling, and electroactive interferents (uric acid, ascorbate, acetaminophen) [48, 49, 52–54]. Optical SPR/fluorescence approaches reach comparable or lower LODs (10⁻⁹–10⁻¹² M for SPR with antibody capture), provide label-free multiplexing in tears or interstitial fluid, and are largely immune to redox interferents; however, they require photodiode/laser modules that increase device footprint and power draw to ~50–200 mW (vs. <1 mW for amperometry), and they exhibit 5–20% signal loss per hour from photobleaching and ambient-light interference [65]. Piezoelectric QCM/ZnO transducers offer mass-sensitive detection at ~1 ng·cm⁻², are well suited to large analytes (proteins, cytokines) at sub-nanomolar levels, and consume only microwatts in passive mode, but their non-specific mass response and the need for >40 dB amplification make them unreliable for small molecules in sweat or saliva [66, 67]. Thermal (calorimetric) sensors achieve lactate resolutions of ~50 µM with intrinsic enzyme specificity, yet skin-temperature fluctuations of ±1–2 °C and motion-induced thermal drift translate into 10–25% measurement error during ambulatory use [68, 69]. Taken together, electrochemical methods are best matched to small-molecule, low-cost, low-power continuous monitoring; optical methods are advantageous when interferent-rich matrices or label-free protein detection are required; piezoelectric methods are favored for mass-based biomarker recognition; and thermal methods occupy a niche in stable-temperature laboratory or implantable contexts. Hybrid electrochemical–optical patches that integrate two or more of these modalities have reduced cross-interference by 30–60% and improved analytical accuracy (R² vs. reference assays) from ~0.85 to >0.95, supporting closed-loop applications that no single modality can achieve alone [61, 70].

Although electrochemical platforms are popular for wearable biosensing, but they present trade-off challenges [70]. Enzyme-based sensors offer high sensitivity and rapid response times, often achieving micromolar or sub-micromolar limits of detection and responding in under a minute [34]. However, their suitability for continuous monitoring is limited by enzyme instability, biofouling, and temperature sensitivity during long-term use. Non-enzymatic sensors, in contrast, use nanostructured catalytic materials, such as metal nanoparticles, graphene derivatives, and metal-organic frameworks. However, these systems can suffer from reduced selectivity due to interference from electroactive species in biological fluids. Therefore, while enzymatic systems are effective for short-term monitoring, there is a growing effort to explore robust, non-enzymatic, nanomaterial-based platforms for long-term wearables [71–73].

To translate this transduction comparison into actionable selection criteria, we propose a four-axis decision rubric. (i) Target analyte class: amperometric electrochemical transduction is preferred for redox-active small molecules (glucose, lactate, uric acid); potentiometric ion-selective electrodes are optimal for electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻); impedimetric and field-effect transistor (FET) transduction are favored for label-free protein and hormone detection (e.g., insulin, cortisol). (ii) Required limit of detection: optical surface-plasmon-resonance (SPR) and FET architectures dominate at sub-nanomolar levels, while amperometric platforms suffice for micromolar-to-millimolar analytes. (iii) Power and form-factor budget: electrochemical modalities consume <1 mW and integrate into adhesive patches, whereas optical readouts currently require bulkier photodetectors and higher power. (iv) Multiplexing demand: spatially addressable electrode arrays enable scalable multi-analyte readout without wavelength-multiplexing complexity. Applying this rubric, an electrochemical-dominant, optically-supplemented hybrid emerges as the recommended transduction backbone for diabetes-focused wearables. Engineering architectures of multi-analyte wearable biosensors

Translating wearable biosensors from laboratory prototypes to clinically viable platforms require sophisticated engineering that integrates miniaturized sensing elements, biocompatible materials, advanced signal processing, and user-centered design [74, 75]. Multi-analyte platforms replace single-analyte glucose monitoring with parallel quantification of metabolic, endocrine, and inflammatory markers, generating multivariate biomarker time-series suitable for downstream machine-learning classification [53, 76]. Sensor design principles and miniaturization

Microfluidic channel architectures

Wearable electrochemical sensors integrated with microfluidic technology enable the non-invasive, real-time analysis of bodily fluids, including sweat, saliva, tears, and Interstitial Fluid (ISF), via continuous sampling. Park et al. provided a comprehensive review of these systems, highlighting their potential for personalized diabetes management through the simultaneous detection of multiple biomarkers [15]. Microfluidic channel designs reproducibly collect 1–5 µL of sweat or ISF per minute, separate target analytes from interfering proteins through size- or charge-selective membranes, and resolve concentration changes on a 30–60 s timescale [77, 78]. Current device configurations feature advanced channel designs incorporating capillary-driven passive pumping, which eliminates the requirement for external power sources. Furthermore, three-dimensional microfluidic networks incorporate spatially separated electrode arrays, each modified with distinct biorecognition elements tailored to specific analytes [15, 77]. This architectural design facilitates simultaneous multi-analyte detection while effectively mitigating cross-contamination. Microfluidic integration routes sub-microliter sweat or interstitial-fluid volumes through capillary channels at controlled flow rates of 0.1–1 µL min⁻¹, lowers per-measurement reagent use roughly tenfold, and shortens the analyte residence time, which preserves enzyme activity and limits oxidative degradation [79, 80]. For example, sweat-based microfluidic systems allow the noninvasive monitoring of biomarkers such as glucose and lactate [81]. However, variations in the sweat rate, evaporation, and contamination can affect the concentration and measurement accuracy [82]. Although recent designs incorporate passive pumping structures and capillary flow channels to improve fluid transport stability, consistent sampling remains a challenge [83]. Future systems will require better fluid regulation strategies and calibration algorithms to ensure accurate and long-term monitoring in real-world applications [81].

Channel optimization and fluid dynamics

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling has shown that optimal channel cross-sectional areas, ranging from 100-500 μm², are necessary to maintain laminar flow profiles while ensuring adequate analyte mass transport to electrode surfaces [15]. Spontaneous biofluid flow through hydrophilic channels, driven by the combined effects of surface tension and capillary forces, eliminates the need for external mechanical pumping [14, 84]. The incorporation of hydrophobic barriers and micro-valves allows for temporal control over biofluid routing, facilitating sophisticated sequential multi-step assays [85]. Rather than vaguely asserting that recent implementations have shown such capability, the published evidence can be quantified and benchmarked [77]. Gao et al. (Nature 2016) and subsequent generations of fully integrated epidermal patches have concurrently quantified glucose (10 µM–1 mM), lactate (1–20 mM), cortisol (1–100 ng·mL⁻¹), Na⁺, and K⁺ (10–100 mM) from eccrine sweat at 1–3 min sampling intervals, with sensor-to-blood correlation coefficients of r = 0.80–0.92 across cohorts of 5–20 subjects [86]. Compared with single-analyte amperometric patches, multi-analyte microfluidic platforms reduce calibration drift by 20–40% by using on-chip Na⁺/K⁺ as an internal reference, and they extend usable wear time from ~6 h to 24–72 h. Their principal limitations remain quantifiable: sweat-rate variability of 0.5–5 nL·min⁻¹·gland⁻¹ introduces concentration errors of up to 30%, motion artifacts during exercise inflate signal noise by 2–5×, and channel evaporation concentrates analytes by 10–20% over 4–8 h of continuous wear [87–90]. Mitigation has been demonstrated empirically: iontophoretic sweat induction stabilizes flow at 1–2 nL·min⁻¹, permeability-controlled PDMS–paper hybrid channels reduce evaporative drift to <5%, and machine-learning calibration (random-forest or LSTM) lowers mean absolute error against venous reference assays by 25–40% [91–93]. These quantitative benchmarks—rather than qualitative claims of feasibility—establish where current microfluidic biosensors succeed (multi-analyte, hour-scale monitoring) and where they fall short (motion-rich or low-perspiration scenarios).

Synthesizing the microfluidic approaches reviewed above, we recommend a tiered architecture-selection logic keyed to deployment context. For passive, always-on sweat monitoring, capillary-driven serpentine PDMS channels (cross-section 100–500 µm², path length 5–15 mm) provide the best trade-off between sample refresh and skin conformity For event-triggered sampling (e.g., post-prandial glucose excursions), valved microfluidic chambers with hydrogel-actuated gating offer controlled aliquoting at the cost of fabrication complexity. For multi-analyte fractionation, Y-junction splitters coupled to sensor-specific reaction zones outperform single-chamber designs by reducing cross-contamination by >80%. Designers should prioritize: (a) sample refresh rate ≥ 5 µL min⁻¹ to avoid analyte aging, (b) channel materials with proven biocompatibility (PDMS, SU-8, or paper composites), and (c) integration with iontophoretic or thermal stimulation modules when targeting sedentary users. These criteria are incorporated into the unified design matrix in Section 3.10.

Biorecognition elements and surface chemistry

Material selection principles

The mechanical interface between wearable biosensors and the human skin dictates device comfort, measurement accuracy, and long-term user compliance [14, 94]. In this context, Ferreira et al. identified substrate material selection as a critical factor because it directly determines whether biosensors can form skin-conformal geometries that accommodate dynamic mechanical deformations and maintain close sensor-skin contact—both of which are required for reliable biofluid sampling [95]. Contemporary flexible substrates are thus engineered with Young’s moduli of 10-1000 kPa, closely matching those of human skin (140-600 kPa) to reduce interfacial stress concentrations [94, 96]. Reducing these stress concentrations in turn lessens discomfort and preserves sustained adhesion [94]. As a result, mechanical matching allows sensors to move naturally with the skin during body motion, which is essential for reliable long-term use [78].

Polymer substrate technologies

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) elastomers are widely adopted as preferred substrates due to their inherent biocompatibility, requisite optical transparency for photolithographic patterning, and tunable mechanical properties, often modulated via crosslinking density. PDMS demonstrates a noteworthy capacity to withstand reversible strains exceeding 100% while preserving the integrity of its electrical contacts [95]. Notwithstanding these advantages, the material’s inherent hydrophobicity compromises its capacity for biofluid wetting. To address this limitation, surface modification, typically through plasma oxidation or hydrophilic polymer grafting, can be employed; however, the time-dependent recovery of hydrophobicity must be considered for prolonged applications [15]. Compared with PDMS, alternative elastomeric substrates, such as Ecoflex and thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPUs), exhibit significantly higher maximum elongations, often reaching 500% [15, 95]. This enhanced conformability to highly mobile anatomical sites constitute a notable advantage of these alternative substrates. Consequently, while PDMS offers notable biocompatibility and transparency, rendering it suitable for biomedical patterning, Ecoflex and TPUs exhibit superior stretchability, making them advantageous for applications in dynamic environments [53, 76, 94].

Carbon-based composite materials

Carbon-based composites integrated within elastomeric matrices are attracting considerable research attention due to their advantageous electromechanical stability, extraordinary electrical conductivity, high specific surface area, and inherent cost-effectiveness [95]. For instance, graphene and carbon nanotube composites maintain exceptional electrical conductivity even under extreme mechanical deformation ( > 200% strain), rendering them optimal materials for highly skin-conformal electrodes [97]. Concurrently, textile-integrated approaches delineate an alternative sensing paradigm. In this approach, conductive fiber electrodes are woven directly into garments, achieving seamless integration with daily attire and enabling large-area physiological sensing [95].

Signal processing and data transmission

Challenges in sustainable power delivery

The sustainable delivery of power imposes significant constraints on the implementation of wearable biosensors, particularly in contexts requiring prolonged monitoring periods lasting weeks to months. Conventional battery-based solutions face critical limitations, including volumetric constraints, weight penalties, and logistical replacement challenges. These limitations are fundamentally incompatible with the tenets of unobtrusive wearable design [98, 99]. Consequently, Engineering efforts have targeted triboelectric and piezoelectric harvesters that supply 10–100 µW from routine wrist motion, sufficient to power continuous electrochemical sensing without battery replacement [100]. These methodologies capture ambient energy from the wearer’s environment or physiological processes, converting mechanical, thermal, or biochemical energy into usable electrical power [101, 102].

Mechanical energy harvesting

Triboelectric Nanogenerators (TENGs) and piezoelectric mechanisms are utilized to convert biomechanical energy from body movements into electrical charge via two distinct processes: contact electrification and strain-induced polarization, respectively [103, 104]. As demonstrated by Zhang et al. [62], TENGs can generate peak power outputs ranging from 1 to 10 mW when subjected to walking motion [102]. The integration of these harvesters with glucose-sensing platforms demonstrates significant potential for battery-free operation; however, power generation is inherently intermittent and reliant on continuous body motion. Hybrid systems combining energy storage components (e.g., supercapacitors) with energy harvesting mitigate this issue by storing excess charge generated during active periods, thereby enabling continuous sensor operation even during quiescent states [102, 105, 106].

Biochemical energy harvesting

Biofuel cells leverage the inherent catalytic activity of enzymes to oxidize substrates like glucose or lactate, simultaneously generating electrical energy and quantifying substrate concentrations [107, 108]. For example, glucose biofuel cells use glucose oxidase—an enzyme also common in traditional biosensors—to achieve this dual function [109, 110]. However, conventional biofuel cells typically produce limited power outputs, generally ranging from (10-100 µW), which significantly restricts their integration into circuits that require higher power levels [111, 112]. Recent advancements in enzyme stabilization and the use of nanostructured electrodes have led to substantial increases in power output, with documented improvements ranging from 3- to 5-fold [113]. As a result, biofuel cells are increasingly viewed as a viable power source for low-power medical and environmental sensing applications [112, 114].

Form factor innovations

The wireless communication infrastructure facilitates the real-time transmission of physiological data from body-worn sensors to external data processing platforms, thereby eliminating the necessity for physical tethering [42, 74]. Table 3 provides a comprehensive overview of the specifications, advantages, and inherent limitations of the primary wireless protocols utilized in wearable biosensing systems.

Implementation considerations

Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) uses frequency-hopping spread spectrum across 40 channels in the 2.4 GHz industrial, scientific, and medical (ISM) band. This method provides robustness against interference and allows coexistence with Wi-Fi [98, 115]. Contemporary BLE transceivers consume about ten milliwatts during active transmission. With duty-cycled operation (1–10 s intervals), the average power consumption stays below 100 microwatts [98]. In contrast, near-field communication (NFC) operates via radio-frequency interrogation. External readers simultaneously power NFC devices and retrieve data [116]. Mondal (2025) studied miniaturized flexible NFC antennas with serpentine and spiral shapes to enhance flexibility while maintaining inductive coupling efficiency [116]. These advances support battery-free biosensors that enable on-demand glucose interrogation through smartphone readers [117]. Radio-frequency identification (RFID)-enabled biosensors use backscatter modulation: sensor impedance modulates the reflected signal, encoding data without an active transmitter [118, 119]. Zou et al. (2025) showed hybrid NFC/BLE systems. These combine passive interrogation for basic monitoring with active transmission for real-time alerts, optimizing power while maintaining continuous surveillance [116].

Biorecognition elements and surface chemistry

Enzymatic recognition systems

Enzymatic biorecognition, using enzymes such as Glucose Oxidase (GOx), is considered the gold standard for wearable sensors, mainly due to its high molecular selectivity and catalytic signal amplification [120]. GOx oxidizes glucose to gluconolactone, with either a concurrent reduction of oxygen to hydrogen peroxide or direct electron transfer to the electrode. These catalyzed reactions generate a current proportional to glucose concentration. Recent GOx variants offer improved thermal stability, greater pH tolerance, and more resistance to interference, allowing operation above 50 °C. Additionally, Lactate Oxidase (LOx) enables dual monitoring of lactate, providing complementary metabolic information that is valuable for exercise physiology, analysis of diabetes complications, and assessment of tissue perfusion [39, 121].

Enzyme immobilization strategies

Multi-analyte platforms utilize microelectrode arrays to arrange different enzyme functionalities, enabling the precise positioning of multiple enzymes onto small sensor surfaces [122]. This design permits the simultaneous measurement of multiple biomarkers from a single biofluid sample, such as blood or saliva. Enzymes are frequently immobilized by physical entrapment within supportive polymer matrices, such as chitosan (a natural biopolymer) or Nafion (a synthetic ionic polymer). While simple, this method can introduce mass transport limitations for the substrate accessing the enzyme active site [123]. Chitosan-based immobilization matrices have shown particular promise for glucose biosensor applications due to their inherent biocompatibility and demonstrated capacity to maintain enzyme activity over extended operational periods [124].

Alternatively, covalent immobilization methods—including carbodiimide chemistry, glutaraldehyde crosslinking, and click chemistry—provide substantially stronger enzyme attachment and enable higher enzyme loading without diminishing catalytic efficiency [15]. Recent studies have demonstrated that EDC/NHS carbodiimide coupling strategies effectively immobilize glucose oxidase within various nanostructured frameworks while maintaining high catalytic activity [125]. Click chemistry approaches, particularly copper-catalyzed and strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition reactions, have emerged as powerful tools for enzyme immobilization in biosensing applications, offering both site-specific attachment and minimal impact on enzyme function [126, 127]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have also shown promise as platforms for enzyme immobilization, with studies demonstrating enhanced enzyme stability and reusability in biosensor applications[128].

Affinity-based recognition

Aptamers and antibodies offer affinity-based alternatives for detecting non-enzymatically active analytes, such as hormones, cytokines, and therapeutic drugs. Aptamers—short single-stranded oligonucleotides selected through SELEX—bind to targets with dissociation constants reaching picomolar affinities [112]. To functionalize aptamers, thiol-gold self-assembled monolayers are used, where terminal thiol groups chemisorb to gold electrodes. Target binding transduces electrochemical signals either through conformational changes that alter electron transfer kinetics to embedded redox reporters (such as methylene blue or ferrocene) or via competitive displacement assays [76, 129].

Molecularly imprinted polymers

Molecularly Imprinted Polymers (MIPs) serve as robust synthetic biorecognition elements, effectively overcoming the inherent stability and cost limitations associated with biological components. The synthesis process involves the polymerization of functional monomers in the presence of template molecules, followed by template removal to create three-dimensional cavities that are sterically and chemically complementary to the target analytes [130]. 1. This biomimetic approach yields synthetic recognition sites with selectivity comparable to antibodies, while simultaneously providing enhanced thermal and chemical stability, an extended shelf life at room temperature, and cost-effective, scalable production.2 Recent studies [e.g., Yeasmin and Cheng (2024)] have distinguished electrocatalytic MIPs, which facilitate direct electron transfer, from embedded redox-probe MIPs, in which target binding events modulate the rate of electron transfer [131]. Building on these advancements, current MIP formulations for glucose detection employ vinyl-functionalized boronic acid monomers that form reversible covalent bonds with glucose diols [112]. Consequently, non-enzymatic MIP-based glucose sensors have achieved wide linear detection ranges, typically 0.1–30 mM, and low detection limits approaching 10 μM [132]. Additionally, the incorporation of nanomaterial scaffolds, including graphene, carbon nanotubes, and metal-organic frameworks, vertically aligned gold nanowire arrays expand the electroactive surface area roughly 8–12-fold relative to a planar electrode, lowering the glucose detection limit from ~50 µM to ~5 µM and extending the linear range to 30 mM and improving electron transfer kinetics [133, 134].

Stability considerations

Enzymatic sensors degrade due to protein damage, oxidation, and contamination. They usually last 6–12 months in the fridge (2–8°C) and 7–14 days when in use [112]. MIP-based sensors are more stable, lasting over 2 years at room temperature and for months when used, thanks to their strong inorganic polymer backbone, which resists heat, chemicals, and germs [130].

Signal processing and data management

Analog front-end electronics

Wearable biosensor signals span dynamic ranges from nanoamperes to microamperes in current, or in the millivolt range for potential, low-noise (input-referred noise <1 nV Hz⁻¹/²), 24-bit sigma-delta analog front-end (AFE) electronics for signal conditioning prior to digitization and wireless transmission. Lakshminarayana et al. (2025) reviewed CMOS-integrated sensing systems-on-chip, emphasizing the crucial role of potentiostatic circuits and transimpedance amplifiers (TIAs) in facilitating robust electrochemical biosensor readout. Specifically, potentiostats function to maintain a constant potential difference between the working and reference electrodes while simultaneously measuring the faradaic currents generated at the working electrode. Miniaturized potentiostats integrate core components, including operational amplifiers, voltage references, and current-to-voltage converters, within compact, millimeter-scale footprints[135, 136].

Signal amplification and filtering

The process of signal amplification involves instrumentation amplifiers with high input impedance ( > 1 GΩ), low noise ( < 10 nV/√Hz), and a high common-mode rejection ratio ( > 100 dB) [98]. These characteristics allow for the rejection of motion artifacts and electromagnetic interference. After amplification, analog filtering is applied using low-pass filters with cutoff frequencies between 1 and 10 Hz. These frequencies effectively capture physiological glucose dynamics while attenuating high-frequency noise. Following this, digital signal processing applies Kalman filtering and wavelet-based motion-artifact rejection, including Kalman filtering for optimal state estimation and wavelet transforms for denoising while preserving signal transients [136, 137].

System-on-chip integration

Integrating signal processing, wireless transceivers, and power management circuitry onto application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) simplifies system complexity, reduces overall power consumption, and minimizes device size. Current biosensor ASICs consume less than 1 milliwatt, enabling battery life of more than 1 week. Emerging ultra-low-power designs approaching 100 microwatts allow for continuous operation primarily from harvested energy [135]. However, this requirement for ultra-low power inherently constrains the achievable sampling rate. For instance, energy-harvesting systems typically reduce the rate to 0.1–1 Hz, which is significantly below the 1–10 Hz sampling rate achievable with conventional battery power [42].

Cybersecurity architecture

Threat landscape

The wireless transmission of sensitive health data introduces critical cybersecurity risks that threaten patient privacy and could enable the malicious alteration of sensor data, resulting in adverse patient outcomes. Sen et al. identified eavesdropping, man-in-the-middle (MITM), and denial-of-service (DoS) attacks as the primary threats. To counter these, security systems must rigorously ensure data confidentiality, integrity, and authentication (CIA triad) across the entire sensor-to-cloud path [138]. Crucially, these solutions must operate under strict power and computational constraints.

Encryption strategies

The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), with 128-bit or 256-bit key lengths, provides a computationally efficient symmetric encryption mechanism that is suitable for resource-constrained implementations, requiring approximately 10-50 microjoules per operation [139, 140]. However, symmetric encryption inherently necessitates robust key distribution and management protocols. In contrast, asymmetric cryptographic approaches, such as Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC), offer simplified key management but incur substantially higher computational costs, approximately ( ~ 1 millijoule per operation) per operation[141]. Hybrid architectures that utilize ECC for initial key establishment and AES for subsequent bulk data encryption effectively balance security and power efficiency [142].

Authentication mechanisms

Authentication methods verify device and user identities to prevent unauthorized access or impersonation. For example, challenge-response protocols let sensors prove possession of a secret key without direct transmission. This significantly reduces the risk of eavesdropping [143]. In contrast, biometric authentication, such as fingerprints, electrocardiogram (ECG) patterns, or gait signatures, enables continuous authentication without user intervention. The false acceptance rate (FAR), however, must remain below 0.01% [144]. Blockchain methodologies also provide immutable audit trails, helping detect unauthorized data changes. However, their high computational requirements often exceed the capacity of current wearable devices, so these methods rely on edge- or cloud-based solutions [145].

Form factor innovations

Wristband and smartwatch platforms

Smartwatch and wristband platforms effectively leverage high consumer acceptance. They provide non-invasive access to radial artery pulsatile blood flow and related cardiovascular physiological signals. Integrating accurate glucose sensing into the smartwatch form factor presents distinct technical challenges. The wrist is an anatomically challenging site due to the scarcity of eccrine sweat glands, necessitating minimally invasive microneedle arrays for extracting subcutaneous interstitial fluid [146]. Wristband platforms, however, excel at combining glucose data with multi-modal physiological signals. These include photoplethysmography for heart rate and oxygen saturation, accelerometry for physical activity tracking, and electrodermal activity for stress assessment [147, 148]. This multi-modal data integration enables highly effective, holistic diabetes management within unified, user-friendly platforms.

Contact lens biosensors

Contact lens biosensors access tear fluid glucose, a minimally invasive biomarker that correlates with blood glucose, though with a 5–10-minute temporal lag and lower concentrations (0.01-0.5 mM versus 3-8 mM in blood) [112]. To enable continuous monitoring without compromising vision, miniaturized transparent electrochemical sensors are integrated into polymer contact lens substrates [76]. Nonetheless, tear fluid volume is minimal ( ~ 7 microliters), and motion of the contact lens during blinking can induce mechanical artifacts, requiring sophisticated signal processing. As a result, clinical translation faces additional regulatory hurdles regarding biocompatibility, sterility, and patient safety.

Textile-integrated systems

Textile-integrated biosensors, designed for the detection of biological signals or chemical analytes, are seamlessly woven into garments to establish an unobtrusive format. This integration enables large-area, distributed physiological monitoring while eliminating the burden of donning or removing separate devices. Conductive fiber electrodes, which serve as the electrical sensing interface, are fabricated from metallized yarns, threads coated with Carbon Nanotubes, or inherently conductive polymers [149, 150]. These materials afford robust electrochemical sensing functionality while retaining essential textile properties: flexibility, breathability, and washability [95]. However, critical challenges include maintaining reliable electrode-skin contact despite fabric draping and dynamic body movement, managing the capillary action and spreading of biofluids across the textile substrate, and achieving high sensitivity given the potential for analyte dilution. Recent designs demonstrate that robust encapsulation strategies can effectively maintain electrode integrity and functional stability across multiple laundry cycles [110]. The capacity of integrated textile biosensors for skin monitoring offers a non-intrusive means of multi-analyte monitoring over large skin areas [151]. First, intermittent and spatially varying skin–electrode contact can cause significant signal instability and baseline drift, especially during vigorous motion and long-term use [152]. Moreover, the capillary spreading and evaporation of sweat in porous textile structures make it difficult to relate the local analyte concentration to the measured electrochemical response [55]. Finally, ensuring wash durability and biocompatibility over repeated use poses an additional material-encapsulation tradeoff. As textile platforms are best regarded as complementary to patch-based and microneedle systems, future studies on standardized calibration protocols and hybrid architectures that combine textiles with more controlled microfluidic interfaces will be essential [153, 154].

Dental appliance platforms

Dental appliances, including retainers and mouthguards, provide access to the oral cavity for salivary glucose monitoring. Saliva offers larger biofluid volumes ( ~ 1 milliliter/minute production) and convenient collection; however, glucose concentrations are even lower than those found in tears ( ~ 0.01-0.1 mM), necessitating highly sensitive detection [155]. Therefore, integration within dental appliances must address mechanical robustness to withstand masticatory forces, biocompatibility with oral mucosa, and food/beverage interference [76].

User-centered design principles

Comfort and wearability

User-centered design is paramount because the clinical utility of even the most technologically advanced devices is undermined if patients perceive them as uncomfortable, stigmatizing, or overly challenging to operate. Research on user acceptability consistently highlights four critical factors: physical comfort, discreet aesthetic appeal, intuitive ease of use, and robust operational reliability [146]. Optimal physical comfort necessitates that the device is flexible and conforms to body movement, possesses a low mass of 50 grams, lacks sharp or complex pressure points, and utilizes biocompatible, breathable materials to mitigate moisture buildup and prevent dermatological complications [74, 78]. Indeed, studies consistently demonstrate that wearability issues, specifically discomfort arising from adhesive materials and sensor displacement during routine daily activities, represent the most significant barriers to the long-term adoption of continuous glucose monitoring technology [74].

Aesthetic and social considerations

A discreet appearance means patients want to avoid drawing attention to their diabetes by choosing skin-colored devices, slim shapes, and items that match what they already wear, such as watches or jewelry [156]. Research has shown that sensor visibility can lead to unwanted attention and negative emotions, particularly among younger patients who are more conscious of appearance [156]. Comfort also matters when using the device for putting it on, setting it up, reading the data, and fixing problems [146, 157].

Operational simplicity

Sensor application procedures have been streamlined, often utilizing a single-button adhesive mechanism, which enables autonomous user replacement without requiring clinician assistance. Minimizing calibration requirements, ideally via factory-calibrated sensors that eliminate the need for blood glucose reference measurements, significantly reduces patient burden [10, 158]. Indeed, contemporary continuous glucose monitoring systems have largely eliminated calibration requirements; devices such as the Dexcom G6 and FreeStyle Libre systems are now approved for nonadjunctive use without mandatory finger-stick verification [158]. Intuitive data visualization platforms (e.g., smartphone or smartwatch applications) display trend arrows, push hypoglycemia alerts at user-defined thresholds (typically 70 mg dL⁻¹), and forward time-in-range summaries to electronic health records, giving clinicians actionable data within the same visit without requiring specialized training by presenting real-time glucose trends, predicted trajectories, and actionable clinical alerts (e.g., impending hypoglycemia warnings or AI-driven pre-meal insulin dose recommendations). Robust operational performance across diverse environmental stressors, including temperature extremes, humidity variations, and mechanical perturbations, is crucial for ensuring reliable data integrity during patients' varied daily activities [15, 76].

Collectively, this integrative framework moves beyond descriptive comparison toward prescriptive design guidance: by aligning the choice of biofluid, transduction mechanism, biorecognition element, and substrate to the four overarching criteria and to specific clinical use cases, developers can prioritize design decisions rather than iteratively rediscovering trade-offs. We anticipate that this matrix will serve as a reusable scaffold for subsequent comparative studies, regulatory submissions, and translational roadmaps in wearable diabetes care.

Integrative design framework and decision-making matrix

In response to the need for a unified, actionable framework that consolidates the biofluid (Section 2.2, Table 2), transduction (Section 2.3), and microfluidic (Section 3.1) options reviewed above, we introduce here an integrative design matrix organized around four overarching design criteria that govern wearable multi-analyte biosensor performance: (1) selectivity and analytical fidelity, (2) mechanical compliance and skin conformity, (3) power autonomy and energy budget, and (4) clinical translatability and regulatory readiness. Each criterion is mapped to specific design choices drawn from the preceding sections, enabling researchers and clinicians to systematically navigate the design space rather than treating each layer in isolation.

Criterion 1—Selectivity and Analytical Fidelity. The combined choice of biorecognition element (enzyme, aptamer, MIP), transduction modality, and channel geometry determines whether a platform meets ISO 15197 accuracy targets. Our synthesis suggests that, for glucose, glucose-oxidase amperometry on Pt/Au nanostructured electrodes paired with serpentine microfluidics delivers the most reproducible MARD (8–11%); for cortisol or insulin, aptamer-functionalized graphene FETs paired with valved sampling provide superior cross-reactivity rejection.

Criterion 2—Mechanical Compliance and Skin Conformity. Effective skin coupling requires moduli matched to the epidermis (10⁵–10⁶ Pa) and stretchability >30%. PDMS- and Ecoflex-encapsulated serpentine interconnects, kirigami-patterned metal films, and conductive textile substrates each occupy distinct niches in the matrix; textile substrates are recommended for long-duration ( > 24 h) wear, whereas thin-film polymer patches are preferred for high-fidelity transient sampling [159].

Criterion 3—Power Autonomy and Energy Budget. Continuous multi-analyte operation demands <5 mW average power; our matrix flags amperometric electrochemical channels ( < 1 mW) and potentiometric channels ( < 0.1 mW) as compatible with flexible Zn–air or biofuel-cell sources, whereas optical and FET modalities require energy-harvesting augmentation (triboelectric, photovoltaic) to maintain >7-day autonomy [160].

Criterion 4— Clinical Translatability and Regulatory Readiness. Beyond benchtop metrics, real-world translation depends on biocompatibility certification (ISO 10993), cybersecurity compliance (EU Cyber Resilience Act 2024), and interoperability with closed-loop insulin delivery [161]. Architectures with modular, replaceable sensing cartridges and standardized Bluetooth Low Energy Continua-compliant interfaces score highest on this axis.

Performance metrics and clinical validation

The translation of novel engineering architectures into tangible clinical utility depends fundamentally on robust performance evaluation employing standardized metrics. This subsequent section synthesizes current evidence concerning both analytical and clinical validation parameters, establishing how rigorous assessment frameworks bridge the gap between laboratory prototypes and real-world diabetes care.

Analytical performance parameters

Quantitative benchmarking of limit of detection ( ≤ 10 µM for glucose), linear dynamic range (0.1–30 mM), and inter-device coefficient of variation ( < 8%) determines whether a biosensor meets ISO 15197 accuracy criteria for clinical use [162]. This encompasses critical parameters such as sensitivity, specificity, Limit of Detection, Linear Dynamic Range (LDR), selectivity, response time, and measurement frequency. These features are paramount for overcoming the limitations inherent in single-analyte systems, where cross-reactivity can severely confound clinical interpretation.

Sensitivity and specificity

Today’s electrochemical microneedle array glucose biosensors exhibit sensitivities ranging from 50 to 100 μA·mM⁻¹·cm⁻². Their specificity exceeds 95% against common interferents such as ascorbic acid, uric acid, and acetaminophen, primarily due to selective enzyme immobilization [20, 130]. Lactate oxidase-based electrodes achieve comparable metrics, with sensitivities of 25–60 μA·mM⁻¹·cm⁻² and specificities of 92–98%, while exhibiting cross-reactivity of less than 5% [36]. Additionally, cortisol immunosensors exhibit sensitivities of 0.5 to 2 nA·nM⁻¹ and greater than 90% specificity against structurally similar steroid hormones [70, 163].

Limit of detection (LOD)

According to Baranwal et al. various glucose sensors can detect glucose levels as low as 0.06 mM (1 mg/dL). Thus, they can alert users to instances of hypoglycemia and/or hyperglycemia at clinically relevant thresholds [164]. According to Zhou et al. (2024), ketone sensors with a LOD of <0.05 mM for β-hydroxybutyrate can enable DKA prophylaxis. Aptamer-functionalized graphene in biosensors for cytokine detection enables LODs of 1-10 pg/mL for interleukin-6 (IL-6) [25]. The linear dynamic range (LDR) of modern glucose sensors is 2.2-22.2 mM (40-400 mg/dL). Non-enzymatic nanostructured electrodes have been reported to extend the upper limit to 33.3 mM (600 mg/dL) with linearity errors better than 2% [78]. Lactate and cortisol sensors have LDRs of 0.1–20 mM and 0.1–100 nM, respectively, which encompass exercise- and stress-induced changes [165].

Selectivity and cross-sensitivity

Multi-analyte platforms necessitate the mitigation of inter-analyte interference, particularly pH-dependent effects on potentiometric electrolyte measurements. To address these challenges, selective membranes and spatially separated sensing domains have been demonstrated to limit cross-sensitivity to less than 5% in integrated platforms [70, 166].

Temporal response characteristics

Response times under 30 s, enabled by microfluidic preconcentration and optimized enzyme kinetics, provide near-real-time feedback. Building on this speed, continuous measurement frequencies of 1–5-min intervals generate >400 data points daily, vastly exceeding intermittent self-monitoring and providing high-resolution input for AI predictive algorithms [8, 25].

Clinical accuracy and correlation studies

Mean absolute relative difference (MARD)

MARD serves as the gold standard metric for quantifying CGM accuracy; it is calculated as the mean percentage difference between sensor-measured glucose values and reference blood glucose measurements. An MARD below 10% indicates excellent clinical accuracy, and regulatory agencies require iCGM devices to meet this standard for approval [167, 168]. Building on these regulatory benchmarks, recent validation studies have demonstrated glucose MARD values of less than 8% across ISF, sweat, and saliva matrices compared to reference methods [78, 130]. Contemporary multi-analyte platforms achieve MARD values of 5–7% for lactate and 6–9% for β-hydroxybutyrate in clinical validation trials [20].

Error grid analysis

The Consensus Error Grid (CEG), updated in 2024 for continuous glucose monitors (CGMs), sorts sensor and reference glucose readings into risk zones: Zone A (accurate results), Zone B (minor errors), Zone C (risk of overreacting), Zone D (missed problem spots), and Zone E (risk of wrong treatment) [168, 169]. Rules require that at least 99% of readings are in Zones A and B. Today’s CGM devices reach 99.4–99.9% in Zones A and B for regular and high blood sugar, but they have more trouble with low blood sugar, getting only 96–98% in those zones when glucose is below 70 mg/dL [170, 171].

ISO 15197

2013 Accuracy Criteria: The ISO 15197:2013 rules state that at least 95% of blood sugar readings should be very close to lab results—either within 15 milligrams per deciliter for values under 100 mg/dL, or within 15% for values 100 mg/dL or higher. Studies show no current CGM meets this rule for low blood sugar, with only 82–89% of readings below 70 mg/dL falling within these limits [170].

Correlation analysis

Multi-analyte biosensors show strong correlations with reference methods for various biological fluids: ISF glucose (R² > 0.95; lag of 5–10 min), sweat glucose (R² = 0.70–0.95; lag of 5–30 min), salivary glucose (R² = 0.50–0.80; lag of 5–20 min), and tear glucose (ρ = 0.95–0.97; variable lag) [44, 70].

Real-world clinical validation evidence and comparative effectiveness

Demonstrating the true benefit of analytical tools requires strong real-world evidence supporting improved patient outcomes, reduced healthcare utilization, and tangible cost efficiencies. This evidence is now emerging, confirming the substantial clinical utility of multi-analyte biosensor platforms integrated with advanced AI algorithms in real-world clinical settings.

Glycemic control and time-in-range outcomes

Ajjan et al. reviewed studies on continuous glucose monitoring in people with type 2 diabetes across different healthcare settings. Their analysis in Nature Reviews Endocrinology showed lasting improvements in blood sugar control; besides better blood sugar control, the time spent in a healthy range improved from 58% to 73% (an increase of 15 percentage points). This is higher than the 5 percentage points considered important by experts [8, 172].

Glycemic ups and downs also got smaller, with a drop from 38% to 31% in the amount of variation in blood sugar levels. Less fluctuation is linked to fewer small blood vessel problems [173]. Serious low blood sugar events that required medical help decreased by 30–40%. This drop was even more noticeable in people aged 65 or older, who saw these events fall by 52% [174]. Importantly, these findings were consistent across diverse patient groups: individuals with suboptimal baseline glycemic control (defined as HbA1c > 9%), elderly individuals at high risk for hypoglycemia, and patients on non-intensive treatment regimens. All of these groups used continuous glucose monitoring and experienced similar improvements [8]. These results highlight the considerable benefits of CGM alone—typically improving TIR by 10–15 percentage points and reducing severe hypoglycaemic events by 30–40% [175, 176]. AI-enabled systems can further enhance glycaemic control by leveraging the continuous data streams generated by CGM. Rather than reacting to threshold crossings, machine learning models trained on high‑resolution CGM data can forecast impending excursions 20–60 min in advance. When combined with additional inputs such as insulin dosing history, meal timing, physical activity, and sleep patterns, these systems can recommend or automatically execute pre‑emptive adjustments [177, 178]. Clinical and in silico studies consistently show that such predictive, multimodal control strategies yield incremental improvements in TIR beyond those achieved by CGM alone, while also attenuating glycaemic variability and further lowering the risk of hypoglycaemia [179, 180].

Healthcare resource utilization and cost-effectiveness

Norman et al. [181] conducted a large retrospective cohort study. They used United States commercial insurance claims from 47,823 patients with type 2 diabetes who started continuous glucose monitoring. These patients were compared with matched controls who continued self-monitoring their blood glucose. Their analysis appeared in the Journal of Managed Care & Specialty Pharmacy. It showed significant reductions in acute care utilization:- Specifically, emergency department visits for diabetes-related conditions showed a 47% reduction within 12 months of CGM initiation (adjusted relative risk 0.53, 95% CI: 0.49–0.58, p < 0.001) [181].

There was a 38% decrease in diabetes-related inpatient admissions (adjusted RR 0.62, 95% CI: 0.56–0.68). This decrease was largely due to reductions in hyperglycemic crises (diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state) and hypoglycemia-related admissions [181]. Furthermore, healthcare cost savings were notable: a mean annual per-patient reduction of $1,847 in diabetes-related healthcare expenditures, with break-even achieved within 8 months despite CGM device costs [181]. Moreover, medication adherence improved, with insulin therapy persistence increasing by 23%, reducing clinical inertia and treatment gaps [182]. Similar cost-effectiveness was demonstrated in European healthcare systems. Guerci et al. (2023) analyzed French national health insurance data from 74,011 patients with type 2 diabetes on basal insulin therapy and reported a 64% reduction in hospitalizations for acute diabetes events within 6 months of Freestyle Libre system initiation, resulting in annual savings of € 2,340 per patient [183].

Multi-analyte platform validation

Single-analyte glucose monitoring (tracking glucose alone) still dominates clinical practice. However, new multi-analyte platforms measure glucose, lactate, ketones, and cortisol (a stress hormone), and these platforms show superior early detection of metabolic decompensation. For example, Gavin and Bailey (2023) reviewed real-world evidence on continuous glucose monitoring in the journal Diabetes Technology & Therapeutics, providing critical insights on integrating multiple biomarkers (multiple biological markers). Integrating additional analytes has yielded substantial benefits: by combining blood sugar and ketone monitoring, we reduced hospitalizations for DKA by 42–58%. This approach allowed high levels of ketones in the blood (over 0.6 mmol/L) to be found before symptoms appeared [184]. Similarly, for exercise physiology optimization, glucose-lactate monitoring enabled personalized exercise prescriptions. Lactate thresholds (2–4 mmol/L) helped identify optimal aerobic training zones and prevented exercise-induced hypoglycemia [185].

Beyond metabolic and exercise uses, monitoring extra stress markers adds insight. For the stress-hyperglycemia link, sweat cortisol monitoring in hospitalized patients showed circadian disruption. These changes related to nighttime hyperglycemia and helped optimize insulin timing [186]. Early studies of electrochemical sensor arrays found that they can simultaneously detect glucose, lactate, and ketones in interstitial fluid. These sensors matched reference laboratory methods 92–96% of the time in relevant ranges [53].

Comparative effectiveness: CGM vs. self-monitoring blood glucose

Direct head-to-head comparisons show that CGM is superior to fingerstick self-monitoring. Morris-Murray and Frazzitta (2024) examined HEDIS quality metrics for diabetes care. They found that CGM adoption improved quality: 68% of CGM users achieved HbA1c < 8% versus 52% of SMBG users (OR 1.96, 95% CI: 1.84–2.09) [182]. CGM also improved hypoglycemia awareness. Impaired awareness, common in 25% of insulin-treated patients, improved in 71% of CGM users through real-time alerts and predictive low-glucose alarms [187].

In addition, patient-reported outcomes improved with CGM use. Diabetes distress scores decreased by 31% (p < 0.001), and diabetes-specific quality of life improved significantly (Effect size d = 0.64) compared to SMBG [188]. Finally, broader evidence supports these findings. A systematic bibliometric analysis by Novihoho et al. covered 1247 clinical trials assessing CGM in diabetes management from 2014 to 2024. The analysis found accelerating evidence generation. Notably, 68% of trials published after 2020 showed real-world effectiveness in diverse populations, including pregnant women, hospitalized patients, and those with chronic kidney disease [189].

Clinical validation gaps and future directions

Despite robust evidence for glucose monitoring, multi-analyte biosensor validation remains incomplete:

One important challenge is variability in biomarker correlations: Sweat glucose concentrations (0.01–0.5 mmol/L) show weaker correlations with blood glucose (R² = 0.70–0.85) than interstitial fluid (R² > 0.95), necessitating biofluid-specific validation [190].

Algorithm generalizability

AI predictive algorithms trained predominantly on Caucasian populations demonstrate reduced accuracy in Asian (MARD + 2.1%) and African American (MARD + 2.8%) cohorts, highlighting the need for diverse training datasets [191]. Furthermore, long-term sensor stability requires attention: Beyond 14-day wear periods, sensor drift increases mean absolute relative difference (MARD) from 8.9% to 13.2%, requiring advanced calibration-free technologies [192]. To clarify priorities, future clinical validation should focus on standardized reporting frameworks, specifically on the adoption of consensus-defined core outcome sets, detailed subgroup analyses by diabetes phenotype, and comprehensive health equity assessments across socioeconomic strata [193].

Longitudinal studies and patient outcomes

Longitudinal evaluations show sustained impacts on glycemic control and quality of life. Significantly, multi-analyte integration further enhances benefits beyond glucose-only monitoring.

Quality of life

SF-36 and the Diabetes Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire (DTSQ) assessments indicate 5–12-point increases in the physical and diabetes-specific domains, due to reduced monitoring burden and enhanced self-management. Hybrid platforms are associated with 15% lower anxiety scores, promoting adherence [8, 164].

Machine learning architectures

Machine learning architectures distill complex physiological patterns into actionable insights through supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning modalities [204]. Beyond biochemical sensing, emerging AI frameworks are increasingly integrating conventional physiological parameters—blood pressure, heart rate, electrodermal activity, skin temperature, and electrocardiographic patterns—with biochemical markers from biofluids [205]. Fusing electrochemical, optical, and motion channels through a transformer-based late-fusion model raises 30-minute hypoglycemia prediction AUROC from 0.82 (CGM-only) to 0.93 and lowers false-alarm rate by ~40% in benchmark cohorts by linking dynamic metabolic data to concurrent hemodynamic and autonomic indicators [206, 207]. Hybrid neural networks or attention-based fusion models can jointly learn the correlations between biochemical fluctuations and broader physiological responses, offering early warnings for glycemic excursions, dehydration, or stress-related disorders [201, 208]. Furthermore, including structured clinical examination data (e.g., anthropometric indices, medication use, and comorbidities) can improve personalization and reduce false positives [209, 210]. Real-world deployment of these combined models requires harmonized data acquisition protocols, sensor time-synchronization, and privacy-compliant interoperability standards to ensure reproducible and clinically actionable outputs [211].

Supervised learning

Logistic regression and random forest models, enhanced by feature engineering from CGM and EHR data, achieve over 85% accuracy in classifying T2DM [212]. These models outperform traditional scoring systems such as FINDRISC by incorporating dynamic variables, including HbA1c trajectories and lipid profiles (Farnoosh et al., 2025). Support vector regression predicts postprandial glucose with a root mean square error (RMSE) of <15 mg/dL, enabling precise insulin adjustments in T1DM cohorts [213]. Ensemble strategies improve the area under the curve (AUC) to 0.92, effectively addressing overfitting in imbalanced datasets [214].

Unsupervised learning

K-means and hierarchical clustering on metabolomic and CGM profiles identify five endotypes in T2DM populations, with severe insulin-resistant clusters exhibiting 2.5-fold higher nephropathy risk (Ahlqvist et al., 2024). Gaussian mixture models delineate prediabetes phenotypes; one cluster shows accelerated progression (HR = 1.8) linked to inflammatory markers [25]. Multi-analyte data integration reduces misclassification by 20% compared to supervised baselines.

Reinforcement learning

Q-learning agents optimize basal-bolus regimens, achieving 15% improvements in TIR over rule-based systems and adapting to meal variability in real-time [215]. Deep RL actor-critic frameworks personalize corrections for exercise-induced fluctuations, achieving 90% adherence to safe glycemic zones in prospective trials [216].

Deep learning applications

Deep learning models automatically discover functional data patterns, achieving high predictive accuracy with recurrent, convolutional, and transformer networks.

Recurrent networks

Bidirectional long short-term memory (LSTM) networks predict 60-minute glucose levels with an RMSE of 10.6 mg/dL. They perform better than Auto Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models by better handling daily patterns [25].

Convolutional networks

Attention-based CNNs, such as Diabetes XpertNet, achieve 90% accuracy in classifying the risk of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) from tabular data by dynamically weighting glucose-insulin interactions [25]. Meanwhile, CNNs applied to retinal imaging achieve an AUC of 0.94 for predicting retinopathy progression [217].

Transformers

Self-attention mechanisms, which allow networks to weigh the influence of all input elements when modeling sequences, excel in capturing long-range dependencies. Hybrid models combining Transformers with LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) networks achieve 96% Clarke Zone A + B accuracy up to 120 min, surpassing standalone LSTMs by 8% [218, 219].

Hybrid architectures

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)-GRU hybrids achieve RMSE 2.6 mg/dL at 60 min by fusing CNNs for local pattern detection with recurrent networks for temporal modeling [216]. Multimodal fusions incorporating CGM with EHR baselines enhance ISF predictions (MAPE 19% at 30 min) [220]. Transformer-VAE variants improve robustness to noisy data and forecast disease burden with 25% lower MAE than LSTMs [221]. AI-enhanced nursing protocols and clinical workflows

The use of AI changes diabetes nursing through intelligent automation, predictions, and improved decision-making. Recent findings show that AI tools not only make care more efficient but also improve patient outcomes by enabling accurate treatments and rapid risk assessments [177, 231].

Intelligent triage and priority setting

AI-driven triage systems help allocate nursing resources and ensure timely care for high-risk patients. Machine learning (ML) tools in live monitoring dashboards can accurately detect blood sugar emergencies. This helps nurses act quickly and reduce dangerous episodes of low blood sugar [213, 232]. For example, Lebech Cichosz et al. (2024) created an ML model using continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data. It predicts each week’s risk of low blood sugar for people with Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) [233]. These smart dashboards combine CGM data, medication records, vital signs, and patient self-reports. They automatically create easy-to-use risk scores that help nurses prioritize their patients [16]. Automated alert generation has evolved beyond simple static thresholds to incorporate sophisticated pattern recognition, thereby mitigating alert fatigue. Context-aware deep learning systems have shown promise in reducing false-positive alerts while maintaining high sensitivity for clinically significant glycemic excursions, thus improving staff alert response rates [17, 234]. Lu et al. (2025) introduced CGM Former, an attention-based, context-aware transformer model for decoding individual glucose dynamics from CGM data, showcasing its potential for highly intelligent and personalized pattern recognition [235]. Multi-tiered escalation pathways automatically adjust notification urgency based on factors like alert persistence, rate of change in glucose, and patient vulnerability profiles [18].

Risk-based workload distribution algorithms further advance workflow optimization. AI-driven nurse assignment systems dynamically allocate patients based on real-time acuity scores, nurse competency profiles, and predicted workload trajectories, showing strong potential to optimize nurse-to-patient ratios and minimize medication delays during high-census periods [236, 237]. Othman et al. (2025) specifically explored how ML models can optimize nurse-patient assignments, thereby influencing care dynamics and strategic staffing needs in acute hospital settings [238].

Decision support systems for insulin therapy

Contemporary AI decision-support systems offer advanced insulin-dosing algorithms. These algorithms integrate multiple physiological parameters while adhering to stringent safety constraints. Panagiotou et al. provided a comprehensive review demonstrating that AI systems, particularly those using reinforcement learning, can personalize insulin dosing and improve blood glucose control without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia [215, 239]. These systems use continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data, along with dietary, activity, and circadian rhythm data, to recommend appropriate insulin dosages. This approach allows clinicians to anticipate how blood glucose levels will change before starting treatment [214]. Building on these capabilities, AI can also adjust insulin sensitivity settings for individuals. Panagiotou et al. demonstrated, through in silico validation, that personalized insulin sensitivity adjustment with reinforcement learning can reduce hyperglycemic episodes and hypoglycemic events. This approach accounts for factors such as stress, medication use, and circadian variations in insulin sensitivity [215]. These systems provide advanced decision support in hybrid closed-loop and open-loop insulin therapy contexts [215, 240].

Furthermore, to prevent dangerously low blood sugar from excessive insulin, systems have been developed to monitor and display insulin activity and predict blood sugar levels, providing advice on dosing timing to reduce insulin stacking and related hypoglycemia [215, 241]. The system displays both insulin activity and predicted blood sugar levels, making it easier for nurses to interpret and apply in clinical practice [242].

Patient education and behavioral interventions

AI chatbots can transform diabetes education by offering scalable, personalized, ongoing support. Using natural language processing, these chatbots tailor learning through adaptive conversations and have demonstrated improvements in knowledge, self-confidence, and glycemic control in patients with diabetes [243, 244]. In their 2025 scoping review, Li et al. reported that AI-driven mobile health applications employing natural language processing are practical tools for diabetes self-management education [245]. By identifying knowledge gaps, providing information at appropriate reading levels, and testing learning using spaced repetition principles, these chatbots facilitate more effective education [246].

Building on the benefits of chatbots, reminders can do more than deliver information—they can be tailored to how users behave rather than following a set schedule. Systems that adjust reminders based on activity, location, and past actions have helped people stick to their medication and monitor themselves better [247, 248]. In the REINFORCE trial, Lauffenburger et al. found that using reinforcement learning to personalize messages improved medication adherence at a low cost, while also providing education and motivation tailored to each patient [249]. These adaptive systems can keep updating the timing and content of reminders based on user feedback and engagement [250]. Furthermore, motivational messages based on user psychology also enhance engagement. Personalized motivational messaging designed for different user types—such as achievement-oriented, social support-seeking, fear-avoidant, and personal-satisfaction-driven individuals—has shown increased engagement and more lasting behavior change compared to generic messaging [251, 252].

Remote patient monitoring and telehealth integration

AI significantly enhances the efficacy of remote diabetes monitoring. Asynchronous telehealth workflows, wherein AI analyzes Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) data, flags complex patterns, and generates clinical assessment summaries, empower nurses to manage substantially larger patient panels while maintaining clinical quality and user satisfaction [253, 254]. This strategy establishes an effective division of labor: AI excels at complex pattern recognition and dynamic risk assessment, while human clinicians apply clinical judgment and sustain the essential therapeutic relationship [255].

When utilized during virtual consultations, AI-synthesized summaries facilitate comprehensive telemedicine management. AI algorithms that synthesize CGM metrics, medication adherence reports, physical activity patterns, and dietary logs provide structured summaries highlighting emergent issues, potentially reducing consultation times while maintaining or improving patient satisfaction [256, 257]. Mbanugo (2025) specifically examined how AI-enhanced telemedicine effectively streamlines clinical workflows and provides intelligent remote monitoring for chronic disease management, including diabetes [194].

Beyond optimizing routine diabetes care, AI plays a critical role in enhancing patient safety during remote monitoring. AI-driven alert systems employ multi-tiered escalation protocols, including severity-based prioritization, multi-modal patient outreach, and automated contact with emergency services when necessary. These systems have demonstrated rapid response times and successful patient contact for critical severe hypoglycemia alerts [258, 259].

Documentation automation and clinical burden reduction

Natural language processing eases significant documentation workloads. NLP systems that automatically generate structured notes from audio-recorded encounters and integrate with EHRs have yielded substantial reductions in documentation time while improving note completeness [248]. Michalowski et al. outlined a vision for an AI-enabled nursing future with markedly diminished documentation burdens via automated data capture and generation [260]. This process applies medical entity recognition, clinical reasoning inference, and template population to translate raw data into compliant documentation [261]. Moreover, beyond documentation, automated regulatory compliance reporting reduces administrative burdens. AI systems that continuously monitor activities, flag reportable metrics, and auto-generate pre-filled reports requiring minimal verification streamline compliance documentation and help prevent missed deadlines [262, 263]. Al Jnainati et al. analyzed how AI-driven data entry and documentation automation accelerate documentation and reduce administrative burdens in healthcare settings [262].

Additionally, efficiency gains from AI implementation are underscored by time-motion studies. Koc and Alpar (2025) demonstrated through time-motion studies that lean hospital practices and technological interventions can significantly impact nurses’ time allocation, with potential reductions in documentation and administrative time leading to increases in direct patient care time [264]. These improvements correlate with reduced burnout and enhancements in patient education delivery and care plan adherence [265]. This synergistic collaboration between humans and AI offers an optimal pathway toward sustainable, high-quality diabetes care, addressing both clinical effectiveness and workforce sustainability [266].

Integration framework: biosensors, ai, and nursing practice

Successful convergence of multi-analyte wearable biosensors, artificial intelligence, and nursing protocols requires coherent ecosystem architecture, human-AI collaboration models, implementation strategies, and ethical governance frameworks.

Closed-loop ecosystem architecture

Modern systems are rapidly transitioning toward integrated closed-loop ecosystems that unify continuous data collection, AI-driven analysis, and clinical decision support within a singular, cohesive platform [267]. These setups use REST APIs with OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect to ensure secure, real-time communication between devices and central systems [268, 269]. Randine et al. suggested using FHIR-based interoperability to solve current problems with real-time data access in hybrid closed-loop systems. Their approach uses standard FHIR resources and clinical codes like SNOMED CT and LOINC to support clinical interoperability and data-sharing across borders [270]. Still, only one CGM device on the market currently provides public APIs, indicating significant gaps that are holding back ecosystem growth [270]. Mackenzie et al. [232] pointed out that the lack of digital interoperability between diabetes devices and EHR platforms is a key obstacle. They also noted that the European Union has required all medical records to be integrated by 2025 [270]. Without standard ways to exchange data, systems become proprietary, making it harder to reuse data and limiting how AI decision support can be used, especially in hybrid closed-loop systems that produce thousands of data points each month [270].

Advanced frameworks facilitate bidirectional communication, enabling AI platforms to receive biosensor data and transmit actionable recommendations to nursing interfaces and automated delivery systems [267, 271]. Van Wilder et al. evaluated Belgian hybrid care pathways with continuous 24/7 monitoring by trained nurse operators, which improved glycemic parameters, though cost-effectiveness analyses revealed challenges posed by recurring operational expenses [272]. Nimri et al. documented that EHR integration requires overcoming substantial technical and organizational barriers, including inconsistent formats, insufficient semantic interoperability, and institutional resistance [239, 273]. Most AI decision support systems effectively synthesize CGM, laboratory results, and medication histories, but remain confined to research or specialized centers rather than distributed across primary care [239].

Human-AI collaboration models

Optimal collaboration exists along a spectrum from fully automated to nurse-in-the-loop architectures. Emerging evidence indicates hybrid models integrating explainable AI with nursing oversight yield superior outcomes compared to extremes of full automation or conventional care [274]. Price et al. found that pediatric diabetes clinics with dedicated nursing staff for care coordination showed significantly higher adherence to evidence-based practice, underscoring that human oversight remains essential for contextualizing AI recommendations within patient circumstances, family dynamics, and psychosocial complexities that algorithms cannot adequately capture [275]. Also, the study documented that trust in AI correlates strongly with automation bias—over-relying on automated recommendations while discounting contradictory clinical judgment [276]. Recent systematic analyses reveal that automation bias in clinical decision support systems poses significant risks, particularly when clinicians become overly reliant on AI recommendations while ignoring their own clinical judgment [277]. Balanced models that require explicit nursing confirmation mitigate automation bias while preserving efficiency gains. Systems that replaced nursing judgment entirely generated professional resistance and safety concerns about accountability.

Explainability enables nurses to understand algorithmic reasoning and calibrate trust appropriately [278]. Bignami et al. documented that European regulatory strategies prioritize explainable AI and require safety frameworks for human-AI collaboration, particularly for high-risk applications such as autonomous dose optimization [279]. Black-box algorithms undermine clinical transparency, complicating professional accountability where nurses cannot validate reasoning when decision pathways remain opaque—particularly salient for insulin dosing and hypoglycemia prediction, where errors carry immediate safety implications.

Appropriate trust calibration emerges as essential. Meimandi et al. demonstrated, through a systematic review, that low-trust calibration—over-trusting unreliable systems or under-trusting reliable ones—substantially undermines safety and efficiency [280]. Trust calibration improves through structured training, providing realistic expectations of AI performance characteristics, including known failure modes and uncertainty quantification.

Professional autonomy concerns remain significant barriers. Correia (2025) analyzed how AI affects bioethical principles such as autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice. Systems that constrain judgment face resistance. Successful frameworks preserve nursing autonomy by positioning AI as support, allowing nurses to override when clinical circumstances require [207].

Ethical governance and regulatory compliance

Biosensor-AI-nursing integration raises complex ethical challenges spanning patient autonomy, data ownership, algorithmic bias, liability frameworks, and regulatory compliance. Continuous health data generation raises unprecedented questions about informed consent and the use of secondary data for research or commercial purposes.

Data rights and accessibility

While GDPR mandates patient control over personal health data, practical accessibility remains constrained [270]. Hybrid closed-loop systems often provide proprietary Comma-Separated Values (CSV) files with inadequate documentation. This can violate GDPR’s Right to Data Portability principles, as technically unsophisticated users cannot effectively understand or reuse their information. The lack of real-time data access and interoperability in diabetes medical devices underscores the need for publicly accessible APIs. These APIs should prioritize transparency, accessibility, and patient-driven innovation [270]. Furthermore, interoperability challenges in medical devices extend beyond diabetes management. Standardized APIs remain critically underutilized across healthcare sectors [281]. Consent frameworks must explicitly delineate how biosensor data will be used. This includes immediate care, algorithm training, quality improvement, and commercial applications. Multistakeholder collaboration among patients, clinicians, researchers, industry, and regulators is required.

Algorithmic bias and health equity

AI models underperform when trained on datasets that do not accurately represent the population, particularly for groups defined by gender, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geography. This can result in incorrect clinical recommendations and worsen health disparities [282]. A systematic review of FDA-approved AI medical devices found that only 3.6% of approvals reported race or ethnicity data, and 99.1% did not provide socioeconomic data, revealing significant reporting gaps that may contribute to algorithmic bias [283]. For instance, in diabetes technology research, 13 of 23 pediatric studies mentioned equity, but most did so only briefly rather than as a primary focus. Disparities also persist in the use of continuous glucose monitoring and insulin pumps among racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic groups in the United States [284]. Ongoing bias assessment, diversification of training data, and continuous performance monitoring across demographic subgroups are essential to achieve equitable AI-enabled healthcare [282].

Legal liability

Traditional liability frameworks assume human decision-makers are accountable, while AI contributes to decisions without legal responsibility. This creates ambiguity about whether liability lies with clinicians who use algorithmic recommendations, with institutions that deploy AI, with developers, or with a combination of these parties [285]. Most jurisdictions lack comprehensive frameworks for accountability in AI-assisted medical decisions. While the EU’s AI Act and Medical Device Regulation include relevant provisions [286], and the FDA has issued guidance on AI-enabled devices [287, 288], significant gaps remain in assigning liability when AI provides incorrect recommendations. The European regulatory landscape now includes the Medical Devices Regulation (MDR) and the AI Act, which establish updated product safety requirements, but challenges in liability attribution persist [286, 289]. Differences between the EU’s decentralized notified body system and the FDA’s centralized approval process further complicate liability and regulatory harmonization [281].

Data protection compliance

In the U.S., safeguards such as HIPAA protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of patient information. The European Union’s GDPR requires consent, data adequacy, minimization, purpose limitation, and rights to access, rectification, erasure, and portability. These regulations impose strict requirements on data usage, creating challenges for AI development, which relies on large, diverse datasets. The GDPR restricts data-sharing and secondary use. Federated learning addresses this by enabling training on distributed datasets without centralizing sensitive information [290]. This approach preserves privacy, maintains high model performance, and complies with GDPR, as demonstrated in healthcare applications such as cancer care and medical imaging [290]. International data transfers for cloud-based systems pose additional challenges, as the GDPR prohibits transfers to jurisdictions that lack adequate data protection. Effective implementation requires robust governance, including encryption, access controls, audit logging, staff training, incident response, and legal frameworks to ensure compliance.

Evolving cybersecurity requirements

The Cyber Resilience Act of the European Union, effective December 2024, sets security requirements for medical devices and imposes obligations throughout the product lifecycle [291]. Similarly, the FDA has issued cybersecurity guidance with recommendations for medical device security [287]. These evolving regulatory frameworks require a clear understanding of compliance and adaptation of organizational strategies. The convergence of cybersecurity regulation and medical device oversight creates a complex compliance environment, especially for connected diabetes devices and other networked health technologies that must comply with both the Cyber Resilience Act and Medical Device Regulation requirements [291]. To address these challenges, healthcare organizations should implement comprehensive cybersecurity asset management strategies to ensure regulatory compliance and operational security of medical devices, including continuous monitoring and improvement to maintain cyber resilience [292].

Clinical case studies and real-world applications

Multi-analyte biosensors integrated with AI-enhanced protocols demonstrate tangible benefits across diverse clinical contexts, enabling proactive interventions that improve glycemic control and reduce complications. Evidence from recent prospective cohorts, randomized trials, and implementation studies reveals emergent trends toward personalized, equitable care.

Type 1 diabetes management

Pediatric CGM implementation

Early continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) following type 1 diabetes diagnosis has demonstrated significant clinical benefits in pediatric populations [293, 294]. A 2024 study showed that starting CGM within 30 days of diagnosis led to better blood sugar control after 1 year and fewer severe low blood sugar episodes compared to delayed initiation [294]. The current 2024 guidelines recommend CGM for young children and suggest strategies to maintain glycemic stability in school settings [293]. A small pilot program combining CGM, activity trackers, and exercise education improved time-in-range for blood sugar by 12% over 6 months and reduced parents’ anxiety through remote monitoring [295].

Artificial pancreas systems

Multi-analyte automated pancreas systems (APS) using lactate, activity, and glucose data enable refined insulin delivery[15, 296]. In a 2024 double-blind crossover trial (n = 30 adults/adolescents), the CamAPS FX hybrid closed-loop system achieved a 12% gain in time-in-range (TIR) (to 72%) compared to standard insulin therapy, without increasing hypoglycemia, especially at night (P = 0.02) [7]. In a 2023 modular interoperable APS trial (n = 50 youth), fusing ketone and cortisol inputs led to an 18% improvement in TIR and a 45% reduction in DKA episodes in high-risk cases. Real-world NHS England data showed 62% HCL uptake among eligible youth by 2025, correlating with 25% fewer emergency visits [297].

Type 2 diabetes and lifestyle modification

A prospective study conducted in 2025 with 54 prediabetic adults used CGM-derived AI algorithms to stratify different subtypes of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) based on glucose excursions. The study reported 92% accuracy (AUC 0.95) in predicting progression over 12 months using CGM, which outperformed standard HbA1c tests [298]. This was presumably because the HbA1c test could not detect inflammatory phenotypes associated with a 2.3-fold increased risk of nephropathy.

A 2023 trial published in npj Digital Medicine recruited 200 prediabetic adults [299]. The intervention included the use of apps integrated with wearables, AI analysis of CGM, and activity data. The intervention prompted personalized interventions, resulting in 8% weight loss and a 14% reduction in HbA1c at 6 months, with 75% participant adherence. A randomized trial (N = 150) in 2024 tested patients with T2D using a mHealth platform combining CGM with dietary and accelerometer logs, with reinforcement learning to tune postprandial insulin delivery [300, 301]. Participants had 22% increases in TIR and 10% increases in step count, and a 28% reduction in cardiovascular events at one year (P < 0.05) [302]. An interdisciplinary review in 2023 recommended using hybrid CNN-GRU models to provide real-time glucose forecasting from multimodal inputs, resulting in 15% fewer hyperglycemic spikes during exercise [303].

Gestational diabetes monitoring

Outcome prediction

A 2023 Lancet Regional Health cohort studied 1,302 women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) at 26 weeks. CGM time above range ( > 140 mg/dL) exceeding 30% predicted a 2.1-fold higher risk of preeclampsia and a 1.8-fold increase in large-for-gestational-age births. Time-in-range (TIR) above 70% reduced neonatal hypoglycemia by 35% (adjusted OR 0.65; 95% CI 0.48–0.88). A 2024 Diabetes Care analysis of 103 overweight or obese Asian women showed that early-trimester CGM predicted GDM with an AUC of 0.95, and glucose variability metrics forecasted large-for-gestational-age with 88% sensitivity [304]. Dietary adjustments based on these findings reduced cesarean rates by 22%.

Clinical integration

The 2024 Standards of Care advocate CGM alongside blood glucose monitoring for GDM, as systematic reviews confirm 15–20% reductions in adverse outcomes with real-time adjustments [305, 306]. A 2024 prospective study linked second-trimester CGM profiles to discrete GDM subtypes, with severe insulin-resistant patterns doubling the odds of preterm delivery, guiding targeted pharmacotherapy [307].

Hospital and intensive care settings

Critical care applications

A 2023 World Journal of Diabetes review reported a 25% reduction in hypoglycemia in ICUs with CGM versus intermittent sampling, with AI-calibrated devices achieving a MARD < 10% during sepsis [11]. A 2023 trial during hypothermic cardiac surgery validated Dexcom G6 accuracy (MARD 9.2%), enabling AI-driven insulin protocols that reduced postoperative hyperglycemia by 18% and ICU stays by 1.2 days [308].

Target optimization

A 2024 Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology analysis (n = 5000 ICU diabetics) supported liberal targets (140–180 mg/dL) per 2024 Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) guidelines, with AI models predicting 30% fewer infections via real-time fusion of CGM and vital signs [309].

Underserved and resource-limited settings

Access expansion

A 2025 study in FQHCs provided CGM to patients with type 2 diabetes, yielding HbA1c reductions at 3 months via telehealth integration, with high retention despite literacy barriers [310]. Another 2025 effectiveness study in a pharmacist-run collaborative drug therapy management service for underserved individuals with diabetes found that CGM use led to a statistically significant decrease in A1C levels, with an average reduction of 2.9 ± 2.2% over 3 months, and 75.8% of participants reached an A1C < 9% [311]. A 2023 study examining continuous glucose monitors and virtual care in high-risk racial and ethnic minority populations demonstrated that integrating CGM with virtual diabetes clinics that use mobile applications improved access and outcomes in diverse populations [312].

Telemedicine integration

A 2025 systematic review evaluated federated learning models, which facilitate machine learning on decentralized data while maintaining patient privacy. The review reported that combining telemedicine with sensor platforms enhances chronic disease management, including diabetes, by enabling privacy-preserving remote monitoring [313]. The findings indicate that federated AI learning enables scalable, private, and equitable diabetes care, particularly benefiting underserved populations [314].

Current limitations and technical challenges

Despite promising real-world applications, widespread adoption faces persistent technical, clinical, and systemic barriers. These limitations, drawn from recent engineering, clinical validation, and implementation studies, underscore the gap between prototype efficacy and scalable deployment.

Sensor technology barriers

Cross-sensitivity and matrix effects

Multi-analyte electrochemical platforms exhibit signal crosstalk in complex matrices due to non-specific adsorption of interferents such as uric acid and ascorbic acid on graphene or carbon nanotube electrodes, as well as pH fluctuations [315]. Recent advances in electrochemical sensors for real-time glucose monitoring have shown that interference from electroactive substances such as uric acid and ascorbic acid remains a significant challenge, particularly in wearable sensor applications [315]. While MIP provide selective coatings for improved specificity, they add fabrication complexity without fully resolving in vivo variability [132]. A 2024 study on electroactive molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticles demonstrated high sensitivity and selectivity for glucose with a fast response time, though challenges in enzyme-free detection persist [316].

ISF Lag and accuracy

The 5–20-minute physiological lag between blood and ISF glucose exacerbates errors during rapid excursions (postprandial spikes, exercise-induced drops), yielding MARD values that can compromise accuracy [10]. A 2024 systematic review examining CGM accuracy across different conditions found that MARD values varied significantly during rapid glucose changes, particularly during exercise and the postprandial period, with some systems exceeding optimal thresholds [317]. Recent validations confirm that ISF underestimation during hypoglycemia can prompt unsafe insulin dosing decisions, highlighting the need for confirmatory measurements in critical situations [318].

Longevity and sustainability

Biofouling and enzymatic degradation limit operational windows to 7–14 days for most commercial systems. A 2024 study on improving in vivo stability and sensor lifetime with new blend membranes demonstrated that CGM sensors can work in vivo for 28 days when protected from biofouling and enzymatic degradation using advanced polymer blend membranes composed of polydimethylsiloxane and Hydrothane [319]. Green nanotechnology approaches using biocompatible gold nanoparticles or quantum dots mitigate immune responses but suffer from calibration drift due to protein adsorption [320, 321]. Solutions require hybrid transduction (optical-electrochemical) and self-calibrating nanomaterials to achieve extended wear time beyond 30 days [315]. The environmental impact of single-use CGM patches represents an emerging concern, as disposal practices and recycling inefficiencies hinder the long-term sustainability of widespread CGM adoption. Future sensor development must address both technical performance and environmental considerations through improved materials and recycling strategies.

AI model constraints

Data quality

Longitudinal CGM streams suffer 10–25% gaps from sensor failures or user non-compliance, introducing biases that degrade model performance (RMSE > 15 mg/dL in glucose forecasting) [11, 322]. Furthermore, a 2024 systematic review noted that class imbalance in datasets (e.g., underrepresentation of mild diabetic retinopathy cases) can yield false negatives of up to 18% [323]. In addition, missing multimodal inputs can amplify prediction errors[4]. While generative adversarial network (GAN) imputation strategies mitigate these issues, they can increase computational overhead by 40% [324].

Generalizability

Algorithms trained on Eurocentric cohorts exhibit 12–20% accuracy drops in South Asian or African diabetic subgroups due to unaccounted genetic or socioeconomic confounders [221, 325]. A 2025 scoping review revealed that transfer learning enhances cross-ethnic performance (AUC 0.85–0.92) but falters in low-resource settings lacking diverse validation data, perpetuating inequities [239, 326].

Interpretability

Opaque neural networks obscure decision rationales, deterring nurse-led adoption. Explainable AI (XAI) via SHAP values improves interpretability but remains under-integrated, with only 30% of 2023–2025 studies employing it [327, 328].

Computational constraints

Resource-limited wearable microcontrollers ( < 1 GHz) delay real-time hypoglycemia alerts by 2–5 min—critical during nocturnal events—necessitating cloud offloading, which raises privacy risks and increases bandwidth dependency. Federated learning enables distributed training with 25% latency reductions but demands standardized protocols [329, 330].

Clinical implementation hurdles

IT infrastructure

Legacy EHRs lack interoperability standards (FHIR gaps), resulting in 40% data silos that fragment multi-analyte inputs and delay AI-driven triage by h [331, 332]. A 2025 analysis found that 55% of AI deployment failures stemmed from API mismatches, particularly in under-resourced clinics [333].

Training and literacy

2024 surveys revealed that only 35% of diabetes educators are proficient in AI interpretation, leading to 28% of alerts being overridden due to mistrust [222, 334]. Tailored curricula integrating simulation-based explainable AI modules are needed, though scalability remains elusive amid workforce shortages.

Compliance and abandonment

Device abandonment rates reach 25–40% within six months due to usability issues (adhesive irritations) and alert fatigue, disproportionately affecting low-literacy and elderly users [156]. A 2025 mHealth review attributed 32% of discontinuations to unclear feedback loops [247]. Co-designed adaptive interfaces are essential.

Reimbursement

Fragmented coverage (e.g., U.S. Medicare lags for non-CGM biosensors) imposes out-of-pocket costs >$500/year, deterring 45% of eligible T2D patients despite cost-effectiveness ratios of $15,000–$25,000/QALY[335, 336]. Value-based models tying reimbursements to outcomes (e.g., TIR improvements) require policy harmonization.

Regulatory and standardization gaps

Validation protocols

Different rules from the FDA and EMA for measuring accuracy (e.g., using MARD instead of AUC cutoffs) make it hard to run global trials [167, 337]. A 2024 report found that only 20% of AI and machine learning devices are checked throughout their lifespans, leading to 15% of issues in blood sugar models going unreported [338].

Adaptive algorithm oversight

Building on validation challenges, adaptive algorithm oversight also faces gaps. Evolving frameworks emphasize lifecycle approaches to the total product, yet oversight of adaptive algorithms remains incomplete [285].FDA’s 2023 Predetermined Change Control Plan fails to mandate real-time retraining validations, exposing T1D closed loops to 10–15% efficacy loss due to dataset shifts [339].

Post-market surveillance

Voluntary MAUDE reporting captures <5% of AI anomalies (e.g., false hypoglycemia alerts), with 2024 EMA audits flagging 25% non-compliance in CGM surveillance [340, 341]. Addressing these gaps requires international standards such as the IMDRF’s SaMD guidelines, augmented by mandatory explainable AI logging and federated pharmacovigilance [339].

Future directions and emerging innovations44

The trajectory of diabetes care is evolving toward the graphene-based field-effect transistor sensors and federated transformer models, expanded biomarker panels, holistic care ecosystems, and precision medicine, enabling unprecedented capabilities for proactive, personalized management that goes beyond glucose-centric approaches to optimize overall metabolic health.

Next-generation sensing technologies

Implantable long-term systems

Fully implantable continuous glucose monitoring systems with a lifespan of more than 1 year represent a shift from transcutaneous sensors, which require replacement every week to every 2 weeks [342]. Emerging platforms use biocompatible polymer encapsulation to minimize the foreign-body response while maintaining analyte permeability. Other features include advanced telemetry for wireless data transmission and inductive power transfer, eliminating the need for battery replacement. Functional stability of over 180 days with an average MARD of less than 10% in early-stage clinical investigations. To successfully develop commercial systems that function reliably for more than 365 days, materials science innovation is needed. Such innovation can eliminate long-term biocompatibility issues and sensor drift [343].

Microneedle technologies

Micron-scale needle arrays penetrate the stratum corneum to access dermal ISF without stimulating pain receptors, striking a balance between invasiveness and analytical performance [20]. In terms of sensitivity, contemporary platforms achieve detection limits below 0.1 mM for glucose, lactate, and ketones, with correlations exceeding r = 0.85 with plasma measurements. Furthermore, multiplexed electrochemical arrays that integrate spatially separated working electrodes functionalized with distinct oxidases enable simultaneous three-analyte quantification from a single-site application [130]. In addition, dissolving microneedles fabricated from hyaluronic acid provide a simplified application without sharp waste, as the needle tips dissolve after penetration. However, challenges remain, such as optimizing mechanical strength for consistent penetration across variable anatomical sites and integrating with low-power wireless electronics [344].

Non-invasive breath-based biosensing

According to Woollam et al., the volatility of organic compounds in exhaled breath reflects changes in metabolic behavior associated with glycemic dysregulation [345]. Acetone concentrations in diabetic patients are 300-900 parts per billion (ppb), compared to less than 300 ppb in controls, so it can differentiate disease states using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry or metal oxide semiconductor sensors [346]. Recent clinical validations identified breath volatile organic compound (VOC) panels including isoprene, pentane, and aldehydes as hypoglycemia predictors in type 1 diabetes (T1D), achieving sensitivities over 80% with 15 to 30-min lead times prior to blood glucose decline below 70 mg/dL [345]. The profile of urine VOCs is a potential diagnostic tool for diabetic kidney disease based on disease-related metabolite patterns, including furan derivatives [347]. Efforts continue to standardize breath sampling and mitigate environmental contamination.

Nanomaterial integration

The high surface-area-to-volume ratio of nanomaterials leads to a significant increase in analytical sensitivity [348]. For instance, Xu et al. (2023) report that electrochemical sensors based on graphene and carbon nanotube technology have glucose detection thresholds approaching picomolar levels, which is significantly lower than physiological levels [349]. Furthermore, biosensors based on gold nanoparticle-enhanced surface plasmon resonance facilitate label-free, real-time quantification of inflammatory cytokines and adipokines. Multiplexing allows the simultaneous assay of over 10 analytes in the microliter volume range. Additionally, quantum dot-based fluorescent nanosensors exhibit photostability and wavelength tunability, enabling spectral multiplexing. However, clinical translation of these nanosensors requires thorough toxicological validation to assess safety. Finally, metal-organic framework materials with high porosity and tunable chemical functionalities can be used to selectively capture and concentrate analytes, thereby enhancing detection sensitivity while suppressing matrix interference [350].

Advanced AI methodologies

Federated learning

Decentralized training approaches maintain raw data locally at participating institutions while sharing only model parameter updates to central aggregators, preserving patient confidentiality and institutional data sovereignty [224]. Recent implementations demonstrate that federated glucose prediction models achieve accuracy comparable to centralized training while maintaining HIPAA compliance. Differential privacy techniques provide mathematical guarantees against re-identification risks through controlled noise injection, though balancing privacy protection against performance degradation requires careful parameter tuning. Future personalized federated learning—wherein models adapt to individual metabolic phenotypes through local fine-tuning while maintaining population-level knowledge—reconciles precision medicine objectives with privacy preservation [351, 352].

Transfer learning

Pre-training on large general datasets followed by fine-tuning on disease-specific cohorts addresses data scarcity challenges, particularly for rare glycemic events and underrepresented populations [166]. For instance, deep learning models pre-trained on ImageNet for diabetic retinopathy screening demonstrate superior performance compared to models trained exclusively on ophthalmologic images [353]. Similarly, glucose prediction models pre-trained on thousands of patients exhibit improved performance when adapted to individual users via brief personalization periods, thereby accelerating model convergence [204]. Furthermore, cross-modal transfer learning enables models trained on one data modality (genomic sequences) to predict outcomes from complementary modalities (clinical phenotypes), integrating multi-modal data despite incomplete feature overlap [166].

Causal inference

Current AI mainly works by finding correlations. However, clinical reasoning needs causation to separate real from false mechanisms [354]. To bridge this gap, new methods—like instrumental variable analysis, propensity score matching, and structural equation modeling—help us estimate treatment effects from observed data. Using these causal techniques, discovery algorithms for directed acyclic graphs can map metabolic pathway networks from complex omics data, finding main regulatory points for treatment [214]. Also, using continuous glucose monitoring and multi-analyte biosensor data may clarify what drives blood sugar swings—such as meals, stress hormones, and daily rhythms—so we can target the cause of high blood sugar rather than treating only the symptom [27].

Multi-modal deep learning

Diabetes pathophysiology is heterogeneous, encompassing genetic predisposition, lifestyle, comorbidities, and treatment responses. To synthesize such diverse data modalities, integrative frameworks are essential [355]. Multi-modal architectures that utilize modality-specific encoders and cross-modal fusion layers for joint analysis address this need. These frameworks can jointly analyze genomic sequences, continuous glucose traces, retinal fundus images, and electronic health record data, thereby identifying complex interaction patterns not evident in single-modality analyses [356]. For example, recent integration of CGM and whole-genome sequencing has improved T2D progression prediction, showing that genetic risk scores modulate individual glucose variability [357]. Furthermore, attention mechanisms in these models can automatically learn optimal weighting of complementary data sources, adapting to each individual’s data availability and quality [204].

Expanded multi-analyte panels

Miniaturized metabolomics

Microfluidic chip metabolomics platforms that integrate sample preparation, chromatographic separation, and mass spectrometric detection are promising innovations for comprehensive point-of-care metabolic assays [358]. Recent prototypes can isolate and quantify over 16 amino acids within two min from microliter blood volumes. These prototypes can specifically detect diabetes-related changes, such as elevated branched-chain amino acids. They can also detect intermediates of the tricarboxylic acid cycle that are altered. Further, they can detect dysregulated lipid species. The wearable biosensors target a few analytes, while periodic metabolomics provides comprehensive phenotyping, and the architecture is hybrid, representing near-term, realizable implementations. A study by Song and colleagues in 2025 shows that panel analysis can be combined with machine learning to classify patients into metabolic subtypes, which are distinct from one another and differ in risk of complications and treatment response profiles [298, 359].

Therapeutic drug monitoring

Therapeutic Drug Monitoring measures the concentration of available medicine in circulation, marking a shift from intermittent pharmacokinetics to closed-loop dosing [360]. Building on this shift, prototype sensors have been developed to quantify metformin, sulfonylureas, and insulin analogs in real-time. These sensors utilize antibody-based or molecularly imprinted polymer recognition elements. However, clinical validation and regulatory approval are still in progress [349, 361]. Paired with glucose-sensing and AI-driven pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic modeling, these devices will enable personalized dose titration. This titration is determined according to the individual’s absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion characteristics. As a result, these capabilities particularly benefit new diabetes drugs, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors, which exhibit high inter-patient pharmacokinetic variability [362, 363].

Microbiome metabolite monitoring

The gut microbiota influences the pathophysiology of diabetes with metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acid derivatives, and trimethylamine N-oxide. Thus, biosensors targeting them were developed. Sensors that can be worn or implanted can detect circulating butyrate, propionate, or secondary bile acids. This enables real-time evaluation of metabolic interactions between the microbiome and the host. Biosensors determine dietary and probiotic regimens based on continuous patient data, providing feedback to doctors and thereby improving the quality of metabolic care. Interactions with hormonal systems, such as the gut-brain axis (GLP-1, ghrelin) and the adipose-liver axis (adiponectin, FGF21), provide more insight into how metabolism is regulated than focusing on the pancreatic islet [358, 364].

Holistic care ecosystem evolution

Social determinants integration

According to Papadakis (2025), food insecurity, unstable housing, lack of transportation, and unsafe neighborhoods all negatively affect diabetes outcomes [365, 366]. To address these factors, social screening data are combined with biosensor measurements. AI that combines CGM patterns with local social risk levels improves predictions of hospitalization and glycemic control over clinical data alone. AI-powered programs also help community health workers identify high-risk individuals and connect them to needed services for housing, food, and transportation, as well as medical care [334].

Mental health integration

The researcher claims that depression doubles diabetes risk, and diabetes triples the rate of depression, underscoring the connection between mental and physical health. Given this link, mental health surveillance should be integrated [367]. Wearable biosensors that track cortisol, heart rate variability, sleep, and activity can act as physiological markers of psychological distress, offering passive mental health assessment without active symptom reporting [84, 368]. Machine learning models that combine these markers with validated psychiatric screening questionnaires can detect depressive symptoms with over 85% sensitivity and identify individuals needing referral.

Community health worker empowerment

AI-powered mobile apps that interpret patient biosensor data in real-time, track medication adherence, and provide evidence-based intervention advice help community health workers deliver care aligned with guidelines during home visits and phone calls [369]. NLP interfaces enable voice-based data entry and culturally relevant health education, improving usability for users with varying technical skills and language needs. NLP enables computers to process human language [370]. Federated learning frameworks, which let models improve using data from different sources without sharing raw data, drive continuous improvement by aggregating community health worker experiences across settings [339].

Blockchain data ecosystems

Distributed ledger technologies create secure, auditable frameworks for the exchange of health data. They let patients, providers, payers, and researchers share data while patients keep control over who can access it. Smart contracts automatically enforce data-sharing rules and consent. This ensures that only authorized recipients receive biosensor data and that all transfers are recorded permanently. Tokenization allows patients to earn from contributing data to research. This balances incentives while maintaining ethical standards, such as informed consent and benefit sharing [371, 372].

Precision medicine and pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenomic integration

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms in OCT genes SLC22A1 and SLC22A2 can alter metformin pharmacokinetics. A specific variant reduces metformin exposure and glycemic response, suggesting the need for genotype-based dose adjustments or alternative agents [373, 374]. Similarly, Padmanabhan et al. report that cytochrome P450 variants that affect sulfonylurea metabolism can increase the risk of hypoglycemia, enabling preemptive dose reductions [375]. Integrating pharmacogenomic testing with EHR decision support improves glycemic outcomes and reduces adverse events, though cost and clinician education remain barriers to broader use [376].

Metabolomic and proteomic biomarkers

Baseline concentrations of circulating lysophosphatidic acid inversely correlate with liraglutide-induced HbA1c reduction. Pro-inflammatory lipid mediators may disrupt GLP-1 receptor signaling, which could explain this relationship. Screening may therefore help identify individuals most likely to benefit from GLP-1 therapy [358, 377]. Lower baseline levels of 3-hydroxyisobutyric acid—a valine catabolite reflecting mitochondrial stress—are associated with greater reductions in cardiovascular risk after starting SGLT2 inhibitors, suggesting this metabolite could serve as a potential biomarker. Combining predictive indicators with AI-based recommendations can effectively and promptly match drugs to patient profiles [358, 378].

Glycemic variability signatures

Individual glucose variability is genetically determined and influenced by factors such as microbiota, daily rhythms, and stress response mechanisms, in addition to mean glucose levels [379]. Importantly, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data, when combined with machine learning clustering, have identified distinct variability phenotypes: stable euglycemia (minimal glycemic fluctuations during the day and night), excessive fluctuations (frequent transitions between hyper- and hypoglycemia), nocturnal hypoglycemia (predominantly low glucose levels during the nighttime), and postprandial hyperglycemia (marked glucose elevations specifically following meals). Each phenotype is associated with specific complication risks, allowing for targeted interventions [8, 380]. In addition, findings indicate that time-in-range metrics provide a more comprehensive view of glycemic control than HbA1c alone. These metrics further reveal detailed phenotypes by considering the rate of change, hypoglycemia frequency, and circadian consistency [381]. Notably, pharmacogenomic studies demonstrate that specific inherited variants—such as those in circadian clock genes and glucose transporter isoforms—are linked to glycemic variability. Together, these findings help identify inherited components relevant to genetic screening and optimize gene-informed therapy [382].

Comparative analysis and technology benchmarking

The evolution of diabetes management technologies necessitates rigorous comparative evaluation frameworks to assess clinical utility, accuracy, and cost-effectiveness. This section synthesizes recent evidence-based comparisons of wearable biosensor systems and AI-driven solutions, offering insights into performance disparities and technological trade-offs that influence clinical decision-making and resource allocation.

Systematic comparison of commercial devices

Head-to-head performance

Eichenlaub et al. conducted the first manufacturer-independent, simultaneous evaluation of three current-generation continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems: Dexcom G7, FreeStyle Libre 3, and Medtronic Simplera [383]. The study included 24 adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and used standardized testing protocols to recreate clinically relevant glycemic excursions. The researchers compared CGM readings against venous Yellow Springs Instrument (YSI) reference measurements. MARDs were 11.6% for FreeStyle Libre 3, 12.0% for Dexcom G7, and 11.6% for Medtronic Simplera, showing similar overall accuracy [1]. Key differences appeared when results were divided by glycemic range. FreeStyle Libre 3 and Dexcom G7 both showed better accuracy in the normoglycemic (70-180 mg/dL) and hyperglycemic ( > 180 mg/dL) ranges. Medtronic Simplera performed better during hypoglycaem episodes ( < 70 mg/dL), but still did not meet International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 15197:2013 criteria [383, 384]. These require at least 95% of measurements within ±15 mg/dL for glucose <100 mg/dL.

Moreover, the choice of the comparator method significantly affected the accuracy metrics. For example, when using the COBAS INTEGRA venous comparator, MARDs were 9.5% for Freestyle Libre 3, 9.9% for Dexcom G7, and 13.9% for Medtronic Simplera [383]. These results revealed a significant bias between measurement approaches. Taken together, these methodological differences help explain the variation across manufacturer-sponsored studies. Such studies often report lower MARDs (7.5-10.2%) than independent evaluations [383, 385, 386], likely because they optimize study procedures for each device.

Reliability assessment

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed survival to the end of life: 91.7% (95% CI: 70.6%-97.8%) for FreeStyle Libre 3, 100% for Dexcom G7, and 81.5% (95% CI: 67.5%-89.9%) for Medtronic Simplera [383]. Sensor errors caused all nine Medtronic Simplera failures. These reliability differences affect patient satisfaction, healthcare costs, and the availability of glycemic data. These factors are critical for closed-loop insulin delivery systems [387, 388].

Hypoglycemia detection

Dávila-Ruales et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of 22 studies involving 2,294 patients and 320,216 paired measurements [170]. The review found that newer CGM systems are reasonably accurate for standard and high blood sugar levels, with an average MARD of 9.4%. However, they are less sensitive to detecting low blood sugar. The average sensitivity for detecting hypoglycemia at or below 70 mg/dL was 85.7%, and specificity was 95.3%. This is better than older sensors, which had a sensitivity of 69.3% [170]. Still, these limitations mean that the devices are not accurate enough to be used without a confirmatory finger-stick blood test. None of the devices met the ISO 15197:2013 standard for accuracy at low blood sugar levels [170].

Glycemic variability metrics

Comparative analysis showed apparent differences in time-in-range metrics across CGM systems [389, 390]. When people wore different systems at the same time, the absolute TIR difference was 1-2%. Even this slight difference—14–29 min per day [389]—can affect treatment choices, pump automation, and reimbursement, which often depend on TIR. Freckmann et al. emphasized that such metric differences demand system-specific guidelines and warned against directly comparing studies without standard testing protocols [389]. Clear, harmonized standards are essential to ensure fair and accurate assessment of TIR data across CGM systems.

Economic evaluation

Van Wilder et al. demonstrated that digital supportive continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) pathways are more cost-effective than usual care for type 2 diabetes in Belgian healthcare settings, as incremental cost-effectiveness ratios favor CGM adoption when quality-adjusted life years are considered [272]. Additional economic analyses from multiple countries have also confirmed benefits: real-time CGM systems improve quality-adjusted life expectancy compared with self-monitoring of blood glucose in insulin-treated type 2 diabetes patients. Sensor longevity plays a critical role in cost-effectiveness. To illustrate, the FreeStyle Libre 3 sensor offers a 14-day wear period, while Medtronic’s Simplera lasts only 7 days; this difference leads to 50% fewer sensor changes per year. As a result, both direct costs (such as sensor units and clinical time) and indirect costs (including patient inconvenience and adhesive-related dermatological complications) are reduced [391–393].

Patent landscape

Litvinova et al. identified Dexcom Inc., Abbott Diabetes Care, and Medtronic as leading innovators in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) sensor technology. Their patent activity is concentrated on enzyme-based electrochemical sensors (which use biological enzymes to produce measurable electrical signals in response to glucose), signal-processing algorithms (software routines that interpret sensor data), and miniaturized architectures (designs that reduce device size and improve portability) [394]. This trend emphasizes optimizing accuracy through advanced calibration algorithms (methods used to adjust sensor readings to match actual blood glucose levels) rather than fundamentally changing the sensing mechanism. As a result, performance improvements may be approaching a plateau within existing technological paradigms (Table 5) [394, 395].

AI platform evaluation

Open-source vs. proprietary systems

The emergence of AI platforms has divided open-source collaborative frameworks from proprietary commercial ones. Public platforms like GluPredKit allow algorithm inspection, reproducible research, and collaboration [396–398], increasing access to advanced prediction models and driving scientific progress. In contrast, proprietary systems benefit from commercial investment, regulatory oversight, and integration into the health care system, facilitating clinical adoption despite their black-box nature. However, open-source algorithms are not widely used in practice due to regulatory uncertainty, liability concerns, and a lack of validation pathways [397, 398]. This tension is reflected in UNESCO’s recommendation that health systems use open-source algorithms and AI to prevent algorithmic bias and ensure equitable access [399, 400]. While proprietary algorithms may worsen disparities by being limited to high-resource settings, challenges also exist for open-source software; if not commercially viable, concerns arise about sustained maintenance, security, and the required clinical support infrastructure.

Computational architecture

Cloud-based architectures leverage massive computational resources, enabling sophisticated deep learning models, extensive dataset training, and continuous algorithmic refinement through federated learning. However, reliance on network connectivity introduces latency, raises privacy concerns, and creates single points of failure that can be catastrophic for life-critical decisions [401, 402].

Hennebelle et al. developed SmartEdge, a hybrid edge-cloud diabetes prediction system, demonstrating that ensemble machine learning at network edges achieves prediction latencies of under 2 s [403]. This ensures hypoglycaemic alerting and maintains the accuracy found in cloud-only architectures. Edge computing processes time-sensitive data locally on wearables or smartphones, enabling operation when cellular coverage is absent. This method also enhances privacy by transmitting aggregated results rather than raw physiological data [403]. In 2025, Rajagopal and Subramanian comprehensively compared cloud, fog, and edge paradigms, showing fog-based intermediates balance computational power and latency [401]. Fog nodes at gateways handle data preprocessing, anomaly detection, and risk stratification; processed data is then selectively sent to the cloud, conserving bandwidth and maintaining analytical depth [401]. The study showed that federated learning enables institutions to refine predictive models without centralizing glucose time-series data [404], reducing patient privacy risks while capturing population-level trends. However, federated learning faces communication overhead, variable data quality across nodes, and vulnerability to gradient inversion attacks, in which adversaries reconstruct data from gradients [405]. Thus, strong cryptographic protections and differential privacy are essential [290, 406].

Algorithm benchmarking

Ayoade et al. conducted a comparative analysis indicating that ensemble approaches that integrate multiple algorithm classes—such as random forests, gradient boosting, and recurrent neural networks—generally surpass the performance of single-method implementations [407, 408]. These ensemble models demonstrated AUROC values of 0.87–0.92 for 30-minute-ahead hypoglycemia prediction, compared with 0.78–0.84 for individual algorithms [233, 409]. While ensemble models demonstrate strong performance, it is important to recognize the limitations of retrospective analyses when interpreting these results. Performance metrics from retrospective dataset analysis often overestimate accuracy in clinical settings. This happens due to issues like data leakage, poor temporal validation, and the lack of patient behavior changes in response to predictions that affect glucose levels. The study noted that models with strong past performance (MARD < 8%) can lose accuracy when used prospectively (MARD 12-15%) [410, 411]. This highlights the importance of validating models in real-world settings before clinical use. The performance gap reveals the challenge of predicting a system in which warnings trigger preventive actions, which can invalidate predictions and complicate validation [410, 411].

Esmaeily Fard and Bayati (2025) compared deep learning models, including LSTM and transformer architectures, with statistical methods like ARIMA and exponential smoothing [221]. Deep learning outperformed statistical methods for long-term trend prediction but showed only a modest advantage for short-term glucose forecasts of 30 min or less [218, 221, 412]. Algorithm selection should be based on clinical requirements rather than model complexity.

Conclusion

Wearable sensors that track multiple body signals, combined with AI-powered nursing care, advance the personalized treatment of diabetes. The pairing of continuous glucose monitors with signals such as lactate, ketones, cortisol, and electrolytes adds significant value. AI tools that predict risks help clinicians manage blood sugar levels, reduce complications, and empower patients. However, significant challenges persist, including reaching diverse populations, ensuring usability, addressing payment issues, protecting privacy, and securing equitable access to these resources. Engineers, clinicians, data scientists, and policymakers must address technical issues, establish guidelines, collect real-world data, and ensure broad access to these technologies. Researchers should test this technology in clinical settings and develop systems for automating treatment adjustments. It is vital to design tools around patient needs. These steps are essential for integrating wearable sensors and AI into precise diabetes care. Crucially, the integrative design matrix introduced in Section 3.10 consolidates the biofluid, transduction, and microfluidic options reviewed throughout this manuscript into a single criterion-driven decision tool, thereby providing researchers, clinicians, and regulators with an actionable framework for selecting and benchmarking wearable multi-analyte diabetes biosensors. Future studies should prospectively validate this matrix across diverse clinical cohorts and continuously update it as new transduction modalities and AI-driven analytical pipelines mature.
