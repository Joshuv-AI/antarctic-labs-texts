Mid-term Patient-Reported Outcomes Following Isolated Gastrocnemius Recession for Recalcitrant Plantar Fasciitis
Authors: Matthew R Rohl, Joseph Long, Brent Whitehead, Sydney Rohl, Nicholas A Cheney, Patrick M O’Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Most patients with plantar fasciitis experience symptom relief with nonoperative management. However, a subset remains refractory to conservative treatments and may benefit from surgical intervention. Prior studies support the use of isolated gastrocnemius recession, demonstrating favorable short-term outcomes and low complication rates. Herein we assessed the outcomes following an isolated gastrocnemius recession for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis extended to midterm outcomes (36-89 months).Methods:Eighty-eight patients who underwent isolated gastrocnemius recession for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis between September 2014 and June 2017 were identified through procedural codes. Patients were contacted by phone to complete the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) and American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Foot and Ankle Outcomes Questionnaire, and to report satisfaction, willingness to repeat the procedure, and willingness to recommend it.Results:Of 88 eligible patients, 43 completed follow-up (33 females, 10 males) at a mean of 5.0 ± 1.3 years postoperatively. Mean FAAM scores were 92.0 ± 15.0 for activities of daily living and 86.2 ± 24.2 for sports; mean ADL daily function was 91.0% ± 19.8%, with 39 patients reporting normal or near-normal function. Mean AAOS standardized norm-based score was 93.9 ± 11.4, and mean shoe comfort score was 83.5 ± 25.8. Among the 23 patients who provided pain data, mean VAS was 1/10, with 87% rating pain ≤2/10. Overall satisfaction was high: 95% were satisfied, 93% would undergo the procedure again, and 91% would recommend it. One patient required revision gastrocnemius recession with subsequent symptom resolution.Conclusion:Isolated gastrocnemius recession for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis was associated with durable midterm outcomes with high patient satisfaction, substantial functional scores, and low complication rates. These findings support its use in patients who fail conservative management.Level of Evidence:Level IV, therapeutic.Keywords: plantar fasciitis, gastrocnemius recession, hindfoot, heel pain, patient reported outcomes, functional outcomes, foot and ankle surgery

Introduction

Plantar fasciitis is among the most common causes of heel pain, affecting up to 10% of individuals over their life span and accounting for nearly 1 million outpatient visits annually in the United States alone.1,2 Historically attributed to repetitive microtrauma and inflammation, current evidence suggests that plantar fasciitis is primarily degenerative in nature, resulting from chronic tensile overload of the plantar fascia.3,4 Patients typically report medial heel pain, particularly with first steps in the morning or after rest.5-7 Risk factors include obesity, decreased ankle dorsiflexion, repetitive overuse, and advancing age, all of which contribute to excessive strain on the plantar fascia.8,9

Most cases improve with conservative modalities such as stretching, orthotics, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, activity modification, and physical therapy.3,10 Advanced nonsurgical options may benefit some patients, such as corticosteroid injections, platelet-rich plasma, dextrose prolotherapy, and extracorporeal shockwave therapy.11,12 However, up to 10% of patients remain symptomatic despite these interventions.10,13 Long-term studies demonstrate that nearly half of affected individuals continue to experience symptoms a decade or more after onset, underscoring the chronic, recurrent nature of the disorder. 14

Surgical intervention has traditionally included partial or complete plantar fasciotomy, though this approach carries risks such as medial arch instability, lateral column pain, and nerve injury. 13 As understanding of equinus contracture’s role in plantar fascial tension has evolved, gastrocnemius recession has emerged as a less disruptive alternative that preserves the plantar fascia while addressing a primary biomechanical contributor.1,13 Tightness of the gastrocnemius increases ankle equinus, raises plantar fascial tension during gait, and perpetuates pain.15-17 Studies demonstrate limited ankle dorsiflexion in more than 80% of patients with plantar fasciitis. 18 Lengthening the gastrocnemius reduces fascial strain, improves dorsiflexion, and may provide durable symptom relief.15-17

Compared with plantar fasciotomy, gastrocnemius recession offers lower morbidity, faster recovery, and fewer biomechanical consequences.13,19,20 Early outcomes have shown significant improvements in pain and function, but limited research has evaluated medium- or long-term patient-reported outcomes following isolated recession. The purpose of this study was to assess midterm outcomes (36-89 months) including pain, functional status, and satisfaction among patients who underwent isolated gastrocnemius recession for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis. We hypothesized that patients would demonstrate sustained improvement in pain and functional scores and report high satisfaction at 5-year follow-up.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted following Ohio University institutional review board approval. Patients who underwent isolated gastrocnemius recession for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis between September 2014 and June 2017 by a single surgeon were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) procedural codes. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years of age or older, presented with a chief complaint of heel pain, had a diagnosis of recalcitrant plantar fasciitis that had failed conservative treatment, and underwent isolated gastrocnemius recession. Exclusion criteria included age <18 years, concomitant foot or ankle procedures, and gastrocnemius recession performed for indications other than plantar fasciitis. The primary outcome was the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) activities of daily living (ADL) score at final follow-up. Secondary outcomes included the FAAM Sports subscale, American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) Foot and Ankle Outcomes Questionnaire standardized norm-based score and shoe comfort score, VAS pain score, and patient satisfaction measures. Given the retrospective observational design without a comparison group or preoperative baseline outcome measures, all analyses were descriptive and exploratory in nature.

Eligible patients were contacted by telephone between 2020 and 2022 using the contact information available in the medical record. Standardized verbal informed consent was obtained prior to survey administration. A standardized telephone questionnaire was administered by study personnel and included the FAAM, the AAOS Foot and Ankle Outcomes Questionnaire, a 10-point visual analog scale (VAS) for current pain, and 3 yes/no questions assessing patient satisfaction, willingness to undergo the procedure again, and willingness to recommend the procedure to others. Multiple attempts were made to contact eligible patients before they were considered lost to follow-up. For cases of bilateral procedures, consent was obtained for each limb and scores were recorded for both limbs.

The FAAM includes ADL and sports subscales, each converted to a 0% to 100% score. Patients also rated their current daily functional performance (ADL and Sports) as a percentage of preinjury function and categorized their overall functional level (normal, near normal, abnormal, or severely abnormal). The AAOS questionnaire provided a standardized norm-based score and a separate shoe comfort score.

Demographic and operative data were extracted from medical records. To reduce potential bias, all patient-reported outcomes were collected using a standardized telephone script. Multiple attempts were made to contact eligible patients to minimize nonresponse bias. Descriptive statistics were calculated using means with SDs for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables.

All procedures were performed using an open gastrocnemius recession technique under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position and a thigh tourniquet. Depending on patient anatomy and surgeon preference, either a standard open Strayer recession through an approximately 2- to 3-cm posteromedial incision at the gastrocnemius myotendinous junction or a proximal medial gastrocnemius recession through a similar incision at the medial knee flexion crease was performed. In both techniques, the gastrocnemius fascia was released under direct visualization with the ankle held in maximal dorsiflexion while protecting the sural nerve and surrounding soft tissues. Adequacy of the release was confirmed intraoperatively using the Silfverskiöld test. Following wound closure, patients were placed in a boot and allowed immediate weightbearing as tolerated. The boot was worn continuously for the first 2 postoperative weeks to maintain the stretch achieved by the procedure. After 2 weeks, the dressings and sutures or staples were removed and supervised physical therapy was initiated. Physical therapy emphasized maintenance of gastrocnemius length through stretching while progressively restoring plantarflexion strength.

Results

Eighty-eight patients met inclusion criteria. Fifty-two were successfully contacted, with 9 declining participation, leaving 43 patients who completed the study (33 females, 10 males). Three patients underwent bilateral procedures, yielding 46 operative limbs for analysis. The mean age at surgery was 48.1 ± 12.0 years. Laterality distribution was equal (23 left, 23 right). Mean follow-up time was 5.0 ± 1.3 years.

FAAM ADL scores were available for all 46 feet and averaged 92.0 ± 15.0, and ADL daily function averaged 91.0% ± 19.8%. Sports scores were available for 42 feet and averaged 86.2 ± 24.2, with sports daily function averaging 84.0% ± 28.7%. Four patients did not report an FAAM sports score because they did not regularly perform any of the activities described in the questionnaire (running, jumping, cutting activities, and other sports-related movements). Thirty-three patients rated their current function as normal, 9 as near normal, 3 as abnormal, and 1 as severely abnormal. AAOS standardized mean scores were available for all 46 feet and averaged 93.9 ± 11.4 (normative mean 50.9 ± 9.8), and shoe comfort averaged 83.5 ± 25.8 (normative mean 53.4 ± 8.8) for 45 feet. Among 23 patients who provided VAS pain data, the mean score was 1.0 ± 2.0, with 87% reporting pain ≤2/10. Patient satisfaction was high as 95.7% (44/46) were satisfied with their outcomes, 93.5% (43/46) would undergo the procedure again, and 91.3% (42/46) would recommend it to others. (Table 1)

Complications were minimal, with 1 patient (2.5%) undergoing revision gastrocnemius recession 17 months after the index procedure because of incomplete symptom resolution. Following revision surgery, the patient reported resolution of symptoms and favorable patient-reported outcomes, including satisfaction with the procedure, willingness to undergo the procedure again, and willingness to recommend the procedure to others. At final follow-up, this patient scored 85% on the FAAM ADL subscale, 90% on the FAAM sports subscale, and 87 and 60 on the AAOS standardized mean and shoe comfort scores, respectively.

Discussion

This midterm study suggests that open isolated gastrocnemius recession is associated with durable, favorable outcomes in function and pain relief for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis. Mean scores on the primary outcome measure, the FAAM ADL subscale, were high, and functional outcomes were strong across FAAM and AAOS measures, and pain scores remained low at an average 5-year follow-up. High patient satisfaction further supports favorable long-term patient-reported outcomes with this procedure.

These results align with and extend findings from previous short-term studies. Monteagudo et al 13 reported similarly high satisfaction rates for the gastrocnemius recession but far lower endorsement rates for plantar fasciotomy, highlighting an important patient-centered advantage of recession. Short-term improvements have been well documented, including significant reductions in VAS scores and functional gains across multiple scoring systems.10,21,22 Our study adds midterm data showing that favorable outcomes are sustained at midterm follow-up.

Only a few studies have assessed long-term outcomes beyond 2 years, and even fewer have evaluated isolated recession. Long-term data from Slullitel et al, 23 Riiser et al, 24 and Shah et al 25 similarly demonstrate sustained pain relief and high functional scores. Our results reinforce the durability of recession and contribute additional evidence specifically focused on isolated procedures for plantar fasciitis.

The complication rate in this study was low and compares favorably with plantar fasciotomy, which carries risks of medial arch instability and altered biomechanics.26-28 Only 1 patient required revision gastrocnemius recession for persistent symptoms, and this patient ultimately reported symptom resolution, favorable functional outcomes, and high satisfaction following the revision procedure, suggesting that persistent symptoms after the index operation may still be amenable to additional surgical intervention. Ward et al 28 noted complication rates of 8.9% with endoscopic plantar fasciotomy. Monteagudo et al 13 similarly highlighted a higher complication rate and worse outcomes after plantar fasciotomy compared to gastrocnemius release.

Concerns regarding postoperative calf weakness have historically been cited as a potential drawback of gastrocnemius recession. However, these concerns are more relevant to gastrocnemius-soleus recession rather than isolated gastrocnemius lengthening. Gastrocnemius recession avoids disruption of the plantar fascia and appears to better preserve foot mechanics, potentially reducing the risk of long-term complications.1,10,29 Concerns regarding postoperative calf weakness have not been supported by prior research, and our strong functional outcomes indirectly support a similar conclusion.19,30 It should be noted that the patients in this study underwent an open gastrocnemius recession, which may provide greater visualization and depth control than an endoscopic approach, potentially reducing the risk of sural nerve injury and inadvertent violation of the soleal fascia.

Limitations include retrospective design, reliance on telephone follow-up, incomplete survey responses from some participants, and lack of preoperative baseline scores. Only 43 of 88 eligible patients (49%) completed follow-up. Patients lost to follow-up may have differed systematically from responders, potentially introducing attrition bias. Individuals with poorer outcomes may have been less likely to participate, which could lead to overestimation of favorable outcomes. Additionally, VAS pain scores were available for only 23 of 43 respondents because this measure was not consistently collected during the follow-up survey process. Consequently, VAS outcomes should be interpreted cautiously because the potential for bias resulting from the missing data cannot be excluded. Also, detailed baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including medical comorbidities, prior nonoperative treatments, and symptom duration, were not collected. Thus, the influence of these factors on postoperative outcomes could not be evaluated. Lastly, this was a single-surgeon study, and the results may not be generalizable to other surgeons or alternative surgical techniques. A prospective design with standardized assessments would strengthen future research.

Conclusion

Patients undergoing open isolated gastrocnemius recession for recalcitrant plantar fasciitis reported favorable midterm patient-reported outcomes, including substantial functional scores, minimal pain, and high satisfaction at a mean follow-up of 5 years. These findings support the isolated gastrocnemius recession as a surgical option for patients with recalcitrant plantar fasciitis who have failed nonoperative treatment. Further prospective, long-term studies are warranted to refine patient selection and compare outcomes across surgical interventions.
