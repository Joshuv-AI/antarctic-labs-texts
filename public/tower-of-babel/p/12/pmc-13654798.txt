The baseline vitreous proteome in rhegmatogenous retinal detachment: a case–control study of proliferative vitreoretinopathy
Authors: Mary-Grace R. Reeves, Matthew Yuan, Sohini Rebba, Philip A. Ruzycki, P. Kumar Rao, Rithwick Rajagopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) is the leading cause of failure after rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) repair, yet no baseline biomarkers exist to stratify risk. In this retrospective, nested case–control study we asked whether the vitreous proteome at the time of primary RRD repair predicts subsequent PVR. Undiluted vitreous (1.0 mL) was obtained by pars plana vitrectomy from 16 eyes of 16 patients (8 who later developed PVR and 8 matched RRD controls) and analyzed by tandem mass tag (TMT)-multiplexed liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Of 918 proteins identified, 876 were retained after contaminant filtering. After Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons, no proteins were differentially expressed between cases and controls. Six proteins (decorin, arrestin-C, pre-mRNA processing factor 6, C-reactive protein, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 and an immunoglobulin kappa variable chain) reached nominal significance (p < 0.05) but did not survive correction. The most abundant vitreous constituents included albumin, retinol-binding protein 4 and serotransferrin. In this limited cohort, no large or statistically robust baseline proteomic differences were detected between eyes that subsequently developed PVR and matched controls. We hypothesize that the molecular drivers of PVR may emerge postoperatively rather than being present at the time of the index detachment, although larger studies will be required to confirm or refute this possibility. Introduction

Despite excellent overall outcomes, repair of primary rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) can be hindered by the development of proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) in the postoperative period, making it a worthy area of further study1,2. Between 5 and 10% of rhegmatogenous retinal detachments are complicated by PVR3,4. Certain risk factors have previously been described, including hemorrhage, choroidal detachment, larger retinal tears or detachments, uveitis and inflammation, worse visual acuity at presentation, presence of cataract, and longer duration of detachment prior to surgery4,5. However, much remains to be understood regarding the molecular basis of PVR. Previous attempts to characterize the pathway have identified contributing cytokines and other inflammatory markers associated with signaling pathways of apoptosis and fibroblast proliferation6–8. Therapies that effectively address PVR are largely absent; steroids have shown no benefit and studies of intravitreal methotrexate are conflicting and require more extensive investigation in larger cohorts3,9.

From both mechanistic and diagnostic standpoints, analysis of vitreous proteins is an attractive approach for PVR. Population-based epidemiological studies identify systemic factors that associate with the risk of PVR, including an association of cutaneous keloids and hypertrophic scarring with PVR10. Mechanistically, such studies suggest that certain individuals have as-yet undefined predispositions for PVR formation and that these predispositions could manifest as differences in protein expression within the vitreous. From the diagnostic standpoint, baseline testing of vitreous samples at the time of vitrectomy to stratify PVR risk could be advantageous in informing surgical approach and postoperative monitoring. Proteomic analysis of the vitreous has previously been used in the contexts of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, proliferative diabetic retinopathy, and epiretinal membrane11–13. There is an increasing understanding that the vitreous is the site of key inflammatory pathways and markers that contribute to the pathogenesis of retinal disease14. Prior proteomic studies in RRD have identified specific proteolytic signatures that are significantly upregulated compared with control eyes. These proteolytic patterns are associated with dysregulated angiogenesis, cell adhesion, and cellular signaling pathways15. Preliminary investigations of the vitreous proteome further suggest that metabolic shifts, specifically impaired glycolysis and mitochondrial dysfunction, may contribute to the pathogenesis of PVR16. Consequently, characterizing these protein interactions and signaling cascades remains essential to defining the molecular pathophysiology of PVR and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

To further elucidate the molecular pathogenesis of PVR, we characterized the baseline vitreous proteome in patients undergoing primary vitrectomy for uncomplicated rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. A critical distinction of our study, compared with existing proteomic analyses of established PVR, is the collection of vitreous samples at the time of initial repair, prior to clinical evidence of failure6,7,17,18. This approach allows for a true longitudinal assessment of predictive biomarkers, effectively isolating the baseline proteomic environment from the confounding inflammatory signatures associated with established PVR.

Results

Study population

Of 60 patients undergoing primary RRD repair, eight (13.3%) met the clinical criteria for PVR within the six-month follow-up period and were classified as cases. These eight cases were matched 1:1 with eight controls from the remaining RRD cohort (n = 52) who did not develop PVR. Matching was prioritized based on sex, age, lens status, diabetic history, and baseline macular status (macula-on versus macula-off).

The PVR cohort consisted of 8 patients (2 female, 6 male) with a mean age of 62.7 ± 13.1 years. The matched control group (n = 8; 2 female, 6 male) had a mean age of 64.8 ± 11.4 years (p = 0.74). Baseline clinical characteristics, including the distribution of macular involvement and lens status, were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). Eyes were matched for lens status (37.5% phakic in the PVR group and 25.0% phakic in the non-PVR group; p = 1.00) and macula attachment status at presentation (25.0% mac-on in both groups; p = 1.00). Patients who developed PVR showed a trend toward a longer duration of RRD symptoms (median 10.5 [IQR 5.0–33.0] days vs. 4.5 [IQR 2.3–6.8] days; Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.13).

PVR progression and reoperation

The mean interval between the index surgery and reoperation for PVR was 70.2 ± 39.1 days (range 35–128 days). Surgical management of PVR involved silicone-oil tamponade in 87.5% of cases (n = 7/8), while the remaining case (12.5%, n = 1/8) was managed with long-acting perfluoropropane (C3F8) gas. Additional surgical maneuvers required to achieve retinal reattachment included placement of a supplemental scleral buckle in 50.0% of cases (n = 4/8) and a relaxing retinectomy to relieve PVR-related traction in 37.5% of cases (n = 3/8). Proteomic characterization and differential expression

Vitreous biopsy samples were processed and analyzed using tandem mass spectrometry as illustrated in Fig. 1a, b, identifying a total of 918 distinct proteins across the study cohort. Following a curated post-acquisition contaminant filter, which excluded hair-associated and cuticular keratins (KRT5, KRT6A, KRT9, KRT14, KRT16, KRT17), the cytokeratins KRT1 and KRT2 (identified as known sample-preparation contaminants in human vitreous mass spectrometry by Aretz and colleagues in the canonical in-depth characterization of the human vitreous proteome19), keratinocyte proline-rich protein (KPRP, a cornified-envelope precursor expressed by differentiated keratinocytes and excluded as a likely epidermal contaminant in the same spirit), desmosomal and cell-junction proteins (e.g. DSP, JUP, DSG1, DSC1) characteristic of surface epithelium, laboratory reagents (porcine trypsin) and sheep-derived keratins originating from the surgical drape, yielding a final dataset of 876 endogenous vitreous proteins was retained for downstream analysis (Supplementary Table S1). Initial comparative analysis between PVR cases and RRD controls revealed several proteins with nominal significance (p < 0.05) prior to multiple-comparison correction. In this uncorrected analysis, three proteins demonstrated lower abundance in PVR cases: decorin (DCN; log2 ratio = − 1.09, p = 0.009), arrestin-C (ARR3; log2 ratio = − 1.04, p = 0.04), and pre-mRNA processing factor 6 (PRPF6; log2 ratio = − 0.96, p = 0.01). Conversely, three proteins demonstrated nominally higher abundance in PVR cases: C-reactive protein (CRP; log2 ratio = 0.98, p = 0.01), fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1; log2 ratio = 0.85, p = 0.01), and immunoglobulin kappa variable 2D-40 (IGKV2D-40; log2 ratio = 0.49, p = 0.049). These findings are visualized in Fig. 2a, b. However, following stringent bioinformatic correction for multiple comparisons, no proteins maintained statistically significant differences in expression between the PVR and control groups, as shown in Fig. 3a, b.

To characterize the fundamental protein composition of the vitreous in RRD, we ranked identified proteins by absolute abundance using the exponentially modified Protein Abundance Index (emPAI), which estimates protein abundance by comparing observed to theoretically observable peptides, correcting for protein-size bias inherent in spectral counting20. Consistent with established vitreous physiology, albumin (ALB) was identified as the most abundant constituent (emPAI = 554.71). The top 11 most abundant proteins also included several key transport and structural proteins: retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4), serotransferrin (TF), immunoglobulin kappa constant (IGKC) and cystatin-C (CST3). Further notable abundance was observed for apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4), apolipoprotein E (APOE), apolipoprotein A-I (APOA1), alpha-1-antitrypsin (SERPINA1), retinol-binding protein 3 (RBP3) and complement C3 (C3). Discussion

Although our analysis did not identify significant differential protein expression between the PVR and RRD cohorts after stringent correction, the identification of 876 endogenous vitreous proteins, after rigorous contaminant filtering from an initial set of 918, provides a high-resolution characterization of the vitreous proteomic landscape in primary retinal detachment. Comprehensive proteomic data in vitreoretinal disease remain limited, largely due to the technical challenges of standardized vitreous sampling and the sensitivity requirements of tandem mass spectrometry. By defining this protein atlas, we provide a foundational reference for identifying potential therapeutic targets and for understanding the molecular environment of the vitreous during the acute phase of RRD.

The most abundant proteins identified in our cohort are involved in critical homeostatic processes, including lipid metabolism, serine-protease inhibition and complement activation. Notably, many of these constituents (such as the apolipoproteins and cystatin-C) are highly enriched in exosomal fractions, suggesting a robust system of intercellular signaling via extracellular vesicles in the setting of retinal detachment. Although several of these proteins align with prior mass-spectrometry datasets, our findings expand the known proteomic complexity of the RRD vitreous. This baseline atlas serves as a comparator for future studies investigating the shift from uncomplicated detachment to the proliferative and inflammatory milieu characteristic of PVR and other vitreoretinal pathologies.

Our results confirm previously identified associations between certain protein biomarkers and RRD. Albumin was the most abundant protein in the vitreous proteome, as was also seen in an experimental study examining retinal detachment in rabbits, where it is thought to play a role in controlling apoptosis, cell transport and starvation response21. The second most abundant protein was retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4). Prior work has shown that RBP4 levels are elevated in RRD22. RBP4 has been a target for drug development since the retinol-dependent pathway facilitates lipofuscin bisretinoid formation, which contributes to the lipofuscin toxicity seen in age-related macular degeneration and Stargardt disease23. Levels of APOA1 have also been confirmed to be elevated in rhegmatogenous retinal detachment22. One of the 50 most abundant proteins in our dataset was vimentin (VIM), which has previously been identified as a potential biomarker for PVR17.

The six proteins that reached nominal significance prior to multiple-comparison correction warrant brief biological consideration as candidates for hypothesis-generating follow-up. Decorin (DCN) is a small leucine-rich proteoglycan with documented anti-fibrotic activity in ocular wound healing and TGF-β regulation; a lower vitreous decorin level in eyes destined for PVR is biologically plausible and aligns with the observation by Hill and colleagues that vitreal decorin is diminished in patients who subsequently develop PVR after retinal detachment24, and with preclinical studies demonstrating that exogenous decorin supplementation can attenuate experimental PVR25. C-reactive protein (CRP) is a classical acute-phase reactant; its nominal elevation in PVR cases is consistent with the well-established inflammatory dimension of early PVR and parallels the elevated CRP signal reported in rabbit models of severe PVR26. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1) is a gluconeogenic enzyme, and its higher vitreous level in PVR cases is consistent with prior reports of metabolic reprogramming and altered glycolytic flux in vitreoretinal proliferative disease16. The remaining three candidates are arrestin-C (ARR3), a cone-specific photoreceptor regulator; pre-mRNA processing factor 6 (PRPF6), implicated in inherited retinal disease; and an immunoglobulin kappa variable chain (IGKV2D-40), reflecting B-cell adaptive immunity. These proteins have no established mechanistic link to PVR but provide putative anchors for orthogonal validation in larger cohorts. We emphasize that none of these proteins survived multiple-comparison correction in the present study and they should be regarded as exploratory only.

A primary limitation of this study is the modest sample size, which reflects the fortunately low incidence of PVR in uncomplicated detachments despite nearly a decade of longitudinal sample collection to build our biorepository. With n = 8 per group, the study had approximately 80% power at α = 0.05 to detect only large standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≈ 1.5), corresponding to roughly a 1.4- to 1.7-fold protein-abundance difference under typical TMT-vitreous biological variance; after Benjamini–Hochberg correction across 876 proteins, the minimum detectable Cohen’s d rose to approximately 2.9, equivalent to fold changes greater than approximately 1.8- to 2.8-fold. Our analysis was therefore well powered only to identify very large baseline differences and cannot exclude smaller, yet potentially biologically meaningful, perturbations. Comparable case-control sample sizes have been used by prior vitreous-proteomic studies of PVR, reflecting the practical difficulty of obtaining undiluted baseline vitreous samples in this disease setting: Shahlaee et al. analyzed 13 PVR cases and 13 matched controls in a baseline-vitreous design closely paralleling our own,17 Roybal et al. used 7 PVR patients and 3 epiretinal-membrane controls in their initial discovery cohort,7 and Miller et al. characterized the proteome of 6 PVR specimens against a single epiretinal-membrane control18. We elected a 1:1 case–control ratio to balance statistical power against the cost and labor of TMT-10-plex acquisition; an unbalanced ratio with additional controls would have improved power but at substantial additional expense. Matching variables were age, sex, lens status, diabetes history and macular status. Active uveitis and ocular trauma were formal exclusion criteria for the biorepository protocol, and no patient in the final cohort had preoperative vitreous hemorrhage, choroidal detachment, or ocular hypotony (IOP < 8 mmHg), nor was any primary surgery judged surgically complex; these features therefore did not contribute to between-group variability in this study, though we cannot claim to have systematically excluded them by prospective design. Several other clinical variables that are known PVR risk factors, including extent of detachment, number and size of retinal breaks, smoking history and overall surgical complexity, were not consistently captured in the biorepository database and could not be incorporated into the matching, representing a residual confounding source. The observed trend toward longer duration of RRD symptoms in PVR cases (median 10.5 vs. 4.5 days, p = 0.13) is consistent with the known association between detachment chronicity and PVR risk and warrants explicit acknowledgment; we note, however, that symptom-onset duration relies on subjective patient recall and may be imperfectly recorded. Our cohort also does not represent every RRD treated at our institution during the study period, since RRD surgery is frequently performed on an emergent or after-hours basis when the administrative requirements for research consent were occasionally unavailable in the urgent surgical suite; however, we do not believe this introduced systematic selection bias, as the decision to enroll was dictated by logistical availability rather than clinical phenotype or disease severity.

Regardless of these limitations, the principal contribution of this work remains its design, which allowed for the analysis of baseline molecular factors. By analyzing the vitreous proteome at the time of primary repair, prior to clinical failure, and thereby avoided the confounding signatures of established fibrocellular membranes that have limited prior retrospective studies. Although our findings suggest that a baseline vitreous biomarker panel may not yet be viable for PVR prognostication, the nominally significant proteins identified here warrant further investigation. Future large-scale studies targeting these specific candidates may further elucidate the transition from uncomplicated detachment to proliferative disease and ultimately inform novel strategies for PVR prevention.

Methods

Study oversight

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Washington University in St. Louis and conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants included consecutive patients aged 18 years or older undergoing primary pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) for RRD. Patients with a history of prior vitreoretinal surgery in the study eye, a history of penetrating ocular trauma, or active uveitis were excluded. Trauma was excluded by design to ensure that the study cohort reflected the dominant, non-traumatic etiology of PVR; while ocular trauma is a recognized PVR risk factor, traumatic detachments carry a mechanistically distinct intraocular milieu (intraocular blood, dislocated lens material, mechanical disruption of the vitreoretinal interface) that would confound a baseline proteomic comparison with non-traumatic RRD. To focus on predictive rather than diagnostic markers, eyes with clinical PVR at the time of the index surgery were excluded. The final cohort, as enrolled, also did not include any patient with preoperative vitreous hemorrhage, choroidal detachment, ocular hypotony (intraocular pressure < 8 mmHg) or a primary repair judged surgically complex; these features were therefore absent from the analytic dataset. A minimum of 6 months of postoperative follow-up was required for inclusion in the final analysis to accurately determine the clinical outcome.

Study design and cohort selection

We performed a nested case–control analysis within a larger longitudinal biorepository of vitreous samples collected at our institution between 2014 and 2023. From a total of 587 biopsies obtained from 512 patients, we identified a sub-cohort of 60 unique samples collected from eyes undergoing primary repair for RRD. To ensure a focused analysis on early molecular predictors, inclusion was restricted to patients undergoing their initial surgical intervention for RRD in the study eye. Electronic health records were reviewed for all 60 participants to identify those who developed recurrent detachment requiring reoperation within 6 months of the index surgery; the operative note from the second surgery was then manually reviewed for terminology indicative of PVR (preretinal or epiretinal PVR membranes, fixed folds, star folds, retinal contraction with traction) and patients meeting these criteria were classified as PVR cases. Standardized formal PVR grading was not applied. Importantly, adjudication of the PVR outcome was performed before any proteomic data were generated: vitreous samples were banked throughout the longitudinal collection period, and TMT-LC–MS/MS analysis was initiated only after case and control identifications had been finalized by clinical chart review. The adjudicators were therefore necessarily masked to all proteomic information at the time of outcome assignment. Chart review further confirmed that no recurrent detachment in the case cohort was attributable to an alternative etiology (e.g., diabetic tractional retinal detachment or exudative detachment), and in 7 of 8 PVR cases membrane peeling was performed during the secondary repair (Table 2), providing intraoperative confirmation of the PVR diagnosis.

Surgical technique and sample handling

All participants underwent scheduled 23- or 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) under sterile operating-room conditions. To ensure the integrity of the proteomic signature, a 1.0 mL undiluted vitreous biopsy was obtained at the initiation of the procedure, prior to activation of the balanced salt solution infusion. Samples were immediately labeled and snap-frozen on dry ice before being transferred to long-term storage at − 80 °C.

Peptide preparation and isobaric labeling

A minimum sample of 150 µL of each case and each control was submitted to the mass-spectrometry laboratory for analysis. The samples were digested as previously described using a modification of the filter-aided sample preparation method27. An equal volume of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) buffer (5% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0) was added to each vitreous sample to a final SDS concentration of 2.5%. Samples were sonicated on a Covaris S220X instrument using the following settings: peak incident power (PIP) 100, duty cycle (DC) 10%, cycles per burst (CPB) 500, time 8 min. After determining protein concentration by Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, part no. 23225), 50 µg of total protein were reduced by addition of 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 95 °C for 10 min. A reference pool sample was made from 15 µg of each sample in the study. The samples in SDT buffer (2.5% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) were reduced by addition of 100 mM DTT at 95 °C for 10 min. Reduced protein solutions were mixed with 400 µL of 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8.5 containing 8 M urea (UA buffer) and transferred to the top chamber of a 30 kDa filter unit (Millipore, part no. MRCF0R030). The samples were spun in a microcentrifuge (Eppendorf, model no. 5424) at 14,000 × g for 20 min, followed by addition of 200 µL of UA buffer and centrifugation (14,000 × g for 20 min). The flow-through was discarded and proteins were alkylated by addition of 100 µL of 50 mM iodoacetamide in UA buffer to the top chamber. Samples were gyrated at 550 rpm (Thermomixer, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Filter units were spun at 14,000 × g for 20 min and the flow-through discarded. Two UA buffer centrifugation cycles were used to remove unreacted iodoacetamide and residual SDS. The urea buffer was exchanged with digestion buffer (50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer, pH 8.0) using one centrifugation cycle with 200 µL of the digestion buffer. The filters were transferred to a new collection tube and 80 µL of LysC (1 µAU) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (pH 8) was added. Samples were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C followed by addition of trypsin (1:20) and incubation overnight at 37 °C. Filter units were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min to collect the peptides in the flow-through. Filters were washed with 50 µL of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer and the wash collected with the peptides. Peptides were acidified with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 1% (v/v) and desalted using 5-plug stage tips (C18)28. Peptides were eluted with 50% (v/v) acetonitrile (MeCN) in 0.1% (v/v) TFA and dried in a Speed-Vac (Thermo Scientific, model Savant DNA 120).

Peptides were dissolved in 20 µL of 2% (v/v) MeCN in water. An aliquot (10%) was removed for quantification using the Pierce Quantitative Fluorometric Peptide Assay kit (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 23290). The equivalent volume of 1 µg of peptide from each sample, and 2 µg of reference pool sample, were transferred into a 0.5 mL Eppendorf tube and dried in the Speed-Vac. Dried peptides were dissolved in 12 µL of HEPES buffer (100 mM, pH 8.0) (Sigma, H3537) and labeled with tandem mass tag reagents (TMT10) (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Labeled samples assigned to each ten-plex were pooled, dried and dissolved in 120 µL of 1% formic acid (FA). TMT10-labeled samples were desalted as described above for the unlabeled peptides. Eluates were transferred to autosampler vials (Sun-Sri, cat. no. 200046), dried and stored at − 80 °C for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis.

Nano-LC–MS/MS

The samples in formic acid (1%) were loaded (2.5 µL) onto a 75 μm i.d. × 50 cm Acclaim PepMap 100 C18 RSLC column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on an EASY nano-LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a constant pressure of 700 bar at 100% A (1% FA). Prior to sample loading, the column was equilibrated at 100% A for a total of 11 µL at 700 bar. Peptide chromatography was initiated with mobile phase A (1% FA) containing 5% B (100% ACN, 1% FA) for 1 min, then increased to 25% B over 195 min, to 35% B over 40 min, to 70% B over 6 min, then to 95% B over 2 min and held at 95% B for 18 min, at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired on an Orbitrap mass analyzer with a scan range of m/z 350–1800 and a mass resolving power of 70,000. Twelve data-dependent high-energy collisional dissociation scans were performed with a mass resolving power of 35,000, a fixed lower value of m/z 100, an isolation width of 1.2 Da, and a normalized collision energy of 32. The maximum injection time was 60 ms for parent-ion analysis and 120 ms for product-ion analysis. Target ions selected for MS/MS were dynamically excluded for 40 s. Automatic gain control (AGC) was set to 3 × 106 ions for full MS scans and 1 × 108 ions for MS2 (fragmentation spectra of peptides selected within a narrow 2 Da mass range from the MS1 scan). Peptide ions with charge states of one or ≥ 7 were excluded from high-energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD) acquisition.

Protein identification

MS raw data were converted to peak lists using Proteome Discoverer (version 2.1.0.81, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with integration of reporter-ion intensities of TMT 10-plex at a mass tolerance of ± 3.15 mDa29. MS/MS spectra with charges ≥ 2 were analyzed using Mascot (Matrix Science, London, UK; version 2.7.0)30. Mascot was set up to search against a SwissProt database of human (version October 2020, 20,492 entries) and common contaminant proteins (cRAP, version 1.0, 1 January 2012, 116 entries), assuming the digestion enzyme was trypsin/P with a maximum of 4 missed cleavages. Searches were performed with a fragment-ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da and a parent-ion tolerance of 20 ppm. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was specified as a fixed modification. Deamidation of asparagine, formation of pyroglutamic acid from N-terminal glutamine, acetylation of protein N-terminus, oxidation of methionine and pyro-carbamidomethylation of N-terminal cysteine were specified as variable modifications. Peptide-spectrum matches (PSM) were filtered at 1% false-discovery rate (FDR) by searching against a reversed database and the ascribed peptide identities were accepted. The uniqueness of peptide sequences among database entries was determined using the principle of parsimony. Protein identities were inferred from peptides using a greedy set-cover algorithm from Mascot (spectral counting estimate by Mascot: prot_empai) and identities containing ≥ 2 Occam’s razor peptides were accepted.

Protein relative quantification

Processing, quality assurance and analysis of isobaric-labeled peptide LC–MS data were performed with proteoQ (version 1.5.0.0, https://github.com/qzhang503/proteoQ), software developed with the tidyverse approach (tidyverse R package version 1.3.1) using open-source software for statistical computing and graphics. The reporter-ion intensities from each 10-plex tandem mass tag (TMT) channel (m/z) were converted to logarithmic ratios (base 2), relative to the average reporter-ion intensity of the reference samples within each 10-plex. Within each sample, Dixon’s outlier removals were carried out recursively for peptides with more than two identifying PSMs. The median of the ratios of PSMs that could be assigned to the same peptide was taken to represent the ratios of the incumbent peptide; the median of the peptide ratios was then taken to represent the ratio of the inferred protein. To align protein ratios across samples, likelihood functions were first estimated for the log-ratios of proteins using finite-mixture modeling, assuming two-component Gaussian mixtures31. The ratio distributions were then aligned so that the maximum likelihood of log-ratios was centered at zero for each sample. Scaling normalization was performed to standardize the log-ratios of proteins across all samples. To reduce the influence of outliers from either log-ratios or reporter-ion intensities, values between the 5th and 95th percentile of log-ratios and between the 5th and 95th percentile of intensities were used in the calculations of standard deviations.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between PVR and non-PVR groups using GraphPad Prism (version 10; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; age was compared using Welch’s t-test and duration of RRD symptoms using the Mann–Whitney U test because of non-normal distribution. Categorical variables, including sex, laterality, lens status, macula-on status, and diabetes, were compared using Fisher’s exact test given the small sample size per group (n = 8). Metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) and principal component analysis (PCA) of protein log2-ratios were performed with the base R functions stats::cmdscale and stats::prcomp, respectively. Heat-map visualization of protein log2-ratios was performed with heatmap. Linear modeling was performed using the contrast-fit approach in limma to assess the statistical significance of protein-abundance differences between indicated groups of contrasts32. Adjustments of p-values for multiple comparisons were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction.
