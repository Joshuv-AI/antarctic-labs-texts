Squamous cell carcinoma in a captive Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica): clinical management and pathological findings
Authors: Shin Min CHONG, Charlene Yen-Feng YEONG, Ali Anwar AHMAD, Yirui HENG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An adult male Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica) exhibited thickened, hyperkeratotic skin between dorsal scales. Initial wound management led to temporary resolution, but a proliferative, hemorrhagic mass developed 11 weeks later. Histopathological examination confirmed squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Surgical excision followed by cryotherapy was performed. Despite histological evidence of complete tumor ablation, the animal’s condition deteriorated due to secondary infection and poor wound healing. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing SCC in pangolins due to their unique integumentary anatomy. This is the first documented case of cutaneous SCC in a Sunda pangolin and expands the known disease spectrum in this critically endangered species.

A captive adult male Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica), weighing 8.5 kg and estimated to be at least 14 years old, presented in July 2023 with thickened skin between dorsal scales. The animal had a prior history of minor superficial skin wounds on the feet, which resolved with treatment. Originally rescued in 2010 as a young adult (estimated age 1.5–2 years), the pangolin was rehabilitated and subsequently integrated into the zoo population at the Night Safari, Mandai Wildlife Reserve, Singapore. From 2010 to 2023, the animal was housed in an indoor den with a 30 cm depth of natural substrate, with minimal exposure to sunlight or rain. Two water bowls were provided and refreshed daily, although no large water areas for bathing or swimming were available. Until 2022, the animal had intermittent nocturnal access to display exhibits with partial or full sun exposure; this access ceased thereafter. In late 2023, he was moved to new facilities with optional access to partial sunlight.

The animal was anaesthetized for further evaluation of the abnormal skin condition (Supplementary table for anaesthesia protocols). Physical examination showed a body condition score of 3/5 [7] and generalized accumulation of dark brown, greasy, thickened and adhesive material between the dorsal scales. The thick greasy material was removed, and the underlying skin was generally grossly normal, except for mild bleeding in some areas. Collected materials were fixed in 10% buffered formalin saline (BFS) and submitted for histopathological analysis. Histopathological examination (BX 43, Olympus, Singapore) of Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections showed predominantly compacted keratin sheets interspersed with abundant brown organic debris, indicating hyperkeratosis. Some keratin fragments exhibited underlying necrotic cellular debris with intralesional bacteria, which were a mixed population of Gram positive cocci and Gram negative short rods expected from the environment. No evidence of neoplasia was observed. The animal recovered uneventfully from anaesthesia and was returned to its enclosure for topical wound management. All lesions healed within four weeks after the examination and remained stable at eight weeks post-treatment.

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is one of the most frequently diagnosed integumentary neoplasias in domestic animals [13] and has been reported across a wide variety of zoological and wildlife species, including large felids, white rhinoceros, giant anteaters, birds and reptiles [3, 8, 10, 14, 17, 19, 20]. In nondomestic felids under human care, SCC ranks as the third most frequently reported neoplasm [17]. Risk factors include chronic ultraviolet (UV) light exposure, lack of epidermal pigmentation at predilection sites, sparse hair coverage at affected sites, and advanced age [13]. In cats, up to 50% of SCCs affecting the nasal planum have papillomavirus DNA detected, suggesting a potential viral etiology [13]. However, in this case, there was no presence of inclusion bodies, cytopathic effects or koilocytes to suggest involvement of a papillomavirus. Although SCC is generally locally invasive, metastasis is uncommon, except in oral forms which may spread to regional lymph nodes. Reports of disease in Sunda pangolins remain scarce [1, 6, 23, 25], and to date, only one case of neoplasia has been documented in a subadult Chinese pangolin (Manis pentadactyla) [22]. The present case is notable for the atypical location of the SCC (beneath a dorsal scale), an area not typically exposed to direct sunlight or UV radiation. Sunda pangolins kept in captivity require a habitat with at least 60–90% humidity to prevent dry skin problems [26]. The animal was housed in environments with varying substrate types and humidity levels consistent with Singapore’s ambient range (70–93%) [18], and consistently had access to shaded areas and opaque nest boxes. Compared to African pangolins’ scale overlapping patterns, the shape and ratio of overlapping of the internal and external surfaces of the Chinese pangolin are lower, resulting in a higher chance of skin exposure [21] to ultraviolent radiation. Although the scale pattern of Sunda pangolins is similar to that of Chinese pangolins, the contribution of UV exposure in this case remains speculative. The animal’s advanced age likely played a more significant role as a predisposing factor. The confinement of hyperkeratotic and proliferative lesions to the skin between the scales is unusual. Hyperkeratosis may result from abnormal cornification, chronic irritation, or nutritional deficiencies. Given that the animal was maintained on a specially formulated, nutritionally balanced diet [4], dietary insufficiency is unlikely. Reduced skin exfoliation due to limited behavioral enrichment or lack of access to water for swimming may have contributed to abnormal skin turnover. Unfortunately, no biopsy was obtained during the initial anesthetic examination, precluding early histological assessment of potential preneoplastic changes. This underscores the importance of proactive dermatologic monitoring and environmental management in the care of this species. Wound management in pangolins presents unique challenges due to their overlapping keratinized scales, which hinder visualization and access to underlying skin. Innovative approaches, such as the use of platelet-rich fibrin, have shown promise in Chinese pangolins, promoting faster wound healing compared to conventional methods [5]. In this case, cryotherapy was employed as a treatment modality for SCC. Cryotherapy is widely used in veterinary medicine for SCC management in domestic species, and has also been applied in zoo animals, including wild felids and elephants [2, 9, 11, 12, 15, 16, 24]. In many cases, liquid nitrogen was used with various freeze-thaw cycles. Although histopathology confirmed complete ablation of neoplastic cells post-mortem, the cryotherapy site exhibited extensive tissue necrosis and granulomatous inflammation, likely contributing to delayed wound healing. This highlights the need to balance cryotherapy efficacy with its potential for collateral tissue damage, particularly in species with specialized integumentary structures. Moreover, the inability to perform serial biopsies during treatment limited the ability to monitor tumor recurrence or progression in vivo.

This report documents the first known case of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma in a Sunda pangolin (Manis javanica), expanding the spectrum of spontaneously occurring neoplasms in this critically endangered species. The lesion’s atypical location beneath a dorsal scale, combined with the animal’s advanced age and complex integument, presented diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Surgical excision followed by cryotherapy achieved histological clearance of neoplastic tissue, although wound healing was compromised by secondary infection and tissue necrosis. Future considerations may include using an applicator for cryotherapy for more targeted treatment to minimize collateral tissue damage and increasing the time interval between cryotherapy sessions. Removal of scales over and near the wound to facilitate wound management was considered but not pursued due to potential complications of recovery.

This case underscores the importance of early dermatologic surveillance, tailored wound management strategies, and consideration of species-specific anatomy in the treatment of integumentary neoplasia in pangolins. It also contributes valuable clinical insights for the veterinary care of pangolins under human care and highlights the need for further research into the pathogenesis and management of neoplasia diseases in this taxon.
