Larvivorous potential of Metriaclima callainos and Cynotilapia afra as biological agents for mosquito control
Authors: Watson Mbizi, Themba Mzilahowa, Gilson Njunga, Moses Lackson, Martin Chiumia, Wilson W. L. Jere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a major health challenge in sub-Saharan African countries, including Malawi. Insecticides resistance is a significant challenge to malaria vector control. In Malawi, larval source management (LSM) is promoted as a supplementary malaria vector control strategy. However, biological control using larvivorous fish, which has proven effective in other countries, is not practiced due to lack of information to guide the application of native larvivorous fish in biological mosquito control. This study was conducted to assess the larvivorous potential of native Metriaclima callainos and Cynotilapia afra on susceptible Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto (Kisumu strain) mosquito larvae under laboratory conditions.MethodsA 24 h bioassay was conducted to assess predation rate on fourth instar larvae of An. gambiae. Secondly, the predation on 4th instar larvae was assessed in the absence and presence of alternative fish feeds (zooplanktons and fish flakes). Thereafter, fish predation on the second, fourth larval instars and pupae stage of An. gambiae was assessed.ResultsIn a 24-h bioassay, M. callainos consumed 883.4 ± 66.7 An. gambiae larvae while C. afra consumed 247.5 ± 25.2 An. gambiae larvae. The presence of alternative fish feeds had no significant effect on larvae consumption by the two fish species. Metriaclima callainos preferred fourth-instar larvae and pupae over second-instar larvae, while C. afra preyed equally on all three developmental stages of mosquitoes.ConclusionUnder laboratory conditions, the two Lake Malawi cichlids, M. callainos and C. afra demonstrated high larvivorous potential. This study provides first documentation and knowledge on the presence of potential larvivorous fish in Lake Malawi and further studies should focus on assessing their predation in the natural environment.

Background

Mosquitoes (Diptera: Culicidae) transmit major diseases of humans and animals, rendering them insects of significant public health importance. Taxonomically, 112 genera of mosquitoes are known, with three genera; Anopheles, Aedes, and Culex being major vectors of viral, parasitic and bacteria diseases [1, 2]. Among human diseases, mosquitoes transmit malaria, chikungunya, zika, lymphatic filariasis, yellow fever, dengue, and others [3, 4]. In Malawi, malaria remains the main cause of morbidity and mortality, with cases estimated at 4 810 000 despite numerous interventions to control the disease [5].

Larval source management (LSM) is one of important integral components of global integrated mosquito control programmes and focus on controlling mosquitoes by targeting its aquatic developmental stages; by removing the breeding sites or direct killing of mosquito larvae and pupae, thereby reducing adult mosquito population [6, 7]. In Malawi, LSM approach uses application of larvicides in mosquito breeding grounds and habitat manipulation and is localized to large commercial sugar estates. However, there is lack of sustainability of these intervention due to budget adequacy, labour and time demand [6].

Integrating larvivorous fish in LSM is known to be cost effective, less time and labour demanding, and it is a sustainable approach [8]. The use of fish in mosquito control has been extensively researched and widely implemented as a major biological agent. Larvivorous fish have been documented to significantly contribute to decline and eradication of malaria in countries like Russia [9], Turkey and Iran [1] Italy [10], Afghanistan, Egypt, Djibouti and Morocco [9]. Larvivorous fish have the ability to significantly reduce over-reliance on chemical insecticides, thereby, reducing the emergence of insecticides resistance, avoid contamination and harmful impacts of these insecticides on environment and non-target species [11, 12]. Larvivorous fish are not currently utilized as mosquito control agents in LSM in Malawi due to lack of information on larvivororus potential of native fish. Therefore, this study aimed at assessing the larvivorous potential of two indigenous fish species from Lake Malawi, Metriaclima callainos (Stauffer and Hert, 1992) and Cynotilapia afra (Günther, 1894), as potential biological agents for mosquito control under laboratory conditions.

Methods

This study was conducted at Malaria Alert Centre and Communicable Diseases Action Centre (MAC-CDAC), a research center under Kamuzu University of Health Sciences (KUHeS) in Blantyre, Malawi from January to April, 2024 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of Malawi showing location of the Malaria Alert Centre, Kamuzu University of Health Sciences, Blantyre, Malawi

Fish collection and acclimatization

Fish were collected from Lake Malawi by the divers at Mundola and Lions Cove in Nkhata Bay district, northern part of Lake Malawi. Fish were transported to Stuart M Grant ornamental fish farm in Salima for treatment and conditioning for 5 days before being transported to MAC-CDAC laboratory in Blantyre. The fish were kept in a 60 L capacity aquarium during the experiments and were fed fish flakes.

Description of fish species used

Cynotilapia afra

The C. afra is a rock-dwelling cichlids, that typically inhabit deeper regions, often beyond 50 m, but they can also found in open waters [13, 14]. The genus Cynotilapia is among the top ten most common genera of ornamental fishes exported to different countries [15]. Indigenous to the northern and central regions, they primarily feed on zooplankton and phytoplankton in the water column [16, 17]. Males exhibit different colouration, including blue barred, yellow, orange, white, or black dorsal fin and foreheads, while females have a light blue-brown colour and lack vertical blue-black barring [13, 17]. When fully grown, both males and females can reach a maximum standard length of 10.1 cm [13]. Species used in this study were predominantly brown with blared black vertical bars in females and clear vertical black bars in males (Fig. 2). Additionally, the dorsal and caudal fins of males were black with yellow margins. In this study, males had an average weight of 3.4 ± 0.23 g and standard length of 53.75 ± 1.32 mm, while females had an average weight of 3.16 ± 0.3 g and a standard length of 51.2 ± 2.27 mm.Fig. 2Cynotilapia afra. Metriaclima callainos

The genus Metriaclima currently comprises 26 formally described species endemic to Lake Malawi, forming the most species-rich complex of mbuna species [18]. Metriaclima callainos is a rock-dwelling cichlid naturally found in Nkhata Bay. It was later introduced to other areas such as Likoma, Namanje and Thumbi West Island and has since spread to all islands within the Nankumba Penisula [19]. Females are light blue, while males are deep blue with yellow spot on the anal fins (Fig. 3). The adult can grow to the maximum of 9.77 cm standard length. Like other cichlids, M. callainos are mouth-brooders [20]. The fish used in this study had an average weight of 8.4 ± 0.4 g and standard length of 72.5 ± 1.19 mm for males, and 7.9 ± 0.3 g with a standard length of 65.5 ± 1.936 mm for females.Fig. 3Metriaclima callainos: Female (a) and Male (b). Susceptible Anopheles gambiae colony rearing and maintenance

Insectary reared adult mosquitoes, larvae and pupae of susceptible An. gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) (Kisumu strain) hereafter referred as An. gambiae at MAC-CDAC insectary were used during the study. Male and female adult An. gambiae were kept in 30 × 30x30 cm plastic Bugdorm cages (MegaView Science Co Ltd. Taiwan) and allowed to mate freely. For maintenance, they were fed 10% (w/v) sucrose solution through the wetted cotton wool which was placed on top of the mosquito cage. Female An. gambiae were blood fed using Cow blood through Hemotek membrane feeding system. Gravid females were provided with oviposition cups with a Whiteman’s filter paper as a substrate for oviposition. Eggs were hatched at the temperature of 27 ± 2 o C and relative humidity of 80 ± 10%. Mosquito larvae were fed on powdered Tetramin® Tropical fish flakes once a day during the whole development stages.

Predation efficacy of M. callainos and C. afra on 4th instar An. gambiae larvae within 24-h period

A randomized controlled experiment was conducted in 18 plastic containers of 4 L volume which were filled with 2 L of underground water obtained from the borehole. The water surface area and depth of the plastic containers were 324 cm2 and 6.5 cm respectively. For each fish species, 8 fish were used (4 males & 4 females) and individual fish were placed in separate experimental container. Each experimental container had sand stone connected to air pump to ensure availability of dissolved oxygen throughout the experimental period (Fig. 4). Fish were introduced in the containers 24 h before the introduction of mosquito larvae and the fish were starved during this period to standardize hunger level.Fig. 4Experimental container filed with 2 L of water with individual fish with inside

Experimental container filed with 2 L of water with individual fish with inside

Two containers were used as control treatments where no fish was introduced but same number of mosquito larvae was introduced. Due to high predation rate established during pilot experiments especially in M. callainos, initial 500 4th instar larvae from the insectary were introduced into each experimental container (Fig. 5). When approximately 75% of the larvae are consumed, addition of 200 mosquito larvae was added in the containers. The experiment was conducted for 24 h (9 am to 9 am of the following day) and was run for two consecutive days to determine optimal predation in 24 h and the experiment was conducted three times, and each individual fish was considered an independent biological replicate. The emerging pupae were being removed and replaced with the larvae. The water temperature and room temperature were maintained at 24 ± 1 °C. The standard length and weight of each individual fish were recorded before being introduced into experimental containers. Larval consumption was determined by subtracting the number of larvae remaining in the experimental container from the total number of larvae introduced.Fig. 5An. gambiae 4th instar larvae in a cup

Preference for An. gambiae larvae, fish flakes and zooplanktons in M. callainos and C. afra

To assess the effects of alternative prey (zooplankton) and other fish feeds (fish flakes) on the predation of 4th instar An. gambiae larvae by M. callainos and C. afra, the experiment was conducted in 4 L plastic container filled with 2 L of chlorine-free water obtained from the borehole. Experimental units consisted of individual fish introduced in the container 24 h before the experiment and the experiment was run in four replicates. To standardize hunger level, fish were fed fish flakes at a rate of 0.5% of their body weight 1 h before the actual experiment. For M. callainos, four fish received 150 An. gambiae larvae each, other four fish received 150 An. gambiae larvae and 360 zooplanktons (1 ml of water with zooplanktons) each and the other four received 150 An. gambiae larvae and fish flakes at 10% body weight each. For C. afra, all other treatment remained the same as in M. callainos except that only 30 larvae were given due to their slow predation rate. Fish were allowed to predate on mosquito larvae for the period of 30 min for M. callainos and 2 h for C. afra. The experiment was repeated twice, in two consecutive days, using the same fish. The number of An. gambiae larvae consumed by the two fish species in all treatments was recorded.

Developmental stage-specific Predation of An. gambiae by M. callainos and C. afra

This completely randomized experimental design was conducted to assess a single factor (mosquito developmental stage) with three levels of larvae instar 2 (L2), larvae instar 4 (L4) and pupae stage on the number of these prey consumed by the fish. In both fish species, 2 males and 2 females were used during the experiments. The fish were introduced to experimental containers 24 h before the experiment, and hunger was standardized by feeding them 1 h prior to the experiment at 0.5% body weight. For M. callainos, individual fish received 50 of each L2 instar, L4 instar, and pupae. For C. afra, each fish received 30 of each mosquito stage at the same time. The predation was limited to 15 min, after which the fish were removed from the experimental containers, and the remaining number of each stage (L2, L4 and pupae) were recorded. The experiment was repeated twice, in two consecutive days, using the same fish and was replicated four times.

Data analysis

Poisson regression model was used to assess the relationship between fish weight, sex, standard length and species on the number of larvae consumed and also the impacts of other fish feeds and alternative prey on the number of larvae consumed by the two fish species. In situation of over dispersed data, negative binomial regression was used. The final reduced model was derived from the full model by removing the predictors with p-values greater than 0.05. One-way Analysis of Variance was used to assess the significant difference in the mean number of consumed 2nd, 4th instar larvae, and pupae of An. gambiae by M. callainos and C. afra. All data were analysed using R statistical software version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2023) at 5% significance level.

Results

In a 24-h bioassay, M. callainos consumed 883.4 ± 66.7 A. gambiae larvae, while C. afra consumed 247.5 ± 25.2 A. gambiae larvae. There was significant difference (p = 0.038) in mean number of larvae consumed between the two fish species. M. callainos consumed greater number of larvae than C. afra. Discussion

Mosquito-borne diseases remain major challenges in tropical and subtropical countries [21]. Mosquitoes transmit pathogens that causes deadly diseases like malaria, yellow fever, and dengue in humans [22]. Irrigation canals, fish ponds and rice paddies, among other provide breeding grounds for the mosquitoes [23]. Larval Source Management using biological predators like fish, offers cost-effective and sustainable approach to control mosquitoes in these water bodies, including other large natural water bodies [8]. The selection of fish for the biological control of mosquitoes relies on the effectiveness of the fish in feeding on mosquito larvae while coexisting with other natural food sources in the environment [24]. Additionally, ideal larvivorous fish should feed on all aquatic stages of mosquito development, with a preference for later stages- third and fourth instar larvae and pupae- so that they can maintain continuous feeding without periods of prey switching or starvation when specific stages are absent [25, 26].

Predation efficacy of M. callainos and C. afra on 4th instar An. gambiae larvae within 24 h period

This study presents the first documentation of the predation of M. callainos and C. afra, Malawi cichlids, on An. gambiae larvae in a laboratory setting. The number of mosquito larvae consumed by these two fish species in 24 h were greater compared to other recommended larvivorous fish species reported under similar laboratory studies. For instance, Gambusia. affinis consumed 180–190 larvae/day, as reported by Noreen et al., [27], Orieochromis mossambica, Esomus danricus, Oryzias melastigma, Puntius sophore (50–100 larvae/day) as reported by Das et al. [12]. Betta splendens, Tropheops tropheops, Osphronemus gorami, Pterophyllum scalare which predated 40 ± 9.608 to 238 ± 27.536 in 24-h as reported by Ghosh et al. [28] and Aphanius dispar which consumed 128 ± 0.2 Anopheles stephensi larvae in 24 h as reported by Haq and Yadav [29].

The observed lower predation rate of C. afra compared to M. callainos can be attributed to age of the fish, as noted by Permata et al. [30], who reported an increase in predation rate in older fish than young ones. Unlike M. callainos used in the study, which were adults [16], C. afra used during the experiment were juveniles since the SL of adult male and female of C. afra typically range from 70–90 mm standard length [31]. Therefore, future studies should include adult C. afra to determine their optimal predation capacity.

Predation of M. callainos and C. afra on An. gambiae larvae in the presence of fish flakes and zooplankton

In both fish species, presence of zooplanktons and fish flakes did not significantly affect their predation on An. gambiae larvae. Similar results were reported by Matias and Adrias [32], who also observed high preference towards mosquito larvae by Nothobranchius guentheri fish over the zooplanktons. The observed preference towards larvae than zooplanktons may be due to prey size, affecting prey visibility and profitability to the predator, as proposed by Optimal foraging theory [33–35]; which suggests that predators, such as larvivorous fish, will select larger prey over the smaller ones for greater energy gain and visibility. An. gambiae larvae in their 4th instar stage are larger in size (approximately 3.5–5 mm) compared to zooplanktons which are microscopic and measuring approximately 0.2 mm [32, 36]. The fish’s preference for larvae over fish flakes may be due to prey movement and detection cues, which influence predator preference between live and stationery food [37]. These findings highlight the potential of both M. callainos and C. afra in suppressing mosquito larvae population in natural water bodies containing other food sources like zooplanktons, as well as in managed water systems, such as fish ponds where supplementary feeds like fish flakes are provided.

Developmental stage-specific predation of An. gambiae by M. callainos and C. afra

In this study, M. callainos preferred 4th instar larvae and pupae than the 2nd instars. These findings also conform to the optimal foraging theory. The size of 4th instar and pupae of A. gambiae are nearly the same and larger than larvae instar 2, hence the observed preference. Similar results were also reported by Ghosh et al. [28]. Non-selective pattern observed in C. afra can be attributed to other factors such as fish size and visual resolution as reported by Kao et al. [35]. In their experiments, Kao et al. [35], used a model which predicated a direct relationship between fish size, visual resolution and prey selection by fish. Neuroanatomical evidence also suggests that visual resolution increases as fish grow larger [31]. Since the C. afra fish used in this study were juveniles, it can be argued that their visual resolution was still under development. Therefore, non-selective behavior in C. afra can possibly be attributed to poor visual ability to recognize and select the potential prey. The study recommend that further experiments be conducted using fully grown C. afra fish.

Despite several attempts, the divers were unable to catch adult C. afra fish, necessitating the use of juvenile’s C. afra. Due to the limited resources and study period, the focus was primarily on conducting core experiments that could guide the decision to consider the two predator fish for field trials to assess their potential in mosquito control.

Conclusion and recommendation

Integration of larvivorous fish in LSM strategy in mosquito control is sustainable, cost effective and environmental friendly approach. Two Lake Malawi cichlids, M. callainos and C. afra have demonstrated high lavivorous potential under laboratory conditions. Presence of other feed (fish flakes) and alternative prey (zooplankton) did not affect the predation of An. gambiae larvae by the two fish species. Since, the conditions in the laboratory are quite different from the natural environment, there is need for further research to understand 1) their performance in natural environment such as rice paddies or fish ponds, 2) with Lake Malawi having over 1000 cichlids, there is need to evaluate the larvivorous potential of other cichlids present in Malawi.
