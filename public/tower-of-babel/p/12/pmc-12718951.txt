1, 6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose ameliorates lipopolysaccharide-induced septic acute kidney injury via inhibiting caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis formation in rat
Authors: Ping-Hsun Yu, Yu-Hsuan Cheng, Shiu-Dong Chung, Chiang-Ting Chien
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory state associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) and high mortality. However, sepsis-induced AKI cannot be effectively prevented or treated using current antimicrobial therapies and supportive measures. We explored the therapeutic effect of newly developed fructose esters on sepsis-induced AKI (S-AKI).Methods:We used the surface plasmon resonance technique and ultrasensitive chemiluminescence analyzer to characterize the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)/endotoxin binding activity and antioxidant capability of fructose esters. We assessed the extent of fructose ester gastrointestinal digestion using rat intestinal acetone powder. We examined the therapeutic effect of fructose esters on LPS-induced S-AKI by evaluating the blood and renal reactive oxygen species (ROS) amounts, caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis, inflammation, microcirculation, and renal dysfunction.Results:Our data showed that the fructose esters are not easily hydrolyzed by the rat intestinal acetone powder, suggesting their high stability in the gastrointestinal tract. 1,6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose (FDL) dose-dependently scavenged H2O2 and displayed a higher binding affinity to LPS compared to sialic acid and fructose did. LPS significantly enhanced caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis and increased leukocyte infiltration, blood and renal ROS amount, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine level, whereas FDL significantly depressed these LPS-enhanced parameters. In addition, the increased plasma inflammatory cytokines levels using LPS could be reduced by intravenous fructose ester FDL treatment.Conclusion:Our data suggest that FDL, with its antioxidant activity against H2O2, can neutralize LPS toxicity using a high binding affinity, and attenuate S-AKI by inhibiting caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis, thereby ameliorating renal oxidative stress and dysfunction.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Antioxidant activity, Lipopolysaccharide, Pyroptosis, Sepsis

1. INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a serious disease characterized by a systemic inflammatory response caused by bacteria, molds, viruses, and other pathogens and cytokine release.1 Acute renal failure arising from insufficient blood flow to the kidneys from severe sepsis may evoke high mortality.1 Sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (S-AKI) affects at least 10% of hospitalized patients and more than 50% of patients in intensive care units.2 Sepsis is a severe pathological symptom leading to fatal organ failure.3 Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), one of the cell wall outer membrane layers in gram-negative bacteria, is also known as endotoxin. The LPS structure comprises two main components: the hydrophobic lipid fractions lipid A and hydrophilic polysaccharides. When LPS enters the circulatory system, it activates Toll-like receptors, particularly Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) and TLR 4 (TLR4) pathways, which initiate the inflammatory NF-κB signaling that causes severe inflammation, endothelial cell dysfunction, multiple organ failure, and septic shock.4 LPS induces S-AKI using the increased monocyte/macrophage (ED-1) infiltration and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production to enhance MyD88/AP-1/NF-κB mediated inflammation,4 p-IRE1α/p-JNK/CHOP/GRP78/ATF4-mediated endoplasmic reticulum stress, Bax/caspase 3/PARP-mediated apoptosis, Beclin-1/Atg5-Atg12/LC3-II-mediated autophagy, and caspase 1/Gasdermin D/IL-1β-mediated pyroptosis.5,6 These data indicate that LPS could induce a wide-range inflammation with several types of programmed cell death. Trying to block the messages related to gram-negative bacteria/LPS/toll-like receptors/NF-κB mediated inflammatory signaling could prevent and improve S-AKI.

Conventional sepsis treatment includes intravenous fluids and antibiotics. S-AKI can worsen the patient’s condition. Some evidence suggests that the kidney injury process occurs early and persists when treated with intravenous fluids and antibiotics. Supplementation with different body fluids such as saline during shock can maintain blood pressure and reduce lactemia, but only 3% hypertonic saline or 4% succinylated gelatin and 6% hydroxyethyl starch, can maintain intestinal microcirculation. These synthetic gums, however, cause excess renal ROS production.7 The development of effective ingredients with endotoxin-neutralizing properties and antioxidant properties is a necessary measure for sepsis precision medicine.

Sugar esters are compounds composed of hydrophilic sugars and hydrophobic fatty acids, which are nontoxic, nonirritating, biodegradable, and can be widely used as emulsifiers, stabilizers, and detergents. Fructose esters can exhibit antibacterial activity due to the presence of sugar hydroxyl groups.8 Both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria can be inhibited using glycoesters. Fructosyl esters can strongly inhibit the growth of Streptococcus mutans, the pathogenic bacteria that cause dental caries.9 Fructose esters are highly valued in the food, cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and dental care industries.10 However, fructosyl esters use in medicine has rarely been reported as an antibacterial agent, especially in the treatment of diseases caused by gram-negative bacteria containing endotoxin-LPS. Fructose esters can be chemically synthesized. However, chemical methods usually produce a variety of complex products with varying degrees of fatty acid acylation and isomers at different acylation locations, making product separation and purification difficult. Enzymes are highly plasm-specific, structurally position-selective, thus avoiding side reactions, and can be used to synthesize high-purity fructose esters. Lipase (EC 3.1.1.3) catalyzes hydrolysis in aqueous solutions and can also facilitate esterification and transesterification in nonaqueous solutions.11,12 Based on its excellent structural position selectivity and stereoselectivity, lipase is currently considered a feasible method for catalyzing fructose ester synthesis.13-15 At the same time, the enzyme can undergo conversion reactions at lower temperatures, thus saving energy. We prepared and purified several fructose ester derivatives synthesized using long and short-chain lipase and fructose. Sepsis is a serious systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by infection. Acute renal failure due to severe sepsis is the main cause of high mortality, but there is currently no effective prevention and treatment. Our goal was to develop a novel, effective, and safe nano-D-fructose fatty acid ester derivative as a precision medical target to mitigate LPS-induced AKI toxicity.

2. METHODS

2.1. Preparation of Fructose Esters through Lipase-Catalyzed Transesterification Reaction

Fructose was used as the alcohol in the reaction and was combined with fatty acid esters of varying carbon chain lengths (including short-chain vinyl propionate (3C), ethyl butyrate (4C), medium-chain methyl octanoate (8C), ethyl laurate (12C), vinyl laurate (12C), long-chain methyl palmitate (16C), and vinyl palmitate (16C). 50% of the relative oil phase volume of water. The aqueous phase contained fructose 0.270 g and 10 mg/mL immobilized lipase (immobilized Novozymes 435), reacted at 60°C for 24 hours, and analyzed the remaining fructose in the aqueous phase after the reaction with High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The HPLC system included a refractive index detector (RI 2000 refractive index detector; Schambeck SFD GmbH, Bad Honnef, Germany) and a pump (SFD 2000 Quaternary gradient pump; Schambeck SFD GmbH) using SUGAR columns (10 μm, 6.5 × 300 mm, 1/pkg; Waters, Milford, MA). The mobile phase contained distilled water and 50 μg/L Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). The detector was set at 40°C, the string temperature was 60°C or 80°C, and the flow rate was set at 0.5 mL/min. For the trans esterified oil phase samples analysis, Hypersil™ ODS C18 column (5 μm, 250 × 0.4.6 mm; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA™) was used with methanol: distilled water = 90:10 mobile phase, detector temperature also set at 40°C, string temperature at 40°C, and flow rate set to 1 mL/min for the analysis of more hydrophobic fructose ester products. Novozymes 435 was used as catalysis, and after 24 hours of reaction, HPLC (SUGAR tube column) was used to analyze the aqueous phase containing fructose. The fructose had a significant decrease after the reaction with vinyl propionate and vinyl laurate. When using the vinyl propionate and fructose reaction, HPLC was used to analyze the remaining fructose in the reaction aqueous phase, and the conversion rate was defined as the percentage of fructose consumed. The highest conversion rate was 57%. If vinyl laurate and fructose were used, the conversion rate reached 46.7%.

2.2. Fructose esters purification (FMP1, FMP2, FDP, and 1,6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose)

The fructose ester liquid chromatograph (MPLC) purification was prepared using medium pressure purification by Agela Technologies Flash system/Cheetah MP Series preparative MPLC. When purifying FDP, the Flash tube column used Spherical C18 40 to 60 μm 4 g series three, string temperature: 25°C. When purifying FMP1 or FMP2, three tubes were connected in series using the string Flash column spherical C18 20 to 35 μm 4 g. The string temperature was 60°C. Mobile phase A: water, B: MeOH, the moving phase A and B proportion at any time (%), flow rate 8 mL/min, and collection unit size (mL/fraction) were controlled using the system program: Detector, UV detector, detector wavelength: 205 nm; monitor wavelength: 240 nm. The pure product was identified and collected using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) method. The solvent was extracted at a vacuum of 10°C to 60°C and 27.5 to 29.9 inch Hg using a gyratory concentrator to obtain a dry, pure product. The 1,6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose (FDL) purification method was the same as that for FDP.

2.3. Molecular structure identification methods

The molecular structure was determined by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), using 600 MHz for 1H-NMR (1D and 2D HMBC, HSQC) and 151 MHz for 13C-NMR (1D, DEPT-90, DEPT-135 and 2D HMBC, HSQC), with deuterated dichloromethane (CDCl2) or deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) as solvents. The hydrogen spectra (400 MHz NMR) and carbon spectra (600 MHz NMR) of FMP1, FMP2, and FDP were analyzed using NMR instruments. The hydrogen spectrum used solvent D2O and the carbon spectrum used solvent D-dichloromethane.

2.3.1. FMP1

The 1-propionyl-beta-D-fructofuranose (FMP1) molecular weight was the same as 6-propionyl-alpha-D-fructofuranose (FMP2) using mass spectrometry. It was speculated that FMP1 and FMP2 were the same as fructose monopropionate. FMP2 is known to be 6-propionyl-fructofuranose, while FDP is 1,6-propionyl-fructofuranose. According to the Novozyme 435 enzyme specificity principle, it can be speculated that FMP1 should be 1-propionyl-fructofuranose.

2.3.2. FMP2

The FMP2 13C-NMR pattern, according to the signal intensity, shows a larger set of signals and a smaller set of signals, which should correspond to the two fructoglycol isomers (anomor 1, anomer 2) produced by the FMP2 fructose based on C-2 spontaneous isomerization, namely 6-propionyl-α-fructofuranose or 6-propionyl-β-fructofuranose. Because anomer’s molecular formula is the same, the following structural analysis only analyzes the signal of FMP2 anomor 1.

2.3.3. 1,6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose

FDL synthesis and characterization were previously described.14

2.4. Evaluation of interaction between fructose ester and LPS using surface plasmon resonance

We evaluated the binding affinity between LPS and fructose esters using surface plasmon resonance (SPR BIAcoreX) as described earlier.16 SPR can detect the affinity between two molecules to assess the degree of binding ability. SPR chip (biosensor) used 8-mercaptooctanoic acid (MOA) as the linker layer, and connected with 3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane (APTES). After that fructose, sialic acid, or fructose ester was respectively fixed onto the chip to prepare three kinds of SPR biosensors. We used the Biacore T200 optical biosensor and the CM5 biosensor (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ) to determine biomolecular interactions between fructose esters and LPS. The Biacore sensor plot was processed based on Biacore kinetic measurement data from the Biacore T200 software. The KD and ka values were calculated using experimental curves from the BIAcore software.

2.5. Intestinal enzyme decomposition test for fructose esters

The method used for evaluating fructo-glycoester hydrolysis using rat intestinal acetone powder (RIAP, Sigma I1630) as an intestinal enzyme is described as follows. RIAP was mixed at a concentration of 50 mg/mL 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (SPB) at pH 7.0, then centrifuged 14 000 × g at 4°C for 20 minutes to remove insoluble matter. The resulting supernatant was RIAP stock. In the reaction group, fructose esters were diluted to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL with RIAP stock at 0.1 M SPB, pH 7.0. The control group performed the reaction at 37°C for 0, 0.5, 1, 2 hours at 37°C without RIAP. We added five times the reaction volume of MeOH to stop the reaction, centrifuged the product at 14 000 × g for 5 minutes, and removed the precipitating enzyme. The product was analyzed using TLC chromatography (Merck silica 60 F254), and the lamination solution was EA: MeOH: acetic acid (HAc) = 90:10:2, and the layers were expanded twice. After the solvent volatilizes, the layer was evenly sprayed with a color developer (Folin & Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent: EtOH = 2:8) and baked at 120°C for 20 minutes to complete the product color development.

2.6. In vitro macrophages response to LPS and FDL

RAW 264.7 macrophages were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cell line was cultivated in adequate incubator conditions (5% CO2, 37°C) with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Big Cabin, OK) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. In vitro, cells were treated with or without FDL at different concentrations (25, 50, and 100 μg/mL) for 1 hour and stimulated with 40 μg/mL of LPS for the appropriate time at 37°C in an incubator. The effect of FDL on the viability of LPS-induced macrophages was assessed using the MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide).

2.7. Animals and grouping

Female Wistar rats (220-250 g) were purchased from BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd. (I-Lan, Taiwan) and housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of National Taiwan Normal University at a constant temperature and a consistent photoperiod (light from 07:00-18:00 hours). All surgical procedures and experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of National Taiwan Normal University (Approval no. 108030; Approval date: March 10, 2020) and in accordance with the guidelines of the National Science Council of the Republic of China (NSC 1997). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering on the animals used throughout these experiments. On the day of the experiment, rats were anesthetized with subcutaneous urethane (1.2 g/kg). The body temperature was maintained at 36.5°C to 37.0°C using infrared light and monitored with a rectal thermometer. The PE-50 catheter was placed in the left carotid artery to measure heart rate and arterial blood pressure (ABP) using an ADI system (PowerLab/16S, ADI Instruments; Pty Ltd, Castle Hill, Australia) with a pressure transducer (P23 1D; Gould-Statham, Quincy, MA). LPS (Escherichia coli 0127:B8; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO) was injected into the rats using a femoral vein to induce sepsis and acute kidney injury. A total of 32 rats were randomly divided into the following groups: (1) the normal group was given saline (n = 8); (2) LPS was administered in the LPS group (50 mg/kg, i.v., n = 8); (3) LPS + fructose ester derivative, rats were treated with LPS (50 mg/kg, i.v., n = 8) and fructose ester (10 mg/kg, i.v.) 30 minutes after LPS administration. We measured the amount of ROS from the kidneys and blood and the blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine biochemical markers (Sigma, St Louis, MO). At the end of the experiment, animals were sacrificed with intravenous potassium chloride injection (0.1 mg/mL). The kidney was immediately removed and divided into two parts. One portion was stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin for immunocytochemistry assays. The other portion was frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for protein separation or other assays.

2.8. In vivo CL-ROS determination

We adapted a technique for in vivo ROS determination in the kidney using a Chemiluminescence (CL) Analyzing System (CLD-110; Tohoku Electronic Industrial Co., Sendai, Japan).17 The CL was measured in a dark chamber of the CL Analyzing System including a photon detector (Model CLD-110), CL counter (Model CLC-10), water circulator (Model CH-200), and a 32-bit IBM personal computer system. The cooler circulator was connected to the Model CLD-110 photon detector to maintain a temperature at 5°C. The Model CLD-110 is extremely sensitive to detecting photons as weak as 10−15 W of radiant energy according to the manufacture’s specifications. Photon emissions from the intact kidney were counted at 10-second intervals at 37°C and under atmospheric conditions. After surgery, the animal was placed into the sample chamber (Model TLU-17, CLD-110; Tohoku Electronic Industrial Co.), and the kidney was exposed for measuring CL. To exclude photons from other sources than kidney CL, the animal was housed in the dark box and covered with a shielded plate. Only the renal window was left unshielded and positioned under a reflector, reflecting the photons from the exposed kidney onto the detector photo-sensitive area.

2.9. Whole blood sample preparation

For all rats, 0.2 mL whole blood sample was obtained from each rat with heparinized plastic syringes after experiments. After blood sampling, an equal volume of blood from a donor rat was administered to the experimental rat. Sham-operated animals received similar operative procedures. The blood samples were immediately wrapped with aluminum foil and kept in the iced box until the testing for CL with usually done within 2 hours.

2.10. Lucigenin-amplified CL determination in whole blood

The blood lucigenin-initiated CL measurement was like that previously described.17 Briefly, 0.1 mL of PBS buffer (Ph = 7.4) was added to 0.2 mL sample. The CL was measured in a dark chamber of the CL Analyzing System (CLD-110; Tohoku Electronic Industrial Co.). After 100 seconds, 1.0 mL of 0.1 mM lucigenin (bis-N-methylacridinium nitrate, Sigma) in PBS (pH = 7.4) was injected into the cell. The CL in the blood sample was continuously measured for a total of 600 seconds. The CL total amount was calculated by integrating the area under the curve and subtracting it from the background level, which is equivalent to the dark average. The assay was performed in duplicate for each sample and expressed as CL counts/10 s for blood CL. A mean (±SE) of CL level of each sample was calculated.

2.11. Quantitative endotoxin determination by Pierce LAL Chromogenic

Endotoxin Quantitation Kit was added to 1 mL of endotoxin-free water at room temperature to endotoxin standard stock at a concentration of 26 EU/mL, and mixed by shaking with an oscillator for at least 15 minutes. One point 4 mL of endotoxin-free water was placed into an limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) bottle, gently hand-shaken until the powder was dissolved, chromogenic substrate; each vial contained approximately 7 mg of dry-frozen powder. Six point 5 mL of endotoxin-free water was added to the configuration to achieve a final concentration of ~2 mM. A 96-well dish was preheated on a heating plate at 37°C ± 1°C for 10 minutes. Fifty microliters of endotoxin standard solution or sample were added to the grid. Fifty microliters of LAL reagent were added to each cell at time T = 0 minutes, 37°C ± 1°C for 10 minutes; at time T = 10 minutes, add 100 μL of chromogenic substrate (prewarmed to 37°C ± 1°C) to each cell at 37°C ± 1°C for 6 minutes; At time T = 16, add 100 μL of 25% acetic acid, take out the 96-well dish from the heating plate after addition, tap the bottom of the plate to mix well, and measure the absorbance value at a wavelength of 405 to 410 nm.

2.12. Hematoxylin and eosin and periodic acid-Schiff stain of tissue section

The tissues were soaked in formalin, fixed, and sent to the Department of Pathology, National Taiwan University College of Medicine, for paraffin embedding. After obtaining the wax block samples, the samples were sectioned using a microtome (RM125RTS; Leica, Wetzlar, German) with a thickness of 5 μm. Hematoxylin (Hematoxylin, HHS-16; Sigma-Aldrich) was stained with eosin (Eosin, 3871; J. T. Baker, Phillipsburg, NJ). The renal tubular cell damage was evaluated with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stain.

2.13. Pyroptosis and inflammation evaluation by Western blot

To explore the pyroptosis role in LPS-induced acute kidney injury, we used Western blot and immunohistochemistry to examine the caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis in the kidney tissues. Twenty micrograms of protein were electrophoresed as follows. The total proteins were homogenized with a prechilled mortar and pestle in extraction buffer consisting of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH = 7.6), 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethyl sulfonyl fluoride, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% deoxycholate, 2% β-mercaptoethanol, 10 µg/mL pepstatin A, and 10 µg/mL aprotinin. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 12 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentrations determined using BioRad Protein Assay (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Antibodies raised against caspase 1 (GTX62815; GeneTex, Irvine, CA), C-GSDME (monoclonal antibody #55879; Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Danvers, MA), NLRP3 (ab263899; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), phosphor NFBp65 (ab86299; Abcam), IL-1β (ab9722; Abcam), and IL-18 (ab191860; Abcam) and monoclonal mouse antimouse β-actin (Sigma, Saint Louis, MI) were used at 1:400. The membranes were incubated with goat antirabbit or antimouse IgG secondary antibodies conjugated into horseradish peroxidase (Biolegend, San Diego, CA) for 1 hour, washed in TBST and imaged with Image QuantTM LAS 4000 (GE, Boston, MA).

2.14. Immunohistochemistry

Five-micrometer tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in an ethanol series and submitted to antigen retrieval step. The sodium citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) was used for heat-induced epitope retrieval. After 15 minutes of antigen retrieval step, the sections were blocked for nonspecific binding with 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibodies for 18 hours at 4°C. These sections were washed with PBS three times and then incubated with primary antibodies caspase 1 (GTX62815; GeneTex; 1:100). Renal apoptosis was determined using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay (DNA Fragmentation Kit; BioVision, Milpitas, CA).

2.15. Microcirculation determination

We determined the fructose esters effect on S-AKI using a full-field laser perfusion imager (MoorFLPI; Moor Instruments Ltd., Devon, UK). These methods were previously described.5 The full-field laser perfusion imager was used to continuously record the microcirculation blood flow intensity in the kidneys. The amount of blood cells moving within the detection zone (ROI) was processed to generate a 16-color coded image correlated with renal blood flow values. The ROI in blue is lower blood flow, while the red ROI is higher blood flow. The microcirculatory blood flow intensity for each ROI is shown as perfusion units. We also measured the microcirculatory blood flow intensity in the heart, lungs, liver, and small intestine at the same time, and compared their FDL response effects on LPS stimulation.

2.16. Multiple cytokine antibody array

Multiple cytokine expression levels in plasma were determined using RayBio® C-Series Rat Cytokine Antibody Array 2 Kit (RayBiotech Inc., Norcross, GA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.17. Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± SE mean (SEM). Differences within groups were evaluated using paired t test. One-way analysis of variance was used to establish differences among groups. Intergroup comparisons were made using Duncan multiple-range test. Differences were regarded as significant at p < 0.05.

3. RESULTS

3.1. The preparation of several fructose ester types

Among many microbial lipases, Candida antarctica lipase and its immobilization enzyme Novozym 435 are currently the most used enzymes for synthesizing glycoesters.14 We recently completed the use of C. antarctica lipase and its immobilization enzyme Novozym 435 to initially complete D-fructose esters with 3-carbon short chains or 12-carbon long chains. We completed HPLC purification and used NMR and other equipment to measure its molecular weight and structural formula (Fig. 1). No organic solvents were added in the process, which is a safe and nontoxic preparation process. In these three carbons, we prepared and identified one FMP1 structure, two FMP2 structures, and one FDP. In 12-carbon lipase, we purified FML and FDL (Fig. 1). Among them, three-carbon FMP1, FMP2, and FDP were highly hydrophilic and soluble in water, while 12-carbon FML and FDL are highly hydrophobic and insoluble in water.

The preparation and structure of several types of fructose esters. The use of D-fructose via Novozyme 435 completed FMP1, FMP2, FDP, and FDL compounds. FDL = 1,6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose; FMP1 = 1-propionyl-beta-D-fructofuranose; FMP2 = 6-propionyl-alpha-D-fructofuranose.

3.2. FDL with strong antiendotoxin and antioxidation ability

As shown in Fig. 2, we first compared the capability of several types of fructose esters on endotoxin adsorption values. In addition, the well-known sialic acid and various monosaccharides were also determined. We demonstrated the endotoxin assay (Fig. 2A) and indicated the correlation coefficient (Fig. 2B). Using the endotoxin assay kit, our data indicated that the order of binding affinity to LPS was FDL = FML > FMP1, FMP2, and FDP. Our results showed that compared with endotoxin-free pure water, the fructose esters FDL significantly reduced the LPS concentration in solution, and compared with sialic acid, the endotoxin adsorption in FDL was better than that of sialic acid and a variety of simple sugars (Fig. 2C). To examine the antioxidant H2O2 activity using an ultrasensitive CL analyzer, our data demonstrated that the purified fructose ester derivatives of FMP1 (Fig. 2D), FMP2 (Fig. 2E), FDP (Fig. 2F), and FDL (Fig. 2G) exerted significant effect on scavenging H2O2 activity. FDP and FDL also demonstrated in a dose-dependent manner, H2O2 activity inhibition. Our data found that the order of H2O2 scavenging activity in four types of fructose esters was FDP > FMP1 > FMP2 = FDL. Among them, FMP1, FMP2, and FDP had high hydrophilic capabilities, whereas FDL was a fructose ester with high hydrophobicity. Based on these in vitro data, we therefore used FDL to explore the therapeutic effect in an in vivo study. Because FDL displayed both antiendotoxin and antioxidative activity, we applied FDL on LPS-induced S-AKI. The FDL in vitro effect on the viability of LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages using MTT assay was that LPS at 40 µg/mL significantly decreased cell viability around 60%, whereas FDL at 25, 50, and 100 µ/mL increased the cell viability to 74%, 84%, and 91%, respectively.

3.3. Fructose esters are not easily hydrolyzed into fructose

To evaluate whether fructose esters could be hydrolyzed by digestive enzymes through oral administration, we tried to hydrolyze fructose esters using RIAP as an intestinal enzyme. The TLC analysis results for RIAP hydrolysis of FMP1, FMP2, FDP, and FLP are shown in Fig. 3. FMP1, FMP2, FDP, and FDL were hydrolyzed into fructose with time after adding RIPA, showing that these four fructose esters were not easily decomposed and were absorbed and utilized by the intestine after entering the intestine. These hydrolysis results suggest that the four fructose esters may be absorbed by the intestine into the circulatory system in the digestive tract to neutralize/bind endotoxins and reduce the severity of bacteremia. However, the actual effect requires animal tests to verify reduced fructose-1 phosphate accumulation efficacy and reduced inflammation and triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes.

Correlation diagram of four RIPA hydrolyzed fructose esters. TLC results showed that the four fructose esters were not easily hydrolyzed into D-fructose. RIPA = radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer; TLC = thin layer chromatography.

3.4. FDL exerted higher binding affinity with LPS than fructose and sialic acid

Using SPR analysis, when 10 to 50 ng/mL LPS was injected, the conjugate angular shift signal detected by the fructose chip was similar to that of the sialic acid chip. The ka value of the fructose chip was 1 × 1010 M−1S−1, the kd was 0.877 S−1, and the KA value was 4.3 × 1010 M−1 in Fig. 4A. The sialic acid chip had a ka value of 8 × 109 M−1S−1, a kd of 0.364 S−1, and a KA value of 4.2 × 1010 M−1 in Fig. 4B. The ka value of the fructose ester chip was 7.5 × 109 M−1S−1, the kd was 0.351 S−1, and the KA value was 6.7 × 1010 M−1 in Fig. 4C. If the KA value is greater than 105, it means that there is a high adsorption force between the two molecules. Comparing the resonance angle displacement results obtained by the interaction between the three chips and each LPS concentration, it can be seen that the fructose and sialic acid adsorption force on LPS was similar, while the fructose ester (FDL) adsorption force on LPS was higher than that of the first two.

The fructose relation (A), sialic acid (B), and fructose ester (FDL, C) interact with LPS using SPR analysis. FDL = 1,6-dilauroyl-D-fructofuranose; LPS = lipopolysaccharide; SPR = surface plasmon resonance.

3.5. FDL efficiently attenuated sepsis-depressed microcirculation

We determined the FDL effect on microcirculation in several organs and the ABP response in LPS-induced sepsis. As shown in Fig. 5, LPS-induced septic syndrome including the decrease in ABP (Fig. 5A) and microcirculation in five organs (Fig. 5B, C). The microcirculation statistical data were indicated in the heart (Fig. 5D), lung (Fig. 5E), kidney (Fig. 5F), liver (Fig. 5G), and small intestine (Fig. 5H) in the sham, LPS and LPS plus FDL treated rats, respectively. The microcirculation level in the heart, kidney, liver, and small intestine was significantly decreased around 300 minutes in LPS vs Sham group. The FDL + LPS group significantly improved the depressed microcirculation in the heart, kidney, and small intestine compared to the LPS group. As shown in Fig. 5, we suggest that FDL could inhibit the caspase 1 signaling pathway activation and ameliorate renal ischemia.

4. DISCUSSION

Sepsis is a severe complication of infection caused by bacteria, viruses, surgery, or burns. Sepsis subsequently triggers a systemic inflammatory response.1 Among sepsis-induced injuries, AKI is the most common sepsis complication with elevated serum creatinine and BUN.5 There are no effective methods or drugs to cure S-AKI. It is therefore urgent to explore new novel drugs and mechanisms to treat it. It is well known that LPS could induce S-AKI. Upon acid hydrolysis, LPS is split into its polysaccharide and lipid A moieties. The polysaccharide contains neutral (glucose, heptose, fructose) and amino (glucosamine, quinovosamine) sugars.18 Our previous studies successfully established LPS-induced S-AKI in rats.5,6 Our in vitro study found that FDL treatment dose-dependently increased the cell viability of macrophages in response to LPS cytotoxicity. We administered fructose esters like FDL through the intravenous route and found improvement in LPS-induced septic hypotension, ROS production, depressed renal microcirculation, and renal function by FDL. Our data also implicated the possible FDL administration route via oral intake. This route can prevent sepsis because RIAP (an intestinal enzyme) was not digested in our study. However, the FDL oral administration efficacy on LPS toxicity neutralization was not explored in the present study. This will be determined in a future study.

Our previous study suggested that sialic acid was a therapeutic candidate to attenuate LPS-induced sepsis and improve liver dysfunction.16 Using the endotoxin assay kit, we found that fructose, sialic acid, and FDL could reduce LPS activity. Our data in Fig. 2 indicated that the order of binding affinity to LPS was FDL = FML > FMP1, FMP2 and FDP. Our data further informed that FDL fructose esters significantly reduced the LPS concentration in solution and the FDL activity on endotoxin adsorption was better than that of sialic acid (Fig. 2C). Our data also found that using SPR analysis, FDL was stronger than sialic acid with a higher KA value to bind LPS (Fig. 4) subsequently to attenuate LPS toxicity. Our data also found that FDL dose-dependently scavenged H2O2 activity in vitro.

Sepsis is one of the main causes of hospitalization and mortality in intensive care units. The factors causing S-AKI are still not well known, and may be multifactorial, as perfusion changes, inflammation, oxidative stress, and glycolytic metabolism alterations.19 Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, an intermediate high-energy metabolite of the glycolytic route, has been reported to be a promising candidate in sepsis therapy.20 Catarina et al19 reported that fructose-1,6-bisphosphate preserves glucose metabolism integrity and reduces ROS in the brain during experimental sepsis. Markov et al21 and Alves Filho et al22 indicated the beneficial effect of fructose-1,6-diphosphate on endotoxin-induced lung injuries and carrageenan-induced pleurisy in rats. Meng et al23 reported that 1,5-anhydro-D-fructose attenuates LPS-induced inflammatory cytokine release via NF-κB p65 phosphorylation suppression. Our data also evidenced that phosphor-NF-κB p65 expression was significantly enhanced using LPS and was significantly decreased by FDL treatment. These studies implicated the importance of fructose derivatives on attenuating sepsis-induced tissue injury. In the present study, our developed fructose ester FDL was intravenously administered and had therapeutic potential to reduce LPS-enhanced leukocyte infiltration, inflammatory cytokines levels, blood and renal ROS amount, BUN, and creatinine levels leading to S-AKI improvement. Our study also demonstrated that the FDL intravenous route can immediately neutralize endotoxin/LPS in the blood or tissue to attenuate endotoxin/LPS toxicity in early treatment. For sepsis prevention, the oral intake of FDL fructose esters may also be available because FDL was not easily digested into fructose in the gastrointestinal tract according to our study in Fig. 3.

During sepsis, endothelial dysfunction is an important pathogenetic mechanism in S-AKI. LPS-induced endotoxemia associated with renal hemodynamic changes such as alterations in renal blood flow, vascular resistance, and glomerular filtration rate.24 As shown in Fig. 5, LPS-induced septic syndrome includes a marked decrease in ABP and microcirculation in five organs: the heart, lung, kidney, liver and small intestine, implicating the systemic vasodilatory effect of LPS on sepsis. The kidney impairment resulted in the elevation in renal blood ROS, BUN, and creatinine, subsequently leading to S-AKI. Intravenous FDL treatment effectively reduced renal and blood ROS and efficiently improved the decreased microcirculation in these organs and ABP evidencing the antioxidant and antihypotensive effects of FDL.

Pyroptosis is one type of inflammation-associated caspase 1-dependent programmed cell death, which confers a crucial role in sepsis25 implicating the existence of inflammation in S-AKI. Caspase 1 inhibition could inhibit pyroptosis and ameliorate S-AKI.26 Our study found that LPS-enhanced the leukocyte infiltration into the kidney by H&E stain and the caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis response in S-AKI by Western blot and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 6). Caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis may also evoke inflammatory cytokines release, which may be secreted by the increased and activated leukocytes. Cytokine array is more sensitive than traditional ELISA assay to detect the cytokine level. Our data with cytokine array clearly indicated that LPS significantly increased several proinflammatory cytokines including CINC2, ICAM-1, interleukin 1 R6 (IL-1 R6), LPS-induced CXC chemokine (LIX), and thymus chemokine-1 (TCK-1) level in the plasma of the LPS group. FDL intravenous treatment with LPS binding capability significantly depressed the LPS-enhanced cytokines level in the LPS + FDL group, implicating that FDL can counteract LPS-induced inflammatory pathways.

In conclusion, our developed fructose ester FDL with antioxidant H2O2 activity and high binding affinity to LPS can improve LPS-induced oxidative stress, decreased microcirculation, hypotension, and renal dysfunction through caspase 1 mediated pyroptosis and inflammation inhibition.
