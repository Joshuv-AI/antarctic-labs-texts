Letter to the editor concerning ‘Creatinine and cystatin C-based indices for predicting sarcopenia, frailty and disability in older community-dwelling adults’
Authors: Longqing Yu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Bai et al. titled "Creatinine and cystatin C-based indices for predicting sarcopenia, frailty and disability in older community-dwelling adults" [1]. The authors' prospective analysis of CHARLS data provides valuable evidence supporting serum creatinine/cystatin C ratio (CCR) and sarcopenia index (SI) as accessible biomarkers for adverse geriatric outcomes. Their work addresses a critical need for low-cost sarcopenia screening tools in community settings. While we commend the rigorous methodology, we wish to offer constructive perspectives to enhance clinical translation.

Regarding methodological considerations, the reliance on anthropometric equations rather than device-measured muscle mass (e.g., DXA/BIA) may influence diagnostic accuracy [2]. Although validated in prior studies [3], the modest AUCs (0.59–0.63) suggest potential misclassification. Future studies could therefore compare CCR/SI against gold-standard measurements to clarify whether discordance arises from methodological limitations or biological variability. Additionally, chronic inflammation accelerates muscle loss and affects cystatin C levels [4,5], so adjusting for inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, IL-6) might elucidate if CCR/SI's predictive value is independent of inflammatory pathways.

Moving to clinical applicability and subgroup heterogeneity, the exclusion of chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients limits generalizability, as renal impairment alters creatinine/cystatin C metabolism [6]. Validating CCR/SI thresholds in CKD populations is thus essential, given their high sarcopenia risk. Furthermore, the observed sex-based performance differences (higher SI AUC in women vs. CCR in men) warrant exploration of sex-stratified cutoffs in clinical practice. In terms of implementing CCR/SI in primary care, pragmatic protocols are needed. Specifically, how should clinicians interpret conflicting CCR/SI results? Also, could combining CCR/SI with point-of-care tools (e.g., handgrip dynamometry) improve feasibility [7]?

Looking ahead to future research directions beyond refining diagnostic thresholds, we suggest several avenues: First, mechanistic studies investigating CCR/SI's relationship with muscle protein turnover and metabolic pathways (e.g., insulin signaling) would be valuable; second, exploring multi-biomarker panels integrating CCR/SI with emerging markers (e.g., myostatin) could enhance precision; and finally, investigating health policy integration, such as embedding CCR/SI into national elder health screenings paired with cost-effectiveness analyses, is warranted.

In conclusion, Bai et al. provide compelling evidence for CCR/SI as pragmatic sarcopenia biomarkers. Addressing these considerations could accelerate their adoption in resource-limited settings and inspire novel interventions. We applaud the authors' contribution to geriatric health equity.
