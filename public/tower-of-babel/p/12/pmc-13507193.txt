Stochastic allocation of photovoltaic energy resource and electric bus parking lot in distribution systems using an improved weighted average algorithm via sine–cosine strategy
Authors: Abdulaziz Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a stochastic framework for the joint allocation of photovoltaic (PV) resources and electric bus parking lots (EBPLs) in radial distribution systems, aiming to minimize power losses, enhance voltage stability, and improve the voltage profile under uncertainties in PV generation and load. PV and EBPL siting and sizing are determined using an Improved Weighted Average Algorithm (IWAA) that incorporates a sine–cosine search to escape local minima and achieve a stronger exploration–exploitation balance. In the stochastic framework, PV output uncertainty is modeled via Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) using beta probability density functions (PDFs), while load uncertainty is represented with normal PDFs. The proposed methodology is evaluated across four cases—Case I: deterministic PV; Case II: deterministic PV + EBPL; Case III: stochastic PV; Case IV: stochastic PV + EBPL—on the IEEE 33- and 69-bus radial distribution systems. The outcomes show that the IWAA consistently outperforms the traditional WAA by yielding lower power losses, lower voltage deviations, and better voltage stability index in Cases I and II. Also, the results demonstrated that allocating the EBPL with PV (Case II) increases the net saving by 9.81% and 5.08% for the 33-bus and 69-bus systems, respectively, over Case I (without EBPL). Also, the stochastic model (Case III) outcomes showed that the net saving falls against the deterministic model (Case II), highlighting the effects of the uncertainties. Overall, these findings underscore that explicitly modeling uncertainty and co-locating EBPL with PV leads to more robust planning decisions and improved distribution-system performance under realistic operating conditions.

Introduction

Motivation and background

The accelerated increase in demand for electricity, the exorbitant price of commissioning and constructing major power plants, and the dwindling reserves of fossil fuels have increasingly suggested the need for small-capacity dispersed generation (DG) plants1. These are positioned near end consumers and offer numerous advantages including minimal capital investment, fast lead time, and zero or negligible pollution1. Photovoltaic (PV) systems, being among the most important distributed generation resources of renewable power, can be utilized to provide low-cost and clean energy2. They also possess other advantages in addition to energy generation, for example, voltage profile enhancement, power loss minimization, and enhancement of power quality in the distribution system3. Electric bus parking lots (EBPLs) have also become an important factor in distribution networks in recent years. Because of their high charging load and possible V2G function4, EBPLs can be manageable loads and adaptive storage devices. Accurate integration not only enhances the reliability of public transport electrification but also assists network operation by mitigating variation caused by renewable generation. One of the greatest challenges in the deployment of such resources, though, is the optimum placement and sizing of the resources in the system. Inefficient siting and improper allocation of power by either EBPLs or PV systems could be helpless to enhance network performance and even accentuate losses, degrade voltage deviation, and introduce stability problems4. Optimization models in such instances have to be formulated in a manner that they optimize PV resources and EBPLs simultaneously taking into account actual real-world network conditions with uncertainties and stochastic variables. Some of the variables related to distribution networks operation like photovoltaic generation, EBPL charging demand, and network conditions are inherently influenced by stochastic effects4. Seizing such uncertainties in optimization is thus a sheer requirement. Inaccurate estimation of photovoltaic generation or EBPL load, for example, can cause inefficient allocation, extra losses, and network imbalance4,5. Stochastic models, which can accommodate such uncertainties, become the center of the resource allocation process. Given these uncertainties, stochastic optimization techniques can optimally decide on PV system size, EBPL site and their interactions6,7. The models enable effective planning of resources for varying operating conditions and prevent voltage instability and excessive losses. Hence, simultaneous integration of PV systems and EBPLs based on stochastic optimization is likely to increase efficiency, flexibility, and resilience of distribution networks to unexpected fluctuations.

Literature review and gaps

The last few years have witnessed a lot of work being carried out in the optimization of DG location and size, particularly for PV systems8. Most of this work has addressed the application of stochastic models in this area, demonstrating that the models have significant advantages over analytical and conventional techniques in terms of dealing with uncertainties and optimizing network performance. Because of the nonlinear and complex nature of the optimum DG allocation issue, evolutionary and meta-heuristic methods proved to be the best solutions in the last few years. These methods are highly appreciated for their ability to deal with the complexity of distribution network optimization. In9, a probabilistic metaheuristic framework has been presented for the multi-objective placement of wind generation units within distribution networks. It targets, among other objectives, the reduction of active power losses, the increase of voltage stability and profile, and the improvement of supply reliability, quantified by the cost of energy not supplied. The proposed methodology explicitly takes into consideration uncertainties inherent in both renewable power output and load demand. In10, the authors propose a multi-timescale stochastic scheduling framework for PV-based charging stations with integrated energy storage, aiming to cut operating costs while accounting for uncertainties in PV output. In11, an integrated framework is proposed for simultaneous network reconfiguration and allocation of DG in distribution systems. The key objectives of the model are the minimization of system losses and the improvement in various power quality indices. For achieving these objectives, the recently developed Antlion Optimizer (ALO) is employed as the solution method. Other approaches are Bacterial Foraging Optimization (BFO) algorithm in12, which takes optimal planning of DGs in the networks into account13 uses the Imperialistic Competitive Algorithm (ICA) to minimize network losses as well as determining the DG optimal location. In14, a game-theoretic model is introduced to derive an optimal deployment strategy for charging stations, explicitly accounting for participation uncertainty. In15, an improved genetic algorithm is proposed for tackling the coordinated optimal sitting and sizing problem of fast electric vehicle charging stations, enabling V2G capabilities, and deploying capacitor banks to increase grid economic returns using a multi-objective formulation that minimizes capacitor-related expenditures and optimizes key power quality parameters simultaneously. In16, a framework is developed to predict the power requirements and load patterns for electric public transport systems. This model utilizes open-source data available from more than 150 metropolitan and state-level sources across the globe, with its efficacy validated through two separate application cases. In17, a multi-objective optimization model is first established to minimize the operational expenses of electric bus charging facilities, considering both energy costs and congestion pricing, and is solved using the NSGA-II technique. These financial mechanisms are strategically applied to influence and optimize charging schedules, leading to a reduction in overall travel and idle time. A deterministic method for PV planning is introduced in18, where the Ant Lion Optimization (ALO) algorithm is applied for the objective of minimizing loss, improving voltage profile, and enhancing system stability. In4, a progressive deployment strategy is executed for the strategic planning of charging infrastructure, incorporating the anticipated evolution of demand. The optimal placements for the associated power supply systems are then designed according to the finalized charging station locations. Also, in19, a multi-objective PSO is applied to determine the site of PVs in order to minimize the power loss and improve voltage stability. A multi-criteria optimization problem is formulated in20 for the reconfiguration of radial distribution networks with photovoltaic and wind-based distributed generation. A weighted-sum approach aggregates the objectives that are related to loss minimization, voltage profile enhancement, and system stability improvement. This problem is solved using the MFO algorithm. In21, a multi-objective optimization method is presented towards placement of both dispatchable and non-dispatchable renewable DG units minimizing the losses, improving the voltage situation, and minimizing environmental impact. In22, the topology planning of an active distribution network under the wind power uncertainty is studied through a hybrid Tabu Search (TS) and PSO technique to schedule the size and site of batteries with minimizing the cost incorporating the technical restrictions. Planning of DG resources for minimum loss is addressed through the Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) in23. In24, the effects of photovoltaic (PV) integration on distribution networks are studied through various methods. PV system installation was found to significantly decrease power loss and improve the voltage profile. Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) was employed in25 for the allocation of energy sources and proved to be capable of solving challenging, nonlinear, and bound multi-variable optimization problems. Due to its dynamic and adaptive characteristics, the SMO algorithm efficiently searched the search space, always converging towards high-quality solutions. In26, Inherited Competitive Swarm Optimization (ICSO) was used for optimizing the size of the wind turbine (WT) capacity in an unbalanced distribution network. The results confirmed that coordinated energy storage system (ESS) dispatching could enhance the performance of the network. The Spider Monkey Optimization approach was again applied in27 to determine the PV and WT system optimal positions in radial distribution systems minimizing the losses and voltage conditions enhancement satisfying power balance and boundary constraints. The approach capitalizes on the social behavior of spider monkeys such that the approach is highly flexible in handling large-scale nonlinear problems. In28, the best battery siting in the distribution networks was investigated under uncertain renewable generation and load demand scenarios. MOPSO is used to optimize minimization of expenses and maximize the reliability index, while Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) is developed to simulate uncertainty. The findings confirmed that optimal battery sizing and siting could significantly contribute to enhanced system reliability. The article in29 presents the best optimization algorithms to find the optimal site, size, and operating power factors of PV and wind sources using the White Shark Optimizer (WSO). The targets are minimizing power losses and voltage deviations, with the improvements in electrical networks being achieved. Comparison with other approaches demonstrated the superiority of the methodologies presented. In30, flicker emissions and distribution network planning were studied. Power loss, flicker emissions, and voltage deviation were all incorporated into the objective function, addressing the problem of power quality degradation and irregular load distribution. The result showed a significant reduction in flicker emissions of 11% and a 14% reduction in switching flicker. In31, a concurrent and optimum allocation of photovoltaic (PV) and wind turbine (WT) systems, along with radial distribution network reconfiguration, was established in order to reduce power loss and improve reliability. With the use of an enhanced Salp Swarm Algorithm (ISSA), the outcomes indicated that the simultaneous optimization of PVs and WTs, in combination with reconfiguration, yielded the minimum power losses, cost of reliability improvement, and maximum net savings, with the overall network performance being better than in other cases. Reference32 investigates the size, location, and number of PV systems to be installed on a distribution feeder, as well as optimal control parameters of the PV inverters, to achieve the highest possible PV hosting capacity (HC) using a genetic algorithm (GA). In33, optimal allocation of PV in distribution networks is formulated considering losses and voltage conditions enhancement. Along with technical and cost constraints, the method involves geospatial constraints to identify the locations and sizes of the generation units that would be optimal. Lastly34, carried out a comprehensive analysis on optimal site, size, and number of PV penetrations in a distribution network to minimize losses. The optimization process depended on a Newton–Raphson-based optimizer (NRBO), which allowed attainment of minimum losses as well as efficient operation of the network.

Problem formulation

The problem of finding the best placement for PV resource and EBPL in the network is presented as a multi-objective problem. The primary objectives are minimizing loss, voltage profile enhancement, voltage stability improvement, and tackling the uncertainty of generation by the PVs. The choice variables, which are size and position of the PVs, are determined by the Improved Weighted Average Algorithm (IWAA). The multi-objective problem is then addressed by the weighted coefficients approach. To begin with, the problem formulation, both the objective function and the associated constraints, is given. Modeling of the power of the PVs, in comparison to their natural uncertainties, is then accomplished using probability distribution functions.

PV, EBPL, and load modeling

The power output of the PV system relies heavily on the environment in the form of primarily solar radiation, which is subject to variation over the day and season. The power generation by a PV is typically characterized by values acquired from the manufacturer or real-time solar radiation measurements. Mathematically, the output power of a PV system can be presented by24,36.19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta}_{PV}={\delta}_{rated}\times (\frac{IR}{{IR}_{ref}})\times {\eta}_{MPPT}$$\end{document}where, δ rated represents the nominal PV power, IR is irradiance, IR ref is the standard irradiation (1000 W/m2), and ηMPP is the PV efficiency, which in this paper is 0.95. The efficiency value represents the capability of the system to track and optimize its power output under varying sunlight conditions.

However, renewable energy sources like PVs intrinsically comprise an uncontrollable and variable character. Their performance is largely dependent on environmental and natural factors, i.e., solar irradiance, temperature, and clouds. As the uncertainty factors are random, the power output of PV systems is not deterministic but uncertain in nature. Because of the uncertainty, it is necessary to account for the variability of the energy output during design and operation of a power network incorporating these renewable resources. Thus, power systems with renewable generation must make provision for these uncertainties probabilistically rather than the deterministic assumptions.

In this paper, uncertainty of PV power is encountered within the context of determining the allocation of PV within distribution networks. In representing this uncertainty, probability density functions (PDFs) that quantify the probability of various levels of power output under specific conditions are used. Beta distribution is the most suitable stochastic PDF to simulate the PV power uncertainty, as it offers a versatile model to capture solar radiation variability.

The Beta distribution for modeling the uncertainty in PV power is expressed as follows:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{b}\left(IR\right)=\left\{\begin{array}{c}\frac{\Gamma \left(\alpha +\beta \right)}{\Gamma \left(\alpha \right)\Gamma \left(\beta \right)}\times {IR}^{\left(\alpha -1\right)}\times (1-IR{)}^{\left(\beta -1\right)} for 0\le IR\le 1,\alpha ,\beta \ge 0\\ 0 otherwise\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}where, fb(IR) represents the Beta PDF for irradiance, and α and β are parameters of shape. α and β are defined by21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta =(1-\mu )\times (\frac{\mu \times (1+\mu )}{{\sigma }^{2}}-1)$$\end{document}22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =\frac{\mu \times \beta }{1-\mu }$$\end{document}where, μ and σ denote the Beta PDF’s average and std, respectively. They control the distribution’s shape and, thus, the spread of probability over solar radiation levels. Different conditions of solar radiation, ranging from clear to cloudy or partially cloudy, are mimicked by adjusting α and β.

Apart from PV power, load demand in a distribution network is another variable parameter with a significant impact on network planning and operation. The load demand, which varies from day to day and season to season, should be taken into account when determining the optimal PV allocation. The best PDF for expressing uncertainty in modeling load demand is the normal distribution, frequently used for variables that have a symmetric distribution around an average. The load normal distribution is presented as follows:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{n}(LD)=\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi }}{e}^{\frac{-(LD-\mu )}{2{\sigma }^{2}}}$$\end{document}where LD represents the network load, which is modeled by a normal distribution fn​(LD) with a mean μ and standard deviation σ. The parameters reflect the expected demand for the load as well as the variation around the mean, making it possible to model variable demand patterns throughout the day.

The growing importance of new modes of transport, particularly electric buses (EBs), has created new opportunities for the flexibility and sustainability of distribution networks. Parking lots, which have traditionally been nothing more than depots for automobiles, can now be considered as distributed energy hubs by concentrating large numbers of EBs and enabling them to feed the grid with energy. This integration is not only to the advantage of peak load management but also improves grid reliability and clean energy consumption deployment.

EB parking lots (EBPL) in this model act as group storage devices by cumulating several EBs’ battery capacities. Their economic impact needs to be modeled in depth in terms of revenue and cost of operation. Among the key factors in the output power of parking lots is the initial charge state (SOC) of EB batteries, which directly decides the amount of energy that can be utilized for charging or discharging. Consequently, the required charge time for each EB i to be fully charged is given by the f24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t(i)=\frac{(1-SO{C}_{i}){C}_{i}}{{P}_{v}}$$\end{document}

In this, SOCi is the initial state of charge of the ith electric vehicle, Ci is the capacity of the EB battery, and Pv is the charging input power to the vehicle in kilowatts.

Power taken from EBPL is furnished by the following equations:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta}_{EBPL}=n\cdot {P}_{v}$$\end{document}where, n is available vehicles number in parking lot.

In this study, the parking space of the EBPL is 50 buses in operation with batteries of 100 kWh each and has a bus to network (B2N) dispatch level of 5840 h per year35. Every bus can supply a maximum of 20% of its peak battery to the grid. Hence, the peak capacity of EBPL which can be transferred to the network is equivalent to 1 MW.

The Monte Carlo (MC) simulation method is utilized for simulating and modeling the uncertainty of network loading and the PV generation. This method generates a sequence of random samples based on the Beta and normal PDFs, respectively, for simulating the array of possible outcomes in varying conditions. A set of random samples is obtained for each uncertain variable before a predetermined number of them are used, and an optimization operation is performed on each sample set. Such outputs in simulations allow for more realistic modeling of the network performance in the presence of uncertainties. The optimization problem is run based on the random samples, and the resultant outputs—such as optimal capacity and site of PV systems—are presented as outputs, along with bus distribution and other network parameters.

Through the integration of uncertainty in the network planning process through stochastic modeling, operators and planners can make informed decisions based on variability in load demand as well as in power generation. By this approach, the network will be maintained as robust and efficient even with uncertain conditions and allows for a more intensified integration of renewable sources such as PV systems.

Proposed optimizer

The conventional Weighted average algorithm (WAA)

The Weighted Average Algorithm (WAA)37 employs a weighted average position to achieve solutions for optimization problems where the higher weighted elements contribute more towards the solution. The technique takes into consideration the relative importance of each element and provides a more accurate global mean estimation. In WAA algorithm, the global and individual best positions are merged with the weighted average position to guide the population movement and improve convergence towards the optimum solution. This balance of exploration and exploitation allows WAA to effectively adapt to the evolving search landscape.

The improved WAA (IWAA)

The standard WAA, although simple and straightforward to implement, possesses some inherent weaknesses in handling complex optimization problems. One of its significant weaknesses is its susceptibility to premature convergence. Because WAA places a high priority on the weighted mean of the top-performing answer, the population can quickly converge to inferior regions of the search space. This results in a loss of exploration ability and does not enable the algorithm to move out of local optima effectively. The second main weakness is the rapid loss of population diversity. As iterations continue, the averaging operation reduces the variation among candidate solutions, which destroys the global search capability and results in premature convergence to potentially poor solutions. Furthermore, WAA exhibits a bad trade-off between universal discovery and local exploitation. While the incorporation of random walks such as Lévy flights provides some degree of exploration, the mechanism is too weak to ensure ongoing diversification during the entire search process. Therefore, the algorithm may spend a lot of time exploring and be unable to converge effectively or exploit local regions and be unable to explore the overall solution space well enough. Both these shortcomings reduce the accuracy and reliability of the standard WAA.

Motivation for including the sine–cosine strategy: To eliminate the disadvantages of the conventional WAA, an improvement mechanism is included using the sine–cosine strategy (SCS). The reason for inclusion is the sine and cosine functions’ oscillatory and dynamic nature38, because of which agents can move in more than a single direction with varying step sizes. This oscillatory property is a natural source of population diversity, in that agents are no longer constrained to linear or fixed trajectories. Instead, they follow nonlinear, wave-like trajectories, which provide greater ability to cover complex and multimodal landscapes. Another motivation is due to the flexibility of step size adaptation. With the inclusion of sine and cosine functions, the algorithm is endowed with dynamic control of its moving amplitude, thus supporting widespread exploration at the start of the optimization and precise exploitation towards the end. In addition, the randomness of sine and cosine angles dissuades particles from staying near local optima, thereby increasing the possibility of convergence towards the global optimum. Finally, with the addition of sine–cosine dynamics, WAA’s weighted averaging mechanism is complemented with directional oscillations about personal and global best solutions that better balances exploration and exploitation.

The new improved WAA (IWAA) generalizes the standard WAA updating rule by adding the sine–cosine movement strategy to it, thereby integrating with weighted averaging and oscillatory dynamics. The position update rule is as follows:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{i}\left(it+1\right)={X}_{i}\left(it\right)+{r}_{1}\cdot \mathit{sin}({r}_{2})\cdot |{r}_{3}\cdot {X}_{Pbest,i}\left(it\right)-{X}_{i}\left(it\right)|+{r}_{4}\cdot \mathit{cos}({r}_{2})\cdot |{r}_{3}\cdot {X}_{Gbest}\left(it\right)-{X}_{i}\left(it\right)|$$\end{document}

In this equation, variables r3 and r4 are random scaling factors that introduce stochasticity and enforce search diversity. Parameter r2, which follows a uniform distribution in the range [0, 2π], controls the direction of oscillatory movement so that the agents can move forward, backward, or around the best points in an oscillatory manner. Most importantly, control parameter r1 is defined as an adaptive coefficient that decreases over time. This property ensures an adaptive search behavior, where the step size decays to facilitate a gradual shift from exploring the global solution space to exploiting local optima.

The integration of sine- and cosine-based movements enables the algorithm to generate oscillatory search trajectories. These trajectories equip agents with the capacity not only to converge toward promising regions but also to occasionally diverge and explore other parts of the solution space. This dual capacity significantly enhances search efficiency, enhances the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima, and makes it more robust for solving highly nonlinear optimization problems. By combining the weighted averaging methodology of WAA and oscillatory dynamics of sine and cosine functions, the IWAA achieves a more balanced exploration–exploitation process. Consequently, the improved algorithm demonstrates better convergence rate, higher solution accuracy, and greater stability compared with the conventional WAA and other metaheuristic algorithms.

Conclusion

This work proposed a stochastic optimization model to solve the key challenge of optimally allocating PV systems and EBPLs in 33-bus and 69-bus distribution networks. The study is inspired by the unavoidable uncertainties that are rampant in renewable generation and dynamic load patterns, shortcomings that traditional deterministic techniques barely emulate. To robustly model these uncertainties, variability in the load demand is described using a Normal Probability Density Function (PDF), and variability in PV power generation is modelled using Beta PDF. The main optimization problem, involving the determination of the optimal placement and sizing of these facilities, is optimally solved by applying the robust Improved Weighted Average Algorithm (IWAA). Lastly, this work convincingly shows that the proposed stochastic approach is more precise and reliable planning tool when compared to deterministic methods and ensures improved system performance in real-world uncertain scenarios.The PV and EBPL allocation problem was also solved in both deterministic and probabilistic settings, more specifically on radial 33-bus and 69-bus networks.
