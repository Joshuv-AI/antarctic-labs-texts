Genomic predictions for growth and feed efficiency traits in a duck breeding population
Authors: Wentao Cai, Chengming Han, Linxi Zhu, Mengdie Wang, Qinglei Yang, Zhenlin Liu, Zhengkui Zhou, Jian Hu, Shuisheng Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the commercial broiler duck industry, optimizing breeding practices is crucial, especially for growth and feed efficiency traits. Although genomic selection (GS) has been successfully applied in livestock, its use in duck breeding is not yet widespread. This study aims to investigate genetic parameters and refine GS strategies for feed efficiency and growth traits in ducks, paving the way for more precise and efficient breeding programs.ResultsWe investigated genetic parameters of 12 growth and feed efficiency traits in a commercial breeding Line of 52,610 ducks across 10 generations. We applied genomic predictions to 2779 ducks from the latest three generations. Heritability of these traits ranging from 0.16 to 0.51. Genomic prediction accuracy was higher for GBLUP under cross-validation than forward validation. This performance discrepancy was influenced by reference population recency and trait complexity. Notably, single-step GBLUP consistently outperformed pedigree-based BLUP, particularly for feed efficiency traits. Expanding the reference population with recent generations improved forward validation accuracy by 27.7%, highlighting the critical role of updated genetic data in enhancing across-generation predictive accuracy. The newly proposed residual feed intake adjusted for breast muscle volume demonstrated a higher heritability and predictive accuracy compared to its predecessor. Pruning variants using Linkage disequilibrium thresholds of 0.075 resulted in an increase of 0.05 in the average predictive accuracy. Similarly, omitting the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium threshold generally resulted in higher predictive accuracy for most traits. However, for traits such as BMW, BMT, and BMV, we observed enhanced predictive accuracy when applying a specific threshold for HWE test pruning. The BayesRC model, when informed by cis-eQTLs or their regulated genes, particularly from adipose and muscle tissues, increased predictive accuracy for various traits, highlighting the importance of integrating biological data into genomic prediction frameworks.ConclusionsThis study offers encouraging evidence for utilizing GS to enhance growth and feed efficiency traits in ducks. It offers valuable insights into optimizing GS for duck breeding, emphasizing the critical roles of model selection, marker density refinement, and the strategic integration of prior biological information.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12087-7.

Background

Ducks have long been popular in Asia and remain incredibly sought after in Europe. Over the past twenty Years, there has been a significant increase in duck meat production. China is the leading producer, supplying over 60% of the global duck meat production and 80% of regional production [1]. A wide variety of duck meat products are available to meet diverse consumer preferences, and duck breeding programs are expanding in scope. Like chicken breeding programs, Pekin ducks are bred using specific male and female lines, which are crossed at the grandparent and parental levels to produce the commercial broiler duck populations. The selection criteria for broiler duck lines primarily include growth rate, feed efficiency, carcass yield, and egg production. Historically, genetic advancements in duck breeding have largely relied on phenotypic and pedigree data based on family selection or best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP). However, there remains a significant gap in the application of genomic selection (GS) within these breeding programs.

GS is a powerful tool in animal breeding that increases the efficiency of the selection and reduces costs. The livestock industry, including pig [2], cattle [3], and sheep [4] has widely adopted GS. Genetic merit estimates were made more accurate and generation intervals were shortened by GS, which combined genotypic, phenotypic, and pedigree data. Poultry farming has seen tremendous success with GS, resulting in the creation of specialized breeds that greatly surpass their wild counterparts in terms of egg production and quick meat growth [5, 6]. The genomic predictions can achieve significant increases in predictive accuracy for carcass, growth, and breast morphological traits within a crossbreed duck population [7, 8]. In a commercial breeding population, genomic prediction has been primarily focused on several weight traits in Pekin ducks [9]. However, GS for many important feed efficiency and breast morphological traits remains unknown in breeding population. Furthermore, the advantages of genomic prediction have not been fully realized compared to BLUP due to the limited sample sizes involved [9].

The predictive accuracy of GS is affected by a number of factors, including the statistical genetic architecture of target traits, marker density, relatedness between individuals in reference and validation population, sample sizes of training and testing, and model performance [10]. With the decrease in genotyping costs, GS has been applied to more species. However, due to the absence of a commercial single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array for breeding, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) technology remains the preferred method for genotyping, as it can identify many markers. However, previous studies have indicated that excessively high marker density does not significantly enhance predictive accuracy and instead greatly reduces computational efficiency [11, 12]. Many pairs of variants exhibit very high or complete linkage disequilibrium (LD), resulting in redundancy in genomic information among high-density markers [13]. Eliminating loci due to high levels of pairwise LD is commonly referred to as LD pruning. It is standard practice to remove one SNP from each pair of SNPs that have an LD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}^{2}$$\end{document} value of 1 in genomic prediction [14]. A previous study reported that LD pruning can improve predictive accuracy in 600 K chip data and imputated WGS data [15]. However, the impact of LD pruning on predictive accuracy in true WGS data remains unknown.

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) describes the expected distribution of genotype frequencies in a population that is not subject to evolutionary forces such as selection, mutation, migration, or genetic drift. HWE pruning for genotype quality control has been widely adopted in human genetics [16, 17]. Many animal breeding populations have also utilized different HWE thresholds for genotype quality control [18–20]. Due to the high-intensity selection in animal breeding populations, many loci have deviated from HWE. Therefore, the misuse of HWE quality control may cause significant implications for GS. In addition, it has been proposed to enhance genomic prediction models by incorporating prior biological information, prioritizing markers likely to be causal, and thereby improving prediction accuracy [21, 22]. The expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) can explain large proportions of the heritability for complex traits [23, 24], which implied eQTLs have potential to improve the accuracy of genomic predictions. Previously, we identified many cis-eQTLs of seven duck tissues in ducks [25]. Investigating the potential improvement in predictive accuracy using cis-eQTLs prior information is essential.

Therefore, the objectives of this research are as follows: (1) To compute the genetic parameters related to duck growth and feed efficiency traits, including a newly proposed residual feed intake formula that adjusts for breast muscle volume trait; (2) To assess the predictive accuracy of BLUP and GBLUP models; and (3) To examine the effects of LD and HWE pruning, as well as the incorporation of cis-eQTL prior information, on predictive accuracy.

Methods

Animal population and phenotype data

This study used a breeding population of Zhongxin white duck (a commercial breed developed from the Pekin duck), which consisted of 52,610 individuals from 1 to 10 generations (Table S1). The ducks were sourced from the Shandong Lijin Duck Breeding Farm (Dongying, China), owned by Shandong Zhongxin Food Group Co., Ltd. All animals were raised on the same farm, under uniform feeding and management conditions. Two weeks after hatching, all ducklings were relocated from the duckling house to a designated individual shed where their feed intake (FI) was monitored until they reached six weeks of age. We measured their body weight at 14 days (BW14) and 42 days (BW42). The breast muscle width (BMW, distance between the two shoulder joints) and keel length (KL, the distance between the front and rear ends of the keel) were measured by a caliper at 42 days. Breast muscle thickness (BMT) was measured using a portable B-ultrasound device. Measurements were taken at the midpoint of the keel and 2 cm to the left of the keel’s edge. Breast muscle volume (BMV) was calculated using KL×BMW×BMT. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the ratio of feed intake (FI) to weight gain (WG) during the period from 14 to 42 days. Genotyping

A total of 2779 ducks from the latest three generations (G8-G10, Table S1) were genotyped for genomic predictions. Blood samples were collected at six weeks. We extracted genomic DNA using the salt-out procedure of TIANamp Blood DNA kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). A NanoDrop spectrophotometer was used to evaluate the quality and concentration of the DNA samples, and agarose gel electrophoresis was used to confirm the DNA’s integrity and purity. Paired-end Libraries were created for each sample in accordance with standard Illumina protocols after quality confirmation. The sequenced Libraries featured a read length of 150 bp and an average insert size of 400 bp. Utilizing the DNBSEQ-T7 platform, sequencing was carried out, yielding an average raw read depth of ×5 coverage for the population. Clean reads were aligned to the duck reference genome (ZJU1.0) using BWA software (v0.7.17). The alignment quality was improved using Picard (v2.24.1) [27]. Variants calling was performed with the GATK HaplotypeCaller module (v4.2). Variants were then filtered using standard quality control metrics, including minimum quality score (QUAL ≥ 30), depth (DP ≥ 3) thresholds. Additionally, following strict quality control measures like filtering for call rates (< 90%), and minor allele frequency (MAF < 0.05), a total of 10,349,420 variants were kept for subsequent analysis.

Cross-validation: The data were divided into five subsets at random to conduct the cross-validation using a five-fold approach. For each subset, estimated breeding values (EBVs) were calculated using the remaining subsets as the reference population. Forward validation of G8 and G9: The 2198 individuals of G8 and G9 generations were used as the reference population, while their 296 offspring of the second hatch of G10 generation served as the validation population. The reference population of ssGBLUP included 2198 genotyped and 9223 non-genotyped ducks of G8 and G9 generations. Forward validation of G8, G9 and G10A: The 2483 individuals of G8, G9 and the first hatch of G10 generations were used as the reference population, while their 296 offspring of the second hatch of G10 generation served as the validation population. Predictive accuracy was assessed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between the EBVs and the corresponding adjusted phenotypic values.

Pruning markers based on LD and HWE

In a population, non-random associations of alleles at various loci are referred to as LD. More precise genetic merit prediction is possible when there is a high LD between markers and quantitative trait loci (QTL) since markers are better able to capture the genetic variance linked to selected traits [33]. We carried out the LD pruning in this study using nine thresholds of LD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} (0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.075, 0.05, 0.025, 0.01). For a pair of biallelic variants (e.g., SNP1: alleles A and a; SNP2: alleles B and b) at two genomic loci. We calculated their LD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}=\frac{{({\text{p}}_{\text{A}\text{B}}-{\text{p}}_{\text{A}}{\text{p}}_{\text{B}})}^{2}}{{\text{p}}_{\text{A}}(1-{\text{p}}_{\text{A}}){\text{p}}_{\text{B}}(1-{\text{p}}_{\text{B}})}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{p}}_{\text{A}}$$\end{document} is the frequency of allele A, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{p}}_{\text{B}}$$\end{document} is the allele frequency of B. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{p}}_{\text{A}\text{B}}$$\end{document} is the frequency of haplotype AB. The LD were calculated using PLINK’s --indep-pairwise command, with a window size of 500 Kb and a step size of 50 for each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} threshold [34]. GBLUP model was used to estimate EBVs. Five-fold cross-validation was used to determine the predictive accuracy for each LD threshold. To eliminate the influence of marker density on predictive accuracy, we randomly selected an equal number of variants for each LD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} threshold from the original 10.3 million markers. To ensure stability, the results were averaged over 20 replicates for each LD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} threshold. In the context of genomic prediction, deviations from HWE can significantly impact the accuracy of predicting genetic merit. In this study, we pruned the variants using different thresholds of HWE p-value (0.5, 0.1, 10−2, 10−4, 10−6, 10−8, 10−10, 10−13, 10−16, 10−20, 10−30, 10−40, 10−60, 10−80, 10−100, 10−150, 10−200). The HWE p-values were calculated using Chi-squared test from PLINK --hwe command [34]. We assessed predictive accuracy under these different HWE thresholds using GBLUP model. We also randomly selected an equal number of variants for each HWE thresholds from the original 10.3 million markers with 20 replicates.

Genomic predictions using bayesian models

Furthermore, we calculated the predictive accuracy of Bayesian models comprising BayesC, BayesN, BayesB, BayesR, and BayesS using LD threshold (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} < 0.075) genotype panel. These five Bayesian models with different assumed distributions of SNP effects were described in our previous study [7]. The Bayesian models were implemented using the GCTB software (V2.01), which employs a Gibbs sampling approach based on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations [35]. Using the same fivefold cross-validation process outlined for the GBLUP model, the predictive accuracy of the Bayesian models was assessed.

Impact of prior information on genomic predictions

Integrating prior information strategically into GS frameworks is a promising method to enhance both the predictive power and practical utility of GS in breeding programs [36]. In our previous study [9], we analyzed the publicly available RNA sequencing data from seven duck tissues (adipose, muscle, liver, blood, spleen, lung, and ovary), and identified 113,374 cis-eQTLs associated with 12,266 genes. In this study, we employed the BayesRC model to incorporate prior biological information from cis-eQTLs (including their LD related variants\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:\text{r}}^{2}$$\end{document} >0.1) and eGenes (variants located in eGenes and their flanking down/upstream 5 Kb regions) to enhance the prediction accuracy of GS. Genomic predictions using bayesian models

Due to the inefficiency of Bayesian models in handling WGS data, we performed genomic predictions based on various Bayesian methods (BayesB, BayesC, BayesR, BayesS, and BayesN) using pruning variants with thresholds of R² ≤ 0.075 (168 K variants). All Bayesian methods achieved similar genomic prediction accuracy with GBLUP using LD pruning panel, except for BayesN with a 0.005 decrease (Table S5, Fig. 3c). BayesR had a relatively higher accuracy than other Bayesian models on feed efficiency traits. Compared to premutation results of 168 K randomly selected markers from WGS data with 10 replicates, we also observed a notable increase in predictive accuracy of Bayesian models for the LD pruning panel (Fig. 3c). For the permutated markers, the accuracy of GBLUP were lower than Bayesian models for most traits (Fig. 3d). BayesB had a relatively higher accuracy than other Bayesian models in BW42, WG, BMW, BMV and FCR. The accuracy gaps between BayesN and other Bayesian models were narrower, with BayesN demonstrating higher accuracy specifically for BW14, and KL. The advantages of genotype LD pruning in terms of predictive accuracy were more pronounced for GBLUP models, while the benefits were less significant for BayesN and BayesB models (Fig. 3d).

Genomic prediction by pruning markers based on HWE test

We selected the markers using various HWE thresholds to assess their impact on genomic prediction accuracy (GBLUP) (Fig. 4a and Additional file 1, Table S6). A total of 17 thresholds of HWE p-value (0.5, 0.1, 10−2, 10−4, 10−6, 10−8, 10−10, 10−13, 10−16, 10−20, 10−30, 10−40, 10−60, 10−80, 10−100, 10−150, 10−200) were used to filter variants. As the HWE thresholds were tightened from a p-value of ≤ 10−200 to ≤ 0.5, the accuracy of genomic prediction initially decreased, followed by an increase, and then a final decline for all traits, with the exception of FI, which consistently exhibited a decrease. The increasing trend in the middle was not evident for BW42, WG, and MW. Using HWE test pruning, the predictive accuracy for FI, KL, BW42, WG, and MW traits never exceeded that of randomly permuted variant datasets (Fig. 4b and Additional file 1, Table S6). Although the predictive accuracy of BW14, FCR, RFI1, and RFI2 could be higher than that of randomly permuted variant datasets, it remained close to that of the original panel containing all variants. We observed higher predictive accuracy for BMW, BMT, and BMV with a specific threshold of HWE test pruning compared to the original panel containing all variants. When the variants were pruned based on a p-value of ≤ 10−13, the average predictive accuracy for the 12 traits reached its peak (Fig. 4b and Additional file 1, Table S6). However, it is important to note that the average predictive accuracy of these 12 traits remained lower than that of the original panel containing all variants.

Genomic prediction using prior information of cis-eQTLs

In our previous study [25], we identified 113,374 cis-eQTLs for 12,266 genes by analyzing publicly available RNA sequencing data involved in adipose, muscle, liver, blood, spleen, lung, and ovary tissues. We utilized prior biological information based on cis-eQTLs and eGenes (genes regulated by cis-eQTLs) separately. The original genotype panel, consisting of 10.3 million variants, was divided into two groups based on whether the variants fell within the prior biological information. Compared to BayesR without prior information, incorporating prior information of cis-eQTLs using BayesRC in muscle tissue resulted in an average increase in genomic prediction accuracy of 0.04% (Fig. 5a and Additional file 1, Table S7). The predictive accuracy of both WG and BMT can be improved by incorporating prior information from all seven tissues, resulting in an average increase in predictive accuracy of 0.63% and 0.79%, respectively. We observed significant improvements in predictive accuracy for WG, with increases of 1.77% and 1.73% when using muscle and spleen cis-eQTLs, respectively. Additionally, BMT showed an improvement of 1.68% with the incorporation of adipose cis-eQTLs, while BW42 demonstrated a 1.21% enhancement using blood cis-eQTLs.

Discussion

In this study, we collected growth and feed efficiency traits in duck breeding populations and estimated their heritability. Furthermore, we investigated the predictive accuracy of GS for these traits, assessed the impact of variants pruning based on LD and HWE, and explored the integration of prior biological information using the BayesRC model. Our results highlighted the potential of genomic prediction to improve breeding outcomes in ducks.

Overall, weight traits exhibited relatively high heritabilities, consistent with previous studies [8, 39, 40], making them strong candidates for genetic selection in breeding programs due to their substantial genetic influence. The heritability of feed efficiency traits were moderate, which aligns with finding from a prior study on Pekin ducks [40]. The heritabilities of breast morphological traits ranged from low to moderate (ranging from 0.16 to 0.22), consistent with earlier research conducted on a crossbred duck population [41]. We observed differences in heritability estimates across the three models (animal model, maternal environment effect model, and maternal genetic effects model), reflecting the impact of maternal effects on phenotypic traits in animals [42]. Heritability values derived from the maternal genetic effects model and the maternal environmental model were consistently lower than those obtained from the animal model, highlighting the reduced significance of maternal contributions to trait variation. In a previous study on Pekin ducks, the maternal genetic effects on body weight decreased from the 3rd week to the 7th week [43]. Similarly, our observations indicate that both maternal genetic effects and maternal environmental effects on body weight diminish from the 2nd week to the 6th week.

Our investigation of the genetic and phenotypic correlations among the 12 traits uncovered several important insights with significant implications for duck breeding strategies. Notably, the residual FI indicators, RFI1 and RFI2, showed extremely high positive genetic and phenotypic correlations (0.998), indicating that these two indicators shared nearly identical genetic background and observable characteristics. This suggested that selection for one could effectively enhance the other [44]. Additionally, FCR was positively correlated with the RFI indicators [45, 46], consistent with our findings, highlighting FCR’s strong association with feed efficiency traits, which makes it a valuable target for breeding programs focused on improving resource efficiency [47]. However, FCR exhibited negative correlations with several body composition and growth traits, including BMW, KL, BMT, (BMV, WG, and MW. For instance, the genetic correlation of either FCR or RFI1 with WG was considerable negative. These negative correlations suggested potential trade-offs between feed efficiency and growth or body composition traits. However, the newly proposed RFI2 adjusted for breast muscle volume exhibited a lower genetic correlation with WG, which could effectively reduce the negative selection pressures on WG when implementing feed efficiency selection.

The GBLUP model exhibited predictive accuracy based on cross-validation ranging from 0.29 to 0.50 for feed efficiency traits, which were close to a previous study in chicken [48]. Interestingly, the newly proposed RFI2 demonstrated a higher predictive accuracy of 0.38 compared to its predecessor RFI1 (0.37). This improvement suggests that advancements in breast muscle volume and modeling of feed efficiency traits are crucial for enhancing predictive accuracy. For weight and growth traits, GBLUP model accuracy was moderate, ranging from 0.24 to 0.32, indicating potential for improvement in selecting these traits. Conversely, the predictive accuracy for breast muscle traits were lower, except for BMW. Overall, the weight and breast morphological traits in our pure breed exhibited relatively lower predictive accuracy compared to a previous study involving F2 crossbred ducks [8]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the F2 design, which included more closely related individuals and a larger LD, thereby enhancing predictive accuracy [49]. A significant finding from our study was the considerable enhancement in predictive ability using GBLUP compared to traditional pedigree BLUP methods, as evidenced by the paired t-test result. This improvement in predictive accuracy is consistent with findings from other research, which indicates that incorporating genomic information can significantly boost prediction accuracy [8, 41]. The most noticeable increments in predictive accuracy using GBLUP were observed for BMW, BW14, and KL, suggesting that these traits may be particularly amenable to GS strategies.

The cross-validation framework demonstrated markedly higher predictive accuracy compared to forward validation across all traits, which was consistent with previous studies [50, 51]. Cross-validation, which randomly partitions data within the same generation, inherently benefits from shared genetic and environmental covariance between training and validation sets. In contrast, forward validation mimics real-world breeding scenarios by predicting future generations (e.g., G10) using historical data (G8/G9), thereby introducing temporal genetic drift, genotype-environment interactions, and reduced relatedness between reference and target populations. Expanding the reference population with the first batch of G10 ducks improved average GBLUP accuracy by 27.7% (0.130 → 0.166), highlighting the critical role of reference population size and recency. The superior performance of ssGBLUP over BLUP in both forward validation paradigms confirm the added value of genomic information. However, the diminished advantage in forward validation (3.1–4.0% vs. 9% in cross-validation) emphasizes that genomic predictions face greater erosion than pedigree-based predictions when applied to future generations. This observation aligns with the larger accuracy increments for BLUP when incorporating non-genotyped individuals (BLUP: 0.121 → 0.179; ssGBLUP: 0.133 → 0.184), suggesting that pedigree data provide more stable across-generation connections than genomic data alone. Notably, the inclusion of G10 data shifted model superiority patterns: BLUP outperformed ssGBLUP for weight/growth traits (BW42, WG, MW, FI) in the extended validation, whereas ssGBLUP maintained advantages for feed efficiency traits (FCR, RFI). This dichotomy implies that conventional pedigree models retain value for highly heritable traits with stable genetic architectures, while genomic models excel for polygenic traits where marker data capture cryptic relatedness.

When QTLs are in strong LD, employing the unweighted genomic relationship matrix in GBLUP can lead to a upward bias in the estimation of heritability [52]. LD pruning, a strategy for reducing the number of genetic markers, has been successfully applied in humans [53] and pigs [54], but similar research in ducks has been lacking. In our study, we examined the effects of varying LD thresholds on genomic prediction using the GBLUP method. We observed an increase in predictive accuracy for all traits when we pruned variants with moderate LD (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}^{2}\:$$\end{document}0.6 ~ 0.1). A similar result was also found in a previous study [15]. With a tighter LD pruning criterion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}^{2}$$\end{document}<0.075), we observed an decrease in predictive accuracy. This suggests that a stricter pruning criterion may Limit the available genetic information beyond a certain threshold, which can adversely affect predictive performance. Overall, our maximum average predictive accuracy for the 12 traits at an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}^{2}$$\end{document} threshold of 0.075 emphasizes the critical role of strategically selecting LD thresholds in genomic prediction studies. The observed improvement of 0.05 in predictive accuracy, compared to no LD pruning, reinforces the necessity of this approach in enhancing genomic prediction accuracy. This finding indicates that careful LD management can lead to better outcomes in GS.

Although BayesN faces challenges in predictive accuracy compared to other models, it particularly excels in predicting BW14 and KL when applied to permutated genotype data. This suggests that BayesN retains specific advantages in certain traits that could be exploited in breeding programs. Other studies have also recognized its potential in GS [8, 55]. The BayesR model assumes four marker distributions and has ability to heavily shrink large effects, often outperforming GBLUP, especially when only a few loci with substantial effects are present for a trait [56]. BayesR demonstrated relatively higher accuracy compared to other Bayesian models for feed efficiency traits, suggesting that substantial effects exist within these traits. In terms of permutated markers, GBLUP exhibited lower accuracy than the Bayesian models for most traits, indicating a potential advantage of Bayesian approaches in situations with high dimensionality and possibly greater noise. This advantage arises from the fact that Bayesian methods assign different weights to different markers [56, 57]. BayesB demonstrated relatively higher accuracy than other Bayesian models when using permuted genotypes; however, this advantage diminished with LD-pruned genotypes. This suggests that BayesB may be better suited for handling genotypes characterized by considerable LD. The benefits of genotype LD pruning in enhancing predictive accuracy were more evident for GBLUP than other models, indicating that genomic relationship matrix may be more susceptible to the influence of redundant markers.

The assumption of HWE is often employed in quality control of genotype, as it simplifies the computation of allele and genotype frequencies, which are crucial for accurate estimation of genetic parameters and breeding values [58]. In this study, tightening the HWE thresholds resulted in an initial decrease in the accuracy of genomic prediction, followed by an increase, and ultimately a final decline for most traits. This pattern suggests that while some level of HWE pruning can be beneficial, overly stringent criteria may inadvertently eliminate valuable genetic information essential for accurate predictions. Notably, the exception of FI demonstrates a consistent decline in predictive accuracy, indicating that HWE may not be a suitable filtering method for this trait. Using HWE test pruning, the predictive accuracy of most traits did not surpass those of the original panel containing all variants. However, BMW, BMT, and BMV demonstrated higher predictive accuracy at specific thresholds of HWE pruning compared to the original panel, suggesting a complex relationship between the HWE threshold stringency and the predictive accuracy of genomic predictions across different duck growth and feed efficiency traits. The maximum average predictive accuracy for the 12 traits at an HWE p-value ≤ 10−13 emphasizes the critical role of strategically selecting HWE thresholds in genomic prediction, while the average predictive accuracy remained lower than that of the original panel containing all variants. These findings suggested a need to rethink traditional genotype quality control practices and lean toward more inclusive genomic datasets to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of breeding programs.

The genetic architectures of traits can influence genomic prediction accuracy [56], and the locus-specific priors have been shown to offer advantages over common priors [59–62]. In this study, we utilized prior information from cis-eQTLs of seven duck tissues to improve genomic prediction accuracy, consistent with the broader understanding of the value of biological priors in GS [63]. Incorporating prior information from cis-eQTLs and eGenes in muscle tissue leading to a modest average increase in genomic prediction accuracy across the 12 traits, with notable improvements in traits, such as WG, BW42, BW14, BMV and RFI2. Similarly,, utilizing eGenes from adipose tissue yielded a modest average increase in genomic prediction accuracy of 0.19% across the same traits, suggesting that regulatory information specific to muscle and adipose tissue could effectively enhance the predictive accuracy of certain metabolic traits. Certain traits, such as BMT and WG, exhibited a significant increase in predictive accuracy when incorporating prior information from cis-eQTLs, while traits like BW42, BW14, and RFI2 demonstrated substantial enhancements in predictive accuracy with the inclusion of prior information from eGenes. The use of cis-eQTLs and their eGenes prior information produced mixed results across yielded mixed results across different tissues, highlighting the sensitivity of prior information to the accuracy of genomic predictions. These reinforced the importance of selecting the right biological priors based on the trait of interest. Capturing a larger portion of genetic variance through molecular markers does not necessarily result in higher prediction accuracy [64]. Our results underscore the potential of different tissue cis-eQTLs to variably influence genomic prediction accuracy. Future research could focus on integrating multi-omics data to capture the genetic architecture more effectively and enhance predictive power, ultimately contributing to more precise and effective breeding strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides essential insights into optimizing GS for duck breeding, underscoring the significance of model selection, marker density refinement, and the integration of prior biological information. Our findings reveal the advantages of GBLUP over traditional BLUP models, particularly for feed efficiency traits, suggesting that expanding GBLUP’s application could significantly benefit breeding programs. The accuracy of genomic prediction was notably higher in ssGBLUP compared to GBLUP during forward validation, especially for feed efficiency traits. Furthermore, expanding reference populations with more recent generations enhanced the accuracy of forward validation. This underscores the importance of utilizing updated genetic data for robust predictions across generations. The newly proposed residual feed intake adjusted for breast muscle volume demonstrated a higher heritability and predictive accuracy compared to its predecessor, warranting its promotion in duck breeding programs. Additionally, we highlight the importance of tailoring LD and HWE thresholds to specific traits for optimal predictive accuracy. This analysis enhances our understanding of genomic prediction dynamics and lays a foundation for future research, encouraging exploration across diverse populations and traits to refine GS methodologies and maximize genetic gains in breeding strategies.
