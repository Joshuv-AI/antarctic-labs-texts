RESPOND intervention: protocol for a cluster-randomized stepped-wedge trial of a human factors intervention to reduce failure to rescue in emergency general surgery
Authors: Peter McCulloch, Sachal Safdar, Mark Sujan, Nicola Macintosh, Mudathir Ibrahim, Joanne Kitchin, Laurie Earl, Abhishek Dey, William Plath, Saydia Wellesley, Rose Baker, Felix Achana, Lesley Booth
Published: 2026

Background

Healthcare is, by its nature, a dangerous activity. It involves subjecting humans whose bodies (or minds) are malfunctioning to a range of interventions including chemicals, knives, and radiation of various types, in an attempt to restore their well-being. It has been recognized since antiquity that medicine can kill or maim instead of curing, but the extent of the unintended damage it can cause has only been quantified relatively recently.1 Harm caused directly by healthcare interventions represents only a fraction of the lost potential of healthcare—many patients suffer or die because an ineffective treatment was chosen instead of an effective one, or because the right treatment plan was selected but was not executed correctly or fast enough.

In low-income settings, the main constraint on healthcare is lack of trained staff and resources, but in higher-income countries, lost potential benefits are often caused by inappropriate use of equipment and deficiencies in systems of work.2 These represent an important opportunity for improving healthcare outcomes, since they can in principle be corrected without massive investment, retraining, or recruitment, using a human factors (HF) approach. The detailed analysis of modern healthcare harm and its causes, which began in the 1990s has repeatedly demonstrated the major roles played by faults in systems design and poor team function.3 4 However, attempts to improve patient outcomes by tackling these issues directly have yet to achieve their apparent potential. Large, well-designed studies of HF interventions are difficult to execute, have therefore been relatively rare, and have often failed to show benefit to patients.5 6

Surgery represents an area of healthcare in which patient outcomes are often transformational (for good or ill), and their causation is usually not contested. As such, it represents a very suitable field for studying the effects of systems and teamwork on patient outcomes, and studies of formal quality improvement interventions have demonstrated promising results.7 8 Studies of surgical outcomes have repeatedly shown that mortality is lower in large, high-volume units,9 and initially, the inference drawn was that operating teams benefit from repeated practice together. However, Ghaferi et al 10 highlighted the fact that complication rates were much less clearly associated with volume than mortality. The implication of this finding was that large units did not necessarily do better surgery, but they were better at rescuing patients who developed complications. This, in turn, suggested that efforts to reduce surgical mortality should focus on rescue systems rather than on events in the operating theater. The concept of ‘failure to rescue (FTR)’ was developed and has become a benchmarking measure for surgical unit quality.11 12

The most common high-mortality operation performed worldwide is emergency laparotomy, with a current mortality rate in the UK approaching 10%.13 Emergency abdominal surgery is also an area in which HF issues have frequently been highlighted as a cause of avoidable morbidity or mortality, with reports providing considerable evidence of the need for improvement in both teamwork and systems of work.14 We have therefore identified emergency abdominal surgery as a suitable target for an HF-based intervention to reduce FTR. Our previous studies with surgical teams demonstrated the importance of addressing both system and team factors, co-designing interventions with staff, and having a comprehensive engagement strategy to encourage staff involvement.15 We have also drawn lessons from EPOCH, a large randomized study of a quality improvement intervention in this clinical area, which failed to improve mortality.5 The EPOCH authors shared their conclusions with us, particularly regarding the ‘dose’ of input required and the number of interventions that staff teams can manage simultaneously. Finally, we have conducted research with trainers and coaches of elite sports teams and military units to support the development of a new approach to strengthening clinical teams, since the evidence of benefit to patients from current systems is unconvincing.16 Based on the literature and our own findings, therefore, we have developed a ‘bundle’ of four HF interventions, designed to impact different parts of the rescue/escalation system. Initial observational and analytical work led to a description of the influences, which can affect the performance of rescue attempts for seriously ill postoperative patients,17 and subsequent co-design work led to the development of an intervention, which we tested in a three-hospital pilot study (manuscript in preparation). The bundle intervention was associated with a significant decrease in response time, which encouraged us to proceed to a cluster-randomized trial to evaluate the effect of our intervention on FTR.

Proposed study

Aims

Our primary aim is to test the hypothesis that a bundle of HF interventions, designed to address issues across the whole of the rescue response process, can decrease mortality from complications after emergency abdominal surgery (FTR). Secondary aims include the evaluation of the effects of the program on other relevant clinical end points, specifically length of hospital stay, length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, response time, and patient quality of life at 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery. We will also conduct a qualitative process evaluation to gain information on what parts of the intervention worked, and why, and to explore staff engagement with and reactions to the program. Finally, we will conduct a health economic analysis of cost-effectiveness. The study reporting will follow the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines.

PICO question

The central study question is defined as:

In

P

Patients admitted as an emergency to general surgery units with acute abdominal pain, who become critically unwell following an emergency surgical operation or other invasive therapeutic procedure.

Does

I

A four-stranded bundle of HF interventions targeting patient involvement, staff team cohesion, escalation process fidelity, and cooperation between staff groups and departments.

Compared with

C

Normal practice in the same unit prior to the intervention.

Affect

O

FTR, measured as mortality from surgical complications (primary outcome). Secondary outcomes are: length of hospital stay, length of stay in intensive care, speed of clinical response, overall mortality (in-hospital and 90 days) and healthcare costs, and quality of life at 3, 6, and 12 months after the emergency surgery.

Study design

We propose a cluster-randomized stepped-wedge design with 24 individual hospitals randomized to receive the intervention at different times in six regional clusters of four hospitals each. The clusters will begin the intervention at 3 month intervals. Each cluster will undergo a 3-month ‘induction’ phase, during which the intervention will be implemented with intensive support from HF experts, and a 6-month ‘maintenance’ phase, during which a lower level of continuing advice will be available by video call or email. Units will continue to use the new systems of work thereafter, supported by online resources and a weekly helpline call.

Data collection on eligible deteriorating patients will begin in all 24 hospitals 3 months before the first cluster begins the intervention, and will continue throughout the induction, maintenance, and postintervention periods, until 3 months after the last cluster has exited the maintenance phase—a total of 30 months (figure 1). This will create a balanced grid, which ensures roughly equal numbers of patients experiencing standard care and care using the RESPOND intervention.

Gantt chart showing proposed study timetable. There are six clusters of four hospitals each, which are randomly assigned to intervention start dates at 3-month intervals. Induction (face-to-face) support is supplied for 3 months and maintenance (online support) for a following 6 months. Setting

Inpatient emergency general surgery (EGS) units at 24 general hospitals with 24-hour EGS admissions in England. This includes some hospitals under the same management (same hospital Trust) that manage EGS patients independently and have different catchment areas.

Ethics and informed consent

Patients without capacity to consent are eligible for the study, since such patients form an important percentage of the predominantly elderly population being studied. The study intervention does not involve any new treatments but instead uses HF techniques to modify treatment efficiency and speed at the unit level. Therefore, the only consent required is for examination of clinical records and sending of quality of life questionnaires. Fully informed written or online consent is the norm, but the use of consultee advice will be necessary where the patient lacks the capacity to consent either permanently or temporarily. All eligible patients are unwell, and many of them are unstable, making consent a difficult undertaking at times. A subgroup of the eligible patients will be critically ill, and there is a risk that they might die despite treatment. These patients are among the most important in the study, as they are the most likely to benefit from the study intervention. It is therefore very important that their data are not lost. We have therefore put in place emergency consultee advice provisions, allowing friends or next of kin or, in the absence of these, the on-call consultant for the hospital’s ICU to approve examination of the notes. Where patients recover capacity after their period of acute illness, consent will be confirmed with them. These stipulations are summarized in the submissions for ethical approval accepted by the UK Health Research Authority.

Study interventions

The study intervention comprises four separate programs which target different parts of the escalation process and the surgical team. All four are based on an initial HF analysis of escalation processes in three hospitals17 and were co-designed with staff and with patients who had recovered after complications of emergency surgery. In addition to the strands, the intervention includes a predefined engagement strategy, since previous work had shown that staff engagement was often weak and determined the success of HF interventions.18

Strand 1: patient involvement

This strand enables patients to contact the Critical Care Outreach Team (CCOT) directly if they believe their physical condition is seriously declining. Patients will be given a physical ‘red card’ on admission, with the CCOT number printed on it. They are encouraged to use this if they are unable to get help from their assigned ward nurse or are worried that reassurance from ward nurses may be erroneous. In hospitals without a CCOT, a dedicated mobile phone will be carried at all times by a senior nurse from the general surgical ward. An associated information leaflet encourages relatives and other patients to use the system on behalf of other patients if they appear to be unwell and unable to use it.

Strand 2: team strengthening

This strand aims to improve team performance by using team strengthening strategies identified through interviews with expert sports and military trainers. Thematic analysis of the interviews and subsequent development of a prototype program are described in a paper currently under review (paper under review). The aim is to create a culture of mutual support and determination to achieve team goals in the face of psychological pressure. The components of the program include:

Co-creation and maintenance of team values by the clinical team members, using an initial ‘Awaydays’ event and integration into daily work using reminders at natural pauses, for example, safety huddles, handovers, and ward rounds.

Strand 3: developing a resilient operating procedure for escalation

The escalation process for deteriorating patients in England varies somewhat between hospitals but is based on a national template tied to the NEWS2 alert system.20 The ‘Safety 2’ approach to HF21 argues that complex real-life work situations often defy efforts to prescribe a single best pathway via an algorithm and instead recommend measures to strengthen system resilience to unexpected combinations of events. Using this approach, we will develop customized emergency reference cards for frontline staff to guide them during time-urgent responses to critical situations. The cards will be modeled on the emergency cards available to pilots in civil aviation. They will remind staff of the core tasks which are required and the different options available to them, and provide strong guidance on when and how to seek help from others. Cards will be tailored for use by nurses, junior doctors, and specialists, and accompanied by educational materials delivered via WhatsApp groups, e-boards, and short briefings at natural staff meeting points such as ward rounds and handovers.

Strand 4: shared ownership

One frequently reported problem in achieving timely treatment for deteriorating postoperative patients is failure of the parts of the hospital system to coordinate effectively. To counter this, the ‘Shared Ownership’ strand provides (a) RICHER, a tool to ensure better communication between junior doctors in surgery and more experienced doctors in their own team or in other specialties such as radiology and anesthesia; (b) an analysis of the structural challenges to coordination in each hospital by the HF team, resulting in a report to the Chief Executive highlighting problems that need higher level help, and proposing both short-term and long-term solutions; (c) multidisciplinary simulations using a dummy ‘avatar’ patient created on the electronic patient record to test the system. The simulation will run in real time and will be made as realistic as possible, so that staff are not necessarily aware that the scenario is a simulation until after they have made a decision (eg, on whether to admit a patient to intensive care). Simulations will be directly supervised by research staff to ensure there is no disruption of care to real patients, and precautions will be taken to ensure information security.

Engagement strategy

The key to success in process change interventions is frontline staff engagement,22 which faces great challenges in the National Health Service hospitals at the present time. We devised our engagement strategy based on the ‘supported champions’ model used successfully in a previous project,23 but experience in our pilot study identified a lack of clinical leadership support as a key problem. We have therefore modified our strategy to involve four key leaders on the surgical unit (lead consultant surgeon, lead nurse, and two others) in introducing and encouraging use of the interventions. A carefully planned communications campaign will be conducted, using posters, leaflets, social media, electronic message boards, and regular informal chats with refreshments, to improve staff understanding and support of the project. Incentives for staff involvement will include certificates and letters of commendation, support for junior doctors in delivering quality improvement projects required for their training portfolios, online HF resources, and opportunities to participate directly in the research, leading to publication and presentation credits.

Data about deterioration episodes and their outcomes will be collected via the hospital EPR. A measure of team climate in relation to safety, the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire,24 will be sent to all clinical team members before and after the intervention. Two additional forms of evaluation will be carried out—a health economics study and a qualitative process evaluation.

Health economic data

Patients will be asked to complete a standard EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level form about their quality of life at the time they are being consented into the study, recovery, or first opportunity following rescue surgery (to establish a baseline) and subsequently at 3, 6, and 12 months after their emergency surgery. At 3, 6, and 12 months, they will also be asked to complete a short questionnaire about use of healthcare resources. Patients can complete the forms online or on paper and can be followed up by phone, mail, or email.

Process evaluation

A qualitative researcher will visit six of the 24 hospitals in the study, purposively selected using maximum variation sampling, to take account of the balance of teaching versus non-teaching hospitals, urban versus rural hospitals, and hospitals serving populations with higher or lower socio-economic status. The researcher will interview 10 staff and six patients per site to understand perceptions of value and acceptability of RESPOND in practice and make observations to assess the uptake and use of interventions and the factors that facilitate/inhibit their use. Clinical protocols, standard operating procedures, training records, meeting attendance, and audit/implementation data used by frontline staff in their work will be reviewed as part of this study.

Interviews will be conducted either in person at the workplace or remotely via telephone or Microsoft Teams, focusing on participants’ perceptions of how the RESPOND interventions worked if successful, and on contextual factors enabling or hindering implementation. All interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed using a confidentiality-assured external transcription service.

Semi-structured interviews with patients and family members (six per site, 36 total) will be undertaken after discharge, at the patient’s home or remotely via telephone or Microsoft Teams. They will focus on their experience of participation (or non-participation) in the patient involvement strand of the intervention.

All staff on the unit will also be asked to complete a NOMAD questionnaire.25 The data from the above studies will form the basis of a qualitative process evaluation based on normative process theory.

Data analysis and statistics

Power calculations

Based on the National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) data26 and information from a three-hospital pilot study, the predicted sample size during 27 months observation is 9064 laparotomies divided equally between the before and after intervention groups, excluding the 3-month induction period. Assuming 10% overall mortality pre-intervention, this would predict 906 deaths. Based on data from NELA and the ELPQUIC study,27 a 30% relative risk reduction for mortality appears achievable using systems change approaches, that is, 634. This gives a reduction in FTR from 22.2% to 15.7%. Using a significance level of 5%, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.1, and adjustments for actuarial trends in step-wedge designs, the predicted sample size gives over 90% power to detect a 20% reduction in FTR and 80% power to detect a 15% reduction. We believe this degree of ‘excess’ power is appropriate in view of the widely varying estimates of achievable benefit arising from different relevant data sources. Increasing the ICC to 0.2 does not significantly reduce power. Due to the nature of the outcome and trial design, we anticipate minimal missing data rates without notable effects on estimated power.

Statistical analysis plan

FTR and mortality: for these binary variables, a logistic regression will be used, with a dummy variable for control/active, and covariates such as linear and quadratic terms in time. This will control for secular trends, for example, improvement in outcomes over time. Any hospital-specific covariates that might affect FTR rate could be included here, such as bed numbers. Time since switching regime is another hospital-specific variable, which can be used to test for carry-over problems. A random effect would also be included for hospitals. Patient-level data need not be included in assessing the RRR of the intervention, as differing patient mix across hospitals is modeled by the random effect term.

Health economic analysis

Hospital records will be extracted to collect resource usage information covering length of stay, days in ICU, and procedures performed. Study researchers will document data on resource usage of the interventions (staff time usage; training time and costs; person hours spent in meetings). Resource use will be weighted by unit cost (BNF, PSSRU, and National reference cost, and hospital finance). EQ-5D questionnaire results will be translated into a utility score using the UK tariff. Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) will be estimated using an area under the curve approach. The health economic outcome will be presented as incremental cost per mortality reduction and costs per QALYs gained. Economic analysis will follow the same approaches as in statistical analyses. Missing values will be addressed by multiple imputation. Bootstrapping will generate incremental cost-effectiveness ratios. Cost differences between intervention and control groups will be adjusted for baseline characteristics using generalized linear modeling. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves will be produced to reflect the probability of the intervention being cost-effective at different willingness-to-pay values per QALY gained. Major assumptions will be tested in sensitivity analyses. Should the trial show the hypothesized differences in outcomes, the predeveloped model from the feasibility study will be updated using trial information. This model will be used to explore the long-term cost-effectiveness beyond the trial period and in broader settings. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to illustrate the cost-effectiveness of the intervention.

Discussion

The value of an HF approach to improving care quality and reliability has been advocated on the basis of theory, and the results of studies addressing very specific single-issue problems, such as central line infections.28 29 The usefulness of a discipline that analyzes and directly addresses systems issues that are repeatedly identified as the main factor in adverse events is intuitive, but the overall gain in the safety and effectiveness of the system from solving individual problems is small. The vision of a radically improved system of care brought about through the use of HF methods was much discussed after the initial wave of analyses, which showed the extent of safety problems in healthcare,30 31 and examples of other disciplines in which an impressive degree of reliability and safety had been achieved were frequently highlighted. Our failure to achieve this kind of transformation in healthcare has complex causes, but one of them has been lack of ambition. Large-scale studies seeking to make major changes in the outcome of entire care processes have been uncommon, and rigorous evaluations (especially randomized controlled trials) have been even rarer. The majority of such large programs have not achieved their goals, but a considerable amount has been learned from analyzing their outcomes. The design of this study was based on the lessons learned from previous trials, which aimed to address the key problems in an entire process of care, especially the EPOCH study of pre-operative and peri-operative care for emergency abdominal surgery patients. EPOCH did not deliver the goal of improvement in mortality and was criticized for employing too many separate interventions with too little resource to support staff in implementing them. We therefore designed our study around a bundle of four interventions, each addressing different parts of the escalation process. These four areas were selected on the basis of previous work with staff focus groups and a FRAM HF analysis of the escalation system for deteriorating patients.17 We recognized the difficulty and importance of achieving staff engagement from our own previous work and from other studies and developed a strategy to improve this. We have trialed our interventions in a 1-year pilot study in three hospitals, leading to a number of changes in our approach. We have taken a radical approach to minimizing the burden on staff from participation in the study, as our experience and knowledge of other studies suggested that this was the largest single barrier to engagement. In RESPOND, clinical staff—apart from the four key study leaders—will have no involvement in screening, consent, data collection, or data analysis, and there are no requirements for training meetings that take staff away from their duties, except for a single half-day session at the beginning of the intervention.

Although we have attempted to learn from previous relevant experiences and studies, our study has unavoidable limitations. The successive shocks to the English NHS from Brexit, COVID-19, and subsequent economic difficulties have had serious effects on staffing and morale, which have made engagement with service improvement even more challenging than before. Although our study design includes analysis methods designed to allow for secular trends, a persistently low level of morale and engagement could seriously affect the chances of success. Our interventions have been developed and designed appropriately, but have been tried out only in the pilot study and may still require further development. Qualitative data from the pilot gave us some indication of which interventions were best adopted, but the nature of the outcomes we are measuring, and the complex interactions between the effects of the separate strands mean that it will not be possible to confidently determine the contribution of each strand to the overall outcome. We have worked to ensure that the amount of staff training, information, and engagement is maximized given our resources, but we cannot know what the necessary ‘dose’ of the intervention is for success. Two lessons from our pilot study were around the importance of a comprehensive and carefully designed communications strategy to support staff understanding and motivation, and the critical role of ward level leadership in determining staff attitudes.

Our interventions are also limited by the limited extent of ‘buy-in’ from executive-level hospital management, who welcome interventions that may improve safety while not disrupting routine work, but are unlikely to support more radical approaches for which strong arguments could be made on the basis of existing knowledge. Ensuring appropriate needs-based access to emergency operating theaters around the clock, modifying intensive care admission policies, and shortening decision times by having senior doctors more rapidly available in emergencies would likely be effective in reducing FTR and mortality, but would require major investment or significant changes in hospital management, making multicenter trials impractical. Our inclusion of a process evaluation as well as a health economic assessment will allow further learning about the effects of context on the adoption of interventions, and about which interventions were most readily adopted and why, while analyzing both costs and quality-of-life benefits from the intervention.
