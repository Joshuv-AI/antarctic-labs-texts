STRIDE-II treatment target achievement in inflammatory bowel disease: a cross-sectional study from a tertiary IBD centre in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Noorah Al Hosani, Maitha Al Hosani, Abdulla A. Aljanahi, Mohammed N. Quraishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Data on STRIDE-II treatment target achievement in Middle Eastern inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) populations remain limited. We assessed the prevalence and predictors of deep remission at a tertiary IBD center in the United Arab Emirates.Methods:A cross-sectional analysis was conducted on 389 IBD patients (242 Crohn’s disease [CD], 147 ulcerative colitis [UC]) on stable treatment for ≥6 months. Deep remission required concurrent clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic remission. Predictors were identified using logistic regression.Results:Complete STRIDE-II assessment was available in 264 patients (67.9%). Deep remission was achieved in 164 patients (62.1% available-case; 42.2% conservative, treating incomplete assessments as failure). In the advanced therapy subgroup (n = 182), deep remission was 64.3% available-case and 43.8% conservative. Clinical remission was 78.7%, biochemical remission 72.4%, and endoscopic remission 69.2%. Rates were similar between CD and UC, and complicated CD phenotypes achieved equivalent deep remission to non-complicated disease (62.7% vs 63.9%, P = 0.85). Disease duration was the strongest overall predictor (OR 1.06 per year, 95% CI 1.02–1.10, P = 0.009). CD-specific predictors included B3 penetrating behavior (OR 5.49, P = 0.027) and male sex (OR 0.47, P = 0.039). In UC, older age and male sex were independently associated with remission. Higher advanced therapy exposure predicted lower remission odds in CD (adjusted OR 0.70, P = 0.034). No individual therapy demonstrated significant superiority in adjusted drug comparisons. Efficacy was preserved at second-line (67.4%) with decline at third-line (50.0%).Conclusion:Deep remission rates in this UAE tertiary center cohort were comparable to or numerically higher than international reports. Further validation in prospective, geographically diverse populations is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) represents a growing global health challenge, with incidence rising across all epidemiologic transition stages from emergence to compounding.[12] The Global Burden of Disease Study documented over 3 million affected individuals in 2017.[3] In the Arabian Gulf region, IBD incidence and prevalence has increased substantially over the past two decades,[456] with evidence of aggressive phenotypes and younger age at diagnosis compared to Western populations.

This rising burden has coincided with an expansion of therapeutic options, with advanced therapies now available spanning multiple mechanisms of action.[7] Clinical trials and guidelines now advocate for risk-stratified top-down therapy and treat-to-target approaches, representing a paradigm shift from conventional step-up strategies.[891011] The STRIDE-II consensus established treatment targets encompassing short-term (clinical remission), intermediate-term (biochemical remission), and long-term (endoscopic remission) goals.[12] However, real-world STRIDE-II implementation remains challenging. The multinational IBD-PODCAST study, spanning over 100 sites across 10 countries, revealed that approximately half of patients exhibited suboptimal disease control despite most receiving advanced therapies.[13] Regional cohorts from Spain, Italy, the United Kingdom, and Canada have replicated these findings, with impaired quality of life the predominant contributor to suboptimal control in over half of cases.[14151617]

The United Arab Emirates has witnessed substantial evolution in IBD clinical burden over the past two decades, with increasing case complexity, rising proportions of moderate-to-severe disease presentations, and particularly aggressive pediatric phenotypes characterized by extensive disease at diagnosis and high complication rates.[45] Despite the expanding therapeutic arsenal and defined treatment targets, substantial gaps persist in understanding real-world STRIDE-II achievement rates in Middle Eastern populations, limiting our ability to benchmark regional outcomes against international standards.[18] This study provides the first systematic STRIDE-II evaluation from a tertiary IBD center in the Gulf region. We assessed remission rates across clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic domains; compared outcomes between disease types and advanced therapy classes; and identified predictors of deep remission. Our findings provide regional benchmarks for treat-to-target strategies in an era where global data demonstrate that approximately half of patients fail to achieve optimal control despite advanced therapy use.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This cross-sectional analysis used prospectively collected routine clinical data from the IBD Center of Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City (SSMC), Abu Dhabi, UAE. Patients were eligible if they had[1]: confirmed diagnosis of CD or UC according to established criteria[2]; diagnosis at least one year prior to enrolment[3]; prospectively collected remission data at clinic visits between October 2024 and December 2025; and[4] current treatment for at least six months or no active treatment, corresponding to the STRIDE-II long-term assessment timeframe. Patients with IBD-unclassified, prior proctocolectomy, or current enrolment in interventional trials were excluded. Two populations were defined: the primary cohort comprising all eligible patients, and the advanced therapy (AT) subgroup comprising patients receiving advanced therapy for at least six months. The study was approved by the SSMC Research Ethics Committee (reference: SSMCREC-585) on 02 March 2025.

The six-month minimum therapy duration was selected to ensure patients had completed induction and early maintenance phases, consistent with the STRIDE-II treatment phase framework and the IBD-PODCAST study design, which similarly focused on patients in the long-term treatment phase.[1213] Assessment before 6 months may capture patients still responding to induction therapy or with active disease not yet optimally controlled, rather than reflecting established treatment outcomes.

Outcome definitions

Clinical remission was defined as Harvey-Bradshaw Index of 4 or less for CD and Partial Mayo Score of 1 or less for UC. Biochemical remission required CRP of 5 mg/L or less and/or faecal calprotectin below 250 micrograms per gram. These biochemical thresholds are based on STRIDE-II Recommendation 12,[12] which defines faecal calprotectin of 100–250 mg/g as an intermediate treatment target in both CD and UC. We adopted the less stringent end of this range (faecal calprotectin <250 mg/g). The STRIDE-II consensus notes that lower thresholds reflect deeper histological healing but are less universally achieved. Endoscopic remission was defined as SES-CD of 2 or less for CD or Mayo endoscopic subscore of 1 or less for UC. Deep remission required simultaneous clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic remission.

Disease phenotype classification

CD patients were categorized by Montreal classification. Complicated CD was defined as B2 structuring, B3 penetrating, or perianal disease. Non-complicated CD comprised B1 inflammatory behavior without perianal involvement. Advanced therapies were classified as anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab), anti-integrin (vedolizumab), anti-IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), anti-IL-23 (risankizumab, guselkumab), and JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, tofacitinib). Treatment line was first-line (no prior AT), second-line (one prior), or third-line and beyond.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using RStudio (version 2024.12.1).[19] Remission rates were calculated with 95% Wilson confidence intervals. Univariable logistic regression identified predictors of deep remission. Drug comparisons were conducted separately for CD and UC, adjusting for disease duration, bio-experienced status, prior surgery, treatment duration, and disease-specific factors (complicated phenotype for CD; extensive disease for UC). Infliximab served as reference. Remission rates were compared between patients on established advanced therapy (≥6 months) and those not receiving advanced therapy using logistic regression, adjusted for disease type, age, disease duration, and prior surgery. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant. Given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses and multiple comparisons tested, results from CD, UC, and drug-specific subgroups should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; subgroup findings require confirmation in larger or prospective cohorts.

RESULTS

Study population

Of 521 IBD patients identified, 389 (242 CD, 147 UC) met inclusion criteria [Supplementary Figure 1]. The AT subgroup comprised 267 patients (194 CD, 73 UC). Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Mean age was 35.3 years (SD 13.7) with 60.2% male. Mean disease duration was 8.1 years (SD 6.9), and 63% had disease duration of five years or more. Advanced therapy was used by 68.6% overall, with higher utilization in CD (80.2%) than UC (49.7%). Among CD patients, 53.7% had complicated phenotype (B2 stricturing, B3 penetrating, or perianal disease). Mean AT duration was 33.6 months, and 47.2% of the AT subgroup were AT-experienced. Only six patients were on concurrent systemic corticosteroids.

Of 389 patients in the primary cohort, clinical remission was assessable in all 389 (100%), biochemical remission in 344 (88.4%), and endoscopic remission in 279 (71.7%) patients. Incomplete biochemical assessment primarily reflected unavailable faecal calprotectin; incomplete endoscopic assessment reflected medical contraindication, patient refusal, or assessment not performed during the study window. Complete STRIDE-II assessment (all three components) was therefore available in 264 patients (67.9%). We present both available-case analysis (n = 264), and conservative sensitivity analysis treating incomplete assessments as remission failure.

Remission rates

Remission rates are presented in Table 2 and Figure 1. In the primary cohort, clinical remission was 78.7% (306/389, 95% CI 74.3–82.4%), biochemical remission 72.4% (249/344, 95% CI 67.4–76.8%), endoscopic remission 69.2% (193/279, 95% CI 63.5–74.3%), and deep remission 62.1% (164/264, 95% CI 56.1–67.8%). Deep remission rates were similar between CD at 61.0% (97/159) and UC at 63.8% (67/105). In the AT subgroup, remission rates were higher across all domains: clinical remission 84.3% (225/267, 95% CI 79.4–88.1%), biochemical remission 76.0% (184/242, 95% CI 70.3–81.0%), endoscopic remission 76.2% (144/189, 95% CI 69.6–81.7%), and deep remission 64.3% (117/182, 95% CI 57.1–70.9%). Deep remission was 63.3% (81/128) in CD and 66.7% (36/54) in UC. Notably, UC patients on AT achieved higher endoscopic remission (83.3%) compared to CD (73.3%), likely reflecting more accessible mucosal healing assessment in colonic disease. In a conservative sensitivity analysis treating all patients with incomplete assessments as not having achieved remission, deep remission was 42.2% (164/389) in the primary cohort, and 43.8% (117/267) in the AT subgroup.

Deep remission by Crohn’s disease phenotype

Among CD patients in the AT subgroup, complicated phenotype achieved 62.7% deep remission (42/67) compared to 63.9% (39/61) for non-complicated CD (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.50-1.78, P = 0.85). By Montreal behavior, B3 penetrating achieved the highest rate at 87.5% (14/16), followed by B1 inflammatory at 63.3% (50/79), and B2 stricturing at 51.5% (17/33). The high B3 remission rate likely reflects successful fistula healing with contemporary biologic therapy, while the lower B2 rate may reflect limited reversibility of fibrotic strictures. Patients with perianal disease achieved 60.0% (24/40) compared to 64.8% (57/88) without perianal involvement [Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figure 2]. Among UC patients in the AT subgroup, disease extent did not significantly predict deep remission. Neither left-sided colitis (E2; OR 1.48, P = 0.412), pancolitis (E3; OR 0.78, P = 0.555), nor extensive disease as a composite (OR 1.20, P = 0.792) were associated with outcomes, suggesting that disease extent may be less important than other factors in patients already selected for advanced therapy.

Advanced therapy and treatment line analysis

Drug comparisons were conducted separately for CD and UC [Supplementary Table 2]. These exploratory analyses are limited by small subgroup sizes and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Infliximab achieved 70.6% (24/34), with numerically similar rates for adalimumab (72.2%), vedolizumab (83.3%), risankizumab (60.6%), ustekinumab (56%), and upadacitinib (50%). In UC, vedolizumab achieved 87.5% deep remission (14/16) and showed a trend toward superiority over infliximab at 58.8% (adjusted OR 6.39, 95% CI 1.02–58.83, P = 0.064). No other drugs differed significantly from infliximab. Pairwise comparisons are in Supplementary Table 2.

Predictors of deep remission

Univariable predictors are presented in Figure 2. In the primary cohort (n = 264), disease duration was the strongest predictor (OR 1.06 per year, 95% CI 1.02–1.10, P = 0.009), with age also significant (OR 1.02 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.04, P = 0.040). Prior surgery showed a trend toward improved outcomes (OR 2.17, 95% CI 0.98–5.31, P = 0.07). In CD (n = 159), B3 penetrating behavior was the strongest positive predictor (OR 5.49, 95% CI 1.47–35.66, P = 0.027), while male sex was associated with lower odds (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.23–0.95, P = 0.039). Multiple prior AT exposure showed a trend toward worse outcomes (OR 0.44, P = 0.057). Complicated phenotype was not significant (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.50–1.78, P = 0.85). In UC (n = 105), age (OR 1.06 per year, 95% CI 1.02–1.11, P = 0.003), disease duration (OR 1.12 per year, 95% CI 1.04–1.23, P = 0.010), and disease duration of 5 years or more (OR 2.43, 95% CI 1.08–5.59, P = 0.033) were significant predictors. Male sex showed a trend toward improved outcomes in UC (OR 2.17, P = 0.061), contrasting with CD.

Multivariable logistic regression [Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Figure 4] identified disease duration as a significant independent predictor in the overall cohort (adjusted OR 1.05 per year, 95% CI 1.01–1.11, P = 0.038). In the CD subgroup, higher total advanced therapy exposure (per additional prior therapy) was associated with lower odds of deep remission (adjusted OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.50–0.96, P = 0.034). In UC, age (adjusted OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.11, P = 0.011) and male sex (adjusted OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.08–6.90, P = 0.036) were independently associated with remission.

Remission rates by advanced therapy status

To describe differences in remission rates by advanced therapy status, we compared patients on established advanced therapy (n = 267) and those not receiving advanced therapy (n = 122). Patients on advanced therapy demonstrated significantly higher remission rates across clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic outcomes [Supplementary Figure 3]. Clinical remission was achieved in 84.3% (225/267) of patients on advanced therapy compared with 66.4% (81/122) of those not on advanced therapy (adjusted OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.47–4.52; P < 0.001). Similarly, biochemical remission rates were higher in the advanced therapy group (76.0% vs 63.7%; adjusted OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.13–3.45; P = 0.02), as were endoscopic remission rates (76.2% vs 54.4%; adjusted OR 3.13, 95% CI 1.74–5.74; P < 0.001). Deep remission showed a numerical advantage for advanced therapy (64.3% vs 57.3%) that did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for disease type, age, disease duration, and prior surgery (adjusted OR 1.47, 95% CI 0.82–2.66; P = 0.20). The magnitude of difference was most pronounced for endoscopic remission, with patients on advanced therapy demonstrating three-fold higher odds of achieving mucosal healing compared with those managed without advanced therapy.

DISCUSSION

This study provides the first systematic evaluation of STRIDE-II treatment target achievement from a tertiary center in the Gulf region. We assessed deep remission rates in patients on established advanced therapy, examined outcomes across disease phenotypes and therapeutic classes, and identified predictors of treatment success in this Middle Eastern cohort.

Our principal finding was that approximately two-thirds of patients on established advanced therapy achieved deep remission, a rate comparable to or numerically higher than contemporary international reports. The IBD-PODCAST study reported suboptimal disease control in over half of patients across predominantly European sites despite advanced therapy use in 77% of CD and 65% of UC cases.[13] Other Western cohorts have validated these findings: Spain (53.1% CD, 41.5% UC with suboptimal control),[14] Italy (53.4% CD, 49.0% UC),[15] the United Kingdom (52.4% CD, 45.3% UC),[16] and Canada.[17] In our cohort, 79% of the total IBD population was receiving advanced therapy for ≥6 months, comparable to IBD-PODCAST rates. Across all these cohorts, impaired quality of life (SIBDQ <50) emerged as the predominant contributor to suboptimal disease control in 56–72% of cases, followed by failure to achieve endoscopic remission and presence of extraintestinal manifestations. Our cohort’s achievement of deep remission in approximately two-thirds of patients therefore represents performance comparable to or numerically higher than global real-world benchmarks.

Direct comparison with IBD-PODCAST, which employed SIBDQ-based definitions of suboptimal control, is limited, as this represents a fundamentally different construct from STRIDE-II deep remission based on integrated clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic criteria. Several factors may explain our cohort’s favorable outcomes. First, high advanced therapy utilization reflects contemporary treatment paradigms emphasizing early intervention and risk-stratified therapy selection. The landmark PROFILE trial demonstrated that top-down treatment with combination biologic and immunomodulator therapy achieved substantially higher rates of symptom control, inflammatory marker normalization, and endoscopic remission compared with conventional step-up approaches, while markedly reducing the need for urgent surgery.[11] These paradigm-shifting findings underscore that contemporary treatment strategies, including judicious use of early intensive therapy and, in select cases, upfront surgical intervention, can fundamentally alter disease trajectories. Second, our single-center design ensures uniform assessment protocols, consistent treatment algorithms, and cohesive multidisciplinary care delivery. The IBD-PODCAST investigators noted that endoscopy was performed in fewer than expected patients, faecal calprotectin analyses in less than half, and imaging in fewer than 15% of cases across participating sites,[20] suggesting incomplete STRIDE-II implementation in routine practice. Third, these findings demonstrate that dedicated IBD services with comprehensive advanced therapy access can achieve outcomes comparable to Western benchmarks, even in populations with aggressive disease phenotypes.[2122] This contradicts assumptions that Middle Eastern populations might experience inferior outcomes due to genetic, environmental, or healthcare system factors. The higher remission rates in patients on advanced therapy likely reflect both therapeutic efficacy and more intensive monitoring protocols, reinforcing the value of structured assessment in achieving STRIDE-II targets.

The equivalent remission rates between complicated and non-complicated CD phenotypes challenge traditional assumptions about prognostic stratification by disease behavior.[23] However, patients with complicated phenotypes in our cohort likely received earlier and more intensive treatment, reflecting appropriate risk stratification that may mitigate baseline phenotypic disadvantages. The behavior-specific analysis revealed important heterogeneity warranting nuanced interpretation. The high remission observed in penetrating disease likely reflects confounding by surgical resection, as patients with penetrating complications frequently undergo definitive surgery prior to reassessment.[24] Our penetrating disease subgroup’s outcomes must therefore be interpreted in this surgical context. Conversely, the lower remission in stricturing disease aligns with emerging mechanistic understanding of intestinal fibrosis which remains one of the largest unmet needs in IBD, affecting approximately 35% of CD patients within 10 years of diagnosis.[25] These findings support treatment approaches individualized by specific disease behavior rather than composite phenotype classifications.

In CD, no agent demonstrated statistical superiority, with wide confidence intervals reflecting sample size limitations. These findings are hypothesis-generating and cannot exclude either substantial efficacy differences or therapeutic equivalence. Contemporary head-to-head trials comparing newer biologics and network meta-analyses provide important context, establishing efficacy benchmarks for IL-23 inhibitors in bio-experienced patients while demonstrating comparable performance between anti-TNF and anti-IL-12/23 agents in bio-naïve populations.[26272829] In UC, our observation of vedolizumab’s trend toward superiority aligns with the VARSITY trial, the EVOLVE real-world study demonstrating superior treatment persistence and fewer disease exacerbations with vedolizumab versus anti-TNF in bio-naïve UC patients, and recent network meta-analyses demonstrating vedolizumab among the most effective and best-tolerated advanced therapies, particularly for biologic-naïve patients.[303132333435363738] Importantly, our study was not designed or powered for definitive drug comparisons, and the observational design introduces confounding by indication. Drug selection in CD likely reflected disease behavior considerations, with anti-TNF agents preferentially used for penetrating phenotypes based on superior fistula closure rates, while vedolizumab was reserved for inflammatory phenotypes or patients with contraindications to anti-TNF therapy. These analyses therefore remain hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger regional cohorts or registry data.

The preservation of efficacy at second-line with decline at third-line observed in our cohort aligns with contemporary evidence demonstrating diminishing returns with sequential therapy failures. Large real-world studies support mechanistic switching after first-line failure rather than cycling within the same drug class, with certain agents (notably vedolizumab in CD) showing reduced effectiveness when used later in treatment sequences.[3435] Predictor profiles differed between CD and UC in our cohort, reflecting disease-specific pathophysiology. In CD, the association of penetrating behavior with higher remission likely reflects surgical confounding as discussed above. The association of male sex with lower remission in CD lacks clear mechanistic explanation and requires replication. In UC, older age and longer disease duration predicted higher remission, a counterintuitive finding potentially reflecting survivor bias, as patients with aggressive disease may have undergone colectomy earlier, leaving a cohort enriched for treatment-responsive phenotypes. The heterogeneity in predictors across studies underscores the need for larger regional cohorts to validate these patterns.

Our study has several important limitations. First, this is a single-center cross-sectional analysis from a tertiary IBD referral center, which may not represent broader IBD populations or primary care settings. Second, selection bias is introduced by the inclusion criteria requiring at least six months of stable therapy, which enriches our cohort for patients with established, well-controlled disease. Third, we employed an available-case analysis including only 264 of 389 patients with complete STRIDE-II assessment; the conservative sensitivity analysis yielded 42.2% deep remission (compared to 62.1%). Fourth, biochemical thresholds (faecal calprotectin <250 mg/g) represent an intermediate target; stricter thresholds would yield lower proportions. Fifth, subgroup analyses are exploratory without formal multiplicity adjustment. Sixth, the AT vs non-AT comparison is subject to confounding by indication. Finally, categorical clinical remission assessments were available rather than Harvey-Bradshaw Index or Partial Mayo Score continuous scores. The absence of histological remission assessment represents a limitation given emerging evidence for “disease clearance” (simultaneous clinical, endoscopic, and histologic remission) as an optimal treatment target and is being systematically explored in the VERDICT study.[36373839] Histological data were not systematically recorded in our cohort as pathology reporting lacked consistency, and we opted to exclude incomplete data to avoid bias. Future iterations of STRIDE may incorporate histological endpoints as standardized assessment tools and reporting practices become established. The absence of comprehensive patient-reported outcome (PRO) data beyond clinical remission assessment represents another limitation. Given that impaired quality of life was the predominant driver of suboptimal disease control in IBD-PODCAST cohorts, systematic PRO assessment using validated instruments would enhance characterization of treatment success from patient perspectives.[13141516] Objective remission measures may not fully capture patients’ disease burden and functional status. Additionally, the lack of transmural healing assessment via intestinal ultrasound or magnetic resonance enterography represents a limitation, as emerging evidence suggests discordance between endoscopic and transmural remission in a subset of patients.[40]

In conclusion, in this single-center tertiary IBD cohort, 62.1% of patients with complete STRIDE-II assessment achieved deep remission, comparable to or numerically higher than international reports. However, a conservative sensitivity analysis yielded a more conservative 42.2% rate. These findings are from a single tertiary center with exploratory subgroup analyses; prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate STRIDE-II achievement across diverse IBD populations
