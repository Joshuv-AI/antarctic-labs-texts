Oral feeding practices in medically complex infants receiving prolonged high‐flow nasal cannula support: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Emily Cox, Jasneek Chawla, Madison Moore, Sandra Schilling, Miriam Cameron, Sally Clarke, Chelsea Johnstone, Jeanne Marshall
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimTo characterise the feeding profile and care pathway for infants receiving prolonged high‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) respiratory support for management of a chronic condition at one facility from January to December 2021.MethodsData regarding medical history, HFNC admission details (reason for HFNC, HFNC duration, flow rate), feeding outcomes and speech pathology care were collected from electronic records of HFNC‐dependent infants (requiring HFNC ≥2–3 L/kg for ≥5 consecutive days). Infants with acute respiratory conditions (e.g. bronchiolitis) were excluded.ResultsThis study included 24 participants (median corrected age at admission 5.3 weeks, range −6 to 18.6). Of these, 15 (60%) had a condition/s that affected more than one body system (e.g. congenital diaphragmatic hernia), requiring the care of multiple specialities. Median length of HFNC use was 37.5 days (range 11–188). Twenty (83.3%) infants were referred for speech pathology (SLP) input while on HFNC support. For those referred, frequency of SLP input was variable (0–3 sessions/week), and HFNC support requirements were the most common barrier to SLP intervention (n = 9, 45%). Twelve (54.5%) infants demonstrated improvement in their primary feeding method by discharge; however, only two (9.1%) infants were discharged on full oral feeds.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates variability in oral feeding management in infants with prolonged HFNC‐dependence at our centre. Respiratory support with HFNC was identified as a barrier to progressing oral feeding. Further research is required to determine if oral feeding can be safely undertaken in this cohort. This is imperative to ensure that long‐term feeding outcomes are not negatively impacted by current practice.

High‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy is a form of non‐invasive ventilation that works by providing a heated and humidified mixture of air and oxygen at a flow that is greater than the patient's inspiratory flow allowing for improved oxygen delivery and prevention of small airway collapse. 1 The past decade has seen rapidly increasing use of this intervention. In infants, HFNC is primarily used in the management of acute respiratory conditions such as bronchiolitis. 2 However, HFNC can also be utilised in infants who require prolonged respiratory support early in life, to support management of upper airway obstruction or chronic cardiorespiratory conditions, such as preterm chronic neonatal lung disease or complex cardiac abnormalities. 3

HFNC use increases pharyngeal pressure and it is hypothesised that this constant pharyngeal airflow may have a sensory and/or mechanical impact on the structures that protect the airway during the swallow. 4 , 5 As a result of this, many health professionals express concerns about the risk of aspiration‐related events when orally feeding infants managed with HFNC. This concern has also been raised with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, an alternative method of respiratory support. 4

Most research into the safety of oral feeding on HFNC to date has focused on children with acute illness (e.g. acute bronchiolitis), with studies describing oral feeding as well‐tolerated. 6 In most cases, these infants are established oral feeders, who require only a short period of time on HFNC, while recovering from their acute respiratory distress. Therefore, they are often able to maintain enteral or oral feeds and hydration during this time. 6 Adverse events such as respiratory distress or aspiration are rarely reported in this group of infants managed acutely with HFNC and any feeding difficulties are generally temporary, associated with the acute illness itself, 7 , 8 , 9 and do not typically lead to any long‐term feeding consequences.

For infants with complex medical conditions who are managed with prolonged high‐flow respiratory support, there is minimal practical information to guide clinical decision‐making around oral feeding commencement or maintenance. This has resulted in discrepancies in practice across different facilities and individual practitioners. 10 , 11 Infants in this group include, for example, those with severe chronic neonatal lung disease, pulmonary hypoplasia secondary to congenital lung abnormalities, upper airway obstruction and cardiac abnormalities. In these infants, HFNC is often initiated shortly after birth and oral feeds are frequently avoided due to medical complexity, but also due to potential safety concerns related to feeding on HFNC support. While this practice is cautious and ensures safety, there is concern that, in some cases, delayed opportunities for oral feeding may contribute to the development of chronic nasogastric or gastrostomy tube dependency and feeding disorders. Additional research in this area is required to evaluate the risks versus benefit of oral feeding in this population. Clinically, this is an essential area of study to guide practice and to promote developmentally appropriate oral feeding experiences as early as possible in this cohort of infants. This study aims to explore this gap in the literature and describes the feeding management and care pathway for a cohort of medically complex infants managed with prolonged HFNC at our tertiary centre over a 12‐month period. The findings will help to identify how feeding practices can be improved for these infants and inform areas for future research development.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study regarding feeding practices in infants on prolonged HFNC respiratory support at Children's Health Queensland Hospital and Health Service.

Study setting

The Queensland Children's Hospital and Health Service is a 359‐bed specialist public children's hospital, located in [Brisbane, Australia] that offers care for children from [across Queensland]. It is not a birthing facility, and does not have a neonatal intensive care unit, but is co‐located with several facilities that provide this care. In this setting, speech pathologists are allied health professionals that provide inpatient and outpatient services for children with feeding and swallowing needs.

Study specific definitions

Prolonged HFNC dependency was defined as the infant requiring HFNC ≥2–3 L/kg for ≥5 consecutive days. Given the lack of precedent in the literature for this cohort of children, this cut‐off was defined arbitrarily based on our clinical experience with children with acute and chronic HFNC needs. The period of HFNC dependence was determined as starting from the initiation of HFNC until the patient no longer required the therapy for 4 consecutive days. In cases where the patient was on HFNC for multiple periods of time (i.e. where therapy was initiated, discontinued for >4 days and then reinitiated), only the longest period of HFNC dependence was selected for review.

Oral feeding during the HFNC episode was defined as the patient having any oral feeds during the period of HFNC dependence where the volume consumed sufficed to replace a proportion of their enteral feed. This included oral feeding during any HFNC weaning attempts. For example, if the child fed orally only when the HFNC was turned off during a HFNC weaning attempt, this was recorded as the child feeding orally during the HFNC‐dependent period. The details of this type of accommodation during oral feeding were recorded as feeding modifications, such as feeding ‘only on low‐flow nasal cannula (LFNC)’.

In some cases, children were not having oral feeds as such, but were receiving active intervention to support oral stimulation, including via the use of drop tastes or very small oral volumes (e.g. 5–10 mL). These did not add to the overall oral intake volume for the child and were recorded as ‘therapeutic oral trials’.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Infants were included if they were <6 months old at the commencement of HFNC use and discharged from the paediatric intensive care unit to the ward dependent and unable to wean from HFNC support. As this study aimed to capture infants with chronic HFNC needs, otherwise healthy infants with short‐term HFNC needs that precipitated their need for HFNC were excluded (e.g. secondary to an acute resolving infection such as bronchiolitis or pneumonia).

Data collection

A retrospective chart review was undertaken by one investigator (MM) for all infants who met the inclusion criteria during the 2021 calendar year, obtaining demographic information, birth and medical history details and information regarding feeding and respiratory support (Table 1). Information was sourced from each patient's integrated electronic medical record which also included previous discharge or transfer summaries that enabled medical history details to be extracted. For each infant, only the encounter that included the longest period of HFNC dependence was reviewed.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted on all results using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28 0.1.0).

Continuous variables were initially assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All continuous variables were not normally distributed and therefore are presented as median and range. No further statistical analyses were conducted due to small sample size.

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was sought and granted by the Children's Health Queensland Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/21/QCHQ/81552). Approval was also gained to obtain the health information used in this study under the Public Health Act 2005 (PHA81552).

Results

Study population

A total of 24 infants (15 male, 9 female) were included. Demographics of the study population are presented in Table 2, and specific information regarding medical history is presented in Table S1, Supporting Information. There was a mortality of 8.3% (n = 2). Fourteen (58.3%) infants were full term, as defined ≥37 weeks gestation, with the remainder being infants born preterm. Median age of admission was 9.2 weeks as many infants had transferred to the hospital from other facilities, such as a co‐located birthing facility (n = 14; 58%). For most infants, the admission captured was their first admission to this facility (n = 22; 92%). Fifteen (60%) of the 24 included infants were considered to be medically complex patients, with more than one body system affected. All infants required multiple medical specialist involvement (100%).

Speech pathology involvement and outcomes

Speech pathology involvement and outcomes are summarised in Table 5. All but four of the infants received a SLP referral for feeding or pre‐feeding intervention during their HFNC episode (n = 20, 83.3%). Of those referred, 90% (n = 18) received active interventions (defined as the SLP providing direct therapeutic input such as drop tastes or offering oral feeds) and the remaining 10% received non‐active interventions (e.g. chart reviews, monitoring only). However, active SLP sessions only accounted for a median of 59% of total SLP occasions of service received by each referred infant. The most common barrier to active SLP involvement was that the child was deemed unsuitable for oral feeds by their medical team secondary to their HFNC requirement (n = 9, 45%).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study provides an insight into the management of oral feeding in medically complex infants receiving prolonged HFNC therapy in our tertiary centre. Variability in care was observed, suggesting that not every patient received the same opportunities for pre‐feeding treatment or oral feeding. In many of these cases, oral feeding opportunities were withheld due to HFNC use, and most children were discharged on full or partial tube feeds. Findings are consistent with previous research from other facilities, which have also described service‐level inconsistencies in the approach to oral feeding in infants on HFNC support. 10 This is likely due to the lack of evidence around the safety of oral feeding in infants on HFNC, which continues to be a topic that is heavily debated internationally. 11 For this cohort of infants, who require more than just a short period of acute HFNC therapy, there are even fewer studies to guide feeding practices. Some studies have demonstrated that the incidence of adverse events, such as aspiration or feeding intolerance in infants managed with HFNC, are no different compared to those treated with CPAP long term. 12 , 13 However, other studies 5 advise caution with orally feeding infants managed with either HFNC or CPAP due to concern around altered swallowing physiology. More research is warranted to investigate the true safety of oral feeding in HFNC dependent infants, particularly using instrumental swallow assessments. We identify this as an essential area for future research to avoid negative impact on the establishment of feeding practices in this growing population of children.

In our study, safety concerns regarding oral feeding for infants while they were being managed with HFNC frequently led to modifications to either their respiratory or feeding support. The most common modification identified in this study was that HFNC was turned down to low‐flow oxygen while the infant was receiving oral feeds (n = 14, 87.5%). Physiological stability is a cornerstone of feeding safety, efficiency and effectiveness in infant care. 14 It is hypothesised that removing respiratory support from a vulnerable infant during feeding may pose more risk than feeding on HFNC itself. Leibel et al. 13 conducted a study on preterm infants that included reducing the HFNC to low‐flow oxygen during oral feeding, and they identified no adverse respiratory or oral feeding events in their study. While this is encouraging, this was a pilot study conducted in preterm infants and further investigation is warranted to determine the safety of oral feeding in infants in the cohort described in this study. Studies that evaluate larger numbers of infants are also required to determine any potential differences between infants with different underlying conditions. For example, it may be that maintaining HFNC support during feeding renders an infant with upper airway obstruction more stable by preventing upper airway collapse during a feed, while the same may not be true for an infant with a primary cardiac or lower airway respiratory pathology.

Speech pathology was frequently involved in the oral feeding management of this cohort of infants with 87.5% (n = 20) of the included infants receiving a referral for feeding or pre‐feeding assessment during their HFNC episode. However, only a median of 59% of the total SLP sessions for each infant were defined as active sessions, where intervention was directly applied. In addition, despite referral, some children received zero active sessions/week, and others received up to three sessions/week, highlighting some inconsistencies in service frequency. The major barrier to consistent and/or active SLP care appeared to be the service position on oral feeding for infants on HFNC, with many infants not able to commence/continue oral feeding due to HFNC use. Agreement on practice and consistent institutional policies regarding oral feeding on HFNC are required to help support better care in this area.

The major limitation of this study is its retrospective study design. Despite extensive chart review, this study relied on the accuracy of prior medical records for its data, which may have contributed to the possibility of information bias or incomplete data. This study also had a small population that came from a single centre which could limit the generalisability of our results to other facilities and to different countries. The small sample also limited our ability to conduct comprehensive statistical analyses. Finally, it is well understood that medical complexity itself may predispose children to feeding difficulties in the short‐ and long term, and that early intervention is recommended to support their feeding development. 15 , 16 Therefore, there may have been other risk factors that contributed to oral feeding outcomes for this cohort, other than their experience of HFNC dependency.

Conclusions

This study highlights the variability in the approach to oral feeding in medically complex infants dependent on prolonged HFNC support within our centre. The use of HFNC respiratory support was identified as a barrier to commencing oral feeding and the majority of infants did not achieve full oral feeds by the time of discharge. Further research into oral feeding safety while on HFNC is required to assist in improving oral experiences for infants to ensure developmental progress is not hindered by over‐cautious feeding practices. This work would inform clinical guidelines and facilitate consistency in practice internationally.
