Transdiaphragmatic extension of a thymoma invading the peritoneum and the spleen
Authors: Kenza Horache, Manal Jidal, Ola Messaoud, Youssef Omor, Rachida Latib, Sanae Amalik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThymoma, a rare tumor originating from thymic epithelial cells in the anterior mediastinum, presents various diagnostic and clinical challenges, particularly when exhibiting unusual invasive behaviors. We report a case involving a 66-year-old woman with progressive dyspnea and a dry cough, where a CT scan revealed a large, lobulated mass in the anterior mediastinum that had extended through the diaphragm into the abdominal cavity, infiltrating the pleura, spleen, and adjacent structures. Histopathological examination confirmed a type B2 thymoma, classified as T3N2M0 stage IV B. The case underscores the importance of early and accurate diagnosis, highlighting the need for a multidisciplinary approach involving radiologists, oncologists, and thoracic surgeons to manage such advanced thymoma cases. The unusual transdiaphragmatic extension into the peritoneum and spleen emphasizes the necessity of considering extensive local invasion in the staging and treatment planning of thymomas, which often necessitates a combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy before potential surgical intervention.Keywords: Thymoma, Anterior mediastinum, Peritoneal invasion, CT scan, Masaoka-Koga staging

Introduction

Thymomas are rare tumors arising from thymic epithelial cells in the anterior mediastinum, characterized by slow growth and variable clinical presentations. These neoplasms often present diagnostic challenges due to their diverse histological subtypes and potential for invasive behavior into adjacent structures. Imaging, particularly CT and MRI, plays a crucial role in the early detection and characterization of thymomas, as well as in assessing local invasion. In this report, we describe an exceptional case of a 66-year-old woman with a thymoma demonstrating transdiaphragmatic extension into the peritoneum and spleen, a rare manifestation that poses significant clinical implications. This case underscores the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration among radiologists, oncologists, and thoracic surgeons for optimal management, with the goal of achieving timely diagnosis, effective treatment strategies, and improved patient outcomes.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old woman was referred to our radiology department, complaining of progressive dyspnea over the past 6 months, accompanied by a dry cough. She reported no hemoptysis, anorexia, or recent weight loss.

Upon examination, her vital signs were stable, and chest auscultation revealed diminished breath sounds on the left side. The cardiovascular system examination was unremarkable, and routine blood tests were within normal limits.

The patient underwent a computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, which revealed a large, multi-lobulated soft-tissue mass in the anterior mediastinum and left thorax. After contrast injection, the mass showed heterogeneous enhancement with areas of low attenuation suggestive of necrosis and multiple scattered calcifications within the tumor (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The mass was contiguous with the pericardium and abutted adjacent vascular structures. Additionally, multiple nodular pleural masses and infiltration of surrounding fat tissue were observed (Fig. 1). The mass extended into the left hemi-diaphragm, reaching the spleen, with obliteration of the fat planes. It also extended into the left neural foramen at T5 (Fig. 2). Enlargement of mediastinal lymph nodes was also noted (not shown).

CT-guided core-needle biopsy revealed a type B2 thymoma (Fig. 3), and the tumor was diagnosed as a T3N2M0 stage IV B thymoma. Initial treatment involved neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy aimed at increasing the tumor's resectability. However, follow-up imaging revealed that the tumor remained unresectable due to extensive invasion into the mediastinum and major vascular structures. As a result, the treatment was adjusted to include concurrent chemotherapy and radiation therapy. The current chemotherapy regimen consists of cisplatin combined with anthracycline-containing regimens. The patient is still undergoing treatment, with recent assessments indicating disease stability.

Discussion

Thymic epithelial tumors encompass thymoma, thymic carcinoma, and thymic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), with thymoma representing the most prevalent type, accounting for 66.3% of all thymic tumors [1].

Thymoma is a rare, slow-growing tumor that originates from thymic epithelial cells in the anterior mediastinum, although its exact etiology remains unknown [[2], [3], [4]]. It constitutes the most common tumor of the anterior mediastinum, despite its low incidence rate of 0.15 cases per 100,000 population [1,3].

Thymic epithelial tumors encompass thymoma, thymic carcinoma, and thymic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), with thymoma being the most prevalent type, accounting for 66.3% of all thymic tumors [1]. Thymoma is a rare, slow-growing tumor that originates from thymic epithelial cells in the anterior mediastinum. Despite its low incidence of 0.15 cases per 100,000 population, thymoma is the most common tumor of the anterior mediastinum [1,3]. The exact etiology remains unknown, and thymomas can develop at any age, though their highest incidence occurs between 50 and 60 years, without significant gender predilection [2].

Approximately 50% of thymomas are incidentally detected during routine chest imaging in asymptomatic patients [2,4]. Symptoms typically arise from compression or invasion of adjacent anatomical structures, leading to presentations like dysphagia, diaphragmatic paralysis, or superior vena cava syndrome [2]. About one-third of patients may experience chest pain, dyspnea, or cough. Additionally, thymoma is associated with paraneoplastic syndromes, such as myasthenia gravis, observed in 30% to 50% of cases, along with hypogammaglobulinemia and pure red cell aplasia [2].

The World Health Organization classifies thymomas into 5 subtypes (A, AB, B1, B2, B3), based on the morphology of tumor cells and the degree of lymphocyte infiltration (Table 1) [1,2,4]. It is important to note that multiple subtypes may coexist within the same tumor, although this observation holds no clinical significance [2]. Invasive behavior is more common in type B subtypes, particularly B2 and B3 [4].

Various staging systems have been proposed for thymomas, with the most widely recognized being the Masaoka-Koga system, which provides a comprehensive assessment of disease extent and prognosis. This system categorizes thymomas into stages I through IV based on the degree of local invasion, presence of distant metastases, and involvement of adjacent structures (Table 2) [5].

Computed tomography (CT) is the modality of choice for the assessment of thymomas. Typically, thymomas present as round, well-defined masses in the anterior mediastinum, ranging from 1 to 10 cm in diameter. Calcifications may be observed, either confined to the capsule or scattered throughout the tumor. In approximately one-third of cases, thymomas exhibit invasive characteristics, infiltrating the capsule and adjacent structures such as the pleura, lungs, great vessels, pericardium, chest wall, or diaphragm. Pleural dissemination, or "drop" metastases, can occur, presenting as one or more pleural nodules, typically ipsilateral to the primary tumor [2]. Necrosis and hemorrhage may occur [6], and elevated hemidiaphragm with phrenic nerve involvement can also be noted [7].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers complementary information to CT, especially in evaluating soft tissue involvement, such as vascular invasion or diaphragm involvement. Thymomas typically appear with intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and increased signal intensity on T2-weighted images [6].

According to Han et al., who analyzed the radiographic characteristics of 159 patients with thymomas, tumors classified as B2 and B3 tend to be larger, exhibit irregular contours, infiltrate surrounding fat, and are often associated with pleural effusion. This highlights the more aggressive behavior of these subtypes, consistent with the findings in our patient [7].

While thymomas typically originate in the anterosuperior mediastinum, they can also develop in other mediastinal compartments, as well as in the neck, thyroid, pulmonary hilum, lung parenchyma, or even the pleura. However, extra thoracic invasion and distant metastases are exceedingly rare, with limited documentation [8]. The transdiaphragmatic extension into the peritoneum and spleen, as observed in our patient, is particularly uncommon. To our knowledge, no other documented cases of such extensive local invasion have been reported, highlighting the exceptional nature and complexity of this case.

The 10-year overall survival rates for thymomas vary by stage, with figures of 84% for stage I, 83% for stage II, 70% for stage III, 42% for stage IVA, and 53% for stage IVB [2].

The primary management of thymomas focuses on surgical resection, which remains the cornerstone of treatment. Complete resection is the most significant predictor of a favorable outcome [2]. Postoperative radiotherapy is recommended for incompletely resected thymomas [3].

In cases of locally advanced thymomas, a combination of induction chemotherapy followed by surgical evaluation is advised. Postoperative radiotherapy may be considered based on the surgical findings [2,8]. For patients presenting with solitary or ipsilateral pleural metastases, induction chemotherapy or surgery may be appropriate [2]. In instances where the disease is deemed unresectable, radiotherapy, with or without chemotherapy, is generally recommended [2].

In conclusion, this case exemplifies an unusual instance of extra thoracic invasion of thymoma, illustrating both the rarity and complexity of such presentations. The role of imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), is crucial for the accurate assessment of thymomas. A multidisciplinary approach involving radiologists, oncologists, and thoracic surgeons is essential for managing thymoma effectively and improving patient outcomes.
