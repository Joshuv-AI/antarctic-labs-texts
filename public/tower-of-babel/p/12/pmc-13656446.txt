Potential cardiovascular benefits of sustained improvement in depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults: a longitudinal hypothetical intervention study
Authors: Zhiwen Deng, Yuanyuan Chen, Haijing Hong, Xu Ma, Xingbo Gu, Ye Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRandomized trials have not clearly characterized the long-term cardiovascular benefit and preventable disease burden that may correspond to sustained improvement in depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older adults. Under hypothetical intervention scenarios, we quantified the long-term cardiovascular risk differences relative to the natural course, aiming to reflect the potential benefit of non-specific strategies for sustained depressive symptom improvement.MethodsThis longitudinal hypothetical intervention study was conducted in two population-based ageing cohorts, the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including adults aged 50 years and older who were free of CVD at baseline. Depressive symptoms were modeled as binary depressive status or continuous CES-D score. Using the parametric g-formula, we estimated 9–10-year cumulative CVD risk under sustained depressive symptom-improvement scenarios versus the natural course, and quantified the absolute risk difference in CVD events and the number of potentially preventable events at the population level. Robustness was assessed through a series of sensitivity analyses, including alternative missing-data approaches, model specifications, CES-D cut-offs, within-wave time-ordering assumptions, lagged exposure definitions, exclusion of early CVD events, intervention probabilities, exclusion of potentially mediating behavioral covariates, inverse probability-of-censoring weighting treating death as a competing event, quantitative bias analyses for outcome and exposure misclassification, negative-control exposure analysis, and E-value analysis.ResultsAcross both cohorts, hypothetical sustained improvement in depressive symptoms was associated with lower cumulative CVD risk than the natural course. Under the intensive scenario, the 9-year risk difference (RD) in CHARLS was − 3.8% (95% CI, − 4.6% to − 2.9%), with a risk ratio (RR) of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.81 to 0.88) and a potential impact fraction (PIF) of 15.40%. In ELSA, the corresponding 10-year RD was − 1.7% (95% CI, − 2.2% to − 1.1%), with an RR of 0.91 (95% CI, 0.88 to 0.94) and a PIF of 8.89%. Feasible scenarios yielded smaller but directionally consistent reductions. Analyses using continuous CES-D score showed a graded pattern, with larger symptom reductions associated with progressively lower cumulative CVD risk. Findings were broadly consistent across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsHypothetical sustained improvement in depressive symptoms was associated with lower cumulative CVD risk over 9–10 years in two population-based ageing cohorts. These findings provide quantitative reference estimates for the potential long-term cardiovascular benefit and preventable disease burden that may correspond to non-specific strategies for sustained depressive symptom improvement. Importantly, these estimates should be interpreted as modeled risk contrasts under hypothetical symptom-change scenarios, rather than as effect estimates for any specific pharmacological treatment, psychotherapy, social support, or other clinical or public health intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28564-x.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and continues to impose a substantial clinical and socioeconomic burden, particularly among middle-aged and older adults [1–4]. Epidemiological studies have established that depression is associated with an increased risk of incident CVD across diverse populations [5–9]. These associations have also been reported in middle-aged and older adults across both Western and Chinese populations [9–12]. Beyond this static characterization, recent longitudinal studies suggest that depressive symptoms fluctuate over time and may represent a dynamic, cumulative exposure across the life course rather than a fixed baseline state [9, 13–15]. Against this background, a key unresolved issue is the magnitude of potential long-term cardiovascular benefit that might be expected if depressive symptoms were durably improved over time.

To examine whether depression continues to exert cardiovascular risk when depressive symptoms are viewed as a time-varying exposure, prior studies have predominantly relied on longitudinal assessments of depressive symptoms [13, 15–19]. Using repeated measurements collected over follow-up, this body of work has characterized how depressive symptoms evolve over time and has compared cardiovascular risk across different symptom trajectories or cumulative exposure patterns [13, 20]. Such analyses have highlighted heterogeneity in cardiovascular risk across individuals experiencing persistently high, worsening, or fluctuating depressive symptoms, thereby extending earlier static descriptions to a dynamic framework [15, 17]. However, these longitudinal comparisons remain limited to naturally occurring symptom patterns and do not quantify the long-term cardiovascular benefit that might be expected under sustained improvement in depressive symptoms over time.

If depressive symptom patterns were indeed altered over time, the next practical question concerns how such changes could be evaluated with respect to long-term cardiovascular risk. From a conventional study-design perspective, randomized controlled trials (RCT) or pragmatic intervention studies would appear to be natural candidates for addressing this question [21–24]. However, in the context of depression and CVD, extending these approaches to long-horizon cardiovascular outcomes poses substantial structural challenges. Depressive symptoms are characterized by marked temporal fluctuation and substantial inter-individual heterogeneity, making it difficult to sustain stable and consistent symptom improvement over prolonged follow-up periods [25–27]. At the same time, adjudicated cardiovascular events accrue slowly and at relatively low rates, placing heavy demands on follow-up duration, sample size, and statistical power when hard cardiovascular endpoints are used [25, 28–31]. As a result, the potential long-term cardiovascular benefit of sustained improvement in depressive symptoms remains difficult to quantify through conventional randomized or real-world intervention pathways [30, 31].

In this context, a counterfactual hypothetical intervention perspective offers a principled way to translate the prevention question into an explicit, decision-relevant contrast, without assuming that a specific real-world programme already exists that can reliably sustain symptom improvement over time [32, 33]. In this study, the natural course was defined as the reference scenario in which depressive symptoms, covariates, and outcomes evolved according to the longitudinal processes observed in the cohorts; the corresponding hypothetical interventions were defined as prespecified counterfactual changes to depressive symptom status or the CES-D score distribution at each follow-up wave [24]. By leveraging repeated measurements over time, it can appropriately address confounding that changes during follow up and may be influenced by prior symptom history, thereby strengthening interpretability for long term prevention planning [21, 34]. Importantly, the hypothetical interventions in this study refer to counterfactual changes in symptom status or score distributions, rather than to the direct effects of pharmacological treatment, psychotherapy, social support, or any other specific real-world intervention.

In light of this, we quantified the potential long-term cardiovascular benefit and preventable disease burden that may correspond to sustained improvement in depressive symptoms as a public health goal, using two population-based ageing cohorts. Using a counterfactual hypothetical intervention framework, we estimated long-horizon absolute risks of incident CVD and the corresponding absolute risk differences under sustained symptom-improvement scenarios, and further calculated potential impact fractions and the number of potentially preventable CVD cases per 1,000 individuals [35].

Methods

Study design and causal framework

This study was designed as a longitudinal hypothetical intervention analysis within the potential outcomes framework. We estimated long-horizon CVD risk under prespecified sustained depressive symptom-improvement strategies versus the natural course in each cohort. The causal estimand was defined as the risk difference, risk ratio, and potential impact fraction for cumulative CVD incidence in the eligible study population under each strategy versus the natural course, where individuals were hypothetically maintained at a lower level of depressive symptoms at each follow-up wave or their CES-D scores were restricted to a prespecified target range. This framework defines the causal question independently of any specific treatment modality; therefore, the estimates should be interpreted as modeled risk contrasts under hypothetical symptom-change scenarios, rather than as cardiovascular effects of antidepressant medication, psychotherapy, social support, or any other specific treatment strategy.

Data sources and study population

We used data from two population-based prospective ageing cohorts: the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [36, 37]. Both cohorts include repeated follow-up assessments that allow longitudinal measurement of depressive symptoms, covariates, and cardiovascular outcomes. For CHARLS, data from waves conducted between 2011 and 2020 were included, with the 2011 wave defined as baseline; for ELSA, waves conducted between 2008 and 2018 were included, with the 2008 wave defined as baseline. Baseline was defined as the first visit within the selected waves at which participants met all eligibility criteria.

Participants were eligible if they were aged 50 years or older at baseline, free of CVD at baseline, and had at least one follow-up visit after baseline. Individuals with prevalent CVD at baseline or missing information required for defining depression exposure or CVD outcomes were excluded. Participants were followed from baseline until the first occurrence of incident CVD, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the predefined observation window, whichever occurred first. Definition of depression exposure and hypothetical intervention strategies

Depressive symptoms were defined as a time-varying exposure repeatedly assessed at each follow-up wave, using cohort-specific measurement instruments and thresholds. In CHARLS, depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) [38], whereas in ELSA they were measured using the 8-item version (CES-D-8) [39]. Within each cohort, depression exposure was represented in two forms. First, a binary depressive status was defined at each wave using scale-specific cut-off values, classifying participants as depressed if their CES-D-10 score was ≥ 10 in CHARLS or their CES-D-8 score was ≥ 3 in ELSA, and as non-depressed otherwise. Second, depressive symptom severity was represented using the continuous CES-D score to capture variation in symptom burden over time. Binary and continuous depression measures were defined within each cohort and were not harmonized across cohorts.

Hypothetical intervention strategies were defined as prespecified rules that modified the observed depression exposure at each follow-up wave, while allowing all other variables to evolve according to their observed or modeled histories. The natural course, in which depressive symptoms followed their observed trajectory without modification, served as the reference scenario. For binary depressive status, two sustained intervention strategies were specified within each cohort. Under the feasible intervention, a stochastic intervention was specified such that, at each follow-up wave, participants who met the cohort-specific definition of depression had a 50% probability of being reassigned to the non-depressed state. The 50% probability was chosen to represent a conservative partial-improvement scenario relative to the intensive scenario in which all depressed participants were reassigned to the non-depressed state, and to provide a clinical anchor to the commonly used treatment-response concept of at least a 50% reduction in symptoms from baseline in depression treatment studies [40, 41]. Under the intensive intervention, all participants who met the cohort-specific definition of depression at that wave were reassigned to the non-depressed state. These reassignment rules were applied repeatedly at each wave. Additional intervention strategies were specified using continuous CES-D scores: at each follow-up wave, among participants whose CES-D score met the cohort-specific depression threshold, the observed score was deterministically truncated to prespecified target values defined relative to the baseline CES-D distribution among non-depressed participants in that cohort.

Across all intervention scenarios, depression exposure was updated at each follow-up wave according to the cohort-specific rules described above, while all other variables followed their observed or modeled histories.

Outcome definition and follow-up

The outcome of interest was incident CVD occurring during follow-up [9]. In both CHARLS and ELSA, CVD events were identified using cohort-specific ascertainment procedures based on self-reported physician diagnoses collected at each follow-up wave, including heart disease (e.g., heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure) and stroke. Both heart disease and stroke were treated as binary variables, coded as 0 for “No” and 1 for “Yes”. Participants with a history of CVD at baseline were excluded to ensure that all observed events represented incident outcomes.

Follow-up time was defined from baseline until the first occurrence of incident CVD, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the prespecified observation window, whichever occurred first; death before incident CVD was treated as a censoring event. CVD occurrence was treated as a discrete-time event assessed at each follow-up wave, consistent with the longitudinal structure of exposure and covariate measurements. Participants who experienced incident CVD were considered no longer at risk thereafter, whereas those without CVD were administratively censored at their last observed wave if they were lost to follow-up or at the end of follow-up.

Covariates

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed based on prior subject-matter knowledge to clarify the temporal ordering and confounding structure underlying the association between depression and CVD (Supplementary Fig. 1) [42]. Within this causal framework, covariates were classified into baseline covariates and time-varying covariates according to their timing and causal roles. Baseline covariates were treated as fixed over follow-up and were used to characterize individuals’ pre-existing risk profiles and structural differences. These included age, gender, ethnicity, educational attainment, and place of residence.

Time-varying covariates were repeatedly measured at each follow-up wave and were allowed to update over time, capturing lifestyle- and health-related factors. These covariates included smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, sleep-related indicators, social participation, and other cohort-specific variables (detailed in Supplementary Method 3). In the assumed causal structure, these factors constitute time-varying confounders affected by prior depressive symptoms: at any given wave, they may influence subsequent depression and CVD risk, while simultaneously lying on the causal pathway from prior depression to later CVD. This structure implies that naïvely adjusting for time-varying covariates in conventional regression models may induce bias by inappropriately conditioning on exposure-affected variables and may fail to correspond to the counterfactual estimand of interest, namely the sustained application of depression-improvement rules at each follow-up wave.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted separately in CHARLS and ELSA, and estimates were not pooled across cohorts. Baseline characteristics and key study variables were summarized using means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables.

To maximize sample retention while reducing potential bias due to missing data, a prespecified tiered missing-data handling strategy was applied prior to model fitting. For time-varying covariates, if missingness occurred at a single follow-up wave, the last observation carried forward (LOCF) approach was used to impute the missing value using the most recent valid observation [43]. If a participant had missing values at two or more follow-up waves, or if values remained missing after LOCF, follow-up was censored at the first unresolved missing wave and subsequent intervals were excluded to limit reliance on model extrapolation. This approach was intended to preserve the longitudinal time order and avoid future-information leakage in the g-formula framework, while limiting the repeated use of LOCF reduced the assumptions required when carrying forward potentially outdated covariate values over extended periods. For baseline covariates, participants with missing values were excluded, as such missingness could not be addressed using longitudinal information and might compromise baseline comparability between intervention scenarios.

Within each cohort, standardized estimates under the sustained depression-improvement rules were obtained using the parametric g-formula [44], accounting for the time-varying confounding structure. Data were organized in a discrete-time person-interval format, and interval-specific CVD risks were modeled using pooled logistic regression. The g-formula implementation included conditional models for each time-varying covariate given baseline covariates and prior history, and an outcome model for interval-specific incident CVD risk conditional on baseline covariates, current depression exposure, and relevant covariate history terms. Monte Carlo standardization was used to generate simulated follow-up data under each intervention scenario. At each follow-up wave, depression exposure was updated according to the prespecified intervention rule, time-varying covariates were generated conditional on the updated exposure, and interval-specific CVD risk was predicted using the outcome model. Cumulative risk up to each wave was calculated as one minus the product of predicted interval-specific survival probabilities, and intervention effects were summarized as cumulative risk differences, risk ratios, and PIFs comparing each intervention scenario with the natural course.

Statistical uncertainty was quantified using nonparametric bootstrap resampling at the individual level, with the full modeling, simulation, and risk accumulation procedure repeated within each bootstrap sample to derive 95% confidence intervals. Robustness of the primary estimates was further evaluated through prespecified sensitivity analyses addressing key assumptions. These included alternative missing-data assumptions (multiple imputation and complete-case analyses) [45, 46], model specification (alternative functional forms for selected continuous predictors), exposure operationalization (alternative CES-D cutoffs), and temporal ordering including potential reverse causation (lagged exposure definitions, exclusion of early events, and alternative within-wave ordering assumptions) [47]. Additional analyses examined different intervention probabilities, exclusion of potentially mediating behavioral covariates, inverse probability-of-censoring weighting treating death as a competing event, and quantitative bias analyses for misclassification of self-reported CVD outcomes and depressive status [48, 49]. In addition, because unmeasured confounding is a central limitation of observational causal inference, we used complementary strategies combining quantitative threshold assessment and empirical testing to evaluate its potential influence. We calculated E-values for selected risk-ratio estimates to quantify the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding required to explain away the observed intervention contrasts [50]. We also conducted a negative-control exposure analysis using childhood parental or guardian alcohol, drug, or mental-health problems as the negative-control exposure to probe the possibility of residual confounding. Detailed descriptions of selected sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Methods S4–S9.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0). The parametric g-formula was implemented using the gfoRmula (version 1.1.1) package. Multiple imputation was conducted using the mice (version 3.19.0) package. Bootstrap resampling and all simulation procedures were carried out within the R environment. E-values were calculated using the EValue (version 4.1.4) package in R.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 8,411 participants from CHARLS and 5,686 participants from ELSA were included. The mean age at baseline was 61.5 years (SD 7.8) in CHARLS and 63.8 years (SD 8.7) in ELSA. In CHARLS, depression was more prevalent among women, rural residents, and participants with lower educational attainment, whereas non-depressed participants were more likely to be men, urban residents, and those with higher education. A similar pattern was observed in ELSA: participants with depression were older, more often women, and tended to have less favorable distributions of socioeconomic circumstances and health-related behaviors (Table 1).

Hypothetical binary depression remission interventions

Across both cohorts, compared with the natural course, hypothetical interventions that repeatedly reassigned depressed individuals to a non-depressed state at each follow-up wave were associated with lower cumulative CVD risk over follow-up, with a clear intensity gradient such that the intensive intervention yielded larger risk reductions than the feasible intervention. In CHARLS, cumulative risk at the end of follow-up was highest under the natural course and progressively lower under the feasible and intensive interventions; the corresponding absolute and relative effect estimates also indicated greater benefit under the intensive intervention (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 2). ELSA showed the same directional pattern, although the estimated reductions were smaller in magnitude. From a population-impact perspective, at approximately 9–10 years, the feasible and intensive improvement scenarios corresponded to about 22 and 38 fewer CVD events per 1,000 individuals in CHARLS, respectively, with potential impact fractions of approximately 8.83% and 15.40%. In ELSA, the corresponding estimates were about 10 and 17 fewer CVD events per 1,000 individuals, with potential impact fractions of approximately 5.36% and 8.89% (Tables 2 and 3).

Hypothetical depression score interventions and dose-response associations

When CES-D was modeled as a continuous time-varying exposure and reduced at each wave to prespecified values (defined by the baseline CES-D distribution among the non-depressed population), we observed a dose–response pattern in both cohorts. In CHARLS, progressively larger symptom reductions (P75 → P50 → P25 → set to 0) were associated with stepwise lower cumulative CVD risks across follow-up intervals, yielding increasingly negative RDs and smaller RRs, consistent with a clear monotonic intensity gradient (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 3). In ELSA, the estimated effects were directionally consistent but smaller. Moreover, after a moderate reduction, the risk reduction showed an apparent “plateau”, largely because the target values for P50, P25, and “set to 0” coincided (all equal to 0) in the baseline non-depressed distribution, resulting in nearly overlapping counterfactual risk estimates for these scenarios (Supplementary Table 3; Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses of hypothetical depression interventions

Across most prespecified subgroups, the direction of effect was consistent with the main analysis. Both feasible and intensive interventions were associated with lower cumulative CVD risk than the natural course, with the intensive intervention generally showing larger absolute risk reductions. However, the magnitude of benefit varied across population strata in both cohorts. In CHARLS, larger reductions were generally observed among older participants, women, rural residents, non-current smokers and drinkers, and those with lower educational attainment. In ELSA, the overall pattern was similar, although subgroup differences were generally smaller. These findings suggest variation in the magnitude of benefit across subgroups, while the overall direction of benefit remained broadly consistent (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5; Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Sensitivity analyses results

A broad set of sensitivity analyses, including alternative missing-data strategies (multiple imputation with Rubin’s rules and complete-case analysis), alternative exposure definitions (different CES-D cut-points), alternative model specifications (e.g., nonlinear terms), time-ordering and reverse-causation checks (lagged depression, exclusion of early events, and varying within-wave simulation order with or without same-wave dependence), alternative intervention probabilities (25% and 75%), exclusion of potentially mediating behavioral covariates, inverse probability-of-censoring weighting for death as a competing event, quantitative bias analyses for misclassification of self-reported CVD outcomes and depressive status, and negative-control exposure analysis, yielded findings that were directionally consistent with the primary results in both cohorts, supporting the robustness of the main conclusions (Supplementary Tables 6–11).

Model diagnostics indicated that the parametric g-formula generally reproduced the observed mean trajectories of key time-varying covariates and the cumulative CVD risk process under the natural course. Comparisons of the observed and simulated distributions over time under the natural course showed broad agreement, with only minor deviations in a few follow-up intervals (Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5). These diagnostics support the ability of the fitted model system to reasonably reconstruct the main time-varying covariates, depressive symptom exposure process, and risk accumulation process under the natural course, although model misspecification and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Discussion

In this longitudinal hypothetical intervention analysis of two population-based ageing cohorts, we used the parametric g-formula to estimate long-term cumulative CVD risk under sustained depressive symptom-improvement strategies, compared with the natural course. Across both CHARLS and ELSA, hypothetical sustained improvement in depressive symptoms was consistently associated with lower cumulative CVD risk over follow-up. This overall pattern was observed in analyses using both binary depression-status interventions and continuous CES-D reduction strategies. The estimated risk reductions were generally greater under more intensive intervention scenarios, and findings were broadly robust across sensitivity analyses. Importantly, these estimates should be understood as modeled risk contrasts under hypothetical symptom-change scenarios, reflecting the potential CVD risk changes that may correspond to non-specific strategies for sustained depressive symptom improvement, rather than the cardiovascular effects of antidepressant medication, psychotherapy, social support, or any other specific treatment strategy.

Building on this counterfactual, intervention-focused framing, our main analyses suggested an intensity–duration gradient: the intensive strategy was associated with larger reductions in cumulative CVD risk than the feasible strategy, and the absolute risk separation increased with longer follow-up. To interpret this pattern, it is useful to foreground absolute risk because recent methodological and applied epidemiology papers emphasize that reporting both absolute and relative effect measures improves interpretability and better reflects preventable burden, particularly when baseline risk differs across populations and across time horizons [51–53]. Life-course cardiovascular research further supports a cumulative-risk view, highlighting that cardiometabolic risk often reflects risk accrual and loss of optimal cardiovascular health over time, which makes progressively larger absolute differences under sustained symptom improvement biologically and epidemiologically plausible [54, 55]. Mechanistic syntheses updated in the past few years also describe how persistent or recurrent psychological distress can contribute to long-term CVD vulnerability through repeated autonomic, neuroendocrine (HPA-axis), and inflammatory activation, consistent with effects that may compound over time rather than remain static [56, 57]. In parallel, recent longitudinal evidence increasingly indicates that changes and trajectories of depressive symptoms (including worsening versus improvement/remission) are meaningfully related to subsequent cardiovascular outcomes, reinforcing the relevance of modeling depression as a time-varying exposure and aligning with our intervention contrasts defined at repeated follow-up waves [15, 20, 58].

Beyond the binary intervention contrasts, our continuous CES-D intervention analyses suggested a graded pattern in which larger hypothetical reductions in symptom levels were associated with progressively lower cumulative CVD risk. This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence that depression-related cardiovascular vulnerability is not confined to a diagnostic threshold, but can be observed across symptom severity levels, with graded associations reported in contemporary cohort analyses and clinical syntheses [59–61]. Recent longitudinal work has further shown that changes in depressive symptoms over time matter clinically, with progression from no depression to mild depression associated with higher CVD risk and improvement associated with lower risk, supporting the relevance of symptom improvement even when it is partial rather than complete [9]. Recent longitudinal and meta-analytic evidence further suggests that cardiovascular risk increases progressively across levels of depressive symptom severity, without a clear threshold effect, underscoring the clinical relevance of incremental symptom reduction rather than dichotomous classification alone [62]. In addition, cardiovascular guidance and consensus discussions increasingly recognize mental health, including depression, as a clinically relevant factor for cardiovascular prevention and management, which provides a practical rationale for focusing on symptom reduction targets that are achievable in routine care rather than requiring complete symptom elimination [61, 63]. Taken together, these contemporary data provide external context for our observation that risk reductions were not limited to the most intensive “symptom elimination” scenario, but were also evident under moderate improvement targets, consistent with a clinically meaningful dose-response relationship between depressive symptom burden and long-horizon cardiovascular risk.

Across cohorts, the direction of the estimated effects was consistent, but the magnitude of risk reduction was larger in CHARLS than in ELSA. This pattern of “directional consistency with context-dependent effect size” is not unexpected in intervention-oriented absolute-risk analyses, because absolute risk reductions can diverge when populations differ in baseline risk, sociocultural context, healthcare systems, and access to mental health services, even if relative contrasts are similar [64]. Cross-cohort differences may also partly reflect measurement and comparability issues in depressive-symptom scales, as recent cross-national psychometric work has documented differential item functioning and cross-country differences in CES-D item expression across settings that include China and England [65]. In addition, contemporary longitudinal studies in diverse ageing populations suggest that the cardiovascular implications of changes in depressive symptoms can vary across contexts, which supports interpreting between-cohort differences in effect size as potentially arising from differences in symptom dynamics, background risk-factor control, and social environments rather than as evidence against the underlying association [9, 14, 58]. Recent work focusing on depressive-symptom trajectories prior to incident CVD also indicates that symptom evolution can carry prognostic information over long preclinical periods, implying that cohort-specific distributions of symptom patterns and timing may influence estimated long-horizon intervention effects [14]. Contemporary cardiovascular consensus statements further recognize that mental health and CVD are interconnected through multidirectional biological and behavioral pathways, and that the translation of mental health improvements into cardiovascular benefit may depend on healthcare infrastructure and sociocultural context [66, 67]. This perspective supports interpreting the CHARLS–ELSA differences as plausible contextual heterogeneity rather than as evidence against the underlying association. Differences between ageing populations in China and England, including CVD ascertainment, mental health service models, symptom expression, and CES-D versions and thresholds, may all influence effect size and comparability. Despite these measurement and contextual differences, the main findings were directionally consistent across both cohorts and remained stable across alternative exposure definitions, model specifications, time-ordering assumptions, missing-data approaches, and other sensitivity analyses. This consistency suggests that sustained improvement in depressive symptoms may have cross-cohort public health relevance for lower CVD risk. However, specific estimates of risk difference, risk ratio, or PIF should be interpreted in relation to the target population, healthcare system, mental health service context, and CVD outcome ascertainment method. Future studies in additional countries and regions, particularly those with validated CVD outcome data, are needed to further evaluate the generalizability of these findings.

An important aspect of interpreting these intervention contrasts is that the main patterns did not depend on a single modeling or timing choice. Methodological work on longitudinal causal estimation has shown that when exposures and covariates co-evolve, conclusions can shift under alternative functional forms or temporal ordering assumptions, so it is informative to probe those choices directly rather than treating them as fixed [68, 69]. In our analyses, the direction of risk reduction was stable when we varied CES-D cut-points, allowed more flexible functional forms, used lagged exposure definitions, excluded early events, and permuted within-wave updating order, and the “natural course” simulations reproduced observed patterns reasonably well, which is commonly used as a practical check that the fitted model system captures the joint data structure the intervention contrasts build on [70]. We also found that conclusions were not materially altered when we changed missing-data handling, which matters because modern missing-data work makes clear that complete-case and imputation-based approaches can behave differently depending on the missingness mechanism and covariate relationships, and should be considered as part of robustness rather than treated as a purely technical footnote [71–73]. For unmeasured confounding, we keep the interpretation appropriately cautious: our E-values are in the modest range, so confounding of moderate strength could attenuate estimates, but recent discussions of sensitivity analysis stress that such metrics are best read as transparent context for uncertainty rather than as a single pass–fail test [74, 75]. At the same time, the negative-control exposure analysis did not suggest an obvious signal of systematic residual confounding, providing empirical support for the robustness of the main findings. Nevertheless, neither E-values nor negative-control analyses can fully rule out unmeasured confounding, particularly from insufficiently measured factors such as antidepressant use, subclinical CVD, anxiety, cognitive status, and heart rate variability. Taken together, the cross-cohort directional consistency and the broad stability across specification, timing, and missing-data perturbations support a measured reading that the findings reflect a coherent counterfactual signal under the stated hypothetical interventions, while still leaving room for residual confounding that observational data cannot fully rule out.

Building on the robustness evidence above, it is important to be clear about the level of inference our hypothetical interventions represent [76, 77]. The intervention rules we evaluated correspond to sustained, strategy-level changes in depressive symptom status or level over follow-up, which aligns with recent causal-inference writing that distinguishes sustained intervention strategies from the effects of any single treatment component [78–80]. The same improvement in symptoms could arise from psychotherapy, pharmacological treatment, spontaneous remission, improved physical health, reduced pain, enhanced social support, or changes in symptom reporting, and these different pathways may themselves have different implications for CVD risk. In this sense, our estimates should be interpreted as long-term CVD risk changes corresponding to hypothetical symptom-improvement scenarios under explicitly specified rules, rather than as the expected effect size of antidepressant medication, psychotherapy, or any specific care programme [15, 77, 81]. This distinction is especially relevant when the exposure is not directly manipulable in a single, well-defined way, because the same symptom profile could be achieved through multiple mechanisms and implementation pathways [82, 83]. From a translation perspective, the feasibility and durability of achieving sustained symptom improvement at scale, and the pathway through which any cardiovascular benefit might be realized, will depend on access to mental health services, integration with cardiovascular prevention, and broader healthcare-system context [26, 61, 84]. Framed this way, our results complement, rather than replace, randomized trials of specific treatments by quantifying the potential population-level cardiovascular gains that could accrue if durable symptom improvement can be achieved in real-world settings. From a population-health perspective, the results can be viewed as estimates related to a potential impact fraction question: how much CVD burden might be reduced if the distribution of depressive symptoms in the target population were shifted to a more favorable, although still hypothetical, alternative distribution. In real-world practice, more feasible strategies may include regular screening, primary-care follow-up, collaborative care, psychotherapy, appropriate pharmacological treatment, social support, pain and chronic disease management, and interventions promoting physical activity and sleep improvement. With the development of digital therapeutics, mobile health tools, remote psychological interventions, wearable devices, and AI-based risk identification and follow-up management, long-term, continuous, and individualized mental health management may become more accessible. In this sense, the absolute risk differences, PIFs, and numbers of potentially preventable cases per 1,000 individuals provided by this study may serve as quantitative benchmarks for future evaluations of the cardiovascular benefits of specific intervention strategies, such as collaborative care, digital therapeutics, or integrated chronic disease management, and may inform cost-effectiveness comparisons, resource allocation, and public health priority setting. However, maintaining symptom improvement over long periods in large populations, avoiding treatment crossover, ensuring adherence, and accruing sufficient CVD events remain major challenges. Therefore, whether specific strategies can safely and effectively achieve sustained or partially sustained symptom improvement and reduce CVD risk requires further evaluation in randomized trials or target trial emulations of clearly defined interventions.

This study has several strengths. We used two large population-based ageing cohorts from different sociocultural and healthcare contexts, with repeated assessments of depressive symptoms and long follow-up for incident CVD, allowing the same hypothetical intervention contrasts to be evaluated across settings. Repeated measurements also enabled us to examine both binary and continuous symptom-improvement targets within a longitudinal framework and to address time-varying confounding affected by prior depressive symptom history. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, CVD outcomes were primarily self-reported, which may introduce outcome misclassification and could attenuate effect estimates, particularly if reporting varies by depressive symptom status. Although quantitative bias analyses suggested that the direction of the findings was relatively stable, these analyses depend on externally specified sensitivity and specificity parameters and cannot replace outcome validation based on medical records, disease registries, or clinical diagnosis. Second, depressive symptoms were measured using CES-D instruments that are not fully identical across cohorts, and cross-cultural differences in symptom expression or thresholding may partly contribute to differences in effect magnitude. Third, the hypothetical intervention rules represent sustained symptom improvement over time and do not correspond to any single clinical treatment, so the estimates should not be interpreted as the expected effect of antidepressant medication, psychotherapy, or a specific program. Because repeated information on treatment initiation, treatment type, adherence, psychotherapy, and other treatment pathways was unavailable, we could not distinguish the specific sources of symptom improvement or fully address confounding by indication. Fourth, the spacing of survey waves limited resolution of short-term symptom changes, within-wave temporal ordering, subclinical CVD, and time-varying treatments, which may obscure faster dynamics linking depressive symptoms and cardiovascular outcomes and means that residual reverse causation cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, the DAG and models could not include all potentially relevant factors, such as antidepressant use, anxiety, cognitive status, heart rate variability, and subclinical CVD indicators. Although negative-control exposure analysis and multiple sensitivity analyses provided robustness information, residual confounding may still affect the estimates. Sixth, behavioral variables such as physical activity, sleep, and social participation may act as both time-varying confounders and potential mediators. Sensitivity analyses excluding these variables yielded directionally consistent results, but we did not conduct formal mediation decomposition and therefore could not distinguish direct from indirect effects. Seventh, death is an important competing event in ageing cohorts. Although IPCW sensitivity analyses showed that the main findings were directionally stable, treating death as informative censoring cannot fully eliminate potential residual bias from competing risks. Finally, this study could not directly evaluate the independent effects of high-intensity interval training, digital health technologies, cognitive interventions, or other specific strategies; these questions remain directions for future research.

Conclusions

In two population-based ageing cohorts from China and England, hypothetical sustained improvement in depressive symptoms was associated with lower long-term cumulative CVD risk compared with the natural course. Larger symptom reductions were generally associated with greater estimated risk reductions. These findings provide quantitative evidence for evaluating the potential long-term cardiovascular benefit and preventable disease burden under hypothetical sustained depressive symptom-improvement scenarios within a hypothetical intervention framework. Because the same symptom improvement may arise through multiple real-world pathways, and these pathways may have different cardiovascular implications, the results should be interpreted as strategy-level contrasts under hypothetical symptom-change scenarios rather than as the effects of any specific treatment modality. Randomized trials or target trial emulations of clearly defined intervention strategies are still needed for further validation.
