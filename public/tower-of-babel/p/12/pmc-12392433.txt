The impact of alternative care pathways adopted during the COVID-19 pandemic on the management of non-communicable diseases at a tertiary care hospital in Thailand
Authors: Jongkonnee Chongpornchai, Tuangrat Phodha, Thanawat Wongphan, Kamonwan Soonklang, Peter C. Coyte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundManagement of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including hypertension (HT) and diabetes mellitus (DM), was significantly impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Many institutions adopted alternative care pathways, e.g. pharmacy at home (PAH), and the deferred care (DC). While PAH has been studied for clinical outcomes, evaluation of the DC remains limited. Consequently, this study evaluates both the clinical and economic outcomes of the PAH and DC as alternatives to usual care.MethodA retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Thailand from 1 July 2021, to 30 June 2023. Data from outpatients with HT and DM were classified into PAH, DC, or discharged home with follow-up at the hospital. Clinical outcomes included changes in systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and fasting blood sugar (FBS), calculated from baseline to follow-up. Economic outcome was the cost of illness (COI) per patient visit. Multivariate multilevel mixed-effects linear regression assessed clinical outcomes, while log-linear regression evaluated economic outcome.ResultsThere were 3,518 patients in the pandemic period and 4,135 patients in the post pandemic period. There was no statistically significant impact of PAH and DC on changes in SBP, DBP, and FBS. However, both care pathways significantly reduced COI during both periods (p < 0.001). During the pandemic period, COI reductions were 32.3% in PAH and 93.5% in DC compared to usual care. Similar trends were observed in the post pandemic period, with COI reductions of 40.0% for PAH and 96.1% for DC.ConclusionPAH and DC pathways did not worsen the clinical outcomes and reduced costs during and following the pandemic. As a result, these two pathways, developed during the COVID-19 pandemic, can be adapted for regular use. When these pathways are integrated into regular use, they can be promptly and fully reactivated in future emergencies.

Background

In 2021, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) accounted for 7.3 trillion global cases (Li et al., 2025). According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 data, the global incidence of NCDs was estimated at approximately 13.1 billion new cases, with a prevalence of 7.1 billion existing cases (Shu & Jin, 2023). This reflects a high burden of multiple chronic conditions, where one individual may contribute to multiple disease episodes. NCDs accounted for up to 71% of all deaths worldwide, the majority of which occurred in low – and middle-income countries in the year 2022 (World Health Organization, 2022). The four leading causes of death from NCDs worldwide are cardiovascular diseases, cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes mellitus (DM) (United Nations Thailand, 2021; World Health Organization, 2022). Thailand reported an estimated 14 million people living with NCDs and approximately 2 million new cases annually in the year 2023 (Health Data Center, 2024). This national-level data reflects a person-based approach. NCDs in Thailand accounted for 75% of all deaths in the year 2021 (United Nations Thailand, 2021). Studies in several countries have identified DM and hypertension (HT) as the most common NCDs (El Haidari et al., 2024; Elyas et al., 2024). HT, DM, coronary artery disease, and stroke are highly prevalence in Thailand and exert considerable impact on the healthcare system (Bhoothookngoen & Sanchan, 2024). Globally, HT affected approximately 1 in 3 adults, with an estimated 1.39 billion people living with the condition in 2010 (Mills et al., 2020). Several countries in the Asia-Pacific region have reported higher prevalence from 2017 to 2021 such as 45% in Japan, 34.1% in Indonesia, 33.5% in Singapore, 30.7% in India, and 30.1% in Myanmar. In contrast, the lowest prevalence of HT was observed in Cambodia and Laos, at approximately 15% (Shariff et al., 2024). In 2024, the incidence and the prevalence of HT in Thailand were reported to be 1.38% and 24.7%, respectively (Health Data Center, 2024). HT was estimated to cause 1.75% of annual deaths in Thailand in the year 2019 (Division of Non Communicable Disease, 2021), while 12% of global deaths in the year 2024 (Goyal et al., 2025). In 2024, an estimated 589 million adults were living with DM worldwide (International Diabetes Federation, 2024). The average DM prevalence in the South-East Asia region was 9.7% in the year 2024 (International Diabetes Federation, 2024). Several countries in the region, such as Bangladesh and Sri Lanka reported higher prevalence rates exceeding 10% in that year (International Diabetes Federation, 2024). Thailand had an estimated incidence rate of DM of approximately 0.7% per year and a prevalence rate of 5.6% in the year 2024 (Health Data Center, 2024). DM was estimated to cause 3.13% of annual deaths in Thailand in the year 2019 (Division of Non Communicable Disease, 2021), while 11.3% of global deaths in the year 2024 (Goyal et al., 2025). Overall, Thailand had a moderate incidence and prevalence of both HT and DM relative to other countries in the Asian region. However, these conditions were strongly associated with serious complications such as stroke, kidney failure, and cardiovascular diseases (World Health Organization, 2022). These complications posed significant threats to quality of life and imposed a considerable financial burden on the national health system.

NCDs require continuous treatment and coordinated care including regular monitoring, medication adherence, and routine healthcare visits to maintain disease control and prevent complications. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, Thailand’s public health system was generally effective in providing consistent care for patients with NCDs. The pandemic significantly disrupted healthcare service delivery (Agrawal et al., 2021; Elbeddini & Yeats, 2020; Leelahavarong et al., 2022), with services for HT, asthma, DM, and cancer among 168 WHO member countries in the year 2020 (Leelahavarong et al., 2022). These disruptions may contribute to worsening health outcomes (Kendzerska et al., 2021; Prvu Bettger et al., 2020) (Ministry of Public Health, 2021). To cope with these challenges and ensure uninterrupted care while minimising infection risk among patients with NCDs, many large hospitals in Thailand implemented new service models, including telehealth along with pharmacy at home (PAH) and telehealth along with deferred care (DC) (Leelahavarong et al., 2022). Although the pharmacy at home or deferred care programme had previously been implemented before the COVID-19 pandemic period, these programmes had not been integrated with telehealth services. The integration of these service models aimed to reduce hospital visits, the risk of COVID-19 transmission among patients and alleviate the workload of healthcare personnel during times of limited resources. Previously, the pharmacy at home programme by mail in Thailand was available exclusively at psychiatric hospitals after an in-person consultation with a physician (Sumrankong, 2013). The programme aimed to enhance convenience and reduce waiting times, particularly for patients living in remote areas. These patients were allowed to receive medical consultations at nearby hospitals, while medications were dispensed and delivered from a central hospital. Pharmacy at home by mail service had not been implemented for patients with HT and DM. Similarly, the deferred care programme was occasionally applied on a case-by-case basis for several conditions, particularly in situations where patients or healthcare providers were unable to attend appointments as scheduled. It was never formalised as a routine policy or systematic service model. This is similar to a telehealth programme, which refers to the method of communication that does not require in-person contact, such as telephone or digital media (World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe, 2020). Telehealth has not been widely or clearly implemented in Thailand. Most evidence came from international studies using telehealth for various conditions to minimise time and cost for both patients and healthcare system (Atmojo et al., 2020; Whitten et al., 2002). International evidence supports the effectiveness of individual components such as telehealth or pharmacy at home programme in improving disease control, reducing hospital admissions, and lowering healthcare costs (Atmojo et al., 2020; Schmittdiel et al., 2011; Whitten et al., 2002). Previous evaluations typically examined these components in isolation. There is still a lack of research that assesses integrated service models such as the PAH and DC. In the context of Thailand, previous studies investigating the PAH during the COVID-19 pandemic focused on clinical outcomes, and demonstrated that the PAH’s effectiveness in disease control was comparable to usual care (Boonlert, 2022; Rattanapan et al., 2019). Care for HT and DM patients can be delivered remotely using home-based devices like BP monitors and glucometers. Clinical guidelines support this approach with clear indicators, structured protocols for medication adjustments, and options for remote counselling and lifestyle guidance (Pitayarangsarit et al., 2023). However, evaluations of the economic impact of the PAH pathway remain limited. Furthermore, there is currently no published research assessing the DC pathway within the Thai context. This study is the first to evaluate both the clinical and economic outcomes of PAH and DC pathways among patients with HT and DM in Thailand. Clinical outcomes in this study refer to changes in systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and fasting blood sugar (FBS), which indicate the effectiveness of disease control. Economic outcomes refer to the cost of illness (COI), which reflects the financial burden of care. These outcomes are relevant for evaluating both the health impact and cost of service models. The findings provide evidence-based recommendations to support policy decisions for post-pandemic healthcare delivery and prepare the system for future health crises.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies (Von Elm et al., 2007).

Study design

This study is a retrospective cohort study to evaluate clinical and economic outcomes among care pathways of interest.

Setting and period

Previous studies in Thailand showed that PAH and DC services were predominantly provided in large hospitals (Leelahavarong et al., 2022). The study was conducted in the outpatient department (OPD) of a tertiary care hospital in Thailand, selected due to its capacity and adequate patient population. Notably, the implementation process of PAH and DC was quite similar within tertiary care settings.

The study was conducted over 2 periods. The first period included patients with DM and HT who visited the outpatient department (OPD) between July 2021 and December 2021, representative of the pandemic period. These patients were followed up for 6 months until June 2022. The second period included patients between July 2022 and December 2022, representative of the post pandemic period, with follow-up continuing for 6 months until June 2023. The division of study periods aligned with the timeframe of the COVID-19 pandemic in Thailand, which officially began in January 2020 and continued with several waves until July 2022, when COVID-19 was declared an endemic disease (National Health Security Office, 2022).

Study population

The study population comprised DM or HT patients who received treatment at the OPD of a tertiary care hospital in Thailand. Inclusion criteria were HT and DM patients who visited a tertiary care hospital during the two study periods, had an unchanged medication regimen for 6 months, and had no complications including stroke, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, diabetic retinopathy, foot ulcers or amputations. The exclusion criteria included participants with missing values for any study variable and those with implausible values.

HT and DM were selected as the target population due to their high prevalence and suitability for remote management. Both conditions require long term care and were impacted by service disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Sample size

The sample size for regression analysis was suggested at a minimum of 50 participants per covariate used in the analysis (VanVoorhis & Morgan, 2007). In this study, there were eight independent variables that were considered as potential covariates for regression analysis. The sample size for regression analysis was at least 400 participants.

Sampling procedure

There were two units of analysis in this study. First, one tertiary hospital in Thailand was purposively sampled for this study. Second, patients were randomly sampled based on their visits during the defined study period within the selected hospital. The unit of analysis in this study was patient visits.

Study procedures

Data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital database by the Computer Center for patients who met the inclusion criteria. Records that met any of the exclusion criteria were excluded from the study. The research protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee Saraburi Hospital, COE No.EC040/2566.

Patients were allocated to each care pathway based on routine clinical practice, determined by the physician’s judgment and the hospital’s service protocols. The PAH and DC were implemented for HT and DM in April 2020. As a result, no HT and DM patients were included from the pre-pandemic period. Accordingly, this study employed a concurrent comparison of care pathways within the same timeframe. Data collection

Secondary and clinical data were retrieved from the hospital database, focusing on dependent and independent variables. The dependent variables were economic and clinical outcomes. The economic outcome was defined as the COI per patient visit. The clinical outcomes included changes in the following parameters measured as the difference between baseline and follow-up values: SBP, DBP and FBS. The independent variables consisted of the predictor variable of interest and other potential confounders. The predictor variable of interest was the care pathways (discharge home with follow-up at the hospital, PAH and DC). The potential confounders were comorbidities (≤2 disease, and >2 disease), sex (male, and female), age (<60, and ≥60), medical benefit scheme (Universal coverage (UC), Social security scheme (SSS), Civil service medical benefits scheme (CSMBS), and other), polypharmacy (yes, and no), body mass index (BMI) (<25 kg/m2, and ≥25 kg/m2), and history of COVID-19 infection (yes, and no) (Bhaskaran et al., 2021; Hennelly et al., 2021; Kahlberg et al., 2021; Lee, 2022; Perkin et al., 2020; VanVoorhis & Morgan, 2007; Yeates et al., 2022). Polypharmacy refers to the use of more than five medications (Hennelly et al., 2021). Data analysis

Cases with missing values for any study variable or implausible data were excluded based on the predefined exclusion criteria. Only complete and valid records were retained for analysis. Descriptive analysis of demographic characteristics was conducted using the chi-squared test for category variables and the kruskal–wallis test for continuous variables. The multivariate log-linear regression model was conducted to assess association between the care pathways and economic outcomes. The multivariate multilevel mixed-effects linear regression model was performed to assess association between the care pathways and clinical outcomes.

The multivariate log-linear regression and the multivariate multilevel mixed-effects linear regression were performed to assess the impact of the care pathways on the changes in SBP, DBP, and FBS between baseline and follow-up visit, as well as COI, while holding all other confounding factors constant. A multilevel model was applied to account for the nested structure of clinical outcome data arising from individual patients receiving different sequences of care pathways during the study period, which could not be adequately addressed by traditional regression techniques (Stata.com, 2024). For multivariate multilevel mixed-effects linear regression, level 1 was care pathway and level 2 was hospital number (HN). The service duration in months was used as a covariate of HN. The care pathway was defined as discharge home with follow-up at the hospital, PAH and DC. The service duration in months, defined as the total length of time a patient remained within the respective care pathway during the study period.

Models were checked by model diagnostics including selection bias, multicollinearity, goodness of fit and omitted variables. Selection bias was assessed across the three care pathways, with pairwise comparisons between care pathways by the Inverse Mills Ratio (IMR) derived from the Heckman selection model (Xu et al., 2017). The results indicated the presence of selection bias. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All predictor variables had VIF values below 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Therefore, no further adjustments to the model were necessary (O’brien, 2007). The stepwise backward elimination method was explored for model fit with the data. Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), Log-likelihood ratio, Root mean square error (RMSE) and R-squared were explored for measuring model fit and prediction model accuracy. The dependent and independent variables for all models to be analysed in this study were as follows.

Model 1: The dependent variable was the change in SBP. The independent variables included care pathways, comorbidities, sex, age, medical benefit scheme, polypharmacy, and BMI during the pandemic period, with history of COVID-19 infection added in the post pandemic period.

Model 2: The dependent variable was the change in DBP. The independent variables were care pathways, age, medical benefit scheme, and BMI for the pandemic period, and care pathways, sex, age, and comorbidities for the post-pandemic period.

Model 3: The dependent variable was the change in FBS. The independent variables were care pathways, comorbidities, sex, age, medical benefit scheme, polypharmacy, BMI, and history of COVID-19 infection in both pandemic and post-pandemic periods.

Model 4: The dependent variable was cost COI. The independent variables were care pathways, comorbidities, sex, age, medical benefit scheme, polypharmacy, BMI, and history of COVID-19 infection in both periods.

Statistical analysis was conducted using the STATA 18 software (StataCorp, 2023). The level of significance was set at 0.05.

Results

A total of 8,030 patients with DM and HT without complications were identified during the pandemic period. After excluding cases with medication changes within six months (n = 4,312) and those with missing or incorrect values (n = 200), the final sample comprised 3,518 patients (Figure 1). In the post pandemic period, 13,335 patients were identified. A total of 8,835 patients were excluded due to recent medication changes, and 365 were excluded due to missing or incorrect values, resulting in a final sample of 4,135 patients (Figure 2). Characteristics of NCD patient

During the pandemic period, a total of 3,518 patients were categorised into three care pathways: discharge home with follow-up at hospital (n = 2,903), PAH (n = 297), and DC (n = 318). Among the 3,518 patients, the mean age was 59.93 years, and 63.64% were female. The average BMI was 27.81 kg/m², and 66.03% had two or more comorbidities. Polypharmacy was common, with a mean of 5.41 of medications. Most patients had no history of COVID-19 infection (92.15%). Regarding medical benefit scheme, the majority were under the UC (48.32%). Following the post pandemic period, care pathways had a statistically significant impact on COI. Compared to patients discharged home with hospital follow-up, those in the PAH had an estimated 40.0% lower COI (95% CI [ – 0.57, – 0.44], p < .001), while patients in the DC had an approximately 96.1% lower COI (95% CI [ – −3.31, – 3.21], p < .001). Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical (changes in SBP, DBP, and FBS) and economic outcomes (COI) of PAH and DC among patients with DM and HT in Thailand. The findings demonstrated that, during both the pandemic and post pandemic periods, care pathways were not significantly associated with the changes in SBP, DBP, and FBS. However, the care pathways were significantly associated with reduced COI in both periods.

The difference in SBP, DBP and FBS between baseline and follow-up, which represent clinical outcomes, showed no significant differences across care pathways in both the pandemic and post pandemic periods. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic period, service models such as telehealth or pharmacy at home were assessed separately, as patients were still able to visit the hospital as usual without restrictions from public health emergencies. A systematic review of 34 studies summarised the clinical outcomes of chronic disease management delivered through telehealth. The chronic diseases included HT, DM, anticoagulation, depression, hyperlipidemia, asthma, heart failure, Human Immunodeficiency Virus, post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic kidney disease, stroke, COPD, and smoking cessation. Most of the examined studies (25 studies) involved telephone-only interventions of telehealth and reported the laboratory outcomes, medication adherence, physical examinations, and mortality. Of 34 studies, 23 demonstrated improved clinical outcomes, while 10 studies showed non-inferior clinical outcomes and only one showed poor clinical outcomes. Limitations such as internet access and the lack of in-person interactions were noted as contributing factors to poor clinical outcomes. Most previous studies demonstrated favourable clinical outcomes or non-inferior clinical outcomes, which were consistent with the findings of the present study, as both studies utilised telephone-based telehealth communication. Additionally, the eligibility criteria for the PAH and DC services in Thailand included an assessment of patients’ readiness, which helped eliminate barriers such as limited internet access or communication difficulties during service delivery. Likewise, several United States studies found that pharmacy at home by mail was associated with better medication adherence and improved clinical outcomes in DM patients such as HbA1c control below 8% and fewer emergency visits ( Schmittdiel et al., 2011). These findings are consistent with the present study, as the PAH or pharmacy at home by mail contributed to improved patient adherence, which may explain why the clinical outcomes observed also remained within desirable therapeutic targets. As only stable patients were included and no significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed among the three care pathways in this study, it can be inferred that therapeutic targets were maintained across all care pathways. During the pandemic period in Thailand, telehealth was combined with the pharmacy at home and deferred care into the PAH and DC pathways. Two studies in Thailand (Rattanapan et al., 2019) also reported no significant changes in clinical outcomes such as BP or HbA1c level between PAH and usual care. These findings align with the present study, which also observed no significant differences in clinical outcomes between the PAH and usual care. This may be attributed to the inclusion of patients with stable chronic conditions, as was the case in this study. Previous studies on the PAH differed from the present study in both design and methodology. One study involved a within-subject comparison of HT patients who initially received usual care and were later transitioned to the PAH during the COVID-19 pandemic. Another study evaluating PAH in diabetes care aimed to implement PAH for all diabetic patients whose HbA1c level was <7%, but some opted for usual care, enabling comparative analysis between PAH and usual care. There is currently no published study evaluating the clinical outcomes of DC. Relevant guidance from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (U.S. CDC) provides useful insights. U.S. CDC recommends that routine primary and special care, care for well-controlled chronic conditions, and routine screening for asymptomatic conditions may be deferred if necessary. The results of deferral were dependent on an individual's condition and the length of time that care is delayed. Continuous deferred care is more likely to result in patient harm (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020). This recommendation aligns with this study’s use of the DC pathway, which involved stable patients and brief deferral. Careful selection and monitoring supported acceptable outcomes. However, evidence on DC remains limited. This study is the first in Thailand to evaluate PAH and DC pathways during and after COVID-19 using real-world data. It provides evidence supporting decentralised models for stable chronic patients and demonstrates feasible strategies for ensuring care continuity during crises. The use of multilevel regression and a well-selected sample enhances the study’s validity and generalizability.

The PAH and DC had an impact on COI, which represents economic outcomes. There is currently no published study evaluating the economic outcomes of the PAH and DC. However, some studies had investigated telehealth alone prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. A previous study (Atmojo et al., 2020) reported the benefits of telehealth in reducing the cost of healthcare services and patients’ out of pocket expenses. Cost of healthcare services included equipment, building supplies, and wages. Patients’ out of pocket expenses included transportation, meals, and accommodations. The systematic review of cost-effectiveness analysis found that telehealth was claimed to be cost-effective (Whitten et al., 2002). This study provides the first economic evaluation of PAH and DC in Thailand, offering key evidence on their effectiveness and cost to inform decisions on future implementation in routine care and emergency preparedness.

This study shows positive clinical outcomes, contrasting with national reports of worsening NCD control (Ministry of Public Health, 2021) and rising NCD-related deaths during COVID-19, especially from late 2020–2021 (Department of Disease Control, 2021). Most deaths involved patients with HT (53%) and DM (36%). These trends reflect limited nationwide implementation of PAH and DC. Findings support expanding these models to maintain care continuity for HT and DM during and after pandemics. In a post-hoc analysis, this research further analysed the appropriate duration of PAH and DC by defining the cut-off-point for desired target clinical outcomes of interest following the guidelines of American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the European Society of Hypertension (ESH) (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee, 2023; Kreutz et al., 2024) as 140 mmHg for SBP, 80 mmHg for DBP, and 130 mg/dL for FBS. Univariate logistic regression was performed to assess the impact of duration of receiving the PAH and DC on the desired target clinical outcomes. Based on different durations of receiving the PAH and DC, the clinical outcomes met the desired targets. However, the mode of duration for receiving the PAH and DC pathways was 5 months and 1 month, respectively. Therefore, the PAH and DC pathways would be useful for at least 5 months and 1 month, respectively, without worsening in clinical outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first study in Thailand to comprehensively assess both clinical and economic outcomes of care pathways using real-world data during the pandemic and post pandemic period. Second, the use of multilevel models strengthened the robustness of the findings by accounting for hierarchical data structures. Third, the inclusion of stable patients ensured comparability and internal validity. Finally, the large sample size enhanced generalizability.

This study has the following limitations. First, selection bias may have occurred due to non-random allocation of patients to care pathways, which is typical in observational studies but does not compromise patient safety. Second, the follow-up period was limited to six months, restricting evaluation of long-term outcomes. Third, the data were drawn from a tertiary care hospital, limiting generalizability to primary and secondary care hospitals, where most HT and DM patients in Thailand are treated, approximately 88% (Health Data Center, 2024). PAH and DC were predominantly implemented in tertiary care hospitals with greater infrastructure, logistics, and workforce (Pitayarangsarit et al., 2023). In contrast, secondary and primary care hospitals employed simpler approaches, such as home delivery, drive-through pickup, or extended prescription. Fourth, there are other covariates, such as smoking, drinking, education, and caregiver status that may have an impact according to literature reviews. However, the available database did not allow for data collection.

Future research

Future research should reassess the effectiveness of ongoing service models, investigate the long-term outcomes of PAH and DC when combined with other evidence-based interventions, evaluate their applicability in primary and secondary care hospitals, and incorporate broader covariates such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, education level, and caregiver support.

Policy recommendation

Policymakers should adopt PAH and DC as standard care models for stable HT and DM patients, expand their implementation in all tertiary hospitals, and develop standardised protocols with robust monitoring systems. Integrating these models into national emergency preparedness plans can strengthen healthcare system resilience.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that PAH and DC maintain clinical effectiveness while significantly reducing healthcare costs for patients with HT and DM. The findings support the continued implementation and expansion of these care pathways in Thailand's healthcare system, both for routine chronic disease management and as essential components of pandemic preparedness strategies. The substantial cost reductions, combined with maintained clinical outcomes, demonstrate that these innovative service delivery models can contribute to sustainable healthcare system development while ensuring continued access to quality chronic disease care. The research fills a critical knowledge gap in the literature and provides evidence-based recommendations to guide healthcare policy decisions for post-pandemic healthcare delivery systems, not only in Thailand but potentially for other countries with similar healthcare challenges and resource constraints.
