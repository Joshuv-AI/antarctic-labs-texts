Indication for durable mechanical circulatory support: an expert opinion review
Authors: Felix Schoenrath, Michael Bonios, Attilio Iacovoni, Isabell Anna Just, Luise Roehrich, Oliver Miera, Guillaume Baudry, Tuvia Ben Gal, Maria Generosa Crespo-Leiro, Emer Joyce, Chris J Kapelios, Hoong Sern Lim, Hadi Skouri, Kevin Damman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced heart failure represents a significant and expanding clinical challenge, establishing durable mechanical circulatory support as the critical therapeutic pillar, whether utilized as a bridge to transplantation or as a standalone therapy. This expert review provides a contemporary clinical decision framework that supplements existing guidelines by shifting the primary focus towards the pre-operative phase and the essential multidisciplinary processes required for clinical success. This review emphasizes precise patient selection and pre-operative optimization strategies designed to mitigate medical harm. This is particularly important in complex scenarios with relevant cardiac and non-cardiac comorbidities and in early stages of advanced heart failure to adequately address specific high-risk populations and conditions. By codifying the necessity of interdisciplinary team dynamics, targeted ambulatory as well as in-hospital workups, and shared decision-making, this review provides a structured approach to ensure equitable access and optimal long-term survival within the evolving landscape of advanced heart failure care.

Introduction

Aetiology and epidemiology of advanced heart failure

Advanced heart failure (AdvHF) is characterized by severe and persistent symptoms alongside frequent hospitalizations for clinical worsening. At this stage, patients often experience secondary organ dysfunction and require specialized interventions, as standard guideline-directed medical treatments and device therapies are no longer sufficient to stabilize the condition.1–3 The underlying aetiologies include coronary artery disease and non-ischaemic causes; dilated, valvular, hypertrophic, restrictive, infiltrative, and toxic cardiomyopathies, which are increasingly recognized due to advances in diagnostics.2,4–7 Findings from various cohort studies indicate that the population prevalence of AdvHF ranges from .2% to 2.7%. These rates rise significantly with age, improved survival of acute cardiac events, and are projected to increase further over the coming decades.2–11 Prognostic tools facilitate identification of candidates for advanced interventions.3,12

Advanced therapeutic concepts and their outcomes

Conventional therapies are insufficient in AdvHF patients, requiring escalation to individualized strategies within specialized multidisciplinary programmes.1,6,13

Heart transplantation continues to represent the standard, delivering the most favourable long-term survival and producing substantial improvements in functional status and quality of life in well-selected recipients.1,6,14 Persistent scarcity of donor organs, coupled with the aging and increasingly comorbid recipient population, constrain the broader adoption of heart transplantation. Durable mechanical circulatory support (d-MCS) has been an established alternative for patients ineligible for transplantation or as a bridge to transplant (candidacy).1,6 Technological improvements have led to enhanced outcomes (1-year survival 86%, 5-year survival 60%), but long-term complications such as right heart failure (RHF) and driveline infections remain significant determinants of morbidity and mortality.15–17 Optimal outcomes replicating those of transplantation depend on precise patient selection and comprehensive longitudinal follow-up within highly experienced centres. Recent comparisons between heart transplantation and fully magnetically levitated, continuous-flow left ventricular assist devices (LVAD) suggest comparable outcomes for both therapies at the 2-year mark in younger patients (< 50 years old). However, a cautious interpretation of these findings is warranted, as the analysis relied on comparing clinical trial data with registry-based data for one device.18 The concept of net life prolongation achieved by sequentially combining both treatment modalities merits increasing attention.18,19 While d-MCS weaning remains rare in real-world registries, significant weaning rates have been achieved in young patients with a short duration of non-ischaemic heart failure (HF).18,20,21

Purpose and scope of this expert opinion review

The purpose of this expert review is to provide a definitive clinical decision framework for multidisciplinary teams, synthesizing European expertise with the latest registry data and randomized controlled trials to address current unmet needs in the field. Its scope encompasses the entire spectrum of AdvHF, with a primary focus on the pre-operative phase and the essential team dynamics required to optimize patient selection and mitigate medical harm. By addressing complex and high-risk scenarios in AdvHF and significant comorbidities, the document offers actionable insights for holistic optimization and targeted workups.

Interdisciplinary team approach and program structure

Role of the advanced heart failure team

The AdvHF team’s strength lies in structured collaboration. Leadership roles, accountability frameworks, and continuous quality improvement sustain program effectiveness. The AdvHF team model integrates diverse expertise to deliver care that is patient-centred, evidence-based, and ethically grounded (Figure 1).22,23 Crucially, patients and their families are viewed as active team members. AdvHF treatment is guided by regular interdisciplinary board meetings. Cardiologists, surgeons, and anaesthesiologists/critical care physicians discuss treatment options together with mental health professionals and experts in palliative care to ensure a holistic and standardized care.24 This team is completed by HF coordinators, ventricular assist device (VAD) technicians, and social workers (Figure 1).24–27 Instruments such as the DECIDE-LVAD tool aid can help elicit patient’s values.28,29 Integrating multiparametric scoring (HM3 Score) provides realistic outcome expectations.30 Integrating palliative care alongside disease-modifying therapies at the time of diagnosis, ensures that quality-of-life interventions coexists with AdvHF management rather than serving as a terminal alternative. This integrated approach provides a critical framework for patients transitioning towards d-MCS, facilitating ‘preparedness planning’ and the establishment of clear exit strategies.31 Infectious disease specialists, pharmacists, and cardiac rehabilitation physicians constitute further important, though non-mandatory members of the multidisciplinary AdvHF team.

Patient education, integration of primary care providers, outcome and quality of life

Patient-centred approaches are fundamental to contemporary clinical practice and become especially critical when evaluating candidacy of AdvHF patients for d-MCS. Although modern d-MCS devices are associated with significantly improved quality of life and survival, these benefits are accompanied by substantial effects on daily living. Comprehensive education of patients and their primary caregivers is paramount to successful long-term management of these patients. This includes information regarding device function, anticipated clinical benefits and pitfalls, procedural and long-term risks, expected lifestyle modifications, and how to maintain device safety and effectiveness. Early integration and close contact with primary care providers ensure routine health needs; other chronic disease management and preventive measures remain appropriately addressed despite the complexity of d-MCS therapy. Discharging patients to a safe and hygienic environment with reliable power support is essential for successful d-MCS treatment.25

Identification and patient selection for durable mechanical circulatory support

Durable mechanical circulatory support in hospitalized patients in/after cardiogenic shock and in inotrope-dependent patients

Indication and assessment of durable mechanical circulatory support (specifics in cardiogenic shock patients)

Patients in cardiogenic shock (CS) represent a high-risk and heterogeneous population in whom the indication for d-MCS requires careful, structured, and dynamic evaluation.32 This process can be conceptualized in three sequential steps: Establish haemodynamic stability, assess candidacy and a clear indication for advanced therapies, and optimize to achieve operability. The recognize/rescue—optimize—stabilize—exit/de-escalation (ROSE) heuristic provides a useful framework for the management of patients with CS prior to d-MCS implantation.33

Timely rescue with escalation to temporary mechanical circulatory support (t-MCS) is often required for initial stabilization and to avoid irretrievable multi-organ failure in ‘Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support’ (INTERMACS) profile 1.34–36 In general, d-MCS implantation is typically advisable after stabilization to INTERMACS profile 3.37,38 INTERMACS profile 2 represents a grey zone with the need for an individualized decision to proceed with d-MCS vs t-MCS implantation vs further escalation of inotropes.38 Reality still shows that most d-MCS implantations are performed in INTERMACS 1 and 2 outside the propagated window of this statement and current guidelines.16,39 Early referral to AdvHF centres and multidisciplinary heart team discussions are crucial to align clinical feasibility, patient values, and realistic outcomes.40–42

After achieving haemodynamic stability, indication, and candidacy for exit strategies (d-MCS, prolonged inotropes/t-MCS, heart transplantation, or palliative care) are discussed. The time-limited access to transplantation under t-MCS in some allocation systems often accelerates decision-making towards d-MCS.43,44 Indications for d-MCS encompass several clinical scenarios, including de novo HF and acute-on-chronic HF after addressing treatable disease and conditions.40,45

Haemodynamic compromise markedly complicates evaluation for advanced therapies, particularly in patients supported on t-MCS under progressive risk of device-related complications beyond the first 7–10 days. This constrained timeframe can be mitigated by utilizing current-generation micro-axial flow pumps rather than veno-arterial extra-corporal life support (ECLS), provided their use is feasible for the specific patient profile.

Evaluation of biventricular function includes echocardiographic assessment and invasive haemodynamic monitoring after weaning from inotropes with flow adjustments of t-MCS, if applicable. Accurate assessment of right ventricular (RV) function is particularly challenging under t-MCS as altered pre-load, unloading conditions and device-related flows may mask intrinsic RV dysfunction. Upfront discussion of temporary right ventricular assist device (RVAD) implantation vs durable biventricular assist device is necessary, if weaning of mechanical RV support is unlikely and haemodynamic compromise is evident under t-MCS flow reduction. Ventricular arrhythmias (VA) and aortic valve regurgitation (AR), both affecting long-term prognosis on d-MCS, are also difficult to evaluate, as t-MCS may transiently improve arrhythmias while exacerbating or unmasking AR.44

The diagnostic workup of non-cardiac comorbidities should, in principle, not deviate from that of more stable patients, as explained below, but is often restricted to essential assessments. Frequently, this results in a suboptimal evaluation of malignancy, infection, frailty, and psychosocial contraindications in patients with stabilized CS with consecutive challenges in long-term care on d-MCS. Available diagnostics with reasonable risk profile for intensive care patients include computed tomography (CT) scan/ultrasound, laboratory workup including tumour markers and trajectory under ventricular unloading/haemodynamic stabilization, endoscopic evaluation, and (collateral) history of the patient, potentially provided by his caregivers. This places interdisciplinary patient assessment even more at the forefront, with a distinct focus on the potential reversibility of current end-organ dysfunction after d-MCS implantation.

Neurological evaluation and prognostication

Assessment of psychosocial factors, including readiness, medical adherence, mental health, substance use, living situation, and social support, is essential to predict long-term outcomes after advanced therapies and may be profoundly affected by neurological injury, altered mental status, mechanical ventilation, and sedation.46 Accordingly, preserved neurological function is a prerequisite for d-MCS implantation and neurological assessment is a multimodal and iterative task (Table 1).25,47,48 It should be noted, however, that recent Society of Thoracic Surgery INTERMACS data demonstrate that outcomes, in younger patients (<50 years old), are comparable between those with psychosocial risk factors and those without.49 Prior haemodynamic stabilization and clearance of confounding factors is crucial as timing of prognostication is critical. In cases of neurological uncertainty, cautious delay, and reassessment are preferred over premature implantation.25,50,51

Timing of durable mechanical circulatory support (specifics in cardiogenic shock patients INTERMACS profile 1–3)

Bridging of patients with CS from t-MCS to d-MCS is regarded as a high-risk endeavour. The 1-year survival in patients with CS who were bridged from ECLS to d-MCS was 53% in one study, but more recent registry data showed an overall 1-year survival rate of up to 80% for patients in CS.49,52 It is recognized that this represents a selected cohort of survivors of the initial phase of CS after careful selection.

In contrast to acute myocardial infarction-related CS, no prospective randomized data currently exist to evaluate the potential benefits of micro-axial flow pumps in HF-related CS, particularly as a bridge to d-MCS. Observational data suggest that when deployed in HF-related CS, survival after bridging to heart replacement therapies (LVAD or heart transplantation) is excellent, reaching up to 93.7% at 12 months, positioning these systems as the therapeutic option of choice in many centres, provided they align with the patient’s shock profile.53 High-flow devices provide superior haemodynamic support compared to lower-flow systems, resulting in improved morbidity, though without a significant impact on mortality.54 In Europe, bridging CS patients with an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is rare compared to other global regions. This practice is further supported by the recently published ALTSHOCK-2 trial, which specifically evaluated this concept in HF patients and found no clinical benefit associated with IABP treatment.55

Therapeutic optimization on t-MCS aims to improve oxygen delivery (to reverse organ dysfunction), minimize iatrogenesis, and provide supportive care (Figure 2). Features of optimization include liberation from respiratory support and sedation, diminution of vasopressor requirements, and improvement or normalization of lactate levels, kidney function, liver enzymes, bilirubin, and platelet count. These are associated with better ‘post-exit therapy’ outcomes56; consequently, planning for d-MCS at this stage is appropriate. In some cases, prehabilitation, including physiotherapy, nutrition, and psychology services, may improve candidacy for d-MCS and heart transplantation. Although ambulation is feasible, femoral cannulation significantly limits out-of-bed activities.57 Where prehabilitation is desirable, conversion to central or axillary t-MCS modalities are options to facilitate mobilization.58

End of life care

Withdrawal of t-MCS is a challenging decision, especially in awake patients, but is increasingly commonly observed.59 This decision requires a multidisciplinary team, including specialized palliative care teams in close communication with the patient and family.

Durable mechanical circulatory support in patients with advanced heart failure in the ambulatory setting (INTERMACS profile 4–7)

Worsening heart failure in the outpatient setting

Worsening HF as the gateway to AdvHF is defined as the aggravation of symptoms and clinical signs despite previously stable therapy, leading to decompensation and requiring an intensification of treatment.60 This phase of disease progression is often marked by HF-related hospitalizations, unscheduled emergency room visits or outpatient consultations necessitating intravenous diuretics or an escalation of oral diuretic doses. Worsening HF represents a critical inflection point where the risks of clinical deterioration and mortality significantly increase corresponding to the American Heart Association Stage C2D.42,61 Timely referral to an AdvHF centre is advisable before the onset of irreversible secondary end-organ damage, most notably renal, hepatic, and pulmonary manifestations or increased pulmonary vascular resistance (Figure 3).12,61 Worsening events triggered primarily by inadequate therapy, dietary indiscretion, or medication non-adherence do not necessarily warrant such a transfer. Given the emergence of specific clinical trials for this patient population, proactive optimization of medical therapy is of utmost importance. This includes nuanced diuretic decongestion, optimized by implementation of telemedicine or telemonitoring, cardiac rehabilitation and the potential addition of therapies such as vericiguat or cardiac glycosides.62–65 Severity of HF tends to be underestimated in women and young patients, especially if the dynamics of disease progression are accelerated by genetic predisposition (Table 2).7

Indication and assessment of durable mechanical circulatory support (non-inotrope-dependent patients)

The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines provide a unanimous definition for AdvHF and the window for d-MCS evaluation, as illustrated in Figure 4.61 In patients meeting the AdvHF criteria, all-cause death exceeds 40% within 1 year.66 Despite this clarity, referral in clinical practice remains frequently delayed or misguided. Several factors can contribute to this discrepancy: the misinterpretation of clinical signs, a focus on symptoms rather than disease progression, and a lack of familiarity with the formal referral process.1,40,41 In AdvHF, moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation (MR) is often an inherent manifestation of the disease progression rather than primary or out-of-proportion valvular disease. Nevertheless, clinical referrals are frequently directed towards valve intervention rather than the underlying HF itself, with evaluation of advanced therapy options. While selected patients with AdvHF may benefit from valve intervention, the residual mortality risk remains substantial; even in an optimal post-procedural scenario, this risk exceeds the mortality rates associated with contemporary d-MCS devices.67–69 A similar pattern emerges among patients with VA, if the focus often shifts away from the underlying cardiomyopathy. Ventricular tachycardias display a distinct risk factor that is often missed, although it carries a mortality risk of 55% at 1 year after failed ablation in patients with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of <33%.70 These clinical parameters (recurrent hospital admissions, worsening renal function, and decreased tolerability of neurohormonal therapy) alongside the high-risk features highlighted in this manuscript (such as moderate-to-severe MR and VAs) and the loss of lean body mass align with the ‘rule of three’ paradigm introduced by Mehra et al. This framework underscores the critical triggers that should prompt timely referral and transfer to an AdvHF centre.19

Group 2 pulmonary hypertension (PH) presents another clinical scenario reflecting this pattern. In patients with elevated diastolic pulmonary artery pressures (dPAP) estimated by implantable devices, a lack of decrease in dPAP within 6 months is associated with a 26.7% increase in all-cause mortality.71 A clinical trial evaluating this specific setting is currently underway (www.clinicaltrials.gov: NCT06526195).72

With elevated filling pressures, impaired secondary organ function as well as impaired cardiac output, AdvHF shares distinct clinical features with CS. This clinical framework is supported by pathophysiological considerations of primed shock in non-ischaemic HF patients, which further underlines the high-risk setting, warranting immediate evaluation and therapy.73,74 Therapeutic nihilism regarding elderly patients remains prevalent yet unjustified. Current registry data reveal a relevant underrepresentation of this cohort, despite their excellent clinical outcomes; notably, 5-year survival for ≥ 65-year-old patients reaches 51% following d-MCS implantation.16

The pathophysiology of RHF in d-MCS candidates often stems from (longstanding) biventricular involvement or PH type II.88,89 Over time, RV afterload stress results in remodelling, dilation, and subsequently failure.87 Left ventricular (LV) unloading via a d-MCS can exacerbate RHF primarily through septal shift and secondarily through increased RV pre-load. This may lead to early (within 30 days) or late-onset RHF.90 Conversely, it may improve RV function by restoring pre-load and reducing RV afterload. Thorough pre-operative evaluation and risk assessment of post-operative RHF remain essential. Evaluation begins with clinical assessment, focusing on signs of systemic congestion, low-output states, and laboratory biomarkers. Imaging and invasive haemodynamic measurement forms the cornerstone of prediction (Table 4). Patients who develop early RHF typically present with higher right atrial pressures (RAP), elevated RAP to pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ratios, a lower pulmonary artery pulsatility index, and more severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), alongside biochemical markers of end-organ stress such as elevated creatinine and bilirubin. The often subtle nature of these abnormalities suggests that attempts to entirely prevent RHF might inadvertently exclude a significant number of viable d-MCS candidates.78 In cases where diagnostic evaluation remains inconclusive, LV unloading via t-MCS can facilitate decision-making by simulating RV performance under conditions of restored pre-load and reduced afterload. Despite detailed pre-operative evaluation, clinical focus should also be directed towards proactive strategies that mitigate the severity of RHF and optimize its management as soon as it manifests. Current evidence identifies both peri-operative and post-operative interventions to reduce RHF incidence, including the use of thoracotomy with pericardial preservation, the minimization of blood products, and meticulous pump speed titration to prevent septal shifting. Post-operatively, the implementation of standardized treatment protocols, incorporating inhaled nitric oxide and the precise management of pre-load and contractility, remains essential to optimizing RV function and reducing the risk of severe failure. Research consistently demonstrates that patients undergoing planned RVAD implantation achieve superior outcomes compared to those requiring an unplanned, emergent rescue intervention. Future research to predict RHF post-d-MCS aims to refine predictive models incorporating machine learning and biomarkers as well as markers of pulmonary artery stiffening and RV performance scores.91,92

Pre-operative valvular heart diseases

d-MCS implantation in patients with concomitant valvular heart disease is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, particularly when clinically significant lesions are left uncorrected.25,93 Data from the MOMENTUM trial indicate that concomitant valvular procedures, are associated with higher peri-operative morbidity.94 This includes longer cardiopulmonary bypass time and increased rates of bleeding, stroke, and RHF without adversely affecting short- or long-term survival compared with isolated LVAD implantation.94 These findings support a comprehensive assessment and selective, multidisciplinary planning of an individualized approach to concomitant valve intervention, balancing peri-operative risk against anticipated haemodynamic benefit and d-MCS weaning potential.25,95,96

Aortic valve

Moderate or greater AR is present in up to 15% of d-MCS candidates and frequently progresses after implantation due to continuous-flow physiology, reduced aortic valve opening, commissural fusion, and leaflet fibrosis.97 Risk factors for development of AR during t-MCS with micro-axial flow pumps are length of support and device size.98 Significant AR leads to recirculating flow, which compromises both ventricular unloading and the overall efficiency of d-MCS. A thorough evaluation to determine the necessity of correcting moderate or greater AR prior to or during device implantation is essential. Evidence from the International Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (IMACS) suggests that concomitant aortic valve procedures do not increase mortality risk, provided that precise diagnosis and meticulous operative planning are performed.99 Transcatheter aortic valve replacement is feasible, especially if AR is aggravated after chest closure or in the post-operative period.25,100 Aortic valve stenosis is generally well-tolerated following d-MCS implantation, as the device bypasses the native outflow tract. In most centres, the standard approach involves replacing existing mechanical aortic valve prostheses with bioprostheses during d-MCS implantation, case reports have described transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures performed within the remaining surgical ring after leaflet excision.25,101

Atrio-ventricular valves

In AdvHF patients, MR is typically functional and frequently resolves following d-MCS implantation as a result of LV unloading and subsequent reverse remodelling. Consequently, routine mitral valve intervention is generally unnecessary, unless the regurgitation is symptomatic or the patient is considered a candidate for potential d-MCS weaning.25,94 Moderate-to-severe TR can be addressed at the time of d-MCS implantation to improve forward flow and reduce RV volume overload.25 However, surgical correction does not reduce the incidence of RHF post-d-MCS, as shown in prospective trials.94 Severe mitral stenosis and tricuspid stenosis are rare findings and correction during d-MCS implantation can optimize haemodynamic conditions for both ventricles.25 Routine replacement of a normally functioning mechanical mitral prosthesis is typically avoided due to the increased surgical complexity and the high likelihood that transmitral flow will improve following d-MCS implantation. Retaining the mechanical valve may necessitate a higher international normalized ration (INR) target.25

C3c transcatheter edge-to-edge repair for mitral and tricuspid valve

Mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) improves symptoms and decreases HF hospitalizations in patients with secondary MR in the setting of significant LV systolic dysfunction. Nevertheless, both the 5-year follow-up of the COAPT trial and registry studies confirm that M-TEER may delay but not prevent progression to d-MCS in patients with AdvHF.67,102 Recent data from a retrospective multi-centre study suggest that leaving an M-TEER clip in place during d-MCS surgery is safe. Overall, in-hospital mortality was 11%, rising to 40% in the patients in whom the clip was removed. Notably, residual mitral gradient <5 mmHg was present in the majority of retained clip patients.103 While further larger and prospective studies are needed, prior M-TEER does not appear to be a contraindication for d-MCS implantation with no evidence of adverse impact on effective LV unloading.

Evidence regarding tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) prior to d-MCS implantation remains scarce. While emerging data from a broader HF population with symptomatic moderate-to-severe TR suggest potential 3-year benefits in quality of life and HF hospitalizations, these findings must be interpreted with caution due to the limitations of unblinded trial designs.104 Considering the adverse prognostic impact of pre-existing moderate-to-severe TR, coupled with its development or exacerbation following d-MCS implantation, T-TEER may represent a viable therapeutic target in highly selected patients, specifically those in whom severe TR is not merely a manifestation of irreversible RHF.25 Case reports have recently demonstrated feasibility and safety of T-TEER after d-MCS implantation.105

Supraventricular arrhythmias

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is reaching a prevalence of 50% in New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class IV patients.106 Patients with pre-operative permanent AF develop RHF earlier and show lower survival at 2-year follow-up after d-MCS implantation, as altered ventricular rates and the lack of atrial contraction can mitigate RHF after d-MCS implantation.107 Catheter ablation in combination with medical therapy was shown to be beneficial over medical therapy alone in patients with AdvHF and symptomatic AF in the single-centre, randomized, open-label CASTLE-HTX trial.108 Further investigation is warranted due to the early termination of the trial, the limited maintenance of sinus rhythm post-ablation (5%), and the high prevalence of paroxysmal AF (32%).109

Cardiac implantable electronic devices and ventricular arrhythmias

VA are estimated to be present in up to 33% of chronic ambulatory HF patients, and 39% of the patients experienced VAs requiring internal cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) therapy during the year prior to d-MCS implantation.110–112 A comprehensive genetic screening is indicated for patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and VA, see Table 2. Pathogenic mutations are present in up to 55% of DCM cases. Post-implantation clinical stability may be compromised by a patient’s genetic predisposition. Carrying a disproportionately high risk for malignant arrhythmias, it can interact with extrinsic peri-operative factors to alter the recovery trajectory. Catheter ablation is the established primary treatment for VA in ischaemic cardiomyopathy or following failed medical therapy in DCM.70,113 Even following successful ablation, this patient population retains an exceptionally high mortality risk, particularly those with severely impaired LVEF. Such cases should immediately trigger an evaluation for AdvHF therapies, including d-MCS or transplantation.70 For patients with significant pre-operative VAs, performing ablation prior to d-MCS implantation is generally preferred to avoid the heightened technical challenges associated with peri- or post-implantation procedures. In selected patients intraoperative ablation is feasible. Cardiac implantable electronic devices are present in more than two-thirds of d-MCS patients.114 In those who are being assessed for d-MCS therapy without an ICD already in situ, it is not essential to implant an ICD particularly in the absence of VA.25 Given that d-MCS patients typically exhibit greater haemodynamic tolerance to VAs, ICD programming is generally conservative, prioritizing the minimization of shocks over immediate rhythm conversion.25,115,116 Subcutaneous ICDs were associated with relatively frequent electromagnetic interference (70%) and inappropriate shocks (20%) in a multi-centre study of 30 patients with concomitant d-MCS. Through careful reprogramming, 90% of patients remained eligible for ongoing subcutaneous ICD therapy.117 A meta-analysis involving 1157 d-MCS recipients (575 with cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT)) demonstrated no significant differences in mortality, hospitalization, or arrhythmic outcomes associated with active left-sided pacing.115 Guidelines suggest considering the deactivation of the LV lead to prolong the device’s battery life, as the clinical benefit of CRT is often superseded by d-MCS.25 This strategy is particularly relevant as generator replacement in this population is associated with significantly higher complication rates.116

Evaluation of non-cardiac comorbidities

Pulmonary disease

Chronic obstructive lung disease is a common comorbidity in AdvHF patients. Pulmonary function testing is essential prior to d-MCS implantation, whenever possible in an euvolemic state. Bedside testing for bedridden patients is not a feasible alternative due to poor result reproducibility. Instead, a high-resolution CT scan of the lungs to rule out morphological abnormalities of the pulmonary tissue can be performed. Saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and diffusing capacity of the long for carbon monoxide (DLCO) are important markers for prognostication before cardiac surgery: SpO2 < 93% at rest, DLCO ≤ 50%, and FEV1 ≤ 50% marking thresholds for further investigation according to the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines.25,118 In patients with elevated pulmonary artery pressures not solely attributable to Group 2 PH, as well as those with structural lung disease such as pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonological consultation and individualized therapeutic concepts are essential. This approach is necessary to delineate the primary underlying pathology and effectively alleviate the patient's symptoms. Pulmonary restriction without pulmonary fibrosis, i.e. in patients with diaphragmatic elevation, constitutes a challenging situation, since intrathoracic d-MCS may aggravate the restrictive physiology. The comparative benefits for these patients often remain unclear, necessitating interdisciplinary elucidation prior to implantation. Outpatient oxygen therapy, often initiated for palliative dyspnoea management, requires a thorough diagnostic workup if HF is the suspected cause. If a primary pulmonary indication for oxygen supplementation is established, the suitability for d-MCS therapy must be carefully re-evaluated, as this poses a relative contraindication depending on disease dynamics.

Chronic kidney disease

Chronic kidney disease with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 is an important, yet common comorbidity. Urea and sodium are the only two pre-operative serological renal parameters found to be predictive of mortality within a prospective, randomized d-MCS cohort.30 To estimate the prognosis after d-MCS implantation and degree of contraindication, it is important to distinguish prerenal kidney dysfunction due to low cardiac output and/or venous congestion from intrinsic kidney damage. To evaluate the potential for reversibility, strategies include optimizing haemodynamics using positive inotropic agents or t-MCS, where applicable. Further assessment includes urine analysis and quantification of proteinuria to evaluate the severity of chronic kidney disease and potential glomerular damage, complemented by imaging to assess kidney size.119 Those patients with other comorbidities related to intrinsic kidney damage such as diabetes, hypertension, and atherosclerosis do not show full recovery in case of nephron loss, despite normal eGFR. Rather than excluding patients with non-severe renal impairment from d-MCS candidacy, impaired renal function, and clinical risk scores are used to guide shared decision-making regarding projected outcomes with the patient and their caregivers.31 Chronic renal replacement therapies are regarded as an absolute contraindication in many centres, with 1-year mortality after d-MCS implantation exceeding 35%.120

Liver disease

Patients with AdvHF or shock frequently have hepatic dysfunction, which often improves with haemodynamic optimization before and after d-MCS implantation, but states a risk factor for post-operative RHF, bleeding, and vasoplegia.121 It can be related to the (chronic) congestive state with higher right-sided pressures in the case of RV dysfunction and/or ischaemic hepatitis caused by severe LV dysfunction, however, intrinsic liver diseases and irreversible fibrotic conversion need to be excluded.121 Pre-operative conditioning involves decongestion and haemodynamic optimization, correction of synthetic anomalies to prevent bleeding and post-operative vasoplegia and optimization of nutritional status.121 After optimization and decongestion, the Model of End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) or MELD XI score ≥13 points as well as a fibroscan of the liver (≥13 kPa) are able to distinguish degree of contraindication patients in this setting: morphological evidence of overt liver cirrhosis precludes d-MCS implantation.25,122–126 In patients with pre-operative vitamin k antagonists, the MELD XI score may be more reliable, as the MELD score is INR dependent.127

Coagulopathies

Haemocompatibility-related adverse events (HRAEs) decreased significantly with current-generation d-MCS devices and declined further with the avoidance of aspirin in patients with fully magnetically-levitated d-MCS managed on vitamin k antagonists.128,129 Congenital (e.g. factor V Leiden mutation) and acquired (e.g. heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, antiphospholipid syndrome, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura) coagulopathies have been recognized as contributors to HRAEs and early mortality.130,131 Assessment for coagulopathy includes the identification of reversible causes, comprehensive coagulation testing, and haematological consultation. Severe coagulopathies may constitute a relative contraindication to d-MCS depending on clinical severity, bleeding history, and anticipated complexity of peri-operative management.

Infectious disease

Active infection prior to d-MCS is associated with increased risk for infection and mortality following device implantation. Pre-operative colonization with multidrug-resistant organisms is associated with increased rates of post-operative infections and significantly influences the selection of peri-operative antimicrobial prophylaxis.132,133 d-MCS-specific infections often involve skin-related species (Staphylococcus spp., P. aeruginosa, and Candida spp.), therefore routine colonization screening is essential to guide preventive strategies.134 A comprehensive history, physical examination, and individualized approach are warranted to exclude pre-operative infection or colonization in candidates. If either is confirmed, the addition of an infectious disease specialist to the AdvHF team provides significant value to address this risk factor.25 Well-controlled chronic viral infections, such as human immunodeficiency virus, do not constitute a contraindication to d-MCS implantation.135

Malignant disease

A history of malignancy is present in 6.5% of patients undergoing d-MCS implantation.136 Cancer is associated with a modestly increased risk of bleeding and mortality after d-MCS.136 Post-d-MCS outcomes vary significantly depending on malignancy type, staging, timing and modality of oncologic therapy, and potential interactions with d-MCS-related management.137 A life expectancy of <2 years due to active malignancy has been proposed as the threshold for d-MCS ineligibility, but d-MCS is a feasible strategy in patients with malignant disease with, but unclear remission status and a life expectancy >2 years, as heart transplantation is absolutely contraindicated in this situation.25 Multidisciplinary input, especially from oncology, haematology, and surgery, is essential for accurate prognostication and informed decision-making.

Diabetes mellitus

At the time of d-MCS implantation, over 40% of patients have a history of diabetes. Although not an absolute contraindication for d-MCS therapy, it constitutes a risk factor as long-term outcomes are impaired by poor glycaemic control. If already present, extensive diabetic end-organ involvement can be regarded as a contraindication to d-MCS therapy. Diabetes is associated with impaired wound healing and predisposes the d-MCS recipient to a higher infection risk, including device-related infections.25

Adiposity and nutrition

Obesity and malnutrition are highly prevalent in patients with AdvHF. Higher body mass index (BMI) is associated with worse outcomes after d-MCS implantation, when paired with sarcopenia.138 International guidelines suggest obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) is not an absolute contraindication for d-MCS, but many implanting centres will restrict d-MCS implantation to patients with BMI < 35 kg/m2, due to the association with high rates of device-related infections and RHF in obese patients.139 It must be noted that the BMI fails to account for individual body composition, and no prospective evaluation of a specific BMI cut-off has been performed to date. In obese patients with AdvHF, pre-operative weight optimization via diet, exercise, or pharmacological intervention is a priority, whenever feasible.140 However, while this optimization remains a critical target, these goals are achieved in only a minority of patients. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have demonstrated significant weight reduction and improved cardiovascular outcomes in broader HF populations. It has to be stated that there is a strong demand for further data regarding medical interventions, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, specifically within the context of AdvHF and d-MCS patients. Without robust clinical trial data, it remains unclear whether GLP-1 receptor agonists, which are known to induce muscle loss and prolong gastric transit time, can effectively improve clinical outcomes, given that many patients with AdvHF present with sarcopenic obesity and polypharmacy dependency.138

Frailty and age

Frailty assessment is essential in evaluating candidates for d-MCS, as it independently predicts long-term mortality and prolonged hospital stay after implantation.141,142 Registry data indicate that, in carefully selected older patients, post-implant survival may approach that of younger cohorts, supporting frailty assessment rather than age-based exclusion.143 A multidomain evaluation covers physical status (Fried criteria), cognition, nutrition, social frailty, and resilience factors.77,144 Reversible aspects of frailty can be addressed by a structured prehabilitation.145

Psychiatric and psychological disorders and substance abuse

Although pre-existing psychosocial risk factors are known to predict poorer adherence and clinical outcomes following d-MCS, current data support the use of d-MCS implantation, particularly in younger patients, even when such risks are present.146 Comprehensive psychosocial evaluation is a cornerstone of the pre-implantation workup. This assessment addresses treatment adherence, mental health, active substance abuse, and cognitive capacity to ensure a thorough understanding of treatment options, device operation, and the adequacy of social support.25 Use of tools developed for transplantation candidates may also help identify patients at higher risk for adverse outcomes after d-MCS implantation.147,148 Alteration of mental status due to low cardiac output must be ruled out during evaluation, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment is an easy and reliable evaluation tool in this setting.146,149 Candidates with unresolved concerns in any of these domains present a significant contraindication to d-MCS implantation. In such cases, further evaluation is indicated. For patients who undergo d-MCS implantation despite psychosocial challenges, comprehensive post-operative support is essential.

Sex-specific aspects

Women with AdvHF represent a notably underrecognized subgroup within the population eligible for d-MCS. Despite a considerable disease burden, women have long been underrepresented in HF trials and device registries, resulting in limited sex-specific evidence to guide clinical management.150–153 Although symptom burden is often comparable or even greater, women with AdvHF are less likely to receive timely referral for advanced therapies. Early experience suggested higher complication rates—including stroke, RHF, bleeding, and psychosocial distress, compared with men.151,153,154 An additional consideration is that women with smaller body surface and therefore with unfavourable pump size-ratio pose an additional concern, as these patients have a predisposition to pain caused by local irritation of the ribs. However, with advances in pump design and multidisciplinary care, contemporary data now demonstrate comparable survival and event-free outcomes in women as that for men.15,155

Adults with congenital heart disease

With a relative proportion of .7%–1.6% of all d-MCS patients, only a minority of adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) require d-MCS support.156,157 The proportion of ACHD supported with a d-MCS before heart transplantation relative to all adults with ACHD who needed a heart transplantation has increased from below 4% to nearly 15%. However, it is key to note that data are limited to registries with up to 128 patients.156–160

It has been identified that d-MCS are implanted in patients with biventricular circulation with a morphologically systemic LV (relative frequency 52%), biventricular circulation with a morphologically RV (34%), and univentricular heart (13%).157 In almost all cases, intracorporeal centrifugal VADs have been used to support systemic circulation. Subpulmonary support alone or in combination with a systemic d-MCS in univentricular hearts is a rare exception.159,161 Typical risk factors for d-MCS support include multiple previous sternotomies, dextrocardia, transposition of the great arteries, and trabecularization of a systemic RV.

In biventricular circulation, timing of d-MCS is similar to patients with normal heart anatomy, whereas univentricular hearts have specific indications such as protein-losing enteropathy or plastic bronchitis. ACHD patients on d-MCS support tend to be younger, less likely to have a high BMI, more likely to have pulmonary vascular disease, and allosensitization.157

Reported 12-month mortality risk is 27.6%–33.3% with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.98 (95% confidence interval (95% CI) 1.35–2.91) compared to adults with acquired heart disease.156,157,159 Patients over 50 years of age and those who require a biventricular VAD or a total artificial heart are at particularly high risk.157 While an increased bleeding risk (HR 2.1 (95% CI 1.40–3.16)) has been described, other adverse event rates as well as improvements in functional capacity and quality of life seem to be comparable to adults with acquired heart disease on d-MCS support.156,157

Conclusion

This expert opinion should provide a clinically actionable and contemporary roadmap for the implementation of d-MCS through the evolving landscape of AdvHF and CS management. The document emphasizes a multidisciplinary team approach to ensure precise patient selection and holistic care. It highlights that while technological advancements have improved 5-year survival rates to 60%, successful outcomes depend heavily on early referral, careful pre-operative evaluation and optimization, as well as the management of complex comorbidities.
