Modulation of Neuronal Mitochondrial Injury by Gualou Guizhi Decoction in a Mouse Ischemia-Reperfusion Model
Authors: Ri Xu, Lixu Wu, Hongmei Wang, Linye Zhao, Dongxu Yin, Mengyuan Dai, Kunqiang Yu, Minya Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Objective: To investigate the effect and mechanism of Guilou Guizhi decoction (GLGZD) on CI/R injury.Materials and Methods: A middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) mouse model was used to mimic ischemic stroke. GLGZD was administered by gavage, and the PI3K pathway inhibitor LY294002 was injected intraperitoneally. Neurological deficits, infarct volume, brain edema, and neuronal survival were assessed using mNSS scoring, TTC, HE, Nissl, and TUNEL staining. Mitochondrial function was evaluated by measuring membrane potential, ATP, and ROS levels. Western blot detected Cytc release, apoptosis markers, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation.Results: After GLGZD intervention, mNSS score, infarct area, and cerebral tissue water content were dramatically lowered in MCAO/R mice, histopathological damage and neuronal apoptosis in the cerebral cortex and CA1 region were significantly improved. Additionally, the mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP content were elevated, the level of ROS was lowered, the release of Cytc into the cytoplasm was reduced, and the level of neuronal apoptosis-related proteins. Furthermore, GLGZD therapy dramatically increased the phosphorylation status of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway molecules, and the pathway inhibitor LY294002 effectively reduced GLGZD’s ameliorative effect on a variety of lesions in MCAO/R mice brain tissues.Conclusion: GLGZD restores mitochondrial function via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to alleviate neuronal apoptosis in MCAO/R mice, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for ischemic stroke.

1. Background

Stroke is a common cerebrovascular disease caused by the rupture or obstruction of blood arteries, causing inadequate local blood flow and oxygen supply to the brain tissue and impaired function of the local brain tissue. It has the characteristics of high morbidity, high lethality, high disability, and high recurrence rate, and it forms one of the top causes of death and long-term impairment among residents around the world ( 1 ). The incidence rate of stroke in China has kept rising, accounting for nearly 1/4 of global instances, and around 86.8% of stroke patients over 40 years of age have ischemic stroke ( 2 - 4 ). Ischemic stroke patients frequently experience sequelae such as hemiplegia and aphasia, which place a significant burden on patients and their families. Currently, the primary treatment strategy for ischemic cerebrovascular illness is to unblock occluded blood arteries as soon as feasible, administer intravenous thrombolysis, and restore blood circulation to ischemic brain regions. Although cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (CI/R) can regulate dying brain cells and relieve infarction, it will also exacerbate the damage to ischemic cells, resulting in cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, which will cause damage to various organs. To increase the therapeutic effect of ischemic stroke patients, it is vital to look for neuroprotective medications that can reduce post-ischemic brain tissue damage while also promoting functional recovery.

Mitochondria are closely related with CI/R injury, and aberrant mitochondrial function is a major contributor to cellular damage ( 5 ). It is well understood that the primary problem of cerebral ischemia is the absence of nutrients like oxygen and glucose in neurons of the ischemic regions, and mitochondrial respiration requires the participation of oxygen to break down glucose and release energy, which is the major site of ATP generation and the metabolism area of ROS, which maintains the steady level of ROS production and consumption ( 6 ). Studies have revealed that targeted prevention of ROS-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis can reduce CI/R injury ( 7 ), additionally, improving mitochondrial activity can considerably raise intracellular ATP levels, thereby mitigating a variety of brain lesions caused by hypoxia/reoxygenation ( 8 ). Therefore, improving mitochondrial activity may be a viable way to prevent the likelihood of reperfusion injury in patients.

Phosphoinositide-3 kinase (P13K)/serine/threonine kinase (Akt) is a well-known anti-apoptotic signaling pathway during apoptosis, as well as a critical signaling route that supports neuronal cell repair and survival following ischemia injury. Multiple research investigations have discovered that activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis promotes neuronal recovery after CI/R ( 9 - 12 ). Chinese medicine extracts have recently been a popular research topic among medical professionals due to their diverse origins, safety and efficacy, and minimal toxicity and adverse effects. Numerous studies have shown that traditional medicines, such as Chinese herbal remedies, possess multi-target and holistic regulatory advantages, demonstrating unique efficacy in neuroprotection and functional recovery ( 13 ). Traditional medicine treatments for cerebral ischemia are stable and long-lasting, and can significantly reduce drug side effects and adverse reactions ( 14 ). Integrating traditional medicine with modern medical approaches offers new perspectives for the treatment of ischemic stroke. Gualou Guizhi decoction (GLGZD), a Chinese drug in China, is commonly served as alleviate muscle spasms after stroke, epilepsy, or spinal cord injury. It exerts neuroprotective effects against focal CI/R by modulating numerous antioxidants and anti-excitotoxic pathways ( 15 , 16 ). Some research suggests that GLGZD may treat coronary artery disease by activating the PI3K-Akt pathway ( 17 ), however, it is unclear whether GLGZD can mediate this pathway to reduce CI/R injury. By clarifying the mechanism by which GLGZD improves mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, it may provide a scientific basis for the development of new therapeutic strategies and support the clinical application of GLGZD in the treatment of ischemic stroke.

2. Objectives

The current study was designed to investigate the molecular mechanisms of mitochondrial damage and neuronal apoptosis in mice following MCAO/R of blood supply by GLGZD via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, with apoptosis of neurons after CI/R serving as an entry point, in order to provide a basic scientific foundation for its clinical use.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Laboratory Animals and Breeding

Vital River provided 30 healthy C57BL/6 J mice (body weight 24~26 g). Mice were kept in the laboratory at a temperature of (24±2) °C and humidity of (50±2) % in accordance with the natural rhythm. They were fed normal dietary standards for a week to acclimatise. Mice with suitable body weights and conditions were chosen for subsequent experiments. The experiments followed the Guidelines for the Management and Use of Laboratory Animal Feeding issued by the Lishui Second Hospital, Wenzhou Medical University Ethics Committee.

3.2. GLGZD Preparation

GLGZD is composed of Trichosanthes kirilowii, Cinnamomum cassiae, Paeonia lactiflora, Glycyrrhiza glabra, Ginger, and Jujubae , with the proportions of each herb being 30:9:15:6:9:30 ( 18 ). The GLGZD extract was decocted twice with distilled water for 1 hour each time. The combined decoctions were then concentrated to a final concentration of 0.78 g.mL-1.

3.3. Mice MCAO/R Model Construction and Grouping

The MCAO/R model was prepared by modified thread-occlusion technique, and the mice were allowed to drink freely, and were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% avertin following a 12-hour fast. The modified thread bolus approach was used to produce the model. The mice were placed supine on the operating table, shaved, and sterilized. The skin was then incised obliquely at a point 3 mm away from the neck’s midline, exposing the right common carotid artery. The subcutaneous tissues were then separated to reveal the artery, and then the vagus nerve and the internal and external carotid arteries. Finally, the proximal ends of the external and common carotid arteries were tied off, and the internal carotid artery was pinch off. Prepare a slipknot wire, make a small diagonal incision in the common carotid artery 4 mm from the bifurcation, and incrementally push the body weight-corresponding bolus down the carotid artery into the internal carotid artery to approximately 15 mm of depth. Stop when the bolus encounters resistance, indicating that it has entered the middle cerebral artery successfully. Finally, pull the live knot tightly to stabilize the bolus. The CI/R model was set up by cautiously opening the ligature and removing the bolus outward to accomplish reperfusion following 2 hours of ischemia. After the mice were awoken from anesthesia, a Zea-Longa score (0-4) ( 19 ) was completed, with a 1~3 indicating that the MCAO/R animals have been effectively modeled and included in subsequent investigations.

Mice with the MCAO/R model were randomly divided into three groups: the MCAO/R group, the GLGZD group, and the GLGZD+LY294002 group. In addition, a sham-operated (Sham) group was established, in which mice underwent the same surgical procedure without occlusion of the cerebral artery. Rats in the MCAO model group underwent ischemic surgery but did not receive GLGZD treatment.Mice in the MCAO/R group were only surgically modeled. Mice in the GLGZD and GLGZD+LY294002 groups were administered GLGZD (7.8 g.kg-1) and LY294002 (PI3K inhibitor, 10 mg.kg-1, HY-10108, MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) by continuous gavage for 7 days and intraperitoneally for 3 days, respectively, beginning on the 1st postoperative day.

3.4. mNSS Score

The modified neurological deficit score (mNSS) was utilized to evaluate the neurological deficits in mice on days 1, 3, and 7 following CI/R modeling. The mNSS scores ranged from 0 to 18, and included motor (muscle status and abnormal movement), sensory (vision, touch, and proprioception), response, and balance tests. The higher the neurological function level score, the more severe the impairment ( 20 ): 0 for normal mice, 1~6, 7~12 and 13~18 were for mild, moderate and severe impairment, respectively.

3.5. TTC Staining

After 7 days of modeling, the entire brain tissue was extracted, frozen at -20 °C for 20 minutes, and sectioned into slices (5-6 slices in each group). These slices were then immersed in 2% TTC (17779, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 minutes away from light. The slices were flipped over during the incubation time and then soaked in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours. Photographs were obtained, and infarcted lesions were examined. The results were reported as infarct volume/whole brain volume ×100%.

3.6. Brain Tissue Water Content Assay

After 7 days of MCAO/R construction, the mice were slaughtered, and the entire brain was removed and weighed for wet weight (WW). The brain was then dried at 100 °C for 24 hours and weighed for dry weight (DW). The water content of brain tissue was determined using (WW-DW)/DW×100%.

3.7. HE Staining

Brain tissues were cut into coronal slices and soaked in 4% paraformaldehyde for overnight fixation. They were subsequently dried, embedded, and produced as paraffin sections of 5 µm thickness. The sections were first deparaffinized with xylene before being dehydrated with gradient ethanol. Then the HE staining test kit (G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China) steps were followed for operation.Hematoxylin staining for 2 minutes, rinsing for 10 minutes, differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 3 minutes, eosin staining for 1 minute, rinsing and air-drying, followed by mounting with neutral balsam (G8590, Solarbio). The lesions in the cortical and CA1 areas of the hippocampus were examined using a light microscope (BX43, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

3.8. Nissl Staining

Paraffin slices of mice brain tissue were stained with 1% toluidine blue (G3668, Solarbio) at 60 °C for 30 minutes. Clearing treatment included ethanol gradient dehydration and xylene, which were subsequently sealed with neutral gum. Finally, the loss of Nissl bodies in the cortical area of the tissue and the CA1 area of the hippocampus was seen and photographed using a light microscope.

3.9. Immunofluorescence

The fixed coronal slices were thirsty, incorporated, and produced as 5 µm thick paraffin sections. The portions were dewaxed, rehydrated, and repaired using a microwave oven. The repaired slices were air dried, blocked with BSA for 30 minutes, and incubated overnight with Neun (1:1000, ab288715, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) primary antibody at 4 °C. After incubation with secondary antibody (1:5000, ab150079, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at room temperature for 1 hour, the slices were stained with Tunel and DAPI using the C1091 and C1002 assay kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Sections were sealed after staining, and the staining of tissue sections was identified.

3.10. Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Detection

Brain tissue from the infarcted side was collected, and mitochondria were isolated using a tissue mitochondrial isolation kit (C3606, Beyotime). After 24 hours of reperfusion, the brain cortex on the ischemic side was separated on ice, weighed, and cut into pieces before adding mitochondrial isolation solution. To get purified mitochondria, make a homogenate (tissue: mitochondrial isolation solution A=1:10) and centrifuge at 4 °C for 5 minutes. Aspirate the supernatant into another centrifuge tube and centrifuge again for 10 minutes. Following that, the JC-1 staining working solution was diluted and reacted with the extracted mitochondria for 20 minutes under light protection, dripped onto slides and covered with coverslips, and then the fluorescence changes were observed and images were captured using a fluorescence inverted microscope. Red fluorescence indicates higher membrane potential, while green indicates lower potential (depolarization, JC-1 monomer).

3.11. ATP Detection

Using the ATP assay kit (S0026, Beyotime) and referring to the instruction manual, mitochondrial lysate was added to the extracted mitochondria, the supernatant of the lysate was collected by centrifugation, the substrate solution was added, the chemiluminescence intensity of the solution was detected by a chemiluminescence analyzer (2805880, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and the standard curve was used to calculate the concentration of mitochondrial ATP (mtATP).

3.12. Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) Assay

The isolated mitochondria were suspended, and the mtROS concentration was measured by adding a working solution of fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (C400, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and incubation at 37 °C for 20 minutes. mtROS can oxidize DCFH to fluorescent dichlorofluorescein (DCF), and mtROS level correlates favorably with DCF conversion rate ( 21 ). The Ex/Em=488 nm/525 nm was set, and DCF fluorescence intensity (Fold) was evaluated using a fluorescent enzyme marker, with the findings reported as F.mg-1 protein.

3.13. Western Blot Analysis

Lateral brain tissue was promptly detached from the brain on ice, put to the corresponding amount of RIPA lysis solution including PMSF (P0013, Beyotime), and then thoroughly ground, centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 minutes, and the supernatant was aspirated, containing the entire protein of the tissue. To acquire precipitated tissue samples, the infarcted lateral brain tissue was broken and put to pre-cooled PBS, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded after 30 minutes in an ice bath. The tissue was digested with pre-cooled trypsin, resuspended in mitochondrial isolation reagent A, and centrifuged to produce the tissue precipitate. After centrifugation at 4 °C, the supernatant was aspirated into another centrifuge tube and centrifuged again to obtain the precipitate and supernatant. The precipitate obtained was the isolated mitochondria, which were lysed by adding mitochondrial lysate containing 1% PMSF, and then centrifuged at 4 °C to aspirate the supernatant for detection of the mitochondrial proteins. The supernatant obtained was centrifuged again at 4 °C, and then the precipitate was discarded to detect cytosolic proteins. Brain tissue proteins, mitochondrial proteins, and cytoplasmic proteins from mice with infarcted sides were heat denatured, after separation by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, the total protein was transferred to a PVDF membrane and incubated for 15 minutes with protein free rapid blocking solution. Then the membranes were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies separately. Primary antibodies were rabbit anti-Cytc (1:1000, ab182451, Abcam), Bax (1:1000, ab32503, Abcam), Bcl-2 (1:1000, ab182858, Abcam), Cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, ab32042, Abcam), Cleaved caspase-9 (1:1000, #9505, CST, Danvers, MA, USA), PI3K (1:1000. ab302958, Abcam), p-PI3K (1:1000, #13857, CST, Danvers, MA, USA), Akt (1:1000, ab8805, Abcam), p-Akt (1:1000, ab38449, Abcam), mTOR (1:1000, ab134903, Abcam), p-mTOR (1:1000, ab109268, Abcam), and the endogenous protein β-actin (1:5000, ab8227, Abcam), incubated at 4 °C overnight. The secondary antibody was sheep anti-rabbit (1:10000, ab6721, Abcam), incubated for 1 hour at 25 °C. Protein strip maps were created with a Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Shanghai, China) and evaluated for grayscale values in Image J. The findings are presented in the following table.

3.14. Statistical Analysis

All of the statistical studies described above were carried out with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (La Jolla, CA, USA), and data was analyzed for significance using Statistic Package for Social Science (SPSS) 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Independent t-tests or ANOVA tests were used to compare groups, and Turkey post-hoc analysis was conducted. Significant distinctions are highlighted by *P<0.05.

4. Results

4.1. GLGZD Reduces Brain Injury in MCAO/R Mice

The MCAO/R model mice were treated with GLGZD (7 days) and LY294002 (3 days) consecutively at 1 day postoperatively, and neurological deficit scores (mNSS) were measured at 1, 3, and 7 days after modeling, as shown in Figure 1A. The Sham group of mice (score 0) showed no neurologic abnormalities, but mice in the MCAO/R and GLGZD groups developed neurologic deficits. The mNSS scores of mice in both groups declined in a time-dependent manner, in comparison to the MCAO/R group, the mNSS scores of the GLGZD group decreased significantly (Fig.1B), showing that GLGZD effectively alleviated the neurological deficiency symptoms of MCAO/R mice. TTC staining was used to distinguish between normal and infarcted regions (red indicates normal, white indicates infarction). The results showed that after 7 days of feeding, the brain tissues in the MCAO/R group showed obvious white infarcts, and the red volume of the brain tissues in the GLGZD group increased when compared to the MCAO/R group (Fig. 1C-1D). Furthermore, the water content of mice in the GLGZD group was considerably less than that of the MCAO/R group (Fig. 1E). The experimental results showed that GLGZD greatly reduced the neurological impairment and brain tissue edema produced by CI/R in mice.

GLGZD Reduced Brain Damage Resulting From CI/R. A) Timeline for mice feeding and medication administration. B) Detection of brain injury in mice at 1, 3, and 7 days after CI/R based on mNSS score (n=12). C-D) Comparison of brain infarct volume in each group of mice using TTC staining. E) Calculation of water content in mice brain tissue. n=6. ns P>0.05, * P<0.05.

4.2. GLGZD Reduces Neuronal Pathologic Damage and Apoptotic Levels in MCAO/R Mice

HE staining revealed that the cells in the cortical and hippocampal CA1 areas of the Sham group were neatly arranged and had normal morphology, whereas the cells in the MCAO/R group were loosely arranged, with some swelling, rupture, and necrosis visible, which was significantly improved after the GLGZD intervention (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the Sham group mice had a stable number of Nissl bodies, homogeneous staining, and clear coloration of Nissl, while the MCAO/R group had a significantly reduced number of Nissl bodies, disorganized arrangement, and ambiguous structure, and the GLGZD group had an increased number of Nissl bodies compared with the MCAO/R group, which were tightly arranged and clearly colored (Fig. 2B-2C), suggesting that the neuronal damage in MCAO/R mice was effectively attenuated by GLGZD. Neuronal apoptosis was demonstrated in Figures 2D-2F: TUNEL/NeuN fluorescence double staining (NeuN is used as a specific marker for neurons. Under pathological conditions such as cerebral ischemia, hypoxia, and trauma, the number of NeuN-positive cells decreases, and immunofluorescence staining decreases as evidence of neuronal apoptosis). In the cortical fetch area and hippocampal CA1 area in the CI/R group, green fluorescence (apoptotic neurons and glial cells, etc.) was significantly enhanced, red fluorescence (surviving neurons) was weakened, and neuronal apoptosis had been significantly reduced following GLGZD intervention. The HE, Nissl, and Neun/Tunel staining results showed that 0.78 g.mL-1 of GLGZD substantially reduced CI/R-induced neuronal pathologic damage and death.

4.3. GLGZD Attenuates Mitochondrial Dysfunction in Brain Tissue of MCAO/R Mice

Normal or aberrant mitochondrial function influences neuronal development, differentiation, survival, migration, and transmitter transmission ( 22 ). The results of JC-1 fluorescence labeling revealed that the red/green fluorescence of neuronal mitochondria in the CI/R group was weakened compared to that in the Sham group, and the intensity of red/green fluorescence increased significantly after GLGZD administration (Fig. 3A-3B), indicating that GLGZD effectively weakened CI/R-induced damage of the neuronal mitochondrial. Furthermore, the membrane potential is the foundation for mitochondria to carry out oxidative phosphorylation to generate ATP ( 23 ), which is accompanied by the creation of ROS, so we investigated variations in mitochondrial ATP and ROS levels. As shown in Figures 3C-3D, ATP and ROS generation were decreased and increased in the CI/R group, respectively, indicating that inhibiting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation promoted mitochondrial production of excessive ROS and neuronal damage, which was mitigated by GLGZD. Excessive ROS attack dilated the mitochondrial membrane permeability transition pore, releasing cytochrome c (Cytc), which activated the caspase cascade process and caused apoptosis ( 24 ). After CI/R, Cytc content in cytoplasm and mitochondria increased and decreased, respectively, as did the amounts of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, Cleaved caspase-3, and Cleaved caspase-9, as well as the inhibitory apoptotic protein Bcl-2 in the mice’s lateral brain tissues, which were efficiently reversed after the GLGZD intervention (Fig. 3E-3H). The study found that CI/R caused mitochondrial depolarization, inhibited oxidative phosphorylation, and increased ROS overexpression in mice brain tissue, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis, and that GLGZD prevented apoptosis by reducing mitochondrial damage.

4.4. GLGZD Activates the PI3K/AKT/Mtor Axis in Brain Tissue of MCAO/R Mice

GLGZD is known to heal mitochondrial damage and improve neuronal damage in the brain tissue of MCAO/R mice, although the exact regulatory mechanisms are unknown. Several investigations have found that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis promotes mitochondrial autophagy and regulates mitochondrial damage ( 25 - 27 ). Thus, in this investigation, we investigated whether GLGZD could affect the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in mice brain tissue. Figures 4A-4D shows that mice in the MCAO/R group had lower expression of PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR compared to the Sham group. GLGZD promoted the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. However, the 10 mg.kg-1 doses of LY294002 reduced the level of PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR, reversing the activating effect of GLGZD on the pathway. GLGZD was found to regulate the activation of this pathway, which could be the primary explanation for its ability to repair mitochondrial malfunction and protect neurons.

4.5. LY294002 Inhibits GLGZD’s Ameliorative Impact on Mitochondrial Dysfunction in MCAO/R Mice

LY294002 is known to effectively reverse GLGZD’s activating action on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis. To determine whether GLGZD prevents mitochondrial damage through stimulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, we investigated the effect of LY294002 on mitochondrial dysfunction in MCAO/R mice. The assay results showed that compared to the MCAO/R group, GLGZD intervention resulted in increased mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5A-5B), increased and decreased ATP and ROS content, respectively (Fig. 5C-5D), increased Cytc release from mitochondria into the cytosol (Fig. 5E-5F), and increased and decreased levels of pro-apoptotic proteins in the brain tissues (Bax, Cleaved caspase-3, and Cleaved caspase-9) and anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 levels were increased and decreased, respectively (Fig. 5G-5H). LY294002 treatment partially reversed these phenomena and reduced the effect of GLGZD intervention, suggesting that GLGZD modulates mitochondrial function via stimulating the axis.

4.6. LY294002 Reduces the Effect of GLGZD on Neuronal in MCAO/R Mice

The previous findings showed that GLGZD reduces neuronal damage in MCAO/R mice, and it was postulated that it does so by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. To confirm the conjecture, we investigated the effect of LY294002 on neuronal damage in MCAO/R mice. 10 mg.kg-1 LY294002 acted continuously on MCAO/R mice for 3 days, and mNSS scores were performed on days 1, 3, and 7 (Fig. 6A): The mNSS scores of MCAO/R mice in all groups dropped over time. On day 1, the results showed no major shift in the neurological impairments of the mice in both of the groups, however, on days 3 and 7, the difference was clear. LY294002 effectively reduced the improvement impact of GLGZD on neurological deficits in MCAO/R mice. Furthermore, GLGZD reduced the extent of cerebral infarction and cerebral edema, and improved brain tissue injury, nidus loss, and neuronal apoptosis in the cortical and hippocampal CA1 areas in mice compared to the MCAO/R group. However, LY294002 intervention resulted in increased infarct volume and brain tissue water content (Fig. 6B-6D), exacerbation of lesions in cortical area and hippocampal CA1 area, loss of Nissl bodies (Fig. 6E-6G), and increased neuronal apoptosis (Fig. 6H-6J), suggesting that GLGZD mediates the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which ameliorates neuronal injury and promotes neurological function recovery in MCAO/R.

5. Discussion

In this study, we looked at GLGZD’s protective effects and regulatory mechanisms in neurons from MCAO/R mice’s brain tissues. The study’s findings revealed that following GLGZD treatment, the volume of cerebral infarctions was greatly reduced, the degree of brain edema was reduced, and neuronal damage and neurobehavioral were significantly improved in MCAO/R mice. Furthermore, GLGZD dramatically reduced mitochondrial dysfunction in the brain tissue of MCAO/R mice, which was linked to the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

During cerebral ischemia and reperfusion, mitochondria give ATP to the brain, maintain cellular calcium homeostasis, govern cellular metabolism, and modulate cellular signaling and apoptosis, all of which are linked to brain tissue injury following reperfusion ( 28 ). During ischemia, the interruption of cerebrovascular blood supply causes a decrease in oxygen and glucose delivery to brain tissue, abnormal mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, the conversion of aerobic respiration to anaerobic respiration, and an increase in H+ concentration, resulting in an abnormal mitochondrial electrochemical gradient, which causes mitochondrial swelling and calcium overload. During reperfusion, oxidative stress caused by oxygen re-input is a major contributor to reperfusion injury, and mitochondria play an important role. Following cerebral ischemia/reperfusion, various pathological factors such as hypoxia and calcium overload cause abnormal mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, a sharp increase in ROS content, and a decrease in clearance capacity, and a large amount of ROS accumulation in the mitochondria triggers an oxidative stress response, exacerbating mitochondrial damage and eventually inducing cell death ( 29 , 30 ). In this study, after administering 0.78 g.mL-1 GLGZD continuously for 7 days, the mitochondrial membrane potential of the brain tissues of MCAO/R mice was elevated, and the contents of ATP and ROS were increased and decreased, resulting in reduced Cytc release, inhibition of apoptosis, and alleviation of mitochondrial dysfunction. GLGZD-induced mitochondrial function restoration resulted in remarkable improvement of motor, sensory, response, and balance in MCAO/R mice, with a significant reduction in mNSS scores, infarction, edema, and neuronal death in mice brain tissues were all suppressed. This is consistent with recent findings that restoring mitochondrial activity can considerably reduce CI/R and enhance the treatment of ischemic stroke and other cerebrovascular illnesses ( 31 - 33 ).

Furthermore, treatment with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 exacerbated mitochondrial functional impairment in mice brain tissues compared to the GLGZD alone treatment group, implying that mitochondrial function restoration is associated with PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation. After CI/R, neurotrophic and growth factors can activate PI3K. After trophic factors bind to the extracellular structural domains of the transmembrane receptor, the receptor is phosphorylated, and PI3K is recruited to the site of receptor phosphorylation, where it catalyzes inositol phosphorylation on the plasma membrane surface, resulting in phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate. Among them, phosphatidylinositol-3 can act as a second messenger to activate AKT, a direct target of P13K downstream of P13K, which translocates to the inner surface of the cell membrane and eventually activates p-Akt to activate the downstream mTOR, which inhibits apoptosis and maintains cell survival by up-regulating Bcl-2 expression and down-regulating Bax expression. Our study found that GLGZD activated the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and improved neuronal injury in MCAO/R mice’s brain tissues. However, LY294002 partially reversed the effect of GLGZD, resulting in elevated mNSS scores, increased cerebral infarct volume, loss of Nysted’s vesicles, and apoptosis of neurons in the mice. This suggests that GLGZD has an ameliorative effect on neuronal damage in MCAO/R mice was achieved by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, demonstrated that GLGZD can boost the resistance of neuronal cells to ischemia and hypoxia ( 34 ) and inhibit neuronal apoptosis ( 35 ) through stimulating the PI3K/Akt pathway, hence maintaining neural function.

Animal brain neurons, with their complex cellular shape, high metabolic rate, and ability to perform certain specialized functions, are often the organism’s most oxygen-consuming organs, which is consistent with the amount of aerobic metabolism of highly active mitochondria in neurons. Maintaining mitochondrial activity helps to reduce brain damage and fight CI/R. Compared with conventional treatments for ischemic stroke, such as thrombolytics, antioxidants, and neurotrophic factors ( 36 ), GLGZD exhibits unique neuroprotective effects by repairing mitochondrial function and activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signal-ing pathway. In addition, GLGZD provides holistic regulation with relatively low toxicity and side effects, potentially resulting in better long-term efficacy ( 37 ). Therefore, GLGZD may serve as a supplement or optimization to existing stroke treatment strategies. At the level of drug development, unlike modern drugs that focus on a single target, GLGZD demonstrates therapeutic potential for “holistic regulation” by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, while simultaneously regulating mitochondrial function, inhibiting oxidative stress, and suppressing apoptosis. This suggests that future drug development can draw on the concept of “network pharmacology” to develop novel multitarget combination therapies, or directly isolate, identify, and optimize key active component combinations from GLGZD, aiming to achieve better efficacy and lower toxicity than single-target drugs. In terms of biotechnological applications, this study has confirmed that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is an important molecular mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effects of GLGZD. This finding provides a clear molecular target for the development of high-throughput screening platforms. For example, cell models based on pathway activity reporter genes can be constructed for rapid screening of candidate drugs or natural products that can mimic or enhance the efficacy of GLGZD. Furthermore, the expression levels of key proteins in the pathway (such as p-AKT and p-mTOR), as well as mitochondrial functional indicators (such as membrane potential and ATP content), can serve as biomarkers for evaluating the efficacy of candidate drugs, providing objective and quantifiable evaluation criteria for preclinical research and future clinical trials. Future research may focus on developing standardized GLGZD extracts and designing novel drug delivery systems (such as Nanodelivery) to improve its bioavailability and brain targeting, thereby maximizing its clinical therapeutic potential ( 38 ).

6. Conclusion

In this study, we determined GLGZD’s repairing effects on mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis in MCAO/R mice brain tissues, as well as its potential regulatory mechanisms. The findings indicate that GLGZD can effectively reduce CI/R injury in model mice and may be a stroke neuroprotective agent. In addition, other herbal preparations, such as Taohong Siwu ( 39 ) and Xiao-Xu-Ming decoction ( 40 ), have been shown to reduce CI/R injury. Crossing and combining the components of various herbal compound preparations may contribute more to the neuroprotective effect of CI/R than using them separately ( 41 ). Therefore, future studies should further investigate the synergistic effects among different components of herbal compound formulations, optimize the dosage ratios of each component, and evaluate the efficacy and safety of GLGZD and its combined applications as neuroprotective agents in stroke treatment through animal experiments and clinical studies.
