“Trust Women”: Characteristics of and learnings from patients of a Shield Law medication abortion practice in the United States
Authors: Angel M. Foster, Alice Mark, Kyle J. Drouillard, Maureen Paul, Susan Yanow, Sarah Shahi, Dipesh Suvarna, Andrea Peña
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe 2022 Massachusetts Shield Law protects telemedicine providers who care for abortion seekers in other states from criminal, civil, and licensure penalties. In this article we explore the characteristics of patients of The Massachusetts Medication Abortion Access Project (The MAP).MethodsThe MAP is an asynchronous telemedicine service that offers mifepristone/misoprostol to abortion seekers in all 50 states who are at or under 11 weeks pregnancy gestation on initial intake. The MAP charges USD250 using a pay‐what‐you‐can model. We analyzed medical questionnaires and payments submitted by patients who received care from The MAP during its first 6 months of operations using descriptive statistics and for content and themes.ResultsFrom October 1, 2023–March 31, 2024, 1994 patients accessed care through The MAP. Almost all (n = 1973, 99%) identified as women/girls and about half (n = 984, 49%) were aged 20–29. The MAP cared for patients in 45 states; 84% (n = 1672) of these patients received pills in abortion ban or restricted southern states. Patients paid USD134.50 on average; 29% (n = 577) paid USD25 or less. Nearly two‐thirds (n = 1293, 65%) received subsidized care; financial hardship featured prominently in patient comments.DiscussionConsiderable demand exists for medication abortion care from Shield Law providers. The MAP demonstrates that providers can trust women and other pregnancy capable people to decide for themselves whether to obtain medication abortion pills by mail and to pay what they can afford without being required to justify their need. Identifying ways to support Shield Law provision and further subsidize abortion care are needed.

INTRODUCTION

In June 2022, the United States (US) Supreme Court overturned nearly 50 years of precedent by eliminating the constitutional right to an abortion in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (Dobbs). 1 In the wake of Dobbs a number of states enacted or enforced severe restrictions on abortion care. As of August 2024, 14 states had banned abortion in almost all circumstances and an additional four states restricted abortion after approximately 6 weeks of gestation. 2 , i About 1 in 3 American women ii of reproductive age live in these ban or restricted states. 3

In June 2022, the legislature of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts passed An Act Expanding Protections for Reproductive and Gender‐Affirming Care, commonly referred to as the Massachusetts Shield Law. 4 The first of its kind in the US, the Massachusetts Shield Law protects clinicians, helpers, and funders from criminal, civil, and licensure penalties for providing legally‐protected reproductive or gender affirming health care and protects information about patients who receive these services in the Commonwealth. When receiving telemedicine services from a clinician who resides in, is licensed in, and practices from within the Commonwealth, the patient is defined as a resident of Massachusetts.

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the use of mifepristone for early abortion in 2000; in 2020 more than half of all abortions through the formal health care system in the US involved the mifepristone/misoprostol regimen. 5 The safety and effectiveness of the mifepristone/misoprostol regimen is well established and medication abortion can be provided by a range of health service providers and through different service delivery strategies.6, 7 Research conducted in the US demonstrates that in‐person, synchronous telemedicine, and asynchronous telemedicine provision of the mifepristone/misoprostol regimen yield comparable outcomes.8, 9

On September 28, 2023, Cambridge Reproductive Health Consultants launched The Massachusetts Medication Abortion Access Project (The MAP), an asynchronous telemedicine service offering medication abortion care to abortion seekers in all 50 states, the District of Columbia (DC), and all US territories as well as to those with a military address. 10 The MAP offers the mifepristone/misoprostol regimen for immediate use, future use, and use around the time of an expected period. 11 , iii In this article we explore the characteristics of The MAP's patient population over its first 6 months of operations and reflect on early learnings from this Shield Law practice.

METHODS

Description of The MAP service

The MAP offers the mifepristone/misoprostol regimen to patients who are within 11 weeks from the first day of their last menstrual period (LMP) and have no contraindications to either medication. Because of the Commonwealth's parental involvement law, The MAP only provides care to those who are 16 years old and older. Patients complete an electronic medical questionnaire and e‐sign consent forms, which are then reviewed by a licensed and trained clinician. After being approved for The MAP's service and paying the fee, patients are mailed the medications as well as detailed instructions for use; information about ectopic pregnancy, the optimal time to use the pills, digital privacy protection, and resources for free legal, medical, and counseling support; and a hand‐written note wishing them well. The MAP ships through the United States Postal Service and patients receive their packages in 2–5 business days. Members of The MAP team are available by email, text, and phone to answer questions and create additional treatment plans, as needed, and all patients receive follow‐up questionnaires from The MAP 2 and 6 weeks after the pills are shipped.

The MAP adheres to all Massachusetts laws, policies, and regulations governing both abortion and telemedicine and follows the National Abortion Federation's Clinical Policy Guidelines. 7 The MAP charges USD250 but uses a “pay‐what‐you‐can” model; The MAP does not require patients to provide income information or means test. The MAP asks patients requesting pills for immediate use or as period pills to make a minimum payment of USD5 and waives this minimum on a case‐by‐case basis. Patients who are unable to pay the full price receive subsidized care through a combination of support from abortion funds (local or national organizations that support abortion seekers and provide The MAP with either targeted or general support grants), individual donations to The MAP, or in‐kind donations from The MAP team. Over its first 6 months, The MAP partnered with more than a dozen abortion funds, including national funds, local funds in Massachusetts, and local funds in ban and restricted states. Patients requesting pills for future use are not eligible for subsidized care.

Data used in this article

The MAP's patients complete a medical questionnaire that includes sections related to demographics, iv pregnancy status, pregnancy and reproductive health history, and general medical history, including allergies and medication use. Patients also confirm that they are not being forced to obtain abortion pills and have an opportunity to respond to a final open‐ended free‐response question, “Is there anything else you would like us to know about you?” The MAP collects and temporarily stores these questionnaires in a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act‐compliant electronic medical record system.

For this article, we analyzed the medical questionnaires submitted by all patients who received care from The MAP between October 1, 2023 and March 31, 2024. v In addition we analyzed the payment information associated with each patient, including the amount paid, the payment platform, and whether or not an abortion fund provided additional support, as entered into the patient record by members of The MAP team. We did not analyze information from the 2‐ and 6‐week follow‐up questionnaires.

Data analysis

We exported de‐identified information from The MAP's electronic medical record system to Microsoft Excel® for analysis. We calculated age based on the date of birth and the date on which the patient submitted the medical questionnaire. We calculated gestational age based on the number of days between the self‐reported first day of the patient's LMP and the day on which The MAP shipped the package. We grouped states into the US regions defined by the Massachusetts Department of Public Health. 12 We analyzed these data using descriptive statistics. We analyzed the free‐text responses to the open‐ended question for content and themes; in the results section we present unaltered illustrative quotes and preserve word choice, punctuation, and syntax.

Ethical considerations

All patients signed a consent form agreeing that the information they provided to The MAP could be used for research purposes. We also received approval to analyze these data from the University of Ottawa's Social Sciences and Humanities Research Ethics Board.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

In the 6 months after its launch, The MAP provided care to 1994 patients. We provide detailed demographic information about The MAP's patients in Table 1. Almost all patients (n = 1973, 99%) identified as being women/girls and about half (n = 984, 49%) were aged 20–29. About 44% of The MAP patients identified as white (n = 876), 29% identified as African American/Afro‐Caribbean/Black (n = 481), 17% identified as Latinx/Hispanic (n = 338), 3% as East or South Asian (n = 53), and 2% as Indigenous (n = 29 including Native American, Native Alaskan, or Native Hawaiian). Of the 1571 patients who The MAP asked to report on their sexual orientation, most (n = 1453, 93%) identified as heterosexual; 73 patients (5%) identified as bisexual. As showcased in Figure 1, The MAP sent pills to patients in 45 states and Washington, DC, as well to those with military addresses (not shown). Over 80% of The MAP's patients received their pills in a state in the Southeast (n = 817, 41%) or the Gulf Coast (n = 855, 43%) regions. The MAP also sent pills to 15 patients (1%) in Massachusetts.

Pregnancy and reproductive health history

Most patients (n = 1858, 93%) were seeking medication abortion pills for immediate use, 16 patients for future use (<1%), and 120 patients (6%) for use around the time of an expected period. Nearly half of The MAP's patients (n = 962, 48%) were at less than 6 weeks of gestation (41 days or less) when they received services; 48 patients (2%) were at 10 or more weeks of gestation. The majority of patients (n = 1186, 59%) had already given birth to at least one child and 144 patients (7%) had given birth four or more times. Most patients (n = 1381, 69%) had not had a previous abortion. All patients that The MAP served confirmed that they were not being forced to obtain medication abortion pills. Patient payments and funding

On average, patients paid USD134.50 for their abortion care. Patient payments were highly variable and often specific; one patient made a payment of USD9.22. Nearly two thirds of The MAP's patients (n = 1293, 65%) required a subsidy; 577 patients (29%) paid USD25 or less. About 4% (n = 52) of The MAP's patients who received care at a reduced price made additional payments after receiving the pills or completing the abortion process, including one patient who paid USD5 a week for 4 weeks. Seven hundred and sixteen patients (35%), including all future use patients, paid the full USD250; about 3% of these patients (n = 20) paid more than USD250 and instructed The MAP to direct the extra amount as a donation to subsidize the care of another patient. In total, 525 patients (26%) received support from one or more abortion funds. Individual donations and in‐kind contributions, including donated services from members of The MAP team, covered the remaining 768 patients (39%) who received subsidies. Patient comments

A total of 361 patients (18%) wrote a response to the open‐ended question on the medical questionnaire. Patients used this space to ask questions about the medication abortion process (duration, amount of bleeding, and pain management) and the logistics of the service (timing of shipment and discreteness of the packaging). Patients also shared more information about their health and life circumstances. For example, one woman who received pills in a state where the abortion ban does not include a clear health exception wrote, “I was scheduled to have a lumpectomy, but found out I was pregnant. They cannot operate until I abort the pregnancy. The lump could be cancerous and is growing larger.” Another woman who was beyond her state's gestational age ban wrote, “I was sexually assaulted and cannot afford an abortion and do not want to deliver my rapist's baby.” Still others wrote with words of gratitude or expressions of frustration at the legal restrictions on abortion. As one patient who received pills for future use wrote:“You guys are doing great and important work! It scares me to death to know that some people want to take away our freedom to choose. Thank you from the bottom of my heart. I'm not pregnant right now but my husband and I live in a very republican state so we are afraid that if I get pregnant, I may be forced to carry the pregnancy. We are planning for him to get a vasectomy but it's quite expensive, so I'd like to have pills just in case.”

However, the most salient topic in the written comments centered on money. For some patients, like this woman from a ban state in the Southeast who wrote “I need help with funding”, the comments were direct and focused on paying for The MAP service. But for most, financial hardship or precarity was offered as one of the primary reasons for seeking abortion care. As a woman living in a Gulf Coast state wrote, “I am a single mother of 3 beautiful girls. I'm only 24 I recently moved to a new state with no family no help just my kids and I don't have the financial or physical help to care for any more kids.” Another patient living in the Southeast wrote: “I am an immigrant woman, I have a 12‐year‐old son, I do not have a stable job, I honestly need your help.”

Financial precarity was woven into the stories shared by The MAP's patients. As one patient from a restricted state in the Southeast wrote:“This was the result of assault. I am currently unemployed and make all of my inome [income] from heloing [helping] my cousin clean houses and other odd jobs when I can. I have an 18 month old and can't afford the medication. But this is my worst nightmare and I just really need some help.”

DISCUSSION

The turmoil in the US abortion landscape created by the Dobbs decision cannot be overstated. Clinic closures, long wait times for in‐person care, the logistics and expenses associated with interstate travel, confusion about the status of bans and their enforcement, and an overarching lack of clarity about the scope of restrictions and their exceptions have both created new and exacerbated longstanding barriers to accessing timely abortion care.13, 14, 15 However, the promulgation of Shield Laws that protect telemedicine providers, in eight states as of mid‐2024, 2 represents an innovative strategy to help US abortion seekers no matter their zip code get the high quality abortion care they need, want, and deserve. Indeed, in May 2024 #WeCount, a project of the Society of Family Planning dedicated to documenting changes in national and state abortion volumes following Dobbs, reported that in 2023 the number of abortions was higher than in 2022, one in five abortions was provided through telemedicine, and Shield Law providers cared for 40,000 patients in ban, restricted, and telemedicine ban states. 16

As of this writing, The MAP is one of two Shield Law providers offering medication abortion care via telemedicine in all 50 states. That a new Shield Law practice provided care to nearly 2000 patients in the 6 months after its launch demonstrates that this model of service delivery is feasible and demand exists. The MAP's patients are concentrated in the Gulf Coast and Southeast, regions of the US comprising states that now ban abortion in almost all circumstances and at all or almost all gestational ages. Many of the states in these regions had few brick and mortar facilities, a plethora of state‐level restrictions on abortion service delivery, and prohibitions on insurance coverage for abortion care prior to Dobbs. The demographic characteristics of The MAP's patients with respect to gender identification, age, race and ethnicity, and sexual orientation are broadly comparable to those who were seeking in‐clinic abortion care pre‐Dobbs.17, 18 However, more research needs to be done to determine if Shield Law providers like The MAP are serving abortion seekers who previously obtained care from clinics in their state, those who were unable to get abortion care prior to Dobbs due to financial and logistical barriers, or both.

Provision of medication abortion care through telemedicine has increased significantly in the US over the last decade. 19 The FDA's temporary (2020) and then permanent (2021) removal of the in‐person dispensing requirement for mifepristone facilitated the provision of medication abortion pills by mail during the COVID‐19 pandemic.19, 20 Virtual clinics providing both asynchronous and synchronous medication abortion care proliferated in the year after Dobbs. 21 However, prior to the development of Shield Laws, telemedicine medication abortion care through the formal health system was generally not available in southern states due to explicit or effective bans on this form of service delivery. Compared with the demographic characteristics of 1600 patients using telemedicine services in states that permitted this service delivery modality in 2021–2022, 22 The MAP's patients are more likely to identify as African American/Black (29% vs. 9%) and less likely to identify as white (44% vs. 53%) or Asian/Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (3% vs. 7%). [Correction added on October 29, 2024, after first online publication: In the previous sentence, “33%” was changed to “53%”.] This is likely, at least in part, reflective of the different population demographics in different US regions. However, additional research is needed to explore how patients obtaining care from Shield Law providers compare with patients who obtained in state, in‐clinic abortion care prior to Dobbs, telemedicine abortion patients in states where this modality of service delivery is permitted, patients from ban and restricted states who travel to other states for medication abortion care post‐Dobbs, and abortion seekers who obtain medication abortion drugs outside of the formal health care system.

A striking characteristic of The MAP's patients is the large number who need subsidized care. Most of these medication abortion patients live in states without public insurance coverage for abortion and all patients are self‐pay; nearly two thirds of The MAP's patients received subsidized care including a quarter who received support from an abortion fund. In 2020 (pre‐Dobbs) the median self‐pay price for medication abortion care at brick‐and‐mortar facilities in the United States was USD560, an amount that did not include travel costs. 18 In contrast, the full price of medication abortion care from The MAP is USD250, patients paid USD134.50 on average, and a substantial minority (29%) paid USD25 or less. Research that explores the socio‐economic status of patients who obtain care from Shield Law providers like The MAP and how this population compares to in‐clinic medication abortion patients in both the pre‐Dobbs and post‐Dobbs eras could provide valuable insight into met and unmet need. Compared with both abortion patients in restricted states 23 and those using a telemedicine service outside of the formal health care system 24 prior to Dobbs, a larger proportion of The MAP patients received subsidized care. This speaks to the tremendous need for low‐cost abortion care, as repeatedly showcased in patient comments, and for identifying ways to expand avenues for subsidizing care for Shield Law patients, including developing innovative strategies to reimagine the overarching financing system that governs abortion care in the US.

For two decades the abortion rights movement has embraced various permutations of the slogan “Trust Women.” Credited to Dr. George Tiller, an abortion provider in Kansas who was known for his aphorisms and generous spirit, 25 “Trust Women” has long reflected an understanding that women (and other pregnancy capable people) are the experts on their own bodies and lives and are able to decide for themselves if they are pregnant and do not want to be at any gestational age. In 2010, SisterSong launched the national Trust Black Women partnership. 26 Although the impetus behind its creation was to challenge the racist and sexist billboard campaign that Georgia Right to Life sponsored in the Atlanta area, Trust Black Women reflects an intersectional and reproductive justice‐informed approach to lifting up Black women's experiences, leadership, and voices. In 2019, the Nevada legislature passed the “Trust Nevada Women Act,” which decriminalized medication abortion and reformed the state's informed consent process, giving greater agency to abortion seekers. 27 And when the Governor of Massachusetts signed an executive order protecting access to emergency abortion care in 2024, the President of the State Senate issued a statement of support that read in part, “Massachusetts has always been clear that we trust women to make their own healthcare decisions.” 28

The experience of The MAP highlights how Shield Law provision embodies this trust—trusting that patients in ban and restricted states in the post‐Dobbs era can decide for themselves whether obtaining medication abortion pills by mail makes sense in their specific context. The MAP experience also offers a model of trusting abortion seekers with respect to money, by trusting that women and other pregnancy capable people will pay what they are able to afford without being required to justify their need. Indeed, even though every patient could have obtained pills for USD5, the overwhelming majority paid more than the minimum. In the new abortion landscape, keeping the concept of trust front and center is more important than ever.

Limitations

All patient characteristics are self‐reported and The MAP does not verify accuracy. The MAP collects a limited amount of information about its patients and thus we were constrained in what we could explore with respect to patient characteristics. Notably, we have no information about household income or other markers of socio‐economic status about The MAP's patients.

CONCLUSION

The early experience of The MAP demonstrates that creating a Shield Law practice is feasible and demand for this type of care exists. The MAP is reaching a diverse group of patients in ban and restricted states, the majority of whom need subsidized care. Identifying ways to support Shield Law provision and other innovative service delivery strategies and expand mechanisms to provide low‐cost abortion care are needed.
