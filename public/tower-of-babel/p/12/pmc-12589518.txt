Development and validation of a risk prediction model for re-tear after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair
Authors: Yiwen Hu, Shaojian Chen, Heng Zhang, Changzhao Huang, Zixi Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRe-tear remains a frequent complication after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR), adversely affecting functional recovery. This study aimed to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for re-tear risk following ARCR. This retrospective study included 570 patients undergoing ARCR from 2018 to 2023, randomly assigned to training (n = 399) and validation (n = 171) cohorts. Demographic, imaging, and intraoperative variables were analyzed. Independent predictors from multivariable logistic regression were used to construct a nomogram. Model performance was assessed by discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Six independent predictors were identified: age, maximal tear diameter, Patte grade III tendon retraction, high-grade fatty infiltration (Goutallier ≥ 3), positive tangent sign, and intraoperative repair tension. The nomogram showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.76 training; 0.74 validation) and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.735 and 0.721, respectively). DCA demonstrated clinical utility across wide threshold probabilities, outperforming “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies. This nomogram, integrating preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings, provides individualized re-tear risk estimates after ARCR, offering a practical tool for surgical planning and patient counseling.Keywords: Rotator cuff tear, Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, Re-tear, Nomogram, Risk prediction, Shoulder surgerySubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Rotator cuff tears are a leading cause of shoulder pain and disability in adults, with prevalence increasing markedly with age; epidemiologic studies have reported rates exceeding 20% in individuals over 60 years old1,2. Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) has largely replaced open techniques for the management of full-thickness tears, owing to its minimally invasive nature, superior joint visualization, and favorable short-term clinical outcomes3,4. Despite advances in surgical instrumentation, suture anchor technology, and repair techniques, structural failure after ARCR—most often manifesting as a re-tear—remains a frequent and challenging complication. Contemporary series and meta-analyses report re-tear rates ranging from 20 to 40% within the first postoperative year5,6. Such failures are clinically significant, as they are associated with persistent pain, decreased muscle strength, impaired shoulder function, and an increased likelihood of revision surgery, ultimately compromising the long-term success of ARCR7,8.

Extensive evidence indicates that patient- and tear-related factors such as advanced age, large tear size, severe tendon retraction, high-grade fatty infiltration, and supraspinatus muscle atrophy are strongly associated with impaired tendon healing and higher re-tear rates after ARCR9–11. High-grade fatty infiltration, in particular, is a robust predictor of poor healing potential even when anatomic repair is achieved12. Surgical technique may also influence outcomes; double-row fixation can provide greater tendon–bone contact and initial fixation strength than single-row repair, potentially lowering re-tear risk in large or massive tears13. In addition, metabolic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus can impair tendon-to-bone healing through microvascular compromise, altered collagen metabolism, and chronic inflammation, thereby increasing the risk of structural failure14,15.

While these risk factors are well documented, current clinical practice lacks a robust, validated tool to integrate them into individualized risk assessment. Existing studies often focus on isolated predictors or small patient cohorts, with limited external validation and inconsistent incorporation of perioperative variables16,17. This knowledge gap means that surgeons often rely on subjective judgment rather than quantitative, evidence-based tools when counseling patients and tailoring surgical strategies. In an era of precision medicine, there is an urgent need for predictive models that synthesize diverse clinical, imaging, and intraoperative parameters into an accurate, user-friendly format to guide decision-making.

Nomograms—graphical representations of multivariable regression models—are increasingly used for individualized risk prediction in surgery18. They enable rapid estimation of outcome probabilities, support shared decision-making, and guide perioperative management. Although such tools have been applied in shoulder surgery, no validated nomogram currently predicts re-tear after ARCR by integrating both patient-related and intraoperative variables and undergoing external validation.

Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study to develop and validate a nomogram for predicting re-tear after ARCR. By integrating demographic, clinical, imaging, and surgical data, we aimed to create a model with high discrimination, accurate calibration, and demonstrable clinical utility. This tool is intended to support preoperative risk stratification, optimize surgical planning, and improve long-term functional outcomes for patients undergoing ARCR.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent ARCR at a single tertiary referral hospital between January 2018 and December 2023. All surgeries were performed by senior orthopedic surgeons with subspecialty expertise in shoulder procedures, following a standardized ARCR protocol and a unified postoperative rehabilitation pathway. Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with full-thickness rotator cuff tears confirmed by preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and at least 12 months of follow-up, including postoperative imaging to assess repair integrity. Repair tension was categorized a priori as low, moderate, or high using predefined operative criteria (low: approximation without undue tension after standard releases; moderate: requiring additional releases/positioning to achieve tension-free contact; high: difficult approximation despite adequate releases); no tensiometer was used. MRI was the protocol-preferred 6–12-month follow-up modality; high-resolution ultrasonography was used only when MRI was contraindicated (e.g., non-MRI-conditional pacemaker, severe claustrophobia) or could not be scheduled within the 6–12-month window. A uniform structural definition (Sugaya IV–V) and a standardized reporting template were applied across modalities, and all examinations were independently interpreted by two musculoskeletal radiologists blinded to clinical and intraoperative data, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. At our institution, routine structural imaging (MRI or high-resolution ultrasonography) at 6–12 months after ARCR is part of standard postoperative care, irrespective of symptoms. Patients with incomplete follow-up or missing postoperative imaging were excluded a priori. Patients were excluded if they had partial-thickness tears, irreparable massive tears, or subscapularis-dominant tears; a history of ipsilateral shoulder surgery; concurrent shoulder infection, advanced glenohumeral osteoarthritis, or fracture; or incomplete clinical or imaging data.

Data collection

Data were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical records by two independent reviewers, with disagreements adjudicated by a senior investigator. Demographic and baseline variables included patient age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and osteoporosis. Rotator cuff pathology was assessed using preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and intraoperative findings. Variables included maximal tear diameter (cm) measured intraoperatively, tendon retraction graded according to the Patte classification (Grade I: near the footprint; Grade II: at the humeral head midpoint; Grade III: at the glenoid level)19, fatty infiltration graded according to the Goutallier classification (Grades 0–2 vs. ≥ 3)20, and muscle atrophy evaluated using the tangent sign on sagittal MRI21. Operative variables, extracted from operative reports, included operative time; repair configuration (single-row, double-row, or suture-bridge); number of suture anchors used; acromion morphology classified per the Bigliani system (Type I: flat; Type II: curved; Type III: hooked), which remains widely employed in clinical studies despite limited recent validation22; intraoperative repair tension, subjectively categorized as low, moderate, or high, reflecting surgeon-perceived suture-site stress—an approach supported by evidence linking tensions ≥ 10 N with significantly increased re-tear risk23; and any concomitant acromioplasty or biceps procedure.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was postoperative re-tear within 12 months, defined as Sugaya type IV or V on follow-up MRI or high-resolution ultrasonography performed 6–12 months after surgery24,25. A re-tear was characterized by a full-thickness defect in the repaired tendon. Imaging assessments were interpreted by two musculoskeletal radiologists.

Imaging protocol

Preoperative MRI was conducted on a 3.0-T scanner using standard shoulder protocols, including T1-weighted and fat-suppressed T2-weighted sequences in axial, coronal oblique, and sagittal oblique planes. Fatty infiltration was assessed on sagittal oblique images according to the Goutallier classification. Postoperative MRI followed the same protocol. When ultrasonography was employed for follow-up, examinations were performed by experienced musculoskeletal sonographers using a high-frequency (≥ 12 MHz) linear transducer to evaluate tendon continuity, echotexture, and footprint integrity.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). Patients were randomly assigned to training and validation sets (7:3) using a fixed seed (123). Continuous variables were compared with the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables with the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis entered multivariable logistic regression. A nomogram was built from the final model. Discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed by C-index, ROC, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis, with internal validation via 1,000 bootstrap resamples. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Results

Patient characteristics

Based on predefined criteria, 570 of 762 screened patients undergoing ARCR between January 2018 and December 2023 were eligible and included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The mean age was 57.35 ± 10.53 years, and 353 patients (61.9%) were male. Patients were assigned to training (n = 399) and validation (n = 171) cohorts, showing no baseline differences (all P > 0.05, Table 1). The overall incidence of structural re-tear at 12 months was 22.1% (126/570), with similar rates in the training (22.3%) and validation (21.6%) sets (P = 0.860). When stratified by re-tear status, patients with re-tear were significantly older (59.23 ± 10.60 years vs. 56.88 ± 10.40 years, P = 0.026), had larger maximal tear diameters (3.63 ± 1.23 cm vs. 3.14 ± 1.12 cm, P < 0.001), and presented with more advanced tendon retraction (Patte grade III: 30.2% vs. 18.5%, P = 0.008) compared with those without re-tear. High-grade fatty infiltration (Goutallier classification ≥ 3) was more prevalent among re-tear patients (50.8% vs. 31.3%, P < 0.001), as was a positive tangent sign indicating supraspinatus muscle atrophy (39.7% vs. 26.6%, P = 0.006). Intraoperative findings revealed that high repair tension (27.8% vs. 14.0%, P < 0.001) and a greater number of anchors used (2.87 ± 0.92 vs. 2.68 ± 0.92, P = 0.041) were both more common in the re-tear group. No significant differences were observed in sex distribution, BMI, smoking status, or repair technique (all P > 0.05).

Multivariable analysis

Multivariable logistic regression identified six independent predictors of re-tear: age (OR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01–1.06; P = 0.047), maximal tear diameter (OR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.18–1.73; P < 0.001), Patte grade III retraction (OR, 2.26; 95% CI, 1.22–4.18; P = 0.010), high-grade fatty infiltration (OR, 2.72; 95% CI, 1.74–4.26; P < 0.001), positive tangent sign (OR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.26–3.16; P = 0.003), and intraoperative repair tension (moderate: OR, 1.75; 95% CI, 1.05–2.92; P = 0.032; high: OR, 4.03; 95% CI, 2.19–7.42; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Nomogram development

A nomogram was developed based on six independent predictors—age, maximal tear diameter, tendon retraction grade (Patte), fatty infiltration grade (Goutallier), muscle atrophy (tangent sign), and intraoperative repair tension—to estimate the probability of structural re-tear at 12 months after ARCR (Fig. 2). Each predictor was assigned a weighted score derived from its regression coefficient, as detailed in Table 3. For age, 25 years was assigned 0 points, with an additional 3 points for every 5-year increment, up to 36 points at 85 years. For maximal tear diameter, 0 cm corresponded to 0 points, with an increase of 6.67 points per 0.5 cm, reaching a maximum of 100 points at 7.5 cm. Retraction grade (Patte) was scored as 0, 20, and 45 points for grades I, II, and III, respectively. Fatty infiltration (Goutallier) was scored as 0 points for grades 0–2 and 42.5 points for grade ≥ 3. Muscle atrophy (tangent sign) was scored as 0 points if negative and 30 points if positive. Intraoperative repair tension was scored as 0, 22.5, and 50 points for low, moderate, and high tension, respectively. The total score was obtained by summing all component scores and then projecting the result onto the probability scale of the nomogram to estimate the risk of re-tear. Clinical example: A 65-year-old patient (24 points) with a maximal tear diameter of 4.0 cm (53.36 points), Patte grade II retraction (20 points), Goutallier grade 3 fatty infiltration (42.5 points), positive tangent sign (30 points), and moderate repair tension (22.5 points) had a total score of 192.36 points, corresponding to an estimated 12-month re-tear probability of approximately 47%.

Model performance and validation

In the training set, the nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.76 (Fig. 3A), indicating strong discriminative ability. This performance was consistent in the validation set, where the AUC was 0.74 (Fig. 3B). Calibration analysis showed close concordance between predicted and actual outcomes in both cohorts (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.735 for training, Fig. 3C; P = 0.721 for validation, Fig. 3D), with the non-significant values indicating an adequate model fit. DCA demonstrated confirmed the nomogram’s practical value, yielding a positive net benefit over threshold probabilities of ~ 8–80% in the training set (Fig. 3E) and ~ 7–62% in the validation set (Fig. 3F), both exceeding the “treat-all” and “treat-none” approaches. These findings confirm both the predictive accuracy and practical applicability of the model in estimating 12-month re-tear risk after ARCR.

Discussion

In this study, we identified six independent predictors of structural re-tear following ARCR—age, maximal tear diameter, tendon retraction grade, fatty infiltration, muscle atrophy, and intraoperative repair tension. These variables were integrated into a nomogram that demonstrated favorable discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. This tool is intended for staged clinical use: preoperatively, a preliminary risk range can be estimated from demographic and imaging variables to support counseling and scenario planning; the individualized final probability should be updated intraoperatively once repair tension is known. Our findings support the hypothesis that a multifactorial model incorporating demographic characteristics, preoperative imaging parameters, and intraoperative findings can enable accurate, individualized prediction of postoperative structural failure. Such an approach has the potential to assist surgeons in preoperative risk stratification, optimize surgical strategies, and facilitate informed discussions with patients regarding prognosis and postoperative expectations, serving as a decision-support aid rather than a definitive preoperative predictor.

Our findings align with prior observational and meta-analytic evidence identifying age, tear size, tendon quality, and biomechanical loading as key determinants of re-tear2,3,8,26. Our study differs in several aspects. First, whereas most earlier investigations examined isolated predictors, we integrated six independent variables into a unified model, enabling individualized risk estimation. Second, few prior predictive models included intraoperative variables such as repair tension; our inclusion of this parameter, despite its subjective nature, incorporates a potentially modifiable surgical factor. Third, although recent machine-learning approaches have been applied to ARCR outcomes, they often lack interpretability, whereas our nomogram balances predictive performance with clinical transparency. Finally, by combining preoperative and intraoperative data, our model is applicable to both surgical planning and intraoperative decision-making, enhancing its practical utility. In clinical use, a preoperative, imaging-based estimate should be treated as an approximate range and then refined intraoperatively when repair tension is available; references to “optimizing surgical strategies” pertain to this staged workflow. Clinically, preoperative risk estimates can guide counseling, tension-reduction strategies, and selection of more robust fixation (e.g., double-row or suture-bridge constructs) and—when risk is high—consideration of partial repair, graft augmentation, or superior capsular reconstruction, together with a more protective rehabilitation plan.

Advancing age was significantly associated with an increased risk of re-tear, aligning with findings from large MRI-based series and meta-analyses demonstrating that patients aged ≥ 60 years have markedly reduced tendon healing potential, attributable to diminished vascularity, decreased fibroblast proliferation, and altered collagen composition27,28. Although age is inherently a non-modifiable factor, its quantified weight in our model—integrated alongside modifiable intraoperative parameters—facilitates a more refined risk stratification and enables tailored, evidence-based preoperative counseling.

A larger maximal tear diameter was strongly associated with an elevated risk of re-tear, consistent with previous evidence demonstrating that tear size is a key determinant of postoperative structural integrity. Meyer et al. reported that tears ≥ 3 cm were associated with a more than two-fold increase in re-tear risk, largely attributable to greater repair site strain and reduced tendon-to-bone contact area29. Unlike earlier studies that examined tear size in isolation, our model incorporates tear size alongside tendon retraction and intraoperative repair tension, thereby capturing their combined biomechanical influence on healing potential.

Severe tendon retraction—specifically Patte grade III—emerged as a strong predictor of re-tear. This finding is supported by MRI-based studies demonstrating that modified Patte grade III provides high specificity for predicting both irreparability and postoperative re-tear in large-to-massive tears30. Unlike prior research that often regarded retraction merely as a surrogate of chronic tear involution, our analysis—adjusted for tear size and fatty infiltration—suggests that tendon retraction independently compromises repair integrity, likely by increasing tension at the tendon–bone interface and reducing effective tendon mobilization.

High-grade fatty infiltration (Goutallier grade ≥ 3) was identified as a strong independent predictor of re-tear. This finding aligns with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which reported steadily increasing odds of re-tear with higher grades of fatty degeneration: moderate infiltration conferred nearly double the risk (OR ≈ 1.95), while severe fatty infiltration increased the risk more than threefold (OR ≈ 3.37) compared to mild degeneration12. These observations underscore that fatty infiltration reflects muscle degeneration and structural poor tendon quality, which compromise healing. In contrast to prior models that often treated fatty infiltration in isolation, our model highlights its synergistic role alongside intraoperative repair tension, emphasizing the combined impact of poor biological tissue quality and mechanical stress on repair failure.

A positive tangent sign—indicating marked supraspinatus muscle atrophy—was an independent predictor of re-tear in our model. This aligns with MRI-based studies showing that a positive tangent sign has high specificity for poor outcomes and irreparable tears, effectively differentiating severe atrophy from milder cases. For instance, in a cohort of full-thickness tears, a positive tangent sign was strongly associated with irreparability (post-test probability exceeding 80%)31. Similarly, other research has correlated positive tangent sign with larger tear size and higher fatty infiltration grades, reinforcing its role as a marker of advanced pathology32. While atrophy is often viewed as a chronicity indicator, our multivariable model—adjusted for tear size and fatty degeneration—confirms that supraspinatus muscle atrophy independently compromises repair integrity, likely through biomechanical weakening of the muscle–tendon unit.

High intraoperative repair tension was associated with more than a fourfold increase in re-tear risk in our cohort. This finding aligns with biomechanical and clinical evidence demonstrating that excessive repair tension significantly compromises rotator cuff integrity. A controlled study using a tensiometer found that tension ≥ 35 N at the footprint was strongly predictive of repair failure, exhibiting an area under the ROC curve of approximately 0.80 (sensitivity 84%, specificity 68%)33 . In another clinical series, repairs performed under tension ≤ 30 N showed markedly lower failure rates and improved functional outcomes34. While previous work has qualitatively recognized the detrimental effect of overtensioning, few models have quantitatively incorporated it. Our findings confirm that intraoperative tension plays an independent and modifiable role in repair success, even after controlling for tear size and tendon retraction.

Variables such as sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, repair configuration, and acromion morphology were not independent predictors of re-tear. The null effect for repair configuration is consistent with evidence that, once tendon quality and biomechanical factors are addressed, configuration alone has limited impact on outcomes35. Age remained independently associated with re-tear, though with a modest effect size, plausibly attenuated by mediation through fatty infiltration, tangent sign, retraction, and repair tension. By contrast, diabetes did not persist as an independent predictor after adjustment, likely reflecting perioperative glycemic optimization at our institution, a low proportion of poorly controlled cases, and adjustment for mediators (e.g., fatty infiltration/atrophy, repair tension) that can absorb diabetes-related risk. Differences across studies in exposure definitions (diagnosis vs. HbA1c/control status), case mix, covariate adjustment, and outcome ascertainment (imaging modality and timing) likely contribute to discrepant findings. Prospective capture of glycemic control metrics (e.g., HbA1c and diabetes duration) will enable a more granular evaluation of diabetes-related effects.

This study’s single-center, retrospective design may limit external generalizability; caution is therefore warranted when extrapolating to other institutions and populations, and prospective multicenter external validation—with potential local recalibration—will be required. Retrospective selection may introduce bias. We used consecutive enrollment and protocolized 6–12-month imaging (uniform Sugaya, blinded dual reading), but residual and attrition-related bias may remain. Surgeon effects were not modeled; despite standardized technique and rehabilitation, residual inter-surgeon variability may persist. Imaging-modality selection (MRI vs ultrasonography) may introduce heterogeneity and selection effects despite uniform criteria and blinded dual reading; future studies will standardize modality use and conduct modality-stratified external validation. The model was calibrated to predict re-tear at 12 months; longer-term structural outcomes and their correlation with functional recovery remain unverified. Future studies will include longer-term (≥ 24–36 months) structural and functional follow-up. Additionally, potentially relevant predictors—including quantitative MRI biomarkers, tendon histology, and rehabilitation compliance/protocol differences—were not available; prospective capture and inclusion are planned. Future research should aim to externally validate the model in multicenter prospective cohorts, incorporate objective intraoperative tension measurements, and integrate advanced imaging biomarkers for tendon quality assessment. Repair tension was recorded subjectively without tensiometry; accordingly, preoperative estimates that omit tension are approximate, and future multicenter studies will incorporate objective tension measurement and external validation/recalibration. Linking predicted re-tear risk to long-term functional outcomes and conducting cost-effectiveness analyses will be essential to establish its utility in guiding surgical decision-making and resource allocation.

Conclusion

This study developed and internally validated a nomogram incorporating age, tear size, tendon retraction, fatty infiltration, muscle atrophy, and intraoperative repair tension to predict re-tear risk after ARCR. The model demonstrated good accuracy and clinical utility, offering a practical tool for individualized risk assessment and surgical planning. External validation and integration of objective biomechanical and biological measures are needed to confirm its broader applicability.
