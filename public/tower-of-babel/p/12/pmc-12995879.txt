Improvements in sustainable energy utilization in global urban areas: Insights from remote sensing surveys
Authors: Wen Song, Shisong Cao, Linlin Lu, Mingyi Du, Meizi Yang, Jingyi Chen, Jie Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study investigates the global status of sustainable energy use in urban built-up areas (UBAs), addressing a critical data gap for achieving Sustainable Development Goals. By integrating multi-source remote sensing and gridded data from 2001 to 2019 regarding GDP, population, electricity, and carbon emissions, the researchers constructed a Sustainable Utilization Energy in Urban Built-up Areas (SUEUBA) index for 41 countries. The analysis reveals a steady global improvement, characterized by consistent progress in developed nations and a polarized performance among developing countries, where some show rapid growth while others stagnate. Major economies face distinct challenges due to complex socioeconomic conditions. These findings underscore the urgent need for differentiated policy strategies—ranging from infrastructure upgrades in high-income regions to expanded renewable access in rapidly urbanizing areas—and provide a scalable framework for monitoring and guiding sustainable urban energy transitions.Subject areas: Environmental science, Applied sciences, Energy sustainability

Introduction

Promoting sustainable utilization energy in urban built-up areas (SUEUBA) is crucial for achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).1 Although cities account for less than 3% of Earth’s land area,2 they accommodate approximately 55% of the global population, consume nearly 78% of total energy resources,3 and contribute over 80% of global GDP.4 The rapid pace of urbanization exacerbates energy demand. For example, a 1% increase in urbanization has been associated with a 2.27% rise in energy intensity in Saudi Arabia during 1971–2012.5 Similarly, in China, electricity and coal energy intensity increased by 0.753%–1.473% between 1995 and 2012,6 jeopardizing progress toward more than half of the SDGs.1 However, the complexity and opacity of available data hinder a comprehensive assessment of energy utilization in urban built-up areas (UBAs), leaving the sustainability of urban energy use uncertain.7

Satellite observations provide a viable approach for assessing SUEUBA.8,9 High-resolution remote sensing data have been employed to estimate energy generation potential on building surfaces.10,11,12 However, these localized assessments are insufficient for characterizing overall energy consumption patterns in UBAs. Although remote sensing has been applied to analyze urban transportation systems,13 industrial activities,14 and infrastructure development, significant structural barriers continue to impede the effective integration of satellite data into urban energy governance frameworks.15 Existing research often examines satellite-derived energy data in isolation for specific applications, lacking a comprehensive, cross-sectoral analytical framework.

Global mapping of electricity consumption provides continuous spatial and temporal datasets, facilitating a more holistic assessment of SUEUBA and enabling optimization strategies for emission reduction.16,17 The integration of socioeconomic variables such as population density and GDP allows for efficiency evaluations and structural analyses of urban energy sustainability. For instance, combining electricity consumption and CO2 emissions with GDP and population statistics enables the calculation of energy intensity and per capita emissions, offering insights into regional energy efficiency dynamics.18,19 Additionally, Wang et al.20 further advanced this field by developing an urban energy consumption model that integrates multi-source spatial data—including radar imaging, points of interest, and building footprint information—based on the location, geometry, and function of structures. This model has been applied to simulate energy consumption in UBAs of Beijing and Shanghai, China. While existing studies have leveraged multi-source datasets to assess energy efficiency, substantial gaps remain in the spatial evaluation of energy structures and consumption heterogeneity across UBAs.

While existing studies have leveraged multi-source datasets to assess energy efficiency, significant gaps remain in spatially evaluating energy structures and consumption heterogeneity across UBAs. We addressed these gaps by introducing a comprehensive spatial evaluation framework that incorporates SDG indicators, using satellite data and socioeconomic variables for a more holistic understanding of sustainable energy utilization in urban contexts. To bridge this gap, we identified and adapted energy-related SDG indicators21,22,23 that can be quantified using remote sensing techniques, enabling a systematic assessment of sustainable energy utilization in UBAs. We employed four key indicators within the SDGs framework for global SUEUBA evaluation: SDG 7.3.1 (energy intensity), SDG 9.4.1 (carbon intensity), SDG 9.4.2 (power generation carbon emission intensity), and SDG 13.2.2 (annual per capita CO2 emissions) (detailed descriptions are in the Methods section). The SUEUBA score was derived as the average of these indicators. We further examined the relative contributions of these indicators to the SUEUBA score, identified temporal trends, and analyzed spatial clustering patterns across countries. Results

Heterogeneity in the sustainable utilization of urban energy globally

The SUEUBA scores and their four associated indicators exhibit significant correlations across 41 countries (Figure 1). Between 2001 and 2019, Paraguay recorded the highest annual median SUEUBA score at 92.11, with nine countries—primarily in the Mediterranean region—achieving scores above 90 (Figure 1A). In contrast, only Trinidad and Tobago (62.88) and Uzbekistan (64.12) fell below the threshold of 70. Among major economies, the United States and China scored 82.63 and 77.47, respectively, with a difference of 6.67 points.

Overall, the four indicators positively contribute to the SUEUBA score. These include a decline in the ratio of electricity consumption to actual GDP (Figure 1B), a reduction in carbon dioxide emissions per unit of value-added (Figure 1C), a decrease in carbon dioxide emissions per unit of electricity consumption (Figure 1D), and a decline in per capita carbon dioxide emissions (Figure 1E). These improvements are particularly evident in Western European countries such as the United Kingdom, France, and Germany, as well as in Japan, Southeast Asia, Latin America nations, including Peru and Argentina. However, notable regional variations exist. While all four SUEUBA indicators positively influence the overall score in the United States, a different trend is observed in China. In China’s UBAs, rising per capita carbon dioxide emissions have driven energy efficiency improvements (Figure 1E). This is largely attributed to strengthened governmental regulations and policy interventions in response to increasing emissions, fostering technological advancements and higher energy efficiency standards.29,30 Similar patterns are observed in Iran (Middle East) and Sudan (Africa), where heightened awareness of sustainable development and energy efficiency—driven by rising urban carbon dioxide emissions—has led to continuous improvements in energy efficiency and the optimization of urban energy use structures.31,32

Continuous improvements in urban energy utilization

Globally, SUEUBA has exhibited a continuous upward trend (Figures 2A and 2B), with the intensity of energy utilization improvement (Theil-Sen, TS) ranging from 0.06 in Italy to 3.52 in Sudan, and an average growth of 0.52. Only Trinidad and Tobago (−0.96) and Tanzania (−0.31) experienced a declining trend in SUEUBA. Developed countries (Table S4), including the United States, Japan, and France, demonstrate steady improvement in SUEUBA, but with lower intensity. In contrast, developing countries show higher intensity but weaker overall trends. For instance, Sudan has the highest improvement intensity (3.52) but a relatively modest trend of 0.64.33,34 This difference arises because developed countries typically start from a higher SUEUBA baseline and continue to implement policies that sustain incremental progress. In contrast, developing countries often exhibit greater potential for improvement but face higher levels of uncertainty. China stands out with an intensity of 0.40 and a trend of 0.94, surpassing developed nations such as France (0.92) and the UK (0.91). This can be attributed to policy-driven energy transitions, technological advancements, and economic restructuring associated with rapid urbanization.35,36

Most countries exhibit strong positive trends in the indicators SDG7.3.1 (Figures 2C and 2D), SDG9.4.1 (Figures 2E and 2F), and SDG9.4.2 (Figures 2G and 2H). However, several countries show negative trends in SDG13.2.2 (Figures 2I and 2J). SDG7.3.1, which measures energy intensity, shows a highly significant positive trend in most countries, highlighting substantial progress in energy efficiency improvements (Mann-Kendall, MK). Notable examples include Uzbekistan (MK = 0.97, TS = 1.21) and China (MK = 1, TS = 1.03). In contrast, Italy (MK = −0.46, TS = −0.04) and Germany (MK = −0.45, TS = −0.16) exhibit slight downward trends, indicating an increase in electricity consumption per unit of GDP. SDG9.4.1, which measures carbon intensity per unit of economic value-added, shows strong positive trends (Figure 2E), with 11 countries reporting an improvement in intensity exceeding 1, and 4 countries exceeding 2. Latvia and Poland demonstrate particularly remarkable gains, with MK values above 0.9 and TS values over 1.55. Conversely, Trinidad and Tobago (MK = −0.49, TS = −2.15) show a sharp increase in carbon intensity, indicating a notable rise in CO2 emissions relative to GDP. SDG9.4.2 follows a similar pattern to SDG9.4.1, with most countries reducing CO2 emissions per unit of electricity consumption. Sudan stands out with a TS value of 9.50—more than three times higher than Slovakia, the second-ranked country in this metric. On the other hand, Tanzania and Trinidad and Tobago exhibit worsening trends, with the latter’s decline approaching 1.8. Unlike the other indicators, SDG13.2.2, which tracks per capita CO2 emissions, shows negative trends in nine countries, including Trinidad and Tobago. The most pronounced decline is observed in Belarus (MK = −0.37, TS = −0.16). These negative trends are closely linked to urban population migration patterns pronounced decline is observed in37 and economic expansion. Additionally, economic expansion often leads to higher overall energy consumption, as efficiency improvements may be offset by increased production, a pattern evident in China.38 The variation in trends across these four indicators suggests that not all countries are progressing uniformly in all aspects of sustainable energy utilization. Differences in outcomes are influenced by national policy choices, resource allocation, and broader economic conditions.

Diverse improvements in urban energy utilization

The rate of improvement in sustainable energy utilization in urban built-up areas varies significantly across countries (Figure 3). Among the top ten nations with the highest annual SUEUBA growth rates, eight are developing nations (Figure 3A), while Ireland (0.83%) and Poland (0.72%) are the only developed countries. Countries with annual growth rates exceeding 1% include Slovakia (3.58%), Sudan (2.27%), Uzbekistan (2.07%), Latvia (1.94%), and Peru (1.33%). The leading three countries demonstrate substantial increases in individual indicator scores: Slovakia’s SDG 9.4.1 improves by 29.19% annually, Sudan’s SDG 9.4.2 by 14.63%, and Uzbekistan’s SDG 7.3.1 and SDG 9.4.1 by 40.12% and 11.58%, respectively. However, all three countries exhibit a declining trend in SDG 13.2.2, indicating increasing per capita CO2 emissions. Conversely, among the ten countries with the lowest SUEUBA growth rates, seven are developing countries, with France, Japan, and Italy representing the only developed economies (Figure 3B). Trinidad and Tobago (−0.73%) shows negative SUEUBA growth, with Trinidad and Tobago showing negative growth in 75% of its indicators. Overall, developed countries tend to exhibit stable but slow SUEUBA growth, with a median growth rate of 0.19%, a standard deviation of 0.23%, and a ratio of top 10 to bottom 10 countries at 38.46%. In contrast, developing nations show more polarized growth, with a median growth rate of 0.26%, a standard deviation of 0.87%, and a ratio of top 10 to bottom 10 countries at 53.57%.

A clustering analysis of different countries’ SUEUBA development patterns (Figure 4) reveals that nations with lower clustering values tend to have stronger overall SUEUBA performance. Nine countries, including five developed and four developing economies, show relatively strong performance across the four indicators. Notably, SDG9.4.1, SDG9.4.2, SDG13.2.2, and overall SUEUBA scores are divided into two clusters, while SDG7.3.1 is categorized into five distinct clusters. For SDG9.4.1, SDG9.4.2, SDG13.2.2, and overall scores, countries in Cluster 1, compared to those in Cluster 2, demonstrate lower energy consumption, lower CO2 emissions, cleaner and more efficient electricity generation, and lower per capita CO2 emissions while maintaining the same or higher GDP levels. Regarding SDG 7.3.1, the five-cluster division represents distinct levels and structural characteristics of energy efficiency among countries, ranging from high-efficiency, technology-driven systems to economies with substantial efficiency improvement potential. Cluster 1 represents the highest-performing nations, with Germany standing out due to its Energiewende initiative,39 which has significantly reduced energy intensity and expanded renewable energy deployment. Clusters 2 and 3 include countries with moderate-to-strong energy efficiency performance. Algeria and China, for example, have made substantial investments in renewable energy—particularly solar and wind power—while also improving industrial and electrical distribution efficiency.40,41 However, while Algeria’s progress places it closer to Cluster 2, China’s vast energy demands continue to pose challenges, positioning it in Cluster 3. Cluster 4 includes nations such as Costa Rica, which has made steady progress due to its reliance on renewable energy, particularly hydropower. Nonetheless, challenges remain in energy-intensive sectors such as transportation, where further efficiency improvements are needed.42 Finally, Cluster 5 comprises countries with significant energy efficiency potential, such as Norway and Uzbekistan. Norway’s energy sector is dominated by hydropower, which supplies over 96% of its electricity demand, making it a global leader in renewable energy utilization.43 Meanwhile, Uzbekistan, despite its reliance on natural gas, has initiated a series of energy reforms aimed at improving efficiency and expanding renewable energy capacity.44

Discussion

Comparison with other studies mapped in urban energy sustainability

The study presented an innovative methodology that integrates remote sensing data with key SDG indicators to evaluate sustainable energy utilization in urban built-up areas. The conceptual framework of this approach is illustrated in Figure 5. By combining multi-source remote sensing data, we constructed four key indicators along with an overall SUEUBA score to assess global energy consumption. Unlike prior research, which typically focuses on administrative units or single-sector data, our approach targets UBAs specifically, providing a more detailed and localized assessment of urban energy demand. This enhanced spatial resolution allows for a more accurate analysis of energy use patterns and trends in urban environments.7 Most studies on urban energy consumption rely on administrative boundaries,17 which may not fully capture energy use patterns in cities that span multiple jurisdictions. For instance, Chen et al.45 leveraged nighttime light remote sensing to estimate energy consumption per unit of GDP across 336 Chinese cities and 2,735 counties from 1997 to 2017, revealing a broad downward trend. Yet, assessing energy utilization at the urban built-up area (UBA) level yields far greater spatial precision in evaluating urban energy demand and sustainability. Complementing this, population- and GDP-based efficiency metrics offer sharper insights into underlying socioeconomic dynamics.46 Furthermore, Hemati et al.47 harnessed Sentinel-5P TROPOMI daily observations to track methane concentrations across six major North American metropolitan areas, achieving superior precision over prior studies and indicating that historical methane emissions may have been substantially underestimated. By integrating per capita emissions and emissions per unit area, their method delivers more actionable policy insights. Nonetheless, these efforts fall short of explicitly anchoring results to SDG indicators.

Concept of sustainability research on urban energy utilization

Additionally, recent analyses have broadened the lens to renewable energy’s role in economic stability. Yadav48 applied a dynamic panel threshold model to show that renewable energy consumption nonlinearly dampens economic risk—an effect markedly strengthened in economies with high trade openness and GDP per capita. While our study centers on UBAs through remote sensing-derived SDG indicators, Yadav’s work enriches this perspective by highlighting the macroeconomic stabilizing role of renewable energy. Together, these findings underscore the importance of cohesive policies that integrate renewable energy deployment with trade liberalization and economic expansion to support both urban sustainability and broader economic resilience.

Within the SDG framework, our study selected indicators that enhance spatial analysis and assessment,49 allowing for a more detailed understanding of disparities and challenges in urban sustainable energy development. Improved data accuracy enables comparative analyses of SDG indicators across urban areas, helping to identify regions that require energy conservation and emissions reductions. Our study also builds on previous research by integrating diverse remote sensing datasets, including GDP, population, electricity consumption, and CO2 emissions, similar to the methodology proposed by Avtar et al.50 However, our approach normalizes these indicators on a 0–100 scale, enabling standardized cross-national comparisons. Additionally, our findings highlight top-performing countries, such as Paraguay, and reveal a polarization trend among developing nations, aligning with conclusions from the Global Sustainable Development Report.51 By linking urban energy utilization with SDG indicators, our research provides a more comprehensive framework for evaluating sustainable energy use in built-up areas.

Influencing factors and policy response

The sustained improvement in global SUEUBA arises from a complex integration of technological advancements, regulatory frameworks, economic instruments, heightened societal awareness, and effective urban planning. Innovations in energy technologies and the adoption of energy-efficient construction methods have substantially reduced energy consumption, directly supporting SDG target 7.3 (doubling the global rate of improvement in energy efficiency). Governments contribute through targeted incentives and strict regulations,52 aligning with SDG target 9.4, which emphasizes upgrading infrastructure and improving resource-use efficiency. Meanwhile, market-based approaches and sustainable financing mechanisms provide essential support for energy conservation efforts,53,54 reinforcing SDG target 13.2 by integrating climate considerations into national policies. Additionally, growing public recognition of environmental challenges has encouraged sustainable consumption and low-carbon lifestyles, further strengthening energy efficiency gains.55

The alignment between policy implementation and practical outcomes is evident in SUEUBA trends from 2001 to 2019. Paraguay ranked first in SUEUBA scores, primarily due to its government-supported hydroelectric infrastructure, which contributes to SDG target 7.2 by ensuring a high share of renewable energy in the national energy mix. By exporting surplus electricity to neighboring countries, Paraguay not only alleviated its domestic energy burden but also improved energy efficiency and generated economic benefits.56 Additionally, Paraguay’s relatively low level of industrialization and limited energy demand from the industrial sector further reinforced progress toward SDG targets 9.4 and 13.2.57 Sudan recorded the highest growth in SUEUBA, driven by significant investments in grid expansion and modernization, including the replacement of outdated transmission systems and the adoption of smart grid technologies.58 These efforts directly support SDG target 9.4 by enhancing infrastructure efficiency and resilience. The country also advanced its renewable energy sector, particularly in solar and wind power, in line with SDG target 7.2, supported by government-led initiatives and international cooperation.59 Encouraging private and international investments in renewable energy played a crucial role, exemplified by Sudan’s collaboration with the United Arab Emirates in developing solar power plants.59 Slovakia achieved the fastest improvements in energy efficiency, largely due to comprehensive government policies, including energy efficiency legislation and dedicated financing mechanisms. These measures explicitly targeted SDG target 7.3, enabling energy-saving renovations across residential and commercial sectors.60 while also contributing to long-term climate mitigation goals under SDG 13.2.

The identified clusters reveal distinct developmental pathways—from mature, stable systems (Cluster 1) to rapidly transforming but uneven ones (Cluster 5)—providing policy-relevant insights for differentiated energy transition strategies. Accordingly, our findings suggest that developed economies with mature infrastructure should prioritize deep retrofitting, low-carbon technological upgrading, and carbon neutrality targets, closely aligned with SDG targets 7.3, 9.4, and 13.2. In contrast, rapidly developing nations should focus on improving energy equity, grid reliability, and renewable energy access, consistent with SDG targets 7.1 and 7.2. These differentiated approaches support more context-specific and equitable pathways toward sustainable urban energy transitions.

Limitations of the study

Using multi-source remotely sensed products, we selected indicators from the SDGs framework to map and assess energy utilization in UBAs, effectively evaluating SUEUBA’s trends. However, several uncertainties remain. Integrating multiple remote sensing datasets for urban energy analysis may introduce cumulative errors. Variations in data acquisition, processing, and analysis can lead to discrepancies when combining different remote sensing products, potentially affecting the accuracy of the final results.61,62 Although we conducted correlation and robustness analyses using Spearman and Pearson’s coefficients to examine the relationship between the four indicators and the SUEUBA score, significant correlations were observed in only 41 countries—less than one-fourth of all nations. This limitation suggests that the method may be more effective in specific contexts and requires further refinement for broader applicability.

Additionally, the spatial resolution of several remote sensing datasets remains a notable limitation. For instance, CO2 emission data provided at a 0.1° resolution (approximately 11 km per grid cell) may not adequately capture intra-urban variability or localized emission hotspots.2 Such coarse resolution can smooth fine-scale differences within cities, potentially biasing national or regional patterns toward larger metropolitan areas. As a result, the findings presented here are likely more representative of major cities and urban agglomerations, while smaller or emerging urban centers may be underrepresented. This limitation reflects a broader challenge in global-scale urban sustainability research, where the harmonization of multi-source datasets often comes at the expense of spatial detail. Future studies should prioritize the use of urban CO2 emission datasets with spatial resolutions of ≤1 km to better capture intra-urban heterogeneity and localized emission dynamics. In this regard, the integration of emerging high-resolution greenhouse gas datasets (e.g., ODIAC, GOSAT, or TROPOMI-based products) and advanced data-fusion techniques could substantially improve the spatial representation of urban energy use. Despite these constraints, the present study provides a transparent and scalable framework for identifying spatial patterns in urban energy utilization, offering valuable insights for the spatial assessment of SDG-related energy sustainability. Continued refinement of data quality and analytical methods will further enhance the robustness and policy relevance of such global assessments.

In summary, this study examined sustainable energy utilization in global urban built-up areas using multi-source remote sensing data, revealing both overall progress and persistent regional disparities. Between 2001 and 2019, the global SUEUBA score showed a steady upward trend, with developing countries demonstrating notable advancements. Paraguay achieved the highest SUEUBA score, Sudan exhibited the most significant improvement, and Slovakia recorded the fastest annual growth rate. Improvements across the four key indicators contributed positively to the observed changes in SUEUBA performance. Although limitations remain regarding data resolution, this work provides a scalable framework for assessing sustainable urban energy use worldwide and offers valuable insights to support targeted energy-transition strategies in urban contexts.

Method details

Overview of the methodology

The extensive range of remotely sensed data products helps address a core theoretical challenge in sustainable urban energy utilization research: the lack of consistent, spatially comparable information across countries and over time (Figure 5). Figure 5 presents the conceptual framework of this study, illustrating how remotely sensed data support the assessment of sustainable energy utilization in UBAs through their alignment with SDGs. Our conceptual framework links this data challenge to SDGs, which provide a structured basis for defining the dimensions of sustainable energy use in UBAs. To operationalize this framework, we select energy-related indicators from SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), and SDG 13 (Climate Action). These goals capture three fundamental components of theoretical models of sustainable energy systems: efficiency, structural decarbonization, and climate mitigation performance. We evaluate each SDG indicator based on three criteria. First, the indicator must represent a core theoretical dimension of sustainable energy systems, such as energy intensity, carbon intensity, or emissions outcomes, grounded in established environmental–economic modeling principles.64 Second, it must be quantifiable in a way that supports policy accountability within the SDG monitoring framewor.65 Third, it must be measurable using remote sensing products or gridded datasets to enable spatially explicit assessments in UBAs, where traditional statistical reporting is often unavailable.49 Building on these theoretical principles, our framework treats the four chosen indicators as complementary dimensions of sustainable urban energy performance. Their aggregation through a simple averaging method follows the standard SDG normalization approach,66 which assumes equal importance across indicators when no empirical or normative weighting scheme is justified.

The framework is then extended through time-series analysis to capture dynamic patterns of improvement or deterioration. By linking indicator trajectories with socioeconomic and infrastructural characteristics, the analysis helps reveal system-level transitions, which are central to theoretical models of sustainable urban energy development. These trends allow us to identify regional disparities and support evidence-based policy design for urban energy transitions.

Data sources and indicator selection

By selecting, modifying, and incorporating SDG indicators based on their representation through remotely sensed data,21,22,23 we identified four key indicators: SDG 7.3.1 (original), SDG 9.4.1 (original), SDG 9.4.2 (added), and SDG 13.2.2 (modified). These SDG indicators were chosen because they are widely recognized in the literature as central to evaluating urban energy sustainability and directly align with key objectives in sustainable development, such as energy efficiency and carbon emissions reductio.64,65

SDG 7.3.1 and SDG 9.4.1 are directly adopted from the SDGs framework, addressing energy efficiency and carbon emissions reduction, respectively. SDG 7.3.1 measures energy intensity, defined as the ratio of electricity consumption to GDP, reflecting the relationship between energy usage and economic output. A lower value indicates greater energy efficiency, meaning less energy is required to generate the same or greater economic output. Similarly, SDG 9.4.1 assesses carbon intensity per unit of economic value added, expressed as the ratio of carbon dioxide emissions to GDP. A lower value suggests a reduction in carbon emissions relative to economic output, signaling more sustainable economic practices. SDG 9.4.2 was introduced to quantify the carbon intensity of electricity production, an essential factor not adequately captured by the existing indicators. This indicator, defined as the ratio of CO2 emissions to electricity consumption, provides insights into the carbon efficiency of electricity generation, especially in urban areas. It reflects the shift toward cleaner energy production, with lower values indicating increased reliance on renewable energy sources or more efficient fossil fuel technologies. SDG 13.2.2 was modified to focus on annual per capita carbon dioxide emissions, which is a more appropriate indicator for urban-level climate-related impact assessments. A decrease in per capita emissions signifies improved energy efficiency and greater adoption of low-carbon energy sources, contributing to overall sustainability.

The calculation of the indicators relied on multi-source remote sensing and gridded socioeconomic datasets covering economic activity, energy consumption, population distribution, and urban entities. Each dataset has been defined with its measurement units, temporal coverage, spatial resolution, and preprocessing steps, and the data sources are now provided in Table 1. Real GDP is reported in constant 2015 USD, electricity consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh), and population data as the number of residents per grid cell. EDGAR v8.0 provides emissions in tonnes per grid cell, while global urban entity data represent built-up areas in categorical form. To ensure spatial consistency, all continuous variables (GDP, electricity consumption, population, emissions) were resampled to a uniform resolution of 0.1° using bilinear interpolation. Urban entity data were converted into vector format and assigned using nearest-neighbor methods to delineate UBA boundaries. Based on these standardized datasets, we calculated energy utilization trends for global UBAs from 2001 to 2019.

Missing data were limited. When grid-level gaps occurred within a dataset, we filled them using spatial interpolation from adjacent valid cells. When a variable was missing for an intermediate year, we used linear interpolation between the nearest available years. No extrapolation was applied beyond the temporal ranges of the original datasets.

To make the indicators comparable, all values were normalized to a 0–100 scale, where 0 represents the lowest performance and 100 the highest. The adjustment formula is as follows:

City-level sustainable energy utilization scores were calculated as the arithmetic mean of four indicators (SDG 7.3.1, SDG 9.4.1, SDG 9.4.2, and SDG 13.2.2), with equal weights assigned to each indicator. At the national level, we used the median city-level score to reduce the influence of extreme values. This approach better reflects typical urban patterns while recognizing that it may underrepresent the performance of very large or highly efficient cities.

All data processing and numerical analyses were conducted in PyCharm, and spatial visualization was performed in ArcMap.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Influence of indicators on overall scoring

By analyzing the four indicators derived from remotely sensed data and their relationship with the SUEUBA score, we gained deeper insights into the sustainable development performance of urban built-up areas. This analysis not only evaluates the effectiveness of different cities in reducing energy consumption and carbon emissions but also highlights variations in sustainable energy practices across UBAs in different countries, identifying which national policies and technological innovations are most effective.

To quantify the relationship between the four indicators and the SUEUBA score, we applied Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ),68 which is well-suited for assessing nonlinear monotonic relationships and is robust against outliers.69 A p-value below 0.05, combined with a positive ρ value, indicates a significant positive contribution of the indicators to the SUEUBA score, whereas a negative ρ value indicates a significant negative contribution. This approach allows us to determine which indicators most strongly influence the sustainable development performance of UBAs.

A country was considered to meet the assessment criteria if all four indicators showed a significant relationship with the SUEUBA scores. A total of 41 countries satisfied this criterion, reducing the impact of potential cumulative errors arising from remotely sensed data processing.70 Additionally, we employed the Pearson correlation coefficient as a secondary validation to test the robustness of our findings.69,71 The results from both Spearman and Pearson analyses were consistent for 35 countries (85.37%), confirming the reliability of the findings (Table S2 reports the Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ < 0.05) between individual SDG indicators and the SUEUBA scores at the country level, with Pearson correlation coefficients provided as a robustness check).

Analyzing temporal and cluster patterns

The temporal trend analysis of the SUEUBA score and its four associated indicators provides critical insights into long-term patterns of urban energy utilization patterns, revealing the effects of policy and technological advancements. To identify significant long-term trends on a global scale, we applied the non-parametric Mann-Kendall (MK) test at a 5% significance level.72,73 The MK test is particularly well-suited for time series analysis as it detects monotonic trends without assuming a specific data distribution, making it robust against skewed data and missing values.74 This characteristic is especially valuable when analyzing time series data, derived from remotely sensed sources. To quantify the magnitude of detected trends, we employed the Theil-Sen slope estimator (TS), a non-parametric method that provides a robust trend estimate even in the presence of outliers.75,76 This approach enhances the reliability of the trend assessment by minimizing the influence of extreme values (more detailed data are provided in Table S3).

For clustering analysis, we employed the K-means algorithm, an unsupervised machine learning method well suited for detecting patterns in long time-series datasets.77 K-means was selected for its simplicity, computational efficiency, and strong scalability to large datasets, whereas alternative approaches such as hierarchical or density-based clustering (e.g., DBSCAN) were deemed less optimal due to higher data dimensionality and lower interpretability. The algorithm minimizes within-cluster variance by assigning data points to the nearest cluster centroid, making it particularly effective in identifying temporal similarities across multiple years.78,79 To determine the optimal number of clusters (k), we applied the silhouette coefficient, which quantifies the quality of clustering by measuring how well each data point fits within its assigned cluster relative to other clusters. Silhouette coefficients range from −1 to 1, with higher scores indicating better-defined clusters. We tested various values k from 2 to 10 and selected the cluster configuration that maximized the silhouette score, ensuring clear and well-separated groupings. Before clustering, input variables were normalized to account for differences in scale. The clustering analysis incorporated key urban energy indicators, including energy intensity, carbon intensity, and other relevant sustainability metrics. The K-means algorithm was then applied to identify distinct patterns in energy utilization and sustainability practices across countries, providing a comparative framework for evaluating urban energy transitions.
