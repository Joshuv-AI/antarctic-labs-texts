Potentials and practical challenges of terbium-161 labeled radiopharmaceuticals
Authors: Carolline M Ntihabose, Maryana Handula, Amber Piet, Savanne Beekman, Louise van Dalen, Negin Eskandari, Asude Aydogan, Debra Stuurman, Corrina de Ridder, Mark Konijnenberg, Yann Seimbille, Erik de Blois
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to promising preclinical studies and clinical case reports, Tb-161 labeled radiopharmaceuticals for targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT) have gained interest. Unlike Lu-177, Tb-161 not only emits β− particles, but also Auger and conversion electrons, which may improve the current therapeutic efficacy of TRT. However, before implementing Tb-161 for clinical use, several steps are required (E.g., development, optimization, validation). Therefore, this study focuses on the purity of Tb-161 as well as the detection and quantification of Tb–Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals. As multiple studies are currently aiming to determine the therapeutic effect of Tb-161 labeled radiopharmaceuticals, standardizing and evaluating Tb-161 is essential to be able to compare its therapeutic potential against Lu-177 and other radionuclides. Therefore, we established accurate detection methods, impurity measurements, radiolabeling protocols, and quality control for Tb-161 labeled pharmaceuticals. Parameters of Tb-161 labeled radiopharmaceuticals were investigated and exemplified by [161 Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE, in comparison with [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE.ResultsAnalysis of Tb-161 stock solution demonstrated the presence of metal impurities (ΣFe, Zn, Cu, Gd, and Dy) at the reference day and increased over time. The geometric effect of vial type demonstrated a decrease in activity when a vial was used without point-source. For both Lu-177 and Tb-161 labeled DOTA-TATE, high radiochemical yield and purity (> 95%) were obtained and remained stable (> 90%) up to 24 h in solution.ConclusionAnalysis of Tb-161 stock showed an increase in metal impurities over time, which could interfere with the production of Tb-161 labeled radiopharmaceuticals. The low gamma energy (48.9 keV and 74.6 keV) of Tb-161 needs to be considered in (pre)clinical applications when quantifying activity. For Tb-161 labeled pharmaceuticals, similar radiolabeling conditions as Lu-177 have been shown to be successful and highly stable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-025-00390-3.Keywords: Terbium-161, Lutetium-177, Targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT), Radiopharmaceuticals, Active pharmaceutical ingredient (API)

Introduction

In the field of targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT), lutetium-177 (Lu-177) (β−) is considered a pioneering radionuclide in TRT for the treatment of (metastasized) cancer patients (Vogel et al. 2021). Several targeting agents have been radiolabeled with Lu-177, such as [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE and [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T for the treatment of neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), respectively (Brabander et al. 2017; Schuchardt et al. 2022; Hofland et al. 2022). These radiopharmaceuticals are only examples of why Lu-177 is indispensable and has an important role in the field of nuclear medicine.

Over the years, a variety of radiopharmaceuticals for TRT have been developed using Lu-177 for the treatment of a range of diseases, resulting in a high demand for this radionuclide (Damiana et al. 2023; Baum et al. 2022; Koustoulidou et al. 2022). As such, a growing scarcity of Lu-177 as an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) for the clinical production of radiopharmaceuticals has arisen. This limitation has been underlined on several occasions (2008, 2010, 2022) when the main Lu-177 producing reactors were unexpectedly out of order, causing a worldwide shortage in Lu-177 and a delay in the treatment of several patients (Vogel et al. 2021). To prevent similar events from occurring in the future, the search for solutions to improve the sustainability of TRT is highly needed. Therefore, an alternative radionuclide could be terbium-161(Tb-161). Terbium has four potential radioisotopes which might be useful for clinical use: Tb-149 (for α-therapy), Tb-152 (for positron emission tomography, PET), Tb-155 (for single photon emission computed tomography, SPECT), and Tb-161 (for β− -therapy) (Laere et al. 2024).

Tb-149 (T1/2 = 4.1 h) could be the ideal candidate for alpha therapy as it has a short range (25–28 µm) with high linear energy transfer (LET), and almost no alpha-emitting (< 0.001%) daughter isotopes. The combination of these properties makes the radionuclide ideal for sparing the healthy surrounding tissue. Studies have proven Tb-149 to be applicable for PET/CT scans. However, it should be noted that the production of this radionuclide has restrictions, as despite many efforts, the availability of Tb-149 remains limited (Laere et al. 2024; Müller et al. 2017; Favaretto et al. 2024).

As for diagnostics and the desire for a theranostic pair, Tb-152(T1/2 = 17.5 h) is ideal to be used as a theranostic pair, as it decays for 83% in electron capture and 17% into positron emission for PET. Recent studies have shown the advantages of Tb-152 over the current frequently used positron emitters, such as Ga-68 (T1/2 = 68 min), whose half-life is significantly shorter than that of Tb-152 or Zr-89 and Cu-64, as they often require other chelators (HBED, macrocyclic chelators vs the standard DOTA, DOTAGA), which might cause a mismatch in biodistribution properties (Collins et al. 2023; Müller et al. 2019).

Another terbium isotope for diagnostic use is Tb-155 (T1/2 = 5.23 d), which is suitable for low-dose SPECT imaging and could be ideal for biodistribution, as it emits a sufficient amount of energy. It has an advantage that the nuclide has a significantly longer half-life than the commonly used nuclides for imaging as Ga-68(T1/2 = 68 min), F-18 (T1/2 = 110 min), and Tc-99 m (T1/2 = 6.01 h). Additionally, Tb-155 has a closer match in physical attributes to the therapeutic lanthanides as Tb-161 and Lu-177, and would therefore be a better match as a theragnostic pair than Ga-68, F-18, or Tc-99 m (Müller et al. 2014).

The terbium with the most successful progress for clinical application is Tb-161. The production of Tb-161 can be achieved by direct and indirect reactor production. However, the indirect method is the most frequently used and preferred as it results in the non-carrier added (n.c.a) Tb-161. N.c.a Tb-161 is produced by neutron irradiation of (enriched) gadolinium-160 (Gd-160) that generates Gd-161(T1/2 = 3.66 min) which decays by β− to Tb-161 (Holiski et al. 2024; Lehenberger et al. 2011). Tb-161 has a half-life of 6.89 days and decays to the stable dysprosium (Dy-161).

Tb-161 emits β− -particles (137.7 keV; 25.7%, 157.4 keV; 6%, mean β−energy of 154 keV) which can be used for therapeutic purposes, and gammas (48.9 keV; 17.0% and 74.6 keV; 10.2%) which can be used for SPECT imaging similarly to Lu-177. As SPECT images have reduced sensitivity, compared to PET/CT, optimization of the SPECT/CT imaging protocol for Tb-161 is still needed. Preclinical and clinical evidence demonstrate that selecting the 75.4 keV peak is preferred as the 48.9 keV peak introduces artifacts in the images. Optimizing the use of low-energy, high-resolution collimators also improved the obtained images. Case studies have proved that even with low administered activity (596 MBq) of Tb-161, metastatic lesions could be identified, and was confirmed by previously performed PET/CT scans. Importantly, as with Lu-177 SPECT/CT, Tb-161 images are not intended as a diagnostic tool but to verify the distribution and assess the tumor uptake. Initial clinical imaging indicates it is feasible to determine reliable images for regions ≥ 1–2 cm. Additionally, the emission properties of Tb-161 mirror those of Lu-177 (Marin et al. 2020; Baum et al. 2021; McIntosh et al. 2024).

As indicated in Table 1, Tb-161 and Lu-177 have key similarities. However, Tb-161 has additional advantages of emitting conversion electrons (3–50 keV) and 2 Auger electrons per decay, which could potentially result in a higher cellular dose on a shorter range. In dosimetry modeling, it was determined that Tb-161 provides 1.3-fold higher dose per MBq compared to Lu-177. Altogether, this makes Tb-161 an ideal addition to Lu-177 for the treatment of malignancies ranging from multiple metastases (< 1 mm) to single cells (10 µm) (Holiski et al. 2024; Lehenberger et al. 2011; Bernhardt et al. 2021; Verburg et al. 2023).

Recently, several preclinical studies and case reports illustrated the value of Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals, causing an increased interest in the radionuclide. Tb-161 could be employed in radiopharmaceuticals for the somatostatin receptor (SSTR2), such as [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TOC, [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TOC-NLS, and [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-LM3, potentially allowing the treatment of NETs. Multiple studies aimed to determine the therapeutic effect of Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals in comparison to Lu-177. In vitro cell viability showed that the [161Tb]Tb-SSTR2 analogues were more effective at killing tumor cells by at least five-fold compared to [177Lu]Lu-SSTR2 analogues (Baum et al. 2022, 2021; Fricke et al. 2024; Marin et al. 2020; Borgna et al. 2022).

However, to this day, the most successful and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)/European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved radiopharmaceutical for the treatment of NETs is [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, also known as Lutathera® (McIntosh et al. 2024; Hennrick and Kopka 2019). Currently, there is no standardized full good manufacturing practice (GMP) phase I trial of NETs with Tb-161. As a first step, this study aims to explore the feasibility of implementing Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals in comparison to Lu-177-labeled radiopharmaceuticals in (pre)clinical settings, exemplified by [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE and [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE.

To implement Tb-161 for clinical use, several steps need to be completed. The first step is focused on radiochemistry, where we establish detection methods, radiolabeling protocols, quality control, and stability for Tb-161 labeled pharmaceuticals. Once the radiochemical procedures were standardized, we evaluated the practical challenges with Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals in preclinical and clinical settings. As the low-energy photons of Tb-161 could result in inaccurate quantification, we determined the effect of geometry in vitro and in vivo settings. In this study, we compared radiochemical and preclinical performance of Tb-161 vs. Lu-177 using [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE and [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE as models. With this output, we aim to create a strong base to be able to perform clinical implementation of Tb-161.

The clinical implementation of Tb-161 will be continued by producing test batches of labeled radiopharmaceuticals and, ultimately, dose-escalation studies to determine the optimal dose with acceptable side effects in patients.

Overall, radiochemical and preclinical performance and standardization are fundamental for a successful clinical implementation of Tb-161 radiolabeled radiopharmaceuticals within our field.

Materials and methods

General information

The chemicals and solvents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without any further purification unless specified. Terbium (Tb(NO3)2), dysprosium (Dy2O3), iron (Fe(NO3)2), copper (Cu(NO3)2), zinc (Zn(NO3)2), and gadolinium (Gd2O3) ICP standards dissolved in 2–3% HNO3 with a concentration of 1 g/L were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Lu-177 n.c.a, [177Lu]LuCl3 with a specific activity of ~ 3000 GBq/mg was purchased via PI Medical (Raamdonksveer, the Netherlands). Terbium-161 n.c.a, [161Tb]TbCl3 was kindly supplied by TerThera (Breda, the Netherlands). [111In]InCl3 (370 MBq/mL) was purchased from Curium (Petten, the Netherlands). VIK-202 and VDC 404 dose calibrators from Comecer (Castel Bolognese, Italy) were used to quantify the activity. A HIDEX AMX automatic gamma counter (Turku, Finland) was used for In-111 (370–470 keV), Tb-161 (41–61 and 68–83 keV) and Lu-177 (102–124 and 187–229 keV) measurements for preclinical experiments, activity measurements of Tb-161 were also cross-validated against the automated gamma counter 2480 Wizard-2 (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). DOTA-TATE was purchased from BACHEM (Bubendorf, Switzerland). All radiolabelings were performed in 1.5 mL vials from Sarstedt (Breda, The Netherlands). Ultra-performance liquid Chromatography (UPLC) characterization was performed by using an Acquity UPLC H-Class from Waters (Etten-Leur, the Netherlands) with dedicated software (Empower 3).

The radiochemical yield (RCY) was determined by instant-thin-layer chromatography (iTLC) on silica gel-coated glass fiber iTLC-SG sheets from Agilent (Folsom, CA, USA), cut into 0.5 × 10 cm strips and dried for 20 min at 200 °C, before use. Quality control was performed using a sodium citrate solution (0.1 M, pH 5) as mobile phase, and RCY was determined by a radio-TLC-scanner from bSCAN Brightspec (Zelik, Belgium) that contains an NaI scintillator detector, 2.54 × 2.54 cm, and a digital multichannel analyzer (MCA). Validated retention factors were as follows: radiopharmaceutical (0–0.6), background (0.6–0.7), and free/DTPA-bound Tb-161 (0.7–1.0).

The radiochemical purity (RCP) was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an Alliance 2695 XE HPLC from Waters (Etten-Leur, the Netherlands). This HPLC is controlled by Empower 3 software and includes a PDA (W2298) connected to an 1-inch NaI(TI) Scionic crystal (Bunnik, the Netherlands) associated with a BPAD single channel analyzer for Brightspec (Zellik, Belgium). The samples were injected onto a Symmetry® C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm), and eluted with the following HPLC method: Water + 0.1% Trifluoracetic acid (TFA) (solvent A) and methanol (solvent B) according to the following gradient: 0–2 min 100% solvent A (1 mL/min), 2–3 min 45% solvent A (0.5 mL/min), 3–20 min 40.0% solvent A, 20–25 min 100% solvent B (1 mL/min).

Detection

Activity measurements of the Tb-161 batches were conducted using a dose calibrator and compared to the calibrated unopened batches distributed by the vendor. Intermediate activity was assessed by adapting settings from the Tc-99m channel and applying a conversion factor of 1.10. To evaluate the geometric influence of vial shape (point-source vs non-point source), various vials (1.5 mL microtube, 8.5 mL polystyrene tube, 1.5 mL deactivated clear glass, 6 mL borosilicate glass, and 300 µL polypropylene conical vial) were investigated. A stock solution of 100 MBq [161Tb]TbCl3, with DTPA (15 µL, 3 mg/mL), and 85 µL Kolliphor (Kolliphor HS 15 (2 mg/mL; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) to prevent stickiness was prepared. 5 µL (5 MBq) of this stock solution was transferred to each vial in triplicate, and activity was determined by dose calibrator in triplicate.

Two types of gamma counters were calibrated by measuring samples of 1 MBq [161Tb]TbCl3. During measurements, windows were selected according to the known and confirmed peaks (49 keV and 75 keV). Linearity for two types of gamma counters was determined by measuring samples containing 7- 1075 kBq of Tb-161 for 60 s. Each sample was diluted in 1 mL of water in polystyrene tubes (8.5 mL, 75 × 15.7 mm) purchased from SARSTEDT (Numbrecht, Germany) to standardize geometry. These activities and volumes were selected based on the mass of the organs that were selected for biodistribution (50–2000 g) and the expected amount of activity during the biodistribution studies.

To determine the influence of sample volume on gamma counter measurements, ~ 60 kBq of [161Tb]Tb-DTPA was measured in triplicate in volumes of 0.2–3 mL, using the same polystyrene tubes. Volumes were selected based on the volumes used during labeling for preclinical studies, quality control, and dose determination for patients. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Determination of metal impurities in Tb-161 stock solution

Potential metal impurities, including gadolinium-160 from the target material, dysprosium-161 formed by decay, and common contamination such as iron, zinc, and copper, were evaluated for interferences with DOTA-TATE complexation. ICP standards of natGd (III), natTb (III),natDy (III), Fe(III), Zn(III), and Cu(III) were prepared at a ten-fold molar excess relative to 1 nmol of DOTA-TATE. Each metal was individually complexed with DOTA-TATE, sodium acetate buffer (10 µL, 2.5 M), and water (final volume 140 µL) was heated at 90 °C for 20 min. DTPA was added to chelate unbound metals before UPLC analysis. Samples (10 µL) were injected onto a C18 HSS column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm) using a UPLC method as described by Breeman et al. (Breeman et al. 2014), where optimal separation between labeled and non-labeled DOTA-TATE could be obtained. After retention factors were established, a mixture of metal–DOTA-TATE complexes and [161 Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE (10 MBq/nmol, RCY = 99%) was analyzed.

To monitor metal impurities over time, 10 nmol of DOTA-TATE was complexed with 10 MBq of Tb-161 according to the stated radiolabeling protocol (sodium acetate (1 µL, 2.5 M), DOTA-TATE, quenchers ascorbic acid/gentisic acid (10 µL, 50 mM) to minimize radiolysis in a total volume of 140 µL. These mixtures were incubated at 90 °C for 20 min and cooled down for 5 min. DTPA (5 µL, 3 mg/mL) was added after the mixture was cooled down. Measurements were performed on the calibration date of Tb-161 and 2 weeks after calibration. Quantification of target material (Gd-160) was determined according to the information of the certificate of analysis (COA); Table S1 and Eqs. S1–S2. Tb-161 was quantified according to activity measurement (Table S1) and Eqs. S3–S4. Determining the quantity of Dy-161 was by obtained UPLC results and Eq. S5. All detail information are provided in the supplementary.

Radiolabeling

DOTA-TATE was radiolabeled with either Lu-177 or Tb-161 in a total volume of 140 µL. The radiolabeling solution contained water with Kolliphor (Kolliphor HS 15, 2 mg/mL; Sigma Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) to prevent stickiness. Sodium acetate (1 µL, 2.5 M), DOTA-TATE, and the radionuclide (Tb-161 or Lu-177) were added, as well as quenchers ascorbic acid/gentisic acid (10 µL, 50 mM) to minimize radiolysis. These mixtures were incubated at 90 °C for 20 min and cooled down for 5 min. DTPA (5 µL, 3 mg/mL) was added to complex the remaining non-labeled radionuclide.

Determination of maximum achievable molar activity

To determine the maximum molar activity achievable for [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE, labeling was performed at 40, 80, 111, 160, and 222 MBq/nmol under labeling conditions as stated above, 14 days post calibration date of Tb-161. This time was based on shelf-life according to Annex 1. RCY and RCP were determined by iTLC and HPLC, respectively, at 24 h, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 days post-labeling according to previous stated quality control procedures.

Stability studies of [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE and [161 Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE

Both radiopharmaceuticals were radiolabeled with a molar activity of 50 MBq/nmol as previously described. Aliquots of the radiolabeled solutions (2.5 MBq) were incubated in 200 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) from ThermoFisher (Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C. To verify their stability the RCP of the radiopharmaceuticals was determined at each time point (2, 24 h after incubation on a radio- an Arch (U)HPLC from Waters equipped with a Symmetry® C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm) and a diode array detector connected to a Canberra Osprey multichannel analyzer, eluted with a gradient of methanol (0–100% in H2O, containing 0.1% TFA) with a flowrate of 1 mL/min over 25 min.

The stability of the radiopharmaceuticals was also determined in mouse serum by incubating 2.5 MBq in 100 µL mouse serum from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37 °C. To verify their stability at each time point (2 and 24 h post-incubation). After incubation, 30 µL of the mixture was added to a new Eppendorf tube, and the proteins were precipitated by adding 30 µL of acetonitrile. The tubes were vortexed vigorously and centrifuged at 5000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min and analyzed by a radio- an Arch (U)HPLC as reported for the PBS.

Determination of maximum achievable molar activity

Two weeks after of calibration of the vendor of the Tb-161, radiolabeling of [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE with 40, 80, 111, and 160 MBq nmol was successfully achieved. The RCP (7A) for 40 MBq/nmol and 80 MBq/nmol was demonstrated to have an RCP of > 95% for over 2 days and > 90% for 4 days, whereas 111 MBq/nmol and 160 MBq/nmol had an RCP of 95% for over 24 h and were below 90% after 3 days. The RCY was determined above 98% at all time points for 40, 80, 111, and 160 MBq/nmol. The RCY and RCP determined for 222 MBq/nmol [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE resulted in < 20% at all time points (Fig. 7B).

As indicated by the UPLC result, impurities, including the target material, are present during the labeling of Tb-161. Despite these impurities, it was still possible to obtain a molar activity of 160 MBq/nmol 2 weeks after calibration. This is more than sufficient for clinical use, as the desired molar activity for [161 Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE is typically 40 MBq/nmol.

Discussion

Recent studies have suggested Tb-161 as a promising candidate for TRT. Tb-161 can potentially be an alternative or even an improvement for Lu-177 labeled radiopharmaceuticals, due to its additional conversion and Auger electrons. This study aimed to explore the feasibility of implementing Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals, such as [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE, in comparison to Lu-177-labeled radiopharmaceuticals in preclinical settings, exemplified by [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE and [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE.

The first stage of this process focused on optimizing the radiochemistry of Tb-161. We developed and standardized reliable detection methods, radiolabeling protocols, and quality control analyses to ensure the accuracy of Tb-161 radiopharmaceuticals. Our findings underline the importance of carefully considering the influence of matrix and volumes on the measurements of activity. Parameters, such as the volume matrix or type of vial in which Tb-161 is measured, can impact the outcome, with higher volumes leading to reduction in activity measurement. This can result in a significant deviation in activity calculation if not accurately accounted for.

Additionally, the presence of metal impurities, including gadolinium (Gd), dysprosium (Dy), and trace metals (Fe, Zn, Cu), competes with Tb-161 during the labeling process. Chromatographic analysis allowed us to differentiate DOTA-TATE labeled with Fe, Zn, and Cu, with impurity levels having been demonstrated to increase over time. Increase of impurities are not only related to the increase of volume needed over time to obtain 10 MBq Tb-161, but also material (pipets, tweezers, shielding) and surrounding during use. Additionally, an increase in Dy-161 was found over time, the relatively large error bars observed likely related to different production conditions, such as the reactor, amount of target material, and position in the reactor. As well as the variation in the efficiency of the purification procedure between production batches. The detection of Dy-161 are higher than expected, indicating that the current purification processes is focused on the separation of the target material. Nevertheless, the resulting molar activity obtained after labeling remained sufficiently high and are similar to previous published studies (Holiski et al. 2024). These results underline the importance of purification by the vendor, as all herein tested metal impurities complex with the DOTA-chelator and will compete with the Tb-161 in the labeling reaction. Additionally, the purity of target material (Gd-160), flux (reaction), and eradiation time will influence the final yield had have to be further investigated.

Altogether, this results in a feasible molar activity that is approximately four times lower than the theoretically calculated 700 MBq/nmol with [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE (1:1). Nonetheless, it was still possible to achieve a molar activity of 160 MBq/nmol when labeling [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE, which is significantly higher than the 40 MBq/nmol required for standardized patient care. But lower than the highest obtained molar activity with [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE 420 MBq/nmol (Vogel et al. 2021).

These results suggest that the process of implementing Tb-161-labeled radiopharmaceuticals is almost identical to Lu-177, but related to different purification methods and way of quantification. Tb-161 has now been fully characterized on a radiochemical level, and essential biological parameters will be established. Further studies are needed to evaluate the long-term efficacy of Tb-161, particularly compared to Lu-177. However, Tb-161 has demonstrated promising properties, suggesting the significant potential of Tb-161 in TRT. With characteristics, Tb-161 theoretically should achieve an improvement in of therapeutic effect compared to Lu-177, but this still has to be proven. This study excludes that these potential differences are related to the radiochemical properties of Tb-161. Therefore, whether Tb-161 can indeed provide these improved therapeutic outcomes compared to Lu-177 due to these electrons, remains to be explored in more detail.

Conclusion

This study highlights Tb-161 as a promising alternative to Lu-177. While the achieved molar activity is 4 × lower than the theoretical molar activity, a molar activity of 160 MBq/nmol was feasible. [161Tb]Tb-DOTA-TATE, exhibits similar radiochemical properties to [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE. In particular, the optimization of radiolabeling procedures and quality control measures for Tb-161 suggests that the radionuclide can be implemented in a manner similar to Lu-177 clinical settings. Due to the relatively low energy, geometry is of high importance.

Despite the promising radiochemical similarity, the additional conversion and Auger electrons emitted by Tb-161 offer potential advantages that may enhance therapeutic efficacy. However, further in-depth preclinical and clinical studies, such as our planned therapy study in mice and the phase I dose-escalation study, are required to assess the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of Tb-161, particularly in comparison to Lu-177.
