UDP-glucuronate decarboxylase 1 promotes tumor immune evasion by accelerating KMT2D loss in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jiwei Zhang, Yuan Li, Jiafeng Chen, Fen Ma, Ben Feng, Shiling Zhou, Yongchao Cai, Yidi Sun, Zheng Gao, Enfu Dong, Jia Fan, Xiutao Fu, Zhe Li, Zhenbin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis has shown promise in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but clinical responses are often limited in durability. Identifying novel drivers of immune evasion is crucial for improving therapeutic strategies.MethodsWe employed a multi-omics integrative framework to identify HCC-specific immune biomarkers. Functional roles were validated using in vitro and in vivo models, including UDP-glucuronate decarboxylase 1 (UXS1) knockout/overexpression, co-culture assays, and various mouse models. Molecular mechanisms were dissected using immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry, ubiquitination assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation, luciferase reporter assays, and single-cell RNA sequencing.ResultsWe identified the glycosyltransferase UXS1 as a novel immune-related prognostic hub gene in HCC. UXS1 was upregulated due to copy number gain and MZF1-driven promoter hypomethylation. Functionally, UXS1 promoted HCC malignancy independently of its canonical enzymatic activity. Mechanistically, UXS1 interacted with the E3 ligase F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (FBXW7) to promote histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D) ubiquitination and degradation. We further discovered that KMT2D, acting as a non-catalytic scaffold, recruited the transcriptional repressor CCAAT enhancer binding protein beta (CEBPB) to the PD-L1 promoter to maintain its repression. UXS1-mediated KMT2D degradation disrupted this complex, leading to CEBPB dissociation and consequent PD-L1 transcriptional derepression. In vivo, UXS1 suppressed CD8+ T-cell effector function. UXS1 knockout synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy, and in human HCC, high UXS1 correlated with low KMT2D, high PD-L1, and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration.ConclusionThis study defines a novel UXS1-KMT2D-CEBPB-PD-L1 signaling axis, revealing how UXS1 drives immune evasion in HCC via epigenetic reprogramming. Targeting this axis may represent a potential strategy to overcome immune resistance, warranting further clinical investigation.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, Immunotherapy, Solid tumor

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We identify the glycosyltransferase UDP-glucuronate decarboxylase 1 (UXS1) as a prognostic hub gene in HCC. UXS1 drives immune evasion by promoting the degradation of histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D), which abrogates CCAAT enhancer binding protein beta-mediated transcriptional repression of PD-L1. Consequently, UXS1 ablation synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical models, and high UXS1 expression in patients is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by elevated PD-L1 and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration.

Introduction

Primary liver cancer, ranked as the sixth most common and third deadliest malignant tumor, poses a significant threat to human health.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents the most predominant type of liver cancer, accounting for about 90% of liver cancer cases.2 The progression of HCC is a multistep process regulated by genetic and epigenetic changes, involving oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, as well as key genes participating in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation. In addition to mutations, genes can also undergo amplification, deletion, insertion, and translocation, leading to overexpression (OE) and carcinogenesis.3 4 The prognosis for most patients with HCC is poor, mainly due to late-stage diagnosis, with curative treatment applicable to only a minority of patients,5 6 further complicated by the lack of predictive biomarkers exacerbating the challenges in diagnosis and treatment.7,9 Therefore, identifying new biomarkers and therapeutic targets is crucial to improve the diagnosis and treatment of HCC.

In recent years, cancer therapy has progressively shifted towards molecularly targeted approaches aimed at disrupting key protein functions to inhibit cancer progression. Sorafenib stands as the first approved drug for advanced HCC treatment, demonstrating significant improvements in patient survival.10 Other multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as lenvatinib, regorafenib, and cabozantinib are also under investigation; sorafenib is already approved for advanced HCC.11,13 Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) monoclonal antibodies are widely used, with combination immunotherapy strategies aimed at enhancing treatment efficacy.14 However, due to the complex pathogenesis of HCC and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, the response rate of HCC to ICI monotherapy is less than 20%,15 16 with many patients developing immune resistance following treatment, presenting a new challenge for HCC immunotherapy.

The reasons for immune resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in patients with HCC are diverse. Impaired antigen recognition and presentation in immune cells, abnormal activation and proliferation of immunosuppressive cells, and compromised function of antitumor immune cells within the complex tumor microenvironment may all contribute to the development of immune therapy resistance.17 Therefore, gaining a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying immune therapy resistance in HCC and continuously exploring new treatment strategies are crucial for improving patient survival outcomes.

In previous studies, UDP-glucuronate decarboxylase 1 (UXS1) has been identified as an enzyme located in the Golgi apparatus. Deletion of the UXS1 gene significantly inhibits tumor growth in cell lines with high UGDH expression, and use in combination with chemotherapy further accelerates tumor regression.18 Additionally, increased expression of the UXS1 enzyme and activation of its metabolic pathways are linked to susceptibility to gastric cancer, emphasizing its importance as a potential therapeutic target.19 Through analysis of multicohort, large-sample data, we have identified UXS1 as a prognostic biomarker in HCC and delved into its functional and specific mechanisms in HCC pathogenesis. Importantly, we have discovered that UXS1 mediates tumor immune escape by upregulating PD-L1, suggesting that targeting UXS1 may enhance the efficacy of immune therapy. This study deepens our understanding of the complex molecular interactions of UXS1 in HCC pathogenesis, further highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for improving treatment efficacy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and mice

The HCC cell lines Huh-7, Hep-3B, and Hepa1-6 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), while the H22 cell line was obtained from Wuhan Procell Life Science and Technology. All cell lines were cultured according to the respective manufacturer’s instructions. 5–6 weeks old C57BL/6 mice, Nude mice and NOD-PrkdcscidIL2rgem1/Smoc (M-NSG) mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Pd-l1−/− mice and UXS1−/− mice were purchased from Shanghai Immuneonc Biotechnology. NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid H2-K1b-tm1Bpe H2-Ab1g7-em1Mvw H2-D1b-tm1Bpe Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG-MHC I/II DKO, 025216) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory.20 The mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment, in temperature-controlled holding rooms maintained at 20–22°C, with humidity levels between 30% and 34%, and subjected to a 12-hour light cycle (06:00 on, 18:00 off).

Cohort 2 (n=130): RNA samples collected from paired human HCC tissues and ANT (corresponding to figure 1D). Cohort 3 (n=130): DNA samples and tissue samples with clinical data collected from paired human HCC tissues and ANT. Among these, 88 cases had complete clinicopathological data for table 1, and 70 cases were used for multiplex immunofluorescence analysis (corresponding to Figures 7J-7K). The entire cohort (n=130) was used for immunohistochemistry (figure 1G) and copy number variation analysis (figure 2A). Cohort 4 (n=50): DNA samples and RNA samples collected from paired human HCC tissues and ANT (corresponding to figure 2E and figure 2F).

Mice model

All experimental procedures were conducted under the supervision and approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University.

Subcutaneous xenograft mice model

Tumor cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and adjusted to the desired cell numbers using phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Hepa1-6 (2×106 cells per mice), H22 (1×106 per mice), and Huh-7 (2×106 per mice) tumor cell lines were subcutaneously implanted into the dorsal area of Nude or M-NSG mice. Additionally, Hepa1-6 (2×106 cells per mice) tumor cell lines were subcutaneously implanted into the dorsal area of C57BL/6 mice. Treatment interventions were initiated when tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3. In the treatment model, PD-1 antibody (100 µg/200 µL per mice) was administered intraperitoneally starting from day 0, with treatment cycles every 5 days for a total of six treatments. Tumor measurements were performed weekly, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V=(L×W2)/2, where V represents volume, L denotes length, and W signifies width. Each group consisted of five mice. On completion of the study, tumors were harvested for further analysis, including histopathological examination and molecular characterization.

Tail-vein injection mice model

In situ HCC models were established through tail-vein injection of Hepa1-6 (1×106 cells per mice) and H22 (5×105 per mice) cells labeled with luciferase in Nude mice. PD-1 antibody (100 µg/200 µL per mice) and BMN673 (0.33 mg/kg) treatments were initiated post fifth-day injection and continued at days 28 and 35. For fluorescence imaging, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (2.5 mg/100 µL in PBS) for in vivo bioluminescent imaging using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) system (PerkinElmer). Each group comprised five mice. On completion of the study, tumors were harvested for further analysis, including histopathological examination and molecular characterization.

DEN-CCL4 chemically induced mice model

In the second week of the study, Nude mice were injected with AAV-ctrl, AAV-shUXS1, or AAV-shUXS1+KMT2D. Starting from the fourth week, mice were induced every 2 weeks for a total of 6 weeks. The specific induction protocol was as follows: all mice were intraperitoneally injected two times a week with 15% carbon tetrachloride (CCL4), and 0.1% N-nitrosodiethylamine (DEN) was added to their drinking water. Normal water was provided at all other times, and the final experiment concluded in the 23rd week. For survival experiments, the termination criteria are either mice death or a decrease in body weight of 20% from baseline. Each group consisted of six mice. On completion of the study, tumors were harvested for further analysis, including histopathological examination and molecular characterization.

Protein truncation test

RNA was extracted from human cells to isolate histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D) transcripts. Reverse transcription and PCR were then used to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) encoding KMT2D, with various fragments corresponding to distinct functional domains to facilitate protein truncation studies. The resulting cDNA fragments were separated and purified via agarose gel electrophoresis. These KMT2D sequences, tagged for detection, were subsequently ligated into pLVX-IRES-Hyg plasmid vectors to construct fusion protein expression plasmids. The accuracy and integrity of the inserted sequences were confirmed through sequencing. Following successful validation, the plasmids were transfected into Huh-7 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. After 72 hours of transient transfection, the cells were harvested for immunoprecipitation (IP) analysis.

Co-IP assay, GST-Pulldown and Mass Spectrometry analysis

Cell lysates were prepared from HCC tissue and adjacent normal tissue by grinding and homogenizing the tissues followed by lysis using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were incubated at 4°C for 2 hours and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a new tube and subjected to a second centrifugation at 13,000 rpm at 4°C for 5 min. The upper layer containing the soluble proteins was collected and stored at 4°C. Protein concentration in the extract was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit.

For the co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay, cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. After pre-clearing the lysates with protein A+G agarose beads, anti-UXS1 antibody or control IgG was added, followed by overnight incubation with protein A+G agarose beads at 4°C.

For the GST-Pulldown experiment, recombinant GST-UXS1 fusion protein was overexpressed in Huh-7 cells and immobilized on glutathione-agarose beads along with control GST protein. These beads were then incubated with cell lysates to capture proteins interacting specifically with GST-UXS1. Following stringent washing and elution steps, the eluted proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to a membrane, and probed with specific antibodies to detect and identify the proteins that were specifically associating with GST-UXS1. Subsequently, the lysates were subjected to mass spectrometry (MS) analysis to comprehensively identify and characterize the proteins that were specifically bound to UXS1 under these experimental conditions. Detailed information about the antibodies used is provided in online supplemental table 4.

T-cell co-culture and flow cytometry

In the T-cell isolation process from mice’s peripheral blood, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation. The isolated PBMCs were then subjected to T-cell isolation using a kit recommended by the manufacturer to obtain a purified T-cell population. These isolated T cells were subsequently activated overnight in a culture medium supplemented with 100 IU/mL interleukin (IL)-2, 1 µg/mL CD3 antibody, and 1 µg/mL CD28 antibody. Following activation, 10,000 T cells were co-cultured with tumor cells at a 5:1 ratio and co-incubated for 2 days under controlled conditions in a humidified incubator with a temperature of 37°C and 5% CO2 levels. Subsequent to the incubation period, T cells were stained with specific antibodies, and flow cytometry analysis was performed to quantify the number of tumor cells in the co-culture.

For the flow cytometry analysis, cell samples were prepared by generating a single-cell suspension through centrifugation. The cells were then incubated with the anti-PD-L1 antibody at a concentration of 1 µg/mL to facilitate PD-L1 protein detection. After incubation to allow antibody binding, unbound antibodies were washed away. Flow cytometry analysis was then performed to measure PD-L1 expression, assessing both the percentage of cells expressing PD-L1 and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of PD-L1 on positively stained cells. Detailed information regarding all the antibodies and reagents used in this experiment can be found in online supplemental table 4.

[U-13C] glucose labeling experiments

During the logarithmic growth phase, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed two times with PBS (pH 7.4) containing 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 7.3 mM Na2HPO4, 0.9 mM CaCl2, and 0.5 mM MgCl2. Subsequently, the cells were incubated in serum-free medium containing 1 mM [U-13C] lactate and unlabeled glucose (2.5 mM) for 30, 100, 300, and 500 min, respectively. After the incubation, the medium and cells were separated. The cells were then washed two times with 0.9% w/v NaCl and extracted with 70% v/v ethanol. The cells were scraped from the culture flask, and the cell-ethanol mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min to separate the soluble extract from the insoluble cellular proteins. The resulting supernatant was lyophilized and reconstituted in water for mass spectrometry analysis.

Treatment of HCC cells with 5-Aza-dC

HCC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluency. The original culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different concentrations of 5-Aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-dC) (final concentrations of 0, 1, 5, 10 µM) for 24 hours. Subsequently, the medium was changed to normal culture medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 24 hours before harvesting for RNA extraction for subsequent molecular experiments.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and ChIP sequencing

Cells were crosslinked by treating them with 1% formaldehyde at a concentration of 1×106 cells per mL for 5 min at room temperature with gentle agitation to ensure uniform crosslinking. The crosslinking reaction was subsequently quenched by adding 0.125 M glycine for 10 min to neutralize the formaldehyde. Following quenching, the cells were lysed using 1 mL of lysis buffer, and the lysate was centrifuged at 5,000 g to separate the nuclear pellet. The resulting pellet was washed once with digestion buffer to remove residual lysis buffer.

The pelleted chromatin was then digested by incubating with MNase enzyme at 37℃ for 20 min, after which the digestion was terminated by adding 0.5 M EDTA. To further process the digested chromatin, sonication was performed to shear the chromatin into smaller fragments, and the sample was centrifuged to remove the remaining pellet.

The fragmented chromatin was precipitated by incubating overnight at 4°C with specific antibodies against the protein of interest, in the presence of Protein G beads and dilution buffer to enhance the binding of protein-DNA complexes. After the incubation, the beads were washed thoroughly to remove any non-specifically bound material. The protein-DNA complexes were then eluted from the beads using an elution buffer, and the eluted DNA was purified using a DNA purification kit (Proteintech, 58002) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For PCR experiments, primers were designed to target specific DNA regions to assess the enrichment of the DNA fragment of interest following chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). In the case of ChIP-sequencing experiments, library preparation involved fragmenting and amplifying the immunoprecipitated DNA, followed by sequencing the resulting fragments to generate high-throughput sequencing data. This data were subsequently used for comprehensive analysis of protein-DNA interactions on a genome-wide scale.

Identification of immune-related molecular markers

This study initially retrieved 869 liver immune-related molecular markers from the CellMarker database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/). Subsequent differential expression analysis of these 869 immune-related genes was performed using RNA sequencing data from 55 paired HCC tissues and adjacent normal tissues (Cohort 1), integrated with multicenter public transcriptomic datasets (The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), GSE64041, and GSE76297), identifying 99 differentially expressed genes in HCC tissues (screening criteria: |log2 fold change (FC)|>1, p<0.05). The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression algorithm was subsequently employed to construct a diagnostic immune cell signature-based model diagnostic immune-cell signature model (DIL) using these 99 genes. Further screening of immune-related public datasets identified immunotherapy resistance-associated key genes within the DIL framework. The diagnostic performance of the model and the classification efficacy of its pivotal genes across validation cohorts were rigorously evaluated through receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and calculation of the area under the curve (AUC).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard errors of the mean (SEMs) from at least three independent biological replicates. Group comparisons were performed using unpaired/paired Student’s t-test (parametric) or Mann-Whitney/Wilcoxon test (non-parametric) for two groups, and one-way analysis of variance with post hoc tests or Kruskal-Wallis test for multiple groups. Associations between categorical variables were analyzed by χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Correlation analyses used Pearson’s or Spearman method. Survival curves were generated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. Analyses were performed using R (V.3.5.1) or GraphPad Prism.

Results

UXS1 as an immune-related prognostic predictor in HCC

This study implemented a multi-omics integrative framework to systematically identify HCC-specific immune biomarkers (figure 1A). Initial retrieval of 869 liver immune-related molecular markers from the CellMarker database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/) was followed by differential expression analysis using RNA sequencing data from 55 paired HCC and adjacent normal tissues (Cohort 1), integrated with multicenter transcriptomic datasets (TCGA-LIHC, GSE64041, GSE76297). Using defined screening criteria (|log2FC|>1, p<0.05), 99 differentially expressed immune-related genes were identified (figure 1B). LASSO regression analysis was performed on these candidate genes to establish the DIL based on the optimal lambda value. The model achieved an AUC of 0.998 in the Cohort 1 training set. When applied to three independent clinical validation cohorts—TCGA-LIHC, GSE76297, and GSE64041—it maintained diagnostic performance with AUCs above 0.85, supporting the model’s generalizability across distinct datasets (online supplemental figure 1A). Multi-omics integration with CPTAC-LIHC proteomics, PD-1 resistance genes (TCGA-LIHC), and immunotherapy resistance genes (GSE63557) identified UXS1 as the sole hub gene intersecting all four datasets (figure 1C), exhibiting significant classification capacity (online supplemental figure 1B). Molecular profiling confirmed HCC-specific UXS1 OE at messenger RNA (mRNA)/protein levels (figure 1D–1G, p<0.001). Survival analysis revealed significantly shortened median overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with high UXS1 expression (figure 1H and figure 1I), establishing UXS1 as an independent prognostic biomarker. Clinically, high UXS1 expression was significantly associated with aggressive tumor features, including larger tumor size (≥5 cm, p=0.005) and the presence of vascular invasion (p=0.009), but not with gender, age, or tumor number (table 1, Cohort 3). Together, these data support the role of UXS1 as a novel immune-related biomarker associated with poor prognosis and advanced disease in HCC.

MZF1-mediated transcriptional activation of UXS1 through promoter CpG island binding in hypomethylated HCC

Genomic instability during HCC progression manifested as increasing copy number variation (CNV) frequency.21 Whole-genome analysis identified significant CNVs in UXS1, with 54% of samples exhibiting copy number gains (log2 (CNV) >1) and 19% displaying copy number losses (log2 (CNV) <−1)22 23 (figure 2A). These CNV alterations demonstrated a positive correlation with corresponding gene expression levels (figure 2B). Pan-cancer methylation analysis (including LIHC) demonstrated tumor-specific aberrant methylation patterns (online supplemental figure 2A). Notably, UXS1 expression showed significant inverse correlation with DNA methylation status in HCC (figure 2C). The clinical analysis showed that UXS1 hypomethylation was associated with reduced OS (figure 2D, p=0.0028), and promoter methylation levels exerted a dose-dependent negative regulation on its expression (figure 2E). Validation confirmed reduced UXS1 promoter methylation in HCC versus normal controls (figure 2F, p<0.0001). Integrated analysis identified a patient subgroup harboring concurrent UXS1 copy-number gain and promoter hypomethylation, which was diagnosed at a significantly more advanced tumor stage (stage III/IV) compared with patients with other alteration patterns (figure 2G). This compelling association suggests that the combination of UXS1 amplification and epigenetic activation is associated with tumor progression and aggressiveness at diagnosis. In vitro functional assays demonstrated 5-Aza-dC-induced concentration-dependent upregulation of UXS1 mRNA/protein in HCC cell lines (Huh-7/Hep-3B, figure 2H–2I).

Bioinformatics analysis detected the presence of a CpG island in the UXS1 promoter region (online supplemental figure 2B). Transcription factor binding prediction revealed high-affinity MZF1 binding sites within this region (online supplemental table 1). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed MZF1’s enhancement of UXS1 promoter activity (figure 2J). Mechanistically, 5-Aza-dC treatment synergistically increased UXS1 mRNA levels in MZF1-overexpressing cells, while MZF1 knockdown abolished this regulatory effect (figure 2K), establishing that MZF1 promotes UXS1 transcriptional expression by binding to the CpG island in the UXS1 promoter region.

UXS1 acts as an oncogene in HCC

To further investigate the biological function of UXS1 in HCC, we established UXS1 knockout (KO) (online supplemental figure 3A-D) and UXS1 overexpressing HCC cell lines (online supplemental figure 3E,F). Experimental results demonstrated that UXS1 KO in Huh-7 and Hep-3B cell lines significantly inhibited tumor cell growth (figure 3A), clonogenic formation (figure 3C and online supplemental figure 3G), and invasion capability (figure 3D and online supplemental figure 3I), while UXS1 OE exhibited the opposite effect (figure 3B, figure 3E, online supplemental figure 3H, figure 3F and online supplemental figure 3J). In order to specifically explore the impact of UXS1 on tumor cells themselves, we chose immunodeficient nude mice for animal experiments. Subcutaneous xenograft models confirmed that UXS1 KO markedly suppressed tumor growth and reduced tumor volume (figure 3G), whereas UXS1 OE significantly enhanced the metastatic potential of HCC cells (figure 3H). In the subcutaneous tumor model, UXS1 KO significantly inhibited the proliferation capacity of tumor cells within the tumor tissue (online supplemental figure 3K). These results demonstrate that UXS1 plays an oncogenic role in HCC.

UXS1 contributes to malignancy in HCC in an enzyme-activity-independent manner

UXS1 is an indispensable Golgi enzyme in tumor cells expressing high levels of the enzyme UGDH, responsible for converting UDP-glucuronic acid (UDPGA) to UDP-xylose. This dependency implies potential vulnerabilities in tumors with high UGDH levels.18 Prior studies have established Y231, K236, and K361 as critical residues for UXS1 activity.24 25 To assess whether UXS1 enhances HCC cell survival via UDPGA detoxification, we engineered HCC cell lines overexpressing wild-type (WT) UXS1 and five distinct mutants (M1-M5) (figure 4A). Metabolic tracing using 13C-labeled glucose elucidated UDP-xylose biosynthesis dynamics (figure 4B). Results revealed complete abolition of UDP-xylose production in UXS1-KO cells, with M4 and M5 mutants exhibiting marked impairment in UDP-xylose synthesis (figure 4C). Strikingly, both WT-UXS1-OE and M5-OE cell lines demonstrated enhanced clonogenic potential and invasive properties relative to controls (figure 4D and figure 4E). In vivo evaluation through orthotopic and subcutaneous xenograft models confirmed accelerated tumor growth kinetics in WT-UXS1-OE and M5-OE groups compared with controls (figure 4F–4I). Immunohistochemical analysis of subcutaneous tumors revealed heightened proliferative activity in WT-UXS1 and M5-OE 1 xenografts (figure 4J). These findings collectively indicate that UXS1-driven HCC progression operates independently of UDPGA metabolic regulation, implicating non-canonical mechanisms underlying its oncogenic function.

UXS1 destabilizes KMT2D by upregulating FBXW7 in HCC

To elucidate the mechanism underlying UXS1-driven HCC progression, we performed IP coupled with MS using (1) HCC patient-derived tumors with high UXS1 expression paired with adjacent normal tissues, and (2) UXS1-overexpressing Huh-7 cells. This screen identified four candidate UXS1-interacting proteins: KMT2D, ribosomal protein S11 (RPS11), monoamine oxidase A (MAOA), and ribosomal protein L9 (RPL9) (online supplemental figure 4A). Subsequent co-IP validation confirmed a specific physical interaction between UXS1 and KMT2D, but not with RPS11, MAOA, or RPL9 (figure 5A and online supplemental figure 4B). KMT2D, a histone methyltransferase, is among the most frequently inactivated epigenetic modifiers in cancer, and its loss is known to accelerate tumorigenesis.26 27 Notably, an inverse correlation between UXS1 and KMT2D protein expression was observed in human HCC specimens and in UXS1-overexpressing Huh-7 cells (online supplemental figure 4C,D). Further analysis revealed that UXS1 did not affect KMT2D mRNA levels (online supplemental figure 4E) but negatively regulated its protein levels: UXS1 OE decreased KMT2D protein, whereas UXS1 knockdown increased it (figure 5B–5C), suggesting that UXS1 regulates KMT2D stability through a post-translational mechanism.

Given prior links between KMT2D mutations and protein destabilization,28 we assessed KMT2D stability via cycloheximide chase assays. UXS1 OE accelerated KMT2D degradation, whereas UXS1 knockdown extended its half-life (figure 5D). Moreover, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 reversed the UXS1-induced reduction of KMT2D (figure 5E), confirming that KMT2D degradation depends on the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. We further examined KMT2D ubiquitination by immunoprecipitation and found that UXS1 OE enhanced KMT2D ubiquitination, while UXS1 KO reduced it (figure 5F).

To identify the E3 ligase potentially responsible for KMT2D degradation, we queried three independent protein–protein interaction databases (UbiBrowser, HitPredict, and BioGRID) with stringent thresholds: confidence score >0.7 for UbiBrowser, probability >0.7 for HitPredict, and at least one experimental evidence for BioGRID. Under these criteria, UbiBrowser predicted 18 interacting proteins (6 E3 ligases), HitPredict predicted 12 (1 E3 ligase), and BioGRID listed 173 (8 E3 ligases). Cross-database analysis identified F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (FBXW7) as the only common candidate among all three resources (online supplemental figure 4F, table S2). Notably, the N-terminal three-PHD domain of KMT2D has been reported as the recognition motif for FBXW7.29 Co-IP experiments confirmed a direct interaction between UXS1 and FBXW7 (online supplemental figure 4G), and further demonstrated that UXS1 OE enhanced the binding between KMT2D and FBXW7 (figure 5G). Using KMT2D immunoprecipitation followed by ubiquitin detection, we found that FBXW7 depletion largely reversed the enhanced KMT2D ubiquitination induced by UXS1 OE (figure 5H). Accordingly, the UXS1-driven upregulation of PD-L1 was also reversed by FBXW7 knockdown (figure 5H), indicating that FBXW7 is required for UXS1-mediated KMT2D ubiquitination, degradation, and subsequent PD-L1 upregulation. Truncation binding assays revealed that under control conditions, FBXW7 bound to the N-terminal fragments M1 and M2, both of which contain the N-terminal three PHD domains. On UXS1 OE, however, the binding pattern shifted: FBXW7 interaction was no longer restricted to the N-terminal region but became strongly dependent on fragments containing the C-terminal SET domain (M4 and M5) (figure 5I and online supplemental figure 4H). In addition, immunofluorescence co-localization experiments showed that UXS1, KMT2D, and FBXW7 coexist in the cytoplasm (figure 5J). In a DEN-CCL4-induced HCC mouse model, AAV-mediated knockdown of UXS1 reduced tumor burden and prolonged survival (figure 5K–5M). Notably, concurrent knockdown of KMT2D abolished these therapeutic benefits, demonstrating that the antitumor effect of UXS1 inhibition depends on KMT2D (figure 5K–5M). Collectively, these data establish that UXS1 interacts with KMT2D and promotes its ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation by enhancing FBXW7 binding, leading to PD-L1 upregulation in HCC.

UXS1 upregulates PD-L1 to suppress CD8+ T-cell effector function

To investigate the regulatory role of UXS1 in the HCC immune microenvironment, we established a UXS1 KO mouse model (online supplemental figure 5A,B). Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of liver tissues from UXS1-KO and WT mice revealed that myeloid cells, hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, plasma cells, liver stem cells, and B cells maintained comparable proportions between groups (figure 6A and online supplemental figure 5C). Notably, UXS1 deficiency significantly increased T-cell infiltration, particularly CD8+ T cells, while NK cells, CD4+ T cells, and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) remained unchanged (online supplemental figure 5D). Subgroup analysis of CD4+ T cells and ILCs demonstrated stable proportions across CD4+ subtypes (Cd4_naive, Cd4_Th1, Cd4_Treg, Cd4_ISG) (online supplemental figure 5E). Intriguingly, ILC3 populations within the ILC compartment (ILC1, ILC2, ILC3, ILCreg) showed a decreasing trend in UXS1-KO mice (online supplemental figure 5F). Given ILC3’s established role in adaptive immunity through CXCL10-mediated CD8+ T-cell recruitment and Th17/Th1 cell modulation,30 these findings suggest UXS1-mediated ILC3 alterations may influence adaptive immune responses. CD8+ T-cell subpopulation analysis revealed comparable Cd8_Tem frequencies between groups (online supplemental figure 5E). However, UXS1-KO mice exhibited increased Cd8_Tex and Cd8_Teff cells with concomitant reduction in Cd8_Trm cells (figure 6B). Since tissue-resident Cd8_Trm cells demonstrate superior persistence compared with circulating Cd8_Teff cells,31 32 these data imply UXS1 may preferentially suppress CD8+ T-cell effector functions rather than memory formation.

KMT2D mediates UXS1-Induced transcriptional suppression of PD-L1 by recruiting the transcriptional repressor CEBPB

Mechanistic investigations revealed a significant positive correlation between UXS1 expression and PD-L1 levels in clinical HCC samples (figure 6C). Functional validation in HCC cell lines (Hep-3B and Huh-7) confirmed UXS1 as a critical regulator of PD-L1 expression. OE of UXS1 concurrently and significantly upregulated PD-L1 mRNA, total protein, and the level of functional PD-L1 protein on the cell membrane. Conversely, knockdown of UXS1 consistently led to a marked downregulation of PD-L1 at the transcriptional level, in total protein, and on the cell surface (figure 6D–6H, online supplemental figure 6A,B). To dissect the downstream mechanism, we focused on the epigenetic regulator KMT2D. ChIP assays demonstrated direct binding of KMT2D to the promoter region of the PD-L1 gene (figure 6I). Furthermore, KMT2D OE significantly inhibited the activity of a PD-L1 promoter-driven luciferase reporter (figure 6J), suggesting a transcriptional repressive role at this locus.

KMT2D is canonically recognized as a major H3K4 mono-methyltransferase that activates enhancers and promotes gene expression33,36; thus, its repressive function at the PD-L1 locus presented a paradox. We hypothesized that KMT2D might act through a non-canonical mechanism, possibly functioning as a molecular scaffold to recruit transcriptional repressors independently of its methyltransferase activity. This non-catalytic, architectural role is supported by precedents within the MLL/KMT2 family,37 38 and has been documented for other regulators such as the CXXC domain of MLL1 and the core COMPASS component WDR5.39 40 To identify candidate repressors mediating this effect, we performed in silico molecular docking analysis between KMT2D and a panel of known PD-L1 transcriptional repressors.41 AlphaFold3 analysis predicted a strong potential interaction between KMT2D and the transcription factor CCAAT enhancer binding protein beta (CEBPB) (figure 6K). We experimentally validated this interaction via Co-IP, confirming that KMT2D and CEBPB form a protein complex within cells (figure 6L). Furthermore, Co-IP results indicated that UXS1 KO increased the amount of CEBPB co-precipitated with KMT2D (figure 6M), suggesting that UXS1 may interfere with the stability of this complex by affecting KMT2D.

A series of rescue experiments established the functional interdependence of KMT2D and CEBPB in regulating PD-L1. The upregulation of PD-L1 induced by UXS1 OE was effectively reversed by co-expression of the CEBPB LIP isoform (figure 6N–6O and online supplemental figure 6C). Conversely, the suppressive effect of KMT2D OE on PD-L1 was completely abolished on CEBPB knockdown (figure 6P–6Q and online supplemental figure 6D). These data collectively demonstrate that the regulatory effects of both UXS1 and KMT2D on PD-L1 are mediated through CEBPB. We next investigated the functional consequence of the KMT2D-CEBPB interaction on the recruitment of CEBPB to the PD-L1 promoter. ChIP-quantitative PCR analysis showed that OE of UXS1 (which promotes KMT2D degradation) led to a significant decrease in CEBPB binding at the PD-L1 promoter. Importantly, co-OE of KMT2D effectively restored the enrichment of CEBPB at this promoter region (figure 6R). These results indicate that KMT2D is essential for maintaining or facilitating the stable binding of the repressor CEBPB to the PD-L1 promoter. Luciferase reporter assays provided further critical evidence for this mechanism. KMT2D OE suppressed PD-L1 promoter activity, whereas CEBPB knockdown enhanced it. More importantly, in the context of KMT2D OE, concurrent knockdown of CEBPB still resulted in increased promoter activity, definitively proving that KMT2D-mediated transcriptional repression of PD-L1 is dependent on CEBPB (figure 6S).

Collectively, these data support a model of a linear UXS1-KMT2D-CEBPB signaling axis in which UXS1-mediated degradation of KMT2D impairs the recruitment of the transcriptional repressor CEBPB to the PD-L1 promoter, resulting in transcriptional derepression and consequent upregulation of PD-L1.

Enhancing sensitivity to immune therapy through UXS1 intervention

Co-culture experiments demonstrated that UXS1 KO inhibited HCC cell proliferation in the absence of T cells, an effect reversed by T-cell co-culture (online supplemental figure 7A), indicating UXS1-mediated immune evasion counteracts T-cell cytotoxic function. Using NSG-MHC I/II DKO mice with DEN-CCL4-induced hepatocarcinogenesis (to eliminate immune interference), we demonstrated UXS1 OE significantly promoted tumorigenesis—an oncogenic effect reversed by KMT2D co-OE (figure 7A–7C), indicating KMT2D downregulation mediates UXS1’s tumor-promoting function. Given UXS1-driven PD-L1 elevation, we evaluated UXS1 deletion impacts on PD-1 blockade efficacy. In PD-L1-deficient mice bearing subcutaneous tumors, both UXS1 KO and PD-1 monotherapy inhibited tumor growth, with combination therapy showing synergistic enhancement (figure 7D–E, online supplemental figure 7B). To further establish the tumor-cell-intrinsic role of PD-L1, we deleted Pd-l1 specifically in UXS1-overexpressing HCC cells using CRISPR-Cas9 and implanted them into immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice (figure 7F). UXS1 OE alone promoted the fastest tumor growth and the lowest frequency of interferon-γ+ CD8+ T cells, effects that were not reversed by anti-PD-1 treatment (figure 7G–7I, online supplemental figure 7C,D). In contrast, tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 ablation fully reversed these phenotypes, and adding anti-PD-1 provided no additional benefit. These results confirm that tumor-cell-intrinsic PD-L1 is necessary and sufficient for UXS1-driven immune evasion. Immunohistochemistry and multiplex immunofluorescence analyses of human HCC specimens further validated the clinical relevance of the UXS1–KMT2D–PD-L1–CD8 axis. In tumor tissues, regions with high UXS1 protein expression exhibited low KMT2D expression, elevated PD-L1 levels, and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration, whereas regions with low UXS1 showed the inverse pattern (figure 7J). Correlative analyses across the cohort confirmed that UXS1 expression correlated positively with PD-L1 levels and inversely with both KMT2D expression and CD8+ T-cell density (figure 7K). Furthermore, analysis of a public HCC cohort treated with anti-PD-1 therapy revealed that the UXS1-high/KMT2D-low subgroup contained a higher proportion of non-responders, with the difference approaching statistical significance (figure 7L, p=0.0651).

In normal liver tissue, low UXS1 expression and high KMT2D levels are characteristic. The UXS1 protein subsequently interacts with the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBXW7, promoting FBXW7-mediated recognition and ubiquitination of KMT2D, leading to its proteasomal degradation. Discussion

Despite some progress in the era of immunotherapy, the OS rate of liver cancer remains largely unchanged in many patients.1 42 This is largely attributed to the fact that most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, rendering surgery or local treatments ineffective.43 The development of liver cancer is regulated by various complex molecular mechanisms, and the emergence of tumor drug resistance further exacerbates its high mortality. Studies have indicated that patients with liver cancer often exhibit primary resistance to drug treatment, which is partly due to the genetic heterogeneity of tumors.44 Clinical trials have shown that agents such as atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) and bevacizumab (anti-vascular endothelial growth factor), as well as the combination of tremelimumab (anti-cytotoxic T lymphocyte associated protein-4) and durvalumab (anti-PD-L1), achieve certain objective response rates.45 46 However, primary resistance remains a major clinical challenge.

To accelerate the identification of critical immunoregulatory genes in HCC, we developed a DIL Model that identified UXS1 as a robust immune-related prognostic marker. Our investigation revealed that HCC exhibits CNVs (gains) near the UXS1 locus, and UXS1 expression is negatively correlated with promoter DNA methylation—likely due to MZF1-mediated transcriptional activation via CpG island binding. Previous research has shown that, compared with normal cells, many tumor cells exhibit elevated UGDH expression, and loss of UXS1 leads to Golgi morphological abnormalities and glycosylation defects, thereby inducing apoptosis.18 Importantly, our study demonstrates that the tumor-promoting function of UXS1 in HCC is not dependent on the canonical UDPAG metabolic pathway. Instead, UXS1 drives KMT2D degradation, which relieves its transcriptional repression of PD-L1, leading to PD-L1 upregulation and immune evasion. This finding provides a mechanistic basis for exploring new strategies that might help overcome immunotherapy resistance in HCC, though further validation is needed. Additionally, we observed that deletion of the UXS1 gene results in decreased ILC3 levels in the mouse liver. A recent study in an HCC mouse model found that increased IL-17A production by ILC3 promotes tumor growth, and reducing the infiltration of IL-17A–producing ILC3 in combination with ICIs may benefit clinical treatment.47 Furthermore, a study of patients with HCC treated with ICI revealed that ILC3 can exhibit an activated state and may promote antitumor immunity under ICI therapy.48 While the functional consequence of reduced ILC3 on UXS1 deletion in our model requires further investigation, these reports highlight the complex and context-dependent role of ILC3 in HCC immunity.

Notably, our integrated analysis of the TCGA-LIHC cohort showed that patients with concurrent UXS1 copy-number amplification and promoter hypomethylation were diagnosed at significantly more advanced tumor stages (stage III/IV). Although this dual alteration did not independently predict worse OS in this cohort, its significant association with advanced disease stage suggests that UXS1 amplification coupled with epigenetic activation is linked to tumor progression and an aggressive phenotype at diagnosis, further supporting the clinical relevance of UXS1 dysregulation in HCC progression.

The expression of PD-L1 is regulated through a multilayered network encompassing transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, and post-translational mechanisms, with extensive crosstalk between regulatory layers and signaling networks.49 This complexity underscores the necessity to identify dominant regulatory axes within specific tumor microenvironments. Our study elucidates a novel UXS1–KMT2D–CEBPB regulatory axis that controls PD-L1 transcription in HCC. We demonstrate that UXS1 promotes FBXW7-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of KMT2D, thereby dismantling the KMT2D-dependent transcriptional repression of PD-L1. Intriguingly, KMT2D exerts this repressive function independently of its canonical histone H3K4 monomethyltransferase activity, suggesting a non-canonical, scaffold-like role at the PD-L1 locus. Mechanistically, our data position CEBPB as a critical functional intermediary: KMT2D, through direct physical interaction, is responsible for recruiting and stabilizing the transcriptional repressor CEBPB (LIP isoform) at the PD-L1 promoter, thereby directly repressing PD-L1 transcription. Thus, CEBPB serves as the key downstream effector in the functional axis mediating KMT2D-dependent PD-L1 repression.

Our data clearly showed that UXS1 OE enhances the binding between FBXW7 and KMT2D binding, and the precise molecular mechanism by which UXS1 facilitates this interaction was defined by truncation mapping assays. Under basal conditions, FBXW7 recognizes the PHD domains in the N-fragment of KMT2D. UXS1 OE not only enhanced the above interaction relationship, but also promotes a strong binding of FBXW7 to the SET domain in the C-terminal of KMT2D, indicating a UXS1-enforced conformational remodeling. Based on these observations, we propose two non-mutually exclusive possibilities: (1) UXS1 binding to KMT2D induces a conformational change that forms an additive FBXW7 recognition structure within the C-terminal SET domain involved; or (2) UXS1 acts as a scaffold that directly bridges FBXW7 and the KMT2D-SET domain, thereby promoting their interaction. Both models are consistent with the observed enhanced binding between FBXW7 and KMT2D on UXS1 OE. Further structural or mutagenesis studies will be required to dissect the detailed mechanism.

This non-canonical, repressive function of KMT2D stands in sharp contrast to the reported role of its family member MLL1 (KMT2A) as a direct transcriptional activator of PD-L1 in pancreatic cancer.50 This dichotomy highlights the remarkable context dependency of epigenetic regulators within the same protein family and illustrates how their functional output is determined by specific interacting partners, genomic loci, and cellular contexts. The enzyme-independent role we propose for KMT2D aligns with emerging paradigms that highlight non-catalytic, scaffolding functions for chromatin regulators, including within the COMPASS complex.37 38

The dualistic nature of KMT2D in tumorigenesis—functioning as a tumor suppressor in some contexts while its loss or mutation is associated with increased sensitivity to ICIs in others51,58—further emphasizes its complex, context-specific biology. Collectively, our work delineates a novel glycosyltransferase-epigenetic-immune checkpoint axis in HCC and reveals a context-specific, non-canonical role for KMT2D in immune evasion. These findings suggest that the UXS1/KMT2D/CEBPB axis could be a potential target for future therapeutic investigation in overcoming immune resistance in HCC, warranting further preclinical and clinical evaluation. However, as outlined below, several key questions must be addressed to translate this mechanistic insight into therapeutic strategies.

While our study reveals a previously unrecognized UXS1–KMT2D–CEBPB axis that may govern PD-L1 transcription and contribute to immune evasion in HCC, several limitations warrant further investigation and point to important future directions. First, at the mechanistic level, the precise details of how the KMT2D-CEBPB complex assembles and functions at the PD-L1 promoter require deeper characterization. Although we established their physical interaction and functional dependency, the exact molecular architecture of this repressive complex and the potential involvement of additional co-factors remain to be fully elucidated. Second, the dynamics and regulatory signals controlling the formation or dissolution of the CEBPB LIP isoform-containing repressive complex at the promoter need further exploration. Moreover, our functional validation relies primarily on in vitro models; in vivo rescue experiments—particularly those evaluating T cell-dependent antitumor effects—are necessary to consolidate the physiological relevance of this axis and its therapeutic potential. Finally, clinical evidence linking UXS1 expression or activity to patient responses to immunotherapy remains sparse; prospective studies in well-annotated immunotherapy cohorts with transcriptomic profiling will be essential to evaluate whether targeting this pathway can genuinely overcome immune resistance in HCC. Addressing these questions may help translate the molecular insights from this work into more effective clinical strategies in the future.
