Association of gait speed with risk of diabetes mellitus among older adults: findings from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Yan Bai, Rong Wu, Fan Zhang, Hua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGait speed, a simple and reliable measure of physical function, has been associated with various health outcomes. However, limited evidence exists on the relationship between gait speed and diabetes mellitus (DM) risk among older adults in China.MethodsWe included 3,241 older adult participants from the 2011–2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Gait speed was assessed by a 4-meter walking test at baseline (2011–2012). Incident DM events were recorded during follow-up until 2020. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for the association between gait speed, including per 1-unit or 1-standard difference increase and tertile group, and DM risk, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 9.0 years, 347 incident DM events were documented. In multivariable-adjusted analyses that adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle variables, and health status, compared to participants in the lowest tertile of gait speed (< 1.16 m/s), participants in the middle (1.16–1.51 m/s; adjusted HR: 0.791; 95% CI: 0.609–1.026) and highest (> 1.51 m/s; adjusted HR: 0.845; 95% CI: 0.647–1.102) tertile had a decreased risk of DM. Each per standard deviation increase in gait speed was associated with a 1.1% lower risk of DM (adjusted HR: 0.989; 95% CI: 0.885–1.106). In the restricted cubic spline regression, although a potential U-shaped pattern was observed, it was not statistically significant (P for non-linearity = 0.090).ConclusionAlthough faster gait speed showed a potential association with decreased risk of DM in older adults, the observed association did not reach statistical significance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06481-9.Keywords: Gait speed, Diabetes mellitus, CHARLS, Risk

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a progressive chronic disease and one of the most common metabolic disorders worldwide. It was reported that the current number of adults with DM globally is 537 million. By 2045, this number is expected to reach 783 million [1]. DM can damage multiple organ systems throughout the body, including the cardiovascular system, nerves, kidneys, and eyes, severely impacting patients’ quality of life and life expectancy [2]. China has the world’s largest adult population with DM. It is estimated that in 2013, the prevalence of DM among Chinese adults was 10.9%, with an additional 35.7% of the population having prediabetes [3]. Therefore, identifying risk prediction factors for DM is crucial for the early identification of high-risk populations and implementing effective preventive measures to curb the DM epidemic.

Gait speed, as a simple, safe, and easily measurable tool, was initially designed to assess frailty in older populations. Its evaluation results are associated with adverse health outcomes, including patient hospitalization and mortality rates [4]. Due to the ease of measuring gait speed and its potential as a modifiable risk factor, it has received widespread attention from the medical community in recent years [3]. In a study investigating the disability incidence and mortality risk among 27,220 older adults living in communities in the United States and Italy, gait speed was found to be a predictor of disability and mortality in the elderly population [5]. Gait speed can be influenced by an individual’s health status, neuromuscular control, cardiopulmonary condition, physical activity level, sensory and perceptual functions, as well as the characteristics of the environment in which they walk [6].

Evidence suggests that higher gait speed can elicit greater physiological responses [7]. One study found that [8] a faster gait speed significantly increases cardiac output and left ventricular ejection fraction, providing more favorable health benefits [9]. However, the gait speed of individuals with diabetes (1.3 m/s) is slower than that of healthy adults (1.4 m/s) [10]. A meta-analysis including 10 cohort studies found that walking at faster speeds (4–8 km/hour) was associated with a graded decrease in the risk of DM [11]. A study of 2,818 older adults in the United States showed that over a 9-year follow-up period, for every 0.1 m/s decrease in gait speed, the risk of developing DM increased by 12% [12]. However, most of these studies were conducted in Western populations, and the association between gait speed and DM risk among Chinese older adults remains unclear. Given the differences in race, culture, and lifestyle, understanding the relationship between gait speed and DM risk among older Chinese adults is crucial for developing effective strategies for DM prevention and healthy aging.

For now, the contribution of older individual gait speed to incident DM is unclear. This study, using the 2011–2020 data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), investigates the relationship between gait speed and incident DM in the older Chinese population.

Methods

Study population

The data for this study comes from CHARLS, a nationally representative cohort study that selects participants from 150 counties/districts and 450 villages/urban communities across 28 provinces in China. The survey targets Chinese adults aged 45 and older and their spouses. The baseline survey for this database was conducted in 2011–2012, with follow-up surveys conducted every two years thereafter. The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052-11015) [13]. For the current analysis, we included older participants aged 60 years and older who completed the gait speed assessment and had no history of DM at baseline (2011–2012). We excluded participants who missed gait speed and DM diagnostic data. In addition, participants with extreme values defined as >mean ± 3 SD for gait speed and selected laboratory measures were excluded to minimize the influence of likely measurement or recording errors. The final analysis sample included 3,241 participants who were ultimately considered eligible for participation (Fig. 1). This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines (Table S1).

Flow chart of the study population

Assessment of gait speed

Gait speed was assessed at baseline using a 4-meter walking test. Participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace along a 4-meter walkway, starting from a standing position [14]. A stopwatch was used to record the time taken to complete the 4-meter walk. Gait speed was calculated by dividing the distance (4 m) by the time taken (in seconds).

Assessment of DM events

Incident DM was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, or self-reported diabetes (participants were asked, “Have you been diagnosed with diabetes mellitus by a doctor?“) [15]. Individuals were followed up until the occurrence of diabetes events or the end of CHARLS 2020.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates were assessed at baseline through standardized questionnaires and physical examinations, which was according to previous literature [11]. Sociodemographic variables included age (< 70 or ≥ 70 years), gender (male or female), marriage (married or other), education (primary and below, secondary school, middle school, or high school and above), and residence (rural or urban). Lifestyle variables included smoking status (former or current), drinking status (former or current), and sleep duration (< 7 or ≥ 7 h). Health status variables included self-reported health (poor, general, and good), Activities of Daily Living (ADL; impaired or good), body mass index (BMI, underweight or normal or overweight), hypertension (no or yes), and depressive symptoms (no or yes). BMI is calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale was used to assess patients’ depressive symptoms, and a score of 10 and above was judged to have depressive symptoms [16].

Statistical analysis

All covariates were converted into categorical variables. The missing proportions for each covariate are shown in Table S2, and missing values were imputed based on the mode of each variable. Data are presented as median and interquartile ranges for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Baseline characteristics are summarized according to tertiles of gait speed and compared using the χ2 test and analysis of variance, as appropriate.

We used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for the association between gait speed and incident DM. Schoenfeld residuals and found no significant violations (P = 0.341). Follow-up time was calculated from the date of the baseline survey to the date of DM diagnosis or the last follow-up visit, whichever occurred first. Incidence rates of DM events per 1000 person-years were calculated by gait speed tertile. We then examined gait speed as a continuous variable, estimating the HR for each 0.1 m/s or standard deviation (SD) increase in gait speed. Four adjustment models were estimated: Model 1 was the crude model; Model 2 was adjusted for sociodemographic variables; Model 3 was adjusted for Model 2 plus lifestyle variables, and Model 4 was adjusted for Model 3 plus health status.

To assess the robustness of results based on imputed data, analyses were repeated on all outcomes using original data (no imputation), and excluding participants with prediabetes at baseline, which was defined as fasting blood glucose levels of 5.6–6.9 mmol/L or HbA1c levels of 5.7%−6.4% according to the American Diabetes Association criteria. Another sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding participants who were underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²), and those with depressive symptoms at baseline, defined as CESD score ≥ 10.

Additionally, we explored potential nonlinear associations using restricted cubic spline regression with three knots. We also conducted subgroup analyses to investigate whether the potential association between gait speed and DM would be affected by the following demographic and clinical characteristics: age, gender, sleep duration, hypertension, and BMI. P-values for interaction were assessed by interaction terms and the likelihood ratio test. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) and R software (version 4.2.0). Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Clinical characteristics of patients among different gait speed groups

Compared to participants in the lowest tertile of gait speed, those in the highest tertile were younger and more likely to be female. Additionally, regarding lifestyle, participants in the highest tertile of gait speed smoking and drinking less, and had less sleep duration than those in the lowest tertile. In terms of health status, participants in the highest tertile of gait speed had higher ADL scores. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed among different gait speed groups in age, gender, marriage, education level, residence, drinking, smoking, BMI, sleep duration, ADL, self-reported health, and depressive symptoms.

Association of gait speed with risk of DM

During the follow-up of 9.0 years, 347 (10.7%) participants developed DM. In the longitudinal analysis, the incidence rate of DM was 13.12 per 1000 person-years among persons with the lowest tertile, 10.94 per 1000 person-years among the middle tertile group, and 11.63 per 1000 person-years among the highest tertile participants. For each SD increase in gait speed, the risk of DM decreased by 1.1% (HR: 0.989; 95% CI: 0.885-1.106), with not statistically significant. After adjusting for sociodemographic, lifestyle variables, and health status, compared to the lowest tertile of gait speed, middle (HR: 0.791; 95% CI: 0.609-1.026) and highest (HR: 0.845; 95% CI: 0.647-1.102) gait speed participants were associated with a 20.9% and 25.5% reduced risk of DM, respectively, but still did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Similar results were obtained for analysis of the original (i.e., unimputed) dataset (Table S3). When participants with prediabetes at baseline were excluded, the associations between gait speed and incident DM remained obtained. Participants in the moderate gait speed group had a significantly lower risk of developing DM (HR: 0.627, 95% CI: 0.448-0.877), and those in the fastest group also showed a reduced risk (HR: 0.709, 95% CI: 0.506-0.994) compared to the slowest group.

A U-shaped association of gait speed as a continuous variable with incident DM was observed in the restricted cubic spline curve (Figure2). At gait speeds below 1.67 m/s, the risk of DM decreased with increasing gait speed and then tended to increase (Table S4). When participants who were underweight (n=286) or had depressive symptoms (n=1,269) at baseline were excluded, the U-shaped association between gait speed and incident diabetes mellitus risk persisted (Figure S1 and Figure S2). In a prespecified sensitivity analysis excluding participants with prediabetes at baseline, the associations between gait speed and incident diabetes became stronger and achieved statistical significance both on the linear scale (P=0.030) and for non-linearity (Pfor non-linearity=0.011). These findings are not fully consistent with our primary analysis and suggest that baseline metabolic impairment may have obscured the underlying relationship in the full cohort. (Figure S3).

Discussion

Main finding

In this prospective study of older Chinese adults using data from CHARLS, we aimed to explore the association between gait speed and the risk of developing DM. After adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and health status variables, participants with middle or highest gait speed had a 20.9% or 15.5% lower risk of developing DM compared to those with the lowest tertile of gait speed.

Comparison with similar studies

These findings are consistent with the results of a previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Ahmad et al. [11], which included cohort studies exploring the relationship between adult walking speed and DM risk. The study showed that walking at a faster pace was associated with a graded reduction in DM risk. However, the study's fast walking speed was associated with a 39% reduction in DM risk, equivalent to 2.24 fewer cases per 100 patients, which is higher than our study (HR=0.79). This difference may be explained by variations in population characteristics, measurement methods, and ethnic or lifestyle factors between our study and that of Ahmad et al.. Although the previous study included a larger sample size and a longer follow-up period, the relatively narrow confidence intervals in our analyses suggest that our non-significant findings likely reflect a true lack of association in this population, rather than being primarily attributable to limited statistical power. Aqeel et al.'s previous longitudinal study included 4,313 participants in the analysis. From baseline to the 96-month follow-up, the results showed that 301 participants developed diabetes during the follow-up period, with a cumulative incidence of 7.0% [17]. Gait speed decline was a significant predictor of diabetes incidence (RR=0.44, P = 0.018).

When step speed is considered as a continuous variable, we found that each unit increase in gait speed is associated with a 0.7% increase in DM risk. Although this is a minuscule effect size, given the large population base of older adults with diabetes, the results also emphasize the potential importance of maintaining or increasing gait speed as a diabetes prevention strategy [18]. Certainly, the 95% CI for each unit increases in gait speed (95% CI: 0.928-1.063) includes 1, indicating that this specific result should be interpreted with caution. This may suggest the need for further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods to more accurately estimate the strength of this association. Furthermore, an approximate U-shaped association was observed between gait speed and DM risk. Within a certain range, as gait speed increases, DM risk decreases, but when gait speed exceeds a certain range, DM risk increases instead. This implies that DM risk needs to be interpreted differently based on the relevant gait speed. Additionally, while an apparent U-shaped trend was observed in the restricted cubic spline analysis, we were unable to further explore the characteristics of participants with extremely high gait speeds (≥ 3 m/s) due to the small sample size of this subgroup. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to better understand this phenomenon.

It should be noted that our cohort exhibited relatively faster gait speeds compared with some published cohorts of community-dwelling older adults [11]. This may reflect sample composition (e.g., healthier survivors), task standardization (4-m usual-pace protocol), or measurement context. This feature may limit generalizability to populations with slower average gait speed, and effect estimates should be interpreted accordingly.

Potential mechanisms

This study demonstrated the importance of gait speed as a screening tool in the general population. Slow gait speed is associated with frailty, falls, poor quality of life, hospitalization, cognitive decline, and functional dependence [19–21]. The potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between gait speed and diabetes risk involve multiple aspects. Slower gait speed may reflect decreased muscle function, particularly the strength and endurance of lower limb muscles. Reduced muscle function leads to decreased insulin sensitivity, thereby increasing the risk of diabetes. The potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between gait speed and DM risk involve multiple aspects. A slower gait speed may reflect a decline in muscle function, particularly in the strength and endurance of lower limb muscles. Decreased muscle function can lead to reduced insulin sensitivity, thereby increasing the risk of DM [22]. Individuals with slower gait speed may have lower overall levels of physical activity. Long-term lack of sufficient physical activity is a known risk factor for type 2 diabetes [23]. Slower gait speed also reflects subclinical cardiovascular function decline and cardiovascular disease is significantly associated with increased DM risk [24]. Additionally, subtle changes in nervous system function, which is one of the mechanisms leading to decreased gait speed, may begin in older patients even before DM is diagnosed [25]. These mechanisms may interact, collectively intensifying the impact of decreased gait speed on diabetes risk. Exercise programs that focus on enhancing lower limb strength, balance, and overall physical function could be effective intervention strategies for improving gait speed in older patients. Moreover, such programs offer benefits beyond fall prevention and improved mobility, including metabolic health.

Although moderate brisk walking has many health benefits, excessively fast walking may bring some potential hazards: 1) exercise injuries: Walking too fast may increase the stress on the lower limbs and feet, especially when walking for an extended period or at a high intensity. This can lead to injuries to bones, joints, tendons, and ligaments. 2) cardiovascular risks: While moderate aerobic exercise helps improve cardiovascular health, overly intense exercise may increase the burden on the heart, leading to an excessively high heart rate and triggering angina, arrhythmias, and other issues [26]. 3) muscle fatigue and discomfort [27]: Walking too fast or for too long can cause excessive fatigue and discomfort in the lower limb muscles, particularly when lacking proper rest and recovery. This may affect walking posture and efficiency and increase the risk of injury [28]. In a similar study, Arjola Bano et al. [29] explored the relationship between gait speed and thyroid dysfunction and found that multivariable regression models showed a U-shaped association between the two (P<0.001). Our results provide new evidence for exploring the nonlinear relationship between gait speed and DM risk. Additionally, this study found that when analyzing gait speed as tertiles, the DM risk for the second tertile of gait speed was the lowest. Therefore, this study emphasizes that in clinical practice, moderate adjustments can be made to the optimal level of gait speed in the elderly between 1.16-1.51 m/s. These findings may provide some evidence to support the primary prevention of diabetes and reduce the incidence of diabetes risk in the elderly.

The observed U-shaped association between gait speed and the risk of incident diabetes mellitus warrants further discussion. One possible explanation is that individuals with very slow gait speed may have underlying frailty, sarcopenia, or preclinical illness, which are themselves linked to increased metabolic risk and the subsequent development of diabetes. Conversely, those with the fastest gait speed might represent a subset of individuals with other health characteristics-such as increased physical stress, over-exercise, or underlying undiagnosed medical conditions-that could paradoxically elevate diabetes risk. Our sensitivity analyses, excluding participants who were underweight or had depressive symptoms at baseline, showed that the U-shaped relationship persisted, supporting the robustness of this association.

Interesting, including individuals with prediabetes introduces baseline heterogeneity and potential reverse causality. Prediabetes is heterogeneous with respect to pathophysiology and clinical trajectory, and it increases short-term event probability. If prediabetes also affects gait performance at baseline-via early neuropathy, microvascular dysfunction, systemic inflammation, or reduced muscle quality-then gait speed may partly reflect concurrent disease severity rather than preceding risk, biasing the main analysis toward the null and blunting non-linear patterns [30]. Excluding prediabetes likely reduces misclassification (prediabetes vs normoglycemia), attenuates variance inflation, and improves contrast between exposure strata, thereby enhancing signal detection and revealing the observed U-shaped relationship. Nonetheless, because the sensitivity analysis reduces sample size and may preferentially exclude higher-risk individuals, the possibility of selection effects cannot be ruled out.

Implications for clinical practice

These findings have important implications for clinical practice, as gait speed is a simple and quick measure that can be easily incorporated into routine assessments of older adults in clinical settings. Healthcare providers can use gait speed as a screening tool to identify older adults who may be at increased risk of developing DM and implement preventive measures or interventions to reduce this risk. Furthermore, the association between gait speed and the risk of DM highlights the importance of promoting physical activity and maintaining mobility in older adults [31]. Encouraging older adults to engage in regular exercise and physical activity may not only improve gait speed but also help reduce the risk of developing DM and other chronic conditions. In addition, healthcare providers should consider gait speed as one of the factors to assess when evaluating older adults for DM risk factors. Incorporating gait speed measurements into routine clinical assessments may help identify individuals who would benefit from closer monitoring or intervention strategies to prevent the onset of DM. Overall, the findings from this study underscore the importance of considering gait speed as a potential marker for assessing the risk of DM among older adults and highlight the need for healthcare providers to incorporate this simple measure into routine clinical practice to improve preventive care and health outcomes for older adults.

Strength and limitations

The strength of this study is that it is nationally representative, so our findings may be generalizable to the Chinese elderly population. The current study results may be subject to some limitations, and several considerations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Firstly, despite adjustments for various covariates, the influence of residual confounding factors cannot be ruled out, such as family history of DM, duration of DM, and pharmacological treatments. In additionally, although we adjusted for major comorbidities available in CHARLS (e.g., hypertension and depressive symptoms), data on frailty, sarcopenia, and dementia were unavailable or incomplete. The potential confounding effects of unmeasured comorbidities cannot be completely excluded. Secondly, only participants from China were involved in this cohort, the findings of this study may not be generalizable to populations outside of China. Therefore, larger-scale cohort studies are needed to draw more reliable conclusions; Thirdly, because CHARLS does not collect information on DM typing, this study could not distinguish between type 1 or type 2 DM. Nevertheless, all our participants were aged 60 or older, making type 1 DM rare. Therefore, any potential misclassification is unlikely to have influenced the results substantially. Fourth, the relatively small effect sizes and marginal trends observed in our study may reflect limited statistical power or residual confounding. While we noted non-significant HRs below 1, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a definitive association. Sixth, due to the biennial follow-up intervals and the uncertainty regarding the exact timing of diabetes onset, we were unable to perform a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who developed diabetes within the first few years after baseline. This limitation may not fully address concerns about reverse causality, as subtle preclinical stages of diabetes could have affected gait speed measurements at baseline. It is important to note that misclassification bias is distinct from reverse causality; even with improved diabetes ascertainment-by combining self-report, fasting glucose, and HbA1c data-reverse causality could still exist: subclinical metabolic dysfunction at baseline may have influenced gait speed, attenuating associations in the primary analysis; results strengthened after excluding prediabetes (Figure S3), consistent with this possibility.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the role of gait speed in predicting the incidence of DM. The results demonstrate the value of baseline gait speed in predicting the risk of DM incidence in the population, even after controlling various covariates. Furthermore, our study provides new epidemiological evidence suggesting an approximate U-shaped non-linear relationship between gait speed and DM risk among older Chinese adults. Further research is needed to elucidate the actual effects of gait speed on DM risk and the underlying biological mechanisms.
