Effects of Individualized Childbirth Education Program on Anxiety Levels, Mode of Delivery, and Postpartum Adaptation Among Advanced Maternal Age Primiparas
Authors: Yan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:To investigate the effects of an individualized childbirth education program on childbirth anxiety levels, delivery mode choices, and postpartum adaptation among advanced maternal age primiparas, providing scientific evidence for improving perinatal care quality in this population.Methods:A total of 104 advanced maternal age primiparas who registered at the Tianshui Wulin Subdistrict Community Health Service Center Community Health Service Center's obstetrics clinic (perinatal health care clinic) and delivered between January 2023 and December 2023 were enrolled as research subjects. Using a random number table method, participants were divided into an intervention group (n = 55) and a control group (n = 49). The control group received routine prenatal examinations and health education, while the intervention group additionally received an individualized childbirth education program, including: (1) customized knowledge instruction based on individual pregnant women’s needs and cognitive levels; (2) virtual reality technology-assisted simulation of childbirth scenarios; (3) psychological support and relaxation training; (4) partner-involved joint learning; and (5) establishment of a personalized birth plan. The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Childbirth Self-Efficacy Inventory (CBSEI), delivery mode, and postpartum adaptation (Maternal Postnatal Attachment Scale, MPAS), postpartum depression (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, EPDS), and subjective well-being (General Well-Being Schedule, GWB) were assessed at 28 weeks, 36 weeks of gestation, and 42 days postpartum, respectively.Results:At baseline (28 weeks gestation), there were no statistically significant differences in STAI and CBSEI scores between the 2 groups (P > .05). Based on change score analysis, the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater anxiety reduction at 36 weeks gestation (change: −5.45 ± 3.21 vs. −0.48 ± 2.87, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.65) and improved childbirth self-efficacy (change: 25.47 ± 8.92 vs. 4.92 ± 7.34, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.51) compared to the control group. These large effect sizes indicate clinically meaningful improvements. The rate of vaginal delivery in the intervention group (72.73% vs. 44.90%) was significantly higher than that in the control group, while the cesarean section rate (21.82% vs. 46.94%) was significantly lower than that in the control group (both P < .01). At 42 days postpartum, the MPAS score (83.46 ± 7.25 vs. 75.32 ± 8.14, Cohen’s d = 1.06) and GWB score (85.73 ± 8.42 vs. 76.19 ± 9.15, Cohen’s d = 1.09) in the intervention group were significantly higher than those in the control group, while the EPDS score (6.27 ± 2.31 vs. 9.45 ± 3.26, Cohen’s d = 1.14) was significantly lower than that in the control group (all P < .001). Anxiety reduction was sustained at 42 days postpartum (change: −8.74 ± 4.12 vs. 0.14 ± 3.95, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.19). The time to first breastfeeding in the intervention group (30.25 ± 15.43 vs. 52.67 ± 18.21 minutes) was significantly earlier than that in the control group (P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.36), the 48-hour milk secretion volume (118.42 ± 35.67 vs. 96.83 ± 32.94 mL) was significantly higher than that in the control group (P = .002, Cohen’s d = 0.64), and the exclusive breastfeeding rate at 42 days postpartum (81.82% vs. 61.22%) was higher in the intervention group compared to the control group (P = .018).Conclusion:An individualized childbirth education program for advanced maternal age primiparas can effectively reduce childbirth anxiety levels, improve childbirth self-efficacy, increase the rate of vaginal delivery, and improve postpartum psychological adaptation and breastfeeding outcomes, which has positive significance for enhancing the childbirth experience and postpartum quality of life for advanced maternal age primiparas.

Introduction

In recent years, the proportion of women of advanced maternal age has continued to increase globally. Research has shown that the percentage of advanced maternal age women (≥35 years) in the United States has been rising steadily, and some of these pregnancies are not due to women’s voluntary decisions to delay childbearing. Factors affecting advanced maternal age pregnancies include educational level, income level, marital status, degree of urbanization, and racial/ethnic differences.1,2 Advanced maternal age primiparas face higher risks of pregnancy complications and adverse obstetric outcomes, including gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, placental abruption, postpartum hemorrhage, and adverse neonatal outcomes.3,4 Additionally, advanced maternal age primiparas generally experience higher levels of childbirth anxiety and fear, leading them to prefer cesarean sections and other medical interventions.5

Childbirth anxiety not only affects the choice of delivery mode but may also lead to prolonged labor, increased analgesic requirements, and postpartum depression.6,7 Research indicates that childbirth anxiety in advanced maternal age primiparas stems from multiple factors, including fear of childbirth pain, concerns about maternal and infant safety, uncertainty about the unknown birthing process, and physiological and psychological changes associated with increased age.8 However, existing prenatal education often employs standardized content that fails to adequately consider the special needs and psychological characteristics of advanced maternal age primiparas.9

Traditional prenatal education primarily focuses on imparting basic childbirth knowledge, with relatively insufficient attention to psychological support and practical skills training for pregnant women.10 Research has shown that individualized prenatal interventions can significantly reduce childbirth anxiety and improve childbirth self-efficacy in pregnant women.11 In recent years, the application of virtual reality (VR) technology in medical education has gradually increased, providing new possibilities for childbirth education.12 The VR immersive experiences can make abstract childbirth knowledge intuitive and tangible, helping pregnant women pre-experience the childbirth process in a safe environment, thereby reducing fear of the unknown.13 Additionally, cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness training have shown positive effects in reducing anxiety,14 while partner-involved joint learning has been proven to enhance social support systems and promote positive childbirth experiences.15

As the medical model shifts from disease-centered to person-centered, obstetric services are also increasingly emphasizing individualization and humanization.16 However, research on individualized childbirth education for advanced maternal age primiparas is relatively scarce, particularly regarding comprehensive intervention programs that integrate innovative technologies and psychological support strategies.17 Existing studies mostly focus on the effects of single intervention methods on general pregnant women, with insufficient attention to advanced maternal age primiparas as a special group.18 Meanwhile, evaluations of intervention effects are often concentrated on the childbirth period, with fewer studies on the impact on postpartum adaptation.19

This study aims to construct a comprehensive intervention program integrating individualized needs assessment, VR technology, psychological support strategies, partner involvement, and personalized birth plans, and to evaluate its effects on childbirth anxiety levels, delivery mode choices, and postpartum adaptation in advanced maternal age primiparas, providing a basis for improving perinatal care quality for this population.

Material and Methods

Study Participants

A total of 104 advanced maternal age primiparas who registered at the Tianshui Wulin Subdistrict Community Health Service Center’s obstetrics clinic (perinatal health care clinic) and delivered between January 2023 and December 2023 were enrolled as research subjects. Inclusion criteria were: (1) primiparas aged ≥35 years; (2) singleton pregnancy; (3) gestational age ≥28 weeks; (4) planning to deliver at the hospital; and (5) voluntary participation in this study with signed written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of psychiatric disorders; (2) severe medical conditions during pregnancy, such as heart disease, kidney disease, thyroid dysfunction, etc.; (3) fetal malformation or intrauterine growth restriction; (4) absolute indications for cesarean section, such as placenta previa, cephalopelvic disproportion, etc.; and (5) severe pregnancy complications, such as severe preeclampsia, placental abruption, etc.

Using a random number table method, 104 advanced maternal age primiparas were divided into an intervention group (n = 55) and a control group (n = 49). The mean age of the intervention group was 37.42 ± 2.35 years, with a mean gestational age of 38.56 ± 1.24 weeks; the mean age of the control group was 36.98 ± 2.41 years, with a mean gestational age of 38.73 ± 1.31 weeks. There were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of age, gestational age, educational level, occupation, monthly family income, and incidence of pregnancy complications (P > .05), indicating that the groups were comparable. In this study, 104 advanced maternal age primiparas from the Tianshui Wulin Subdistrict Community Health Service Center Community Health Service Center voluntarily participated in the survey. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianshui Wulin Subdistrict Community Health Service Center (approval number: 2022102806; date: October 27, 2022). All research subjects provided informed consent.

Study Methods

Intervention Methods:

Control group: Participants received routine obstetric care and health education, including prenatal examinations, routine prenatal health guidance, general childbirth knowledge, and postpartum care instructions. These were provided through outpatient visits and pregnancy school classes. The main content included prenatal nutritional guidance, self-monitoring during pregnancy, the childbirth process and coping methods, postpartum recovery, and newborn care. Each pregnancy school class lasted approximately 60 minutes, with a total of 4 classes scheduled at 20, 28, 32, and 36 weeks of gestation.

Intervention group: Participants received an individualized childbirth education program in addition to routine care. The intervention team consisted of experienced obstetricians and midwives, each of whom underwent a 2-week standardized training to ensure consistency of the intervention measures. To ensure intervention fidelity, all intervention protocols were standardized and documented in detailed manuals, and regular supervision meetings were conducted weekly to maintain consistency across practitioners. The specific content of the individualized childbirth education program was as follows:

(1) Individualized needs assessment and customized educational content: At 28 weeks of gestation, professional midwives conducted one-on-one interviews (approximately 60 minutes) with each advanced maternal age primipara to assess their level of knowledge about childbirth, concerns, and expectations. The parturation knowledge questionnaire and childbirth attitudes questionnaire were used to evaluate their childbirth knowledge and attitudes, and individualized education plans were developed based on the assessment results.

(2) Virtual reality-assisted childbirth experience teaching: At 32 weeks of gestation, VR technology (device model: VIVE Pro 2, HTC Corporation) was used to provide an immersive childbirth experience. The VR content was developed in collaboration with obstetric specialists and validated through pilot testing with 20 primiparous women prior to study implementation. Content validation was assessed using a 5-point Likert scale questionnaire evaluating realism, educational value, and acceptability (all domains scored >4.0/5.0). User acceptance was monitored throughout the study using a standardized questionnaire, with 95% of participants rating the VR experience as helpful or very helpful. The content included: (1) familiarization with the delivery room environment; (2) three-dimensional simulation of the stages of childbirth (including uterine contractions, cervical dilation, fetal descent, etc.); (3) interactive practice of birthing positions and breathing techniques; and (4) simulation of possible emergency scenarios. Each pregnant woman received approximately 40 minutes of VR experience, with midwives providing guidance and answering questions throughout the process. All VR sessions were conducted using standardized protocols to ensure consistency across participants.

Statistical Methods

SPSS 26.0 software (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For continuous variables, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and independent samples t-test was used for between-group comparisons. Count data were expressed as number and percentage (n, %), and χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparisons between groups.

For repeated-measures outcomes (STAI and CBSEI scores), analyses were conducted as follows: Within-group comparisons: Paired t-test was used to compare baseline vs. follow-up measurements within each group. Between-group comparisons: change scores (difference = follow-up score − baseline score) were calculated for each participant. Independent samples t-test was used to compare change scores between groups. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and reported with 95% CIs. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

As shown in Table 1, there were no significant differences in demographic characteristics including age, gestational weeks, educational level, occupation, monthly family income, and incidence of pregnancy complications between the 2 groups (all P > .05), indicating that the 2 groups were comparable at baseline.

Delivery Modes

The delivery modes of the 2 groups are displayed in Table 3 and Figure 2. The rate of vaginal delivery in the intervention group (78.18%) was significantly higher than that in the control group (53.06%) (P = .006). Correspondingly, the cesarean section rate in the intervention group (21.82%) was significantly lower than that in the control group (46.94%) (P = .006). There was no significant difference in the rate of assisted vaginal delivery between the 2 groups (5.45% vs. 8.16%, P = .713). These results suggest that the individualized childbirth education program effectively increased the rate of vaginal delivery and reduced the rate of cesarean section in advanced maternal age primiparous women.

Postpartum Adaptation Outcomes

The postpartum adaptation outcomes of both groups at 42 days after delivery are presented in Table 4. As illustrated in Figure 3, the intervention group demonstrated superior performance across all maternal outcomes and breastfeeding parameters. Compared with the control group, the intervention group showed significantly higher maternal-infant attachment scores (83.46 ± 7.25 vs. 75.32 ± 8.14, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.65-1.47), indicating better mother-infant bonding. The EPDS scores in the intervention group were significantly lower than those in the control group (6.27 ± 2.31 vs. 9.45 ± 3.26, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.72-1.56), suggesting a lower risk of postpartum depression. Moreover, the intervention group demonstrated significantly higher general well-being scores compared to the control group (85.73 ± 8.42 vs. 76.19 ± 9.15, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.68-1.50). Additionally, 7 women (14.29%) in the control group had EPDS scores ≥10, indicating a risk of postpartum depression, whereas only 3 women (5.45%) in the intervention group showed such risk (P = .127). These results demonstrate that the individualized childbirth education program positively influenced maternal postpartum psychological adaptation.

Peripartum Outcomes

Table 5 presents the peripartum outcomes of both groups. The duration of labor in the intervention group was slightly shorter than that in the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant for either the first stage (9.87 ± 2.76 vs. 10.42 ± 2.93 hours, P = .328) or the second stage (52.63 ± 24.18 vs. 58.84 ± 26.73 minutes, P = .308). The postpartum 24-hour blood loss was comparable between the 2 groups (P = .279). However, the intervention group showed significantly earlier initiation of breastfeeding compared to the control group (30.25 ± 15.43 vs. 52.67 ± 18.21 minutes, P < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.36, 95% CI: 0.93-1.79). Similarly, the 48-hour milk secretion volume was significantly higher in the intervention group (118.42 ± 35.67 vs. 96.83 ± 32.94 mL, P = .002, Cohen’s d = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.25-1.03). The rate of exclusive breastfeeding at 42 days postpartum was also significantly higher in the intervention group compared to the control group (81.82% vs. 61.22%, P = .018). These findings suggest that the individualized childbirth education program effectively promoted early breastfeeding initiation and improved breastfeeding outcomes.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of an individualized childbirth education program on childbirth anxiety levels, delivery mode choices, and postpartum adaptation in advanced maternal age primiparous women. The results demonstrated that advanced maternal age primiparous women who received the individualized childbirth education program showed better outcomes than the control group receiving routine prenatal education in terms of childbirth anxiety levels, childbirth self-efficacy, vaginal delivery rates, and postpartum adaptation.

The study found that after the individualized childbirth education intervention, the anxiety level of the advanced maternal age primiparous women in the intervention group at 36 weeks of gestation was significantly lower than that in the control group, and this difference persisted at 42 days postpartum. The large effect sizes observed (Cohen’s d = 1.65 for anxiety reduction and d = 2.51 for self-efficacy improvement) indicate not only statistical significance but also substantial clinical relevance. The childbirth self-efficacy in the intervention group was also significantly higher than that in the control group. More importantly, the analysis based on change scores from baseline provides robust evidence that these improvements were directly attributable to the intervention rather than baseline differences between groups. These results indicate that the individualized childbirth education program effectively reduced childbirth anxiety levels and improved childbirth self-efficacy in advanced maternal age primiparous women, with effects that continued postpartum.

Several key factors are proposed as potential mechanisms for this phenomenon: first, the individualized needs assessment and customized educational content employed in this study made the intervention more targeted, precisely meeting the individualized knowledge and psychological needs of advanced maternal age primiparous women. This is consistent with the findings of Kaur et al,20 who explored the impact of midwife-led supportive educational interventions on perinatal outcomes in primiparous women with childbirth fear. Their study found significant improvements in newborn birth weight and full-term deliveries (P < .001) among primiparous women receiving the intervention, indicating that individualized supportive education has positive effects on reducing childbirth fear and improving maternal health.

Second, the VR technology applied in this study provided pregnant women with an immersive childbirth experience, allowing them to pre-experience the childbirth process in a safe environment, thus reducing fear of unknown situations.21 The high acceptability of VR technology (95% of participants rating it as helpful or very helpful) suggests that this innovative approach was well-received and contributed meaningfully to the intervention’s effectiveness. The content validation process ensured that the VR scenarios were both realistic and educationally valuable, which may explain the significant improvements in childbirth self-efficacy observed in the study.

Third, the psychological support and relaxation training in this study provided pregnant women with practical anxiety management tools, partner-involved joint learning strengthened social support systems, and the establishment of personal birth plans enhanced pregnant women’s sense of control and participation in the childbirth process. However, it is important to acknowledge that the multicomponent intervention design makes it impossible to determine which specific elements contributed most significantly to the observed outcomes. This represents a limitation of the study design, as the relative importance of VR technology, cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness training, partner involvement, and personalized birth plans cannot be individually assessed. These intervention strategies worked together to effectively reduce childbirth anxiety levels and improve self-efficacy in advanced maternal age primiparous women.

The anxiety levels of pregnant women in the intervention group not only significantly decreased after the intervention but also continued to show this effect at 42 days postpartum. This sustained improvement, as evidenced by the large effect size (Cohen’s d = 2.19) for anxiety reduction at 42 days postpartum, suggests that the intervention had lasting protective effects rather than temporary benefits. This indicates that the individualized childbirth education program not only positively affected psychological states during childbirth but also had a lasting protective effect on postpartum psychological adaptation. This finding is consistent with the results of Suryaningsih et al,22 who found that a childbirth education program based on Roy’s adaptation model effectively reduced anxiety levels during pregnancy.

The results showed that the vaginal delivery rate in the intervention group was significantly higher than that in the control group, while the cesarean section rate was significantly lower. This result suggests that the individualized childbirth education program effectively increased the vaginal delivery rate and reduced the cesarean section rate in advanced maternal age primiparous women. This significant difference may stem from multiple factors: first, the individualized childbirth education program in this study helped advanced maternal age primiparous women form more scientific childbirth cognition by providing comprehensive and individualized childbirth knowledge. Research has shown that correct cognition of childbirth is an important factor affecting pregnant women’s choice of delivery mode.23,24

Second, the VR technology used in this study allowed pregnant women to intuitively understand the process of vaginal delivery, reducing fear of natural childbirth. Third, improved childbirth self-efficacy is also an important factor influencing delivery mode. In this study, the CBSEI scores of pregnant women in the intervention group were significantly higher than those in the control group, and this enhanced self-efficacy may have strengthened their confidence in choosing and persisting with vaginal delivery. There was also a significant difference in elective cesarean section rates between the intervention and control groups, indicating that the individualized childbirth education program affected not only pregnant women’s coping abilities during labor but also their decision-making regarding delivery mode during pregnancy. This influence may be related to pregnant women acquiring more scientific childbirth cognition through education, reduced fear of childbirth, and professional support and encouragement provided by healthcare professionals during the educational process.25,26

The results showed that maternal-infant attachment scores at 42 days postpartum were significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group, postpartum depression risk scores were significantly lower, and general well-being scores were significantly higher. The moderate to large effect sizes observed across these outcomes (Cohen’s d ranging from 1.06 to 1.14) demonstrate clinically meaningful improvements in maternal psychological well-being. These results indicate that the individualized childbirth education program significantly improved postpartum psychological adaptation in advanced maternal age primiparous women.

Furthermore, the intervention group performed better than the control group in terms of time to first breastfeeding and milk secretion volume at 48 hours postpartum, and the exclusive breastfeeding rate at 42 days postpartum was also significantly higher. Notably, while the exclusive breastfeeding rate showed a numerical advantage in the intervention group (81.82% vs. 61.22%), this difference approached but did not reach statistical significance (P = .018), suggesting that larger studies may be needed to definitively establish this benefit. This suggests that the individualized childbirth education program not only improved maternal psychological adaptation but also promoted the establishment and maintenance of breastfeeding. This may be related to the lower anxiety levels and higher vaginal delivery rates in the intervention group, as research has shown that anxiety can inhibit oxytocin secretion, thereby affecting the milk ejection reflex,27 while oxytocin released during vaginal delivery helps promote milk secretion and enhance maternal-infant attachment.28

The individualized childbirth education program in this study has several innovative aspects: first, the program was developed based on individualized needs assessment, ensuring the targeted nature and effectiveness of the intervention; second, VR technology was introduced to provide an immersive childbirth experience, making abstract childbirth knowledge concrete and perceptible; third, the program integrated psychological techniques such as cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness-based stress reduction, providing practical anxiety management tools; finally, it emphasized partner involvement and the establishment of a personal birth plan, enhancing the pregnant woman’s sense of control and support system. The systematic validation of the VR content and monitoring of user acceptance throughout the study adds to the scientific rigor of the approach and provides a foundation for future implementation. These innovative strategies worked together to form a comprehensive and individualized intervention system that effectively met the multifaceted needs of advanced maternal age primiparous women.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged: first, the single-center design and relatively small sample size (n = 104) limit the generalizability of the findings. All participants were from the same hospital and shared similar cultural backgrounds, which may restrict the applicability of results to more diverse populations. The cultural homogeneity of the sample is particularly important to consider, as childbirth perceptions, anxiety levels, and responses to educational interventions may vary significantly across different cultural contexts.

Second, the research period was relatively short, with follow-up only until 42 days postpartum, making it impossible to evaluate the long-term sustainability of the intervention effects. Longer-term follow-up would provide valuable insights into whether the observed benefits in maternal-infant attachment, breastfeeding outcomes, and psychological well-being persist beyond the immediate postpartum period.

Third, due to the multicomponent intervention design, it was not possible to determine which specific components of the individualized childbirth education program had the greatest impact on the outcomes. The intervention included VR technology, cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness training, partner involvement, and personalized birth plans; however, the independent contribution of each element could not be assessed. This limitation is inherent in complex behavioral interventions but represents an important area for future research.

Fourth, this study employed an open-label design due to the nature of the behavioral intervention. While outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation, participants and intervention providers could not be blinded, which may have introduced expectancy effects or social desirability bias. The potential for expectancy effects is particularly relevant given the comprehensive nature of the intervention and the attention received by participants in the intervention group.

Fifth, the study failed to collect objective indicators such as actual childbirth pain levels, physiological stress markers, or behavioral observations during delivery, mainly relying on self-reported subjective assessments, which may have certain biases. The inclusion of biomarkers such as cortisol levels or heart rate variability could have strengthened the objectivity of the findings.

The substantial effect sizes observed in the study suggest that individualized childbirth education programs could represent a cost-effective intervention for improving perinatal outcomes in advanced maternal age primiparous women. The program’s focus on reducing anxiety, improving self-efficacy, and promoting vaginal delivery aligns with current initiatives to reduce unnecessary cesarean sections and improve maternal satisfaction with childbirth experiences.

The successful implementation of VR technology in prenatal education represents a promising avenue for healthcare innovation, particularly as VR becomes more accessible and cost-effective. The high acceptance rate and positive feedback from participants suggest that this technology could be readily integrated into routine prenatal care.

The results of this study indicate that an individualized childbirth education program targeted at advanced maternal age primiparous women can effectively reduce childbirth anxiety levels, improve childbirth self-efficacy, increase vaginal delivery rates, and improve postpartum psychological adaptation and breastfeeding outcomes. The large effect sizes observed across multiple domains provide compelling evidence for the clinical significance of these improvements. These findings provide new intervention strategies for perinatal care of advanced maternal age primiparous women, helping to improve the childbirth experience and postpartum quality of life for this special population. However, future research should address the limitations identified in this study, particularly through larger, multi-center trials with longer follow-up periods and component-specific analyses to optimize intervention delivery and maximize clinical benefits.
