When cellulitis isn’t cellulitis: A case of rapidly progressive cutaneous hemorrhagic bullae and purpura with mucosal involvement
Authors: Lauren James, Phuong Daniels, Shelby Boock, Chelsea Schwartz, Ashley Rice
Published: 2026

Case description

A 77-year-old male with a past medical history of diastolic heart failure, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and stage 3 chronic kidney disease previously-well managed for approximately 2 years with hydralazine, apixaban, and rosuvastatin presented with non-healing lower extremity ulcerations. Two months earlier, he was hospitalized for volume overload that was complicated by the development of lower extremity wounds. Despite completing 3 courses of cephalexin for presumed cellulitis, the patient’s wounds persisted. In the emergency department, he was found to have fever, new-onset anemia, hematuria, patchy lung opacities, and enlarging lower extremity ulcerations, purpura, and hemorrhagic bullae. Over the next 48 hours, his cutaneous findings rapidly progressed to involve the trunk, upper extremities, and multiple mucosal sites. Physical examination revealed numerous dusky, necrotic bullae and palpable purpura involving the bilateral lower and upper extremities (Fig 1, A) and back (Fig 1, B) and hands. Oral bullae (Fig 1, D), bilateral conjunctival injection (Fig 1, E), and superficial lip blistering were also present on exam. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated the following: erythrocyte sedimentation rate 88 mm/h, C-reactive protein 180 mg/L, C3 76.7 mg/dL, and C4 11.3 mg/dL. Positive autoimmune studies include anti-double stranded deoxyribonucleic acid 15 IU/mL, anti-histone 6.1 IU/mL, anti-myeloperoxidase >8 IU/mL, anti-proteinase 3 21 IU/mL (ref 0-0.9), and p-ANCA (antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies) 1:320 (ref <1:20). Desmoglein 1 and 3 antibodies, BP180 and BP230 antibodies, cryoglobulin, HIV, antinuclear antibodies, rheumatoid factor, and anti-glomerular basement membrane antibodies were negative. A cutaneous punch biopsy demonstrated a neutrophilic dermatosis with leukocytoclastic vasculitis, fibrinoid necrosis of small vessel walls, prominent karyorrhexis, and numerous cryptococcoid bodies representing degenerating neutrophils (Fig 1, C). Tissue cultures were sterile. Subsequent renal biopsy demonstrated pauci-immune necrotizing glomerulonephritis.

Case discussion

B. Drug-induced vasculitis – Correct. Drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis is a distinct clinicopathologic entity that is mechanistically related to, but diagnostically separate from, the primary idiopathic ANCA vasculitides, including granulomatosis with polyangiitis, microscopic polyangiitis, and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis.1 In this case, the clinical, serologic, and histopathologic features are more consistent with drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis than with primary disease.2 Among the known offending agents, hydralazine is one of the most strongly associated triggers and represents the most likely cause in this patient.3

Clinically, drug-induced vasculitis most often presents with palpable or retiform purpura, ulcerations, or livedo reticularis and may be accompanied by constitutional symptoms such as fever, malaise, or weight loss.2 Severe disease may manifest with mucosal involvement or rapidly progressive necrotic skin lesions and is associated with a higher risk of systemic involvement.2 Renal involvement in the form of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis and pulmonary manifestations such as alveolar hemorrhage are hallmark features of ANCA-associated vasculitis.4

Histopathologically, hydralazine-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis is characterized by leukocytoclastic vasculitis with fibrinoid necrosis, karyorrhectic debris, and neutrophil-rich infiltrates, sometimes with cryptococcoid bodies representing degenerating neutrophils surrounded by clear halos.3 Differentiating primary from drug-induced disease can be challenging due to overlapping features; however, drug-induced cases more commonly demonstrate myeloperoxidase-ANCA positivity and may show dual myeloperoxidase- and proteinase 3-ANCA positivity.1 The presence of additional autoantibodies such as ANA, anti-histone, or anti–double-stranded DNA antibodies further supports a drug-induced etiology.5

In this case, drug-induced vasculitis most likely arose in association with hydralazine exposure. Notably, the patient had no exposure to iodinated radiocontrast prior to the onset of symptoms, thus making iododerma unlikely in this case.3 Although often a diagnosis of exclusion, early recognition is critical, as management requires prompt discontinuation of the offending medication, systemic corticosteroids for organ involvement, and escalation to immunosuppressive therapy in severe disease.4
