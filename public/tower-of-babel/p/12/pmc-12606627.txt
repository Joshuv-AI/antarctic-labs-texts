The Effects of Chirality and Salt Addition on Phase Separation and Complexation Morphology in Mixtures of Polypeptides and Polypeptoids
Authors: Kimiasadat Mirlohi, Anuja Thapa, Donghui Zhang, Whitney C. Blocher McTigue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), particularly through coacervation, offers a groundbreaking approach to drug delivery by encapsulating therapeutic agents within phase-separated droplets, enhancing their stability, solubility, and controlled release. Polypeptides and polypeptoids, with their structural diversity and tunability, emerge as promising candidates for exploring these systems, with polypeptoids offering unique advantages, such as resistance to enzymatic degradation and increased control over interactions. This study examines the impact of chirality, mixing charge fraction, and salt concentration on the phase behavior and morphology of homochiral, mixed-chiral, and achiral polymers. By exploring the role of chirality and ionic strength in determining the presence and morphology of complexation, this research provides critical insights for designing tunable coacervate systems. Our results show that polypeptides and polypeptoids demonstrate chirality-dependent complexation. Additionally, we show that the presence and morphology of phase separation within these systems are influenced by the concentration of charged species in each sample, enabling the control and tunability of complex formation. These findings have the potential to advance the development of biomaterials for applications ranging from gene therapy to vaccine stabilization, offering innovative solutions to pressing biomedical challenges.

Introduction

In the intricate world of biological macromolecules, liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) has garnered considerable attention due to its potential applications in drug delivery systems. Coacervation, a specific type of LLPS based on the electrostatic interactions of polyelectrolytes, facilitates the formation of phase-separated droplets that can encapsulate therapeutic agents, providing a promising avenue for enhancing the solubility, stability, and controlled release of various biologics and small molecules. The ability of coacervate systems to organize biomolecules through weak, noncovalent interactions enables the efficient protection and delivery of therapeutics, including proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids. Recent studies have highlighted the innovative use of coacervation to stabilize nucleic acid–based therapeutics, such as DNA and RNA, against environmental stressors. For instance, coacervation can mitigate the degradation of nucleic acids by forming protective biomolecular condensates, thereby improving their thermal stability and bioavailability. These advancements underscore the role of coacervation not only in drug delivery but also in enhancing the overall efficacy of therapeutic formulations, paving the way for innovative treatments in fields such as gene therapy and mRNA-based vaccines.

At the core of coacervation’s utility in therapeutic applications lie the structural and compositional characteristics of the polymers involved. Parameters such as polymer length, hydrophobicity, and charge distribution significantly dictate the extent and morphology of coacervates. Additionally, chiralitythe geometric property of a molecule that prevents it from being superimposable on its mirror imageintroduces a fascinating layer of complexity to these interactions. The distinction among homochiral, mixed-chiral, and achiral polymers leads to notable differences in their behavior and interaction patterns, which can profoundly impact therapeutic formulations and applications. Chirality influences stability, binding affinity, and overall functionality in biological systems, impacting therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles, thereby underscoring the importance of understanding this property in the context of drug design and delivery.

In addition to the inherent properties of the interacting polymers, external stimuli such as ionic strength, temperature, and pH must also be meticulously considered. Salt, ubiquitous in biological systems, introduces complexity to the dynamic landscape of LLPS. Variations in the ionic strength caused by differing salt concentrations can significantly influence the phase behavior and stability of biomolecules. Changes in salt concentration affect electrostatic interactions through screening and binding effects, leading to potential phase transitions that can alter the behavior of biomolecules. Thus, understanding the multifaceted role of salt in biological systems is crucial, particularly for developing salt-resistant, phase-separated complexes that maintain functionality across varying ionic environments. Moreover, systematically identifying the salt resistance limits before shifts in morphology or phase separation occur for various coacervate systems provides valuable insights that enable the precise manipulation of phase separation through the addition of salt.

Polypeptides and polypeptoids, the latter being polypeptides devoid of chiral centers (commonly referred to as “N-substituted” polypeptides), stand out as promising candidates for investigating complex coacervation due to their remarkable structural diversity and biofunctional relevance. Their inherent versatility allows for precise tuning of critical properties, such as chain length, charge density, and hydrophobicity, which are essential for understanding the principles of phase separation and its influence on biomolecular interactions.

Notably, polypeptoids offer distinct advantages over traditional peptides. Their structure lacks the hydrogen atom bound to the backbone nitrogen found in peptides, which provides enhanced resistance to proteolytic degradation and enables greater control over electronic and steric properties. These attributes make them exceptionally well suited for drug delivery applications, where they can be engineered to enhance the solubility, stability, and bioavailability of therapeutic agents. Additionally, the importance of chirality cannot be overlooked. Historical instances, such as the well-documented case of thalidomidean FDA-approved drug that tragically caused severe birth defects due to the interconversion of its chiral centers upon metabolismhighlight the critical role of chirality in determining the safety and efficacy of peptide-based drugs. This emphasizes the importance of a comprehensive understanding of chirality’s impact in designing stable and reliable therapeutic systems.

Additionally, the biodegradability and biocompatibility of polypeptides and polypeptoids make them particularly appealing for a variety of biomedical applications, including drug formulation and tissue engineering. The ability to modulate their structure to meet specific therapeutic needs enhances their versatility, enabling the creation of delivery systems that are responsive to biological cues. The morphology of complexes formed through phase separation is highly dependent on the chirality of the interacting polymers. For instance, mixtures of homochiral polymers can foster stronger interactions through backbone alignment, leading to solid precipitates, while compositions containing a mix of chiral centers or no chiral centers tend to favor LLPS. Such characteristics not only facilitate the exploration of fundamental principles of coacervation but also provide a platform for translating these insights into innovative therapeutic strategies.

In this research, we delve into the LLPS behavior of systems composed of homochiral, mixed-chiral, and achiral polymers and show that binary mixtures of polypeptides and polypeptoids exhibit chirality-dependent complexation. Additionally, we demonstrate that the concentration of charged species allows for direct control and tuning of the presence and morphology of the complexes. By exploring the chirality, charge fraction, and added salt concentration, we aim to elucidate the underlying principles governing phase separation and polymer behavior. This exploration offers valuable insights that could enhance the design and efficacy of therapeutic formulations. By bridging fundamental understanding with practical applications, this research endeavors to contribute to the advancement of coacervation knowledge and its implications for biomedical innovation. The outcome of this study may pave the way for the development of more robust and customizable drug delivery systems capable of addressing the challenges posed by complex biological environments. By systematically examining the influence of chirality and salt addition on the phase behavior and morphology of polypeptide and polypeptoid complexes, this work aims to provide a foundational understanding of the interplay between molecular characteristics and environmental factors in LLPS.

Such insights are expected to have far-reaching implications, enabling researchers to harness the unique properties of these polymers for a broad spectrum of applications including targeted therapy, gene delivery, and vaccine stabilization. Furthermore, the findings could inform the creation of next-generation biomaterials designed to mimic or interact with biological systems in a controlled and predictable manner. Through this investigation, we seek not only to advance the field of coacervation but also to inspire innovative approaches to solving critical challenges in biomedical science, ultimately contributing to the development of therapies that are safer, more effective, and adaptable to the diverse needs of patients.

Experimental Section

Materials

Poly­(d,l-glutamic acid sodium salt) (PRE­(Na+)) was purchased from Alamanda Polymers. All other polymers were synthesized by Louisiana State University, Chemistry Department: poly­(l-glutamic acid) (PLE), poly­(N-2-carboxyethyl glycine) (PNE) poly­(d,l-glutamic acid) (PRE), poly­(l-lysine bromide) (PLK), poly­(N-4-aminobutyl glycine trifluoroacetate) (PNK), and poly­(d,l-lysine trifluoroacetate) (PRK), as described in the next section. Stock solutions of 10 mM polymer concentration on a monomer basis were prepared gravimetrically in DI water and adjusted to the desired pH ± 0.03 pH units (Mettler Toledo, InLab Expert Pro-ISM) with a few drops of 1 or 0.1 M HCl and 1 or 0.1 M NaOH as needed. 0.5 M 2-(4-(2-Hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazinyl)-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer was prepared at pH 7.0. Sodium chloride (NaCl) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, and stock solutions were prepared at a concentration of 2 M NaCl.

Sample Preparation, Turbidity Measurements, and Optical Microscopy

Coacervate samples for initial stoichiometric studies were prepared by pipetting ultrapure water and buffer into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube (purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by vortexing for 10 s to ensure thorough mixing. Then, the negatively charged polymer was pipetted into the sample and the sample was vortexed. Next, the positively charged polymer was added, and the sample was vortexed one last time immediately after pipetting to ensure fast and complete mixing. A typical experiment varied the ratio of positive to negative monomeric residues over 15 samples at a total monomer concentration of 2 mM for the polymer species while maintaining a constant HEPES buffer concentration of 10 mM. Each sample contained a total volume of 120 μL, 115 μL of which was used for turbidimetric analysis. The charge fractions of positive/total monomeric residue (mol/mol) at which the maximum turbidity measurement was obtained were recorded for each combination. The respective salt resistance study for each system was conducted according to the steps outlined above, where varying amounts of NaCl were added to each of the 15 samples, ranging from 0 mM in sample 1 to 700 mM in sample 15. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Following sample preparation, three 35 μL replicate aliquots of each sample were pipetted into a 384-well plate (Falcon). Turbidity measurements were performed in triplicate using a microplate reader (BioTek Cytation 5) at a wavelength of 562 nm. Turbidity is defined as −ln­(I/I 0), where I 0 = incident light intensity and I = intensity of the light passed through the sample and is measured in absorbance units (a.u.). The measured signal was referenced against a well containing only water and buffer at 10 mM concentration. Samples were then inspected using optical microscopy (Rebel Microscope from Echo) to confirm the presence or absence of coacervation and their respective morphology.

Circular Dichroism Measurements

5 mm path-length cuvettes were purchased from Starna Cells, and CD measurements were made using a Jasco J-815 CD spectrometer. The measurement range was between 190 and 350 nm. Digital integration time (D.I.T., the amount of time the detector collects photons before transferring the signal to the A/D converter for processing) was set to 1 s, with stepwise scanning mode and 1 nm bandwidth. Five accumulations were acquired for each system.

Statistical Analysis

The error bars in all graphs represent the standard deviation determined from the variation in repeated technical and biological experiments and corresponding calculations. A total of three biological and three technical replicates for each system, with three reads each, results in an N = 27.

Results and Discussion

Presence of Complexation and Morphology

This work aims to systematically study the effects of chirality and added salt concentration on the phase separation and morphology of polypeptides and polypeptoids. While other studies have established the effects of these factors within polypeptides, , our study expands the library of polymers by adding polypeptoids to the complexing combinations, as well as monitoring the morphology of these complexes with increasing salt. We obtained turbidity measurements for all combinations of our three polyanions and the three polycations, resulting in a total of nine systems. To identify the optimal charge fraction (positive/total) (mol/mol) for the peak turbidity reading of each system, the relative amounts of polylysine and polyglutamate were varied from 0.1 to 0.9 charge fraction (Figure ). Complexation of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes is dominated by electrostatic interactions. Predictably, the peak turbidity measurement for all systems is close to a 0.50 nominal charge fraction, where theoretically equal amounts of positive and negative charges are available for interaction within the solution.

Turbidity versus nominal charge fraction (+) (mol/mol) measurements reveal the impact of charge composition on turbidity, which correlates with the extent of complexation. (a) PLE + PLK, (b) PNE + PLK, (c) PRE + PLK, (d) PLE + PNK, (e) PNE + PNK, (f) PRE + PNK, (g) PLE + PRK, (h) PNE + PRK, and (i) PRE + PRK.

A few factors could explain the slight variations from an exactly 0.50 charge fraction. First, polylysine and polyglutamate are both weak polyelectrolytes, making them sensitive to slight pH variations, even when all solutions are balanced at pH 7.00 ± 0.03. This means that the local pH may be different than the bulk pH, altering the effective charge of these polymers. − Second, we suspected that the charge contributions from counterions present in each solution are stronger than initially expected, as demonstrated by previous work. We believe that this is due to the deviation in the ionic environment when working with polyglutamate without a counterion distinct from hydrogen. For example, when using sodiated polyglutamate to prepare 10 mM stock solutions, at least 10 mM sodium ions are expected to be present, even after adjusting the pH to 7. With the hydrogen still attached, the initial stock solution would have an acidic pH reading prior to the addition of NaOH for pH balancing. After the pH was balanced to 7, the only sodium ions present will come from the added NaOH. Approximately 90 to 110 μM NaOH was required to achieve a pH 7 in 10 mM stock solutions of different polyglutamate species. This means that not every single glutamate monomer has a sodium counterion in its vicinity compared to the sodiated polymer, affecting its behavior in the solutions, especially in its interaction with oppositely charged polylysine species.

To test the effects of this variation, we compared the complexation of two different PRE stock solutions: PRE­(Na+) from Alamanda Polymers and PRE synthesized in-house with hydrogen still attached to the side chain instead of sodium as the counterion. As mentioned above, to force the PRE into the solution and stabilize at pH 7, hydrogen ions had to be replaced with sodium ions by the addition of NaOH. In contrast, for the preparation of PRE­(Na+), no sodium hydroxide was required for pH adjustment to reach a pH of 7. To understand the effects of this phenomenon, we conducted a comparison study where the same in-house-synthesized PRK was complexed with PRE with no counterion and with PRE­(Na+). The turbidity versus nominal charge fraction curves confirmed that in the presence of a counterion per monomer, the peak in turbidity occurs at 0.50 charge fraction (Figure b). Although there is a slight length difference between the two En species, considering that the samples were all prepared based on monomer charge and that the length difference is approximately only 6%, we hypothesize that the effects are primarily due to the concentration of counterions and the ionic environment. Moving forward, we focused on the polymers synthesized in-house.

Comparing the turbidity changes based on the polycation charge fraction in complexes of PRE and PRK versus PRE­(Na+) and PRK. (a) PRE without a counterion associated with each monomer complexed with PRK peaks in turbidity at 0.40 nominal polycation charge fraction (mol/mol). Previous reports have shown the importance of the chirality composition in polymer solutions on the complexation morphology, demonstrating that mixtures of homochiral polymers lead to precipitation. In contrast, the presence of a mixture of chiral centers, such as racemic polymers, leads to the formation of coacervate droplets. Turbidity measurement is a helpful tool in determining the presence or absence of complexes within a solution; however, to determine the morphology of the complexes formed, further analysis is required. For this purpose, we obtained optical micrographs of our systems to visually identify liquid coacervates or solid precipitates (Figure ). The obtained micrographs show that mixtures of homochiral centers lead to precipitation, whereas the presence of a single racemic (mixed-chiral centers) or achiral polymer is enough to render coacervate droplets instead. This is explained by the fact that two species with spatially organized l-chiral centers exhibit backbone alignment, leading to hydrogen bonding and stronger interactions, which consequently cause the complexes to precipitate out of the solution. On the other hand, the less aligned chiral centers in racemic polypeptides and the absence of chiral centers in polypeptoids enable more fluid and flexible interactions without strict patterns, resulting in coacervate droplets.

The secondary structures of polymers on their own dissolved in aqueous solution at neutral pH, as well as the complexes they form in water, were studied by using CD spectroscopy, which confirmed a change in the secondary structure from random coil for PLE and PLK to β-sheets for the precipitates formed in the mixture of PLE and PLK (Figure ). This shift in morphology is attributed to the ability of homochiral polypeptides to exhibit backbone alignment, resulting in more organized secondary structures. This has been supported in previous work with polyglutamate and polylysine as well. CD spectra were obtained for all polymers and their combinations (Figures S14 and S15).

The Effects of Salt Addition on Phase Separation and Its Morphology

Previous studies have shown that the addition of salt to polyelectrolyte solutions affects the extent and morphology of their complexation. To better understand the influence of the salt concentration on polyelectrolyte complexation, two series of salt resistance studies were conducted. For the first set of experiments, the charge fraction of polymers for each system was kept at the respective charge fraction from the peak turbidity reading of the stoichiometry experiments (Figure ). For the second set of experiments, the salt resistance of all systems was studied, with their charge fractions consistently maintained at 0.50 (Figure ). As expected, the addition of salt can screen the charges, thereby decreasing electrostatic interactions among the polymers and reducing phase separation, which leads to a gradual decrease in turbidity as more salt is added (Figure ). In stoichiometric mixtures of polymers where equal concentrations of positive and negative polymers are interacting but each system is not at its respective peak turbidity, a small amount of salt (50 mM) leads to an initial increase in turbidity for most systems by adding more electrostatic interactions, which is followed by the usual decrease in turbidity as the salt concentration is further increased (Figure ). This is in agreement with what has been seen in previous research as well.

Complexes among polypeptides stay in the precipitate phase, signifying their stability within the solute–solute interactions rather than falling back into the solution, even in the presence of a 1 M NaCl concentration. Figure a shows the shifts in morphology for salt resistance studies at their respective peak turbidity charge ratios, where the general trend, except for the mixture of homochiral species, is to transition from coacervate droplets through transitional morphologies and eventually form precipitates. Figure b shows the morphological shifts in salt resistance studies at a 0.50 charge fraction in stoichiometric solutions and exhibits a pattern similar to that in Figure a, except that a broader range of salt concentrations leads to transitional complexes. This can be explained by the fact that in Figure a, all systems are at their respective peak turbidity charge fractions, which signifies maximum interactions and therefore the most stable complexes. In contrast, Figure b experiments were conducted at a nominal charge fraction of 0.50 regardless of where maximum phase separation was initially recorded, meaning that the complexes are more likely to lose the stable coacervate droplet form. These results support our hypothesis that the ionic strength of the solution and the local charge interactions play pivotal roles in the morphology of complexes and the stability of coacervate droplets.

Conclusions

We conducted a systematic study of complexation in binary mixtures of polylysine and polyglutamate with l- and d,l-chiral centers and their peptoid counterparts without the chiral centers. We qualitatively analyzed our systems using turbidimetry and bright-field microscopy in varying polycation charge fractions and NaCl concentrations. While there are more quantitative and precise methods that can be utilized to study these complexes, turbidity and bright-field microscopy have proven to be reliable methods in characterizing the complexation of polyelectrolytes. Our results showed that chirality is a major determining factor in the morphology of the complexes formed in these binary systems, where mixing homochiral polymers leads to solid precipitates. In contrast, the presence of mixed-chiral centers and species lacking chiral centers aids in the formation of coacervate droplets. This was explained through the interactions that are possible among different chiral centers, where backbone alignment among polymers is possible only in a mixture of purely homochiral centers, specifically l-chiral centers in this study, leading to a stronger association between the polymers and consequently their precipitation. This variation in possible interactions was further demonstrated using CD spectroscopy, where aqueous solutions of l-chiral polyglutamate (PLE) and polylysine (PLK) showed random coil secondary structure on their own. However, in an aqueous mixture with precipitated complexes of PLE and PLK, a β-sheet formation was observed.

Additionally, we demonstrated that the concentration of the added salt directly influences the morphology of the formed complexes. We observed that the precipitates formed in a mixture of PLE and PLK remained stable in the solid phase, even after the addition of 1 M NaCl, whereas other systems underwent morphological transitions at various NaCl concentrations.

Our findings add to previous complexation studies by expanding the polymer library and including polypeptoids. Polypeptoids have become interesting candidates for biomedical applications due to their lack of chiral environments. Although more clinical studies are necessary to fully understand the body’s interactions with polypeptoids, there is a potential benefit that the lack of chiral centers can prevent issues of chirality conversion in the body. Additionally, polypeptoids can exhibit increased stability against enzymatic degradation due to their unique structure, which differs from that of polypeptides.

Our results also provide insights into the step-by-step morphological changes due to varying salt concentrations. Rather than solely highlighting the critical salt concentration at which phase separation is no longer observed, this study reported the gradual, transitional changes in complexation morphology that occur prior to the return to a single-phase state. This work has the potential to enlighten the choice of polymers and chiral centers in peptide- and peptoid-based encapsulation systems for therapeutic formulations. Additionally, the effects of salt and counterion concentration, which are significant environmental factors within biological systems, were confirmed, opening avenues for further exploration of the mechanisms that lead to these observations to better design materials and predict the behavior of peptide- and peptoid-based therapeutic carriers and encapsulation systems when they are exposed to varying ionic strengths.
