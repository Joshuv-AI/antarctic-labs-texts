The Comparative Evaluation of Various Dentin Conditioners with and without Antimicrobial Agent on the Shear Bond Strength of Glass Ionomer Cement to Dentin: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Vinitha Alex, KK Abraham, Ektah Khosla, Arun R James, Elza Thenumkal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundGlass ionomer cement (GIC) dentin conditioners are considered a useful step in the restorative process, as they increase the bond strength of GIC to dentin. Delivering antimicrobial agents through the dentin conditioner may minimize the impact on the physical properties of GIC. It is not known whether such antibacterial conditioning agents may affect the bond strength of GIC to dentin.ObjectiveThe objective of the study was to evaluate the effect of various dentin conditioners with and without antimicrobial agent on the shear bond strength (SBS) of GIC to dentin.Materials and methodsA total of 105 noncarious, unrestored, and sound human maxillary premolars extracted for orthodontic treatment were selected for the study. Tooth specimens were ground to expose a flat dentinal surface. The tooth specimens were embedded in self-cured acrylic resin on metallic molds. Specimens were randomly assigned into seven groups with 15 specimens each, according to the dentin conditioning agent used: Group I with no conditioning, group II with GC dentin conditioner, group III with 50% citric acid, group IV with 36% phosphoric acid, group V with GC conditioner + 1% cetrimide, group VI with 50% citric acid + 1% cetrimide, and group VII with 36% phosphoric acid + 1% cetrimide. After conditioning for 10 seconds, the specimens were thoroughly washed and air-dried. GC Fuji IX was condensed into the Teflon molds. The specimens were then subjected to SBS testing using universal testing machine (UTM).ResultsThe SBS values decreased when 1% cetrimide was added to the dentin conditioners which were, however, statistically insignificant (p > 0.05). The GC dentin conditioner group (group II) showed the highest SBS values and no-conditioning group (group I) showed the least.ConclusionDelivering 1% cetrimide as an antibacterial agent with dentin conditioner showed no significant adverse effect on the SBS of GIC to dentin. Hence, 1% cetrimide can be added to dentin conditioning agent used with GIC in atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) with minimal impact on the physical properties of GIC.Clinical significanceIn Pediatric Dentistry, GIC is most commonly used with ART of cavity preparation. The dental hand instruments used in this technique are less effective when compared to rotary burs in removal of carious dentin, and in such situations, residual caries are most likely to be restored over. If GIC is unable to arrest the caries, the result will be a restoration failure. Incorporating antibacterial agents into glass ionomer powder may cause basic mechanical changes in the physical properties of the GIC. Introduction

In dentistry, a significant attention is given to manage the caries. Nowadays, each and every emphasis is deflected to safeguard tooth structure using tooth-colored restorative materials that cling to tooth by minimal intervention.1 With the introduction of glass ionomer cement (GIC) in early 1970s by Wilson and Kent having the advantages of esthetics, bond to tooth structure, antibacterial activity, and fluoride release made glass ionomer a leading restorative material.2 Restorative dentistry witnessed a revolution from the invasive surgical approach laid by Greene Vardiman Black's extension for prevention to minimum invasive perspective with transformation in adhesion technology.3 The prime expelling forces acting between the tooth and restoration boundary hold shearing sequel and therefore, shear bond strength (SBS) assumes great authority for restorative materials in vivo. High SBS of the restorative material results in enhanced bonding of the restorative material to the teeth.4

The application of dentin conditioners is considered as an advantageous measure on the restorative technique of GIC, as they escalate the bond strength to dentinal surface by generating a pristine surface by eliminating the surface contaminants and smear layer unescorted by unlocking the dentin tubules.5 The dentin conditioner which has been proved to pile up the bond strength and currently in use is 10–20% polyacrylic acid (PAA). The other agents that can be used as dentin conditioner other than PAA include 50% citric acid, 25% tannic acid, 2% ferric chloride, sodium fluoride (NaF), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 37% phosphoric acid, and mineralizing solutions, such as Levine solution.2

In Pediatric Dentistry, GIC is prevalent with atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) of cavity preparation. The dental hand instruments used in this technique are less effective when compared to rotary burs in removal of carious dentin, and in such scenario, residual caries are probably restored over.6 If GIC is unable to arrest the caries, the result will be a restoration failure. Incorporating antibacterial agents to glass ionomer powder could give rise to basic mechanical changes in the physical attributes of the GIC. The application of an antibacterial dentin conditioner is advantageous when filling cavities that have any leftover or residual carious dentin. The diverse means employed to enhance the antibacterial property of GIC include cetrimide, cetylpyridium chloride, benzalkonium chloride, and sodium hypochlorite.7 However, it is not known whether adding such antibacterial agents to dentin conditioners will affect the SBS of GIC to dentinal surface of tooth.

Materials and Methods

Collection and Preparation of Samples

An aggregate of 105 noncarious unrestored extracted human maxillary premolars (Fig. 1) were collected from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Mar Baselios Dental College, Kothamangalam, following the research approval from Institutional Ethical Review Committee. Teeth with any dental anomalies and fracture were expelled from the study. Depth orientation grooves measuring 1.5 mm were constructed on the occlusal surface of the samples by engaging the full depth of the 836R bur (Diatech Dental AG, Heerbrugg, Switzerland). This ensured consistent reduction of dentin of the maxillary premolars. Following this, the dentinal surface was polished with 320, 600, and 1,200 grit silicon carbide abrasive paper for 10 seconds on a polishing machine (Buehler Ecomet V, Beuhler Ltd., Lake Bluff, Illinois, United States) to get rid of the superficial debris.

Specimens used in the study

Mounting and Surface Treatment of Samples

Samples were fixed in auto-polymerizing acrylic resin using a galvanized iron mold of 1” × 1” × 1” dimensions (Fig. 2). Samples were then thoroughly washed under running water to remove the superficial debris. The wet dentinal surfaces were then gently air-dried using a three-way syringe.

Tooth specimen embedded in auto-polymerizing acrylic resin using a galvanized iron mold of 1” × 1” × 1” dimensions

Grouping of Samples

Depending on the conditioning agent employed, the samples were arbitrarily segmented into seven groups. Every group consisted of 15 samples each and conditioned for 10 seconds with an applicator tip except the control group.

Control

Group I: No conditioning.

Conventional Dentin Conditioners

Group II: GC dentin conditioner alone.

Group III: 50% citric acid alone.

Group IV: 36% phosphoric acid alone.

Experimental Dentin Conditioners

Group V: GC dentin conditioner + 1% cetrimide.

Group VI: 50% citric acid + 1% cetrimide.

Group VII: 36% phosphoric acid + 1% cetrimide.

Preparation of 50% Citric Acid and 1% Cetrimide

This was freshly prepared at the Department of Biochemistry, Mar Baselios Dental College, Kothamangalam. A total of 25 gm of citric acid crystals was dissolved in 50 gm of distilled water in a measuring jar to prepare 50% citric acid (Fig. 3); 1% cetrimide was prepared from undiluted cetrimide solution; and 1 mL of 20% cetrimide was diluted to 20 mL with distilled water in a measuring jar to prepare 1% cetrimide (Fig. 4).

Freshly prepared 50% citric acid

Freshly prepared 1% cetrimide

Preparation of Experimental Dentin Conditioners

A total of 1 mL of 1% cetrimide was added to one bottle of GC dentin conditioner and one syringe of 36% phosphoric acid; 5 mL of 50% citric acid was pipetted into a measuring jar and 1 mL of 1% cetrimide was added to it. All of the experimental conditioners were shaken well and vortexed for proper mixing of the cetrimide with the dentin conditioners (Fig. 5).

Vortexing the experimental dentin conditioners

Following conditioning, the surface was thoroughly laundered and dried with a three-way syringe taking care not to desiccate the surface.

Placement of the Restorative Material

The galvanized iron mold with the mounted tooth sample was then arranged perpendicular to unique designed adjustable split Teflon mold (Fig. 6) measuring 3 × 5 mm (internal diameter and height, respectively), which overlaid the conditioned dentinal surface (Fig. 6). The recommended amount of GC Fuji IX (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was mixed as per the manufacturer's instructions. The mixed Fuji IX was packed into the molds and condensed sufficiently unaccompanied by incorporating air bubbles. When the material was set, the adjustable split Teflon mold was removed, and GC Fuji Varnish (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was spread over the entire restoration and blow-dried.

(A) Teflon mold of 3 mm internal diameter and 5 mm height; (B) Teflon mould placed over the conditioned dentinal surface

The samples were hoarded in deionized water at room temperature for 24 hours. The SBS of the glass ionomer restorations to conditioned dentinal surface were then appraised using universal testing machine (UTM) (Tinius Olsen H10KS). For SBS testing, the metallic molds with the prepared specimens adapted in them, were adjusted vertically in the inferior (static) jaw of the UTM (Fig. 7). The placement of the specimens in the mold was such that the restorative material stood parallel to the floor. When the specimen testing started, a metal knife-edge rod moved vertically from the top with a speed rate of 1 mm/minute. Utilizing this knife-edge rod, the shear force was implemented at the dentin–restoration interface until the dislodgement of the GIC from the dentin surface occurred.

Specimen in the mounting platform of UTM

Statistical Analysis

Mean and standard deviations were calculated for every group and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were done for intergroup comparisons. Data were processed using SPSS 17.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Differences were statistically significant when p-value was <0.05. Results

Mean SBS values of GIC with various dentin conditioners are shown in Figure 8. One-way ANOVA displayed no significant difference among the groups (Table 2). GC dentin conditioner group showed maximum SBS (7.96 ± 0.90 MPa), and no-conditioning group showed minimum SBS value (7.11 ± 0.83 MPa) among all the groups. In the conventional group, GC dentin conditioner has shown to have highest effect on SBS of GIC to dentin, and in the experimental group, GC dentin conditioner with 1% cetrimide exhibited the highest SBS (7.74 ± 0.43 MPa). Dentin conditioning yielded an enhanced effect on the SBS of GIC to dentinal surface, when compared to no conditioning. Although the addition of 1% cetrimide reduced the SBS of GIC to dentin, it was ascertained statistically insignificant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Operative dentistry had been using various materials from years to safeguard the missing tooth structure and perpetuate esthetics, function, and form. The glass ionomers, also known as the glass polyalkenoate cements, are legendary materials used in restorative dentistry. The cement consists of calcium alumino-fluorosilicate glass powder incorporated with a water soluble copolymer (acid). Kent named these materials just as GIC, and this cement has progressed into an unavoidable slice of the dental jargon. When mixed together, glass ionomer undergoes a setting reaction which involves the neutralization of the acid groups by the powder. Without decline of physical attributes of the hardened cement, substantial magnitude of fluoride ions is also liberated in the course of setting.8,9

Glass ionomer cements are capable of bonding physicochemically to the facet of the tooth and have a coefficient of thermal expansion, which is almost same to that of tooth. Also, the glass particles in the powder of GIC releases fluoride when the restoration sets, thus contributing to an anticariogenic effect.1,10

Bonding of GIC to tooth is chiefly done by two interrelated phenomena, viz., micromechanical and chemical bonding. Micromechanical bonding becomes apparent by the establishment of short cement tags within the facet of the dentin and an attenuated hybrid layer between hydroxyapatite-coated collagen fibrils at the tooth facet and the surface of the freshly placed GIC.11,12 This advocates that GIC may be pondered to be self-etching, an effect that make an appearance from the existence of the polyalkenoic acid component.13 Chemical adhesion includes genesis of ionic bonds between the carboxylate functional groups on the polyalkenoic acid molecules and calcium ions in the hydroxyapatite surface.14

Procurement of a durable and strong bond between the restorative material and the tooth is the primary objective of adhesion. Adhesion improves longevity of the restoration without surplus sound tooth structure and the demand for bestowing large undercuts and auxiliary retentive aids is eluded. The bonded restoration is capable of transmitting and distributing the functional stresses across the bonded interface. Thus, they can fortify the weakened tooth structure. Also, the strong bond allying the tooth and the restoration prevents marginal gaps that can occur due to polymerization shrinkage.1

Cavity preparation in a tooth produces a 1–2 mm thick layer of debris known as smear layer which results in low surface energy. The smear layer is secured to underlying dentin. Existence of smear layer on dentinal surface suppress successful contact between restoration and tooth hindering a satisfactory bond.15

Eliminating the smear layer generates a pristine and uniform surface for adhesion. Disposal of any smear layer obstructing the dentinal tubules permits freshly placed GIC to infiltrate the facet. The consequence is the degree of micromechanical bond when the cement has been set. Accordingly, the prepared tooth surface should be well cleaned and pretreated before the placement of restorative material to increase the surface energy, thus rendering it more receptive to bonding.16

El-Askary et al. evaluated the effect of various smear layer thicknesses treated with dentin conditioners on adhesion of GIC to dentin. He concluded that the application of dentin conditioner antecedent to the use of the GIC appeared to be a prime step to improve adhesion to dentin.17

Powis et al. recommended PAA in 25% aqueous solution as an effective surface conditioner. Mount recommends a short application time of 10 seconds, as it complexes with metal ions. It is acidic and has a decalcifying action. On cut dentin, its general effect is to remove surface debris and smooth out surface irregularities.18,19 Long et al. studied about the effects of PAA scrubbing of dentin and glass ionomer bond adhesion. The study concluded that the application for 10–20 seconds, followed by rinsing gives better bond strengths.20

Ferrari and Davidson investigated and found that self-curing GIC (Fuji IX) blended with PAA (cavity conditioner) creates an interdiffusion zone of as near as dammit 6 µm thick, both under in vivo and in vitro conditions which was acid resistant and uniform.21 Hoshika et al. determined the adherence and the change in interfacial ultrastructure of a conventional GIC bonded to dentin, with and without pretreatment using a polyalkenoic acid conditioner. After 6 months of aging, the bond strength to nonconditioned dentin was significantly diminished.22

McLean and Wilson first used the term surface conditioning in order to differentiate it from acid etching. Powis et al. agreed that smoother the surface, stronger the bond. Smoothening of the cut dentin and enamel surfaces may be the mechanism whereby surface conditioners improves adhesion.23,24 Wilson and McLean proposed the application of 50% citric acid as a dentin conditioner for the bonding of GIC to tooth. Although not a strong acid, citric acid can be extremely erosive because of its ability to chelate ions.23,25

Careful dissolution of hydroxyapatite crystals through etching is often done with a 34–37% phosphoric acid gel.26 Van Meerbeek, De Munck and Yoshida suggested that at dentin, surface treatment with phosphoric acid reveals a microporous network of collagen which is altogether destituted of hydroxyapatite. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and chemical surface analysis by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDXS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) have validated that circa all calcium phosphates were abolished by the phosphoric acid or at least became under detection limit.27

The initial high viscosity material developed for ART was Fuji IX (GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The triumph of the material includes faster-setting GICs with better physical attributes.28 Fuji IX was developed to be less moisture-sensitive and more resistant to dissolution than conventional GIC because of the fast setting of the resin compound, have become one of the most common restorative materials for restoring deciduous teeth.29,30

Ideal physical properties of GIC are obtained when the powder and liquid are mixed in the ratio as recommended by the individual manufacturer. For the conventional GICs, decreasing the ratio from that recommended by the manufacturer would affect the cement attributes.26 On the contrary, increasing the powder–liquid ratio escalates viscosity and a drier mix, which will curtail the potentiality of the cement to credibly wet the tooth. This may hamper with the adhesion and finally the longevity of the restoration.31 In the present study, GIC was hand-mixed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

In glass ionomers, the cement maturation is comparatively a slow process. The first 24 hours seems to be very critical to nurture water balance within the system. Hence, for the initial GICs finishing and polishing protocols was delayed by at least 24 hours.32 Finishing and polishing of GIC is often advised with rotary instruments, such as flexible discs. Prior to final finishing, it is preferred to lubricate with petroleum jelly or bonding resin.33,34

Earl et al. assessed the upshot of varnishes and other surface treatments on water movement across the glass ionomer cement facet and concluded that coating the freshly placed GIC with a film of either petroleum jelly or varnish disrupts movement of water, and hence ceases the matter of a chalky appearance.35 Hence, in the present study GC Fuji Varnish was used to coat the GIC restoration.

The SBS outshines tensile bond strength that it is more likely to produce failure at the tooth restoration interface and constitute adhesion between the tooth and the restorative material rather than the strength of the restorative material or tooth alone.36–38 Hence, in the current study, the SBS was tested in lieu of the tensile bond strength.

The data obtained from the present study reveals that the SBS of GIC to conditioned dentin was highest for GC dentin conditioner. This accords with the study conducted by Mazaheri et al., who suggested to use 20% PAA which can result in better chemical and micromechanical retention.39 The outcome of the present study is held by the study done by Yap et al. in which he compared the effect of surface treatment and cement maturation on the adhesion of GIC to dentin. The bond strength was higher with the samples conditioned with 20% PAA than 37% phosphoric acid. The control group with no surface treatment showed the least bond strength.40

A research conducted by Hamama et al. also braces the present study. They investigated the utilization of phosphoric acid as a conditioning agent against PAA conditioning for GICs. They concluded that light etch with phosphoric acid does not scornfully affect the bond strength. Even so, it is not better than traditional conditioning with PAA.41

Tuzuner and Ulusu conducted a study to gauge the out-turn of antibacterial means on the surface hardness of a conventional GIC and concluded that though antibacterial agents such as 1% cetrimide reduced the surface hardness, the reduction exhibited tolerable physical properties. Hence, antibacterial agents, such as 1% cetrimide can be wielded with Fuji IX in ART.7 The current study demonstrated that the SBS value of GIC to antibacterial conditioned dentin was decreased when compared to conventional dentin conditioners, which was statistically insignificant. Embodiment of 1% cetrimide was superior to use as antibacterial agent without hampering the physical properties of GIC. This accords with the research conducted by Deepalakshmi et al.42

In accordance with the study conducted by Botelho, 1% concentration antibacterial dentin conditioners can be left in situ unaffecting the adhesion of GIC to dentine.5

The ART technique ought to focus in upshots which include dodging of pain and prerequisite for local anesthetics, preservation of healthy tooth structure, diminished risks for postliminary endodontic treatment, and tooth removal. Direct filling materials used in ART technique need to be adaptable for hand operating, and imperceptive to wetness (moisture) or dryness (desiccation). Potential to withstand chewing force and strong adhesion to tooth also required.43

Conclusion

From the present study the following conclusion was drawn that 1% cetrimide can be used as an antibacterial agent in dentin conditioner along with GIC without having any negative impact on the physical properties of the cement. The results of this study are in accordance with an in-vitro study but further in vivo research and studies are required to predict the performance of the materials tested.

Clinical Significance

In Pediatric Dentistry, GIC is most commonly used with ART of cavity preparation. The dental hand instruments used in this technique are less effective when compared to rotary burs in removal of carious dentin and in such situations residual caries are most likely to be restored over. If GIC is unable to arrest the caries, the result will be a restoration failure. Incorporating antibacterial agents to glass ionomer powder may cause basic mechanical changes in the physical properties of the GIC. The use of an antibacterial dentin conditioner may be useful when restoring cavities that have residual carious dentin.
