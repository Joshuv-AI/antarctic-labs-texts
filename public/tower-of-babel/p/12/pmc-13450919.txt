Assessment of GLUT 1 expression in different grades of oral epithelial dysplasia and squamous cell carcinoma – An immunohistochemical study
Authors: Neethu Kadar, Vishnudas Dinesh Prabhu, Maji Jose, PM Mohamed Haris, Meera K. Pynadath, Sushma Bommanavar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Increased glucose uptake primarily mediated by Glucose Linked Transporter type 1 (GLUT1) is identified as one of the vital predictors of malignancies.Aim and Objective:To compare and correlate GLUT1 expression in normal oral mucosa, different grades of oral epithelial dysplasia (OED) and oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCCs).Materials and Methods:The present retrospective study was conducted during the period of September 2023–August 2024 on a total of 45 (n = 45) histologically graded formalin fixed paraffin embedded sections of OEDs, OSCC and normal oral mucosa that were immunostained with GLUT1. The samples were randomly divided into Study groups: Group 1 (OEDs), Group 2 (OSCC) and Control group (normal oral mucosa). Balanced equivalence sampling method was applied across all the groups with 15 samples each (n = 15). Group 1 and Group 2 were further subdivided into subgroups as subgroup 1.1 (mild dysplasia), subgroup 1.2 (moderate dysplasia), subgroup 1.3 (severe dysplasia), subgroup 2.1 (well differentiated SCC), subgroup 2.1 (moderately differentiated SCC), and subgroup 2.3 (poorly differentiated SCC) with five samples each (n = 5). Samples with membrane staining were classified as having a positive GLUT1 expression. Remmele and Stegner’s immunoreactive score (IRS) was used to rate the staining intensity and percentage of positive cells. A bright field microscope was used to examine the slides, and photomicrographs were obtained to determine the proportion of positive cells. For semi-quantitative analysis, three regions (per case) with the highest GLUT1 expression were chosen at random. The mean value of the percentages was computed after the percentage of GLUT1 expression was measured. SPSS Version 19.0 was used for data analysis. Mean, frequencies, and percentages were used for categorical variables. The statistical analyses were done using Mann–Whitney and Kruskal Wallis Tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.Results:Group 1 had a total mean IRS score of 7.93 ± 2.219 with subgroup 1.1 (5.20 ± 1.095), subgroup 1.2 (9.00 ± 0.000) and subgroup 1.3 (9.60 ± 1.342), respectively, with highly significant P value (P < 0.001). Group 2 had a total mean IRS score of 9.40 ± 2.230 with subgroup 2.1 (7.20 ± 1.643), subgroup 2.2 (9.00 ± 0.000) and subgroup 2.3 (12.00 ± 0.000) with highly significant P value (P < 0.001). However, the control group reported the least total mean IRS score of 2.47 ± 1.407 (P > 0.05). However, there was no significant difference in the Mean IRS between Group 1 (epithelial dysplasia) and Group 2 (OSCC) (P = 0.150). When the mean IRS differences between Group 1 (epithelial dysplasia) and normal control, as well as between Group 2 (OSCC) and normal control, were compared, the results were determined to be highly significant (P = 0.000).Conclusion:Increased expression of GLUT 1 protein in OEDs and OSCC can be a prognostic indicator of malignant changes and treatment outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for more than 90% of the oral and maxillofacial malignancies in the world.[1] Majority of OSCC in India are preceded by precancerous changes; of which lesions with epithelial dysplasia pose a greater risk of malignant transformation.[2] Thus molecular markers detected during dysplastic changes may have a role in disease progression and could be a target for therapeutic strategies.[3] Oral carcinogenesis is a multistep process that often arises from a precancerous phenotype followed by uncontrolled cell proliferation associated with multiple genetic alterations and phenotypic progression to invasive malignancy.[4] Proliferation in carcinogenetic cascade are characterized by accelerated glucose metabolism that helps to maintain energy in hypoxia condition. A malignant cell shows high rate of anaerobic metabolism even in presence of oxygen (aerobic glycolysis) known as Warburg effect. Aerobic glycolysis forms only two Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) per mole of glucose resulting in lactic acid accumulation unlike oxidative phosphorylation that generates 38 ATP per mole of glucose in normal cells. A consistent acidification of microenvironment by lactic acid could be toxic to normal cells; but a phenotype with an adaptive advantage to such changes survives and thereby progress into an invasive cancer. Due to the above factors, metabolic changes during malignant transformation have diagnostic and prognostic significance.[567]

Increased uptake of glucose in cancer cells is mediated by glucose transporters. They are transmembrane proteins that transport glucose across plasma membrane of a cell. They include two families: Sodium glucose linked transporters (SGLTs) and the facilitative glucose linked transporters (GLUTs). SGLTs cause active absorption of glucose within the gastrointestinal tract and reabsorption of glucose from the kidney. Glucose homeostasis within the body is mainly maintained by the various members of the GLUT protein family. Their expression is regulated by src, ras oncogenes, tumour hypoxia related hypoxia inducible factor 1α, hormones, growth factors, and so on. GLUT1 is 1 of the 14 members of the mammalian facilitative glucose transporter family. It is detectable in normal tissues like erythrocytes, perineurium of the peripheral nerves, endothelial cells in the blood–brain barrier vessels, and so on.[8910]

GLUT1 expression has been detected in various malignant tumours like cervical, ovarian, thyroid, oesophageal, pancreatic, breast, gastrointestinal carcinoma and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma.[11] Among 14 isoforms of glucose transporters, GLUT1 isoform expression predominates in OSCC. Nowadays, Positron emission tomography (PET) scans are used to detect the glucose uptake in tumours. Using 18F-Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)—a glucose analogy non-invasive quantitative assessment of glucose metabolism can be performed. Kunkel et al.[12] have linked the expression of GLUT-1 in OSSC with FDG-PET and have correlated with prognosis and overall survival of patients. However, according to Reisser et al.,[13] the precancerous lesions even with a higher GLUT1 expression remained undetectable by PET scan. This was due to the smaller dimension of dysplastic areas in these lesions and limited resolution of PET. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was the most common technique used by many authors to study GLUT1 expression in different lesions.

The interpretation of IHC expression was generally made in a qualitative and subjective manner. Since the requirement for an accurate immunostaining measurement is increasing, semi-quantitative methods are adopted to improve the data precision.[14] In our study interpretation of GLUT1 expression was done using Immunoreactive score (IRS) suggested by Remmele and Stegner.[15] It is a semiquantitative analysis that considers both (A) intensity of colour reaction and (B) percentage of positive cells. Percentage of positive cells were counted using ImageJ software (1.42q, NIH, USA).

Previous studies[51116171819] have shown the prognostic and diagnostic significance of GLUT1 expression in OSCC, but only few are available in the literature, correlating the GLUT1 expression between oral epithelial dysplasia (OED) and squamous cell carcinoma.[1320] Hence, the aim of the present study was to assess GLUT1 expression in different grades of OED and to correlate with that of OSCC. The null hypothesis set was “There is no difference in immunohistochemical expression of GLUT1 in OEDs and OSCC.”

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The present retrospective study was conducted during the period of September 2023–August 2024 at the Department of Oral pathology and Microbiology, Yenepoya dental college and hospital, Mangalore, India. The study was initiated after due approval from the ethical committee bearing the protocol number YUEC 204/15/23. The study was conducted as per the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki (Carlson, Boyd, and Webb, 2004).

Sample size calculation was done based on the study conducted by Ayala FRR et al.[19] (2010) who evaluated the expression of GLUT 1 and GLUT 3 in 142 (n = 142) samples of OSCC cases. This was considered as the parent article for the study. Based on this, the sample size formula applied was:

The predicted total sample size for each group was seven cases per group after these values were entered into the algorithm indicated above. Hence, with 5% level of significance with 80% power, the final sample size of 45 (n = 45) was considered, accounting for about 15% attrition rate.

Eligibility criteria: Histologically diagnosed cases of OEDs, OSCC and normal oral were included. Any faint stained or improper stained slides were excluded from the study.

Sampling method: The total sample (n = 45) were randomly divided into Study groups: Group 1 (OEDs), Group 2 (OSCC), and Control group (normal oral mucosa). Balanced equivalence sampling method was applied across all the groups with 15 samples each (n = 15). Group 1 and Group 2 were further subdivided into subgroups as subgroup 1.1 (mild dysplasia), subgroup 1.2 (moderate dysplasia), subgroup 1.3 (severe dysplasia), subgroup 2.1 (well differentiated SCC), subgroup 2.1 (moderately differentiated SCC) and subgroup 2.3 (poorly differentiated SCC) with five samples each (n = 5) [Annexure].

Immunohistochemical staining: From each selected cases two serial sections of four microns were prepared. One section was stained with Haematoxylin and Eosin and other with immunostaining method. The H and E slides of OED cases were evaluated and graded according to WHO 2005 system as mild, moderate, and severe dysplasia and oral squamous carcinoma were graded based on Broders’ grading system as well, moderate, and poorly differentiated OSCC. Two different observers methodically evaluated all the slides to remove the inter and intra observer bias. IHC for GLUT1 expression were carried out using a monoclonal antibody and Polymer Horseradish Peroxidase Detection System (BioGenex, CA, USA) and the procedure was conducted as per the IHC guidelines. The sections were incubated with monoclonal mouse anti-human GLUT1 antibody (Ready-to-use vial, BioGenex) for 1 hour at room temperature. The reaction was followed by treating the sections with Super SensitiveTM Polymer HRP Detection Kit (BioGenex). The staining was visualized with 3, 3´- diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) chromogen for 5 min. The sections were then counterstained with Mayer’s haematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted with a glass coverslip and xylene based medium. The slides were viewed in a bright field microscope at a magnification of 10X for analysing intensity and pattern of staining.

Interpretation and scoring method: A positive GLUT1 expression was designated for samples showing membrane staining. Erythrocytes present in every section served as an internal positive control. Two different observers methodically evaluated all the slides to remove the inter and intra observer bias. The photomicrographs for assessing percentage of positive cells were taken at 20X magnification using Olympus camera sp-350 attached to microscope Olympus CX-41. Three areas (per case) of maximum GLUT1 expression were randomly selected for quantitative analysis. In epithelial dysplasia and normal mucosa slides, areas were selected from epithelium whereas in squamous cell carcinoma slides areas were selected from tumour islands. The epithelial cells were counted manually using ImageJ software (1.42q, NIH, USA). The percentage of GLUT1 expression was quantified by determining the number of positive cells expressing membrane GLUT1 staining among the total number of epithelial cells in a selected area. The mean value of percentages of three examined areas was considered as the final value.

The staining intensity and percentage of positive cells were graded according to IRS by Remmele and Stegner. The intensity of colour reaction was graded as 0-no colour reaction, 1-mild reaction, 2-moderate reaction, and 3-intense reaction. The percentage of positive cells was graded as 0-no positive cells, 1-<10% of cells positive, 2-10%–50% of positive cells, 3-50%–80% of positive cells, 4->80% of positive cells. The final score represented the product of both values, ranging from 0 to 12 points (0–1-Negative, 2–3-positive weak expression, 4–8-Positive mild expression, 9–12-positive, strong expression).

Inter-examiner and intra-examiner reliability: Two independent examiner assessed the GLUT1 - IHC sample twice to determine the intra-examiner reliability. Another examiner assessed the GLUT 1- IHC samples, and the results of the two examiners were compared to determine the inter-examiner reliability. Both examiners were required to adhere to a uniform assessment technique and received extensive training and practice sessions. The findings demonstrated nearly perfect agreement (ICC: 0.966) between the investigator’s two measurements (intra-examiner reliability). There was nearly perfect agreement (ICC: 0.999) between the measurements made by two distinct investigators, according to the inter-examiner reliability data.

Statistical analysis: The results obtained were statistically analysed using Kruskal Wallis test, Mann-Whitney test, and Fishers’ exact test to validate the comparison between the groups, subgroups, and normal control. A P value less than. 05 was considered statically significant.

DISCUSSION

Many hypotheses have been tested as mechanism involved in over expression of GLUT1 in dysplasia and carcinoma. Most accepted one is that, it could be related to the metabolic changes occurring in these lesions as an adaptation to intermittent hypoxia. During proliferation, the cells are pushed farther from blood supply limiting their substrate availability like oxygen and glucose creating hypoxic areas within the epithelium. At this point, the natural selective force will favour those cell populations that can adapt to hypoxia by initiating aerobic glycolysis and alter the microenvironment by creating acid induced toxicity to the competitors. Such a glycolytic phenotype with an adaptive advantage is sufficient for premalignant lesion to undergo malignant transformation. Over expression of GLUT1 is one of the events initiated for the glycolytic pathway. To surpass death and progress to cancer, multiple genetic alterations are also considered to be crucial. A glycolytic phenotype is needed for progression to malignancy and tumour growth. Persistent glycolysis causes continuous acidification of microenvironment (within epithelium) and failure of substrate delivery would lead to hyperproliferation of blood vessels (angiogenesis) in stroma. Once the BM is breached, the cells enter the connective tissue and the tumour becomes vascular.[21] But the chaotic and disorganised blood circulation creates further tumour hypoxia.[11] The invasive tumour export glucose-derived H1 that leads to destruction of the extracellular matrix and thereby invasion.[22] Up regulation of glycolysis by intratumorally lactate accumulation was observed by Brizel et al.[23] in head and neck cancers with nodal and distant metastasis.

Based on IRS system, in normal epithelium we have observed that GLUT1 expression was negative to mild compared to up-regulated expression in dysplasia and OSCC. GLUT 1 positive cells were less in normal oral mucosa and limited to the basal and first suprabasal compartments similar to other reported studies.[132024] Gherzi et al.[25] observed GLUT1 expression in basal layers can be modulated by keratinocytic differentiation. The reduced GLUT1 expression in superficial keratinocytes represents a shift from glucose-dominated in basal cells to fat-dominated metabolism during the differentiation process.[26] Kuroki et al.[24] found a positive correlation between the distribution of cells containing glycogen to differentiation gradient and inverse relation to GLUT1 expression. Thus we assume that in normal oral mucosa, GLUT1 expression is confined to basal and suprabasal layers that is undifferentiated and have proliferative activity.

In epithelial dysplasia, the pattern of GLUT1 staining was accordance with histological grading like various investigators.[1320] Reisser et al.[13] demonstrated the glycogen content in premalignant lesions was inversely correlated with GLUT 1 expression, showing reduced storage in epithelial dysplasia compared to normal and correlated with the degree of dysplasia, and was associated with increased GLUT1 expression. Authors suggested reduced glycogen storage and GLUT1 over expression in epithelial dysplasia could be related to dedifferentiation of proliferating cells. Thus in our case, GLUT1 over expression in epithelial dysplasia could be suggestive of development of a hypoxia induced glycolytic phenotype modulated by genetic alterations and thereby may have a role in malignant transformation.

The pattern of expression in OSCC subgroups also showed significant variations. In well differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, the GLUT1 expression was confined to the peripheral cells of tumour islands and central cells showed no staining. According to Reisser et al.[13] and Ayala et al.[19] lack of central staining could be due to differentiation. In most cells of poorly differentiated OSCC showed GLUT1 expression including central and peripheral areas of islands as reported by Ayala et al.[19] The authors noted more intense stain in central and perinecrotic areas and related it to be induced by hypoxia. The GLUT1 expression in OSCC correlated well with histological differentiation grading. Thereby we assume that differences in GLUT1 expression in OSCC were mainly due to variation in the differentiation of tumour cells. By IRS system, GLUT1 expression in majority of cases in well differentiated OSCC was found to be mild whereas moderately and poorly differentiated OSCC showed strong expression. As GLUT1 expression was increasing from well differentiated OSCC to less differentiated OSCC, we assume that GLUT1 has a link to cell differentiation and therefore is a significant predictor of tumour aggressiveness and treatment outcome.

Significant differences were found (mean IRS) when dysplasia and OSCC were compared with normal but statistically significant difference was not observed between epithelial dysplasia and OSCC when taken as common group without subdivisions. This is due to reduced IRS values of well differentiated OSCC resulted in bringing down the total score comparable to the level of dysplasia. Despite this, the IRS values remained still higher in OSCC than dysplasia group indicating the prognostic and diagnostic significance of this marker.

Limitations and future scope: Since its as IHC study, spatial localization, and semi-quantitative intensity rather than absolute concentrations of a target protein are observed, resulting in false positive/negative outcomes. Another limitation could be subjective and visual assessment by oral pathologists could result in variability and compromised reproducibility.

CONCLUSION

Overexpression of GLUT1 in both OEDs and OSCC could be a prognostic indicator of malignant changes and treatment outcomes. Moreover, the higher glycolytic metabolism of these proteins at more aggressive tumour cells show possible prognostic utility in stratifying patients for risk.
