Advancing cancer radiotherapy: Harnessing radiosensitizers and nanotechnology for enhanced tumor control
Authors: Fatemeh Shiridokht, Parniya Kehtari, Morteza Eskandani, Alireza Farajollahi, Somayeh Vandghanooni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer affects millions of individuals annually, with radiotherapy playing a crucial role in the treatment of over 70% of cases. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of radiotherapy is frequently limited by tumor radioresistance and the associated toxicity to normal tissues. Radiosensitizers enhance tumor sensitivity to ionizing radiation, optimizing doses and reducing damage. This review evaluates diverse radiosensitizers, including natural compounds (curcumin, resveratrol), chemotherapeutics (cisplatin, doxorubicin), and high-Z nanoparticles (gold, hafnium), which amplify DNA damage and ROS production. Advanced nanocarriers—carbon-, lipid-, and polymer-based—improve targeted delivery, specificity, and bioavailability. Various molecular mechanisms involving proteins such as p53, PARP1, miRNAs (miR-21, miR-155), and siRNAs are discussed in relation to their roles in modulating cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and gene expression to overcome resistance. Radioprotectors like antioxidants and amifostine safeguard normal tissues. Ongoing trials with hafnium oxide and cisplatin regimens show promise. Key findings highlight nanotechnology's role in enhancing radiosensitization via dose enhancement factors and synergistic therapies. Implications include personalized treatments tailored to tumor biology, the reduction of radioresistance through precision medicine and data-driven strategies, and ultimately, the improvement of radiotherapy efficacy and patient outcomes.

Introduction to cancer radiotherapy and radiosensitization challenges

Cancer remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, with approximately 20 million new cases and 10 million deaths annually (Bray et al., 2024).

Projections from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) estimate over 13 million deaths and 22 million cases by 2030 (Gong et al., 2021). Among women, breast cancer is the most prevalent, accounting for 2.3 million new diagnoses (11.7% of total cancer incidence) and 685,000 deaths, making it the primary cause of cancer mortality in women and the fifth overall. Accordingly, breast cancer represents the primary cause of cancer-related mortality among women and the fifth leading cause of cancer deaths globally. It represents 25% of all cancer cases and 16.7% of cancer deaths in women (Sung et al., 2021). Various treatment approaches are employed to combat breast cancer, including surgery, hormone therapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Surgery is the most common local treatment and involves the targeted removal of cancerous tissue. The purpose of hormone therapy is to suppress the growth of cancerous cells by administering hormonal drugs or surgically removing the estrogen-producing ovaries. Chemotherapy, a systemic treatment, utilizes chemotherapeutic agents that interfere with mitosis, cell division, or DNA structure and function to inhibit cancer cell proliferation. However, chemotherapy can adversely affect healthy tissues, leading to side effects such as bone marrow suppression, immune system compromise, mucosal inflammation, skin problems, and hair loss. In addition, drugs such as anthracyclines can cause cardiac, liver, and kidney problems. These side effects highlight the complexity of chemotherapy and its impact on the body, which must be carefully considered in treatment planning (Sobczuk et al., 2022). Radiotherapy, in contrast, is a crucial modality for tumor eradication or control, utilizing high-energy photon beams or particle beams. The induction of molecular damage to DNA, encompassing both single- and double-strand breaks, is the primary mechanism through which this agent exerts its and indirect mechanisms, including the generation of reactive oxygen species causes oxidative stress and consequent cellular damage. Clinical evidence suggests that nearly 70% of cancer patients require radiotherapy, with some relying on it as the sole therapeutic option. Enhancing radiation sensitivity, therefore, remains a critical research focus (Burguin et al., 2021). As a promising strategy, nanotechnology has emerged to boost the therapeutic impact of radiotherapy (Mahmoudi Gharehbaba et al., 2025a; Mahmoudi Gharehbaba et al., 2025b). Ranging from 1 to 100 nanometers in size, nanoparticles exhibit unique properties and can be classified as either organic or inorganic (Vandghanooni et al., 2025; Vandghanooni et al., 2021). Organic nanoparticles, such as polymeric micelles, albumin, chitosan, and dendrimers, are primarily employed in drug delivery applications. In contrast, inorganic nanoparticles, including metals like gold, silver, and platinum, as well as non-metals like carbon and gadolinium, are used in radiotherapy and hyperthermia as radiation and heat sensitizers (Yao et al., 2020). For medical applications, nanoparticles must demonstrate specific characteristics, including non-toxicity (Jaymand et al., 2016; Khalilzadeh et al., 2015), biocompatibility, biodegradability, and stability (Joudeh and Linke, 2022). By boosting the effective dose delivered to cancer cells, radiation sensitizers make radiotherapy more effective. Nanoparticles with high atomic numbers (e.g., bismuth, gold, tungsten, tantalum, hafnium, and silver) are particularly effective due to their significant photon attenuation and ability to boost tumor dose (Chen et al., 2020). The interaction of photons with high-Z nanoparticles depends on the energy of the radiation beam. The photoelectric effect dominates at low energies, while Compton scattering and pair production play major roles at megavoltage (MV) energies. Cellular damage induced by AU results from secondary electrons, free radicals, and ROS generation (Babaye Abdollahi et al., 2021). Quantifying the impact of nanoparticles on radiative effects, the Dose Enhancement Factor (DEF) is defined as the dose with nanoparticles divided by the dose without them. A DEF of 2, for example, means that 5 Gray with nanoparticles is equivalent to 10 Gray without (Her et al., 2017; Schuemann et al., 2016). In addition to nanoparticles that sensitize the effects of radiotherapy, certain treatments can also make cells more vulnerable to radiation therapy. For instance, the study by Shiridokht, F., et al. demonstrated that pre-treatment of the MCF-7 cell line with chitosan nanoparticles containing metformin significantly enhanced the radiosensitivity of breast cancer cells. in the presence of silver nanoparticles (Shiridokht et al., 2025a). The objective of this review is to explore strategies for enhancing the radiosensitivity of breast cancer, with a particular emphasis on the application of nanotechnology. Specifically, this article first outlines the mechanisms of radiotherapy and its associated adverse effects, followed by a detailed discussion of different classes of radiosensitizers, including natural compounds, chemotherapeutic agents, and metallic nanoparticles. In addition, novel delivery systems such as polymeric- and lipid-based nanoparticles, as well as the roles of regulatory molecules such as miRNAs and siRNAs, are examined. Strategies for protecting normal tissues during treatment are also considered. Finally, the review highlights findings from relevant clinical trials, providing an integrated perspective on the current state of research and future directions for improving radiotherapy outcomes in breast cancer.

Radiotherapy

Over the past years, a range of therapeutic strategies such as radiotherapy, surgery, hormone therapy, chemotherapy, stem cell transplantation, immunotherapy, targeted therapy and precision medicine has been developed to optimize cancer treatment and decrease mortality (Gong et al., 2021). Considered an essential and effective approach in oncology, radiotherapy contributes significantly to tumor eradication. Radiotherapy employs high-energy gamma rays, X-rays, or other ionizing radiation to destroy cancer cells via direct (DNA breaks) and indirect (ROS-induced oxidative stress) mechanisms. In a direct reaction, radiation directly causes single and double breaks in DNA, thus ending the division and proliferation of cells and even causing cell necrosis and apoptosis. Through indirect effects, radiation produces reactive oxygen species (ROS) that lead to cellular stress, biomolecular damage, and subsequent modulation of signaling pathways (Fig. 1). Despite advancements in radiotherapy that enhance patient therapeutic efficacy, complications impede oncological treatment. The challenges include tumor heterogeneity, cancer stem cells (CSCs), low cellular radiosensitivity, tumor recurrence, and collateral damage to healthy tissues induced by ionizing radiation (IR) (Gong et al., 2021). Clinical studies show that more than half of patients (about 70%) need radiation therapy, and in specific instances, radiation therapy represents the only available treatment. Thus, developing approaches to increase radiosensitivity is of critical importance (Gong et al., 2021). There are three methods to improve radiation sensitivity. One of them is increasing the radiation resistance of healthy tissues around the cancerous tissue through phytochemical compounds, antioxidants, and amifostine. Another approach is the reversal of pathways that lead to radiation resistance of tumor tissue. Research indicates that pathways contributing to cancer radioresistance include autophagic processes, the Akt/cyclin D1/CDK4 survival pathway, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and NF-κB (Liu et al., 2021). Also, the activation of cellular checkpoint pathways affects the radiation resistance of cancer cells; cells are often stopped in the G1/S or G2/M phase, which provides the necessary time for DNA repair. Cell cycle arrest after being exposed to radiation, mainly in phase G2/M occurs. S-phase cells have more radiation resistance than G2 and G1 cells, and on the other hand, M cells have more radiation sensitivity (Lonati et al., 2021). The induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in cancer cells drives enhanced malignancy and confers increased resistance to radiotherapy (Yang et al., 2020).Fig. 1A schematic illustrating the mechanism of ionizing radiation (IR) in radiotherapy. The presence of cancer stem cells (CSCs) within tumors, which underlie metastasis and recurrence, is a principal determinant of radioresistance across multiple cancer types (Olivares-Urbano et al., 2020). Increasing radiosensitivity of tumor tissue is an alternative method to improve radiation therapy. the main reason for the failure of radiotherapy (alone or combined with chemotherapy) is the radiation resistance of cancer cells. One of the main factors to increase the efficiency of radiotherapy is the use of radiation sensitizers.

Molecular and cellular mechanisms of radioresistance and radiosensitization

The capacity of cancer cells to resist ionizing radiation (IR)-induced DNA damage, termed radioresistance, is often driven by abnormal expression of cellular response to DNA damage proteins, chromatin remodeling factors, and dysfunctional cellular behavior within the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, tumor metabolic states and dietary factors can significantly affect radiotherapy (RT) efficacy (Klement and Champ, 2014). Radioresistance can be categorized as intrinsic or acquired. Intrinsic radioresistance is primarily related to the cancer cells' molecular characteristics. Mechanisms involve DDR pathway disruption, chronic activation of proliferative/anti-apoptotic signaling, and IR-induced ROS levels (Dayal et al., 2014; Panieri et al., 2013; Shu et al., 1998). In contrast, acquired radioresistance often arises from changes within the tumor microenvironment, including an increase in radioresistance that comprises elevated CSC populations, EMT induction, and transcription factor activation that preserves CSC properties (Galeaz et al., 2021; Sato et al., 2019). Acquired radioresistance that develops after repeated IR treatments is associated with enhanced ability to repair DNA damage (Kuwahara et al., 2009; Kuwahara et al., 2017). Heterogeneity of human cancers complicates the contrast between intrinsic and adaptive radioresistance and both forms can simultaneously exist within a tumor. The extent of tumor cell responses to DNA damage from ionizing radiation and their inherent DNA repair capabilities is crucial for determining treatment outcomes. RT primarily induces cancer cell death through double-strand breaks (DSBs), which are the most lethal type of damage caused by IR. Because cancer cells proliferate rapidly, they are particularly sensitive to radiation-induced DNA damage, which significantly impacts the clinical management of many tumors. Nonetheless, aberrant expression of proteins involved in DNA damage, repair, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis contributes to the malignant behavior of cancer cells and is often linked to a radioresistant phenotype (Buckley et al., 2020). DSBs are the lesions most responsible for cancer cell death, but they can be repaired efficiently via homologous recombination (HR) or non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). Deficiencies in key proteins involved in both normal and cancer cells, radiosensitivity is modulated by NHEJ and HR mechanisms, with NHEJ playing a central role in repairing DSBs (Bee et al., 2013; Piotto et al., 2018; Szymonowicz et al., 2020). Notably, these two repair mechanisms respond differently to proton and photon irradiation in cancer cells, with proton-induced DNA damage showing a preference for HR due to its more complex and clustered nature, which can hinder the function of the Ku protein in the NHEJ pathway (Ray et al., 2018; Watanabe et al., 2011). When both repair pathways are functional, especially during the late S and G2 phases, NHEJ typically operates; however, if the DNA damage is too complex, repair shifts to homologous recombination (HR) (Szymonowicz et al., 2020).

Increased expression of RAD51 and DNA-PKcs is linked to radioresistance in various cancers (Hu et al., 2019; Maacke et al., 2000; Piotto et al., 2018; Qiao et al., 2005; Song et al., 2021). In lung cancer patients, particularly those with NSCLC, heightened XRCC1 expression which are crucial for BER and SSB repair, is associated with increased radioresistance (Vaezi et al., 2011). Other proteins in the HR and NHEJ pathways, along with key BER factors, are also dysregulated in radioresistant tumors, as comprehensively reviewed by Carlos-Reyes et al. (Carlos-Reyes et al., 2021). Enhanced DSB repair kinetics have been observed in radioresistant head and neck carcinoma cells using γ-H2AX immunofluorescence (Todorovic et al., 2019). Elevated RAD18 levels have recently been implicated in increased NHEJ activity, which underlies the radioresistance observed in esophageal carcinoma cells (Li et al., 2022).

Modifications in cell cycle progression are prevalent in tumor cells and may impact their radiosensitivity, with p21Cip1/Waf1 overexpression contributing to radiation resistance (Kokunai and Tamaki, 1999; Nguyen et al., 2021). P21 expression is regulated by p53, a key transcription factor in the DDR pathway, which collaborates with other p53-family proteins in the cellular response to IR (Kuchur et al., 2021). Disruptions in the p53 pathway are implicated in radioresistance in various cancers, including pediatric tumors (Gaspar et al., 2006; Yogev et al., 2016; Zhukova et al., 2013), endometrial cancer (Akiyama et al., 2019), head and neck cancer (Skinner et al., 2012), rhabdomyosarcoma (Casey et al., 2021), and oral squamous cell carcinoma (Yamazaki et al., 2003). The NF-κB family of transcription factors contributes significantly to tumor radioresistance by modulating genes implicated in survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and inflammation (Singh et al., 2015).

Radiation primarily induces cell death through apoptosis; however, dysfunctions in apoptotic signaling, characterized by elevated anti-apoptotic and diminished pro-apoptotic protein levels, allow radioresistant cells to survive. XIAP, an anti-apoptotic protein, is often found to be overexpressed in such resistant cell lines (Moussata et al., 2012). Alterations in protein expression often stem from genetic and epigenetic alterations, particularly those affecting the DDR pathway, contributing to the radioresistant phenotype.

Protein-mediated radiosensitization strategies

Changes in histone PTM patterns caused by radiation can modulate chromatin architecture, thereby impacting the proper activation of the DNA damage response (Hunt et al., 2013). Temporary epigenetic changes, such as reduction in histone phosphorylation and acetylation, have been observed following radiation-induced DNA damage (Sharma et al., 2015; Tjeertes et al., 2009). Epigenetic alterations, either inherent or arising during radiotherapy, can influence gene expression and chromatin architecture in the short and long term, thereby facilitating radioresistance. For example, breast cancer cells exhibit increased resistance to radiation when HAT activity is diminished and HDAC activity is elevated (Sharda et al., 2020). Radiation exposure in prostate cancer cells leads to methylation of histones at the ALDH1A1 gene promoter (Peitzsch et al., 2016). This gene is part of the ALDH family, which is often overexpressed in cancer stem cells across various carcinomas, including those of the prostate, skin, liver, brain, head and neck, lungs, and colon (Tomita et al., 2016). According to the study carried out by Kim et al., the presence of H3K27me3 correlates with the cellular radiation response (Kim et al., 2021) Studies have shown that high H3K27me3 levels correlate with increased radioresistance in head and neck cancer cell lines (Gabriel et al., 2022) Loss of H3K27me3 in medulloblastoma is associated with radioresistance due to an epigenetic shift from trimethylation to acetylation at H3K27, activating the AKT pathway. Chromatin structure closely regulates DDR protein function, thereby influencing cellular responses to radiation-induced DNA damage (Mognato et al., 2021). Cells in three-dimensional culture conditions display greater chromatin compaction relative to two-dimensional cultures, with heterochromatin accumulation offering resistance to DNA damage caused by radiation (Storch et al., 2010).

The role of non-coding RNAs (miRNAs and siRNAs) in enhancing radiotherapy efficacy

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), which function in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, regulate gene expression and are classified as either long (>200 nucleotides) or short (<200 nucleotides) (Mognato et al., 2021; Rinn and Chang, 2012; Statello et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2018; Yamamoto and Saitoh, 2019). Recent findings indicate an association of non-coding RNAs with radiosensitivity in NSCLC cells (Song et al., 2021). Extensive research on tumor radiosensitivity has highlighted alterations in the expression of microRNAs (miRNAs), small non-coding RNAs that modulate a wide array of genes across multiple signaling pathways (Mognato and Celotti, 2015). Through comprehensive analyses utilizing microarray technology and RNA sequencing, different miRNA species that are expressed variably in radio-resistant tumors of the lung, breast, nasopharynx, prostate, rectum, head and neck, and cervical cancers have been identified (Aranza-Martínez et al., 2021; de Mey et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2021; McDermott et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2011). Collectively, these observations provide insight into the genetic and epigenetic mechanisms governing radiosensitivity. Additionally, integrated transcriptomic studies have shown potential in classifying subgroups in advanced lung adenocarcinoma and developing more effective treatment strategies based on laboratory, animal, and clinical data (Daemen et al., 2021; Niemira et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the genetic and epigenetic contexts in radio-resistant tumors are complex because genetic and epigenetic signatures not only drive tumorigenesis but are also modified by radiotherapy, resulting in persistent changes that impact radiosensitivity. Nevertheless, the contribution of non-coding RNAs to radiosensitivity regulation and the metabolic profiles of radioresistant cancer cells has been scarcely investigated (Matschke et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022). Although differential expression of genes and ncRNAs has been observed in various tumors following irradiation, the precise associations between specific genes/ncRNAs and radiosensitivity remain poorly defined. This challenge is as a result of the wide variety of biological pathways that serve both as a basis for radiosensitivity and as responsive mechanisms to radiation. Further comprehensive research and clinical approaches are necessary for advancing our understanding of tumor radiosensitivity.

Studies have shown that miR-21 and miR-155 play a role in the progression of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by downregulating genes with tumor-suppressive roles, including SOCS1, SOCS6, and PTEN. This downregulation can lead to increased resistance to radiotherapy. For example, in one study, increased expression of miR-155 and miR-21 was associated with reduced expression of SOCS1, SOCS6, and PTEN in clinical NSCLC samples (Xue et al., 2016). Defined miRNAs such as miR-34a, miR-21, and miR-155 have been identified as key regulators of cancer cell survival and response to radiation. It has been shown that miR-21 can inhibit the expression of tumor suppressor genes such as PTEN, leading to increased survival and resistance to treatment in various cancers, including lung cancer (Qian et al., 2024). miR-155, similarly, contributes to breast cancer progression and resistance by downregulating key apoptotic regulators such as TP53, and promoting cell proliferation through activation of signaling pathways like JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT (Martin et al., 2014).

Recent studies have shown that inhibiting these miRNAs can enhance radiosensitivity, making them promising candidates for therapeutic interventions. For instance, the use of antagomirs, which are oligonucleotide-based inhibitors of specific miRNAs, has been demonstrated to increase the sensitivity of tumors to radiation (Moskwa et al., 2014). In particular, miR-21 inhibition in NSCLC cells has been linked to enhanced DNA damage and apoptosis after radiation exposure, highlighting the potential for miRNA-targeted therapies in overcoming radioresistance (Jiang et al., 2017).

Furthermore, siRNAs (small interfering RNAs), which are designed to target and degrade specific mRNA molecules, have also been explored as a means to improve radiosensitivity. siRNAs targeting genes involved in DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis have been demonstrated to sensitize cancer cells to radiation by interfering with these protective mechanisms (Tian et al., 2020). Unlike miRNAs, which typically regulate multiple genes through binding to complementary sequences in the 3' untranslated regions of mRNA, siRNAs offer a more direct approach by specifically targeting mRNA for degradation, making them a powerful tool in cancer therapy (Guo et al., 2013). However, challenges such as delivery mechanisms and off-target effects still need to be addressed for the clinical application of siRNAs in radiotherapy.

Overview of radiosensitizers in cancer radiotherapy

Radiosensitizers are chemical agents developed to enhance tumor sensitivity to ionizing radiation, achieving greater tumor inactivation than single-factor interventions. The proposed mechanisms of radiosensitizers have evolved with advances in research and technological innovations, facilitating the introduction of novel materials and drugs to potentiate radiotherapy effects (Adams, 1973; Fowler et al., 1976; Gong et al., 2021). The established mechanisms of these agents are thought to involve inhibition of DNA damage repair following irradiation, perturbation of organelle function, and cell cycle to potentiate regulation of gene expression and cytotoxicity that suppresses radioresistance while promoting radiosensitivity (Gong et al., 2021). Radiation sensitizers are classified into three categories, including: Natural-based agents, chemotherapeutic drugs as radiosensitizers, and metal-based nanoparticles. High-Z metal nanoparticles as radiosensitizers

It is noteworthy that materials with high atomic numbers (Z), particularly metals, typically demonstrate chemical inertness. This characteristic could reduce potential health risks in cellular and subcellular systems, which is crucial for clinical applications (Pottier et al., 2015). During surgery for localized solid tumors, the local intratumoral or superselective intra-arterial administration of nano-agents can limit their distribution and thereby reduce side effects. Notably, in 1980, Matsudaira et al. first demonstrated that cultured cells could be sensitized to X-rays using iodine contrast medium (Matsudaira et al., 1980). In recent decades, a significant portion of research has focused on metal-based nanoparticles referred to as “NanoEnhancers” for their synergistic and radiosensitizing properties in radiotherapy. (Brun and Sicard-Roselli, 2016). Irradiated metal-based nanoparticles generate photoelectrons and Auger electrons that enhance radiation dose and radiobiological outcomes (Hossain and Su, 2012; Kobayashi et al., 2010). In a study conducted by Alessia Tudda and colleagues in 2022, gold nanoparticles were utilized to investigate the effects of increasing doses on MDA-MB-231 cells, in the presence of polyenergetic kilovolt and megavolt X-rays. In the presence of nanoparticles and a radiation dose of 2 Gy, the analysis of cell survival curves demonstrated a significant increase in mortality rates. The maximum effect was observed at 190 kV energy and a nanoparticle concentration of 9.74 nM. Also, it was observed that increasing the concentration of gold nanoparticles has a direct effect on the death rate (Tudda et al., 2022). Wei Pan et al. used mesoporous titanium dioxide nanoparticles (MTiO2NPs) as a nuclear-targeted radiosensitizer for drug delivery of 7-ethyl-10-hydroxy-camptothecin (SN-38), causing cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase in treated 4T1_Luc cells. As a result, this led to an increased production of ROS during radiation therapy, boosting the efficacy of the treatment and promoting the death of cancer cells. While high-Z metal nanoparticles demonstrate promising radiosensitizing effects, their biosafety and long-term toxicity remain major concerns. Different Studies have reported that these nanoparticles may accumulate in organs such as the liver, spleen, and kidneys, where insufficient clearance can trigger chronic inflammation or organ dysfunction. Although acute toxicity is often low, subclinical effects, including oxidative DNA damage, altered gene expression, and redox imbalance, have been documented. To mitigate these risks, strategies such as surface modification (e.g., PEGylation), size optimization for renal clearance, and the development of biodegradable or metabolizable formulations are being explored. Nonetheless, standardized long-term in vivo studies are essential to ensure safe clinical translation (Jackson et al., 2024).

Chemotherapeutic drugs as radiosensitizers

Chemotherapy is a cytotoxic treatment that disrupts essential cellular processes, including proliferation, angiogenesis, metastasis, and apoptosis, affecting both malignant and healthy cells. Chemotherapy reduces tumor size, alleviates cancer-related symptoms, prolongs patient survival, and enhances the likelihood of cure when combined with local therapies such as surgery or radiotherapy. Moreover, it can potentiate the effects of immune checkpoint inhibitors by modulating the immune response (Kris et al., 2014).

In this context, Shuyue Zhang et al. investigated the combined delivery of the radiosensitizer Dbait and the chemotherapeutic agent Dox using ch-k5(s-s) R8-An micelle in conjunction with radiotherapy in U251 glioblastoma cells, both in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, this approach increased apoptosis and DNA damage while reducing cell viability and proliferation to 64% and 16.3%, respectively. Likewise, in a study conducted by Noor Nabilah Talik Sisin et al., the effect of combining two natural radiosensitizers, Baicalein-Rich-Fraction (BRF) with metal radiosensitizer bismuth oxide nanoparticles (BRF-BiONPs (BB)), and also the combination of 3 radiosensitizers, BRF, BiONPs, and chemotherapeutic drug cisplatin ((BCB) BRF-Cis-BiONPs) with radiotherapy was investigatedin two cell lines (MCF7 and MDA-MB-231) in breast cancer. The result was that BRF has little radiosensitization compared to BiONPs and Cis due to its antioxidant properties. Also, BCB compounds have twice as much radiosensitization compared to BB, Cis, BRF, and BiONPs (Sisin et al., 2022a).

In conclusion, these studies highlighted the potential benefits of combining radiosensitizers with chemotherapeutic agents to increase the therapeutic impact in cancer treatment. The synergistic effect of these combinations can lead to improved apoptosis, increased DNA damage, and reduced cell viability in cancer cells. Utilizing radiosensitizers and chemotherapeutic drugs together provides a promising approach to enhancing treatment outcomes for various malignancies.

Natural compound-based radiosensitizing agents

Natural active substances found in foods and plants have garnered significant attention for their diverse pharmacological properties (Bahadori et al., 2020). These substances are particularly noted owing to their tumor-suppressive effects (Yi et al., 2019) and protection against radiation (Sasaki et al., 2021) capabilities, making them ideal radiosensitizers to enhance the effectiveness of cancer radiotherapy. These natural radiosensitizers have been demonstrated to produce dual effects in cancer patients in combination with radiotherapy (Szejk-Arendt et al., 2020; Zvyagintseva et al., 2020). Natural radiosensitizers can increase cancer cell radiosensitivity, and their combination with radiotherapy represents a promising therapeutic approach. However, few are commercially available, and research into natural radiosensitizers and radioprotectors is still in its infancy, requiring further preclinical and clinical studies. Various polyphenols, such as EpiGallocatechin gallate (EGCG), Curcumin (CUR), Resveratrol (RSV), Quercetin, Kaempferol, Genistein, Elagic acid, and Caffeic acid phenethyl ester (CAPE) have been investigated for their capacity to exert anticancer effects via regulation of multiple signaling pathways (Sun et al., 2019; Yi et al., 2019) or can act as a cancer sensitizer when used in combination with radiotherapy. Currently, around 60% of cancer treatment strategies involve the use of phytomedicines (plant-based medicines) (Monneret, 2010). Data from the National Cancer Institute, which has screened over 35,000 plant species for anticancer potential, indicate that more than 3,000 species exhibit significant chemopreventive or anticancer activity (Alfonzetti et al., 2021; Gahtori et al., 2023). In cancer therapy, biologically active compounds derived from medicinal plants play a pivotal role, as their synergistic effects have been demonstrated in both traditional and conventional treatment modalities (Mesas et al., 2023). Antitumor properties have been demonstrated by several phytomedicines across various cancer types, including lung cancer (Alfonzetti et al., 2023). For example, Ephedra has been extensively utilized for lung cancer management in Palestine (Danciu et al., 2018). Rhus verniciflua has also demonstrated therapeutic potential in the treatment of lung cancer (Cheon et al., 2011). Rheum officinale, moreover, exhibits potential as a phytomedicine capable of inhibiting lung cancer progression (Yu et al., 2008). Moreover, combinations of natural products with chemotherapeutic agents have been employed in clinical trials targeting lung cancer, particularly non-small-cell lung cancer (Batbold and Liu, 2021; Wu et al., 2023). According to Mai H. Mekkawy, Bromelain (BL) exhibits dual functionality, enhancing tumor cell radiosensitivity while protecting normal tissues from radiation. Bromelain increased the sensitivity of Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC) cells to γ-radiation in vitro and significantly reduced tumor size and weight in vivo when administered in combination with radiotherapy (Fig. 4). It also improved tumor histopathology and attenuated radiation-induced liver damage by modulating the expression of PPAR-α, PARP1, and NF-κB while restoring antioxidant activity and liver function markers. These findings suggest that bromelain may represent a promising adjuvant in cancer radiotherapy, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing adverse effects on healthy tissues(Mekkawy et al., 2020). Nanocarrier systems for targeted radiosensitizer delivery

Nanoparticle-based radiosensitizers (NBRs, 1–100 nm) (Farokhzad and Langer, 2009) are designed to increase tumor cell susceptibility to ionizing radiation, enhancing the therapeutic ratio of radiotherapy (Wang et al., 2018). NBRs can function as therapeutic agents themselves (e.g., drug-loaded polymeric or platinum-based nanoparticles) or as inert high-Z enhancers such as gold nanoparticles (GNPs), which amplify radiation effects by generating secondary electrons and promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation (Boateng, 2017; Kwatra et al., 2013; Ngwa et al., 2017; Ngwa et al., 2014; Ngwa et al., 2010, Ngwa et al., 2012; Paro et al., 2017). Surface-engineered nanoparticles allow targeted delivery and controlled drug release. PEGylation and conjugation with ligands (antibodies, peptides, aptamers) facilitate active targeting, while passive targeting exploits the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of tumor vasculature (Rosenblum et al., 2018; Sinha et al., 2006; Tarighi et al., 2025; Torchilin, 2010).

Both strategies aim to maximize intratumoral accumulation and minimize systemic toxicity, though active targeting offers greater specificity. The physicochemical properties of NPs, including size, shape, surface modification, and hydrophobicity, critically influence biodistribution, tumor penetration, and drug release kinetics (Cho et al., 2007; Ernsting et al., 2013; Gessner et al., 2000; Holback and Yeo, 2011; Sun et al., 2017). Smaller nanoparticles exhibit enhanced penetration into tumors; however, they may release therapeutic agents prematurely. In contrast, larger NPs are capable of carrying a greater payload per particle but tend to release their contents at a slower rate. Optimizing NP properties according to tumor type and microenvironment is essential for maximizing radiosensitization(Ernsting et al., 2013). Preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential of multifunctional NBRs for enhancing radiotherapy outcomes. Polymeric-based nanoparticles for the delivery of radiosensitizers

Polymeric nanoparticles (NPs), ranging in size from 1 to 1000 nm, offer a versatile platform for delivering radiosensitizers due to their ability to encapsulate active compounds either within their polymeric core or on their surface (Dadashi et al., 2025; Dadashi et al., 2024; Karimian-Shaddel et al., 2024; Nazemiyeh et al., 2025; Rouhi et al., 2024; Shiridokht et al., 2025b; Zamanlu et al., 2019). These nanoparticles encompass both nanocapsules and nanospheres, which differ in structural morphology, providing flexibility in drug delivery design. Polymeric NPs have demonstrated significant potential in targeted drug delivery for cancer treatment, particularly in enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy by facilitating localized radiosensitization (Guterres et al., 2007). Polymeric nanoparticles are gaining prominence due to their ease of preparation, high drug-loading capacities, biocompatibility, and biodegradability (Abedi-Gaballu et al., 2018; Biswas et al., 2016; Cagel et al., 2017; Kesharwani et al., 2019; Prabhu et al., 2015; Sherje et al., 2018; Thakor and Gambhir, 2013; Thakur et al., 2017). Common synthetic polymers employed in these systems include polylactic acid (PLA), poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG), poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA), and poly(ε-caprolactone), all of which are known for their stability and ability to provide controlled drug release (Abedi-Gaballu et al., 2018; Biswas et al., 2016; Cagel et al., 2017; Kesharwani et al., 2019; Prabhu et al., 2015; Sherje et al., 2018; Thakor and Gambhir, 2013; Thakur et al., 2017). Additionally, natural polymers such as chitosan and dextran ester exhibit excellent biocompatibility; however, their shorter half-lives and interactions with drug molecules often limit their applications (Abedi-Gaballu et al., 2018; Biswas et al., 2016; Cagel et al., 2017; Kesharwani et al., 2019; Prabhu et al., 2015; Sherje et al., 2018; Thakor and Gambhir, 2013; Thakur et al., 2017). Synthetic biodegradable polymers, such as poly(2-hydroxyethyl-L-aspartamide) and poly(N-isopropyl acrylamide) (PNIPAM), have been developed to overcome these challenges, providing enhanced stability and tailored release profiles (Cuong et al., 2011; Hsieh et al., 2005). Recent advances have further highlighted the benefits of combining natural and synthetic polymers to achieve optimal therapeutic efficacy. For example, the integration of chitosan with PLGA has resulted in hybrid nanoparticles that leverage the biocompatibility of natural polymers and the controlled release properties of synthetic ones, making them highly suitable for radiosensitizer delivery.

For instance, Kostevsek et al. (2016) designed a one-step synthesis of gold-iron oxide nanoparticles coated with chitosan, a biocompatible polymer shell, which integrated photothermal heating capabilities and photoacoustic imaging (PAI) as a contrast agent. This approach allows for the manipulation of the final nanostructures in aqueous environments and the creation of targetable nanoparticles. These features, combined with their demonstrated imaging and therapeutic capabilities, pave the way for future theranostic applications (Kostevsek et al., 2016). Polymeric nanoparticles have shown promise in delivering radiosensitizers to tumor sites, thereby improving the therapeutic index of radiotherapy. For instance, Ling et al. (Ling et al., 2011) designed PLGA-based nanoparticles encapsulating superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) and docetaxel. These nanoparticles demonstrated a triphasic drug release pattern over 30 days and enhanced cellular uptake in prostate cancer PC3 cells, highlighting their potential as multifunctional vesicles for imaging and radiosensitizer delivery. Similarly, Cheng et al. (Cheng et al., 2018) developed polymeric nanoparticles incorporating Gd₂O₃ and aluminum phthalocyanine (AlPc) for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and photothermal therapy (PTT), which could complement radiotherapy by facilitating targeted radiosensitization. PTT, which utilizes nanoparticles/drugs to convert light energy into heat for tumor ablation, complements radiotherapy by enhancing tumor cell sensitivity through localized hyperthermia, synergizing with radiosensitizers to amplify DNA damage and ROS production (Chu et al., 2022a; Chu et al., 2022b). In another study, Rowe et al. (Rowe et al., 2009) created multifunctional nanoparticles using a gadolinium-based metal-organic framework (MOF) that served as both a diagnostic and therapeutic agent. The inclusion of gadolinium, a known radiosensitizer, enabled these nanoparticles to enhance the radiation response while providing imaging capabilities. Emerging research also points to the use of polymeric nanoparticles in combination therapies. For instance, the integration of radiosensitizers with photothermal agents allows for synergistic effects, potentially increasing tumor sensitivity to radiation while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues. According to the study conducted by Mignot et al. (Kotb et al., 2016), gadolinium-based nanoparticles demonstrated a strong radiosensitizing effect in a preclinical model of multiple brain melanoma metastases. As illustrated in Fig. 7, these nanoparticles accumulated in metastatic lesions and significantly enhanced the effect of radiation therapy, leading to better tumor control. Depending on tumor location and the intended therapeutic effect, polymeric nanoparticles can be delivered either systemically or locally. Local delivery systems, such as biodegradable implants made from polymers like PLGA or poly(ε-caprolactone), allow for sustained release of radiosensitizers directly at the tumor site, reducing systemic toxicity. For example, nanoparticle-loaded spacers have been developed to replace inert spacers used in radiotherapy, enabling controlled radiosensitizer release within the tumor (Boateng, 2017; Boateng et al., 2017; Boateng and Ngwa, 2017, Boateng and Ngwa, 2019; Cormack et al., 2010; Kumar et al., 2002; Sinha et al., 2015). Systemic delivery methods, while less localized, benefit from modifications like PEGylation, which enhances nanoparticle stability and circulation time, ensuring effective targeting of tumor cells (Kumar et al., 2013). Li et al. (Li et al., 2014) developed gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) functionalized with PEGylated polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimers and loaded with doxorubicin to enhance therapeutic effectiveness both in vitro and in vivo. These nanoparticles exhibited uniform sizes and strong colloidal stability. The release of the drug was pH-sensitive, allowing for controlled drug delivery based on pH conditions. The study also demonstrated the use of PEG-DOX-PAMAM-AuNR for combined photothermal and chemotherapy treatment, showing effective synergistic hyperthermia ablation of cancer cells in both experimental setups.

Despite their advantages, polymeric nanoparticles face challenges, including scalability of production, variability in drug encapsulation efficiency, and potential interactions with biological systems. The variability in biological responses, driven by the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles such as size, surface charge, and hydrophobicity, underscores the need for standardization in their design and synthesis.

Future research should focus on optimizing polymer compositions and surface modifications to enhance their radiosensitization efficacy and compatibility with clinical applications. Innovations in theranostic nanoparticles, which combine therapeutic and diagnostic functionalities, hold significant promise for advancing radiotherapy outcomes. Additionally, advancements in 3D printing technologies offer the potential for patient-specific nanoparticle-loaded implants, providing personalized treatment strategies that maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

Lipid-based nanoparticles for the delivery of radiosensitizers

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have recently gained significant attention as promising carriers for drug delivery, especially in cancer treatment. Their ability to enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to radiotherapy through various mechanisms makes them an attractive option for improving the efficacy of radiation therapy. The generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), disruption of mitochondrial function, increased drug accumulation intratumorally, and controlled drug release are among the mechanisms involved. One of the main ways in which lipid nanoparticles enhance radiotherapy sensitivity is the increasing ROS production. Radiotherapy itself generates ROS, which are responsible for damaging cellular macromolecules such as DNA, proteins, and lipids. These damages lead to cell death, particularly when DNA repair mechanisms are impaired. Lipid nanoparticles, by encapsulating radiosensitizers, amplify this effect and increase ROS production inside tumor cells. The enhanced oxidative stress resulting from the concurrent radiotherapy and the increased ROS production due to lipid nanoparticles leads to greater DNA damage and a higher likelihood of cell death. This synergistic effect results in a stronger therapeutic response to radiotherapy, making tumor cells more susceptible to radiation-induced damage (Haque et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023). In addition to their role in ROS generation, lipid nanoparticles also assist radiosensitizers by targeting mitochondrial function. Mitochondria play a central role in regulating apoptosis, and disrupting their function can activate cell death pathways. By delivering agents that disrupt mitochondrial membranes, lipid nanoparticles can impair mitochondrial function, leading to the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome C. This disruption of mitochondrial integrity increases tumor cells' sensitivity to apoptosis induced by radiotherapy, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of radiation (Bromma et al., 2019; Lip et al., 2022).

Lipid nanoparticles also take advantage of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, a well-known feature of tumor vasculature. Tumor blood vessels are often abnormally permeable, allowing nanoparticles to specifically accumulate in the tumor tissue. This accumulation is crucial for improving drug delivery to the tumor site. Lipid nanoparticles can pass through these leaky vessels and deliver higher concentrations of therapeutic agents directly to the tumor. Even if only a small amount of the drug reaches the tumor, this targeted delivery results in significantly higher drug concentrations within the tumor compared to free drugs. This enhanced accumulation ensures that radiosensitizers are delivered at optimal concentrations to the tumor site, increasing the tumor's sensitivity to radiotherapy (Mostafavi et al., 2024). Moreover, lipid nanoparticles provide controlled release of encapsulated drugs, ensuring continuous delivery of radiosensitizers to cancer cells during radiotherapy. By modulating the release kinetics, LNPs minimize systemic toxicity and prolong the exposure of cancer cells to radiosensitizers, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of radiotherapy. This controlled release mechanism is particularly valuable for targeting tumor sites, as it ensures that drugs are primarily delivered to the tumor rather than throughout the entire body. This approach reduces the adverse side effects typically associated with traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy treatments. For instance, a study published in Scientific Reports demonstrated that curcumin-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (Cur-SLN) could increase curcumin bioavailability and enhance radiosensitization in cancer cells. The research highlighted the potential of LNPs in improving the efficacy of radiotherapy by ensuring a controlled release of the encapsulated radiosensitizer (Minafra et al., 2019). Additionally, a review article in Frontiers in Oncology discussed the application of nano-radiosensitizers in radiotherapy, emphasizing the importance of controlled drug release in enhancing the effectiveness of radiotherapy. The review highlighted various types of nano-radiosensitizers and their mechanisms at different biological levels, underscoring the role of controlled release in improving therapeutic outcomes (Xie et al., 2021). Moreover, the combination of lipid nanoparticles with other therapeutic modalities, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, can further enhance their radiosensitizing effects. Lipid nanoparticles can be optimized for the simultaneous delivery of multiple therapeutic agents, contributing to a multimodal therapeutic approach in cancer. For example, combining lipid nanoparticles with chemotherapy or immunomodulators not only increases tumor sensitivity to radiotherapy but also stimulates immune responses to promote cancer cell destruction. This combined strategy enhances the effectiveness of radiotherapy and provides a promising approach for improving treatment outcomes in cancer patients (Fan et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2015).

Lipid nanoparticles enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to radiotherapy through several key mechanisms, including ROS generation, disruption of mitochondrial function, improved drug penetration and accumulation in the tumor, and controlled drug release. These advantages make lipid nanoparticles an ideal option for enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy, and ongoing research into optimizing and clinically applying them holds great promise for advancing cancer treatment outcomes (Jahangir et al., 2020; Kwatra et al., 2013). Lipid nanoparticles have emerged as highly effective nano-delivery systems for efficiently transporting cytotoxic chemotherapy drugs, antibiotics, and nucleic acid therapies. During the COVID-19 pandemic, LNP-based mRNA vaccines by Pfizer/BioNTech and Moderna achieved global sales of $37 billion and $17.7 billion in 2021. Building on this success, the advancement of various LNP-based RNA therapies is accelerating, given their potential applications in vaccines and treatments for genetic diseases and cancers (Hou et al., 2021).

Lipid-based nanoparticles can serve as carriers for drug delivery, customized to target tumors precisely while regulating drug release, thereby reducing exposure to healthy organs (Akinc et al., 2019). They increase circulation times, achieving tumor concentrations up to 2–5% compared to just 0.1% for freely administered drugs with short half-lives (Zhigaltsev et al., 2010). Even if only 1% of the LNP drug reaches the target, this yields over tenfold higher drug concentrations within tumors than free drugs (Rosenblum et al., 2018). Furthermore, the small size of LNPs enables them to exploit the leaky vasculature of tumors through the (EPR effect (Yan et al., 2020b). The design of pH-responsive liposomes in lipid-based nanosystems leverages the atypical phase behavior of unsaturated phosphatidylethanolamine, such as 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), which preferentially forms an inverted hexagonal (HII) phase rather than conventional bilayer arrangements (Fan et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2015). The transition of liposomes to bilayers is facilitated by incorporating acidic lipids like Cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS), Linoleic acid (LA) and Oleic acid (OA) Specifically, CHEMS undergoes protonation upon exposure to the acidic environment of tumors, resulting in destabilization of the bilayer and subsequent release of the encapsulated cargo (Fan et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2015).

Liposomes are the most recognized and versatile among various lipid-based nanosystems available today due to their unique characteristics. Consisting of unilamellar lipid bilayers enclosing an aqueous core, they provide biocompatibility, biodegradability, low toxicity, and the ability to carry both hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds (Bozzuto and Molinari, 2015; Cruz et al., 2009; Deshpande et al., 2013; Lamichhane et al., 2018; Pattni et al., 2015). Being extensively researched and successful, liposomal formulations are widely used in biomedicine and are currently undergoing clinical trials.

Beyond their role in delivering a diverse range of chemotherapeutic agents, liposomes have been explored as carriers for various diagnostic agents including 64Cu isotopes (Petersen et al., 2012), 14C isotopes (Al-Jamal et al., 2009), Quantum dots (QDs) (Wang and Chao, 2018), Gadolinium (Gd)-based contrast agents (Lamichhane et al., 2018), Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) (Martínez-González et al., 2016), and Fluorescent probes (Lamichhane et al., 2018; Portnoy et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2017; Xing et al., 2018). Considering these attributes, liposomes emerge as highly promising theranostic tools with broad clinical applications in cancer management.

Carbon- based nanoparticles for delivery of radiosensitizer

Carbon-based nanostructures including carbon nanotubes (CNTs), fullerenes and graphene have attracted remarkable attention due to their physicochemical properties. These carbon molecules are composed of extremely small tubes that have diameters as small as 0.4 nanometers, and their lengths can grow to be one million times longer than their diameter. These nanostructures have great chemical stability and conductivity, which are needed to control radiation-induced free radical reactions(Jaymand et al., 2021). Recent studies show liposome-encapsulated polyhydroxy small-gap fullerenes target human melanoma cells, increasing cellular uptake and radiosensitizing agent concentration, disrupting metabolism, and causing oxidative DNA damage under X-ray irradiation. Consequently, fullerenes represent a valuable class of carbon-based nanostructures for augmenting the radiosensitivity of malignant tumors (Kato et al., 2018). A study found that the combination of fullerene-C60 dissolved in squalene (C60/Sqe) increases the sensitivity of HMV-II melanoma cells to X-ray radiation. Treatment with C60/Sqe reduced cell proliferation compared to squalene alone, while no significant change was observed in the presence of C60/squalane (Sqa) and hydrogenated squalene. Fluorescence imaging showed higher fluorescence intensity in the perinuclear region for C60/Sqe compared to squalene alone. The combination of C60/Sqe and X-ray radiation enhances radiosensitivity by promoting lipid peroxidation (Kato et al., 2014). Carbon nanotubes are being used in cancer therapy due to their unique electrical properties and ability to penetrate cellular membranes. They can be chemically modified or shortened to deliver radionuclides, ensuring precise localization to tumor sites. When exposed to radiation, iron-palladium decorated CNTs cause reactive oxygen species bursts that are stronger than the antioxidant defenses of cancer cells. This causes oxidative stress and cell death. CNTs can also harness their photothermal properties, resulting in localized hyperthermia and enhanced radiosensitivity. This combination enhances radiation response and reduces tumor resistance, making CNTs a promising tool for advanced cancer treatment (Lotfollahzadeh et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021). According to the study conducted by Shengnan Yang et al, FePd nanoparticles exhibit significant radiosensitization properties, while carbon nanotubes enhance the accumulation and retention of these nanoparticles in tumor tissue due to their ability to cross biological barriers. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that FePd@CNTs not only exhibited a stronger radiosensitization effect compared to FePd nanoparticles alone but also showed greater tumor accumulation. These findings confirm the high potential of FePd@CNTs in improving the effectiveness of radiotherapy. However, further research is required to elucidate the precise mechanism of this effect (Yang et al., 2021). Graphene, a single-layer of carbon atoms, provides distinctive chemical and physical characteristics that make it advantageous in uses in biology. The elevated specific surface area and higher conductivity facilitate effective drug loading and fast electron transfer, augmenting radiation-induced cytotoxicity. Graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide have been studied for the ability to augment radiosensitization in different kinds of cancer(Ha, 2014). FePt/GO nanosheets, consisting of functionalized graphene oxide and iron-platinum nanoparticles, have notable radiosensitizing effects in lung cancer cells. Graphene-based materials provide significant adaptability for surface changes, facilitating targeted ligand conjugation and localized hyperthermia, thereby enhancing radiation results (Ma et al., 2019). According to the research by Jing Ruan et al, using IR along with graphene GQDs greatly improved G2/M cell cycle arrest, stopped cell growth, and sped up apoptosis. These effects were primarily attributed to the overproduction of ROS by GQDs upon irradiation, which subsequently activated apoptosis-related regulatory proteins and induced tumor cell apoptosis. Thus, this study suggests that GQDs could serve as a novel class of nano-radiosensitizers, potentially improving the efficacy of radiotherapy in cancer treatment (Ruan et al., 2018). Carbon-based nanostructures enhance radiosensitization through biological and chemical mechanisms, generating ROS that damage DNA, proteins, and cellular membranes. These nanostructures exacerbate cellular damage induced by radiotherapy, leading to increased tumor cell kill rates and disrupting mitochondrial function, causing apoptosis in cancer cells. Carbon nanotubes and graphene exhibit photothermal effects, causing localized heating and thermal ablation of tumor cells (Lotfollahzadeh et al., 2024).

Radioprotective strategies for normal tissue preservation

Irradiating noncancerous tissues during therapeutic radiation can cause side effects, from acute toxicities to severe organ dysfunction. The risk depends on the volume of the irradiated organ, dose, fractionation, use of modifiers, and individual sensitivity. Efforts to reduce these toxicities focus on technological advancements like conformal, intensity-modulated, image-guided, and proton radiotherapy. These methods limit normal tissue exposure, improving dose distribution and side effect profiles. Despite improvements, normal tissue toxicity remains a concern due to the close relationship between tumors and surrounding tissues and the need to irradiate potentially contaminated margins.

Based on timing, chemical, and biological agents that modify normal tissue toxicity from radiation are categorized into three groups: radioprotectors, mitigators, and treatments (Stone et al., 2004). Radioprotectors are administered before or during irradiation to prevent tissue damage, while mitigators are given during or after irradiation but before toxicity manifests. Treatments are used to alleviate established toxicity. There is increasing literature on using various agents for radioprotection and mitigation after total body or localized exposures, though a comprehensive review is beyond this work's scope. Understanding the events following tissue irradiation is crucial for grasping the mechanisms of radioprotectors and mitigators. Radiation damages cells through direct effects (irreparable harm to critical cellular targets like DNA) (Hall and Giaccia, 2006) and indirect effects (damage via free radicals generated from radiation interacting with non-critical molecules). Given that mammals are about 80% water, indirect effects often involve hydroxyl free radicals, which can initiate lipid peroxidation and break chemical bonds within nanoseconds (von Sonntag, 1987).

Free radicals are significant contributors to the damage caused by ionizing radiation. For an agent to effectively protect cells from primary free radical damage, it must be present during radiation exposure at sufficient concentrations to compete with radicals produced through scavenging mechanisms. Numerous radical scavengers and antioxidants exist that can mitigate oxidative stress from free radicals. Examples of natural antioxidants include superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, glutathione peroxidase, and glutathione reductase, each specific to certain substrates. Additionally, low molecular weight antioxidants, such as ascorbic acid, tocopherols, polyphenols, and thiols like glutathione, provide general antioxidant defense by donating hydrogen atoms to neutralize oxidants.

While radioprotectors require radical-scavenging properties and general antioxidant activity, not all antioxidants provide radioprotection (Xavier et al., 2002) arise from the differing reactivity of radiation-induced reactive species compared to those from general oxidative stress (e.g., H₂O₂). Although hydroxyl radicals can be scavenged effectively by both radioprotectors and antioxidants, only radioprotectors offer cellular and in vivo protection. This implies that hydroxyl radicals generate a secondary species responsible for critical DNA damage, which may not be effectively scavenged by conventional antioxidants. Thiols like amifostine and newly developed nitroxides can efficiently scavenge these secondary radicals, unlike well-known antioxidants such as vitamin C and vitamin E, which do not function as classic radioprotectors.

Clinical trials of radiosensitizers in radiotherapy

Recent progress in chemical radiosensitizers includes several candidates in preclinical evaluation, targeting cell signaling, radioprotective substances, pseudosubstrates, and targeted delivery systems. Key signaling pathways linked to radioresistance, such as PI3K–Akt–mTOR (Wang et al., 2014), Wnt (Suwala et al., 2016), MAPK (Saker et al., 2013), MDM2 (Prabakaran et al., 2017), and c-MET–PI3K–Akt (Zhuang et al., 2014), provide potential targets for sensitization. For example, BKM120, a PI3K inhibitor, enhances apoptosis and DNA damage in liver cancer cells (Fig. 8) (Liu et al., 2014), while N-[4-[2-(4-Morpholinyl)ethoxy]phenyl]-6-[4-(2-pyridinyl)–2-pyrimidinyl]–4–quinazolinamine (BEZ235) improves radiosensitivity in colorectal cancer (Chen et al., 2015). Suppressing glutathione (GSH) can enhance radiotherapy effectiveness (Jayakumar et al., 2016), and halogenated pyrimidine analogs like BrdUrd show promise as radiosensitizers (Kinsella et al., 1987). Additionally, existing drugs such as papaverine, identified as a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor, may enhance radiation response (Benej et al., 2018). Role of artificial intelligence and bioinformatics in radiosensitizer development

Artificial intelligence (AI) and bioinformatics have emerged as pivotal tools in the development of radiosensitizers. Radiosensitizers. For instance, the ANAKIN model, an AI-based deep learning framework, has demonstrated high accuracy in predicting radiation-induced cell killing across various cell lines and radiation types. Trained on over 500 cell survival experiments, ANAKIN outperforms traditional radiobiological models like the Microdosimetric Kinetic Model and Local Effect Model in predicting biological endpoints. Moreover, ANAKIN incorporates Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques, allowing for the interpretation of its predictions and providing insights into complex biological phenomena such as the overkilling effect (Cordoni et al., 2023). In addition to predictive modeling, bioinformatics plays a crucial role in identifying molecular targets and biomarkers associated with radiosensitivity. Machine learning approaches have been utilized to construct predictive scores, such as the NPC-RSS, which are associated with radiosensitivity in nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients. These models analyze gene expression profiles to predict treatment responses, thereby guiding personalized radiotherapy strategies (Li et al., 2025). Furthermore, AI and bioinformatics facilitate the design and optimization of nanoparticle-based delivery systems for radiosensitizers. By integrating omics data, these computational tools can predict interactions between radiosensitizers and biological systems, leading to the development of more effective and targeted therapies. Collectively, the integration of AI and bioinformatics offers a comprehensive approach to enhancing the efficacy of radiotherapy through the strategic development of radiosensitizers.

Limitations of current radiosensitizer strategies

Despite promising advancements, radiosensitizers face significant limitations. Poor tumor penetration, particularly in dense or hypoxic microenvironments, reduces efficacy; for instance, high-Z nanoparticles often accumulate peripherally due to EPR variability, limiting deep tumor access. Off-target effects, such as systemic toxicity from chemotherapeutic radiosensitizers (e.g., cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity) or nanoparticle accumulation in liver/spleen, pose risks. Scalability issues in nanocarrier production—e.g., batch-to-batch variability in polymeric NPs and high costs of lipid-based systems—hinder clinical translation. Additionally, acquired resistance via adaptive pathways (e.g., upregulated DNA repair) and biosafety concerns (e.g., long-term inflammation from metal NPs) remain unresolved. Addressing these requires optimized surface modifications for better penetration, biodegradable formulations to minimize off-target accumulation, and standardized manufacturing protocols to enhance scalability and safety.

Conclusion and future perspective

Radiosensitizers and nanotechnology hold transformative potential for overcoming radioresistance in cancers such as breast and lung, enhancing DNA damage, reactive oxygen species production, and targeted delivery while protecting normal tissues. Natural compounds like curcumin and resveratrol, chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin and doxorubicin, and high-Z metal nanoparticles, including gold and hafnium, amplify radiation-induced effects through mechanisms like DNA repair inhibition and cell cycle modulation. Advanced nanocarriers, encompassing polymeric, lipid-based, and carbon-based systems, improve tumor specificity and bioavailability, while molecular targets like p53, PARP1, miR-21, and miR-155 address radioresistance by regulating apoptosis and gene expression. Radioprotectors such as antioxidants and amifostine mitigate collateral damage to healthy tissues, and ongoing clinical trials with hafnium oxide nanoparticles and cisplatin-based regimens demonstrate promising therapeutic outcomes. These advancements underscore the potential of integrating nanotechnology and precision medicine to enhance radiotherapy efficacy and tailor treatments to tumor biology and patient profiles.

Despite these advances, significant challenges persist in translating radiosensitizers into clinical practice. Limited long-term toxicity data for high-Z nanoparticles pose risks of chronic inflammation or organ dysfunction due to accumulation in the liver, spleen, or kidneys. Inconsistent tumor penetration, particularly in hypoxic or fibrotic microenvironments, reduces efficacy, as nanoparticles often accumulate peripherally due to variability in the enhanced permeability and retention effect. Scalability and cost barriers in nanocarrier production, especially for lipid-based and polymeric systems, hinder widespread adoption due to batch-to-batch inconsistency and high manufacturing costs. Acquired resistance mechanisms, such as upregulated DNA repair via RAD51 or DNA-PKcs and epithelial-mesenchymal transition induction, complicate long-term treatment success. Additionally, the lack of personalized biomarkers limits the ability to predict radiosensitizer response in heterogeneous tumors.

To address these gaps, hybrid nanocarriers combining polymer-lipid or polymer-metal systems can leverage biocompatibility, controlled release, and enhanced EPR effects to improve penetration and reduce toxicity. Surface optimization with ligands like antibodies or pH-responsive coatings can enhance active targeting and overcome hypoxic barriers. Artificial intelligence-driven design can model nanoparticle-tumor interactions and predict radiosensitivity using genomic, proteomic, and imaging data, optimizing formulations for individual tumors. Three-dimensional printing enables fabrication of patient-specific, biodegradable implants, such as those based on poly(lactide-co-glycolide), for localized, sustained release of radiosensitizers, minimizing systemic exposure. Biodegradable formulations with metabolizable or renally clearable properties can reduce long-term organ accumulation and improve biosafety.

Future research should focus on conducting multi-omics clinical trials integrating genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics to identify biomarkers predictive of radiosensitizer response, enabling personalized radiotherapy regimens tailored to tumor molecular profiles. Exploring nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems for miRNA, such as miR-21 and miR-155, and siRNA targeting DNA repair genes like RAD51 and PARP1, with preclinical validation in radioresistant cancers like non-small cell lung cancer and glioblastoma, can enhance therapeutic efficacy. Integrating radiosensitizers with immune checkpoint inhibitors to boost immune-mediated tumor clearance post-radiation, particularly in breast and lung cancer models, offers a promising multimodal approach. Adopting three-dimensional tumor spheroid and patient-derived xenograft models that mimic tumor heterogeneity and microenvironment dynamics can improve translational relevance. Optimizing manufacturing protocols, such as microfluidics for lipid-based systems or automated synthesis for polymeric nanoparticles, ensures scalability and cost-effectiveness for clinical feasibility. Longitudinal in vivo studies to assess biodistribution, clearance, and chronic toxicity of high-Z nanoparticles, prioritizing biodegradable designs, are essential to mitigate organ accumulation risks.

By addressing scalability, toxicity, and personalization challenges through these strategies, future research can reduce tumor recurrence, enhance therapeutic ratios, and improve survival outcomes, ultimately transforming cancer care through data-driven, patient-specific approaches that leverage nanotechnology, artificial intelligence, and three-dimensional printing.
