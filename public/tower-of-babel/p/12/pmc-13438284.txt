Integration of laryngeal and cardiorespiratory control by the dorsomedial and perifornical hypothalamus in anaesthetised rats
Authors: Laura Carrillo-Franco, Marta González-García, Carmen Morales-Luque, Alicia Rivera, Belén Gago, Marina Ponce-Velasco, Marc Stefan Dawid-Milner, Manuel Víctor López-González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus and perifornical area (DMH/PeF), historically identified as the hypothalamic defence area, plays a key role in orchestrating autonomic and respiratory adjustments during threat-related states. To investigate how activation of this region affects upper-airway and respiratory motor circuits, we examined its anatomical and functional interactions with laryngeal-related medullary nuclei using combined immunohistochemical mapping and physiological recordings in spontaneously breathing anaesthetised rats. Neuronal activation was assessed using c-Fos immunohistochemistry (c-Fos-ir) combined with ChAT and FoxP2 labelling to identify motoneurons and non-motoneuronal populations across the compact, semicompact and loose formations of the nucleus ambiguus (nA), as well as within the nucleus retroambiguus (nRA), during DMH/PeF electrical stimulation. Hypothalamic activation produced a clear subdivision-specific recruitment in the nA, with significant ipsilateral increases in c-Fos-ir within the semicompact (motoneurons, p < 0.001; non-motoneurons, p < 0.05) and compact formations (non-motoneurons, p < 0.05). The nRA also exhibited robust ipsilateral activation (non-motoneurons, p < 0.001). FoxP2-positive neurons remained stable across groups, indicating that FoxP2 reflects neuronal phenotype rather than acute activation. A second set of experiments evaluated the functional impact of electrical and chemical (glutamate) DMH/PeF activation on subglottic pressure, an index of laryngeal resistance. Both stimulation modalities produced a marked reduction in subglottic pressure (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01), accompanied by a reproducible autonomic–respiratory pattern characterised by tachypnoea, tachycardia and a pressor response. Overall, our results provide new insight into how hypothalamic circuits regulate subglottic pressure and laryngeal activity, suggesting that DMH/PeF activation is associated with the recruitment of specific nA and nRA domains, which may contribute to the adjustment of upper-airway patency during defensive responses.

Introduction

The dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus and perifornical area (DMH/PeF), classically known as the hypothalamic defence area (HDA), constitute a key integrative centre within the central autonomic network. Activation of this region reliably evokes tachycardia, hypertension, increased ventilation and cutaneous vasoconstriction, reflecting its fundamental role in coordinating autonomic and respiratory components of the defence response (Fontes et al., 2001; DiMicco et al., 2002; Samuels et al., 2002; Horiuchi et al., 2004; Bondarenko et al., 2015; Nakamura and Morrison, 2022). These responses emerge from the extensive connectivity of the DMH/PeF with forebrain -structures processing affective and interoceptive signals, such as cortical regions, the limbic system and neighbouring hypothalamic nuclei (Bondarenko et al., 2014; Taugher et al., 2014; Bonnet et al., 2015; Marins et al., 2016; Nobis et al., 2018; Barbier et al., 2021), as well as from its descending projections to brainstem centres that generate autonomic, cardiovascular and respiratory outputs (da Silva et al., 2003; Cao et al., 2004; Díaz-Casares et al., 2009; Díaz-Casares et al., 2012; López-González et al., 2013; López-González et al., 2018).

This hypothalamo-medullary pathway has been implicated in both physiological and stress-related responses. Functional evidence—from electrophysiological, pharmacological and stimulation/inactivation studies—indicates that the DMH/PeF modulates the parabrachial complex (PBc), the Kölliker–Fuse nucleus (KF), the A5 noradrenergic region (A5), and medullary circuits involved in baroreflex and chemoreflex control (Silva-Carvalho et al., 1993; Silva-Carvalho et al., 1995; McDowall et al., 2006; Queiroz et al., 2011; Silva et al., 2015; Zafar et al., 2018). These nuclei serve as critical relays through which hypothalamic activation shapes respiratory patterning and sympathetic drive. Previous work from our group has demonstrated that glutamatergic receptors in the PBc and A5 region play a crucial role in mediating the cardiovascular and respiratory effects of DMH/PeF stimulation (Díaz-Casares et al., 2009; Díaz-Casares et al., 2012; López-González et al., 2013; López-González et al., 2018). Collectively, these findings support the view that the DMH/PeF occupies a strategic position within the central autonomic network, enabling rapid coordination of cardiorespiratory adjustments during threat-related states (Lamotte et al., 2021; Fontes et al., 2023).

Recent conceptualizations of the respiratory network further support this view. According to the integrated model proposed by Krohn et al. (2023), respiration emerges from a hierarchically organised yet highly distributed circuit spanning pontine and medullary structures, including the parabrachial complex and Kölliker–Fuse nucleus (PBc/KF) and the medullary rhythm- and pattern-generating groups (Ghali, 2019). Within this architecture—which couples hypothalamic, limbic and brainstem nodes to generate adaptive defensive responses (Lamotte et al., 2021)—the DMH/PeF occupies an upstream modulatory position, well placed to influence, through pontine and other relays, respiratory rhythm, expiratory drive and upper-airway patency.

Beyond its autonomic and respiratory roles, the hypothalamus has long been associated with vocal behaviour. Early studies showed that electrical stimulation of hypothalamic regions, including the dorsomedial, ventromedial and supraoptic nuclei, could elicit a wide range of vocalizations in cats—ranging from defensive hissing and growling to more structured harmonic calls—and these were often accompanied by sympathetic activation and behavioural arousal (Magoun et al., 1937; Kanai and Wang, 1962; Fernandez De Molina and Hunsperger, 1962; Altafullah et al., 1988). However, research on hypothalamic contributions to vocal control has remained relatively limited compared with the extensive literature on the Periaqueductal Grey Matter (PAG), a midbrain structure now regarded as the principal gating centre for vocalization, operating upstream of the brainstem premotor circuits rather than as a premotor structure itself.

In the hierarchical model proposed by Jürgens (1979), the DMH/PeF occupies a higher-order modulatory level, conveying affective and internal-state information to the PAG. The PAG, in turn, organizes vocal motor patterns and relays them to lower brainstem nuclei (Krohn et al., 2023; Faull et al., 2019), including the nucleus retroambiguus (nRA), which contains inspiratory and expiratory premotor interneurons essential for coordinating respiratory and abdominal musculature (Subramanian and Holstege, 2009; Subramanian et al., 2018; Hartmann and Brecht, 2020; Concha-Miranda et al., 2022). The nRA also projects to the nucleus ambiguus (nA), which houses the motoneurons responsible for controlling pharyngeal and intrinsic laryngeal muscles, regulating airway patency and subglottic pressure through vocal fold adduction and abduction (Ludlow, 2011; Holstege and Subramanian, 2016; Pathak et al., 2020). These motoneurons are cholinergic, expressing choline acetyltransferase (ChAT), and are arranged in three functional subdivisions: compact (oesophageal), semicompact (cricothyroid) and loose (intrinsic laryngeal muscles) (Bieger and Hopkins, 1987; Pascual-Font et al., 2011; Hernandez-Morato et al., 2016). Recent work has also identified non-motor interneurons within the nA, although their functional significance is not yet fully understood (Fogarty, 2024). This framework provides a mechanistic foundation for understanding how hypothalamic activation could modulate laryngeal function via nRA and nA, thereby coupling airway resistance with the enhanced ventilatory demands of defensive states. Importantly, although this hypothalamic–midbrain–medullary axis has been characterised largely in the context of vocalisation, the same descending circuitry governs laryngeal calibre during breathing; in the present study we examine its contribution to airway patency and subglottic-pressure regulation as part of the respiratory (tachypnoeic) component of the defence response, rather than to phonation.

Our recent findings indicate that stimulation of the dorsolateral PAG decreases subglottic pressure and activates specific subdivisions of the nA, demonstrating that midbrain defensive circuits can directly influence laryngeal resistance (González-García et al., 2024). These results, together with the anatomical and functional connectivity between the DMH and PAG (Horiuchi et al., 2009; de Git et al., 2018), suggest the existence of a broader distributed network linking emotional processing centres with respiratory and laryngeal motor control. Within this network, the nRA is positioned as a core premotor structure integrating respiratory rhythm generation and patterning (Ghali, 2019), while PBc/KF and A5 contribute to shaping the autonomic and respiratory components of defensive behaviours (Díaz-Casares et al., 2009; Díaz-Casares et al., 2012; López-González et al., 2013; López-González et al., 2018; Hayward et al., 2004; López-González et al., 2020; González-García et al., 2023). However, despite extensive evidence supporting the involvement of the DMH/PeF in the coordination of autonomic and respiratory output, its potential role in the modulation of laryngeal function remains largely unexplored.

In light of these considerations, the present study aimed to characterize whether the DMH/PeF can influence laryngeal activity through its descending projections to medullary centres. Specifically, we sought to determine whether hypothalamic activation recruits defined neuronal populations within the nA and nRA, and whether it modulates subglottic pressure as an index of laryngeal resistance. To address this question, we combined dual immunostaining for c-Fos and FoxP2, together with ChAT-ir to identify laryngeal motoneurons, across the rostrocaudal extent of the nA and nRA in anaesthetised rats subjected to electrical stimulation of the DMH/PeF. In parallel, we used the validated in situ isolated glottis preparation to evaluate subglottic pressure during electrical and chemical stimulation of the DMH/PeF (González-García et al., 2024; Lara et al., 2002). This integrative approach provides a novel framework for understanding how hypothalamic defensive circuits may contribute to upper airway modulation and laryngeal–respiratory coordination.

Materials and methods

Animals and housing

Experiments were performed on 32 adult male SPF Sprague–Dawley rats (OFA-SD IOPS Caw), 3–6 months old and weighing 300–350 g (Charles River Laboratories, Barcelona, Spain). Animals were housed in groups of six in the Animal House of the University of Málaga under controlled environmental conditions (21 ± 1 °C; 12:12 h light–dark cycle). Food and water were available ad libitum.

General procedures

Surgical procedures followed protocols previously described by our group for hypothalamic and midbrain stimulation experiments (López-González et al., 2013; López-González et al., 2018; González-García et al., 2024; López-González et al., 2020). Anaesthesia was induced with sodium pentobarbitone (60 mg/kg, i.p.) and maintained with intravenous supplements (2 mg/kg, i.v.) as required. The femoral artery and vein were cannulated to measure arterial blood pressure and for drug administration, respectively.

The trachea and oesophagus were cannulated to record respiratory flow and pleural pressure. Airflow was measured using a Fleisch pneumotachograph, and pleural pressure was monitored with an air-filled oesophageal catheter. Animals breathed spontaneously a humidified mixture of room air enriched with oxygen. End-tidal CO₂ was continuously monitored with a fast-response analyser (ADC FM1, The Analytical Development Co. Ltd., Great Amwell, UK) and maintained between 3 and 5%. Body temperature was kept at 37 °C using a heated surgical table and a servo-controlled heating pad coupled to a rectal thermometer.

The depth of anaesthesia was assessed throughout the experiments by monitoring resting cardiovascular variables and verifying the absence of a withdrawal reflex to paw pinching. The head was fixed in a stereotaxic frame with the upper incisor bar positioned 3.3 mm below the interaural line to ensure stable positioning during the procedures.

Electrical stimulation of DMH/PeF

In 10 animals, a burr hole was drilled in the skull to access the right DMH/PeF. A concentric bipolar electrode (SNE-100; Rhodes Medical Electrodes, Summerland, CA, USA) was placed stereotaxically according to Paxinos and Watson (2007).

Animals were assigned to one of two groups prior to any stimulation. In the Control group (n = 5), no electrical stimulation was delivered at any point. These animals underwent the same surgical procedure, including craniotomy and stereotaxic electrode placement within the right DMH/PeF, but the electrode was never activated. Electrode placement in control animals was confirmed post-hoc by histological verification of the electrode track during brain sectioning. In the Stimulated group (n = 5), a single verification train of 1-ms pulses (30–50 μA, 100 Hz, 5 s) was first delivered to confirm the characteristic defence-like cardiovascular and respiratory response and verify electrode placement functionally. In both groups, immediately after the electrode was positioned, guanethidine (10 mg/kg, i.v.) was administered to suppress sympathetically mediated cardiovascular changes. In the Stimulated group, repeated stimulation (same parameters, once every 60 s) was then applied for 1 h.

At the end of the procedure, rats were deeply anaesthetised with sodium pentobarbitone and perfused transcardially with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4). Brains were removed, post-fixed overnight in the same fixative solution and then rinsed in PBS.

For embedding, the brainstem was immersed in 3% agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, The Analytical Development Co. Ltd., Great Amwell, UK) prepared in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4). Coronal sections of the medulla (30 μm) were obtained using a vibrating microtome (5,100 mz, Campden Instruments).

C-Fos/ChAT and FoxP2/ChAT double immunohistochemistry

Neuronal activation was assessed using c-Fos immunohistochemistry (c-Fos-ir) combined with ChAT and FoxP2 labelling to identify motoneurons and non-motoneuronal populations during DMH/PeF electrical stimulation. FoxP2, a transcription factor whose expression in the adult rat brainstem is restricted to upper-airway premotor areas (Stanić et al., 2018), was included as a stable phenotypic marker to characterise the neuronal populations present in these nuclei independently of acute activation state.

Every fifth section, starting from a randomly chosen section, was selected and processed to analyse c-Fos/FoxP2/ChAT immunoreactivity at the nA and nRA nuclei, yielding approximately 20 sections per animal spaced 180 μm apart and covering the full rostrocaudal extent of the target regions.

Free-floating sections from control and stimulated animals were processed using a standard avidin–biotin peroxidase method. After rinsing in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4), sections were incubated for 15 min in 3% H₂O₂ to block endogenous peroxidase activity and then washed again in PBS.

For nuclear immunostaining, sections were incubated for 24 h at room temperature with either a mouse anti-c-Fos monoclonal antibody (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SC-271243) or a sheep anti-FoxP2 antibody (1:1000; R&D Systems, AF5647), diluted in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 (PBS-TX) and 0.02% sodium azide. After washes in PBS, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the corresponding biotinylated secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG or rabbit anti-sheep IgG; Vector Laboratories, 1:500 in PBS-TX and 0.1% sodium azide). This was followed by incubation with streptavidin–peroxidase (1:2000; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h. The peroxidase reaction was developed with 0.05% DAB and 0.02% H₂O₂, adding 0.8 mg/mL nickel ammonium sulphate (Carlo Erba Reagents) added to obtain a black nuclear reaction product.

After washing in PBS, sections were incubated with avidin/biotin blocking kit (Vector Laboratories SP-2001) for 15 min. Afterwards, to identify cholinergic motoneurons and delineate the nA, the same sections were subsequently incubated for 24 h at room temperature with a goat polyclonal anti-ChAT antibody (1:1000; Sigma-Aldrich, AB144P). After washing, sections were incubated for 1 h with a biotinylated anti-goat IgG secondary antibody (1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific). ChAT immunoreactivity was revealed with DAB alone, yielding a brown cytoplasmic labelling. Finally, all sections were dehydrated and coverslipped with DPX mounting medium.

Cell counting

Brightfield images of double-labelled sections were acquired using a BX61VS microscope (Olympus) and the associated Olympus VS imaging software.

A semi-quantitative estimate of the total number of c-Fos- and FoxP2-immunoreactive nuclei was determined by an observer blinded to experimental group assignment using ImageJ (NIH). Besides, motoneurons were identified by the presence of ChAT-positive cytoplasm and immunoreactive cells lacking ChAT labelling were classified as non-motoneurons. Converting images to greyscale was performed to optimise contrast, using consistent threshold parameters applied uniformly across all sections and animals. Counts were performed separately for the ipsilateral and contralateral sides relative to the stimulated DMH/PeF, from the three main subdivisions of the nA (compact, semicompact and loose formations) and nRA. These domains were defined according to Paxinos and Watson (2007), with the compact region spanning from Bregma −12.00 to −12.84 mm, the semicompact region from −12.84 to −13.44 mm, the loose formation from −13.44 to −14.16 mm, and nRA from −14.28 mm to −15.72 mm.

Data are reported as absolute numbers per subdivision. No normalisation to total Nissl-stained cells or section area was performed. Comparisons of absolute counts across subdivisions of differing size and neuronal density should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Interaction of DMH/PeF and nA: electrical/chemical stimulation and subglottic pressure

In a new set of 22 animals, assigned to three experimental groups, the general procedures were adapted to implement a double tracheal cannulation. One cannula was directed rostrally towards the glottis to apply the in situ isolated glottis preparation, while a second cannula was positioned caudally towards the carina to record respiratory airflow. This configuration allowed independent measurement of subglottic pressure and airflow (González-García et al., 2024; Lara et al., 2002).

The original in situ preparation (Lara et al., 2002) was maintained but combined with methodological refinements from our recent work (González-García et al., 2024). Subglottic pressure was recorded with an aneroid transducer (FE141, ADInstruments, ±0.03 psi) while a constant flow (30–70 mL/min) of warm, humidified medical air was delivered rostrally through the larynx using a thermal mass digital flow controller (Bronkhorst Hi-Tec, F-201CV-AGD-22-V). Under these conditions, pressure variations directly index changes in laryngeal resistance. To avoid artefacts during DMH/PeF stimulation, the tongue was immobilized. Data acquisition hardware (PowerLab 16/30) and analysis software (LabChart Pro 8.0) were upgraded relative to previous studies, improving signal stability and sensitivity. Only animals showing stable baseline and stimulus-evoked recordings were included in the analysis.

DMH/PeF electrical stimulation and subglottic pressure

In 8 animals, a burr hole was drilled to access the right DMH/PeF. Electrical stimulation was delivered through a concentric bipolar electrode (SNE-100; Rhodes Medical Electrodes) using 1-ms pulses (30–50 μA, 100 Hz, 5 s).

DMH/PeF chemical stimulation and subglottic pressure

In 14 animals, a stereotaxically guided glass micropipette connected to a 1 μL Hamilton syringe (Model 7,001 KH Neuros Syringe) was used to microinject either PBS (50 nL, pH 7.4 ± 0.1, 5 s; n = 7) or glutamate (0.25 M, 50 nL; n = 7) into the right DMH/PeF. All solutions contained Evans blue, which served both as solvent and injection site marker. The injection volume (50 nL) was controlled with an Ultra Micro Pump II (Micro 4; World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA). Only one microinjection was performed in each animal.

Histological verification and physiological data acquisition

Electrical lesions (250 μA DC, 20 s) and Evans blue deposits were used to mark the sites of DMH/PeF stimulation and microinjection, respectively. Brains were perfused with 10% formal saline, and the hypothalamus was sectioned serially at 50 μm. Sections were counterstained with Neutral Red to verify the anatomical location of stimulation and injection sites.

In all experimental groups, airflow, pleural pressure (as an index of inspiratory effort), subglottic pressure and arterial pressure were continuously recorded and stored for offline analysis. Instantaneous respiratory frequency, inspiratory time (TI), defined as the interval from the onset to the end of the inspiratory phase of the respiratory flow signal, and expiratory time (TE), defined as the interval from the end of inspiration to the onset of the subsequent inspiratory phase, were measured from the respiratory airflow trace. Subglottic pressure, mean arterial blood pressure and instantaneous heart rate were quantified using LabChart Pro (PowerLab System, ADInstruments, version 8.0). Each experiment comprised two phases: (a) baseline assessment of laryngeal and cardiorespiratory parameters at rest, and (b) evaluation of changes evoked by DMH/PeF stimulation (electrical or chemical). Baseline values were measured immediately before stimulation. Post-stimulation measurements were taken 4 min after electrical stimulation or 30 min after chemical stimulation. Respiratory rate was derived per animal as 60/(TI + TE) from the mean of three consecutive breaths in the electrical-stimulation experiments—the group value being the mean of these individual frequencies—and as the instantaneous peak frequency in the chemical-stimulation experiments.

Changes in mean arterial pressure and heart rate were quantified as the peak increase observed during the 5-s electrical stimulus or as the maximum cardiovascular response following chemical stimulation. Respiratory changes were evaluated as the mean response during the 5-s electrical stimulus or as the maximum change during chemical stimulation. The 30-min post-injection measurement window for chemical stimulation was selected to capture the full temporal profile of the glutamate-evoked response, including return to baseline, consistent with the kinetics previously characterised in this preparation (González-García et al., 2024). Peak values were used for statistical comparisons in both stimulation modalities.

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0.1.1), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 9. A parametric analytical framework was applied throughout, consistent with the approach used in our previous studies in this in vivo preparation (López-González et al., 2013; López-González et al., 2018; González-García et al., 2024). Given the sample sizes inherent to this anaesthetised whole-animal preparation (n = 5 per group for the immunohistochemical analyses and n = 7–8 for the physiological experiments), parametric tests were adopted as a conventional and widely accepted choice at this scale, rather than being made contingent on a formal normality test whose statistical power is limited at these sample sizes. Ipsilateral–contralateral differences within each group (immunohistochemistry) and pre- versus post-stimulation values within the same animals (physiological variables) were compared using paired Student’s t-tests. Between-group comparisons—the Δ (ipsilateral–contralateral) values of control versus stimulated animals (immunohistochemistry) and the evoked changes in PBS- versus glutamate-injected animals (physiology)—were performed using one-way ANOVA, which for two-group comparisons is equivalent to an unpaired Student’s t-test. Significance is reported at three levels (p < 0.05, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001), with exact p-values given in the text for the principal between-group comparisons. No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the exploratory immunohistochemical analyses. Only data from animals in which histological examination confirmed that electrodes or micropipettes were located within the DMH/PeF were included in the final analysis.

The ipsilateral predominance observed in control animals indicates that part of this lateralisation reflects an intrinsic property of these nuclei rather than a direct consequence of hypothalamic activation.

In non-stimulated animals (Control), FoxP2-positive cells showed a bilaterally organised pattern, with moderate ipsilateral predominance in specific regions (Figure 1; Table 1). No changes were observed between groups in the amplitude of ipsilateral–contralateral differences (Δ values) in any domain, indicating that the overall distribution of FoxP2-positive motoneurons was maintained after stimulation (Table 1, Figure 1).

Discussion

Functional impact of DMH/PeF activation on laryngeal and cardiorespiratory control

The present study demonstrates for the first time, through the combined use of immunohistochemical and physiological approaches, that activation of the dorsomedial and perifornical hypothalamus (DMH/PeF) exerts a powerful influence on laryngeal activity and subglottic pressure in anaesthetised rats, in parallel with the well-established cardiorespiratory adjustments associated with defensive responses. Electrical and chemical stimulation of the DMH/PeF consistently elicited a robust increase in respiratory rate, tachycardia and a pressor response, together with a marked reduction in subglottic pressure, indicative of decreased laryngeal resistance. These functional changes were accompanied by significant c-Fos recruitment within specific subdivisions of the nucleus ambiguus (nA), notably the semicompact formation (motoneurons and non-cholinergic c-Fos-ir cells) and the compact formation (non-cholinergic c-Fos-ir cells), as well as within the nucleus retroambiguus (nRA), with a clear predominance on the ipsilateral side. In contrast, the distribution of FoxP2-positive motoneurons remained stable across groups, consistent with its role as a phenotypic marker rather than an indicator of acute neuronal activation. Altogether, these findings suggest that the DMH/PeF is associated with the modulation of the laryngeal motor network during defensive-like activation, selectively recruiting nA and nRA subdivisions while simultaneously promoting glottal opening and respiratory facilitation.

Interaction of DMH/PeF and nA: c-Fos and FoxP2 expression

Consistent with the established somatotopic organisation of nA, in which the compact, semicompact and loose formations contain distinct motoneuronal pools innervating the oesophagus, cricothyroid and intrinsic laryngeal muscles, respectively, (Bieger and Hopkins, 1987; Pascual-Font et al., 2011; Hernandez-Morato et al., 2016), electrical stimulation of the DMH/PeF produced a clear and topographically structured increase in c-Fos expression within this medullary column. The strongest effects were observed in the semicompact formation, where motoneurons, non-cholinergic c-Fos-ir cells and the total c-Fos-immunoreactive population exhibited marked ipsilateral activation, and in the compact formation where this effect was restricted to non-cholinergic c-Fos-ir cells and total c-Fos-immunoreactive population. In contrast, the loose formation showed only a mild ipsilateral increase limited to motoneurons and did not display a robust activation pattern. This distribution was obtained under peripheral sympathetic blockade with guanethidine, which suppresses the systemic cardiovascular consequences of hypothalamic stimulation. It should be noted, however, that guanethidine acts on postganglionic sympathetic terminals and does not prevent centrally relayed baroreceptor afferent signalling. Blood pressure fluctuations during stimulation could therefore theoretically contribute to c-Fos expression in nA and nRA neurons through centrally mediated cardiovascular pathways—a residual confound that cannot be fully excluded and represents an additional interpretive limitation of the immunohistochemical data. The nRA also exhibited robust c-Fos activation, as the nRA is a key structure of the caudal ventral respiratory group (VRG), providing expiratory premotor interneurons projecting to abdominal, intercostal and laryngeal motoneurons (Krohn et al., 2023; Subramanian and Holstege, 2009; Subramanian et al., 2018; Hartmann and Brecht, 2020; Concha-Miranda et al., 2022), its recruitment suggests that the DMH/PeF exerts a parallel influence on expiratory drive, consistent with the tachypnoeic pattern induced during hypothalamic activation.

This combined nA–nRA activation supports the view that the DMH/PeF coordinates laryngeal activity together with the expiratory drive during defensive-like responses. This organisation fits within the broader architecture of the central autonomic network, in which hypothalamic nodes integrate behavioural, respiratory and cardiovascular components of defensive states (Lamotte et al., 2021). The pattern of recruitment observed here is also compatible with current models of the distributed respiratory network (Krohn et al., 2023), where hypothalamic inputs, including those arising from the DMH/PeF, interact with pontine structures such as the PBc/KF and with medullary rhythm- and pattern-generating nuclei to adjust respiratory timing and expiratory premotor output. Within this integrative layout, the nRA operates as a major premotor hub linking respiratory drive with laryngeal and abdominal activity, providing a plausible substrate through which hypothalamic activation can simultaneously influence ventilation and upper-airway patency during defence-related states.

A notable methodological difference between our previous study on the dlPAG (González-García et al., 2024) and the present work also deserves comment. In the dlPAG experiments, c-Fos immunoreactivity was analysed in combination with TH, which did not allow us to determine whether the activated neurons within the nA were motoneurons or non-motoneurons (non-cholinergic c-Fos-ir cells); our interpretation therefore remained at the level of “neurons located within the nA.” In contrast, the present study combined c-Fos with ChAT, allowing the unequivocal identification of true laryngeal motoneurons and revealing that the majority of the DMH/PeF-evoked activation occurred in non-motor neurons within the classical subdivisions of the nA.

The existence of non-motor neuronal populations within the nA has been anatomically documented by Fogarty (2024), who provided the first quantitative characterization of the dendritic morphologies of motor neurons and interneurons within the compact and semicompact/loose formations of the nucleus ambiguus, demonstrating that these represent morphologically distinct neuronal populations. Although the precise neurochemical identity and circuit-level function of the non-cholinergic c-Fos-immunoreactive cells identified in the present study remain to be established, converging evidence supports the functional importance of inhibitory interneuronal populations at the level of the nA in shaping laryngeal motor output. Glycinergic inhibition specifically within the ventrolateral medullary region containing laryngeal motoneurons of the nA plays a critical role in separating inspiratory and postinspiratory bursting of laryngeal motoneurons (Furuya et al., 2021), while swallow-related inhibition of laryngeal motoneurons has been shown to be mediated by GABA(A) receptor-dependent IPSPs (Suzuki et al., 2010). In addition, glycinergic neurons of the pre-Bötzinger complex project to cardiac vagal motoneurons of the nA and modulate their activity during inspiration (Buron et al., 2025), providing direct evidence that non-cholinergic inhibitory neurons can selectively regulate specific motoneuronal populations within the nA. Beyond inhibitory circuits, premotor relay neurons of the intermediate reticular nucleus (Toor et al., 2019) and excitatory neurons of the pre-Bötzinger complex (Huff et al., 2022) have been identified as non-cholinergic populations that modulate nA motoneuron activity in the context of laryngeal and swallowing coordination. Collectively, these findings suggest that the non-cholinergic c-Fos-immunoreactive cells activated by DMH/PeF stimulation in the present study may include inhibitory interneurons and/or premotor relay elements capable of modulating laryngeal motoneuron activity, potentially contributing to the reduction in subglottic pressure observed in the physiological experiments through inhibitory or disinhibitory mechanisms acting on laryngeal adductor motoneurons.

FoxP2 expression provided complementary insights into the identity and distribution of the neuronal populations present within the nA and nRA. As shown previously, FoxP2-positive neurons were most abundant in the loose and semicompact formations, with comparatively fewer profiles in the compact formation (González-García et al., 2024), a pattern that was fully reproduced under the present experimental conditions. Importantly, this rostrocaudal organisation displayed a consistent ipsilateral predominance in both control and stimulated animals, as also observed in our earlier dlPAG study, suggesting an intrinsic asymmetry in the distribution of FoxP2-expressing neurons. In the nRA, FoxP2-positive profiles were clearly present in numbers comparable to those observed in the semicompact formation and only moderately lower than those in the loose formation, a distribution consistent with the mixed cellular composition of this premotor nucleus and its involvement in shaping laryngeal and expiratory motor patterns. DMH/PeF stimulation did not modify FoxP2 expression in any subdivision, reinforcing the interpretation of FoxP2 as a stable phenotypic marker rather than an acute activity-dependent indicator.

Thus, under our experimental conditions, the present data indicate that activation of the DMH/PeF is associated with the recruitment of neuronal populations within specific subdivisions of the nA and the nRA. This pattern of activation is concurrent with the marked reduction in subglottic pressure and the tachypnoeic response observed in the physiological experiments, suggesting that these medullary populations may constitute part of the circuitry through which the DMH/PeF exerts its influence on laryngeal resistance and expiratory drive during the defence response. Taken together, our findings show that DMH/PeF activation is associated with the recruitment of the semicompact formation of the nA and premotor elements of the nRA, forming a coordinated pattern concurrent with the glottal opening and enhanced expiratory drive observed during defensive-like activation. Whether this association reflects direct anatomical projections from the DMH/PeF to these medullary targets or involves intermediate relays through pontine structures such as the PBc/KF and A5 region, remains to be established.

Interaction of DMH/PeF and nA: electrical/chemical stimulation experiments and subglottic pressure

Electrical stimulation was first used to delimit the location of the DMH/PeF and to evaluate its influence on subglottic pressure as an index of laryngeal patency. To verify that the effects were attributable to local hypothalamic neurons rather than fibres of passage, this approach was complemented with glutamate microinjections in a separate group of animals. The close similarity between electrical and chemical effects (tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypertension and a marked decrease in subglottic pressure) strongly indicates that the functional changes originated from neuronal populations intrinsic to the DMH/PeF. The magnitude of these responses was nearly identical across stimulation modalities, with arterial pressure being the only clear exception, reinforcing the reliability of the observed laryngeal and cardiorespiratory adjustments. This sympathoexcitatory profile is compatible with the neurogenic mechanisms underlying stress-associated cardiovascular activation described in recent analyses of the defence response (Fontes et al., 2023).

These findings align with a substantial body of evidence demonstrating that the DMH/PeF acts as a key hypothalamic node coordinating the autonomic and respiratory components of the defence response. Previous work from our group has shown that activation of this region recruits the parabrachial complex, the Kölliker–Fuse nucleus and the A5 region, and that glutamatergic transmission within these pontine areas critically shapes the cardiovascular and respiratory effects of DMH/PeF stimulation (Díaz-Casares et al., 2009; Díaz-Casares et al., 2012; López-González et al., 2013; López-González et al., 2018). The present results extend this framework by showing that DMH/PeF activation also produces a robust reduction in subglottic pressure, consistent with decreased laryngeal resistance and enhanced glottal opening. The concomitant recruitment of nA and nRA observed in the immunohistochemical analysis supports this interpretation, as both structures belong to medullary circuits controlling laryngeal and expiratory activity. This pattern is also in agreement with the hypothalamo-medullary organisation proposed for stress-induced cardiorespiratory integration, in which the DMH relays coordinated defensive commands to downstream autonomic and respiratory centres (Nakamura et al., 2022).

The decrease in subglottic pressure—the opposite of the rise in subglottic pressure and vocal-fold adduction required for phonation— is most parsimoniously interpreted as glottal dilation that lowers laryngeal resistance in the service of the tachypnoeic defence response, rather than as a vocal-motor event; it can therefore be understood as a laryngeal adaptation integrated within the classical hypothalamic defence pattern. During tachypnoea, descending pathways projecting from the DMH/PeF to pontine and medullary respiratory nuclei (PBc/KF, A5, nRA, and nA) may override the laryngeal adductor activity typically associated with vocalisation, promoting instead a state of increased glottal opening. This mechanism parallels the response observed in our recent dlPAG study (González-García et al., 2024), where mesencephalic stimulation produced a comparable decrease in subglottic pressure. Together, these observations suggest that hypothalamic and mesencephalic defensive circuits converge onto shared laryngeal premotor substrates to adjust airway patency according to the physiological demands of the defence response.

In summary, electrical and chemical stimulation of the DMH/PeF reveals a consistent and integrated activation of laryngeal and respiratory circuits, characterised by decreased subglottic pressure and enhanced respiratory drive. These results are consistent with the DMH/PeF playing a role in modulating neuronal activity within the nA and nRA during defence-like activation and suggest a potential contribution to the rapid ventilatory pattern and reduction in laryngeal resistance observed under these conditions. Future studies using anatomical tracing and selective circuit manipulation will be required to establish the causal pathways involved.

The ipsilateral predominance of c-Fos recruitment observed here merits explicit comment. The relayed descending pathway invoked in our interpretation —from the PAG to the nRA and onward to the nucleus ambiguus—is organised bilaterally and, in the tracing literature, with a contralateral predominance, so an ipsilateral bias is not its most obvious prediction. Direct projections from the DMH to the medulla, by contrast, although sparse, terminate in the region of the nucleus ambiguus and adjacent reticular formation, and these direct efferents are bilateral but markedly ipsilateral-predominant, being far less numerous contralaterally (ter Horst and Luiten, 1986; Thompson et al., 1996). The ipsilateral bias we observe may therefore reflect, at least in part, this sparse but ipsilaterally-weighted direct hypothalamo-medullary component and/or the unilateral location of the stimulation site in the right DMH/PeF, superimposed on the intrinsic ipsilateral asymmetry already present in control animals. Disentangling these contributions —direct versus relayed connectivity, and stimulus laterality versus a genuine circuit feature—will require anterograde and retrograde tract-tracing from the DMH/PeF, beyond the scope of the present study.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

First, c-Fos immunohistochemistry identifies patterns of cellular recruitment but does not establish direct synaptic connectivity, projection directionality, or causal involvement of specific neuronal populations. Accordingly, the anatomical associations observed between DMH/PeF activation and recruitment of nA and nRA subdivisions should be interpreted as functional correlations rather than definitive circuit relationships. Future studies combining tract tracing, selective pathway manipulation, or local pharmacological inactivation will be required to define the precise neural substrates underlying these laryngeal–respiratory responses.

The immunohistochemical component was based on a relatively small sample size (n = 5 per group), consistent with previous c-Fos mapping studies but limiting statistical power, particularly for smaller effects. In addition, multiple regional and cellular comparisons were performed without formal correction for multiple testing. Although these analyses were anatomically pre-specified, the possibility of type I error inflation cannot be excluded, and the findings should therefore be considered exploratory pending replication in larger cohorts.

Interpretation of the immunohistochemical data is further limited by the use of absolute rather than normalised cell counts and by the absence of a pan-neuronal marker such as NeuN. Consequently, the c-Fos+/ChAT− population cannot be classified unequivocally as neuronal, and a contribution from glial cells cannot be excluded. Moreover, the anatomical analysis was restricted to the nA and nRA and did not examine other medullary populations potentially involved in laryngeal control.

Finally, the physiological experiments used different post-stimulation observation windows for electrical and chemical activation, reflecting the distinct temporal dynamics of each modality. Differences between stimulation methods may therefore partly reflect the phase of the response sampled, and diffusion of glutamate beyond the injection site cannot be completely excluded. In addition, the three physiological arms (electrical stimulation, PBS and glutamate microinjection) were independent groups, each with its own resting baseline, and were not compared directly with one another; all statistical comparisons were restricted to within-arm (stimulation versus rest) or to PBS versus glutamate microinjections. The variability in resting values across cohorts is expected in this anaesthetised whole-animal preparation and was not statistically significant.
