Atopic Dermatitis and Climate: Environmental Stressors and Care Strategies
Authors: Filomena Russo, Alexander Zink, Nina Magnolo, Enrico Scala, Emanuele Scala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder with rising prevalence worldwide. Its pathogenesis involves a complex interplay between genetic predisposition, skin barrier dysfunction, and immune dysregulation. Environmental exposures, including allergens and pollutants, are well-recognized triggers of disease activity. Increasing attention is now being directed toward the role of climate, which may critically modulate AD onset and severity. This review examines climate-related stressors and their impact on AD, with a particular focus on extreme weather events, and outlines strategies for personalized management tailored to local climatic conditions.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Climate, Environmental stressors, Management

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disorder characterized by xerosis, intense pruritus, and eczematous lesions. It affects up to 20% of children and 5–10% of adults worldwide [1, 2]. Globally, AD ranks 15th in terms of disease burden and represents a core component of the “atopic triad,” alongside food allergy, allergic rhinitis, and asthma [3].

The clinical presentation varies with age. In early childhood, AD typically involves the face and subsequently flexural areas such as the inner elbows and popliteal fossae. In adults, lesions more often affect the head, neck, and shoulders. In both groups, the scalp, hands, and feet may be involved, frequently complicating differential diagnosis with other dermatoses, such as psoriasis [4].

Disease course is heterogeneous, ranging from transient flares to chronic, persistent activity. A genetically determined skin barrier defect—commonly associated with filaggrin (FLG) loss-of-function variants—initiates immune dysregulation [5]. This barrier dysfunction promotes the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and activates innate lymphoid and antigen-presenting cells, thereby amplifying Th2- and Th22-driven responses central to AD pathogenesis [3, 6].

Cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 further potentiate inflammation by recruiting eosinophils and mast cells and by inducing IL-31, a pivotal mediator of pruritus [3]. In parallel, they impair keratinocyte differentiation, leading to a thinner stratum corneum and reduced antimicrobial peptide production. The compromised barrier increases susceptibility to infections [3, 7, 8], clinically manifesting as enhanced transepidermal water loss (TEWL), increased sensitivity to irritants, and facilitated penetration of allergens, pollutants, and microbes [7]. Environmental exposure perpetuates itch and inflammation, fueling recurrent flare-ups that disrupt sleep and contribute to psychological comorbidities, including anxiety and mood disorders. This self-reinforcing cycle profoundly impairs quality of life [4, 9].

Epigenetic studies have further highlighted links between barrier dysfunction and environmental factors such as tobacco smoke, air pollution, microbial exposure, and dietary patterns [10]. Given the strong influence of external triggers, the interplay between climate and AD has recently gained increasing attention. The International Eczema Council reported that climate-related hazards linked to greenhouse gas emissions generally exacerbate AD, although effects vary across regions and baseline climate conditions [11].

Epidemiological evidence underscores this complexity. In Australia [12], Japan [13], and the USA [14], lower AD prevalence in children has been associated with regions of higher sun exposure, humidity, and warmer temperatures. Conversely, in Spain, higher prevalence was reported in colder, wetter regions with less sunlight [15]. Adding further nuance, a US prospective cohort study [16] linked greater sun exposure, higher humidity, and warmer temperatures to poorer disease control. Similar associations have been reported in Europe, Brazil, China, and additional US cohorts [16, 17].

Although AD remains more common in developed nations, incidence is rising in developing regions. For example, in Nigeria—where prolonged dry seasons are typical—AD was more frequently observed during the dry season, with infants being disproportionately affected [18]. Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted interplay between climate and AD, underscoring the need to incorporate environmental considerations into both prevention and clinical management.

This review examines how climate-related stressors modulate AD severity, focusing on the effects of extreme weather and proposing tailored care strategies adapted to local climatic conditions.

Environmental Stressors

Air pollution and climatic factors can worsen the clinical course and severity of AD, adding to its population burden. A recent meta-analysis of 42 studies across 14 countries found that elevated ambient pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and SO2) and extreme temperature, both hot and cold, were consistently linked to increased outpatient and emergency visits for AD. For instance, each 10 μg/m3 rise in PM10 corresponded to a 0.8% increase in AD-related visits (RR 1.008, 95% CI 1.003–1.012), with similar associations for PM2.5, SO2, and NO2. While the strength of evidence is moderate-to-high for most pollutants and temperature variations, findings for humidity, precipitation, and sunlight remain less consistent [19].

Mechanistically, air pollutants penetrate the epidermis as a result of their lipophilic properties, activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), inducing oxidative stress, impairing the skin barrier, increasing TEWL, and stimulating pro-inflammatory cytokine release [19–23]. Genetic predisposition, particularly FLG loss-of-function variants that impair barrier integrity, can markedly heighten an individual’s vulnerability to environmental stressors [20]. Large-scale epidemiological evidence from a US cross-sectional study demonstrated a strong association between PM2.5 exposure and AD (adjusted OR 2.38, 95% CI 2.12–2.67), even after adjusting for demographic and clinical confounders such as age, sex, race/ethnicity, income, urbanity, BMI, smoking, food allergy, allergic rhinitis, asthma, and eosinophilic esophagitis [24]. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference leaving the temporal sequence of events uncertain.

In recent years, climate change-driven extreme heat events and wildfires have further increased particulate exposure, exacerbating inflammation and triggering AD flares, with short-term exposure linked to increased healthcare utilization for AD and pruritus [25]. For instance, in patients with AD, itch may be exacerbated by rapid shifts in skin temperature (25–32 °C), exposure to noxious heat (45–49 °C), or harsh winter conditions that impair barrier function and heighten vulnerability to mechanical stress factors that ultimately perpetuate the itch–scratch cycle [26, 27]. Additionally, cold exposure (8–22 °C) contributes to AD pathogenesis by inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, activating TRP (transient receptor potential) channels in keratinocytes, and enhancing TEWL, further exacerbating itch and barrier dysfunction [28]. Notably, birth in the fall or winter is associated with a higher prevalence and risk of AD (OR 1.16 and 1.15, respectively), likely reflecting the combined influence of environmental stressors, temperature fluctuations, and early skin colonization by Staphylococcus aureus [29, 30]. Moreover, early-life exposure to low temperatures and reduced vapor pressure has been linked to an increased risk of AD by age 3 years, with the greatest risk observed for low vapor pressure alone or in combination with low mean or minimum temperatures (HR 1.26, p < 0.0001) [31]. Time-series analyses in Sweden further demonstrated that colder temperatures, lower humidity, and stronger winds are associated with increased AD-related healthcare visits and online search activity, highlighting the greater burden during cold, dry periods [32]. Similar findings across Europe and Asia consistently link winter months with higher AD incidence and exacerbations [27, 28, 33–35]. Accordingly, the International Eczema Council identifies cold, dry weather as a key environmental trigger, disproportionately affecting children and other vulnerable populations [11]. However, other environmental factors such as pollen, mold, and ultraviolet (UV) radiation also warrant attention. Pollen represents an additional climate-sensitive driver: rising temperatures and CO2 levels have lengthened pollen seasons, increased pollen production, and enhanced allergenicity. Pollutants such as ozone and particulate matter further boost pollen potency and penetration, intensifying allergic responses [36]. Although the link between pollen and AD is less established than for respiratory allergies, emerging evidence suggests that prolonged pollen exposure contributes to flares in sensitized patients [36–38]. Similarly, indoor mold growth, promoted by high humidity, may serve as a clinically relevant trigger [39, 40]. In the Northern Europe (RHINE) study, Wang et al. [41] reported that visible mold at home increased the risk of AD (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.12–1.62), with stronger effects observed following prolonged exposure. Thus, monitoring and reducing indoor mold growth should be considered in preventive strategies for AD.

Regarding UV radiation, UVA1 and narrowband UVB enhance antimicrobial peptide expression, reduce S. aureus superantigen production, and improve skin inflammation, providing the rationale for phototherapy [42]. Conversely, reduced UV exposure, such as that resulting from stratospheric ozone depletion, has been linked to a higher prevalence of AD [14]. Climate change, by affecting ozone levels and greenhouse gas emissions, may further influence this relationship [43]. In brief, fluctuations in UV exposure can exacerbate AD by reducing vitamin D synthesis, a key factor in immune regulation and skin barrier integrity. Furthermore, diminished UV exposure may impair photoimmunosuppression, thereby promoting inflammation and disease flare-ups [14].

Overall, evidence indicates that air pollution, temperature extremes, humidity changes, pollen exposure, and UV fluctuations collectively worsen AD. Although disentangling the individual impact contributions of these factors is challenging because of overlapping exposures, acknowledging their impact remains essential for patient management, preventive strategies, and informed therapeutic decision-making. Tropical Extreme Weather

Rising global temperatures drive extreme weather events, such as thunderstorms, hurricanes, and floods, that can worsen AD through multiple mechanisms including disrupted living conditions, increased allergen exposure, and higher pollution levels.

Thunderstorms, particularly during pollen season, can markedly exacerbate AD in sensitized individuals [36, 44, 45]. Rapid humidity shifts rupture pollen grains, releasing starch granules and cytoplasmic fragments capable of skin penetration. Simultaneously, strong winds suspend particulate matter, diesel exhaust, ozone, and nitrogen dioxide, which interact with pollen fragments, disrupt epithelial barriers, and enhance immune activation [46, 47]. Therefore, the combined exposure to allergens, pollutants, and indoor factors like tobacco smoke and mold further amplifies the risk and severity of AD flares [11, 21, 22, 48, 49].

Regional differences in sensitization modulate susceptibility: in a case–control study across Switzerland, Tanzania, and Madagascar, Swiss patients reacted to multiple grasses, whereas sub-Saharan participants showed minimal reactivity despite comparable prevalence of asthma and rhinoconjunctivitis, underscoring the importance of relevant regional allergens over total IgE levels [50].

In tropical and subtropical regions, hurricanes and associated floods create environments conducive to the proliferation of dust mites and molds. These release proteases, spores, and mycotoxins that can penetrate compromised skin, activate protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR-2), and amplify Th2-mediated inflammation, thereby exacerbating AD [51–53]. Accordingly, the World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes skin disease as the most common medical issue following major floods [37]. After the 2010 floods in Pakistan, 708,677 cases of skin disease—including AD—accounted for 19% of nearly four million consultations, exceeding the incidence of acute diarrhea and respiratory infections (14% each). Predominant pathogens included S. aureus and Streptococcus, highlighting the need for prompt topical or systemic therapy [37]. Similarly, in the aftermath of the 2004 Asian tsunami, an Indonesian hospital documented 265 new-onset dermatologic disorders among 235 patients, with infections/infestations (32.5%), eczemas (29.8%), and traumatic skin injuries (29.4%) predominating [38]. These observations underscore that floods not only exacerbate AD but also elevate the risk of secondary skin infections, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and timely management in disaster-affected populations.

Population studies in subtropical regions suggest that, even with current limited data, genetics alone does not fully determine disease occurrence. For instance, data from the Kyushu University Ishigaki AD Study (KIDS) cohort showed no increased prevalence of FLG mutations in patients with AD compared to controls [49]. Another critical consideration is that natural disasters impose substantial mental and physical stress, though their impact varies across populations. Chronic stress is known to exacerbate AD and can intensify the severity and perception of pruritus [54]. Disruptions to daily routines often compromise adherence to medical regimens while interruption or discontinuation of topical and systemic therapies further worsens skin symptoms. Moreover, living in temporary shelters or dusty environments increases exposure to aeroallergens, and limited access to water impairs hygiene, fostering infections and accumulation of human dander on lesional skin [55]. Collectively, these factors can contribute to AD exacerbation; therefore vulnerable populations such as children, the elderly, pregnant women, immunocompromised individuals, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups require special attention.

Geo-climatic Determinants of Allergen Exposure and Implications for Atopic Dermatitis

Geo-climatic conditions shape not only the distribution of aeroallergens but also their concentration and allergenic potency [56]. Historically, regional ecosystems defined distinct allergen spectra, reflecting local flora and fauna. Today, this balance is rapidly shifting: industrialization, urbanization, global warming, and biodiversity loss are reshaping allergen exposure worldwide [57]. Climate change and pollution, acting alone or synergistically, modify allergen abundance, intrinsic potency, and the occurrence of bio-contaminants, with clear consequences for allergic diseases, including AD [58]. Environmental drivers that amplify aeroallergen loads can aggravate skin barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation, contributing to AD onset and exacerbations.

Indoor allergens are more uniformly distributed than pollens, though local climate still plays a role. Molecular allergology has refined quantification of allergen loads in homes, schools, and workplaces, linking them to respiratory sensitization and chronic inflammation. House dust mites remain the most relevant indoor source. Their growth is promoted by humidity and suppressed by altitude and cold temperatures. Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus and Dermatophagoides farinae dominate globally, while Blomia tropicalis characterizes tropical and subtropical climates [59]. Beyond asthma and rhinitis, mite allergens are key triggers of AD, with epicutaneous sensitization contributing to severe and persistent phenotypes.

Cockroach allergens also contribute significantly. Blattella germanica is common in cooler, drier climates of Europe and North America, while Periplaneta americana is characteristic of warm and humid areas such as the Mediterranean, southern USA, Brazil, Taiwan, and Thailand [60]. Their major allergens, Bla g 2 and Bla g 5, are most abundant in kitchens, bedrooms, and schools [59]. In urban and socioeconomically disadvantaged settings, cockroach exposure is linked not only to asthma morbidity but also to AD worsening, highlighting the systemic impact of indoor aeroallergens [59].

Animal dander is another important determinant, with wide variation across regions. Pet ownership ranges from 5–10% of households in Spain to nearly 50% in the USA and up to 64% in New Zealand [61]. Allergen levels are higher when animals live exclusively indoors. For dogs, Can f 1 expression is similar across breeds, including so-called hypoallergenic ones. Cats represent an even greater source: Fel d 1 concentrations in homes are estimated to be 10–100 times higher than dust mite levels [61]. In sensitized individuals, exposure to dog and cat allergens can exacerbate AD via both skin contact and airborne routes.

Outdoor allergens, particularly pollens, are highly sensitive to geo-climatic determinants. Seasonal peaks depend on latitude and vegetation but are also influenced by humidity, wind, rainfall, and time of day [61]. Molecular aerobiology has shown that pollen counts alone do not reliably capture allergenic load, as allergen content per grain varies. This has been demonstrated for grasses (Phl p 5, Lol p 1) [62], birch (Bet v 1) [63], olive (Ole e 1) [64], and wall pellitory (Par j 1, Par j 2) [65]. Importantly, pollen sensitization may also worsen AD, as systemic immune activation during pollen seasons can trigger skin flares.

Certain pollens, such as grass, are globally widespread, while others are region-specific, shaping regional epidemiology of both respiratory and food allergies [56]. Japanese cedar pollen is a notable example, implicated in pollen-food allergy syndrome in Japan and more recently in Europe [66]. Cross-reactive pathways may also influence AD by amplifying cutaneous inflammation in sensitized patients.

Overall, climate, biodiversity and human activity converge to define allergen exposure indoors and outdoors. Beyond respiratory disease, aeroallergens play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and exacerbation of atopic dermatitis, linking skin, airway, and systemic allergic inflammation. Recognizing the regional variability and allergenic strength of exposures offers a critical lens through which prevention can be refined and precision strategies in allergy and dermatology care can be meaningfully advanced.

Care Strategies

Climate change is increasingly recognized as a driver of both incidence and severity of AD and other cutaneous diseases, including psoriasis, pemphigus, acne, melasma, and photoaging [43, 67]. First, patient education is critical to counter misinformation and promote behaviors that reduce exposure to climate-related triggers. Practical tools such as “climate-resilient emergency kits” with moisturizers, topical medications, and protective supplies may empower patients during extreme weather events. Strengthening the skin barrier through regular moisturization remains a cornerstone of AD care but becomes even more essential under environmental stress.

Interventions to improve indoor environments include high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, activated carbon filters, and mechanical ventilation systems to reduce exposure to allergens and pollutants. Humidifiers, though widely used, still lack robust evidence in AD management. Aeroallergens such as pollens play a key role in AD exacerbations [68]. While allergen immunotherapy (AIT) is well established for respiratory allergic diseases [69], emerging evidence suggests that patients with AD and pollen-driven flares might also benefit from AIT [70]. Digital tools, including air-quality and heat-index monitoring apps, may help patients anticipate risk. During heatwaves, behavioral measures—wearing lightweight clothing, avoiding antihistamines that impair thermoregulation in children and the elderly [71], and ensuring adequate hydration—are essential.

Psychological stress is an equally important driver. Extreme weather events and displacement increase rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, which in turn exacerbate itch and inflammation through neuroimmune pathways [72, 73]. Thus, holistic management integrating dermatological, psychological, and social care is warranted.

On a systemic level, healthcare systems and communities must strengthen climate resilience. This includes policies that mitigate greenhouse gas emissions, adaptation strategies tailored to local contexts, and protection of vulnerable populations such as migrants, refugees, and displaced individuals, who are disproportionately exposed to poor housing, overcrowding, mold, and poor ventilation [74–76]. International frameworks, including the Paris Agreement [77], highlight climate as a health challenge, reinforcing the need for coordinated global action.

Conclusions

AD is a multifactorial disorder shaped by genetic, immunologic, and environmental determinants. Yet the contribution of climate change and extreme weather remains underrecognized in routine practice. Rising temperatures, humidity fluctuations, and acute weather events exacerbate disease directly through barrier stress and indirectly through psychosocial strain.

Effective management of climate-related AD requires a shift from conventional pharmacotherapy alone to integrated strategies. These include patient education, avoidance of climate-related triggers, practical preparedness measures, and psychological support. Vulnerable populations—including socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, migrants, and displaced individuals—bear a disproportionate burden and should be prioritized.

Ultimately, a holistic approach is essential, combining dermatologic care with psychosocial interventions and supported by resilient healthcare systems and international collaboration. Dermatologists have a pivotal role in promoting climate health, tailoring management to local conditions, and advocating for global action. Only coordinated, patient-centered strategies can mitigate the impact of climate-related stressors on AD.
