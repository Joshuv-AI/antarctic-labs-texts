Epidemiology of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies: a population-based cohort in England, 2002–2021
Authors: Patrick Gordon, Michael McLean, Maciej Czachorowski, Jason Xenakis, Anna Barkaway, Saqib Rana, Serhan Bahit, Charlotte Curtis, Jennifer Hannah, James Galloway
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIdiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are a heterogenous group of conditions with substantial morbidity but population-based epidemiology is lacking. We aimed to provide a contemporary epidemiological assessment of IIM.MethodsPopulation-based study using the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum primary care database with linkage to hospital episode data. All patients (aged >2 years) from England registered between 1 March 2002 and 31 March 2021 were included. Incidence was estimated for three main IIM subtypes: dermatomyositis (DM), inclusion body myositis (IBM) and other IIM. Incidence rate ratios using Poisson models were compared across subgroups.ResultsWe identified n=1590 DM, n=432 IBM and n=2083 other IIM incident cases. Incidence of DM was 0.79 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.83), IBM 0.50 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.55) and other IIM 1.03 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.08) per 100 000 person-years.Females had a higher incidence of DM (adjusted incidence rate ratio (aIRR) 1.84, 95% CI 1.66 to 2.04) and other IIM (aIRR 1.09, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.19), but a lower incidence of IBM (aIRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.89), compared with males. South Asian, black, other and unknown ethnic groups had a higher incidence of DM and other IIM compared with those of white ethnicity, particularly individuals of black ethnicity for other IIM (aIRR 2.80, 95% CI 2.32 to 3.37).Point prevalence at study end was 0.015% for DM, 0.005% for IBM and 0.016% for other IIM.ConclusionThere are major demographic differences in IIM incidence, notably by sex and ethnicity. Resource planning should account for these marked differences.

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) are a broad group of chronic autoimmune conditions characterised by muscle inflammation (myositis), often accompanied by involvement of the skin, joints, lungs and heart.1 IIM subtypes have traditionally been classified based on clinical presentation and pathology findings.1 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification includes polymyositis (PM), inclusion body myositis (IBM), amyopathic dermatomyositis and dermatomyositis (DM), with juvenile DM and juvenile myositis other than juvenile DM.2 However, it is recognised that the EULAR/ACR classification needs to be updated,3 as advances in diagnostic testing, particularly the identification of myositis-specific autoantibodies, have improved understanding of IIM mechanisms and further highlighted the heterogeneity across IIM subgroups.4

Collectively, IIM is relatively rare, with a prevalence between two and 25 per 100 000, and there is only limited understanding of the epidemiology of IIM subtypes across population subgroups, particularly ethnic differences.5 Although rare, IIM is associated with increased mortality6 7 and a high burden of serious comorbidities, including interstitial lung disease (ILD),8 9 malignancy,1012 cardiovascular disease1315 and infections,16 17 leading to substantial healthcare needs.1820 There are marked differences both in terms of the organs involved and the prognosis by IIM subtypes. For example, DM is associated with skin disease and a higher incidence of cancer-associated myositis.10 21 IBM is characterised by distal and proximal muscle weakness, with a progressive loss of function,2224 and is refractory to immunosuppressive therapy.25 Detailed, contemporary epidemiology of IIM across these subtypes is important for informing healthcare planning and for identifying high-risk subgroups. Since IIM is a rare condition, research has frequently relied on clinic cohorts or clinical trial populations, which may not fully reflect the broader spectrum of affected individuals. This has made it difficult to estimate the total burden of IIM or recognise differences in risk across population subgroups. To date, trends in IIM incidence have been inconsistent. A study from the USA reported a rising incidence,26 whereas other population-based studies from the USA and South Korea have suggested that incidence has fluctuated over time.27 28 However, these estimates are over a decade old, highlighting the need for contemporary epidemiological data.

The rarity of IIM also makes it more difficult to have sufficient case numbers to assess risk across population subgroups, especially by IIM subtypes. While it is established that the risk of IIM increases from midlife and is generally higher in women,6 26 29 there is a lack of detailed epidemiology for other sociodemographic groups, particularly for ethnic groups. A recent analysis using secondary care data in England reported a higher incidence of overall IIM among individuals of Asian, black and other ethnic groups compared with those of white ethnicity.6 Mortality rates were higher among male patients, individuals experiencing greater socioeconomic deprivation and those of white ethnicity,6 suggesting these factors may be important predictors of risk. However, to date it is not known how risk differs for IIM subtypes across these key population subgroups. Furthermore, research in other serious chronic conditions has shown that combining linked primary and secondary care data is required to more accurately capture total disease burden.30 31

In a large, population-based study, we aimed to investigate the age and sex distribution of IIM subtypes and differences across key sociodemographic subgroups, using linked data from both primary and secondary care in England.

Methods

Study population and data linkage

We used primary care data obtained from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum database combined with linked secondary care and other area-based datasets. CPRD contains routinely collected anonymised electronic healthcare records from over 1300 primary care general practices, and is broadly representative of the UK population.32 33 For the version of the dataset used for the study, coverage for currently active patients was 24.21% of the UK population.34 Children (aged >2 years) and adults from England actively registered in the CPRD Aurum database at any point between 1 March 2002 and 31 March 2021, eligible for patient-level linkage to secondary care data from National Health Service (NHS) England Hospital Episode Statistics35 and Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) data,36 were eligible to be included. Linkage of patient-level data was carried out by NHS Digital, a trusted third party, which also cleaned records to remove duplicates. Cleaned, merged, de-identified data were used to generate the final dataset, which was then released to the investigators for the approved study.37 Individuals were excluded prior to data extraction if they opted out of data sharing or if their data failed to pass data quality checks.

Definition of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy subtypes

Individuals with IIM were identified and classified into IIM subtypes using a hierarchical algorithm combining secondary care codes38 with primary care codes, which were developed in conjunction with clinicians (full code lists available)39 (figure 1). We also included patients with ‘unspecified’ myositis, if they also had a clinical code for ILD,38 as ILD occurs in around 40% of patients with IIM40 but is rare in the general population with a prevalence of less than one in 10 000 in European countries.41

First, cases were classified as IBM based on secondary care codes, followed by IBM primary care codes. IBM was assigned only when the age at onset was ≥45 years,42 as symptom onset before this age is uncommon.43 44 Next, DM cases were identified from the remaining cases using secondary care codes, followed by primary care codes, with cases defined as juvenile DM if the age of onset was <18 years. Remaining cases were classified as ‘other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies’ or juvenile other IIM if the age of onset was <18 years. The other IIM cascade was as follows: PM in secondary care, other myositis in secondary care, PM in primary care, myositis unspecified in secondary care, myositis unspecified in primary care (myositis unspecified cases were included only if patients also had a record of IIM-associated ILD in either primary or secondary care) and, finally, inflammatory myopathies in primary care (full code lists available).39

The date of IIM identification was set at the earliest indication of IIM across the entire secondary or primary care record. New-onset incident cases were identified as individuals with a new-onset IIM identified during the study period. To ensure accurate identification of incident cases, individuals were excluded from the incident cohort if their diagnosis occurred within 6 months of registering with the practice. Individuals with <12 months follow-up were excluded from the incident and prevalent cohorts. Prevalent cases were defined as all actively registered individuals classified with DM, IBM or other IIM at the end of the study period.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics evaluated consisted of sex, age group (2–17, 18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79 and 80+ years), ethnicity, socioeconomic deprivation and geographic region. Ethnic group was based on major UK ethnic group classifications: white, South Asian, black, other and mixed.45 46 Ethnicity was derived from recoded clinical codes recorded in primary care records, where it is most commonly self-reported at patient registration. Where ethnicity data were unavailable, including where patients had chosen not to disclose their ethnicity, individuals were classified as ‘unknown’. Socioeconomic deprivation was classified as IMD based on the deprivation classification for small areas, assigned based on patient postcode areas at the point of data extraction (classified in quintile of IMD with 1=least to 5=most deprived).36 Geographic region within England comprised North East, North West, Yorkshire and The Humber, East Midlands, West Midlands, East of England, London, South Central and South West.33

Statistical analyses

Primary analysis included incidence and prevalence of IIM overall and for each IIM subtype. Incidence rates were calculated by dividing the number of incident cases by the sum of person-years of follow-up for the total eligible population over the study period. Incidence rates by sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, IMD quintile and geographical region) were presented, as well as incidence rate ratios (IRR) using multivariable Poisson regression, adjusted for sex, age group, deprivation, ethnicity, region and follow-up time. Temporal trends in IIM incidence were assessed across 5-year intervals over the 19-year study period using age-standardised models to account for demographic changes to the population.

Point prevalence was estimated at the end of the study period (31 March 2021) by dividing the total number of prevalent cases on this date by the total number of actively registered people in the CPRD database on the same date.

The missing indicator method was employed for categorical variables with missing values as covariates for model adjustment. All statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.5.0. The study was conducted and reported in line with the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Data (RECORD) guidelines.47

Results

Incidence cohort

During the study period (1 March 2002 to 31 March 2021), a total of 4105 individuals with incident IIM were identified, including 1590 with DM, 432 with IBM and 2083 with other IIM (table 1, online supplemental figure s1). Of those classified as other IIM, more than half were PM (54.2%), with the remainder classified as other myositis (27.8%), myositis unspecified (11.5%) or inflammatory myopathy (6.5%). Median age at diagnosis for DM was 56.2 years (IQR 40.0, 68.9), 68.6 years (IQR 60.3, 75.4) for IBM and 60 years for other IIM (IQR 43.0, 72.8) (table 1).

Incidence of DM, IBM and other IIM

Overall incidence over the study period for DM was 0.79 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.83), IBM 0.50 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.55) and other IIM 1.03 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.08) per 100 000 person-years (online supplemental tables s1-s3). The incidence of DM, IBM and other IIM increased substantially over the study period (online supplemental tables s4-s6, online supplemental figure s2); however, after age standardisation, the incidence remained broadly similar across the 19-year study period for all IIM subtypes (online supplemental figure s3).

There were distinct differences in incidence between DM, IBM and other IIM across the lifespan and between male and female patients (figure 2). Incidence increased with age from adulthood onwards. There were distinct differences in IIM risk by sex across the lifespan. For female patients, there was a greater risk of DM and a slightly higher risk for other IIM, whereas the risk for IBM was higher in male patients (figure 2). In the fully adjusted models, there were distinct differences in those most at risk for the different IIM subtypes. Female patients had a higher incidence of DM compared with male patients (adjusted IRR (aIRR) 1.84, 95% CI 1.66 to 2.04) (figure 3, online supplemental table s1). There were also distinct differences in the risk of DM across ethnic groups, with those of South Asian (aIRR 1.54, 95% CI 1.26 to 1.89) and black (aIRR 1.62, 95% CI 1.26 to 2.09) ethnicity having a higher incidence compared with those of white ethnicity (reference group). The incidence of DM did not differ across deprivation quintiles (figure 3, online supplemental table s1).

In contrast, incidence of IBM was lower in female patients compared with male patients (aIRR 0.73, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.89) (figure 3, online supplemental table s2). IBM incidence did not differ across ethnic groups (figure 3, online supplemental table s2), but incidence was lower in those living in more deprived areas (IMD quintile 3: aIRR 0.75, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.99; IMD quintile 4: aIRR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55 to 1.00 and IMD quintile 5: aIRR 0.57, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.81) compared with less deprived areas (quintile 1; reference group) (figure 3, online supplemental table s2).

Incidence of other IIM was higher in female patients compared with male patients (aIRR 1.09, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.19) (figure 3, online supplemental table s3). Risk of other IIM differed substantially across ethnic groups, with those of South Asian (aIRR 1.73, 95% CI 1.45 to 2.07), black (aIRR 2.80, 95% CI 2.32 to 3.37) and mixed ethnic groups (aIRR 1.86, 95% CI 1.28 to 2.70) compared with those of white ethnicity (reference group) (figure 3, online supplemental table s3). There was no difference in incidence of other IIM across deprivation quintiles (figure 3, online supplemental table s3).

Prevalent IIM and IIM subtypes

At the end of the study period (31 March 2021), 3810 prevalent IIM cases were identified within CPRD, comprising 1730 individuals with DM, 261 with IBM and 1819 with other IIM (online supplemental figure s4, online supplemental table s7). Overall point prevalence for DM was 0.015% (95% CI 0.015 to 0.016), IBM was 0.005% (95% CI 0.005 to 0.006) and other IIM was 0.016% (95% CI 0.015 to 0.017) (online supplemental tables s8-s10, online supplemental figure s5).

Prevalence of DM, IBM and other IIM by patient characteristics

In similar findings to the incident cohort, point prevalence of DM and other IIM was higher in female patients (online supplemental tables s8 and s10 and online supplemental figures s6 and s8). The age patterns for prevalent cases were also similar to the incidence cohort, with prevalence for DM, IBM and other IIM increasing with age, with a peak prevalence in the 70–79 age group for DM (online supplemental tables s8-s10 and online supplemental figures s6-s8). Those of black ethnicity had the highest prevalence of DM and other IIM (online supplemental tables s8 and s10, online supplemental figures s6 and s8).

Discussion

Our study provides a contemporary description of the epidemiology of IIM subtypes using unselected population-based data. IIM incidence has increased substantially in the last 20 years for all subtypes. This is likely predominantly due to population ageing rather than changing disease incidence, as the age-standardised incidence has remained fairly constant over the same time period. Our principle-based algorithm combined both primary and secondary care data, demonstrating heterogeneity in IIM subtype risk, particularly across sex and ethnicity. Among female patients, the relative incidence of DM was markedly higher (84% higher incidence), while the incidence of other IIM was slightly increased (9%) and the incidence of IBM was lower (27%) compared with male patients. We also observed substantial variation in the incidence of IIM subtype across UK ethnic groups. DM incidence was higher among individuals of South Asian (54% higher incidence) and black ethnicity (62% higher incidence) compared with those of white ethnicity. Other IIM incidence was also higher among those of South Asian (73%) and mixed ethnic groups (86%) and was particularly increased in those of black ethnicity (180%) compared with those of white ethnicity. While we found no evidence of ethnic differences in the incidence of IBM, the incidence was lower in those experiencing the greatest deprivation (43%) compared with those experiencing the least deprivation.

Few studies have provided IIM subtype-specific incidence estimates, and none in the past decade. Where subtype data exist, classification varies between studies, particularly in respect of PM or other IIM, making direct comparisons difficult. However, our incidence estimates for DM are within the range reported by earlier international studies.2629 There has been little population-based data on IBM incidence and our incidence estimates for IBM were greater than those observed in Sweden.29 In terms of sex differences across IIM subtypes, our finding of a higher DM incidence among female patients was also observed in Sweden,29 consistent with higher overall IIM incidence in female patients.62628 This also fits with wider sex-based patterns of greater risk for other autoimmune conditions in female patients.48 Similarly, our finding of higher IBM incidence in male patients also aligns with data from Sweden.29 Notably, no previous epidemiological studies have reported ethnic differences between IIM subtypes. A prior analysis using secondary care data in England found higher overall IIM incidence among those of Asian, black and other ethnicities, particularly among those of black ethnicity, suggesting ethnicity is an important factor in IIM risk; however, this study did not report IIM subtype-specific results.6 Ethnicity has previously been shown to be an important factor in risk of systemic lupus erythematosus within the UK.49 However, it should be noted that ethnicity was not known for around one-fifth of the study population, and it is not possible to disentangle the influence of potential genetic susceptibility from other factors, as ethnicity encompasses environmental and societal differences as well as factors influencing access to healthcare.

The large sample size of our study is a major strength, enabling robust incidence and prevalence estimates across IIM subtypes and by key population subgroups. It should be noted that ethnicity information was not known for around one-fifth of IIM cases, although these data may not be missing at random. Use of a population-based primary care dataset supports generalisability to the wider UK population, but these findings may not be generalisable to other countries, particularly given the ethnic differences we observed. The large sample size also allowed cases and controls to be matched on sex, age, ethnicity and deprivation quintile, although residual confounding can remain and we did not include adjustment for other comorbidities or treatments.

As with all observational studies using routinely collected medical records, our findings rely on accurate and timely coding, which may vary between primary care practices. A further limitation was the absence of validated algorithms for the identification of IIM subtypes in routinely collected primary care data. To address this, we developed a cascading case identification algorithm, in conjunction with clinical experts, which used both validated secondary care codes38 and primary care records to optimise sensitivity and specificity. For myositis unspecified, we additionally required coded evidence of ILD, which is commonly observed in patients with IIM.6 50 Despite these measures, it is a limitation that the other IIM group remains heterogeneous and could include polymyositis, unspecified myositis, overlap myositis as well as those with antisynthetase syndrome and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy. We were also unable to explore the influence of more detailed clinical factors, such as autoantibody profiles. Our study is considerably strengthened by the use of combined primary care and linked secondary care. Previous work has shown that primary and secondary care records do not completely overlap, and the use of linked data in this way has been shown to be extremely important for assessing the burden of other serious conditions, such as pneumonia,30 heart failure31 and venous thromboembolism.51 However, it is a limitation that primary care code lists and our algorithm have not been validated. Further research validating the use of routinely collected primary care electronic medical records for the identification of IIM, including IIM subtypes, would be an important area for further study. Future research using more detailed clinical factors could provide further insights into changes in recognition, management and coding practices.

Our findings provide a detailed, large-scale, contemporary assessment of IIM epidemiology to understand the burden of IIM subtypes across key subgroups. Notably, we demonstrate that there are clear differences in the risk of DM, IBM and other IIM across ethnic groups. While the lower IBM incidence observed in those experiencing the greatest socioeconomic deprivation raises concerns about possible under-recognition. Collectively, these marked differences in the risk of IIM subtypes between ethnic and sociodemographic groups may reflect underlying aetiological differences, as well as potential disparities in accessing healthcare. Further research is needed to understand these differences in terms of aetiology, and to identify any barriers for diagnosis and management, to ensure patients can receive the correct support. This may have important implications for healthcare planning given population changes.

Conclusions

This study provides the first population-based study to characterise the epidemiology of IIM subtypes across key sociodemographic groups. There are major demographic differences in IIM incidence, particularly by sex and ethnicity, including a higher DM and other IIM incidence in females compared with males, higher DM and other IIM incidence in those of South Asian and black ethnicity compared with white ethnicity and lower IBM incidence in females compared with males. Resource planning should account for these marked differences.
