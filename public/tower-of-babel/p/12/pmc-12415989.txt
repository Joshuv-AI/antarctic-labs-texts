Estrogenic regulation of perineuronal nets in the mouse insular cortex and hippocampus
Authors: Rachel Nguyen, Razia Rahyab, Ashna Deshpande, Emily Legge, Jonathas Almeida, Sara M Herz, Alexia L Zylko, M Imad Damaj, Amy W Lasek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstrogen has profound effects on the brain, affecting neuronal plasticity and behavior. Perineuronal nets (PNNs) are perforated extracellular matrix structures that mostly surround parvalbumin (PV)-expressing inhibitory interneurons and regulate neuronal activity, synaptic plasticity and behavior. PNNs have sex-specific effects on behavior, suggesting that hormones like estrogen may be involved in these sex differences. In this study, we investigated the effects of estrous cycle and estrogen on PNNs in the hippocampus and insular cortex of female mice. PNNs and PV protein were detected by fluorescence labeling with the plant lectin WFA and PV immunostaining, respectively, throughout the estrous cycle, following treatment with estradiol, and after chronic administration of the aromatase inhibitor letrozole to block estrogen synthesis. PNN labeling was highest during estrus and bidirectionally modulated by estradiol levels, with estradiol decreasing, and letrozole increasing, PNN labeling intensity. To interrogate potential mechanisms of estrogenic regulation of PNNs, we used RNAScope to probe the estrogen receptors Esr1 and Esr2, and Cyp19a1, encoding aromatase, in PV neurons during estrus and diestrus. In insular PV neurons, Esr2 was elevated during estrus, and in hippocampal PV neurons, Cyp19a1 was elevated during diestrus. Finally, expression of genes encoding matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs/ADAMTSs) and their endogenous inhibitors (TIMPs) were examined by qPCR following letrozole treatment, with expression changes observed in both the insula and hippocampus. Together, these results indicate that PNN accumulation and degradation in females are regulated by estrogen with distinct region-specific mechanisms contributing to PNN structural changes by estrogen.Keywords: Estrogen, Estrous cycle, Estrogen receptors, Extracellular matrix, Matrix metalloproteinases, Perineuronal nets, Parvalbumin

Introduction

The steroid hormone 17β-estradiol (E2) acts in the brain to shape adaptive behaviors essential for survival. E2 enhances spatial learning and memory by acting at estrogen receptors (ERs) in the hippocampus (Taxier et al., 2020). E2 also modulates mood, drug use, and risk-taking behavior (Borrow and Handa, 2017; Finn, 2023; Kundakovic and Rocks, 2022; Orsini et al., 2021; Quigley et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023); in female rodents, high levels of E2 are generally associated with decreased depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors (Borrow and Handa, 2017; Kundakovic and Rocks, 2022) and risk taking (Orsini et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023). Because E2 promotes optimal brain functioning in females, fluctuations or rapid reductions in E2 can contribute to psychiatric disorders in at-risk individuals (Peters et al., 2025; Schiller et al., 2016). Women have a higher incidence of mood and anxiety disorders than men (McGrath et al., 2023), which may be the result of alterations in brain E2 levels (Page and Coutellier, 2019; Wellman et al., 2018).

Fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons that express the calcium-binding protein parvalbumin (PV) regulate the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission by synapsing onto and tightly controlling the activity of excitatory neurons in the cortex and hippocampus (Cardin, 2018; Wingert and Sorg, 2021). The delicate balance between excitation and inhibition is critical for higher-order cognitive functioning during learning and memory tasks (Donato et al., 2013, 2015; Ferguson and Gao, 2018; Ognjanovski et al., 2017) and mood regulation (Page and Coutellier, 2019). When comparing individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder to healthy controls, differentially expressed genes in dorsolateral prefrontal cortical PV neurons were significantly associated with depression in women (Maitra et al., 2023). Cortical and hippocampal PV neurons in females respond to E2 and express ERs and aromatase, the biosynthetic enzyme that converts testosterone to E2 (Clemens et al., 2019; Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022, 2025; Kritzer, 2002, 2006; Simpson et al., 1994). Thus, E2 regulation of PV neuron activity is likely involved in both the normal function of PV neurons in promoting adaptive behaviors and their impairment in psychiatric disorders in females.

The majority of PV neurons in the cortex and hippocampus are surrounded by extracellular matrix (ECM) structures known as perineuronal nets (PNNs). These condensed ECM structures are enriched in chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, hyaluronan, and link proteins (Fawcett et al., 2019). PNNs form around PV neurons during the closure of critical periods of plasticity, stabilize mature synapses, restrict new synapse formation and regulate the intrinsic excitability of PV neurons (Balmer, 2016; Carulli et al., 2010; Dityatev et al., 2007; Favuzzi et al., 2017; Pizzorusso et al., 2002; Tewari et al., 2018). In rodents, PNN disruption in various brain regions affects a variety of learning and memory processes (Alexander et al., 2025; Dubisova et al., 2022; Gogolla et al., 2009; Ramsaran et al., 2023; Sanchez et al., 2023; Thompson et al., 2018), as well drug- and alcohol-seeking behavior (Blacktop et al., 2017; Chen and Lasek, 2020; Lubbers et al., 2016; Martins de Carvalho et al., 2023; Slaker et al., 2015; Xue et al., 2014) and stress-induced depressive and anxiety-related behaviors (Coutens et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Riga et al., 2017; Simard et al., 2018; Smail et al., 2023).

Studies have shown that in female mice, more PV neurons are surrounded by PNNs during estrus than diestrus in the ventral dentate gyrus region of the hippocampus (Laham et al., 2022), and that the fluorescence labeling intensity of PNNs on PV neurons is increased in proestrus compared to diestrus in the dorsal CA1 region of the hippocampus (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2025). These results indicate that ovarian hormones contribute to fluctuations in hippocampal PNNs throughout the estrous cycle. In addition, both gonadally-intact and ovariectomized female mice treated with the aromatase inhibitor letrozole exhibit increased PNN fluorescence labeling intensity on PV neurons in the dorsal hippocampal CA1 region, suggesting that brain-derived E2 can modulate PNN structure (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022).

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that E2 may regulate the structure and/or number of PNNs not only in the hippocampus but also cortical regions of female mice. To test this hypothesis, we examined the intensity and density (i.e. cell numbers) of fluorescence-labeled PNNs using the lectin Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA) in both the insular cortex and dorsal hippocampal (dHPC) CA1 region of female mice. The insular cortex was chosen as a region of interest because it is involved in sex differences in risk-taking (Korucuoglu et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2023), a behavior that is sensitive to E2 (Orsini et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023), and because PNNs in the insula contribute to sex differences in compulsive-like alcohol drinking (Martins de Carvalho et al., 2023). PNNs were examined throughout the estrous cycle, to determine the contribution of cycling ovarian hormones, and in ovariectomized mice treated with E2 or letrozole, to assess the effects of exogenous and endogenous E2, respectively, on PNNs. We also performed fluorescence in situ hybridization to determine if genes encoding ERs and aromatase are expressed in insular cortex and dHPC CA1 PV neurons and to test if their expression changes during the estrous cycle. Finally, to begin to understand how E2 might regulate changes in PNNs, we measured transcript levels of genes encoding chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (PNN structural components such as aggrecan, brevican, phosphacan, and tenascin R), the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs and ADAMTSs) and their endogenous inhibitors (TIMPs), in the insula and hippocampus of ovariectomized mice treated with letrozole. Our results demonstrate that the fluorescence staining intensity of PNNs in the hippocampus and insular cortex is sensitive to changes in ovarian state and brain E2 levels, suggesting that one mechanism by which E2 affects behavior is through modulation of PNN structure and the plasticity of PV neurons.

Materials and methods

Animals

Female C57BL/6J mice (strain #000664) at 8 weeks of age were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and used for experiments at 10–14 weeks of age. Mice were housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled room with 3–4 mice per cage on a 12 h light-dark cycle (lights on at 6 a.m.). All experiments were conducted during the light cycle. Food (7012 Teklad LM-485) and water were provided ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the VCU Institutional Animal Care and Use Program and adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Chemicals and antibodies

17β-estradiol-3-benzoate (EB) and letrozole were purchased from Millipore Sigma. Carbo-free blocking solution, biotinylated Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA), and streptavidin DyLight 488 were purchased from Vector Laboratories (Newark, CA, USA). Normal donkey serum was purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA, USA). Mouse anti-parvalbumin (PV) antibody (#195011) was purchased from Synaptic Systems (Goettingen, Germany). PV antibody specificity was tested by Synaptic Systems using the immunogen (full-length rat parvalbumin) in pre-adsorption experiments in western blots. Donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa-Fluor Plus 594 (#A-21203) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Fluoromount G mounting medium was purchased from Southern Biotech (Birmingham, AL, USA). RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 and probes for mouse Pvalb (#421931), Esr1 (#478201-C3), Esr2 (#316121-C2), and Cyp19a1 (#430821-C2) were purchased from Advanced Cell Diagnostics (Newark, CA, USA). Opal 520, 620, and 690 reagent packs were purchased from Akoya Biosciences (Marlborough, MA, USA).

Estrous cycle staging

Estrous cycle staging started when the mice were 10 weeks old. Vaginal smears were obtained once daily for 14–16 consecutive days prior to euthanasia. Vaginal smears were collected using a sterile cotton swab dampened with water. The cotton swabs were carefully rubbed on the vaginal orifice. The cotton swab was then rolled across a glass microscope slide and immediately imaged using an EVOS M5000 fluorescence microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific) at 10× magnification. Estrous cycle stage was recorded based on cellular characteristics described previously (Satta et al., 2018; Vandegrift et al., 2017).

Ovariectomy

Mice were anesthetized with either intraperitoneal (IP) injections of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (8 mg/kg) or isoflurane inhalation. Hair on the dorsal flanks or abdomen was trimmed and the skin was disinfected. Bilateral incisions were made in the dorsal flanks, and a small hole was teased in the muscle wall to allow access to the abdomen. In some animals, ovaries were accessed instead through a medial abdominal incision. The ovaries and associated fat pads were dissected away from the uterine horns. The muscle wall and skin were sutured separately. Mice received a subcutaneous (SC) injection of 5 mg/kg meloxicam immediately after surgery and once per day for two additional days for analgesia. Mice recovered for 2 weeks prior to beginning drug treatments.

Drug treatments

For EB treatment, 0.2 μg EB in 50 μl (~10 μg/kg) was injected subcutaneously on the first 2 days and 1 μg of EB in 50 μl (~50 μg/kg) on the third day, 1 h prior to euthanasia. Mice were treated with equal volumes of 90 % sesame oil and 10 % ethanol vehicle (VEH). These doses and timing of EB treatment were chosen because they result in increasing EB plasma levels that peak at levels comparable to proestrus (Vandegrift et al., 2017). For chronic letrozole treatment, mice were treated daily by oral gavage with 0.5 mg/kg letrozole (Sigma-Aldrich) in 30 % hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin in 0.9 % NaCl VEH for 14 days and then daily from Mon-Fri for an additional 12 days, for a total of 26 treatments.

Tissue collection

To immunostain brain sections with WFA and PV antibody, mice were perfused with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and 4 % paraformaldehyde immediately following an intraperitoneal injection of a lethal dose of euthanasia solution containing pentobarbital. The brains were post-fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then transferred to 30 % sucrose solution for 48 h until they sunk to the bottom of the tube. Brains were snap frozen with chilled isopentane. For RNAScope in situ hybridization and brain tissue dissections, mice were rapidly decapitated without anesthesia. Brains were removed, rinsed with PBS and either rapidly frozen on dry ice for RNAScope, or sectioned into 1 mm-thick sections for dissections using razor blades and a stainless steel brain matrix (Zivic Instruments, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The insula (~1.7–0.7 mm anterior to bregma) and dorsal hippocampus (dHPC, ~1.2–2.2 mm posterior to bregma) were quickly dissected from sections using a razor blade and then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Whole brains and dissected tissue were stored at −80 °C.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Frozen brains were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned to 40 μm using a cryostat. Four to five random coronal sections from each brain region were collected from each mouse, spanning 1.7 to 1.0 mm anterior to bregma for the anterior insula and spanning −1.2 to −2.2 mm posterior to bregma for the dHPC. Free-floating sections were treated with 50 % ethanol twice for 10 min, washed with PBS, and then incubated in 1X carbo-free blocking solution, 5 % donkey serum, and 0.25 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were incubated with biotinylated WFA (1:1000 dilution) to stain PNNs and PV antibody (1:1000 dilution) in 1X carbo-free blocking solution, 2 % normal donkey serum, 0.25 % Triton-X-100 in PBS for ~48 h at 4 °C. Sections were washed with PBS containing 0.3 % Triton X-100 and then incubated with a secondary antibody containing 1X carbo-free blocking solution, 2 % normal donkey serum, 0.25 % Triton-X-100, streptavidin DyLight 488 (1:400 dilution), and donkey anti-mouse Alexa-Fluor 594 secondary antibody (1:400 dilution) in PBS for 3 h at 4 °C. Sections were rinsed with PBS and mounted onto slides with Fluromount-G mounting medium.

RNAScope in situ hybridization

RNAScope was performed using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 and probes from ACD as per manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, frozen brains were sectioned to 10 μm using a cryostat and mounted onto Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific). Sections were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 1 h at 4 °C and then dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol (50, 70, and 100 %) for 5 min at each concentration. Sections were treated with hydrogen peroxide, followed by RNAScope protease IV. Probes were hybridized and signals amplified as indicated in the manufacturer’s instructions. Opal 520, 620, and 690 fluorophores (diluted 1:750) were used to visualize probes in channels 1, 2, and 3, respectively. DAPI was used for counterstaining nuclei. Coverslips were applied with ProLong Gold antifade mounting medium (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Image acquisition and analysis

Images of WFA and PV antibody fluorescence were acquired using the EVOS M5000 fluorescence microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific) using 10X and 20X objectives and the GFP and Texas Red light cubes. In the dHPC, 10X images were obtained of the stratum oriens and CA1 regions for cell counts and 20X Z-stack images (step size 0.8 μm, 12 images per stack, combined into a single projection image) were obtained for measuring fluorescence intensities of individual cells. Cell counts and fluorescence intensities in the insula were obtained from 20X Z-stack images combined into a single projection image. Light and exposure settings were kept constant for each image to allow for a comparison of fluorescence intensity between images. Images were acquired from 4–5 sections per mouse from each hemisphere (7–10 images per mouse) and analyzed for cell counts and WFA and PV fluorescence intensities around individual cells using ROIs identified with Polygon AI software [Rewire Neuro, Portland, OR, USA; previously known as PIP-SQUEAK, based on ImageJ (Slaker et al., 2016)] with background subtraction, 75 % detection sensitivity and 25 % overlap removal; ROIs for WFA and PV were manually drawn if AI missed cells that were obviously positive. The background intensity (threshold) was determined by Polygon AI, which drew 22 uniformly distributed ROIs around the perimeter of the image. After background ROIs were selected, the rolling ball algorithm settings were finalized and the rolling ball smoothing was performed across the entire image. Background subtraction was done by discarding the brightest 33 % and dimmest 33 % of ROIs in the background samples. The mean pixel intensity and standard deviation was calculated across the ROIs selected for background sampling. These values were then used to set all pixels that were dimmer than the calculated mean two standard deviations to be Not a Number (NaN). The mean fluorescence intensity of WFA and PV ROIs minus background was averaged and plotted for each mouse in addition to violin plots representing the fluorescence intensities of all cells. Analysis was blind to experimental conditions.

For RNAScope, images were acquired at 40X magnification on the EVOS M5000 fluorescence microscope using the DAPI, GFP, Texas Red, and Cy5 light cubes. Three images from independent sections were taken from each mouse (4 in estrus and 4 in diestrus for dHPC; 4 in estrus and 3 in diestrus for insula) for a total of 12 images from mice in estrus and 9–12 images from mice in diestrus. Cell counts and colocalization of Esr1, Esr2, and Cyp19a1 probes within Pvalb-positive cells were performed using QuPath software (open source, version 0.5.1).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) followed by purification using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher), and quantitative real-time qPCR was performed using the SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method by first subtracting the geometric mean of the Ct of three reference genes (Gusb, Hprt, and Rpl13a) from the Ct of the sample to obtain the ΔCt. The ΔΔCt was calculated by subtracting the average ΔCt of the VEH-treated mice from the ΔCt of each sample.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism software (version 10, GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). For each experiment, WFA and PV fluorescence intensity data from two separate cohorts of mice were obtained and normalized within each cohort by dividing the mean value of each mouse by the average intensity of mice in estrus or VEH-treated mice, or by dividing the value of each cell by the average intensity of all cells. Data for WFA and PV fluorescence intensities were plotted as two graphs, the first as a bar graph in which each data point was the average intensity of all cells within each mouse, and the other as a violin plot of all cells, in which each data point was the intensity of a single PNN or PV neuron. When comparing results across the estrous cycle, data were analyzed by Welch’s ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test for means of individual mice or by the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test when comparing ranks of individual cells. When comparing two groups, data were analyzed by Welch’s t-test for means of individual mice or by the non-parametric Mann-Whitney test when comparing ranks of individual cells. For RNAScope data, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare differences in the number of Pvalb + cells that were positive for Esr1, Esr2, and/or Cyp19a1 probes between estrus and diestrus. Full statistical test results are provided in Supplementary Table 2. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of estrous cycle stage on PNN and PV fluorescence staining intensities in the insula

To determine if the number of PNNs and PV-expressing cells and fluorescence staining intensities in the insula change throughout the estrous cycle, we tracked estrous cycles in mice for 14–16 days. Mice were euthanized at each stage of the estrous cycle (n = 6 per stage) and brain sections containing the insula were stained with WFA and an antibody to PV. Fig. 1A and B shows representative images of WFA and PV staining in the insula. The number of PNNs and PV neurons per image and the percentage of PV neurons that were surrounded by PNNs did not significantly change throughout the estrous cycle (Fig. 1C–D, I). However, the intensities of WFA and PV antibody staining were significantly higher during estrus (Fig. 1E–H) compared to the other three stages of the estrous cycle. WFA fluorescence intensity was highest during estrus when examined by individual mice (Fig. 1E; Welch’s ANOVA, W(3.000, 10.46) = 25.54, p < 0.0001; Dunnett’s T3 test, estrus vs. metestrus, p < 0.0001, estrus vs. diestrus, p = 0.0318, estrus vs. proestrus, p = 0.0184) and at the level of individual cells (Fig. 1F; Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 179.3, p < 0.0001; Dunn’s test, estrus vs. metestrus, diestrus, and proestrus, p < 0.0001). Similarly, PV antibody staining intensity was higher during estrus than the other phases of the estrous cycle, both when averaged within individual mice (Fig. 1G; Welch’s ANOVA, W(3.000, 10.86) = 4.068, p = 0.0364; Dunnett’s T3, estrus vs. metestrus, p = 0.0394) and at the level of individual cells (Fig. 1H; Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 59.44, p < 0.0001; Dunn’s test, estrus vs. metestrus, diestrus, and proestrus, p < 0.0001). These results indicate that ovarian hormones modulate PNN structure and PV protein levels in the insula, with more condensed PNNs and higher PV levels during estrus.

Effects of estrous cycle stage on PNN and PV fluorescence staining intensities in the dHPC

We next examined the number of PNNs and PV-expressing neurons and fluorescence staining intensities in the dHPC stratum oriens/CA1 region at different stages of the estrous cycle in sections from the same mice used for Fig. 1. Fig. 2A, B shows representative images of PNNs and PV neurons in the dHPC. The number of PNNs and PV neurons per image and the percentage of PV neurons that were surrounded by PNNs did not significantly change throughout the estrous cycle (Fig. 2C, D, I). WFA staining intensity also did not significantly differ across the estrous cycle when examined by individual mice (Fig. 2E; Welch’s ANOVA, W(3.000, 10.34) = 0.7174, p = 0.5632), but there was a signficant increase in WFA staining intensity during estrus when compared to proestrus at the level of individual cells (Fig. 2F; Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 15.43, p = 0.0015; Dunn’s test, estrus vs. proestrus, p = 0.0007). This discrepancy was likely due to the increased statistical power when each data point was an individual cell rather than an individual mouse because of the high number of cells analyzed. Similarly, PV intensity did not significantly vary across the estrous cycle when averaged by individual mice (Fig. 2G; W(3.000, 10.38) = 0.4629, p = 0.7142), but was higher during estrus than the other phases when examined at the level of individual cells (Fig. 2H; Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 46.83, p < 0.0001; Dunn’s test, estrus vs. metestrus and diestrus, p < 0.0001, estrus vs. proestrus, p = 0.0065). PV intensity was also higher during proestrus than diestrus (p = 0.0404). These results indicate subtle changes in PNNs and PV in the dHPC across the estrous cycle.

Estrogen receptor and aromatase expression in PV neurons in the insula and dHPC

We hypothesized that estrogen may contribute to changes in PNN and PV intensity at different stages of the estrous cycle because studies have shown that the activity of cortical PV neurons in rats is highest during estrus due to E2 acting through estrogen receptor (ER) β (Clemens et al., 2019), and WFA staining intensity correlates with PV neuron activity (Cisneros-Franco and de Villers-Sidani, 2019; Devienne et al., 2021). To determine if ERs are expressed in insula and dHPC CA1 PV neurons and whether their expression changes during the estrous cycle, we performed RNAScope in situ hybridization with probes to ERα (Esr1), ERβ (Esr2) and PV (Pvalb) on brain sections from mice in estrus and diestrus, two phases of the estrous cycle in which estrogen levels differ (Nilsson et al., 2015; Wall et al., 2023). Fig. 3A shows a representative RNAScope image of the insula from a mouse in estrus. Pvalb was expressed in 2–4 %, Esr1 was expressed in 22 %, and Esr2 was expressed in 6–9 % of total cells counted in sections from both the insula and dHPC (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B). The proportion of cells expressing these genes did not appreciably change between estrus and diestrus, except for slight increases in Esr2 in the insula during estrus (estrus, 9 %; diestrus, 7 %) and Esr1 in the dHPC during estrus (estrus, 16 %; diestrus, 14 %). We next examined expression of Esr1 and Esr2 in the Pvalb-expressing cell population. In the insula, the percentage of Pvalb + cells that expressed only Esr2 was more than three times greater during estrus (23 %) than diestrus (7 %; Fig. 3B). The percentage of Pvalb + cells that expressed only Esr1 was also slightly increased during estrus (17 %) compared to diestrus (13 %). In contrast, the percentage of Pvalb-expressing cells that were positive for both Esr1 and Esr2 did not markedly differ between estrus (6 %) and diestrus (7 %). A Fisher’s exact test on the number of Pvalb + cells expressing either Esr1, Esr2, both, or neither, indicated a statistically significant difference between estrus and diestrus (p = 0.0283). These results indicate that in the insula, the proportion of PV neurons expressing ERβ is elevated during estrus.

We next examined Esr1 and Esr2 expression in Pvalb-expressing neurons in the dHPC CA1 region (Fig. 3C). The dHPC had a much higher percentage of Pvalb + neurons that expressed Esr1 and/or Esr2 compared to the insula (Fig. 3B and C; 64–70 % in dHPC vs. 27–46 % in insula). In the dHPC, the proportion of Pvalb + cells expressing Esr2 was slightly higher in diestrus (38 %) than estrus (31 %), as was the proportion of Pvalb + cells expressing Esr1 (diestrus, 11 %; estrus, 4 %). The percentage of Pvalb-expressing cells that colocalized with both Esr1 and Esr2 did not markedly change between estrus (29 %) and diestrus (28 %). A Fisher’s exact test comparing the number of Pvalb + cells expressing Esr1, Esr2, both, or neither in the dHPC indicated no significant difference between estrus and diestrus (p = 0.381). Thus, results in the dHPC differed from the insula, such that the percentage of Esr2-expressing Pvalb-positive neurons was higher in the dHPC than the insula, but the proportion of Esr2-expressing Pvalb-positive neurons in the dHPC did not differ between estrus and diestrus.

E2, in addition to being produced in the ovary, is locally synthesized in the brain by aromatase, the enzyme that converts testosterone to E2 (Roselli et al., 2009; Simpson et al., 1994). A prior study demonstrated that dHPC CA1 PV neurons express aromatase (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022). We therefore examined colocalization of Pvalb, Esr1 and Cyp19a1 (the gene encoding the aromatase enzyme) in the insula and dHPC CA1 using RNAScope fluorescence in situ hybridization. Cyp19a1 transcript was present in a small percentage of the total cells counted in insula and dHPC sections (Supplementary Fig. 1C–D). In the insula, Cyp19a1 transcript was found in 7 % of cells during diestrus and 5 % of cells during estrus, while in the dHPC, Cyp19a1 transcript was observed in 6 % of cells during estrus and 4 % of cells during diestrus. The percentage of Pvalb + cells in the insula expressing Cyp19a1 was higher than the general cellular population (Fig. 3E), with 35 % of Pvalb + cells expressing either Cyp19a1 (20 %) or Cyp19a1 plus Esr1 (15 %) during estrus, and 47 % of Pvalb-positive cells expressing either Cyp19a1 (26 %) or Cyp19a1 plus Esr1 (21 %) during diestrus. There was no significant difference in the number of Pvalb + cells expressing Esr1, Cyp19a1, both, or neither between estrus and diestrus in the insula (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.74). In the dHPC (Fig. 3F), the percentage of Pvalb-positive cells expressing Cyp19a1 was 28 % (19 % Cyp19a1 and 9 % Cyp19a1 + Esr1) during estrus and 44 % (17 % Cyp19a1 and 27 % Cyp19a1 + Esr1) during diestrus. There was a significant difference in the number of Pvalb + cells expressing Esr1, Cyp19a1, both, or neither between estrus and diestrus in the dHPC (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.048). Together, these results indicate that aromatase mRNA is enriched in PV neurons compared to the total cellular population and increases in dHPC PV neurons during diestrus.

Effect of E2 on PNN and PV fluorescence staining intensities in the insula and dHPC

Given that ERs are expressed in insula and dHPC PV neurons, we next tested the hypothesis that E2 alters WFA and PV fluorescence staining intensities in these brain regions. We ovariectomized mice, allowed them to recover for two weeks, and treated them with EB (n = 11) or VEH (n = 8) once daily for 3 days, with the final dose on the 3rd day approximating high serum E2 levels during proestrus (Vandegrift et al., 2017). Brain sections were incubated with fluorescence-labeled WFA and PV antibody to measure cell numbers and staining intensities of PNNs and PV as described in Section 3.1. In the insula, the number of PNNs and PV neurons, and the percentage of PV neurons that were surrounded by PNNs, did not significantly differ between VEH and EB-treated mice (Fig. 4A–C). However, EB treatment resulted in reduced WFA intensity in individual mice (Fig. 4D; Welch’s t-test, t = 2.253, df = 9.470, p = 0.0493) and at the level of individual cells (Fig. 4E; Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). EB treatment did not significantly affect PV intensity when averaged within individual mice (Fig. 4F; Welch’s t-test, t = 1.624, df = 16.91, p = 0.1229), but did result in reduced PV intensity at the level of individual cells (Fig. 4G; Mann-Whitney test, p = 0.04). Similar results were obtained in the dHPC, where the number of PNNs and PV neurons and percentage of PV neurons surrounded by PNNs were not significantly different between VEH and EB-treated mice (Fig. 4H–J), but WFA intensity was reduced in mice treated with EB (Fig. 4K–N), both when averaged within individual mice (Fig. 4K; Welch’s t-test, t = 2.930, df = 15.79, p = 0.0099) and by individual cells (Fig. 4L; Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). PV intensity was not significantly different between VEH- and EB-treated mice when averaged within individual mice (Fig. 4M; Welch’s t-test, t = 0.3807, df = 12.73, p = 0.7097), but was significantly reduced in EB-treated mice when examined at the level of individual cells (Fig. 4N; Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). These results indicate that E2 decreases PNN and PV staining intensity in the insula and dHPC.

Effect of letrozole on PNN and PV fluorescence staining intensity in the insula and dHPC

The observation that Cyp19a1 transcript is enriched in PV neurons in the insula and dHPC (Fig. 3) and the finding that ovariectomized mice treated with the aromatase inhibitor letrozole increases PNN staining intensity in the dHPC (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022) suggests that local production of E2 in these brain regions could alter PNNs and PV levels. We treated ovariectomized mice chronically with either letrozole (n = 8) or VEH (n = 8) and stained brain sections with fluorescence-labeled WFA and PV antibody. In the insula, the number of PNNs and PV neurons per image, and the percentage of PV neurons surrounded by PNNs did not significantly differ between VEH- and letrozole-treated mice (Fig. 5A–C), but mice treated with letrozole had increased WFA staining intensity (Fig. 5D and E). When averaged within individual mice, WFA intensity trended higher in letrozole-vs. VEH--treated mice (Fig. 5D; Welch’s t-test, t = 1.897, df = 10.54, p = 0.0855). At the level of individual cells, WFA staining intensity was significantly increased in mice treated with letrozole (Fig. 5E; Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). PV intensity was not altered by letrozole (Fig. 5F and G). Letrozole had similar effects on PNNs in the dHPC as it did in the insula. Whereas letrozole did not alter the number of PNNs and PV neurons per image, or the percentage of PV neurons surrounded by PNNs (Fig. 5H–J), it did result in increased WFA staining intensity when averaged within individual mice (Fig. 5K; Welch’s t-test, t = 2.456, df = 13.82, p = 0.0279) and when analyzed by individual cells (Fig. 5L; Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). PV intensity was not altered by letrozole treatment when analyzed at the level of individual mice (Fig. 5M; Welch’s t-test, t = 0.5203, df = 12.69, p = 0.6118), but was reduced by letrozole treatment when analyzed at the level of individual cells (Fig. 5N; Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that chronically blocking neuroestrogen production promotes PNN accumulation in the insula and dHPC CA1 region.

Chronic letrozole treatment alters the expression of genes encoding matrix metalloproteinases and their endogenous inhibitors in the insula and dHPC

One cellular mechanism that could alter the intensity of PNN staining is a shift in the balance between the degradation and production of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan components of PNNs. The cleavage of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans such as aggrecan and brevican that contribute to the structure of PNNs is regulated by the activity of the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) and ADAM metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motif (ADAMTS) families of proteinases, while the tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) are endogenous inhibitors of MMP and ADAMTS proteins (Howell and Gottschall, 2012). To begin to elucidate mechanisms that may be involved in E2 regulation of PNNs in the dHPC and insula, we measured the expression of genes encoding MMPs (Mmp2 and Mmp9), ADAMTSs (Adamts4 and Adamts5), TIMPs (Timp1, Timp2, Timp3, and Timp4), and structural components of PNNs (aggrecan [Acan], brevican [Bcan], tenascin R [Tnr], and phosphacan [Ptprz1]) by qPCR in the insula and dHPC of ovariectomized mice chronically treated with letrozole or VEH. Acan, Bcan, Tnr, and Ptprz1 expression were not significantly altered by letrozole treatment in either brain region (Fig. 6). In the insula, Adamts5, Timp3, and Timp4 transcript levels were significantly reduced by chronic letrozole treatment (Fig. 6A; n = 16 VEH and n = 12 letrozole-treated mice; Welch’s t-test: Adamts5, t = 2.169, df = 24.36, p = 0.0401; Timp3, t = 2.167, df = 33.97, p = 0.0373; Timp4, t = 2.035, df = 32.23, p = 0.0502). In the dHPC, Timp1 and Timp3 transcript levels were significantly reduced by chronic letrozole treatment (Fig. 6B; n = 15–16 VEH and n = 14 letrozole-treated mice; Welch’s t-test: Timp1, t = 2.046, df = 26.60, p = 0.0507; Timp3, t = 2.741, df = 21.83, p = 0.0120). These results indicate that chronic letrozole administration disrupts the expression of genes encoding metalloproteinases and their endogenous inhbitors, and suggests that increased PNN staining intensity after chronic letrozole treatment may be due to altered activity of MMPs or ADAMTs proteinases.

Discussion

PNNs are dynamic structures that change diurnally and are sensitive to environmental enrichment, stress exposure, and drug and alcohol administration (Aguilar and Lasek, 2024; Brown and Sorg, 2023; Coutens et al., 2023; Foscarin et al., 2011; Harkness et al., 2021; Pantazopoulos et al., 2020). More recently, PNNs in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus were shown to be regulated by hormonal state, with a role for both ovarian hormones and brain-derived E2 in modulating PNN condensation on PV neurons (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022, 2025; Laham et al., 2022). Here, we demonstrate that PNNs in the insular cortex, like the hippocampus, are modified throughout the estrous cycle and are bidirectionally modulated by E2 levels. PNN intensity was highest during estrus, a point in the estrous cycle where E2 levels have rapidly plummeted from the proestrus peak. PNN intensity was decreased by exogenous E2 treatment and increased following chronic treatment with letrozole, which blocks E2 synthesis. Our results concur with previous studies indicating that PNNs are sensitive to fluctuations in both peripheral and brain-derived E2 (neuroestrogen) levels (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022, 2025). Specifically, Hernandez-Vivanco et al. (2022) demonstrated that ovariectomized mice treated with letrozole exhibited increased WFA intensity in the dHPC CA1 region. We have replicated these findings, expanded them to include the insular cortex and tested the effect of exogenous E2 on PNNs in both the insula and dHPC. Our results are also consistent with a study demonstrating that rats treated with α-estradiol, a stereoisomer of E2, had decreased WFA intensity in the visual cortex (Sengupta et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest that high E2 may render PNNs more permeable, allowing for increased PV neuron plasticity, whereas low E2 may contribute to more condensed PNNs that restrict synaptic plasticity.

With regard to PNNs during the estrous cycle, we found that WFA intensity was highest during estrus when compared to the other phases of the estrous cycle. Another group (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2025) observed higher WFA intensity in the dHPC CA1 region in proestrus when compared to diestrus (estrus was not examined). Interestingly, Laham et al. observed that the number of PNNs on PV neurons in the ventral dentate gyrus region of the hippocampus was higher during estrus when compared to diestrus (Laham et al., 2022). Overall, these results indicate increased PNN accumulation during estrus and suggest that circulating ovarian hormones induce PNN remodeling. Although our results indicate that E2 can modulate PNN structure, progesterone may also be involved in PNN fluctuations during the estrous cycle. Rats treated with a regimen of E2 plus progesterone to mimic pregnancy had increased PNN intensity in the medial preoptic area, whereas E2 treatment alone did not increase PNN intensity (Uriarte et al., 2020). Future studies should examine in more detail the roles of progesterone and combined E2 plus progesterone on PNNs in the cortex and hippocampus.

Similar to estrous cycle and E2-induced changes in PNN intensity, alterations in PV intensity in the dHPC and insula were observed under these conditions. Like PNN intensity, PV intensity in both brain regions was highest during estrus and decreased by E2 treatment. These results are not surprising, as PV intensity correlates with WFA intensity (Lupori et al., 2023) and PV neuron activity (Donato et al., 2013), and PNNs are formed in an activity-dependent manner during development (Dityatev et al., 2007). Moreover, chemogenetically inhibiting PV neurons reduced PV levels and PNN integrity (Cisneros-Franco and de Villers-Sidani, 2019; Devienne et al., 2021). High E2 might decrease PV levels and induce PNN regression by reducing PV neuron activity; alternatively, high E2 might induce PNN regression, which then could result in decreased PV neuron activity (Tewari et al., 2018). Letrozole treatment has been shown, using fiber photometry, to increase the activity of PV neurons in the dHPC and increase PNN intensity (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022), although it is not known whether the increase in PNN intensity is a cause or consequence of higher PV neuron activity. Surprisingly, chronic letrozole treatment increased PNN intensity but did not significantly affect PV intensity in the insula, and led to decreased PV intensity in the dHPC CA1, indicating dissociation between PNN and PV intensity following chronic neuroestrogen depletion. Possibly the long letrozole treatment protocol that we used engaged compensatory mechanisms that normalized PV in the insula and decreased PV in the dHPC CA1.

Using RNAScope fluorescent in situ hybridization, we found that the proportion of PV neurons in the insula expressing Esr2 mRNA was higher in estrus than in diestrus. In rat somatosensory cortex, Esr2 expression was enriched in layer V PV neurons, and the percentage of layer V PV neurons expressing Esr2 was higher in proestrus than metestrus (Clemens et al., 2019). PV neuron activity in the somatosensory cortex was also higher in rats during the window of sexual receptivity, encompassing proestrus and the first day of estrus, compared to other non-estrus phases of the cycle (Clemens et al., 2019). When ovariectomized rats were treated with E2, the fast-spiking activity of PV neurons increased. Similarly, local application of E2 to the somatosensory cortex increased the firing rate of PV neurons, and this increase was blocked by application of a selective ERβ antagonist (Clemens et al., 2019). These results suggest that ERβ activation increases cortical PV neuron activity. We hypothesize that the increase in Esr2 expression in mouse insular cortex PV neurons during estrus may increase their activity, promote PNN accumulation and increase PV expression. Interestingly, the proportion of PV neurons expressing Esr2 did not change between estrus and diestrus in the dHPC CA1 region, and the increase in PNN and PV intensity during estrus was not as pronounced in the dHPC as in the insula. In contrast, the proportion of PV neurons expressing aromatase (Cyp19a1), was higher during diestrus compared to estrus in the dHPC CA1 region. These results imply that more neuroestrogen is produced by PV neurons during diestrus than estrus in the dHPC, but not necessarily in the insula. Together, these results indicate that distinct estrogen-related mechanisms may promote changes in PNNs and PV levels in the dHPC CA1 region vs. the insula during the estrous cycle.

Another important consideration is potentially opposing effects of ovarian-derived vs. brain-derived E2 on PV neuron activity and PNNs, and distinct regional effects. In the insula, it seems likely that ovarian-derived E2 increases the activity of PV neurons and PNN intensity (possibly through ERβ), and in the dHPC CA1, ovarian-derived E2 may increase PNN intensity but not necessarily alter PV neuron activity, because PV neuron activity did not differ between proestrus and diestrus (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2025). In contrast, brain-derived E2 likely reduces PV neuron activity and PNNs in the dHPC CA1 and PNNs in the insula based on the effects of aromatase inhibition in ovariectomized mice in our study and others (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022). The specific ERs mediating the effects of brain-derived E2 in reducing PNNs and PV neuron activity are currently not known.

In several instances, we found significant changes in WFA and/or PV fluorescence intensity across the estrous cycle, and with E2 and letrozole treatment, when we analyzed the data by individual cells but not by individual mice. One explanation for these findings is the increased statistical power when analyzing data by individual cells, but there could also be a biological explanation for this discrepancy. The RNAScope data demonstrated that not all PV neurons express ERs. As a result, not all PV neurons would be expected to respond to fluctuations in E2 levels. When data was analyzed by individual cells, we observed a significant shift in the intensity distribution because we were capturing changes in WFA and PV intensity of both ER-expressing and non-expressing cells. However, when the intensity of all cells was averaged within individual mice, the effects of estrous cycle and treatments were likely diminished. One way to address this issue would be to analyze WFA and PV intensities across estrous cycle and after E2 treatment only in ER-expressing cells, where we might expect to find more pronounced changes at the level of individual cells and within individual animals. Interestingly, in rat somatosensory cortex, the magnitude of the E2-and proestrus-dependent increase in firing rate of fast-spiking neurons in different cortical layers was associated with a higher percentage of PV neurons expressing Esr2, particularly in layer V (Clemens et al., 2019).

Mechanisms that contribute to E2 modulation of PNNs could involve changes in the expression and/or activity of the MMPs or ADAMTSs, families of metalloproteinases that cleave the chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans of PNNs (Howell and Gottschall, 2012; Mitlohner et al., 2020; Nakamura et al., 2000). For example, in the dHPC, E2 enhancement of memory requires the activity of MMP9 (Gross et al., 2022), which has been shown to cleave the PNN protein brevican and attenuate WFA staining (Alaiyed et al., 2019, 2020; Stamenkovic et al., 2017; Wen et al., 2017). One way in which high E2 might therefore alter PNN structure, at least in the dHPC, is by acutely activating MMP9. Transcriptomics on the hippocampus of aged ovariectomized rats treated chronically with E2 found increased expression of Mmp2 and Timp3 in E2-treated rats compared to controls (Sarvari et al., 2015), indicating that E2 may also regulate the transcription of genes involved in ECM proteolysis. Here, we examined transcript levels of Mmp2, Mmp9, Adamts4, and Adamts5, and their endogenous inhibitors (Timp1, Timp2, Timp3, and Timp4) in the insula and dHPC after chronic letrozole treatment to determine if blocking neuroestrogen synthesis could alter the expression of genes involved in PNN degradation. In the insula of letrozole-treated mice, Adamts5, Timp3 and Timp4 expression were decreased, while in the dHPC of letrozole-treated mice, Timp1 and Timp3 were decreased. The PNN proteins ACAN, BCAN, and VCAN are substrates for ADAMTS4 and ADAMTS5, also known as the aggrecanases (Kelwick et al., 2015; Mitlohner et al., 2020). Decreased ADAMTS5 following chonic letrozole treatment is expected to result in accumulation of full-length ACAN, BCAN, and VCAN, possibly contributing to increased PNN thickness. All four TIMPs inhibit the MMPs, while TIMP3 also inhibits ADAMTS4 and ADAMTS5 (Brew and Nagase, 2010). Although lower expression of Timp1, Timp3, and Timp4 are not consistent with increased PNN accumulation following chronic letrozole treatment, the mRNA changes may reflect compensatory mechanisms in an attempt to re-establish homeostasis due to decreased MMP or ADAMTS activity. Further investigation into protein levels and activity of the metalloproteinases is necessary to draw definitive conclusions. However, these results suggest that the balance between PNN degradation and accumulation in both the insula and dHPC is likely disrupted following chronic letrozole treatment.

Estrogenic regulation of PNNs in the dHPC and insula has implications for estrogen-sensitive behaviors and neuropsychiatric and neurological disorders. E2 positively enhances learning and memory, but this might be disadvantageous under certain circumstances. For example, hippocampal PNNs contribute to fear learning (Alexander et al., 2025; Sanchez et al., 2023). E2 modulation of PNNs during fear learning could increase the risk of developing post-traumatic stress disorder (Ellis et al., 2025), which is more prevalent in women than in men. Women diagnosed with hormone-sensitive breast cancer are often treated with aromatase inhibitors such as letrozole to prevent cancer recurrence, but experience negative side effects including cognitive impairment (Rosenfeld et al., 2018). Notably, mice given the same letrozole treatment protocol that increased PNN intensity in the dHPC CA1 region had impaired object location memory (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022), suggesting that high PNN accumulation with aromatase inhibition is detrimental to learning and memory. PNNs are also associated with the formation of drug-associated memories and modulate behavioral responses to drugs (Brown and Sorg, 2023), and there are sex differences in substance use disorders, which may be modulated by E2 (Quigley et al., 2021). Compulsive-like alcohol consumption is lower following disruption of PNNs in the insula of male, but not female, mice (Chen and Lasek, 2020; Martins de Carvalho et al., 2023), which may be due to effects of E2 on PV neuron activity via ERβ in the insula of females. Finally, risk-taking behavior is regulated by the insula and sensitive to local manipulation of ERs. Stressed female mice injected with an ER antagonist in the insula exhibited increased risk-taking behavior (Shi et al., 2023). Future studies are needed to determine if PNNs in the insula are involved in E2 regulation of risky behavior.

One limitation of this study is that we only studied the effects of manipulating E2 on PNNs and PV levels in female mice. Given that males also express aromatase and ERs in the brain, it will be important to determine if exogenous E2 or an aromatase inhibitor can affect PNNs in male mice, although males treated with letrozole did not exhibit changes in WFA intensity in the dHPC CA1 region in a prior study (Hernandez-Vivanco et al., 2022). Another limitation is that we only used WFA to examine PNNs. It will be important in the future to determine if other measures of PNNs are also altered by E2 or aromatase inhibition, such as levels of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (aggrecan, brevican, tenascin R, phosphacan), activity of MMPs and ADAMTSs and proteolytic cleavage of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, or levels of chondroitin sulfate isoforms with different sulfation patterns that are more restrictive or permissive for synapse formation (Miyata et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2021). Finally, it will be important to determine which ERs (ERα, ERβ, or the G-protein coupled ER [GPER]) are involved in the sensitivity of PNNs to E2 in specific brain regions.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that the fluorescence staining intensity for PNNs and PV in the insula and dHPC CA1 region are modulated by E2. In ovariectomized mice, exogenous E2 administration resulted in decreased PNN and PV intensity, while chronic treatment with the aromatase inhibitor letrozole increased PNN intensity. In gonadally intact, naturally cycling mice, PNN and PV intensities were highest during estrus. Possible mechanisms contributing to estrous cycle regulation of PNNs involve changes in ERβ in the insula and aromatase in the dHPC. E2 may also regulate levels or activity of MMPs/ADAMTSs and the TIMPs.
