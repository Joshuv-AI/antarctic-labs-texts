Re: Shalender Bhasin, Peter J. Snyder. Testosterone Treatment in Middle-aged and Older Men with Hypogonadism. N Engl J Med 2025;393:581–91
Authors: Yu-Hsiang Lin, Jau-Yuan Chen, Chun-Te Wu
Published: 2025

The review by Bhasin and Snyder [1] summarizes the benefits and risks of testosterone therapy in men with hypogonadism. The authors note that men with a testosterone level below 200 ng/dl are most likely to benefit and cite this value as a diagnostic benchmark. While pragmatic, reliance on a single biochemical threshold oversimplifies a more complex clinical and biological reality.

The 2025 European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines illustrate this clearly: testosterone deficiency is defined as total testosterone <12 nmol/l (350 ng/dl), confirmed on two separate morning samples and only in the presence of indicative symptoms [2]. Meta-analyses have shown that the benefit of testosterone therapy is greatest below this threshold, particularly <8 nmol/l (230 ng/dl). Importantly, the EAU guidelines stress that symptoms such as lower libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, mood disturbance, and muscle loss are essential to diagnosis. A rigid numerical cutoff such as 200 ng/dl does not capture this clinical context.

Clinical trials also adopt different thresholds. The Testosterone for Diabetes Mellitus (T4DM) trial used a higher cutoff of 14 nmol/l (400 ng/dl) to recruit men with impaired glucose tolerance [3]. In this setting, testosterone deficiency was defined in relation to metabolic risk rather than sexual or hematologic outcomes. This illustrates that the “appropriate” threshold depends on the population and endpoints under study, and highlights the necessity of adapting diagnostic criteria for specific clinical research questions.

Conceptual advances have further challenged fixed testosterone cutoffs. Lin et al. [4] proposed a framework that integrates organ-specific and circadian thresholds. Libido may respond to a lower testosterone therapy target, while bone or reproductive function requires higher levels. Testosterone also follows a diurnal rhythm, with morning peaks blunted in older age groups and among men with disrupted sleep. Within this model, sufficiency varies by organ system and time of day, rather than a single static value.

Clinical evidence supports this multidimensional view. Chang et al. [5] showed that surgical treatment for nocturia due to benign prostatic obstruction improved sleep and restored circadian alignment, leading to an increase in testosterone level, even in men with baseline concentrations >350 ng/dl. This result demonstrates that circadian disruption can suppress testosterone and that restoration of rhythms improves endocrine function. It also shows that men considered “biochemically normal” may still benefit from interventions that optimize circadian testosterone physiology.

Together, these findings indicate that a single testosterone benchmark, such as 200 ng/dl, under-represents the biological and clinical diversity of testosterone physiology. As evidenced by the symptom-contingent testosterone threshold of 350 ng/dl in the EAU guidelines, the cutoff of 400 ng/dl for metabolic risk in the T4DM trial, and emerging concepts of organ-specific and circadian needs, the diagnosis of hypogonadism is highly context-dependent.

While the review appropriately emphasizes the benefits and risks of testosterone therapy, its focus on a single low threshold fails to address a critical point for real-world clinical practice: the ultimate goals of safety, tolerability, and efficacy cannot be achieved without starting with the correct diagnosis. These goals depend entirely on the use of adequate, purpose-driven cutoff values. Too low a testosterone threshold risks undertreatment of symptomatic men, while an overly permissive cutoff value increases the likelihood of overtreatment and adverse events.

Therefore, to answer the crucial questions that remain, future prospective, randomized controlled trials must abandon a one-size-fits-all approach and adopt testosterone thresholds tailored to specific endpoints, whether sexual, metabolic, or functional. We advocate not only for a nuanced understanding but also for the development of a clinically actionable framework that moves beyond rigid cutoff values to truly personalize the management of androgen deficiency.
