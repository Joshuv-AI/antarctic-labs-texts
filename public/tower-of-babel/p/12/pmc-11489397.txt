Application of large-scale and multicohort plasma proteomics data to discover novel causal proteins in gastric cancer
Authors: Weihao Tang, Xiaoke Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposesGastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignant tumors threatening human beings and has a poor prognosis. Therefore, exploring unveiled biomarkers or therapeutic targets for the diagnosis and treatment of GC is crucial.MethodsA total of 5772 protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) were aggregated from four latest large-scale proteomics cohorts. Two-sample Mendelian randomization (two-sample MR) was utilized to identify the causal effect of blood plasma proteins on GC. Heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and directionality analyses were employed to evaluate proteins identified via two-sample MR. The robustness of results was further validated via colocalization. The drug targets of proteins were evaluated to reveal the compounds that can interfere with these proteins.ResultsTen proteins with potential causations in relation to GC were identified: LY6D, SLURP1, MLN, PSCA, THSD1, CFTR, PPM1B, KDM3A, TSC1, and HCG22. Among these proteins, LY6D, SLURP1, and THSD1 were considered as the most reliable biomarkers of GC due to their significant H4 posterior probabilities in colocalization analysis and absence of pleiotropy. Compound 35, nitrosamide, and 0175029-0000 were potential drugs or small molecules targeting LY6D, SLURP1, and THSD1, respectively.ConclusionThis study identified several plasma proteins as potential biomarkers of GC and provided data support and new insights into the early diagnosis, intervention, and therapeutic targets of GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01460-9.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is among the most prevalent malignant tumors affecting human health, with a 5-year survival rate of only 20% [1]. The prognosis for stomach cancer is poor, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and treatment. Identifying early biomarkers and potential intervention targets has become a central focus of medical research. Circulating tumor DNA, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [2], and circulating proteins, including HLA-G, IL-6, and PD-1 [3], serve as diagnostic markers for GC. Despite this, many potential early-stage biomarkers for GC are yet to be discovered. A promising approach is to leverage the human circulating blood proteomics data that have emerged in recent years [4–6]. Human proteome projects offer a vast amount of protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data that can be used to investigate biomarkers and potential therapeutics for various diseases [7, 8]. For instance, IL-6R blockade has shown therapeutic effects in five different cancers [9], and TYK2 inhibition could serve as a therapeutic target for autoimmune diseases [10].

Research on the association between plasma proteins and GC is still in its preliminary stages. Some studies have attempted to elucidate the causality between morning chronotype and digestive tract cancers, including GC, based on limited human circulating blood proteome data [11]. Another study investigated the association between GC and autoimmune diseases but found no genetic causal association between susceptibility to autoimmune diseases and GC [12]. Nevertheless, a comprehensive proteome-wide analysis of GC is lacking, as existing research results are insufficient to clarify the complex role of plasma proteins in GC. The causality of proteins related to disease is a critical indicator for the diagnosis and early intervention of GC. Consequently, there is a pressing need to utilize large-scale plasma protein data to identify novel pathogenic factors or diagnostic markers for GC.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a robust method employed in epidemiology and genetic research to explore causal associations between modifiable exposures and disease outcomes. It has been demonstrated as an effective analytical tool for uncovering the association between plasma proteins and various cancers [13, 14]. In this study, we aggregated large-scale, multicohort plasma proteomics pQTL data and applied two-sample MR, utilizing genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data to identify potential circulating blood proteins that may serve as diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for GC. We conducted colocalization, directionality, sensitivity analyses, and external pQTL validation to verify the robustness of the causal associations between plasma proteins and GC. Additionally, we evaluated the drug targets of the identified causal proteins. This study aims to provide novel data to support further investigations into early blood diagnosis, drug intervention targets, and potential pathogenesis of GC.

Methods

Overall design

The overall design of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. We utilized meta pQTL data from four recent large-scale proteomics studies [4, 5, 15, 16] and examined their associations with GC through two-sample MR. Colocalization analysis was employed to validate the associations between pQTLs and outcomes. Sensitivity analyses, including assessments of heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and random effects models, were performed to ensure the robustness of the causal associations of proteins. A Steiger directionality test was conducted to confirm the direction of causality from proteins to GC. Preparation of genetic instruments

pQTL summary statistics were obtained from the Fenland, deCODE, AGES, and UKBPPP databases [4, 5, 15, 16]. A significance threshold for single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) screening was set at P < 5e−8. The Pink1.9 software within the Linux system was utilized to remove linkage disequilibrium, resulting in SNP clumping (10,000 kb; r2 < 0.001). Further instrument selection was carried out based on the following criteria: (1) SNPs located on the X chromosome were excluded; (2) the sum R2 of SNPs associated with each protein was calculated [17]; (3) proteomics data from various cohorts were consolidated; and (4) duplicated proteins present in the aggregated pQTL were selected based on the maximum sum of R2, generating the final meta-instruments for subsequent analyses. Preparation of outcomes

Outcome GWAS data were downloaded from the IEU OpenGWAS Project by searching for the GWAS summary statistics of GC [18]. In this study, the dataset ebi-a-GCST90018849, derived from a European population comprising 476,116 individuals (1,029 cases and 475,087 controls), including 24,188,662 SNPs, was utilized as the outcome GWAS data [19]. The following columns were retained for analysis: "variant_id," "beta," "standard_error," "effect_allele," "other_allele," "effect_allele_frequency," and "p_value".

Screening casual proteins through two-sample MR

The TwoSampleMR package (https://github.com/MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR) was employed to conduct MR. The function harmonise_data was used to harmonize instruments and outcomes based on shared SNPs between discovery IVs and GWAS outcomes. Instruments with an F statistic greater than 10 were retained for two-sample MR. Associations between each protein and the outcome were computed individually. The Wald ratio method (WR) was applied to pQTL associated with a single SNP, whereas the inverse variance weighted method (IVW) was used for those with multiple SNPs. The P value in the two-sample MR results was adjusted according to the false discovery rate (FDR) method: FDR = p.value × discovery protein number [20]. The WR method was employed for proteins with only one SNP, and the IVW method for those with multiple SNPs. Significant two-sample MR results were determined based on an FDR threshold of < 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses and Steiger directionality test

The function mr_heterogeneity was utilized to assess heterogeneity for proteins associated with more than three SNPs. Results exhibiting heterogeneity (q < 0.05) were re-evaluated using two-sample MR through a random effects model to confirm causality (P < 0.05). The MRPRESSO package [21] was employed to test for pleiotropy among pQTL, with P > 0.05 indicating no pleiotropy for proteins associated with more than three SNPs.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

Different hypotheses were incorporated into the Bayesian colocalization framework: (1) H0: Phenotype 1 (GWAS) and Phenotype 2 (pQTL) are not significantly associated with any SNP loci in the specified genomic region. H1/H2: Either Phenotype 1 (GWAS) or Phenotype 2 (pQTL) is significantly associated with SNP loci in the genomic region. H3: Both Phenotypes 1 (GWAS) and 2 (pQTL) are significantly associated with SNP loci in the genomic region but are influenced by different causal variant loci. H4: Both Phenotypes 1 (GWAS) and 2 (pQTL) are significantly associated with SNP loci in the genomic region and are influenced by the same causal variant locus.

The package coloc was utilized to perform colocalization analysis [22]. Variance and MAF values were computed for GWAS outcome data. For the pQTL data of causal proteins, variance, MAF, and z values were calculated, with leading SNPs identified based on P value. All SNPs within a 1 Mb region upstream and downstream of the leading SNP were selected for further analysis. Joint SNPs between pQTL and GWAS outcome data were employed for colocalization using the function coloc.abf. A colocalization association between pQTL and GWAS was inferred if PP.H4.abf > 0.5. The posterior probability for each of these hypotheses was computed to summarize genetic association data [23].

Validation of causality based on external pQTL data

Proteins outside the discovery cohort, including those from EPIC [24], deCODE, and AGES [5, 15], were utilized for validation. Causal proteins that met colocalization criteria corresponding to the pQTLs identified in these three cohorts were selected as exposures. In terms of preparing genetic instruments for external validation, the threshold of P < 5e−7 was utilized for SNP screening, and other conditions remained the same. The GWAS data for GC (ebi-a-GCST90018849) served as the outcome. Two-sample MR was performed with this GWAS outcome following the established steps to validate the causality between external proteins and GC. The WR or IVW method was applied, with p < 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Exploration of drug targets for casual proteins

The drug targets of causal proteins were evaluated using the DSigDB [25], ChEMBL [26], DGIdb [27], and DrugBank [28] databases. These resources provide vital information regarding drugs that may influence the expression or function of causal proteins. Interventional drugs related to causal proteins were retrieved and summarized.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1). The TwoSampleMR (version 0.6.6) and MRPRESSO (version 1.0) packages were employed to perform the two-sample MR. The WR statistical method was applied to analyze pQTL with a single SNP, whereas the IVW statistical method was used for pQTL with two or more SNPs. A FDR threshold of < 0.05 was established for screening proteins during the discovery phase, whereas a P-value threshold of < 0.05 was set for the validation phase of the screened proteins. Colocalization analysis was conducted using the R package coloc (version 5.2.3), with a significant colocalization indicated by a posterior probability (PP.H4.abf) of ≥ 0.5. The results of the analyses were visualized utilizing R packages including ggplot2 (version 3.5.1), EnhancedVolcano (version 1.22.0), and forestploter (version 1.1.2).

Results

Flowchart of this study

Plasma protein pQTL summary data were compiled from several cohorts: Pietzner (4775 proteins) [4], Ferkingstad (4709 proteins) [5], Gudjonsson (4782 proteins) [15], and Sun (2923 proteins) [16]. A total of 5772 proteins were identified for discovery exposure based on the criteria outlined in Fig. 1. Outcome data for the discovery phase were obtained from GWAS summary statistics (Ebi database-ID: ebi-a-GCST90018849). Exposure and outcome data were harmonized, and significant proteins were identified (FDR < 0.05). Sensitivity and directional analyses, which included heterogeneity analysis, horizontal pleiotropy analysis, and the Steiger directionality test, were performed. Bayesian colocalization analysis was utilized to assess the robustness of the causal proteins, categorized as High (PP.H4.abf ≥ 0.8), Medium (0.5 < PP.H4.abf < 0.8), or Low (PP.H4.abf ≤ 0.5). External pQTLs from additional cohorts were selected as exposure instruments to further validate proteins that passed colocalization analysis. The drug targets of the causal proteins were then evaluated using the DSigDB [25], ChEMBL [26], DGIdb [27], and DrugBank [28] databases.

Causal proteins of GC discovered through two-sample MR

Ten proteins were identified as factors associated with GC based on causal associations, comprising five potential protective proteins and five potential hazardous proteins (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). The protective proteins include KDM3A (OR = 0.51; 95% CI, 0.40–0.65; P = 3.05e − 04), MLN (OR = 0.81; 95% CI, 0.75–0.87; P = 4.09e − 05), PPM1B (OR = 0.30; 95% CI, 0.22–0.42; P = 2.87e−08), THSD1 (OR = 0.71; 95% CI, 0.62–0.82; P = 1.18e−02), and TSC1 (OR = 0.32; 95% CI, 0.22–0.46; P = 4.27e−06), all of which are associated with a decreased risk of GC (OR < 1). Conversely, the hazardous proteins include CFTR (OR = 1.86; 95% CI, 1.49–2.34; P = 4.01e−04), HCG22 (OR = 1.21; 95% CI, 1.12–1.30; P = 9.02e−03), LY6D (OR = 1.65; 95% CI, 1.44–1.89; P = 3.20e−09), PSCA (OR = 1.29; 95% CI, 1.17–1.42; P = 1.03e−03), and SLURP1 (OR = 1.47; 95% CI, 1.27–1.71; P = 2.70e−03), which are associated with an increased risk of GC (OR > 1) (Fig. 2B). Among these causal proteins, seven were derived from the UKBPPP cohort, whereas three originated from Fenland, Decode, and AGES. Notably, CFTR was analyzed with a weighted regression in the presence of only one SNP, whereas all other proteins with multiple SNPs were assessed using the IVW method. Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis was conducted on ten proteins identified through two-sample MR in relation to GC, utilizing the coloc package [30]. Based on the posterior probability of colocalization results [31], the proteins were classified into three levels to indicate the relative extent to which an exposure and outcome share the same causal variance: (1) Level 1: LY6D, SLURP1, and MLN were selected based on their high posterior probability (PP.H4 ≥ 0.8); (2) Level 2: PSCA and THSD1 were classified due to their medium posterior probability (0.5 < PP.H4 < 0.8); (3) Level 3: CTFR, PPM1B, KDM3A, TSC1, and HCG22 were grouped together because of their low posterior probability (PP.H4 ≤ 0.5) (Fig. 3A). External-validation result

The three colocalized proteins LY6D, SLURP1, and THSD1 were validated through external two-sample MR analysis, with results presented in Fig. 3E. The findings are as follows: LY6D (OR = 1.30; 95% CI, 1.15–1.48; P = 4.82e−05), SLURP1 (OR = 1.52; 95% CI, 1.12–2.06; P = 6.91e−03), and THSD1 (OR = 0.86; 95% CI, 0.81–0.91; P = 1.74e−06). The P values indicate that these three proteins have statistical significance in the results of the two-sample MR analysis with external exposure. The OR values suggest that LY6D and SLURP1 may serve as risk factors, whereas THSD1 may act as a protective factor. These results are highly consistent with the findings from the discovery phase of the two-sample MR analysis. All two-sample MR results from this validation are available in Supplementary Table 3.

Drug target evaluation of casual proteins

In our evaluation of drug targets, we identified eight proteins without horizontal pleiotropy as targets for various chemical compounds and drugs with either approved or preclinical statuses (Table 3). Among the hazardous proteins associated with GC, two antineoplastic agents, ENTINOSTAT (not approved) and VORINOSTAT (approved), were recognized as drugs targeting CFTR. Additionally, SLURP1 and LY6D were identified as targets for two antineoplastic agents, AGS-1C4D4 (not approved) and MK-4721 (not approved). Notably, nitrosamide has been shown to decrease the expression of SLURP1 [32]. Furthermore, compound-35 also reduces the gene expression of LY6D by day 7 of implementation [33]. Both hydralazine and valproic acid have been noted to decrease the expression of HCG22. Among the protective proteins, staurosporine and thapsigargin were observed to increase the expression of KDM3A and PPM1B, respectively. Moreover, piperlongumine and parthenolide were found to enhance the expression of TSC1. Discussion

In this study, we identified ten potential protective or hazardous causal proteins associated with GC through two-sample MR based on aggregated pQTL proteomics data. Five proteins—LY6D, SLURP1, MLN, PSCA, and THSD1—passed colocalization analysis and are proposed as potential reliable biomarkers on a holistic level. Our pleiotropy analysis revealed that MLN and PSCA exhibited pleiotropy (P < 0.05); consequently, we excluded these two proteins from the final list of causal proteins. Based on the results of colocalization and pleiotropy analysis, LY6D, SLURP1, and THSD1 were ultimately selected as the most reliable biomarkers, supported by strong evidence. External pQTL validation for these three proteins yielded results highly consistent with our discovery findings, suggesting that the causal association between these proteins and GC is stable across different populations.

LY6D is a lymphocyte antigen anticipated to be located in the extracellular region and plasma membrane, with activity at the cell surface [34]. It may serve as an early specification marker during the initial stages of lymphocyte differentiation between B- and T-cells, specifically marking the earliest phase of B-cell development [35]. Previous studies have reported a association between LY6D and various cancer types. For instance, LY6D has been identified as a potential surface marker for basal cell carcinoma [36]. Additionally, knocking down LY6D can inhibit the proliferation of colon cancer cells [37]. Furthermore, meta-analyses have indicated that the LY6 family is associated with poor patient survival outcomes in several cancers, including GC [38]. The connection between LY6D and surface cells is particularly intriguing, as the human stomach consists largely of epithelial cells on its inner surface. These cells facilitate the formation of gastric mucosa, as well as the production of digestive enzymes and acids that aid in digestion and protect the stomach lining. In the absence of B cells, the intestinal epithelium activates its own protective mechanisms against microbiota [39]. Consequently, irregular expression of LY6D may disrupt the differentiation of B cells and compromise protective responses against GC. Our research represents the first identification of LY6D as a causal protein in GC based on GWAS data. Further experimental studies are required to validate LY6D’s role in GC.

SLURP1 is potentially a marker for the later stages of skin differentiation [40] and plays a crucial role in maintaining the structural and functional integrity of keratinocyte layers in the skin [41]. Its secreted form exhibits antitumor activity [42]. Studies have identified SLURP1 as a biomarker for prostate cancer [43]. However, other research suggests that SLURP1 may inhibit the proliferation of human colon cancer cells [44] and serves as a potential tumor suppressor in pancreatic cancer [45]. In our study, SLURP1 was identified as a hazardous protein, indicating that its function may differ across various tissues. Additionally, SLURP1 has been recognized as a causal gene in Mal de Meleda, where it regulates the proliferation and differentiation of epidermal cells [46]. Given that GC, such as adenocarcinoma, is often caused by the abnormal proliferation of epidermal cells, irregular expression of SLURP1 may be an underlying factor contributing to GC.

THSD1 synthesizes Thrombospondin Type-1 Domain-Containing Protein 1, an extracellular matrix protein that influences endothelial cell adhesion to the extracellular matrix [47, 48]. Previous studies have documented its involvement in various cancer types. Notably, THSD1 is methylated in colorectal cancer [49] and plays a crucial role in preserving vascular integrity, which protects against hemorrhage [50]. In our study, THSD1 was identified as a protective protein against GC. One possible mechanism is that THSD1 may affect the adhesion of cancerous endothelial cells to the stomach lining or help maintain vascular integrity, thereby offering protection against GC.

In regard to proteins that did not pass colocalization analysis, previous studies have reported their associations with different cancer types. CFTR is known to potentially regulate the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cervical cancer cells [51] and can lead to pancreatic adenocarcinoma when mutated [52]. PPM1B may inhibit GC [53], whereas its degradation can promote breast cancer and metastasis [54]. KDM3A is capable of enhancing antitumor activity in GC [55]. Additionally, TSC1 deficiency can accelerate both the initiation and development of GC [56], and its loss is associated with the development of renal angiomyolipoma [57]. HCG22 may act as a tumor promoter in papillary thyroid cancer [58]. The findings from other studies suggest that these newly identified proteins may also play significant roles in various cancers, with their specific roles in GC warranting further investigation.

The drug targets of the three proteins with substantial supporting evidence are also noteworthy. Compound-35 was initially studied alongside other chemicals to assess the expression levels of multiple genes, demonstrating the ability to reduce the expression of LY6D by day 7 [33]. Based on these findings, lowering LY6D expression could potentially have implications for the prognosis and treatment of GC. Furthermore, nitrosamide can decrease SLURP1 expression [32] and is recognized as a common carcinogen that may induce cancer in both humans and animals [59]. This dichotomy presents an intriguing perspective for therapeutic development, as certain compounds, including cyclophosphamide, can either induce or treat cancer depending on the dosage [60, 61]. The compound 0175029-0000, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor [62], has been identified as a potential targeted agent against HBV-associated early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma [63] and prostate cancer [64]. Although the aforementioned drugs/compounds have not previously been reported in the context of GC, their roles warrant further exploration.

The advantages of our study are as follows: (1) We utilized integrated plasma proteomic pQTL datasets from multiple cohorts to identify causal associations with GC on a large scale. (2) The combined colocalization analysis offered additional evidence supporting the two-sample MR findings. (3) Among the newly identified proteins, several exhibited functions aligned with previous literature, specifically in relation to tumorigenesis. These insights offer valuable clues for further exploration of the pathogenesis of GC.

However, our study also has limitations: (1) The data utilized were primarily sourced from European populations, which means that the conclusions may be limited to individuals of European ancestry. Expanding these findings to other populations necessitates additional data from diverse ancestries. (2) The available GWAS data on GC is limited and includes a relatively small number of cases, which may introduce bias into the analytical results. (3) Further molecular biology experiments and clinical investigations are essential to reveal the actual causal associations.

Conclusion

This study has identified several novel causal plasma proteins associated with GC for the first time through two-sample MR utilizing aggregated proteomic pQTL data from multiple large-scale studies. Our results were subjected to sensitivity, directionality, and colocalization analyses, alongside evaluations of drug targets related to the causal proteins. Overall, this research offers new perspectives and robust data to inform future investigations into the precise diagnosis and treatment of GC.
