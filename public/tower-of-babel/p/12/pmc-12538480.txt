Multidisciplinary management of a giant cervical aneurysmal bone cyst: A two-year follow-up case report featuring embolization, staged resection, and cement-augmented fixation
Authors: Sadegh Bagherzadeh, Faramarz Roohollahi, Mohsen Rostami, Diego Soto Rubio, Mark Greenberg, Puya Alikhani
Published: 2025

Introduction

An aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a benign, expansile bone lesion characterized by blood-filled spaces separated by connective tissue septa containing fibroblasts, osteoclast-type giant cells, and reactive woven bone [1]. Diagnosis is typically confirmed through biopsy, which demonstrates these classic histological features. However, imaging can be highly suggestive, especially when “fluid-fluid” levels are visualized on CT or conventional radiography, with confirmation on MRI [2].

ABCs account for approximately 10 % to 30 % of all bone cysts involving the spine and represent about 15 % of all primary spine bone tumors [3]. These lesions are more frequently found in the lumbar spine and often involve the posterior elements in 3 %–30 % of cases. Among spinal ABCs, 22 %–42 % are located in the cervical spine [4,5]. The most commonly reported symptoms are local pain and swelling. However, when the spine is involved, ABCs can exert mass effect on neural elements, leading to cord or root compression, neurological deficits, spinal deformity, and instability [6].

Managing cervical spine ABCs poses unique challenges due to the close proximity of the spinal cord, nerve roots, and vertebral arteries, as well as the inherent risk of instability. The case presented here involves a cervical ABC affecting both the anterior and posterior elements of the C3–C5 vertebrae. We describe the clinical course and multidisciplinary management strategy following the SCARE guideline [7].

Case presentation

Patient information

The patient was a 37-year-old woman with no significant past medical history aside from a family history of chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) in her father. She presented with a four-year history of progressive neck pain, occasionally accompanied by radicular symptoms in the upper extremities.

Approximately four months before presentation, she experienced a sudden “crack” in her neck during an abrupt movement, followed by worsening axial neck pain and bilateral radiculopathy. The pain had a progressive course, exacerbated by movement and occasionally disturbing her sleep. Axial pain was more prominent than radicular pain, and while the former responded to Celecoxib, the latter improved with Gabapentin. She denied any gait disturbance or other neurologic symptoms.

Clinical findings

Upon examination, the patient exhibited midline tenderness over the mid-cervical spine and axial neck pain that was aggravated by movement. Spurling's maneuver reproduced radicular symptoms, particularly over the left C6 dermatome, while cervical distraction provided symptom relief. Motor strength was full (5/5) in both upper and lower extremities, with normal muscle tone and symmetric muscle bulk. No fasciculations were observed. Deep tendon reflexes were 2+ throughout, plantar reflexes were downgoing bilaterally, and no clonus was present. Hoffman's sign was negative bilaterally. The sensory examination was intact without any lateralizing findings.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial cervical MRI revealed a large, expansile, multiloculated cystic lesion extending from C3 to C5, with classic features of an aneurysmal bone cyst (Fig. 1-A). To evaluate the extent of osseous destruction and surgical anatomy, a cervical CT with 3D reconstruction was obtained (Fig. 1-B). A three-phase whole-body bone scan using Tc99m-MDP was also performed to rule out additional skeletal involvement, which returned negative for other lesions (Fig. 2).

Therapeutic interventions

Surgical planning was based on the principle that Gross Total Resection (GTR) provides the best chance of preventing recurrence. Given the absence of systemic skeletal involvement on the whole-body bone scan, our objective was to achieve Maximal Safe Resection (MSR) while restoring cervical spine stability and preventing delayed deformity or instability.

Axial CT imaging of C3–C5 (Fig. 3) revealed significant bony destruction, raising considerable concern for potential intraoperative injury to the left vertebral artery. However, opting for MSR would result in inevitable cervical instability. Our surgical strategy was therefore divided into three key stages: arterial embolization, posterior fixation, and anterior corpectomy with fixation and cement augmentation.

Step 1 – Embolization

The patient was admitted, and a senior neuro-interventionist performed digital subtraction angiography (DSA). After confirming a patent and functional contralateral (right) vertebral artery, embolization of the left vertebral artery was performed using Onyx liquid embolic agent (2 mL total volume), achieving 90 % devascularization without permanent complications. Post-embolization, the patient developed left-sided facial nerve paresis (House-Brackmann Grade II), which completely resolved within five days.

Step 2 – Posterior fixation

One week following embolization, the second stage was carried out. The patient was placed in a prone position with the head secured in a Mayfield device. Under Multimodal neuromonitoring (Motor evoked Potential, Somatosensory Evoked Potential, free-running Electromyography), pedicle screws were placed at C2 (titanium alloy, 30 mm*4 mm) and C6 (titanium alloy, 25 mm*4 mm), identified as the first proximal and distal intact vertebrae. CT imaging (Figs. 1-B and 3) confirmed intact pedicles at C4. Therefore, a short pedicle screw was placed at C4 (titanium alloy, 14 mm*4 mm), taking care to avoid extending the distal screw tip into the vertebral body, thereby preserving space for the forthcoming anterior corpectomy (Fig. 4-A). Tumoral tissues accessible from the posterior approach were resected. Intraoperative blood loss was 300 mL, and the surgery duration was 150 min. The patient tolerated the procedure well, and postoperative examination showed no new focal neurological deficits (FND).

Step 3 – Anterior corpectomy and cement augmentation

Three days later, the final stage was performed. The patient was placed supine with the head again secured in Mayfield. A standard Smith-Robinson anterior cervical approach was used with a longitudinal skin incision. Corpectomies of C4 and C5 were performed using a high-speed drill, and the tumor was debulked as thoroughly as possible. A cage of appropriate size was inserted into the corpectomy site, and a cervical plate was affixed from C3 to C6 using four screws (Fig. 4-B). To further enhance construct stability and reinforce the C3 vertebral body, cement augmentation with 10 mL of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement was performed. Under C-arm guidance, a vertebroplasty needle was introduced into the C3 lesion cavity, and bone cement was injected (Fig. 1, Fig. 2; Fig. 4-B). Intraoperative blood loss was 250 mL, and the surgery duration was 180 min.

Early postoperative course and discharge

Following the final procedure, the patient was monitored in the ICU. She was ambulating independently by postoperative day two and demonstrated no new focal neurological deficits. Mild dysphagia was noted but resolved within four days. She was discharged on postoperative day six with a hard cervical collar and thorough instructions regarding precautions and warning signs. Thromboembolism chemoprophylaxis was initiated with Apixaban 2.5 mg every 12 h for 10 days.

Histopathology results

The curetted surgical specimen was submitted for pathological analysis. Histology revealed reactive fibroblasts, inflammatory cells, and woven bone within the cystic septa. Numerous osteoclast-type giant cells were noted, with clusters more prominent than typically seen. No cytologic atypia, necrosis, or atypical mitotic figures were present, although some mitoses were observed. These histological findings, in combination with radiographic features, confirmed the diagnosis of ABC.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient returned for follow-up two weeks after discharge, reporting complete resolution of axial neck pain and absence of radicular symptoms in the upper limbs. During that visit, she noted left shoulder pain, likely related to surgical positioning, which was effectively managed with NSAIDs (Naproxen 500 mg as needed) due to localized tenderness. At her one-month follow-up visit, the shoulder pain had fully resolved, and she was able to resume daily activities without limitation or new complaints.

Subsequent follow-up visits were conducted at three and six months postoperatively. At the two-year postoperative follow-up, both MRI and cervical CT imaging (Fig. 5) were obtained, demonstrating solid spinal fusion, a stable construct, and no evidence of tumor recurrence or instrumentation failure. We recommend a structured surveillance protocol for postoperative ABCs, consisting of clinical evaluation (including pain and neurologic status) and imaging (CT/MRI) at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, followed by annual evaluations up to 5 years, with additional MRI performed for symptom recurrence.

Discussion

The treatment of ABC exhibits significant variability. Options include simple curettage with or without bone grafting, complete excision, embolization, radiation therapy, or a combination of these methods [8]. While various treatment approaches have shown favorable outcomes, local recurrence remains unpredictable across all therapeutic protocols. Cases treated with ‘en bloc’ resection have demonstrated no recurrence, but this technique is associated with high surgical morbidity [9]. Recurrence rates are notably higher when the lesion is not completely removed. Specifically, recurrence rates for decompression/laminectomy (42.3 %), partial excision (35.7 %), and curettage (25 %) are significantly greater than those for complete excision/resection (8.2 %) [9].

Radiation therapy has been proposed both as a primary treatment and as an adjuvant. However, its use requires caution due to the potential for long-term malignant transformation and the risks posed by proximity to critical neurological structures [10]. Denosumab has emerged as an alternative treatment option in non-surgical ABCs, showing promising effects on pain relief and neurological symptoms. Despite these benefits, the oncological prognosis remains uncertain, with a recurrence rate of 18.6 % reported during or after denosumab therapy. Moreover, in younger patients, severe rebound hypercalcemia can occur several months after discontinuation [11].

Our multidisciplinary approach contrasts with minimally invasive percutaneous sclerotherapy using agents like sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS) or doxycycline, which typically requires 1–3 sessions and shows 10–20 % recurrence in extremity ABCs with low complications like bleeding or infection [12]. However, for multilevel cervical ABCs (C3-C5) with vertebral artery involvement, surgery offers key advantages: direct decompression and stabilization to mitigate neurologic risks from sclerotherapy-induced swelling or incomplete control, plus lower recurrence (<5 %) despite higher initial morbidity [13]. Surgery often requires multiple stages (2 in our case), with potential complications such as blood loss, infection, or hardware failure. Functional outcomes are comparable in non-aggressive lesions; however, surgery excels in restoring stability in cases of multilevel spinal involvement [8].

The decision for preoperative embolization and staged resection was driven by imaging features including extensive cystic expansion on MRI encroaching on the vertebral arteries at C3-C5, vertebral body destruction >50 %, and clinical symptoms of myelopathy (e.g., gait instability and upper extremity weakness). These factors increased the risk of sclerotherapy-related complications, such as intralesional hemorrhage or edema-induced cord compression, prompting a surgical strategy for definitive control and restoration of spinal column stability.

A detailed surgical plan is paramount in the management of cervical ABCs. Innovative, case-specific approaches are crucial for addressing three fundamental goals in treatment [14]. First, achieving MSR is critical for reducing recurrence while minimizing neurovascular risk [15]. Second, restoring or maintaining cervical spinal stability is vital, as cervical myelopathy resulting from instability can drastically impair quality of life. Third, it is essential to preserve neurovascular integrity during surgery and prevent postoperative deformity [16,17].

In our case, we prioritized MSR while maintaining cervical spine stability. The extent of tumor involvement from C3 to C5 presented significant challenges for fixation. We employed circumferential instrumentation, including a short pedicle screw at C4 that terminated at the pedicle-body junction, allowing for anterior corpectomy without interference. The bony destruction also posed a high risk to the left vertebral artery during tumor resection. To mitigate this, we performed preoperative embolization after confirming safety with our endovascular team, which helped reduce intraoperative blood loss [18]. Following embolization and occlusion of the left vertebral artery, right-sided pedicle screws were placed with extreme caution to avoid iatrogenic vertebral artery injury, which could have been fatal [19]. Comprehensive imaging review and anatomical understanding are essential in selecting the optimal surgical strategy for cervical spinal lesions.

Management of cervical ABCs demands a multidisciplinary approach involving spine surgeons and interventional radiologists. The surgical technique must be tailored to the lesion's exact size and location, and the use of appropriate instrumentation is crucial for achieving long-term stability and a successful fusion.

Conclusion

Our patient presented with a complex case of cervical aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC). Successful treatment required detailed imaging evaluation and a three-dimensional understanding of the lesion's anatomy. Maximal safe resection provided the best opportunity for tumor control while reducing the risk of recurrence. The management of cervical ABCs necessitates a personalized, innovative approach, comprehensive radiographic assessment, and collaborative decision-making among a multidisciplinary team to achieve optimal surgical and long-term outcomes.
