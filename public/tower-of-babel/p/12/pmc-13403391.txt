Emotional intelligence and self-compassion as predictors of academic resilience among allied medical sciences students: a structural equation modeling study
Authors: Elham Shahidi Delshad, Afzal Shamsi, Parnia Karimian, Nazanin Mohammadzade Jamalian, Sajad Yarahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAllied health sciences students face considerable psychological demands during clinical training, which may contribute to elevated levels of stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. Identifying protective psychological factors is therefore important for supporting student well-being. This study examined a structural model of the associations among emotional intelligence, self-compassion, and academic resilience, with a focus on the potential mediating role of self-compassion.MethodsThis multi-center cross-sectional study included 452 students from anesthesia, operating room technology, and pre-hospital emergency care programs across four universities in Tehran. Data were collected using the Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form (SCS-SF), Brief Emotional Intelligence Scale (BEIS-10), and Academic Resilience Scale (ARS), all showing acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.70). Data were analyzed using Pearson correlation and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in AMOS. Model fit was evaluated using GFI, CFI, and RMSEA, and indirect effects were tested via bootstrapping.ResultsEmotional intelligence, self-compassion, and academic resilience were significantly and positively correlated (p < 0.001). The proposed model demonstrated acceptable fit (GFI = 0.94, CFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.072). Emotional intelligence (β = 0.440, p < 0.001) and self-compassion (β = 0.419, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with academic resilience, explaining 65% of its variance. Bootstrapping results indicated a significant indirect association of emotional intelligence with academic resilience through self-compassion (β = 0.321, p = 0.004).ConclusionThe findings suggest that emotional intelligence and self-compassion are important correlates of academic resilience among allied health sciences students. The results support an integrated model that may inform the design of interventions aimed at strengthening psychological resources and improving student well-being. Given the cross-sectional design, causal interpretations should be made with caution.

Introduction

Healthcare students frequently experience psychological challenges during their education, which can adversely affect both academic performance and mental health. A recent study reported high prevalence rates of anxiety (85.1%), stress (65%), and depression (51.5%) among these students [1]. These psychological burdens stem from the demanding nature of healthcare education, which requires students not only to achieve academic excellence but also to acquire advanced clinical competencies and interpersonal skills necessary for patient-centered care [2]. Students in allied medical sciences programs, including anesthesia [3–5], operating room technology [6, 7], and pre-hospital emergency care, may be particularly vulnerable because of the high-risk, time-sensitive, and emotionally demanding nature of their clinical training environments [4].

Given these academic and clinical stressors, success in allied medical sciences programs requires a high level of academic resilience (AR) [8]. Academic resilience is recognized as a critical psychological capacity that enables students to effectively cope with academic difficulties, adapt to challenges, and maintain academic achievement [9]. A systematic review of 15 studies further demonstrated that higher AR is consistently associated with improved academic outcomes, including higher grade point averages and more successful progression through educational programs [10].Consequently, identifying psychological factors that contribute to AR has become an important area of research in healthcare education.

Among the psychological factors associated with AR, emotional intelligence (EI) and self-compassion (SC) have received increasing attention [11]. Emotional intelligence refers to the ability to perceive, understand, regulate, and appropriately respond to one’s own emotions and those of others [12]. Previous studies have shown that higher EI is associated with improved clinical reasoning, empathy, stress management, and professional development among allied medical sciences students [13]. In surgical and operating room settings, higher EI has also been linked to reduced burnout, more effective teamwork, and better communication skills [14].

Self-compassion is another important psychological construct that may strengthen students’ adaptive functioning in stressful educational settings. SC is commonly defined as the ability to recognize suffering in oneself and others while maintaining a supportive and nonjudgmental attitude toward that suffering [15]. Self-compassion is recognized as a significant factor in cultivating compassion for others, particularly in recognizing failure and suffering as inherent to the human experience [16]. Students with higher SC report lower academic stress and greater academic self-efficacy [15]. In nursing education, SC has been identified as an important psychological buffer that helps students manage the considerable stress associated with academic and clinical training [17]. Moreover, higher SC is associated with more positive coping strategies and better management of academic pressures, potentially reducing anxiety, depression, and other adverse psychological outcomes during the transition to professional clinical practice [18].

Previous research has examined the relationships between psychological constructs such as EI, SC, and AR among healthcare students [19–21]. However, findings regarding the association between SC and AR remain inconsistent, with some studies reporting no significant relationship [20]. In contrast, studies on general resilience among nursing students suggest that SC positively influences coping and adaptive functioning [22]. Furthermore, EI has been shown to predict resilience through the mediating role of SC among Nursing Auxiliary Care Technicians [23]. Other studies have demonstrated positive associations between EI and AR, mediated by factors such as self-directed learning ability and self-regulation [19, 21].

Despite these findings, important gaps remain in the literature. Most previous studies have focused primarily on nursing students or have examined these variables separately rather than within an integrated framework. In addition, limited evidence exists regarding how these psychological constructs interact in high-pressure allied medical sciences disciplines such as anesthesia, operating room technology, and pre-hospital emergency care. Therefore, the current study aimed to investigate the relationships among SC, EI, and AR among allied medical sciences students and to examine the magnitude, direction, and potential mediating pathways among these constructs. By evaluating these variables within a comprehensive model, this study seeks to provide a deeper understanding of the psychological factors influencing AR in students enrolled in demanding clinical education programs.

Conceptual framework

The present study is based on the assumption that SC, EI, and AR are interrelated psychological constructs that contribute to students’ psychological adjustment and academic success in demanding healthcare programs. Previous evidence suggests that SC may help students cope more effectively with stress and academic setbacks, while EI may enhance emotional regulation, interpersonal competence, and adaptive functioning [11, 19–23]. In addition, EI may indirectly contribute to AR through its relationship with SC.

Although several studies have examined some of these associations separately, their combined relationships have not been evaluated within a single integrated model among allied medical sciences students. Therefore, the current study explores the magnitude, direction, and potential mediating pathways among SC, EI, and AR using a theory-informed structural equation modeling approach within a theory-building framework.

Method

Research design and participants

This multi-center, cross-sectional analytical study employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to investigate the relationships between EI, SC, and AR. Data were collected from January 2025 to September 2025. The study population consisted of students in anesthesia, operating room technology, and pre-hospital emergency care from four universities in Tehran: Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Iran University of Medical Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, and Islamic Azad University of Medical Sciences.

Participants were selected using convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were: being a student in the second semester or higher of a Bachelor’s degree program and willingness to participate. Students were excluded from the study if they were in their first semester or were not in the designated fields of anesthesia, operating room technology, or pre-hospital emergency care.

Measures

Demographic questionnaire

A demographic questionnaire was used to collect participants’ characteristics, including age, gender, and academic semester.

Self-Compassion Scale (SCS-SF)

Self-compassion was measured using the 12-item short form of the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS-SF), originally developed by Raes et al. [24]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“almost never”) to 5 (“almost always”), yielding total scores between 12 and 60, with higher scores indicating greater SC [24]. The Persian version of the SCS-SF was previously translated and psychometrically validated by Khanjani et al., who reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 [25]. In the present study, internal consistency was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82.

Brief Emotional Intelligence Scale (BEIS-10)

Emotional intelligence was assessed using the 10-item Brief Emotional Intelligence Scale (BEIS-10), a shortened version of the original EI Scale developed by Schutte et al. [26]. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), with total scores ranging from 10 to 50, where higher scores reflect higher EI [27]. The Persian version has been previously validated by Haddadian et al., who reported acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.74) [28]. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.71.

Academic Resilience Scale (ARS)

Academic resilience was measured using the 40-item Academic Resilience Scale (ARS), developed by Samuels and Wu [29]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger AR [29]. The validated Persian version of the ARS was reported by Soltaninejad et al. [30]. In the present study, the scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87).

Procedure

This study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval and a project code from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences. The researchers designed an online questionnaire on the Porsline platform. A cover page was presented to participants at the beginning of the questionnaire, explaining the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of their participation, and the confidentiality of their information. Students provided their informed consent by clicking the “I agree to participate in this study” button before proceeding. The questionnaire’s link was distributed by the heads of the anesthesia, operating room, and pre-hospital emergency care departments to student groups via social media platforms such as Telegram and WhatsApp. All responses were kept anonymous and confidential.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0) and AMOS (Version 25.0). Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics, assessment of normality (skewness and kurtosis), and evaluation of multicollinearity. Bivariate associations among the main study variables were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. To examine the hypothesized associations among the study variables, a covariance-based structural equation modeling (SEM) approach was applied in AMOS. Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including GFI, CFI, and RMSEA. The potential mediating role of SC in the associations between EI and AR was evaluated using bootstrapping procedures with 5,000 resamples. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in full compliance with the ethical principles for human research, as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, with the assigned code IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1404.004. All participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. A cover page was presented at the beginning of the online questionnaire, clearly explaining the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of all data. Participants could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty, and non-participation or withdrawal would have no impact on their academic standing or educational processes. All responses were collected anonymously, and data were kept strictly confidential to protect participants’ privacy.

Results

This section presents the findings from the data analysis, starting with a descriptive overview and then detailing the inferential statistical results related to the research hypotheses. The analysis was conducted on a sample of 452 allied medical sciences students, comprising 208 in anesthesia, 126 in operating room, and 118 in pre-hospital emergency care. The mean age of participants was 21.3 years (SD = 2.02; range = 18–35), and the majority were female (59.7%).

Descriptive statistics of the main variables

The mean, standard deviation, and range of scores were calculated for the main study variables: EI, SC, and AR. The mean score for SC was 38.53 (SD = 6.45), while EI had a mean score of 30.80 (SD = 3.79). The mean AR score was 105.94 (SD = 13.79). Correlation analysis

A Pearson correlation matrix was computed to examine the bivariate relationships between the main variables. The results revealed statistically significant positive correlations among all three variables in the total sample. A strong positive correlation was found between SC and AR (r = 0.668, p < 0.001). Similarly, a strong positive correlation was observed between EI and AR (r = 0.649, p < 0.001). The correlation between SC and EI was also strong and positive (r = 0.617, p < 0.001). These results suggest that higher scores in EI and SC were associated with higher AR scores in this sample.

Assessment of statistical assumptions

Prior to SEM, key statistical assumptions were evaluated to ensure the validity of the analysis. The assumption of no outliers was confirmed. The sample size of 452 was considered appropriate and acceptable for SEM, as it exceeds the recommended minimum of 200 cases [31]. Skewness and kurtosis values for all main variables fell within the acceptable range of -2 to + 2, indicating that the data were normally distributed [32]. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values for EI and SC were both 1.39, well below the threshold of 5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Finally, a scatter plot analysis demonstrated a linear relationship between the main variables.

Structural equation modeling

The proposed structural model was tested using AMOS software. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the structural model demonstrated several statistically significant relationships among the study variables. The magnitude and direction of the standardized path coefficients indicate that some constructs exerted stronger predictive effects than others, highlighting the relative importance of these pathways within the proposed conceptual framework. In particular, the significant positive coefficients suggest that increases in the predictor variables were associated with corresponding increases in the outcome variables. Moreover, the overall pattern of relationships supports the theoretical assumptions underlying the model and provides empirical evidence for the interconnected nature of the latent constructs.

Discussion

The findings of this study support a coherent, theoretically model that elucidates the interplay among EI, SC, and AR among allied medical sciences students. Addressing a critical gap in prior research, which was often confined to bivariate associations and predominantly focused on nursing cohorts, our findings, provide empirical support for an integrated structural model within the high-pressure training environments of anesthesia, operating room technology, and pre-hospital emergency care. The proposed SEM, which confirmed excellent fit indices, collectively accounted for a substantial 65% of the variance in AR.

In this context, academic stress represents a key background factor influencing students’ learning processes and coping strategies. Evidence suggests that effective management of academic stress is essential for promoting adaptive learning approaches and psychological well-being in healthcare students, highlighting the importance of targeted educational and supportive interventions within nursing and allied health education [33].

In the descriptive analysis of the variables, the patterns of association differed in intensity. Emotional intelligence demonstrated a moderate presence. Similar studies in Iran and Saudi Arabia also confirm this finding and show that the level of emotional intelligence in paramedical and nursing students is moderate [34–37]. The average level of emotional intelligence suggests that while students generally possess some capacity to recognize and regulate emotions, this skill set is not uniformly strong and may benefit from structured educational support.

Self-compassion showed relatively weaker levels, indicating that many students still struggle with self-criticism and may lack consistent self-supportive attitudes in the face of academic or clinical challenges. This is despite studies in Iran and Turkey showing that self-compassion is at moderate to high levels among nursing students [15, 38–40]. This difference between nursing and paramedic students may be due to the nature of the courses in the disciplines: the nursing curriculum focuses on mental health and psychiatric nursing, which is also necessary in paramedic disciplines.

By contrast, academic resilience was expressed at a comparatively stronger level, reflecting the determination and persistence often inherent in students of paramedical sciences. Many studies still place resilience and academic resilience at a moderate [20], rather than “high” or “very strong” [41]. Also, for paramedic students, there is less data, and what exists tends toward moderate [42]. This profile suggests that while resilience exists as a broad personal strength, its foundations in emotional intelligence and self-compassion require further reinforcement to ensure sustainable adaptation under stress.

The correlations among the variables were aligned with the study’s hypotheses. Emotional intelligence was consistently associated with both higher self-compassion and stronger resilience, underscoring the roles of emotion regulation and empathy in adaptive coping. In this regard, recent studies have shown that paramedic students not only increasingly use emotion regulation strategies such as psychological distance, cognitive framing, and attention management in dealing with critical situations, but also that their level of empathy is significantly correlated with academic resilience and various dimensions of emotional intelligence; this suggests that strengthening emotional intelligence and empathy may be an effective strategy for promoting academic resilience and self-empathy in this student group [42, 43]. Self-compassion also showed a clear association with resilience, a finding confirmed by previous studies in other student communities [44, 45]. This association reinforces the idea that kind and non-judgmental attitudes toward oneself are protective in high-stress educational environments. The moderate-to-strong nature of these correlations suggests that although these constructs are distinct, they mutually reinforce one another in promoting students’ adaptive functioning.

Consistent with this, prior research in nursing education has shown that emotional intelligence is positively associated with reflective thinking and adaptive learning processes, suggesting that students with higher emotional intelligence demonstrate stronger cognitive and metacognitive engagement in clinical decision-making. Furthermore, integrating emotional intelligence training into nursing curricula has been recommended as a key strategy for enhancing emotional regulation, empathy, and interpersonal competence, ultimately improving students’ readiness for clinical practice and professional resilience [46].

The structural model further clarified the dynamics at play. Emotional intelligence directly contributed to resilience but also exerted an indirect effect through self-compassion. This mediating pathway suggests that the ability to recognize and manage emotions may contribute to a more compassionate stance toward oneself, which in turn is associated with higher resilience [47, 48]. It is critical to note that the relationship found is one of partial mediation; the strong, significant direct effect of EI on AR persisted even after accounting for SC. This indicates that EI supports resilience through mechanisms beyond fostering self-compassion. Specifically, core EI dimensions, such as emotion perception and regulation, are crucial for immediate adaptive functioning in clinical settings, facilitating effective teamwork, rapid problem-solving, and cognitive regulation under pressure [49].

Therefore, the findings underscore an integrated model in which EI supports AR through two essential routes: an internal route via SC and an external route through practical and interpersonal skill enhancement. At the same time, the persistence of a direct pathway indicates that emotional intelligence also supports resilience independently, likely through mechanisms such as problem-solving, teamwork, and cognitive regulation. Taken together, the model emphasizes that both direct and mediated pathways are important for explaining resilience in the context of paramedical education.

Limitations and future directions

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences among the study variables; therefore, causal interpretations cannot be established, and longitudinal studies are needed to examine temporal relationships. Second, the exclusive use of self-report questionnaires may have introduced common method bias, which could have inflated the observed associations between variables. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate multi-method or objective assessments to reduce this limitation. Finally, the use of a convenience sample from four universities in Tehran restricts the generalizability of the findings to other cultural and educational contexts. Future research should extend this model by including additional psychological and contextual variables, such as social support, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of academic resilience.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that emotional intelligence and self-compassion are important correlates of academic resilience among paramedical students. While resilience appears to be a relatively strong personal resource, its development and sustainability are closely linked to students’ ability to regulate emotions and adopt a self-compassionate perspective. By implementing targeted educational strategies aimed at strengthening these psychological attributes, academic institutions may better support students in managing academic and clinical demands, thereby promoting their well-being and potentially enhancing the quality of future clinical practice.
