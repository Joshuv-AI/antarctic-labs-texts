Role of one-carbon metabolism variants in the associations between perfluoroalkyl substances exposure and birth size in the Taiwan Birth Panel Study
Authors: Chih-Fu Wei, Mei-Huei Chen, Ching-Chun Lin, Tzu-Pin Lu, Chia-Yang Chen, Wu-Shiun Hsieh, Pau-Chung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:One-carbon metabolism (OCM) is crucial for fetal development, while perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are associated with adverse birth outcomes and lower folate levels. PFAS may compete with folate for receptors, and OCM genes regulate folate metabolism; however, no study examined if OCM variants modified PFAS-birth outcome associations. The study investigated the relationship between OCM variants, prenatal PFAS exposure, and birth outcomes.Methods:We analyzed birth weight and head circumference data from 338 mother-infant pairs in the Taiwan Birth Panel Study. Cord blood concentrations of four PFAS were measured using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Infant genotypes were assessed with the Taiwan Biobank Array, and we focused on five single-nucleotide polymorphisms related to OCM reported in previous literature. Gene-environment interactions were assessed using generalized linear regression and two mixture modeling approaches: quantile g-computation and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression.Results:Prenatal perfluorooctane sulfonate and perfluoroundecanoic acid exposure were associated with lower birth weight, and PFAS mixture exposure was associated with lower birth weight. PFAS exposure-birth weight associations were more obvious among those with reference genotypes. Different PFAS mixture-birth weight associations were observed across MTHFR rs1801133 and genotypes, and there were stronger inverse associations among individuals with MTHFD1 rs2236225 GG genotype. Associations between prenatal PFAS exposure and birth outcomes also differed by MTR rs1805087, MTRR rs1801394, and CBS rs234714 genotypes. In addition, we observed a stronger perfluorooctane sulfonate-head circumference association among participants with MTHFR rs1801133 GG genotype.Conclusion:Genetic variants in OCM pathways modified the associations between prenatal individual PFAS, mixture PFAS exposure, and reduced birth weight and head circumference.

What this study adds

One-carbon metabolism (OCM) plays a key role in fetal growth, and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) exposure is associated with lower folate levels and poor birth outcomes. However, it remains unclear if genetic differences in OCM-related pathways influence these effects. This study shows that prenatal PFAS exposure is common in Taiwan and associated with lower birth weight. The effects of PFAS on birth weight were stronger in individuals with multiple OCM variants. These findings suggest that OCM-related genetic polymorphisms may influence susceptibility to PFAS toxicity. Future studies should explore whether specific one-carbon metabolites can help reduce these effects.

Introduction

Perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are highly persistent chemicals widely found in the environment and human populations around the globe,1 which have been shown to influence birth outcomes.2,3 Observational studies have shown the associations between PFAS exposure and low birth weight, shorter gestational age, and preterm birth.2,4–6 Meta-analyses have also shown that exposure to individual PFAS is associated with reduced birth weight.3,7 In addition to adverse birth outcomes, PFAS exposure has been associated with oxidative stress, immune and thyroid dysfunction, liver and kidney disease, and disruptions in lipid and insulin regulation.8–14 Epigenetic changes, such as altered DNA methylation, may help explain the harmful effects of PFAS exposure, as shown in animal studies.15–17

PFAS exposure may disrupt one-carbon metabolism (OCM). A study using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey found that PFAS exposure was negatively associated with red blood cell folate levels.18 Furthermore, PFAS exposure has also been associated with lower vitamin B12 and higher homocysteine levels, which may increase disease risk.19–21 For example, a study from Project Viva showed that PFAS-related birth weight reduction was observed among mothers with lower folate levels, but not among those with higher levels.6 These findings may be explained by competition for shared transporters with folate in the human body.22,23 Another possibility is epigenetic disruption, as these one-carbon metabolites support DNA methylation, while PFAS exposure alters DNA methylation patterns.24,25 Together, these findings indicated that individual susceptibility to PFAS toxicity may vary by genetic variations in OCM.26–28

OCM is a biologic pathway that supports DNA synthesis, methylation, nucleotide biosynthesis, amino acid homeostasis, epigenetic regulation, and redox balance during fetal development. Folate and B vitamins play central roles in this pathway.26,27,29–32 Adequate folate levels support DNA synthesis and methylation during pregnancy,26,33 which was associated with improved birth weight, gestational length, head circumference, and brain development.12,33–35 Given such importance, guidelines recommended maintaining adequate folate levels through folic acid provision to prevent neural tube defects and preterm birth.36,37 Vitamin B12, an essential component in OCM, is also crucial for fetal growth and neurodevelopment, which was associated with intrauterine growth restriction and cognitive impairment in early childhood.26,38–40 These vitamins support long-term development through their roles in nutrition,38 methylation,24,41 homocysteine metabolism,42,43 and the detoxification of environmental pollutants.44 Multiple OCM genetic variants are involved in these reactions: For example, rs1801133 in MTHFR influences folate availability and methylation capacity;26,27,29,30 rs2236225 in MTHFD1 affects folate derivatives conversion for nucleotide synthesis and cellular methylation;31,44 rs1805087 in MTR and rs1801394 in MTRR are involved in vitamin B12-dependent homocysteine remethylation;26,32 and rs234714 in CBS regulates homocysteine clearance through the transsulfuration pathway.44,45 As PFAS may influence methylation, folate levels, and may compete for the same receptor, interactions between PFAS exposure and OCM-related genes are biologically plausible. However, no study examined the relationship between PFAS exposure, OCM variants, and birth outcomes, which could identify specific targets for precision intervention of PFAS exposure in humans.

Using data from the Taiwan Birth Panel Study (TBPS), we examined the associations of individual and mixture PFAS exposures with birth weight and head circumference, and examined whether these associations varied by key OCM genetic variants. This study extends prior work by examining PFAS mixtures and the key OCM genetic variations in relation to birth outcomes.

Methods

Study population

The study subjects were from the TBPS, a longitudinal birth cohort study conducted since 2004 at one medical center in Taipei, one local hospital, and two clinics in New Taipei City.46 Participants were recruited between July 2004 and June 2005, trained interviewers administered a structured questionnaire to mothers during their hospital stays for delivery. Cord blood was collected at birth and stored at −80 °C until further laboratory analysis. Children underwent follow-up health examination and standardized questionnaire assessment at 4, 6, 12, 24, 60, 84, and 108 months of age. In this study, we included 338 mother-infant pairs with complete records of body weight, head circumference, PFAS levels, and genetic information (Figure S1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). The Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University Hospital approved the study (Project Identification Code: 201112137RIC), and informed consent was obtained from the parents before enrollment in the study and at each wave of follow-up.

Outcome definition

The outcomes of interest in this study were body weight and head circumference at birth, which were measured by trained nurses and recorded in medical records. The study team then abstracted the information from the medical records. The study team also collected the information of gestational age, which was assessed by obstetricians based on the last menstruation or using estimates from the ultrasound during early pregnancy. We also demonstrated the percentage of small for gestational age according to the local standard.47

Exposure assessment

Cord blood PFAS concentrations were tested with ultra-high performance liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry (Waters ACQUITY Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography system and Waters Quattro Premier XE triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). Detailed analytic methods were described previously.48 We included those PFASs with a detection rate >60% in the TBPS study, which were perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), and perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUA). Values below the limits of quantitation were assigned as one-half of the quantitation limit for the main analysis.

Genotyping and candidate single-nucleotide polymorphisms

We extracted the DNA from cord blood within the study population.46 Samples with adequate quality and quantity were genotyped using the Taiwan Biobank array v2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was designed for the Taiwanese population with an enrichment of rare coding risk alleles based on the whole genome sequencing results.49 Genetic variants were annotated based on the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38.

We selected single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with OCM from findings in the GWAS catalog and relevant literature. These SNPs included MTHFR rs1801133, MTHFD1 rs2236225, MTR rs1805087, MTRR rs1801394, and CBS rs234714 (Figure S2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). For each SNP, participants with the most common genotype were classified as the reference group, and those with all other genotypes were classified as the comparison group. In compare to the reference genotypes, the other genotypes of the included SNPs are associated with reduced function of the coded enzyme in general. We summarized the information on the variants, alternative alleles, related genes, and their functions in Table S1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418.27,29–32

Covariates

Data on maternal characteristics were collected via questionnaires and interviews at enrollment. Potential confounders were selected based on prior knowledge and directed acyclic graphs (Figure S2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). The following demographic and socioeconomic variables were included as confounders: gestational age, sex of newborn, maternal age (>35 years old or not), parity (first or others), maternal educational level (high school or above), and household income (monthly income above 100,000 New Taiwan dollars or not), maternal prepregnancy body mass index (BMI, calculated by self-reported weight divided by the square of height (kg/m2), grouped as BMI with 18.5–24 kg/m2 or others), and secondhand smoke exposure during pregnancy (yes or no).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were presented as counts with percentages. We also reported the distribution and Spearman correlation coefficients for the four PFAS concentrations. Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the associations between PFAS concentrations and birth weight or head circumference. PFAS concentrations were analyzed in both original and log2-transformed scales in the regression models, and most results are presented using the log2-transformed scale for improved model fit and interpretability as the related change per doubling of PFAS concentration.50 Additionally, we categorized the PFAS concentrations by tertiles to examine the potential nonlinear associations.

We then studied the SNP-PFAS interactions on birth weight and head circumference by incorporating interaction terms between PFAS concentration and genotype in regression models. Meanwhile, stratified analyses by genotype were conducted to compare PFAS-birth weight or head circumference associations in the most common genotype with those in other genotypes.51

We applied quantile-based g-computation (QGC) and Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) to assess the joint association of PFAS mixtures on birth weight and head circumference.52,53 For mixture analysis, PFAS levels were log-standardized, following the approach of Zhang et al.6 We conducted stratified and interaction analysis for PFAS mixture and the selected SNPs using the same approach.

Sensitivity analysis

We compared the demographic characteristics of included participants with others in the TBPS to assess potential selection bias. We examined the associations between each OCM variant and individual PFAS concentrations using regression models to evaluate potential gene-environment correlation.54 Additional adjustments were made for fish intake and prenatal vitamin use to account for potential confounding from dietary and nutritional status during pregnancy. Delivery mode was additionally adjusted in the head circumference model. For PFAS concentrations below the limit of quantitation, we used multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 20) to impute missing PFAS concentrations using predictive mean matching, and we fitted QGC models among the imputed datasets.55 We also tested the nonlinear association for each PFAS and the PFAS-PFAS interactions using BKMR. The analyses were conducted using packages “qgcomp” (version 2.15.2), “qgcompint” (version 0.7.0), and “bkmr” (version 0.2.2) in R version 4.4.1.56

Results

Study population characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. The mean age of mothers was 30.5 years (standard deviation [SD]: 4.6), with 20.5% of mothers above 35 years old. Participants in this study had relatively higher socioeconomic status compared with the general population in Taiwan, with 41.9% of mothers having attained education beyond college and 38.4% of families reporting a monthly income above 100,000 New Taiwan dollars. Few mothers used alcohol and tobacco use before pregnancy (4.4% and 6.2%, respectively), but 29.3% mothers were exposed to secondhand smoke exposure during pregnancy. There were 172 boys and 169 girls. Approximately half were firstborn (48.4%, n = 165), and 37.8% were delivered via Cesarean section. The mean gestational age was 38.4 weeks (SD: 1.7 weeks), and 8.8% participants were preterm births. The average birth weight was 3,162.0 grams, and the percentage of small for gestational age and low birth weight were 5.6% and 5.6%. The average head circumference was 33.5 centimeters (SD: 1.7).

Perfluoroalkyl substances concentrations and genotype distributions

The mean concentrations of PFOA, PFNA, PFOS, and PFUA were 2.7 (SD: 2.5), 6.8 (SD: 8.9), 7.7 (SD: 7.6), and 17.5 (SD: 17.0, unit: ng/ml). The four PFAS (PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, and PFUA) were detected in 68.9%–100% of participants, which were similar to the detection rates of PFOA, PFNA, PFOS, and PFUA in the TBPS population.57 The concentrations of PFOA, PFOS, and PFNA were higher than the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys and Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes Program in the United States during 1999 to 2019.4 The distribution of individual PFAS concentrations was right-skewed. Moderate correlations were observed between PFOA, PFNA, and PFUA, while PFOS showed no strong correlation with the others (Figure 1).

Associations between perfluoroalkyl substance exposure and birth size

After adjusting for confounders, each doubling of PFOS concentration was associated with 47.28 grams lower birth weight. Participants with the highest tertile of PFOS concentration had 125.64 grams lower birth weight than those with the lowest tertile concentrations. Those with the middle tertile of PFUA concentrations showed 100.33 grams lower birth weight than those with the lowest tertile concentrations. Furthermore, each tertile of increased PFAS mixture concentration was associated with 113.05 grams lower birth weight in the QGC model.

A similar trend was observed for head circumference, with fewer associations observed. Each doubling of PFOS concentration was associated with 0.26 cm lower head circumference in the adjusted model, and those with the highest tertile of PFOS exposure had 0.56 cm lower head circumference than those with the lowest tertiles of PFOS. Meanwhile, no other association was found between other PFAS or PFAS mixture exposure and head circumference in the study population (Figure 2 and Table S2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418).

Associations between perfluoroalkyl substances and birth weight, stratified by one-carbon metabolism single-nucleotide polymorphisms

The associations between PFAS concentrations and birth weight differed by OCM polymorphisms, and we demonstrated the estimates from stratified and interaction models across all included SNPs in Figure 2 and Table S3; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418. For MTHFD1 rs2236225, PFOA, PFOS, and PFAS mixture exposure were associated with lower birth weight among individuals with the GG genotype, while PFUA exposure was associated with lower birth weight among participants with other genotypes. For MTRR rs1801394, the inverse associations between PFOA, PFUA, and PFAS mixture on birth weight were observed among participants with the AA genotype. For CBS rs234714, the associations between PFOA, PFAS mixture, and lower birth weight were observed among participants with CC genotypes but not among participants with other genotypes.

As for mixture analysis, PFAS mixture-birth weight association was found among participants with MTHFR rs1801133 GG genotype (−132.73 gram per tertile increase of PFAS mixture, 95% confidence interval [CI] = −243.69, −21.77) but not for other genotypes. We also observed a negative PFAS mixture-birth weight association for those with MTR rs1805087 AA genotype (−90.30 gram per tertile increase of PFAS mixture, 95% CI = −173.54, −7.06) using the QGC models. However, the PFAS mixture-SNP interaction on birth weight was not significant. The genotype-stratified linear dose-response relationships between mixed exposures and birth weight in QGC models were shown in Figure S3; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418. We further evaluated the nonlinear, dose-response associations between the PFAS mixture and birth weight using BKMR, and the PFAS exposure-birth weight relationships differed by genotype (Figure 3, Figures S4 and S5; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). We found the nonlinear associations between the individual PFASs and birth weight varied across the included SNP genotypes. The association between the overall PFAS mixture and birth weight also differed by genotype. However, we did not observe strong evidence of interaction among individual PFAS compounds, as the credible intervals included the null.

Associations between perfluoroalkyl substances and head circumference, stratified by one-carbon metabolism single-nucleotide polymorphisms

Several associations were observed for head circumference in the genotype-stratified analysis. PFOS exposure was inversely associated with head circumference among those with MTHFR rs1801133 GG genotype (−0.25 cm per doubling of PFOS concentration, 95% CI = −0.50, −0.01), and there was an interaction between PFNA exposure and MTHFR rs1801133 on head circumference (p-for-interaction: 0.038, Figure 2). The association between the PFAS mixture and head circumference also varied by genotype in the quantile g-computation model (Figure S6 and Table S4; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). Although we did not observe significant interaction between PFAS mixture and head circumference in the BKMR analysis, nonlinear relationships between individual PFAS exposure and head circumference varied across different genotypes (Figure 3 and Figure S7; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). The pairwise interactions between individual PFAS also varied according to different OCM SNPs (Figures S8 and S9; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418).

Sensitivity analysis

The selected mother-infant pairs were demographically similar to nonselected participants in TBPS, except that the mothers in nonselected group were slightly older (Table S5; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). We found no significant associations between any candidate SNPs and PFAS concentrations in the study population (Table S6; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). Additional adjustment of fish intake during pregnancy did not dramatically alter the associations between individual and mixture PFAS exposure on birth weight and head circumferences (Table S7; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). Also, adjusting for maternal fish intake during pregnancy did not drastically change the observed PFAS-SNP interactions and genotype-stratified results on birth weight (Table S8; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418) or head circumference (Table S9; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). Similarly, additional adjustment of prenatal vitamin use did not substantially alter the associations between individual and mixture PFAS exposure on birth weight and head circumferences (Table S10; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). The PFAS-SNP interactions and genotype-stratified results on birth weight (Table S11; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418) or head circumference (Table S12; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418) were not drastically altered after additional adjustment of prenatal vitamin use. These findings suggested a lower likelihood of confounding by prenatal diet (proxy using fish intake) and vitamin status (proxy using vitamin intake). Additional adjustment for mode of delivery did not materially change the association between PFAS exposure and head circumference, where we observed the inverse association between PFOS and head circumference (−0.19 cm per doubling of PFOS concentration, 95% CI = −0.36, −0.02, Table S13; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418). Results from the QGC models remained similar after applying multiple imputation for concentrations below the limit of quantitation (Table S14; https://links.lww.com/EE/A418).

Discussion

The study showed the association between PFAS exposure and birth weight varies by genetic variants in OCM, and the associations were stronger among those with reference genotypes. Among individuals with MTHFR rs1801133 GG genotype, PFAS mixture was associated with lower birth weight, and PFOS exposure was associated with smaller head circumference. Individuals with the MTHFD1 rs2236225 GG genotype were more susceptible to birth weight reduction related to PFOA, PFOS, and PFAS mixture exposure. Also, we observed inverse associations between PFAS exposure and birth weight among participants with MTR rs1805087 AA genotype, MTRR rs1801394 AA genotype, and of CBS rs234714 CC genotype, suggesting varying susceptibility to PFAS exposure. These findings provide novel evidence that OCM genes may modify the effects of PFAS exposure, and extend the understanding of PFAS exposure by using mixture analysis in addition to traditional single-exposure models.

In our analyses, associations between PFAS exposure and birth weight were mainly observed among individuals with OCM variants in the reference genotype, which suggested a higher susceptibility of PFAS-related disruption under increase folate demand during pregnancy, potentially through competition at folate receptors.23 Interestingly, we observed slightly stronger associations between PFAS exposure and birth weight among GG carriers of MTHFR rs1801133 and MTHFD1 rs2236225, the OCM variants associated with faster metabolism. There could be faster turnover and increased demand of folate species, which may amplified the competition with PFAS on folate receptors.19,44,45 Meanwhile, the associations were also found for SNPs related to homocysteine levels, which may affect fetal development and reduce the capacity of environmental pollutant detoxification.26 The MTR gene encodes vitamin B12-dependent methionine synthase, converting homocysteine to methionine.26,59 Although no study directly examined the association between the rs1805087 polymorphism and birth weight, the GG genotype has been associated with increased risk of preterm birth in a Korean population.60 The MTRR gene supports methionine synthase regeneration, and the MTRR rs1801394 variant was associated with altered folate species metabolism.61 Our findings of MTRR rs1801394 further support this link, as this variant is closely related with the MTR gene in function, and was associated with lower birth weight.45,62 Lastly, we found the associations differed by CBS genotypes in mixture models. The CBS gene regulates homocysteine levels via the trans-sulfuration pathway,44,45,63 and its variants may contribute to alterations in homocysteine metabolism and reduced birth size.64 The CBS rs234714 variant was associated with increase homocysteine level in human,63 and higher maternal homocysteine levels was associated with 31-gram lower birth weight in a previous meta-analysis.65 Together, these findings highlight on PFAS-related birth outcomes may be modified by OCM genotypes. To our knowledge, no prior study has directly examined interactions between PFAS exposure and genetic variants in OCM; therefore, our findings may help identify susceptible subgroups and inform precision environmental health.

This study presented several implications for precision environmental health. First, competition between PFAS and folate-related molecules may underlie PFAS toxicity on birth outcomes. A recent zebrafish study identified the folic acid receptor as a mediator of PFAS-induced neurodevelopmental toxicity,23 supporting the role of PFAS-folate interactions through molecular competition.22 Second, adequate nutrition to support OCM remains crucial during pregnancy. Folate deficiency remains a global concern,66 as folic acid provision during early pregnancy remains suboptimal with the delayed timing of supplementation and heterogenous folic acid fortification policies.67,68 In Taiwan, the prevalence of folate insufficiency among women aged 19 to 30 has increased over recent decades (25% with ≤6 ng/ml), which coincided with the continued PFAS exposure.69 Optimizing folate status may represent a precision environmental health target with dual benefits by supporting fetal development,12,33–35 and by enhancing detoxification pathways to lower PFAS levels and improving birth outcomes under PFAS exposure.6,18 Third, our study showed the importance of other nutrients in OCM, such as vitamin B12, which works as cofactor in this pathway and lowers homocysteine levels.26,45 We found different PFAS-birth weight associations across different MTR and MTRR genotypes, and the function of MTR-encoded methionine synthase depends on adequate vitamin B12 levels.26,70 As PFAS exposure was associated with lower vitamin B12 and higher homocysteine levels, optimizing vitamin B12 status may help further mitigate adverse health effects.19–21 Overall, these findings demonstrate that maintaining a balanced OCM through proper nutrition may help reduce the adverse effects of PFAS on birth outcomes.34,35

This study has several strengths. First, PFAS concentrations were measured using a standardized and validated method with high accuracy and precision. Second, genotyping was performed on a reliable platform with standardized quality control tailored to the Taiwanese population, ensuring robust and reliable results. Third, mixture modeling approaches were used to estimate the joint effects of PFAS exposures. There are limitations of this study. Potential residual confounding from unmeasured dietary intake during pregnancy could be possible. The study focuses on variants related to OCM; genome-wide analyses are warranted to identify genetic variants outside this pathway that may also modify these associations. Lastly, the inferences should be interpreted with caution, given the limitation of temporality as PFAS concentrations were measured cross-sectionally with birth outcomes. Future studies should explore PFAS-OCM gene interactions using the longitudinal follow-up data from TBPS and other cohorts, extending beyond birth outcomes.

Conclusion

Our findings showed that OCM gene variants, particularly in MTHFR, interacted with PFAS exposure on birth weight and head circumference. Future studies can incorporate genome-wide analyses, longitudinal follow-up, and targeted nutritional interventions to better understand and reduce PFAS-related risks and to inform maternal and child health strategies.
