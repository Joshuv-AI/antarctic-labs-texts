Clinical application and outcome of Tenon’s patch graft: A comprehensive review
Authors: Rakhi Kusumesh, Alisha Kishore, Anitha Venugopal, Sushmita G Shah, Murugesan Vanathi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corneal perforations are potentially sight-threatening conditions if not promptly treated. Management depends on the size, location, cause, and severity of the perforation. Various methods, including tissue adhesives, amniotic membrane grafting (AMG), and keratoplasty, are available. However, the limited availability of donor tissue in emergencies poses a significant challenge. Recently, Tenon’s patch grafting (TPG) has emerged as a promising treatment option due to its autologous nature, availability, and lack of immunogenicity. This review provides a comprehensive overview of various aspects of TPG, along with relevant images. A literature search using keywords such as “Tenon’s patch graft,” “Tenon’s capsule,” “corneal patch graft,” “amniotic membrane,” and “corneal perforations” yielded 37 articles, included in this review. TPG is suitable for cases involving corneal tissue degradation leading to perforation, impending perforation, or descemetocele, with lesion sizes ranging from 2 to 6 mm. For larger perforations, the procedure may be modified by combining it with AMG or tissue adhesives. Postoperatively, the graft undergoes characteristic changes, including epithelialization and collagen deposition, resulting in the formation of a thick scar. Complications such as delayed epithelialization, wound leaks, and graft dehiscence can occur. High success rates have been reported, ranging from 74% to 87%. However, TPG may result in suboptimal visual outcomes, particularly when the graft is centrally located, necessitating future keratoplasty for visual rehabilitation. Overall, TPG is a safe and cost-effective technique for restoring structural integrity in corneal thinning disorders and perforations, especially in emergency settings when donor tissue is unavailable.

Various modalities exist for the management of corneal perforation. These include cyanoacrylate glue with bandage contact lens,[1] fibrin glue,[2] amniotic membrane grafting (AMG),[3] Tenon’s patch graft (TPG),[4] corneal grafting[2] in the form of patch graft, SMILE lenticule,[5] and therapeutic/tectonic penetrating keratoplasty. Modalities may need to be combined to obtain successful outcomes based on the morphology and location of the perforation and the nature and severity of inciting pathology. Thus, the repair of corneal perforations is dependent on the availability of donor corneal tissue or other biological/synthetic additives. The COVID-19 crisis, which posed a significant challenge to eye banking across the globe, also brought to light the importance of having alternate modalities for the repair of corneal perforation that are autologous, readily available, and cost-effective.

TPG involves using Tenon’s tissue, which is a connective tissue forming a part of the outer coat of the eyeball. First described by Vajpayee RB[4] to repair corneal perforations, this modality has undergone various modifications by subsequent authors. The technique is advantageous as Tenon’s tissue is autologous and is non-immunogenic. This review describes various aspects of TPG along with adequate images and surgical video.

Decision-making in corneal perforation

Several methods are available for the management of corneal perforation. Hydrophilic bandage contact lenses are effective for micro perforations ≤1 mm.[6] Tissue adhesives, such as cyanoacrylate, have been employed to treat small perforations ≤3 mm with good results; however, they do not add volume to the site of perforation and can also cause inflammation and blood vessel growth, which may affect future keratoplasty outcomes. Another adhesive, fibrin glue, is better for healing and causes less blood vessel growth, yet poor sealant as it does not last long enough to provide strong support.[7]

When corneal perforations range between 1 and 6 mm and involve tissue/volume loss, multilayered AMG, TPG, and keratoplasty are preferable options.[6] Multilayered AMG aids in healing, but it is suitable for small perforations or descemetoceles due to its rapid absorption.[38] The standard of care for larger perforations (>6 mm) is keratoplasty, which provides good tectonic strength and a transparent visual axis. However, keratoplasty in an inflamed or perforated cornea is challenging.[9] The major concern about keratoplasty is the limited availability of donor tissue in an emergency setting. TPG, an autologous tissue, harvested from the same eye of the patient, provides good sealing and volume. In addition, it can serve as a temporary measure when the donor corneas are unavailable.[10]

Method of literature search

The literature search for this review was completed using PubMed in May 2024. The following keywords and their synonyms were used for the searches: “Tenon’s patch graft,” “Tenon’s capsule,” “corneal patch graft,” “amniotic membrane,” and “corneal perforations.” These were entered into PubMed, generating 4984 related articles. From these articles, publications in the English language and relevant to the disease conditions listed above were included. A total of 37 articles were included in this literature review. The collective experience of the authors has also been added.

Structure and biological properties of Tenon’s tissue

Tenon’s capsule is a fascial sheet of connective tissue covering the scleral coat.[11] Although named after Tenon, who first described it around 1803, Bonnet wrote a detailed description of the layer in 1841 and called it “Capsule of Bonnet.” In the same year, Dr. J. M. Ferrall, an English anatomist also described the layer in detail and named it “Tunica Vaginalis Oculi.”[12] Anteriorly, Tenon’s capsule fuses with the episclera and is inserted 1–2 mm posterior to the limbus, while, posteriorly, it extends to the optic nerve and fuses with the dura. It is intercepted by the extraocular muscles at their insertion sites, thus diving it into anterior and posterior parts. The anterior part is covered by the conjunctiva and fuses posteriorly with the fascial sheath of the extraocular muscles. The posterior part of Tenon’s capsule covers the sclera and is loosely adherent to the episclera. Tenon’s capsule is white, thick, and glistening in young individuals and becomes thinner with advancing age.[13] Furthermore, the anterior part is thicker compared to the posterior part and is also longer than the posterior part in the nasal quadrant as compared to the temporal quadrant. Tenon’s capsule is composed of radially arranged collagen fibers along with blood vessels and fibroblasts. It performs the very important function of transferring forces from the extraocular muscles to the sclera during ocular movements.[14] Tenon’s capsule is vascularized and elastic in structure, thus enabling mobilization anteriorly to the perilimbal area in eyes with scleral ischemia following chemical or thermal burns, thus helping with the restoration of vascularization.[15]

Mechanism of action

TPG may be useful in sealing corneal perforations and in restoring volume to areas of tissue loss in sterile corneal melts and descemetocele. In the former, TPG may be able to obtain an air-tight closure of corneal perforations and help restore the anterior chamber anatomy. Although the exact mechanism of action of TPG in corneal wound healing has not been described yet, Tenon’s tissue contains fibroblasts, which are known to play a significant role in wound healing and inflammation. Fibroblasts modulate wound healing by releasing various cytokines, chemokines, and other mediators. Fibroblasts present in the cornea behave differently from fibroblasts present in Tenon’s tissue, in terms of their response to inflammation. Secretion of inflammatory cytokines by fibroblasts is mediated by IL-1β, a major pro-inflammatory cytokine secreted by epithelial cells and macrophages.[16] These fibroblasts present within the TPG transform myofibroblasts, thus leading to the formation of scar tissue. Fibroblasts are also known to secret VEGF, a growth factor for the production of blood vessels.[17]

TPG gets well integrated into the corneal stroma as has been noted on slit-lamp examination. Anterior-segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) images have also demonstrated successful integration of TPG with the corneal stroma, with the restoration of the corneal epithelial layer over it. Histopathological evaluation of an excised corneal button following TPG has also demonstrated stratified epithelium with underlying irregular collagen, which appears distinct from the regular arrangement of collagen in the corneal stroma.[18]

Indication

TPG can be performed in cases involving corneal tissue degradation leading to perforation, impending perforation, or descemetocele. It is performed for the size of the lesion ranging from 2 to 6 mm.[101920] The primary objective of TPG is to restore ocular integrity and to resolve the ongoing stromal melt. The causative underlying etiologies, detailed in Table 1, encompass both inflammatory and infective causes. Ocular surface inflammation, arising from severe dry eye, autoimmune disorders, or injury, can trigger corneal melting and subsequent perforation [Fig. 1]. Among infective etiology, previously published studies have demonstrated positive outcomes in herpetic stromal keratitis.[19] It is noteworthy that TPG is not considered in cases of suppurative non-viral infections.[18] Patel et al.[21] recently described the use of TPG for the management of sterile pericylindrical corneal melt in three eyes with Auro keratoprosthesis.

Preoperative workup: A comprehensive approach including a thorough history and clinical examination is mandatory. In cases where infection is suspected, microbiological examination is essential to differentiate between infective and non-infective lesions and to determine the causative organisms. Documentation of the lesion’s location and size is crucial to prognosticate the visual outcome. When visibility of the anterior segment is poor, AS-OCT is employed to evaluate peripheral anterior synechiae, indicating the need for surgical peripheral iridectomy [Fig. 2a]. Furthermore, a gentle ultrasound assessment of the posterior segment aids in identifying conditions such as endophthalmitis, choroidal detachment, and retinal detachment. In addition, it is imperative to evaluate for systemic autoimmune disorders as they may necessitate preoperative oral or intravenous corticosteroid or other immunosuppressive therapies. Eyes with a neurotrophic cornea and abnormal ocular surface and tear film need to be identified as they may require tarsorrhaphy for faster epithelial healing.

Postoperative course of Tenon’s patch graft. (a) ASOCT showing corneal perforation in severe dry eye with rheumatoid arthritis. (b) Nonepithelialized, heterogeneous, and thickened graft at 2-week follow-up. (c) Homogenous, well-defined margin of graft at 6-week followup

Preoperatively, in addition to topical antimicrobials and immunosuppressive therapy in patients with autoimmune disorders, Kate et al.[18] recommended the use of topical brimonidine tartrate 0.15%, which acts as a vasoconstrictor, to ensure lesser bleeding during surgery.

Patient counseling regarding the purpose of the surgery, anticipated visual outcomes, and the need for multiple interventions, is imperative to promote compliance during the postoperative phase. Furthermore, patients should be informed about the resultant scarring from the procedure and the subsequent need for keratoplasty to facilitate visual rehabilitation.

Anesthesia: Depending on the size of the perforation and the age of the patient, the surgical procedure may be performed under either local or general anesthesia. Small perforations in cooperative patients can be managed using topical anesthesia. However, for larger perforations, peribulbar anesthesia is necessary. During the administration of a peribulbar block, caution should be taken to gradually introduce the drug to prevent sudden increases in intraorbital pressure. The addition of adrenaline, hyaluronidase, and bicarbonate can enhance the permeability of the anesthesia agent, reducing the overall dosage required.[22] In addition, a facial nerve block reduces external pressure on ocular contents resulting from lid movements. Ocular massage should be avoided.

Surgical procedure

Preparation of host site: The initial step involves meticulous preparation of the recipient site, achieved by removing debris and loosely adhered epithelium surrounding the lesion with fine forceps. In the case of perforated cornea with adherent uveal tissue, wound edges are separated from uveal tissue. In descemetoceles, overlying epithelium is removed using either a merocel sponge or blunt forceps [Fig. 3a].

Steps of surgical procedure: (a) Preparation of the recipient site by removing debris and measuring the lesion. (b) Harvesting and placing the graft. (c) Graft fixation with the fibrin glue and overlay infinity sutures with 10-0 nylon. (d) AC reformation with air and BCL application

Harvesting Tenon’s patch graft: The exact size of the lesion is first measured using calipers. A small conjunctival incision is given 2 mm posterior to the limbus preferably from the superonasal (the anterior part of Tenon’s capsule is longer in the nasal half than other parts) or superotemporal area. However, in cases associated with conjunctival diseases, the graft can be harvested from other areas. After the incision, the underlying anterior Tenon’s tissue, which is rich in fibrous tissue and smooth muscle, is then dissected and excised. To achieve optimal wound coverage, the graft should be 1–2 mm larger than lesion size and not bulky. The repositioning of the conjunctiva is achieved by a simple pressure for a few minutes or by using fibrin glue (TISSEEL kit, Baxter AG, Vienna, Austria), autologous blood, or applying 8-0 vicryl suture [Fig. 3b].

Graft fixation: The edges of the perforation or exposed Descemet membrane are first dried with sponges. TPG is applied to the area of perforation and secured in place by applying fibrin glue. Although 10-0 nylon sutures may also be used to secure the graft, outcomes are better following the use of fibrin glue. Given the loose and adhesive nature of Tenon’s tissue, fibrin glue proves advantageous in enhancing fixation. The TPG is further secured by overlay sutures of infinity shape using 10-0 monofilament nylon. The area of intersection of the sutures must overlap the area of the lesion to provide adequate support to the graft; this step is useful in preventing graft ectasia. Additional interrupted sutures may be applied in cases of large perforation or inadequate sealing [Fig. 3c].

Anterior chamber reformation: Following secure fixation of the TPG, the AC is reformed with air injection, either via a side-port incision or a 26-G needle. Caution is exercised to avoid overfilling the AC with air, fluid, or viscoelastic, and any aggressive maneuvers after TPG fixation is discouraged. Such practices may lead egress of fluid from the graft-host junction, resulting in flat AC and subsequent failure of the procedure. Given the inflammatory nature of eyes undergoing TPG, performing peripheral iridectomy can help mitigate the risk of secondary glaucoma. Following surgery, a bandage contact lens (BCL) is placed and patching is done [Fig. 3d].

Additional procedures such as permanent lateral/medial tarsorrhaphy may be required in eyes with neurotrophic cornea.

Modifications in surgical techniques

TPG, although effectively used to manage corneal perforations of size up to 6 mm, does not form a water-tight barrier as it is made of only connective tissue. For larger perforations, the technique of TPG needs modifications to effectively seal the defect, aid in early epithelization, provide tectonic support, and form a water-tight barrier.[10] The modifications in surgical technique include a) the addition of AMG, Gore-tex, and tissue adhesives to mechanically support the tissue and b) modifications in suturing techniques and placement of the graft [Video 1].

Additive techniques to improve the effectiveness of TPG

Amniotic membrane graft

Addition of the human amniotic membrane (hAM) over the TPG provides tectonic support to seal the wound and increases the stability of the TPG. The epithelium around the placed TPG is gently scraped, and the hAM is either sutured over the graft with 10-0 monofilament nylon or fibrin glue. AMG is placed with the basement membrane side up to hasten the reepithelization. This approach is particularly advantageous in cases where delayed epithelialization is expected, such as in elderly patients or those with autoimmune disorders. It provides a watertight closure and provides tectonic support for fluid-permeable TPG.[1018]

Gore-tex

Gore-tex is an expanded polytetrafluorethylene (eTPFE) material used in vascular, plastic, and cardiac reconstructive surgeries. This material is well-tolerated, resistant to invasion by fibrovascular tissue, and resists infection and inflammation too. It has been used as a sealing material to close corneal perforations way back in 1994 by Huang et al. Anitha et al.[23] described the use of Gore-tex over TPG along with AMG to successfully treat 6-mm corneal perforation of infective etiology. In cases where TPG alone could not perfectly seal the larger area of perforation, Gore-tex can be used to provide better sealing and to provide additional mechanical support to the tissue. Therefore, combined TPG and Gore-tex would be beneficial for sealing perforations with a size of more than 6 mm. The primary disadvantage of Gore-tex is its lack of transparency.

Tissue adhesive

Cyanoacrylate glue is a synthetic glue (ethyl-2-cyanoacrylate) used as an overlay patch to manage acute corneal perforations of size less than 3 mm due to both infectious and non-infectious etiologies. In most of the studies, the success rate was noted to be 91%.[118] However, managing larger perforations is challenging due to the risk of glue seepage into the AC, which can cause toxicity and damage to intraocular structures. Hence, to avoid glue seepage in cases of perforation up to 6 mm, a drop of it can be applied at the edges of TPG and the surrounding deepithelized cornea surface, with or without covering over the entire surface of the graft.[19] Sharma et al.[20] successfully used TPG in 31 cases and secured it into stromal pockets with cyanoacrylate glue.

Fibrin glue is a biological derivative and can be used instead of cyanoacrylate glue in most clinical scenarios due to the benefits of reduced corneal and conjunctival inflammation. Because Tenon’s tissue is a loose and sticky tissue, fibrin glue provides improved fixation, facilitating the suture application and tight sealing at the margin, especially in descemetocle. The disadvantages of fibrin glue include the possibility of viral contamination due to fibrinogen and thrombin components and higher costs. TPG can be secured with glue to provide support in larger perforations.[2024]

Modifications in suturing/placement of the graft

Suturing techniques

Overlay sutures are considered the standard and effective procedure for securing the TPG. The suturing techniques can be modified based on the size of the graft and the area of the perforation, such as overlay sutures in the form of the infinity pattern or the box pattern and interrupted sutures. The number of sutures can be increased to provide additional mechanical strength if the perforation necessitates it. Interrupted suture can be applied to ensure better graft adherence and stability. The points to remember while applying overlay suture is that the area of intersection of the suture must overlap over the graft and secure it tightly, and the longer arm of the suture should be placed over the graft and avoid compression of the surrounding normal cornea to ensure the stability of the graft.[1018]

Tuck in TPG

Sharma et al.[20] described the tuck-in TPG technique to improve the stability of the graft and aid in early epithelization in perforations up to 5 mm. It was used in 31 cases of corneal perforations of size up to 5 mm. The TPG edges were tucked inside the lamellar stromal pockets created 360° around the clear margins of the perforated area along with cyanoacrylate glue. Twenty-seven (87.1%) cases healed with the formation of leucomatous scar at 16.9 ± 2.7 weeks.

Hitch-suture assisted tuck in TPG

Bafna RK et al.[25] used temporary horizontal mattress sutures to hitch the TPG in a lamellar pocket, and then the graft was secured well with fibrin glue. The anterior chamber was then reformed, and the horizontal sutures were removed. The procedure was performed in 22 eyes, for non-infective corneal perforations of size 3–5 mm. All the eyes had a well-formed anterior chamber in the immediate postoperative period, and no major complications were incurred.

TPG with Vascularized conjunctival flap

Singh SN et al.[26] used TPG along with an overlay of the conjunctival flap to seal the 1-mm corneal perforation with sutures. The authors believe that it improved the refractive outcomes in paracentral perforations compared to lamellar grafts.

Annular Sling TPG

Anitha V et al.[27] described a novel technique of using Tenon’s graft as an annular sling to seal the advanced and progressive PUK due to systemic tuberculosis. Tenon’s graft was harvested and placed over the peripheral ulcer area after thorough wound debridement, in an annular fashion and covered with AMG. The limbal graft edges were sutured with vicryl sutures, but the corneal edges were secured with glue.

Postoperative regimen

The postoperative regimen is decided based on the preoperative diagnosis of corneal perforation.

TPG done for eyes with infective etiologies such as bacterial/fungal organisms are treated with broad-spectrum topical and systemic antibiotics/antifungals, respectively. The topical treatment is continued until the resolution of infection and epithelization of the graft. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy with fluorescein staining and AS-OCT must be done during regular follow-ups during the first week and 1 month until epithelization is achieved. At every visit, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and intraocular pressure must be assessed. In cases of TPG done for eyes perforated due to viral infection, long-term systemic antiviral therapy is warranted with either acyclovir 400 mg BD or valcyclovir 500 mg BD for at least 6 months, along with topical corticosteroid therapy in tapering dosage. For TPG done in eyes with corneal perforation due to non-infective pathologies such as dry eyes, rheumatoid arthritis, and other autoimmune disorders, topical corticosteroids are prescribed four times a day in tapering dose for 1 month along with topical antibiotics. If the visual outcome is not satisfactory, optical penetrating keratoplasty may be performed subsequently for visual rehabilitation once the eye is quiescent.[10181928]

Postoperative course

TPG provides tensile strength due to the presence of fibrous tissue and is free from any immunological reactions as it is harvested from the patient’s eye. TPG may look swollen as it imbibes fluid and swells up [Fig. 2b]. As a result, it may also be leaky unless supported by an AMG, especially with large perforations. Gradually, scar tissue formation occurs due to the laying down of collagen by fibroblasts present in TPG along with the restoration of epithelial continuity over the area of TPG. It is important to monitor patients for leaking wounds, displacement of the TPG, progress in corneal melting, and restoration of anterior chamber anatomy during the early postoperative period. Patients must also be counseled about the whitish discoloration that is seen due to a swollen TPG.

Ideal anatomical success would be the complete restoration of the anterior chamber with the integration of the TPG with intact overlying epithelium. Korah et al.[19] reported anatomical success with a formed anterior chamber in 20 out of 27 eyes (74.1%). Sharma et al.[20] reported successful outcomes in 27 out of 31 eyes (87.1%) following their modified technique of tuck-in TPG. The presence of central adherent leucoma with a flat anterior chamber is considered an unsuccessful outcome. However, some eyes that develop peripheral adherent leucoma with centrally formed anterior chambers may also be considered an anatomical success.[10]

The TPG gradually starts to shrink back after initial swelling up and becomes compact [Fig. 2c]. The TPG continues to shrink over the next 2–3 months as described by Chaudhary et al.[29] Histopathological evaluation of a corneal button following TPG showed the presence of pseudocornea with the restoration of the overlying epithelium.[18] The authors further reported progressive thinning of the TPG and recommended not to consider TPG as an effective modality for volume replacement.

Clinical efficacy

The success of TPG has been reported to be 74.1% by Korah et al.,[19] 87% by Kusumesh et al.,[10] and 87.1% by Sharma et al.[20] The major indications for TPG in various studies were corneal thinning/perforation post trauma, infective keratitis, neurotrophic keratitis, and peripheral ulcerative keratitis. The size of perforation in all these cases was in the range from 1–6 mm, with a mean duration of follow-up ranging from 4 weeks to 32 months. The efficacy and success rate in all these cases have been reported in terms of formed anterior chamber with or without adherent leucoma.[192030]

Therefore, the establishment of a stable ocular surface and anterior chamber are the parameters to determine the efficacy and good outcome of TPG. The formation of an adherent leucoma is also considered an acceptable outcome. However, adherent leucoma with a flat anterior chamber is considered a failure. For the TPG to survive, there should be minimal inflammation or a healing trend.[3132]

There is corneal scar formation following TPG in all cases. This scar provides good biomechanical strength to the cornea. Furthermore, as corneal neovascularization is less compared to tissue adhesives, visual outcomes following rigid contact lenses and optical keratoplasties in the later stages have been reported to be better.[29]

Advantages of Tenon’s patch graft

Tissue adhesives are costly and lead to increased postoperative inflammation, vascularization, and ocular discomfort in cases of corneal perforation. In contrast, Tenon’s patch is an autologous tissue with no immune response and vascularization and is readily available. We hypothesize that this will increase the success rate of optical keratoplasty at a later stage. Furthermore, unlike corneal grafting or AMG, this tissue does not rely on donor tissue availability or eye bank facility, thus making it a cost-effective technique in developing countries, where there is a paucity of donor corneal tissues.

Hence, TPG provides an affordable means for restoring corneal surface integrity, preserving biomechanical strength using a simple surgical technique, and reducing the need for long-term postoperative steroids.[33]

Complications

Wound leak: Aqueous leakage is one of the most common complications, with 1.2%–12.9% of cases depending on the technique used and underlying pathology.[10181920] Cases with a flat anterior chamber or persistent shallowing necessitate suture augmentation and reformation of the AC. In cases where anterior-chamber reformation is not achieved, consideration of an alternative method is advisable to restore structural integrity [Fig. 4e].

Complications of Tenon’s patch graft: (a) Delayed epithelization (positive fluorescein stain in inset). (b) Vascularization involving multiple quadrants of the cornea. (c) Graft melt resulting in re-perforation. (d) Graft infection with corneal infiltrates. (e) AS-OCT shows flat AC following TPG. (f) AS-OCT shows graft ectasia

Graft retraction: Graft retraction or displacement is an infrequent complication, and complete displacement necessitating keratoplasty has not been documented to date. Anecdotal cases were reported by Singhal et al.[31] and Sharma et al.[20] in their respective studies; both cases were successfully managed by resuturing and subsequent anterior-chamber reformation.

Graft dehiscence: It may manifest in situations characterized by persistent stromal inflammation, and delayed epithelialization may result in a sterile graft dehiscence. This can be managed with the administration of copious lubricants, tarsorrhaphy, and AMG if required [Fig. 4c].

Intra-Tenon hemorrhage: Intra-Tenon hemorrhage is usually seen in the early postoperative period and gradually resolves with the administration of routine postoperative medication.[18]

Corneal vascularization: TPG induces minimal reaction and lesser corneal vascularization in comparison to cyanoacrylate glue application. Nevertheless, Kusumesh et al.[10] reported its occurrence in 87% of cases. The authors observed that newly formed vessels regressed after keratoplasty [Fig. 4b].

Delayed epithelization: The typical time frame for epithelialization of a Tenon’s graft averages 3–4 weeks. Instances of delayed epithelialization, extending up to 5 weeks, particularly in cases of severe dry eye, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, or in advanced age, have been reported.[10] Notably, the application of tarsorrhaphy may help hasten the process of graft epithelialization [Fig. 4a].

Graft ectasia: Delayed epithelialization, the presence of a large and thick graft, graft-host junction disparity, and persistent ocular surface inflammation are factors that may contribute to graft ectasia. Management includes the trimming of excess tissue, the application of additional sutures to enhance graft strength, guarded surface cauterization,[18] and interventions aimed at promoting rapid epithelialization [Fig. 4f].

Graft infection: Uncontrolled progression of primary infection or secondary infection results in graft infection and subsequently graft dehiscence, wound leak, and flat AC. It has been documented that 30% TPG in non-viral keratitis required therapeutic keratoplasty.[10] Hence, it is advisable not to perform TPG in acute suppurative keratitis [Fig. 4d].

TPG in conditions other than corneal melt and perforation

Various authors have described the use of TPG in clinical scenarios other than corneal melt and perforation. These are mostly anecdotal. Ma et al.[34] described the successful retrieval of a retrobulbar foreign body via the intraocular route through a perforation in the posterior segment, close to the optic disc, and using TPG to seal the defect in the ocular coats. The use of Tenon’s patch as autologous scar tissue has been described for encysted glaucoma filtering bleb[35] as well as for exposed Ahmed glaucoma valve.[36] Lu et al.[37] described the use of Tenon’s tissue in eyes with scleral melt following pterygium surgery.

Summary

In conclusion, TPG is a safe and cost-effective technique to restore structural integrity in corneal thinning disorders and perforations. High success rates have been reported in multiple large case-series. Managing corneal perforations during the COVID-19 era, when donor corneas were not available, posed a significant challenge to all clinicians. This led to the widespread application of TPG usage in a myriad of clinical settings coupled with the adoption of various modifications to the original surgical technique to enhance success rate. Although advanced diagnostic modalities such as AS-OCT play an important role in assessing wound healing and structural integrity during the follow-up period, more studies are warranted to understand the biological processes involved in wound healing following TPG.
