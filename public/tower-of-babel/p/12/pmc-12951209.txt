Bilateral Eyelid Agenesis With Multiple Congenital Ocular Anomalies in an Australian Labradoodle Puppy: Case Report and Surgical Management
Authors: Chi Hin Ng, Ticiana Brasil Ervedosa, José Granados Soler, Megan Elizabeth Climans, Viviana Gonzalez‐Astudillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 15‐week‐old Australian Labradoodle puppy was presented to The University of Queensland Small Animal Hospital for bilateral eyelid agenesis/coloboma and associated ocular complications. Enucleation of the left globe and repair of the right eyelid via a lip‐to‐lid transmucosal flap was performed. Given the subsequent development of prolapse of the gland of the nictitating membrane and a lack of vision of the right eye, enucleation of the right globe was then performed 2 months following the initial procedure. Both globes and a skin wedge from the right eye were submitted to The University of Queensland Veterinary Laboratory Service for histopathology assessment. Microscopic globe examination revealed lesions consistent with bilateral Peters' anomaly (anterior chamber cleavage syndrome, ACCS), characterized by axial defects in Descemet's membrane and the posterior corneal stroma, anterior synechiae at the site of the defects, and closure of the drainage angle. Other important histological findings were persistent pupillary membranes, retinal separation, and aphakia. Histopathology of the skin wedge from the right eye demonstrated complete absence of conjunctival goblet cells, tarsal plate, and meibomian glands, consistent with the finding of eyelid agenesis/coloboma. This report describes an uncommon and underreported presentation of bilateral canine lower eyelid agenesis with concurrent multiple congenital ocular anomalies, including bilateral Peters' anomaly.Keywords: anterior segment dysgenesis, canine, congenital diseases, eye coloboma, ocular malformation, peters' anomaly

Introduction

Congenital ocular malformations are considered uncommon in dogs and cats [1, 2]. However, when present, these malformations are frequently accompanied by various anatomical and functional abnormalities, as well as complications such as corneal erosion and eyelid malposition, as the absence of normal eyelid structure compromises ocular surface protection, leading to increased tear film evaporation, inadequate tear distribution, and a heightened risk of exposure keratopathy and secondary corneal pathology [1], frequently requiring surgical intervention [1, 3].

Eyelid agenesis with concurrent complex ocular malformations has been a well‐established condition in both wild and domestic felid species [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. A syndrome termed Multiple Ocular Coloboma (MOC) syndrome, characterized by a spectrum of congenital ocular anomalies of varying severity including persistent pupillary membranes, microphthalmia, uveal colobomas, cataracts, retinal cysts, and retinal dysplasia, has been described in captive snow leopards ( Panthera uncia ) [4, 8], cheetah ( Acinonyx jubatus ) [5], Texas cougar ( Felis concolor ) [6], and cats ( Felis catus ) [10, 11]. However, eyelid agenesis remains poorly characterized in canines, particularly in cases involving coexisting complex ocular abnormalities.

Peters' anomaly is a rare congenital anomaly of the anterior segment, characterized by central corneal opacification, defects in Descemet's membrane and the corneal endothelium, and adhesions between the iris and the posterior cornea (iridocorneal synechiae) [12, 13], which may or may not involve concurrent complex ocular malformations. This condition is well recognized in canines. It may be misdiagnosed or misclassified as persistent pupillary membranes due to overlapping clinical features [13].

This case report documents the gross and microscopic findings in a puppy presented with bilateral lower eyelid agenesis in association with complex congenital ocular malformations, and the elected surgical technique to correct the lid defect, contributing to the limited existing literature on similar conditions in canines.

Material and Methods

History

A 15‐week‐old male Australian Labradoodle with a history of bilateral lower eyelid agenesis, chronic uveitis with pre‐iridial fibrovascular membrane formation in the left eye, and secondary glaucoma was presented to The University of Queensland Small Animal Hospital, Australia, for reassessment of bilateral eyelid agenesis, trichiasis and worsening ocular signs. The puppy was born with both eyes open. It had previously been evaluated by the primary care service of the same veterinary hospital, and no changes in its clinical condition were noted at the time of presentation to the surgical service. Given the poor prognosis, bilateral enucleation was eventually performed. The globes and abnormal palpebrae were submitted to The University of Queensland Veterinary Laboratory Services for histopathological examination.

Clinical Findings

Clinical examination revealed bilateral absence of the lateral half of the lower eyelid margin characterizing eyelid agenesis (Figure 1A). Ocular examination of the left eye revealed a significant increase in intraocular pressure (31 mmHg), chronic uveitis—suspected due to the presence of an aqueous flare—hyphema, and the formation of a preiridial fibrovascular membrane. On slit‐lamp examination, no slit‐beam reflexes from either anterior or posterior lens surfaces were observed, consistent with lens luxation or aphakia. Examination of the right eye showed focal corneal edema, neovascularization, corneal scarring, microphthalmia, and trichiasis. Slit‐lamp examination could not be performed due to abnormal corneal surface and adhesions between the cornea and the lower eyelid. Schirmer's tear test results for the left and right eyes were within normal limits, measuring 15 mm/min and 21 mm/min, respectively. Menace response was absent in both eyes upon initial presentation. Enucleation of the left eye was performed given the poor prognosis, and a lip‐to‐lid mucocutaneous subdermal plexus flap was performed to repair the right eyelid. Prolapse of the third eyelid gland (Figure 1B) developed in the right eye approximately 1 month following initial surgery. Given the risk of further disease progression and the absence of vision, the owner elected to proceed with enucleation.

Surgical Procedure

Enucleation of the left eye and the right eyelid repair was initially performed in September 2024. The animal was premedicated with methadone 0.2 mg/kg IM, ketamine 1.5 mg/kg IM and alfaxalone 1.63 mg/kg IM. General anesthesia was performed where the puppy was induced using alfaxalone 6 mg/kg IV and maintained through an endotracheal tube with isoflurane in oxygen.

Enucleation of the left globe was performed under retrobulbar block with 1 mL 0.5% bupivacaine. Following globe exposure and eyelid retraction with a Lancaster speculum, the conjunctiva was excised using DeBakey forceps and tenotomy scissors. The globe was removed via combined blunt and sharp dissection, with hemostasis achieved by electrocautery. The optic nerve was clamped with Ged forceps and transacted. A routine three‐layer closure was completed with the inner layer closed using 3–0 polydioxanone in simple continuous pattern, the subcutaneous layer closed using 4–0 Monosyn in simple continuous pattern, and the skin layer closed using 4–0 nylon in simple interrupted pattern.

A 40 × 20 mm block from the right eyelid defect was resected ventrally using monopolar electrocautery. A matching skin flap was harvested from the mucocutaneous junction of the oral commissure, extending caudally along the platysma using a size 15 scalpel blade. The subdermal mucocutaneous flap was sutured to the eyelid using 5–0 Monosyn in a combination of simple continuous and simple interrupted patterns. Skin closure was completed with 5–0 nylon.

At the follow‐up examination approximately 2 months postoperatively, the skin and surgical incisions of both eyelids had healed well, and no further correction of the right eyelid was required. The right eye demonstrated normal postoperative healing with no trichiasis, skin adhesion, or signs of corneal irritation. The concurrent prolapsed third eyelid gland remained non‐reducible, and medical therapy had produced minimal improvement; surgical correction was therefore recommended.

The second procedure was conducted 2 months following the initial surgery. The animal was premedicated with medetomidine 10 μg/kg IM and methadone 0.3 mg/kg IM, while anesthesia was induced with alfaxalone to effect slow IV and was maintained on isoflurane in oxygen. Right globe enucleation was performed 2 months post‐initial surgery. A 1 mL retrobulbar block with 0.5% bupivacaine was administered. Orbital musculature and adnexal structures, including the nictitating membrane, were excised via blunt and sharp dissection. Hemostasis was achieved with electrocautery. The optic nerve was clamped with a Rochester Carmalt for 5 min prior to transection. Closure of the subcutaneous layer was done using 3–0 Monosyn in simple continuous pattern, and the skin layer was closed with intradermal horizontal mattress using 3–0 Monosyn, followed by tissue adhesive and Cutiplast dressing.

Recovery was uneventful following both surgeries. Medetomidine 1 μg/kg IV, trazodone 5 mg/kg per rectum, and acepromazine 0.005 mg/kg IV were given upon recovery of the second surgery.

The patient was followed up 1 month postoperatively and reexamined 7 months later before elective desexing, with a satisfactory surgical outcome and no adverse effects following bilateral enucleation.

Diagnosis

On gross examination, the left globe measured 20 × 20 × 23 mm, with a paraxial to axial 4 mm opacity of the cornea whereas the right globe measured 18 × 18 × 18 mm, with a paraxial to axial 3 mm opacity of the cornea (interpreted as the location of Peters' anomaly, Figure 1C). This opacity corresponded to the corneal scarring and focal corneal edema documented during the clinical examination, confirming that it was not a fixation artifact. A hemisection of both globes exposed a fibrillar off‐white membrane occupying approximately 20% of the vitreous chamber, attaching bilaterally to the anterior uvea and focally to the choroid near the optic nerve, consistent with a detached retina (Figure 1D). Additionally, about 2.0 mL of pink to tan watery to slightly viscous fluid was detected oozing from the vitreal space, posterior, and anterior chambers of the left globe, suggesting a degenerate vitreous, and about 0.8 mL of dense white fluid in the subretinal space, indicating proteinaceous fluid (Figure 1D). Aphakia was detected bilaterally (Figure 1D).

A fragment of haired skin and conjunctiva of the right eye measuring 34 × 3 × 5–15 mm was submitted along with the left globe. The margins of the cutaneous flap were inked in blue, black, yellow, red, and orange, indicating deep, dorsal, rostral, ventral, and caudal margins, respectively.

The enucleated eyes and the right eyelid flap were stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) and submitted for histopathology. The specimens were embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard laboratory protocols. A 3‐step sectioning approach was adopted, with a distance of 50 μm between each section, to verify the absence of lens. The prolapsed nictitating membrane was unavailable for histological evaluation; however, given the normal Schirmer tear test values (21 mm/min), its structure and function were presumed to be within normal limits.

Histopathology of the left globe (Figure 2A) revealed marked corneal epithelial hyperplasia (up to 121 μm and 10 cell layers), keratinization, and variable pigmentation. There was multifocal mineral deposition in the central superficial corneal stroma (band keratopathy). Fibroplasia was also observed, consisting of stromal collagen fibers exhibiting variable disorganization with loss of typical collagen architecture (Figure 2B), and there was corneal edema and stromal neovascularization. A 3.4 mm axial defect involving central absence of Descemet's membrane and the associated endothelium was also noted, accompanied by extensive anterior synechiae to the site of the defect, consistent with Peters' anomaly (Figure 2B), hyphema, and narrowing to collapse of the anterior chamber resulting in obstruction of the iridocorneal angle. In at least one level of the iridocorneal angle, an iris‐like tissue crossed the angle and directly contacted the end of Descemet's membrane without evidence of a trabecular meshwork, ciliary cleft, or distinct pectinate ligaments (Figure 2C). At the level of the corneal defect, the posterior iris blended into disorganized uveal tissue and detached neuroepithelium and neurosensory retina (interpreted as congenital malformation under the umbrella of anterior segment dysgenesis). Aphakia and retinal detachment were observed (Figure 2A), alongside hypertrophy of the retinal pigmented epithelium (“tombstoning” appearance). The retina was thickened, with jumbling of the retinal layers, including rosette formation (retinal dysplasia, Figure 2D). Although the optic nerve head was not captured in examined sections, and the retina could not be evaluated for glaucomatous change due to the presence of extensive retinal abnormalities, thinning of the limbal sclera is suggestive of glaucoma.

The right eye (Figure 3A) exhibited comparable changes, including corneal epithelial hyperplasia, keratinization, and pigmentation, corneal edema, stromal neovascularization and variable fibroplasia, alongside mild neutrophilic and lymphocytic keratitis and stromal pigmentation. There was an axial discontinuity of Descemet's membrane and corneal endothelium, with uveal tissue blended into the cornea at the site of axial Descemet's membrane defect, interpreted as Peters' anomaly (Figure 3A,B). There were multifocal small circular sections of uvea‐like tissue captured in the anterior chamber between the iris and the defect in Descemet's membrane, with at least one strand captured connecting with the anterior iris, interpreted as persistent pupillary membranes (Figure 3B). There was segmental absence of recognizable trabecular meshwork and ciliary cleft/pectinate ligaments, the vessels of the angular aqueous plexus were posteriorly displaced relative to the base of the iris, and iris‐like tissue crossed the iridocorneal angle and met the end of Descemet's membrane (anterior segment dysgenesis, Figure 3C). Aphakia and retinal detachment were also observed (Figure 3A), accompanied by marked segmentally variable retinal thickening and segmental architectural disorganization with rosettes similar to that seen in the left eye (retinal dysplasia, Figure 3D).

Histopathology of the right palpebra and conjunctiva (Figure 4) revealed conjunctival epithelial parakeratotic keratinization with complete absence of goblet cells, and there was complete absence of the tarsal plate and meibomian glands, corroborating a clinically diagnosed eyelid agenesis. Mild hydropic and vacuolar degeneration of epithelial cells was observed in the stratum spinosum and basale. There was a mild dermatitis and folliculitis, characterized predominantly by neutrophilic infiltration, with lesser lymphocytic and monocytic involvement in the peri‐adnexal tissues and follicular epithelium. Multifocal circulating intravascular neutrophils were also present.

Multiple ocular congenital anomalies, including aphakia, retinal dysplasia, axial Descemet's membrane defect with collagen disorganization of the adjacent corneal stroma and associated anterior synechia ± persistent pupillary membranes, anterior chamber narrowing to collapse with segmental to complete drainage angle closure/malformation were present alongside congenital eyelid agenesis and microphthalmia of the right eye (eyelid coloboma). The diagnosis of Peters' anomaly (anterior chamber cleavage syndrome, ACCS), encompassing both dysgenesis Mesodermalis corneae and dysgenesis Mesodermalis iridis, was confirmed based on corneal collagen fiber disorganization, discontinuity of Descemet's membrane, and the presence of subjacent anterior synechiae. Additional changes such as corneal epithelial hyperplasia, keratinization, pigmentation, and mild inflammation were likely acquired secondary to corneal exposure caused by the eyelid defect.

Discussion and Conclusion

Despite being a well described and documented condition in both captive and wild felids, only a few cases of eyelid coloboma (eyelid agenesis with lack of granular formation) in dogs have been identified in the literature [1, 14]. While eyelid defects associated with Peters' anomaly have been reported [15], to the authors' knowledge, this case report documents an underreported condition in dogs characterized by bilateral eyelid agenesis occurring concurrently with bilateral Peters' anomaly and multiple additional ocular anomalies.

Among felids, specifically captive snow leopards, eyelid colobomas are among the most common concurrent MOC abnormalities, most frequently occurring bilaterally, with clinical signs such as ocular discharge, corneal pathology, and blepharospasm reported in 69.4% of cases at initial diagnosis [7]. In humans, dogs, cats and snow leopards, one or more concurrent congenital ocular defects have been described independently, including iris coloboma, persistent pupillary membranes (PPMs), persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous (PHPV), optic nerve coloboma, microphthalmia, juvenile cataracts, choroidal hypoplasia, retinal dysplasia, and anterior segment dysgenesis [2, 7, 10]. The eyelid is developed via a complex interaction between the tissues of the ectoderm and mesoderm [2, 3, 16], two folds of ectoderm, each containing a mesenchymal core, grow rapidly toward each other until they meet and fusion of the epithelial layer occurs, forming a persisting common epithelial lamina [16]. Eyelid agenesis is widely considered to result from aberrant embryonic differentiation, although the precise etiology and pathogenesis remain incompletely understood [1, 17]. In affected snow leopards, 30.6% are reported to exhibit concurrent heritable developmental abnormalities, including hip dysplasia, swimmer's syndrome, osteochondrosis, cardiac anomalies, and hydrocephalus [7], supporting the hypothesis that these lesions may constitute components of a broader syndromic condition characterized by a pleiotropic phenotypic expression and developmental defect despite the underlying pathogenesis remaining incompletely understood [4, 5, 11]. Intrauterine viral or Toxoplasma gondii infection, or malnutrition have been proposed to be the cause of multiple ocular colobomatous syndrome in snow leopards, but this has not been conclusively proven [8].

Mild dorsal eyelid agenesis in humans is typically benign and primarily cosmetic, with correction often achieved through direct closure. Severe lesions, defined as those involving more than one‐third of the eyelid margin, require more advanced surgical techniques, including rotational, semicircular, or bridging flaps [6]. Although the technique has been described multiple times in cats [6, 18, 19, 20], and recently in dogs following trauma or oncological resection [21], to the authors' knowledge, this is the first reported use of a lip commissure‐to‐eyelid transposition flap for the correction of congenital eyelid agenesis in a dog. A case of upper eyelid agenesis correction in a dog has been documented in the literature, involving reconstruction of a new eyelid margin from the adjacent intact eyelid tissue with particular emphasis on preventing inward rotation of the reconstructed skin margin to safeguard the ocular surface, and no postoperative complications were reported [1]. Common reported complications of transpositional flaps includes trichiasis [6, 20], flap necrosis and dehiscence [18, 20, 21], edema [21], wound infection [21], blink dysfunction [21], corneal irritation [20], and episcleral graft adherence [20], secondary to improper flap size or placement [18]. At the 2‐month follow‐up, the patient developed prolapse of the nictitating membrane gland of the contralateral eye. This complication is rarely reported and may be unrelated to the initial surgery [1, 6, 18, 19, 20]. However, it is noteworthy that previously documented cases involved upper eyelid agenesis, whereas the current case involved the lower eyelid. Given the eye was non‐visual and to prevent further complications, the client elected enucleation. The patient recovered uneventfully after the second procedure and remained comfortable at subsequent checks.

Embryological development of the eye is similar among domestic mammalian species, involving the neural and surface ectoderm, as well as neural crest derived mesenchyme [16, 22]. Ocular development begins with bilateral grooves in the prosencephalon forming optic vesicles that extend laterally to contact the surface ectoderm, inducing lens placode formation and optic cup invagination. The proximal portion narrows to form the optic stalks, within which the choroid fissure develops ventrally. The rim of the optic cup continues to grow and forms a continuous structure, the primordium of the pupil, while the lens placode folds into the optic cup to form the lens vesicle [2, 16, 22]. Following the initial formation of the optic cup, further differentiation gives rise to the distinct anatomical structures of the eye, with the retina, iris and optic nerve deriving from the neuroectoderm and surrounding mesenchyme forming vasculature and fibrous coat of the eye [16, 22].

Multiple ocular complications have been reported in humans associated with Peters' anomaly, with microphthalmia and chorioretinal coloboma reported in 25% of patients, and less commonly corneal perforation and retinal detachment [23]. It has been suggested that a monogenic defect, developmental field defect or homeotic gene defect, leading to disruptions in embryogenesis and cellular differentiation, may be playing a role in Peters' anomaly with concurrent ocular malformations in humans [23]. Multiple genetic defects have been identified in human cases of Peters' anomaly, including mutations in PAX6, FOXC1, PITX2, PITX3, COL4A1, PXDN, and GJA8 [24, 25]. However, similar genetic associations remain poorly characterized in dogs [15]. Congenital aphakia and lenticular abnormalities have been reported as part of Peters' anomaly in both humans [26, 27] and dogs [28]. Aphakia is classified as either primary, resulting from the complete failure of lens development due to absence of lens placode formation from the surface ectoderm, or secondary, in which initial lens development occurs but the lens is subsequently lost through resorption or extrusion during embryogenesis [27]. In a study of Portuguese Water Dog eyes with congenital anomalies, Shaw et al. [28] reported that 47% of the eyes exhibited lesions consistent with anterior segment dysgenesis, while 19% of the eyes had aphakia, suggesting a potential genetic association involving either multiple genes or mutation in one gene with variable penetrance. The concurrent presence of multiple congenital anomalies as well as bilateral absence of the lens in the current case favors the diagnosis of primary aphakia.

To the authors' knowledge, there are no published case reports of bilateral eyelid agenesis with concurrent bilateral Peters' anomaly (anterior chamber cleavage syndrome, ACCS) and additional congenital ocular anomalies in canine species. The apparent rarity of eyelid agenesis in dogs, compared to humans and cats, may reflect species‐specific differences in developmental pathways or an underreporting of such conditions. The concurrent manifestation of bilateral eyelid agenesis, anterior chamber cleavage syndrome, anterior segment dysgenesis, and retinal detachment shares similarities with MOC syndrome in felids, suggesting that all observed lesions in this puppy could be potentially associated with a similar etiology, despite the unknown origin/pathogenesis. However, it is noteworthy that in all reviewed reported cases, including the two canine cases [1, 14], the upper eyelid is exclusively affected, in contrast to the presentation observed in the current case. Mellersh [29] described the genetic basis of several congenital ocular disorders across various canine breeds; however, none were associated with anterior segmental abnormalities. Further investigation into the genetic underpinnings of combined ocular and eyelid malformations in dogs is warranted to determine the true rarity of these conditions and whether they are simply under‐recognized in the current literature.
