Short QT Syndrome Masquerading as Lone Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Tae Kyung Yoo, Luis Trujillo-Arvizu, Caroline Kaufman, Venkata Satish Pendela, Sebastian Rodriguez, Tiffany Hu, Derin Tugal, Robert H. Helm, Kevin M. Monahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe present a case of short QT syndrome (SQTS) initially manifesting as atrial fibrillation (AF) and degenerating to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (PMVT), successfully managed with quinidine.Case SummaryA 66-year-old man presented with palpitations and was found to be in AF with a QTc interval of 335 ms. He developed PMVT with hemodynamic collapse, requiring 3 successive defibrillation shocks. Refractory to amiodarone and lidocaine, he met Gollob criteria for SQTS. He was successfully managed with oral quinidine, resulting in QTc normalization to 430 ms. A secondary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was implanted. Genetic testing was unrevealing. He remained arrhythmia-free at 6 months.DiscussionWith an estimated prevalence of 0.02%, SQTS is frequently underrecognized. This case underscores the specific efficacy of quinidine in normalizing the QT interval and suppressing malignant ventricular arrhythmias in SQTS.Take-Home MessagePrompt recognition of a short QTc interval is crucial in patients presenting with PMVT, as diagnosis directs targeted pharmacologic therapy with quinidine.

History of Presentation

A 66-year-old man presented to an outside hospital with a 2-month history of intermittent palpitations. Episodes occurred intermittently, occasionally accompanied by light-headedness, typically lasting less than 1 minute. On the day of presentation, the palpitations increased in frequency and intensity and were associated with light-headedness.

Initial vital signs were relatively unremarkable, with a blood pressure of 128/97 mm Hg, heart rate 75 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, body temperature 98.7 °F, and oxygen saturation 96% on room air. The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) at the outside hospital revealed atrial fibrillation (AF) with a ventricular rate of 83 beats/min and a corrected QTc of 335 ms (Figure 1). Laboratory evaluation revealed normal serum electrolytes (sodium 136 mEq/L, potassium 4.3 mEq/L, magnesium 2.0 mg/dL, calcium 9.9 mg/dL), creatinine of 1.3 mg/dL, and normal thyroid-stimulating hormone level (0.58 μIU/mL). Past Medical History

The patient had a past medical history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia. His home medications included amlodipine 10 mg daily, lisinopril 20 mg daily, and hydrochlorothiazide 20 mg daily. He reported alcohol consumption 2 to 3 times per week and was a former smoker, having quit more than 10 years prior. He was originally from Haiti and denied illicit drug use. There was no known family history of sudden cardiac death or inherited cardiac disease. He denied any known allergies to food, drugs, or the environment.

Differential Diagnosis

Based on the Gollob diagnostic criteria, the patient was classified as having a high probability of short QT syndrome (SQTS) (total score of 5 points: QTc <350 ms [2 points], documented PMVT [2 points], AF [1 point]).1 Metabolic causes of acquired QT shortening, including hypercalcemia and hyperkalemia, were excluded. Serial high-sensitivity troponins normalized (<5 ng/L), and the patient denied chest pain or ischemic equivalents. Further investigation was planned to exclude catecholamine excess, structural heart disease, and myocardial scar, all of which could predispose to ventricular arrhythmias.

Investigations

Urine toxicology screening was negative. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal biventricular size and function (left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%-60%) and no significant valvular or structural abnormalities. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed no evidence of structural pathology or late gadolinium enhancement.

Management

Intravenous amiodarone and lidocaine were discontinued, and oral quinidine sulfate (200 mg every 6 hours) was initiated. Following quinidine initiation, only a single episode of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia was observed within the first 24 hours of telemetry monitoring.

A single-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted for secondary prevention. Apixaban was initiated for stroke prevention in the setting of AF. After confirming tolerability, the patient was transitioned from immediate-release quinidine sulfate to extended-release quinidine gluconate (324 mg twice daily). The QTc before discharge was 430 ms, and the patient was in normal sinus rhythm (Figure 4).Figure 4Pre-Discharge 12-Lead Electrocardiogram Following Treatment With QuinidineElectrocardiogram tracing demonstrates the normalization of QTc interval to 430 ms following titration of oral quinidine.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Genetic testing revealed variants of uncertain significance in the genes encoding alpha-protein kinase 3 (ALPK3), calreticulin-3 (CALR3), and beta-myosin heavy chain (MYH7), but no pathogenic variants in genes known to be associated with SQTS. At 6-month follow-up, the patient denied recurrent palpitations. Repeat ECG demonstrated a QTc of 407 ms, and the ICD interrogation confirmed the absence of recurrent atrial or ventricular tachyarrhythmias.

Discussion

We describe a case of late-presenting SQTS successfully managed with quinidine. SQTS is a rare channelopathy, with a reported prevalence of approximately 0.02% to 0.1% in the adult population, characterized by an abnormally shortened ventricular repolarization and a high propensity for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias.2

Since its initial description in 2000, SQTS has been predominantly recognized in younger individuals, with a median age of diagnosis in the second or third decade of life. However, cases have been reported in patients well into their eighth decade, complicating diagnosis owing to higher prevalence of acquired causes of QT shortening in older adults.3

Cardiac arrest is the most common initial presentation (approximately 34%) of SQTS, although patients frequently report palpitations, presyncope, syncope, or aborted sudden cardiac death.4 Arrhythmias may occur at rest, during exertion, or even after listening to loud music.4 SQTS is commonly associated with atrial arrhythmia, as much as ventricular arrhythmia, with more than 80% of cases of SQTS exhibiting AF, even at a young age.4 Despite his later presentation, our patient exhibited hallmark features of SQTS, including palpitations, short QTc on initial ECG, and AF as the presenting rhythm with subsequent degeneration into PMVT.

Accurate diagnosis of SQTS requires meticulous history taking, ECG analysis, and exclusion of secondary causes of QT shortening. QT measurement should be performed at heart rate below 100 beats/min, ideally below 80 beats/min, to minimize correction error. Conditions such as hyperkalemia, hypercalcemia, hyperthermia, acidosis, catecholamine excess, and digitalis toxicity must be excluded. After ruling out such factors, the SQTS can be diagnosed using Gollob criteria, which incorporates QT interval, interval from J point to T peak, clinical and family history, and genotype to estimate diagnostic probability. If QTc is <330 ms (3 points), probability of short QT increases, whereas QTc <350 ms (2 points) and QTc <370 ms (1 point) can suggest high probability of SQTS based on the coexisting risk factors (Table 1).1 In this patient, the QTc of 335 ms was the silent culprit. Although it is crucial to recognize a short QT interval, the assessment can be complicated by the presence of coexisting AF owing to RR variability and rapid ventricular rates. Despite manually correcting the QTc, correct QTc measurement for diagnosing SQTS remains challenging, as Bazett's formula tends to overcorrect the QT interval at any heart rate over 60 beats/min, further complicating interpretation.5 In borderline cases (ie, borderline shortened QTc interval [330-370 ms]), exercise testing may aid diagnosis by demonstrating impaired QT adaptation to increasing heart rate.6 Although impaired rate-dependent QT shortening is a recognized hallmark of SQTS that can aid in diagnosis, it could not be reliably assessed in our patient because of the continuous presence of QT-modifying antiarrhythmic agents such as intravenous amiodarone and lidocaine, followed by oral quinidine.

Genetic testing in SQTS remains controversial, with pathogenic variants identified in a minority of cases. In 2004, the first mutation that causes abnormal shortening of the action potential duration was reported. The mutation is due to a gain of function in the gene encoding the rapidly acting delayed potassium current (IKr) channel proteins KCNH2 or HERG. This mutation facilitates the cell to repolarize more rapidly, thereby shortening the QT interval. Since this initial discovery, further research has uncovered more mutations. Currently, 6 types of SQTS are reported based on the associated gene mutation. It is believed that in SQTS, the endocardial cells and M cells repolarize faster than the epicardial cells, predisposing to reentry and arrhythmias. This accentuation of transmural dispersion of repolarization accounts for arrhythmogenesis in SQTS as well as in long QT syndrome and the Brugada syndromes. However, the correlation between genotype and phenotype cannot be explained with certainty, and the cases explained and genetically confirmed are relatively rare.7 Current guidelines recommend genetic testing primarily to facilitate screening of first-degree relatives.8,9 Electrophysiological study in SQTS is not recommended, as the sensitivity for inducibility of ventricular fibrillation during the electrophysiological study tends to be low.7 Therefore, clinicians should be reminded that SQTS is primarily a clinical and electrocardiographic diagnosis.

Management depends on clinical presentation. Asymptomatic patients with a short QTc interval may be observed, whereas it is recommended that survivors of cardiac arrest or sustained ventricular arrhythmias undergo ICD insertion.8,9 Quinidine remains the cornerstone of pharmacologic therapy, as it reliably prolongs the QT interval and ventricular refractory period, suppressing ventricular arrhythmias where other antiarrhythmics fail. For example, whereas class Ic and III antiarrhythmic drugs did not produce a significant QT interval prolongation, hydroquinidine administration caused significant QT interval prolongation, extending ventricular effective period, thereby successfully suppressing ventricular fibrillation on electrophysiologic study.10 In this case, a stepwise initiation strategy allowed confirmation of tolerability before transition to extended-release therapy, with excellent clinical response and no adverse events. This precaution was warranted, given the high incidence of quinidine-associated gastrointestinal adverse events, including nausea and vomiting (3%-22%) and diarrhea (24%-35%). The patient demonstrated excellent tolerance with no significant side effects, allowing for a safe transition to maintenance therapy with quinidine gluconate.

Conclusions

This case of a 66-year-old man with SQTS highlights the importance of meticulous ECG interpretation in patients presenting with unexplained PMVT. Recognition of SQTS mandates a distinct therapeutic approach, including QT-prolonging pharmacologic therapy with quinidine and consideration of ICD implantation for secondary prevention.
