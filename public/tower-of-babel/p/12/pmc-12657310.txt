Surface-dependent reorganization of Chlorella vulgaris cell wall components revealed by AFM and SRS microscopy
Authors: Adriana Pereira C Sánchez, Cécile Pouzet, Audrey Beaussart, Bruno Lartiges, Cécile Formosa-Dague
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCharacterization of microalgae cell wall properties using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) depends critically on the immobilization strategy employed. In this study, we investigated how various immobilization surfaces influence the biophysical properties of the Chlorella vulgaris cell wall as probed by AFM. Cells were immobilized on three distinct surfaces, each leveraging different types of interactions with cells: polyoctyl-(PO)-Chitosan-coated glass slides promoting hydrophobic interactions, Superfrost™ positively charged glass slides that interact hydrophobically and electrostatically with cells, and PDMS chambers that immobilize cells through mechanical confinement. AFM imaging, nanoindentation, and force spectroscopy revealed that the biophysical properties of the Chlorella vulgaris cell wall strongly depend on the immobilization strategy. At the same time, all other experimental conditions were kept identical. Cells attached to PO-Chitosan through hydrophobic interactions displayed high surface roughness, higher rigidity, hydrophobic behavior, and positive surface charges. In contrast, cells immobilized on positively charged but less hydrophobic Superfrost™ slides exhibited smoother surfaces, lower rigidity, reduced hydrophobicity, and no detectable positive charges. Cells confined in PDMS chambers exhibited the smoothest and softest cell walls, characterized by hydrophilic and uncharged surfaces. Complementary Stimulated Raman Scattering (SRS) microscopy experiments confirmed distinct lipid rearrangements associated with each immobilization method. Together, these results support the hypothesis formulated in this study that immobilization surfaces can cause the reorganization of cell wall components—polysaccharides, proteins, and lipids—thereby reshaping the physico-chemical and mechanical surface properties. This study thus demonstrates the great significance of immobilization strategies to ensure the reliability of nanoscale biophysical measurements in microalgae research.Keywords: Atomic force microscopy (AFM), Cell immobilization, Microalgae cell wall, Chlorella vulgaris, Biophysical properties

Introduction

Microalgae are photosynthetic microorganisms with significant biotechnological relevance, widely used in applications ranging from biofuel production and wastewater treatment to the generation of high-value compounds such as pigments, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceuticals (Occhipinti et al., 2024; Cunha et al., 2023). Unlocking the full potential of microalgae-based technologies requires a detailed understanding of their structural and surface properties, which play a crucial role in how they interact with their environment and thus directly influence key processing steps such as harvesting and extraction – essential steps for the development of effective engineering strategies. In this context, Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) has emerged as a valuable technology for nanoscale characterization of microalgae surface properties under physiological conditions (Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2021). It enables high-resolution imaging and quantification of surface properties at the nanoscale, including key physical metrics such as surface topography, adhesion force, or nanomechanical properties, which together provide valuable insights to understand their behavior in different conditions and optimize processes such as culture, harvesting, or extraction of the produced compounds (Besson et al., 2019; Cybulska, 2024). To give examples, recent studies have employed AFM: (i) to assess structural and mechanical changes in microalgae after enzymatic treatment, providing insights into cell wall rupture (Duval et al., 2023), ii) to explore the hydrophobic characteristics of the cell wall using functionalized tips, revealing surface chemical heterogeneity (Lesniewska et al., 2024; Maciel et al., 2025), (iii) to characterize the different morphologies that microalgae can adopt (Mišić Radić et al., 2023), or (iv) to introduce novel techniques such as FluidFM for high-sensitivity probing of cell surface hydrophobic properties (Demir et al., 2021).

However, reliable AFM analysis of living microalgae or any biological sample requires effective immobilization strategies that prevent cell lateral displacement during scanning while preserving their native surface properties. Poor immobilization can lead to artifacts and compromise the accuracy of both imaging and mechanical data, making this step critical in live-cell AFM workflows (Mišić Radić et al., 2023; Benn et al., 2019; Formosa et al., 2015). Current strategies are typically classified into two main approaches. The first one relies on physical confinement methods, using tools such as 3D-printed holders, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chambers, or microporous membranes (Joshua et al., 2023; Formosa et al., 2015; Vlassov et al., 2019; Kasas and Ikai, 1995). On the other hand, immobilization on modified surfaces remains a common and versatile alternative. Examples include the use of poly-l-lysine or polyethyleneimine-coated glass slides (Benn et al., 2019), Cell-Tak™ or Vectabond® surfaces (Benn et al., 2019), as well as Superfrost™ positively-charged glass slides (Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2024), each method offering a different degree of effectiveness depending on the type of cells immobilized. Furthermore, tailored immobilization protocols have been designed by specific research groups to accommodate distinct cell characteristics. For instance, our group has recently developed and validated a novel immobilization method using polyoctyl-chitosan (PO-Chitosan), which has proven particularly effective to immobilize cells through hydrophobic interactions (Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2024; Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2023a).

Previous studies on microbial systems have clearly demonstrated that immobilization is a crucial step for AFM investigations. However, most of these works have primarily focused on cell adhesion efficiency and the quality of topographic imaging, while not comparing the physicochemical consequences of immobilization at the nanoscale. For instance, Meyer et al. (2010) compared several immobilization strategies for Bacillus subtilis and Escherichia coli under liquid conditions, identifying polyphenolic adhesive proteins (Cell-Tak™) as the most reliable approach for stable quality imaging (Meyer et al., 2010). Similarly, Kuyukina et al. (2014) reviewed a wide range of immobilization methods and highlighted trade-offs between accessibility, morphological preservation, and viability, but their evaluation remained largely methodological (Kuyukina et al., 2014). More recently, Benn et al. (2019) compared multiple coatings for E. coli and showed that adhesion strength, viability, and imaging suitability were highly dependent on the combination of strain, buffer, and surface chemistry (Benn et al., 2019). While these studies underscore the centrality of immobilization, they also reveal a persistent gap: the lack of systematic analyses addressing how immobilization alters molecular exposure, surface chemistry, and nanoscale biomechanical properties. Our study provides this understanding for C. vulgaris, providing a model for how immobilization can alter molecular exposure and nanoscale mechanics, with potential relevance to other microorganisms, where such investigations remain scarce.

This raises critical concerns, as parameters such as surface adhesion, charge distribution, and nanomechanical properties, central to cell wall characterization, may be affected by the choice of the immobilization substrate (Mišić Radić et al., 2023). The cell wall, an integral structural component in many microalgae species, usually forms a complex structure. Among its different functions, the cell wall is primarily responsible for maintaining the shape of the microalgae cells and for mediating the interactions of cells with the extracellular environment (Lesniewska et al. 2024; Mišić Radić et al., 2023). This structure is particularly dynamic in species like Chlorella vulgaris, a widely used model organism due to its biotechnological relevance in particular to produce biofuels. Indeed, C. vulgaris cell wall is a multilayered structure that evolves throughout the growth cycle (Occhipinti et al., 2024; Cunha et al., 2023) and varies with strain and cultivation conditions (Sekar et al., 2024). Composed of lipids, proteins, chitin-like polysaccharides, and neutral sugars (Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2023b), the cell wall of C. vulgaris undergoes notable structural and mechanical changes during culture aging, shifting from a smooth to a fibrillar surface (Weber et al., 2022; Baudelet et al., 2017; Duval et al., 2023). This composition, which includes both rigid and flexible components, poses unique challenges for accurate characterization (Gerken et al., 2013). Furthermore, surface properties such as hydrophobicity and surface charges influence its interactions with substrates, making it particularly well-suited for investigating how immobilization affects AFM-based measurements (Yuan et al., 2019; Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2023b).

In this study, we investigate how different immobilization strategies affect the apparent nanomechanical and physicochemical properties of C. vulgaris cells. Using AFM and chemical force microscopy (CFM) technique, we compare cells immobilized on either modified substrates (hydrophobic and positively-charged surfaces) or trapped in PDMS chambers, and analyze the modulations of adhesion forces, surface interactions, and Young's modulus. Furthermore, beyond highlighting the impact of immobilization on AFM-based measurements, we further hypothesize that these differences may stem from surface-induced alterations in cell wall organization. To probe these effects, a complementary analysis was performed using Stimulated Raman Scattering (SRS) microscopy, which allowed us to investigate potential changes in molecular distribution at the cell surface. Overall, this work contributes to the establishment of robust nanoscale characterization protocols and provides insights into how physical constraints at the cell–substrate interface can affect biophysical measurements, which is critical for biotechnological processes relying on microalgal surface properties.

Materials and methods

Immobilization techniques. Three types of surfaces were used: PO-Chitosan-coated slides, Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chambers, and commercial Superfrost™Plus (J1800AMNZ, Epredia, Germany) microscope slides. PO-Chitosan was synthesized from chitosan via reductive amination as described in (Demir-Yilmaz, Ftouhi, et al. 2023). Then, glass cover slips were plasma-cleaned for 10 min at 60 % power (0.6 mbar) using a FEMTO-B system (Electronic Diener®, Germany). They were then coated with 60 μL of 1 g/L PO-Chitosan solution by spin-coating (POLOS Spin150i, RK AHT, Germany) at 1000 rpm for 3 min. The coated slides were then dried at 37 °C and stored for 1–10 days before use. Mechanical immobilization was performed using PDMS chambers, produced following the protocol described in (Formosa et al., 2015). PDMS surfaces were then plasma-treated (FEMTO-B, Electronic Diener®, Germany) for 30 s at 60 % power (0.6 mBar) to render them hydrophilic, and further to immobilize cells using convective capillary assembly (as described in (Formosa et al., 2015)).

Cell Culture and Immobilization. C. vulgaris (CCAP 211/11B, Culture Collection of Algae and Protozoa, UK) was cultivated under sterile conditions in Wright's Cryptophyte (WC) Medium (Guillard and Lorenzen, 1972) at 21 °C for 7 days (OD750 ranging between 1.8 and 1.9). Cultures were maintained at 120 rpm with a 16:8 (Light: Dark) photoperiod (40 μmol photons m−1 s−1) in a climate chamber (MLR-352-PE, Panasonic, Japan). Before experiments, cells were harvested by centrifugation (4000 G, 3 min, 20 °C), washed twice in Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS) at pH 8.0, and immobilized for 30 min on PO-Chitosan, Superfrost™Plus glass slides or PDMS Stamps prepared following the protocol described in Formosa et al., 2015. After that, unattached cells were removed by gently rinsing the surfaces with PBS.

AFM cantilevers functionalization with methyl and carboxyl-terminated thiols. Gold-coated cantilevers (NPG-10, Bruker, USA) were first plasma-cleaned for 1.5 min at 60 % power (0.6 mbar) using a FEMTO-B system (Electronic Diener®, Germany). Subsequently, they underwent overnight functionalization by immersion in anhydrous absolute ethanol containing either 1 mM dodecanethiol for methyl group attachment (100 % ethanol) or 1 mM 16-mercaptohexadecanoic acid for carboxyl group modification (both from Sigma Aldrich®) (5 % v/v acetic acid/ethanol) (Willey et al., 2004). After treatment, the cantilevers were rinsed in absolute ethanol (methyl-terminated thiol-CH3) or in 5 % v/v acetic acid/ethanol (carboxyl-terminated thiol-COOH), and dried under a nitrogen stream.

AFM Force measurements. AFM force measurements on C. vulgaris were performed in PBS (pH 8.0) in force spectroscopy mode using a NanoWizard IV XP AFM (Bruker, Billerica, USA) using either thiol-functionalized AFM tips or bubble probes produced using FluidFM technology. First, thiol-functionalized NPG-10 AFM cantilevers (Bruker, nominal spring constant of 0.06 N/m) were used in force spectroscopy mode to record interactions over 1 × 1 μm2 areas on top of cells using an applied force of 0.5nN, an approach-retract speed of 1.0 μm/s, a z-range of 0.3 μm, and 32 × 32 pixels. Then, air bubbles were formed at the aperture of microfluidic cantilevers (aperture of 8 μm, nominal spring constant of 0.3 N/m) using FluidFM (Cytosurge AG, Switzerland) as described in (Demir et al., 2021). In this case, interactions were also recorded in force spectroscopy mode over 1 × 1 μm2 areas on top of cells using an applied force of 1 nN, an approach-retract speed of 1.0 μm/s, a z-range of 1.0 μm, and 10 × 10 pixels for cells and 20 × 20 pixels for the surfaces. In both cases, the surface areas scanned were 10 × 10 μm2 areas, and the cantilevers used were calibrated using the thermal noise method (Hutter and Bechhoefer, 1993). Adhesion forces were then extracted from retraction curves by measuring the height of the biggest peak on the curves. In both cases, at least 7 cells coming from 2 independent cultures were probed.

Nanomechanical analysis. Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) force spectroscopy was used to quantify the nanomechanical properties of C. vulgaris by measuring its Young's modulus (YM). In this method, a calibrated cantilever indents the sample while recording force–distance (FD) curves, which are converted into force–indentation data by subtracting the cantilever deflection. The YM was then calculated using the Hertz model, assuming a Poisson ratio of 0.5 and a tip opening angle of 17.5°, based on 50 nm indentation segments (Hertz, 1882). Measurements were made using MLCT cantilevers (Bruker, nominal spring constant 0.03 N/m), with applied forces ranging from 2.0 to 3.0 nN, over 1 × 1 μm2 areas, and 20 × 20 pixels. Spring constants were determined using the thermal noise method before the measurements (Hutter and Bechhoefer, 1993). For each condition, at least 5 cells from two independent cultures were analyzed.

Roughness analysis. Surface roughness measurements were conducted on C. vulgaris cells to assess potential changes in cell wall topography resulting from different immobilization methods. For this purpose, high-resolution topographic images of 1 × 1 μm2 areas on top of cells were acquired in PBS (pH 8) using the QI™ advanced imaging mode, equipped with MLCT cantilevers. Cantilever spring constants were calibrated using the thermal noise method before imaging (Hutter and Bechhoefer, 1993). For each condition, at least 5 cells from two independent cultures were analyzed. Images were acquired at a resolution of 150 × 150 pixels under an applied force of 0.5 nN to minimize cell deformation. The resulting height images were processed using Bruker's Data Processing software to calculate the arithmetic average roughness (Ra).

Stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) microscopy. Stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) microscopy was used to examine lipid composition of the C. vulgaris cell wall. Two characteristic peaks were detected, 1449 cm−1 assigned to CH₂/CH₃ vibrational modes, typically attributed to saturated fatty acids, and 1661 cm−1 assigned to C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> C stretching modes and characteristic of unsaturated fatty acids. SRS images were acquired on a LEICA Stellaris laser scanning microscope Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany) coupled with a PicoEmerald-S laser system (APE, Berlin, Germany) with a fixed Stokes beam wavelength at 1032 nm and a tunable pump beam wavelength in the range of 720–940 nm. The Stokes beam is modulated at 20 MHz, and SRL signals are detected on a silicon photodiode. Images were acquired with a 40× objective (HC PL IRAPO 40×, water, NA1.1) used in conjunction with an oil immersion condenser (NA1.4). The laser power is 150 mW for the pump laser and 300 mW for the Stokes laser, while the intensity varies between 30 and 50 %. The spectra acquisition was performed in the so-called fingerprint region: 1400–1750 cm−1. The SRS data analysis was performed using Origin software.

Statistical analysis. AFM and SRS results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on a minimum of two independent replicates. In force spectroscopy experiments, the proportion of force curves without adhesive events (n.a.) was calculated relative to the total number of total recorded curves. Histograms were generated using 20 pN bin widths across the range 0–5 nN. Violin plots were generated using a kernel smooth model, with the mean represented as a circle and the median indicated by a horizontal line. Statistical differences in Young's modulus and surface roughness among the immobilization methods were evaluated using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

The different immobilization methods chosen allow immobilizing cells through different interacting modes

The first step in this study was to characterize the different surfaces used for C. vulgaris cell immobilization. For that, we first acquired topographical images of PO-Chitosan-coated glass slides, Superfrost™ glass slides, and plasma-treated PDMS chambers, using Quantitative Imaging (QI) mode. The images presented in Fig. 1 reveal that both PO-Chitosan (Fig. 1A) and Superfrost™ (Fig. 1B) surfaces exhibit smooth and homogeneous topographies, suggesting a uniform coating and minimal surface irregularities. The PDMS surface (Fig. 1C) presents a well-defined microstructured pattern, with microwells where cells can be trapped and mechanically confined.

Surface topography of the different immobilization surfaces. Representative image and AFM height image of (A) PO-Chitosan-coated glass slide, (B) Superfrost™ glass slide, and (C) PDMS surface.

To better understand the mechanisms governing cell immobilization, we then characterized the physicochemical properties of the immobilization surfaces in the absence of cells. Because surface hydrophobicity and electrostatic charge play a central role in mediating cell-surface interactions, adhesion force measurements were performed using functionalized AFM cantilevers designed to probe both hydrophobic and positively charged surfaces. The adhesion force distributions and representative approach-retraction curves are summarized in a 3 × 3 panel matrix (Fig. 2), where rows correspond to the probe type: CH₃-terminated tips (hydrophobic), bubble probes made with FluidFM (hydrophobic, radius of the contact area of approximately 1.2 μm), and COOH-terminated tips (negatively charged, deprotonated COO− at the working pH), and columns correspond to the immobilization surfaces. The use of bubble probes complements the nanoscale precision of CH₃ tips, potentially revealing microscale effects such as heterogeneous adhesion domains that could influence whole-cell interaction behavior. In each case, measurements were performed on two different surfaces, in triplicate, using 1 × 1 μm2 areas for the functionalized tips and 10 × 10 μm2 areas for FluidFM. The representative force distance curves obtained in each case for the surfaces are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Histograms showing the distribution of the adhesion forces (in nanonewtons, nN), recorded in force spectroscopy experiments with different probes on the different surfaces. The values indicated as n.a. represent the percentage of force curves that show no adhesion interactions. Rows correspond to the type of the probe used: CH₃-terminated tips (hydrophobic) (A, B, and C), bubbles made using FluidFM (hydrophobic) (D, E, and F), and COO−-terminated tips (negatively charged) (G, H, and I). Columns represent the tested surfaces: PO-Chitosan-coated glass slides (A, D, and G), Superfrost™ glass slides (B, E, and H), and plasma-exposed PDMS surfaces (C, F, and I). Enclosed in each histogram are representative adhesion maps (color-scale: 1 nN) obtained on 1 μm × 1 μm areas for each surface, with a resolution of 32 × 32 pixels for the cantilevers and of 20 × 20 pixels for the bubble probe produced with FluidFM. In each case, 5 surfaces were probed, using 2 different AFM tips.

The results obtained revealed distinct physicochemical interaction profiles between the three immobilization surfaces. On PO-Chitosan-coated glass slides, moderate hydrophobic interactions were recorded with CH₃-functionalized tips, with an average adhesion force of 0.32 ± 0.44 nN (Fig. 2A). Significantly higher adhesion forces were measured with the bubble probe (3.43 ± 6.86 nN, Fig. 2D), which further confirms the hydrophobic character of this type of surface. The pronounced discrepancy observed between CH₃-functionalized and bubble probe measurements can be attributed to the considerably larger contact area of the bubble probe, which leads to higher adhesion values. It is also interesting to note that, although the height images (Fig. 1) suggested a smooth and regular surface, the adhesion maps (Fig. 2A and D) revealed clear heterogeneities in surface hydrophobicity at both the nanoscale (probed by hydrophobic tips) and the microscale (probed with the bubble), indicating that the PO-Chitosan coating is in fact, rather heterogeneous. Regarding COO−-functionalized tips, they detected negligible adhesion (0.08 ± 0.07 nN, 26.1 % n.a. in Fig. 2G), indicating the absence of positively charged groups on this surface. These findings suggest that PO-Chitosan surfaces can mediate only hydrophobic interactions with cells.

When probed by CH₃-functionalized tips, the forces recorded with Superfrost™ glass slides were comparable to those on PO-Chitosan (0.55 ± 0.20 nN). However, a higher proportion of non-adhesive events was observed (28.0 % vs. 14.6 % on PO-Chitosan), indicating an overall lower hydrophobicity. This trend was further confirmed with the bubble probe, where forces were significantly reduced (0.93 ± 3.55 nN vs 3.43 ± 6.86 nN on PO-Chitosan glass slides), and 28 % of curves showed no adhesion events, compared with only 4.1 % on PO-Chitosan surfaces. In this case as well, the adhesion maps revealed spatial variations in hydrophobicity, indicating that the coating was heterogeneous. With the COO−-functionalized tips, adhesion events were detected with an average force of 0.29 ± 0.35 nN (Fig. 2H). However, the relatively high proportion of non-adhesive curves (42.5 %) indicates that only a limited number of interaction events occurred, suggesting that the positive charges are sparsely distributed across the surface. However, this dual presence of hydrophobic properties and positive charges suggests that Superfrost™ surfaces exhibit a complex and heterogeneous interaction profile. Cells immobilized on these surfaces may adhere through both hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, which can enhance adhesion stability but also introduce variability due to competing or transient binding events.

Finally, on plasma-treated PDMS surfaces, minimal adhesions were recorded with CH₃-functionalized tips, with a percentage of non-adhesive curves of 77.0 %, and an average force of 0.05 ± 0.09 nN (Fig. 2C), indicating the absence of hydrophobic properties after plasma treatment. This was further confirmed by the measurements performed with the bubble probe, where a lower average adhesion force of 0.35 ± 0.24 nN was recorded (25.9 % n.a., Fig. 2F). The same trend was observed with COO−-functionalized tips, where very few interactions were recorded (96.5 % of non-adhesive curves, Fig. 2I), indicating that the surface is rather negatively-charged. This is probably due to the formation of an oxide layer (negatively charged) on the surface during exposure to plasma (Neves et al., 2024). Thus, it is most likely that PDMS can only support weak and transient interactions with cells, confirming that cell immobilization in this case results from physical confinement or entrapment rather than from stable interactions.

The data presented in Fig. 2 illustrate how the different probes can reveal the distinct physico-chemical properties of the various immobilization surfaces. PO-Chitosan surfaces exhibit predominantly hydrophobic characteristics, whereas Superfrost™ surfaces combine moderate hydrophobicity with positive charges capable of inducing electrostatic interactions with cells. In contrast, PDMS surfaces do not support these types of interactions and promote immobilization primarily through mechanical entrapment within the chambers.

The immobilization surface has an impact on microalgae cell wall biophysical properties

After characterizing the physicochemical properties of the different immobilization surfaces used in this study and demonstrating that each can mediate distinct types of interactions, we then analyzed the cells immobilized on these surfaces. It is important to emphasize that both the culture conditions and cell preparation before immobilization were rigorously controlled to ensure that no differences arose from these steps. We first looked at the nanomechanical properties of their cell wall through nano-indentation experiments. This parameter is particularly relevant, as variations in nanomechanical properties can reflect differences in cell wall composition and/or organization, which would indicate a potential effect of the immobilization method. In this type of experiment, a bare AFM tip with known mechanical properties is pressed against the cell wall of individual cells, inducing controlled deformation. By recording the resulting force–indentation curves, it becomes possible to quantify the mechanical response of the cell wall. From these measurements, the Young's modulus, a parameter reflecting the rigidity of the cell wall, can be extracted. The Young's modulus of C. vulgaris exhibited significant dependence on the immobilization surface, as shown in Fig. 3A. In each case, at least 8 cells from 2 different cultures were analyzed (which corresponds to 3156 force curves analyzed in each condition). The representative indentation curves obtained in each case are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2. Cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan had the highest average modulus, 1.57 ± 1.00 MPa, similar to our previous results obtained for the same strain on this same surface (Demir-Yilmaz, Schiavone, et al. 2023). On Superfrost™ slides, the modulus was significantly lower, averaging 0.30 ± 0.29 MPa, while cells on PDMS exhibited the lowest values, averaging 0.12 ± 0.15 MPa. As it has been shown before, it is most likely that these changes arise from changes in the organization of the cell wall (Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2023a, Formosa-Dague et al., 2018). In our experiments, the indentation segments (50 nm) used for Young's modulus determination corresponded to approximately 1 % of the total cell height, ensuring that deformation remained confined to the cell wall. Under these conditions, the influence of the underlying substrate, even in the case of the more compliant PDMS, is considered negligible, and the measured modulus values can thus be attributed solely to the mechanical response of the cell itself (Moura et al., 2025; Dimitriadis et al., 2002).

To complete this analysis, topographic mapping of 1 × 1 μm areas on the upper surface of cells immobilized on the different surfaces was conducted to quantify surface roughness. Surface roughness, like nanomechanical properties, can also serve as a sensitive indicator of a change in the cell wall organization. The distribution of the roughness values that could be extracted from the images is presented in Fig. 3B, and examples of the images obtained in each case are showed in Fig. 3 C, D, and E. From the images presented, a qualitative observation can already be made: cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan glass slides (Fig. 3C) exhibit more surface protrusions, indicating increased roughness, whereas cells immobilized in PDMS chambers (Fig. 3E) appear comparatively smoother. In this particular case, the slight variations observed between scanning lines are attributed to minor cell movements. Cells mechanically confined on PDMS are not fully immobilized, as they are retained primarily through physical confinement rather than through physico-chemical interactions with the surface. Quantitative analysis confirmed these differences: cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan had the highest average roughness (11.1 ± 8.4 nm), followed by those on Superfrost™ (6.2 ± 3.9 nm) and PDMS (4.8 ± 3.1 nm), as shown in Fig. 3C, suggesting underlying variability in cell surface organization. The statistical analysis based on the Mann-Whitney test shows that these differences are not significant. However, when comparing the distribution of roughness values, cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan glass slides exhibit a higher standard deviation (8.4 nm) compared to cells on Superfrost™ surfaces (3.9 nm) or those trapped in PDMS chambers (4.8 nm). Thus, although the roughness values alone are not statistically distinct, their distribution patterns reveal differences in the structural state of the cell dome that is probed. The increased surface complexity observed on cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan reflects a higher density of surface molecules, differences in membrane tension, or rearrangements of cell wall components. This interpretation is supported by the fact that cells on PO-Chitosan also had the highest Young's modulus values. On the other hand, the narrower distributions observed for cells immobilized on Superfrost™ and PDMS chambers align with their lower and more consistent YM values, suggesting a more uniform surface structure.

Thus far, our AFM analysis demonstrates that the immobilization method has a decisive impact on the biomechanical and structural properties of the C. vulgaris cell surface, suggesting that immobilization not only stabilizes the cells but may also influence the organization of their cell walls. Another important question, however, is whether these differences reflect true changes induced by the immobilization procedure, or whether the method instead selects a subpopulation of cells with distinct properties. Indeed, exponential-phase cultures are inherently heterogeneous, comprising newly divided young cells alongside more mature cells originating from the initial inoculation. Previous investigations on C. vulgaris immobilized on polyethyleneimine (PEI) surfaces through electrostatic interactions have revealed heterogeneous variations in cell wall composition, as assessed by confocal microscopy (Duval et al., 2023). In this study, six-day-old cultures exhibited roughness values of 3.0 ± 1.8 nm, within the range observed here for PDMS, while only a subset of cells displayed detectable glycoconjugate signals under confocal microscopy, usually characteristic of older (20-day) cultures. Moreover, the chitin-like layer appeared incomplete or undergoing reorganization, suggesting that C. vulgaris populations comprise cells at different developmental stages and that immobilization could act as a pre-selection step, enriching for specific subgroups. Consistent with this, Duval et al. (2023) reported higher roughness values in 20-day-old cultures (8.2 ± 5.2 nm), more similar to the values observed for cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan surfaces in the present work (Duval et al., 2023). This raises the possibility that PO-Chitosan preferentially retains mature cells, whereas Superfrost™ and PDMS substrates may capture younger ones. Yet, several observations suggest that this is not the case. First, the broad distribution of roughness values recorded for cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan glass slides indicates the presence of cells with different wall structures, consistent with populations at various growth stages. Second, in a previous study from our group, stationary cells immobilized on PO-chitosan glass slides exhibited a significantly higher Young's modulus (2.1 ± 1.3 MPa) than those in the exponential phase (Irem Demir-Yilmaz et al. 2023). If PO-Chitosan selected only mature cells, the modulus values obtained here would likely be higher than the ones recorded. In the same study, stationary-phase cells also showed a greater lipid content in their cell walls compared to exponential-phase cells. If PO-Chitosan surfaces are enriched for these mature cells, one would expect stronger lipid signatures. To further examine this hypothesis, we therefore employed Stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) microscopy to assess the lipid composition and distribution at the surface of C. vulgaris cells immobilized on the different substrates.

SRS microscopy reveals surface-dependent reorganization of cell wall lipids

SRS microscopy enables sensitive, label-free detection of specific molecular vibrations, providing direct insights into cell wall lipid composition in situ, in the same conditions as the ones used to perform the AFM experiments. In particular, two characteristic vibrational bands give relevant information: the 1449 cm−1 band, attributed to CH₂/CH₃ deformations commonly associated with saturated fatty acids, and the 1661 cm−1 band, corresponding to C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> C stretching modes in unsaturated fatty acids, such as PUFAs. Due to the configuration of the system, PDMS could not be included in SRS measurements, as its thickness exceeds the working distance required for signal collection, the forward scattering light being collected by an S1 N.A. 1.4 condenser lens. Additionally, the system's spatial resolution (∼450 nm) implies that the SRS signal predominantly originates from the outermost layer of the cells, thereby selectively probing lipid distribution at or near the cell wall surface.

As shown in Fig. 4A and B, Raman spectra acquired from cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan and Superfrost™ surfaces exhibited distinct signal intensities at both peaks, with visibly higher signals detected on cells immobilized on Superfrost™. Quantitative peak area analysis (Fig. 4C) confirmed a significant increase in the intensity of both lipid-associated bands in cells on Superfrost™-immobilized cells, particularly at 1661 cm−1, where PUFA-related peak areas increased from 1129 to 1655 a.u.xcm−1 in cells immobilized on Superfrost™ glass slides. This difference was further supported by peak intensity comparisons (Fig. 4D), with values at 1449 cm−1 increasing from 26 % to 35 % and at 1661 cm−1 from 34 % to 49 % between PO-Chitosan and Superfrost™, respectively. Together, these results indicate that unsaturated lipids are more prominent at the surface of cells immobilized on Superfrost™.

These findings indicate that while the overall saturated fatty acids content (as reflected by the 1449 cm−1 signal) remained relatively unchanged, the immobilization surface has an impact on the distribution of unsaturated lipids (1661 cm−1 signal) that could be more detected in the cell wall of cells immobilized on Superfrost™ glass slides. It has been established that in lipid membranes, saturated fatty acids increase rigidity, whereas unsaturated fatty acids enhance fluidity (Hąc-Wydro and Wydro, 2007). Although our system involves a cell wall rather than a pure lipid bilayer, our observations are consistent with this. Cells immobilized on Superfrost™ glass slides exhibit a significantly reduced Young's modulus (Fig. 3A) compared to cells on PO-chitosan, which could be explained by an increased proportion of unsaturated fatty acids leading to greater wall fluidity. In contrast, the higher Young's modulus measured in PO-chitosan–immobilized cells may reflect a lower proportion of unsaturated lipids, resulting in a stiffer structure. This difference could also account for the increased cell surface roughness measured in the PO-chitosan condition, whereas in Superfrost™-immobilized cells, the more fluid wall may prevent the protrusion of molecules at the surface, leading to a smoother surface. This effect does not imply membrane damage or leakage, as no morphological signs of disruption were observed during AFM imaging experiments (Fig. 3C-E). Moreover, these results are also a further indication that immobilization on PO-Chitosan does not select mature cells, as in the end, the lipidic levels on PO-Chitosan immobilized cells remain similar to those of cells immobilized on Superfrost™.

Moradi-Kheibari et al. (2022) reported that lipid profiles in C. vulgaris remain relatively stable on a daily basis, with significant changes only observable over periods of several days, illustrating that metabolic shifts require sustained periods (Moradi-Kheibari et al., 2022). In contrast, Mujtaba et al. (2012) showed that lipid accumulation could rise sharply within 12–24 h under nitrogen deprivation. However, this response occurs only in dense, mature cultures, reflecting stress-induced metabolic adaptations (Mujtaba et al., 2012; Zhu et al., 2023). In the present study, however, experiments were conducted at pH 8, close to the physiological conditions of the culture, and thus could not trigger stress-induced lipid production. In light of this, these observations would indicate that immobilization does not induce detectable metabolic shifts within the timeframe of the experiments performed in this study; instead, it seems that the immobilization method promotes a spatial redistribution of existing cell wall components based on lipidic rearrangement. As mentioned above, since such redistribution may also affect the accessibility of certain cell surface molecules, it should also have an impact on the cell surface's physicochemical properties. Thus, the next section investigates hydrophobicity and surface charge at the cell dome, using different probes, similar to what was done to characterize the immobilization surfaces.

Force spectroscopy experiments show the influence of the immobilization method on cell surface physico-chemical properties

To investigate how different immobilization conditions modulate the physicochemical properties of the C. vulgaris cell surface, we conducted AFM force spectroscopy on 1 × 1 μm2 areas on the top-most surface region accessible to the probe. Fig. 5 summarizes the adhesion forces obtained using three probe types: CH₃-terminated tips (hydrophobic), bubble probes made with FluidFM (large hydrophobic, contact area of approximately 1.2 μm in radius), and COO−-terminated tips (negatively charged). The representative force-distance curves obtained in each case for the surfaces are presented in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan exhibited the most consistent and elevated adhesion across all probe types. Using CH₃ tips, the measured adhesion force averaged 0.19 ± 0.18 nN (Fig. 5A), with 46.6 % of the force curves showing no adhesion events. With bubbles, values increased substantially to 4.99 ± 4.89 nN (n.a. 15.5 %, Fig. 5D), as expected with a higher contact area. COO- tips confirmed the presence of positive charges on the surface of cells in this condition, with an average force of 0.32 ± 0.44 nN and 54.6 % of non-adhesive force curves recorded (Fig. 5G). On Superfrost™ slides, cells displayed a markedly different surface physico-chemical profile. CH₃ tips yielded a much lower adhesion force of 0.09 ± 0.09 nN (Fig. 5B), and 87.6 % of the curves showed no interaction. Measurements using the bubble probe also showed decreased adhesion forces compared to cells on PO-Chitosan, averaging at 3.09 ± 2.7 nN, with 41.8 % of curves showing no adhesive events (Fig. 5E). These results indicate that the hydrophobic properties of cells immobilized on Superfrost™ glass slides are reduced compared to cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan surfaces. Interestingly, COO− tips detected minimal adhesion, with forces averaging 0.02 ± 0.01 nN, with 66.9 % of curves presenting no adhesion events (Fig. 5H), indicating that the cell dome surface contains almost no positive charges under these conditions. Cells immobilized on PDMS presented the lowest adhesion signals across all probes. With CH₃ tips, no measurable adhesion was detected (0 nN, 100 % of non-adhesive curves, Fig. 5C). Adhesion measured with the bubble probe was very low compared to the other immobilizing conditions, averaging 0.19 ± 0.13nN, with 89.3 % of curves showing no adhesion (Fig. 5F). COO−-functionalized tips recorded only 0.27 ± 0.26 nN, with 98.5 % of non-adhesive curves (Fig. 5I). Thus, cells immobilized in PDMS chambers are rather hydrophilic compared to those on Superfrost™ and PO-Chitosan surfaces, and present no positive charges on their surface.

These results provide two key insights. First, in terms of hydrophobicity, cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan exhibit increased hydrophobic properties compared to those on Superfrost™, while cells in PDMS chambers appear completely hydrophilic, showing no interaction with hydrophobic probes. Second, regarding surface charge, only cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan surfaces displayed detectable positive charges, whereas in the other conditions, the cell surface accessible to the tip appeared exclusively negatively charged. Taken together, these results highlight that the choice of immobilization method critically affects the surface properties of microalgal cells. They are consistent with the results obtained with SRS microscopy, which revealed that lipid components are not uniformly distributed at the cell surface but instead reorganize depending on the immobilization method. Such rearrangements may explain the differences in hydrophobicity and surface charge observed here, as the relative accessibility of lipid versus polysaccharide or protein-rich regions is most likely different depending on the immobilization strategy. This convergence of AFM and SRS data thus provides strong evidence that immobilization has an important impact on the nanoscale architecture of the cell wall, emphasizing the need for careful immobilization strategy selection to ensure accurate AFM-based measurements.

Proposed hypothesis: Immobilization induces reorganization of cell wall components

The observed differences in the biophysical properties of C. vulgaris cell wall across PO-Chitosan, Superfrost™, and PDMS immobilization surfaces most likely reflect a rapid spatial rearrangement of pre-existing cell wall components rather than metabolic remodeling. The immobilization step after harvesting takes approximately 30 min, and AFM measurements are completed in between 30 min and 3 h maximum, a timescale insufficient for major structural remodeling (Mujtaba et al., 2012). Although transcriptomic changes can be initiated rapidly in cells subjected to stress (Wang et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2017), this timeframe is most likely too short for the corresponding transcripts to be translated, targeted, and incorporated into the cell wall. However, interpreting the different results obtained in this study requires careful consideration of the molecular composition of the C. vulgaris cell wall, as different biomolecular classes can contribute differently to the measured forces and may be differentially exposed depending on the immobilization conditions. Microalgal cell walls are primarily composed of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids. Polysaccharides constitute the main structural components and, in C. vulgaris, include β1,3-galactan, chitin-like glycans (glucosamine), and neutral sugars including rhamnose and galactose (Ferreira et al., 2020; Takeda, 1991, Demir-Yilmaz et al., 2023a). They are hydrophilic, and their net charge depends on ionizable groups: carboxyl (−COOH, pKa < 2) and sulfate (−SO3H, pKa ∼3–4) groups deprotonate at physiological pH (8), generating negative charges, while neutral polysaccharides with hydroxyls (−OH, pKa ∼ 12–14) remain unchanged. As a result, polysaccharides contribute both to hydrophilicity and negative surface charge under our experimental conditions. Then, although lipids represent a minor fraction of the cell wall, they play a critical role in facilitating hydrophobic interactions. Their long hydrocarbon chains limit water accessibility and reduce surface wettability; their presence at the surface of cells can thus explain stronger interactions with hydrophobic probes. Furthermore, lipids can move within the cell wall, as suggested by the SRS results, potentially leading to the formation of microdomains, as it was reported in bacteria and plants, which may serve various functional roles (Barák and Muchová, 2013; Dauphin et al., 2022). Finally, proteins, which are predominantly glycoproteins, are extensively glycosylated, conferring hydrophilicity and limiting their direct involvement in hydrophobic interactions (Mathieu-Rivet et al., 2020). However, some of these proteins may carry positive charges, contributing to electrostatic interactions with the negatively-charged tips used in this study.

The hypothesis that we formulate, based on our results, is that, depending on the immobilization surface, these different components can rearrange their conformation and organization at the nanometric (nm) scale or change their conformations, conferring variable surface properties, i.e., different cell surface structure (roughness) and rigidity (Young's modulus), as well as variable physico-chemical properties. This hypothesis is illustrated in Fig. 6, where three representative cells are shown, each subjected to a different immobilization method: (A) PO-Chitosan, (B) Superfrost™, and (C) PDMS. The schematic focuses on the cell dome, the area probed by the AFM tip, while acknowledging that the base (in contact with the support) may indirectly influence surface presentation through biochemical or mechanical signaling. Cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan may present a dome with more in positively charged residues brought by glycoproteins and hydrophobic lipid microdomains when compared with the other immobilizations, together with a dense network of folded polysaccharides. These features could help explain the higher adhesion forces observed across all AFM probes and in part the increased Young's modulus values. Protrusion of lipid domains may also explain the increased roughness values recorded in this case, as it was mentioned before. One possible interpretation is that the hydrophobic nature of the PO-Chitosan surface could favor the local concentration or stabilization of surface components at the dome, perhaps through hydrophobic partitioning of lipids and structural proteins. In this context, lipid microdomains may further strengthen hydrophobic interactions and enhance local mechanical rigidity (Barák and Muchová, 2013; Dauphin et al., 2022). On Superfrost™ slides, cells appear to exhibit lower hydrophobicity and an absence of positively charged components at the dome, possibly redistributed elsewhere. The strong positive charges of the substrate may also drive polysaccharides' unfolding, increasing their contact area. Such reorganization could account for the reduced roughness and lower Young's modulus, together with the increase of the proportion of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 4) which could drive a fluidification of the cell wall. This would be consistent with a less compact or organized dome, where polysaccharides may shield or mask lipidic microdomains. Finally, PDMS-immobilized cells showed the lowest adhesion forces across all AFM probes. PDMS, which was expected to function primarily as a physical confinement immobilization method due to its low surface energy, hydrophilic properties, and the negative charges introduced by plasma treatment, appeared to drive a redistribution of cell wall components. Positively charged proteins are perhaps in this case directed toward the cell–surface interface, a rearrangement driven by electrostatic interactions, while hydrophilic polysaccharides could also be oriented toward the hydrophilic plasma-treated PDMS surface and walls. The lack of hydrophobic interactions with the substrate, perhaps in this case, did not concentrate lipids in particular regions, leading also to a hydrophilic character.

In summary, our results suggest that the immobilization surface not only holds the cells in place but also actively influences the distribution and presentation of cell wall components. Depending on the physico-chemical properties of the substrate, polysaccharides, lipids, and proteins appear to reorganize, leading to distinct adhesion signatures, mechanical properties, and surface architectures. This highlights the dynamic nature of the microalgal cell wall and emphasizes that immobilization strategies can shape the apparent properties of cells, an important consideration when interpreting AFM-based measurements of microalgae. Recent AFM studies have also investigated microalgal surface properties using functionalized tips, including the work of Lesniewska and co-workers (Lesniewska et al., 2024), where C. vulgaris cells were immobilized on positively-charged polyethyleneimine (PEI) coated glass slides. It is interesting to compare our data to this study in particular because in force spectroscopy experiments performed with CH3-terminated tips, the authors could observe circular nanoscale hydrophobic domains at the apical regions of the cell surface, features that were absent in our experiments. Following our hypothesis, a plausible explanation could be that these circular domains arise from structural remodeling of the cell wall induced by PEI. Branched PEI carries a high density of amine groups, which remain positively charged across physiological pH ranges (Vasilieva et al., 2018). When in contact with the negatively charged polysaccharides and proteins of the C. vulgaris cell wall, strong electrostatic interactions efficiently immobilize the cells but may also generate local mechanical stresses, causing a reorganization of the outer cell wall architecture. An alternative explanation could be that the PEI-induced electrostatic environment promotes lateral redistribution of lipids and cell wall-associated proteins, causing phase separation or clustering at the apical cell regions. In either case, this comparison underscores that using a different immobilization strategy revealed an alternative hydrophobic behavior at the surface of C. vulgaris cells, further supporting our hypothesis that the immobilization surface influences cell wall organization.

Now, it remains to understand how the localized stress at the contact between the cell and the immobilization surface propagates along the cell wall to induce such changes. Future studies could explore other immobilization surfaces, such as Cell-Tak™, Vectabond®, or poly-l-lysine, to better understand these effects. Moreover, while our study focused on Chlorella as a model organism, it would be highly valuable to assess whether similar surface-induced remodeling occurs in other microorganisms, such as bacteria or fungi. Although confirming this experimentally is beyond the scope of the present work, this perspective highlights the broader relevance of our findings and points to an important avenue for future research.

Conclusions

In this work, different surfaces were used to immobilize C. vulgaris cells to investigate their impact on their cell wall biophysical properties. In a first step, we could show that the three surfaces chosen promoted immobilization of the cells through different mechanisms, i.e., hydrophobic interactions for PO-Chitosan glass slides, electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions for Superfrost™ surfaces, and mechanical confinement for PDMS chambers. AFM imaging, nanoindentation, and force spectroscopy experiments further showed that the cell wall of cells had different biophysical properties depending on the immobilization strategy; cells immobilized on PO-Chitosan through hydrophobic interactions showed increased cell surface roughness, increased rigidity, hydrophobic properties, and the presence of positive charges. Cells immobilized on a less hydrophobic surface, but positively-charged (Superfrost™), had, in contrast, a smoother surface, a less rigid cell wall, decreased hydrophobic properties, and exhibited no positive charges on the surface. Finally, cells immobilized only through mechanical confinement in PDMS chambers were very smooth, had a rather soft cell wall that was hydrophilic, and presented no positive charges as well. Complementary SRS microscopy experiments further supported these findings by revealing different lipidic rearrangements in the cell wall depending on the immobilization surface. Based on these results, we could make the hypothesis that these changes in the biophysical properties of the C. vulgaris cell wall could be caused by the reorganization of the different cell wall components that are lipids, proteins, and polysaccharides, all conferring either charged or hydrophobic/hydrophilic properties to the cell surface. Thus, together, AFM and SRS provide complementary evidence that immobilization is not a passive preparative step but an active factor shaping the modeling of cell wall components at the nanoscale in C. vulgaris, highlighting the importance of this required step in AFM experiments. Therefore, careful selection and validation of the immobilization strategy is critical to ensure accurate and comparable analyses in microalgal research. Future studies should aim to expand the spectrum of immobilization surfaces to disentangle the effects of mechanical confinement from biochemical interactions on C. vulgaris cell wall architecture. Incorporating time-resolved AFM and SRS measurements would allow monitoring of dynamic structural changes during immobilization, while combining mechanical mapping with high-resolution compositional analyses could clarify the relationships between stiffness, adhesion, and molecular organization. Comparative assessments across different microalgal strains may uncover species-specific responses, and the development of standardized immobilization protocols will be crucial for ensuring reproducibility and facilitating meaningful cross-study comparisons.
