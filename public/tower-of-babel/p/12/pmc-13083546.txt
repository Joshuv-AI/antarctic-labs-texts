Mind the Litter: The Critical Role of Intralitter Variables in Reproductive and Developmental Toxicology Research
Authors: Lauren T. L. Brown, Megan E. Cull, Delaine Pereira, Perri M. Grant, Louise M. Winn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnimal models remain essential for understanding developmental toxicology, providing insights into how in utero exposures affect fetal and placental outcomes. Litter‐bearing species are widely used due to their efficiency and reproducibility, but conventional approaches often summarize outcomes at the litter level, masking meaningful within‐litter variability.AimThis review highlights three primary sources of intralitter variability: litter size, uterine implantation location, and fetal sex, and their influence on fetal and placental growth, mortality, and malformations.DiscussionLarger litters restrict fetal growth through intrauterine competition, while toxicant‐induced changes in litter size can obscure or exaggerate effects. Implantation location within uterine horns influences perfusion, nutrient delivery, and local exposure to hormones or toxicants, introducing spatially dependent vulnerability. Fetal sex further modifies responses, as male and female fetuses and placentas differ in growth trajectories, gene expression, and adaptive capacity, leading to sex‐specific susceptibility to toxicants.ConclusionIgnoring these sources of variability risks overlooking subtle effects and vulnerable subpopulations, misinterpreting outcomes, and reducing the translational value of animal studies. We argue that incorporating intralitter variables into experimental design and statistical analyses enhances the accuracy, reproducibility, and interpretability of developmental toxicology research. By refining the use of animal models in line with the 3Rs framework, researchers can maximize the information gained per pregnancy and improve risk assessment for human maternal‐fetal health.

Introduction

The use of animals is essential for advancing scientific knowledge, particularly in studies related to human health and disease. Animal models are particularly vital for developmental toxicology research, where they provide insights into how in utero exposures to drugs, chemicals, and environmental factors can affect maternal and fetal health. These models enable researchers to identify harmful agents, understand mechanisms of toxicity, pinpoint windows of susceptibility, and assess risks such as birth defects, growth abnormalities, and long‐term health effects.

Animal pregnancy models remain indispensable for investigating multisystem maternal‐placental‐fetal interactions that cannot yet be reproduced in vitro or in silico. Direct research in pregnant humans is largely limited to pharmacoepidemiological studies, which can reveal associations between exposures and adverse outcomes but cannot establish mechanisms or precisely control confounding variables such as nutrition, substance or medication use, socioeconomic status, or pre‐existing health conditions (Wood et al. 2018). Furthermore, access to embryonic and extraembryonic tissues is highly restricted, and current in vitro models cannot replicate the complex interactions between the mother, placenta, and fetus. Despite these scientific needs, ethical concerns about animal welfare, particularly pain and distress, have driven strict regulations and adoption of the 3Rs framework (replacement, reduction, and refinement), which is applied in research that uses animals (Chapman et al. 2013). These measures improve animal welfare, but no alternative yet replicates the complexity of whole‐organism interactions, underscoring the continued necessity of animal models in biomedical science.

Beyond replicating maternal‐placental‐fetal interactions, animal pregnancy models offer practical advantages for developmental toxicology research. They enable precise control over exposure conditions, including dose, timing, and route of exposure, while avoiding many confounding factors present in human studies. Non‐human pregnancy models can be broadly classified as singleton or litter‐bearing. Species that typically carry singleton pregnancies, such as non‐human primates, provide gestational lengths and placental structures that closely resemble humans, offering strong translational relevance (Carter 2007). However, their long gestational periods, high maintenance costs, and production of a single fetus per pregnancy make studies slow and resource‐intensive (Carter 2007; Carter 2020). In contrast, litter‐bearing species, such as rodents, rabbits, and pigs, have shorter gestation periods and produce multiple offspring per pregnancy, resulting in higher experimental efficiency and larger sample sizes from fewer pregnancies (Carter 2007; Carter 2020). These features make litter‐bearing species the most widely used models in developmental toxicology studies.

While there are benefits to using litter‐bearing species, phenotypic differences can be observed between littermates, known as intralitter. The importance of intra‐litter variability can be traced back to the early 20th century, when the laboratory rat was first standardized at the Wistar Institute (Donaldson 1924). Foundational work by Henry Herbert Donaldson and Helen Dean King involved large‐scale characterization of growth, reproduction, and physiology in the newly established Wistar rat. Although not explicitly framed in modern statistical terms, these early studies consistently documented variation both within and between litters, particularly in relation to litter size, maternal environment, and developmental outcomes. This body of work laid the conceptual groundwork for recognizing the litter as a biologically meaningful unit of analysis.

As such, developmental toxicology studies have historically summarized fetal and placental outcomes at the litter level rather than at the individual level. Fetal and placental weights are typically reported as litter means, while fetal abnormalities are often reported as the litter mean percent, calculated as a proportion of affected fetuses per litter. These practices are rooted in the recognition that pups within a litter are not statistically independent, as they share the same maternal environment and genetic background, making the litter the unit of measure. This litter‐based approach emerged in the 1970s due to limited computing power and uncertainty about how to model the hierarchical structure of fetuses within litters (Golub and Sobin 2020; Haseman and Hogan 1975; Staples and Haseman 1974). In current practice, academic studies often calculate a litter mean and then select multiple random fetuses for further mechanistic analysis, whereas industry studies typically examine all fetuses but still reduce outcomes to litter‐level summaries, with the primary goal of identifying clear safety signals rather than exploring mechanistic variability (Agency 1991; FDA 2000). Because the litter is the experimental unit, regulatory developmental and reproductive toxicity testing strategies are also designed to evaluate sufficient numbers of litters to ensure reliable hazard assessment. For example, segmented study designs evaluate exposures across distinct developmental windows, thereby increasing the number of litters assessed and mitigating concerns about small litter sample sizes (often referred to as the “issue of ten”). While practical, both approaches mask meaningful within‐litter differences in response to toxicants.

Intralitter variability can arise from factors such as litter size, uterine location, and sex. Because trophoblast cells of the placenta originate from the embryo, the placenta reflects the genetic sex of the fetus (XX for females and XY for males). This means placental biochemistry, signaling, and function can differ by fetal sex. Moreover, fetuses within the same litter can differ by up to one gestational day (GD) in their development, which can significantly influence their response to toxicant exposure (Elmore et al. 2022). When outcomes are reduced to a single litter mean, these biological differences are averaged away, increasing the risk of overlooking subtle but important effects.

The primary aim of this review is to highlight the influence of intralitter variability on key measurements commonly used in developmental toxicology studies. By collecting evidence from the literature, we provide examples demonstrating how factors like litter size, uterine location, and fetal sex can obscure, exaggerate, or otherwise modify the observed effects of toxicant exposure. Our goal is to encourage researchers to incorporate and consider intralitter variables into experimental design and statistical analyses, improving the accuracy and reproducibility of developmental toxicology studies. Accounting for these variables not only enhances the translational value of animal models but also supports refinement within the 3Rs framework, maximizing the information gained from each animal and ultimately improving risk assessment for the vulnerable population of pregnant persons and their unborn children.

Common Measurements in Developmental Toxicology Studies: Fetal and Placental Outcomes

Understanding how intralitter variability affects common measurements in developmental toxicology is essential for accurately interpreting the impact of toxicants on fetal and placental development. In this review, we will focus on fetal weight, mortality, and malformations, as well as placental weight and morphology, and how these outcomes can be influenced by differences among littermates.

Fetal weight is a widely used indicator of fetal growth and developmental health. Deviations from normal growth can reflect disruptions in physiological processes and are associated with adverse health outcomes later in life (Desai and Hales 1997). Fetal mortality is commonly assessed through resorptions, which occur when a developing fetus is absorbed back into the uterine tissue, often following the loss of its fluid component (England 1998). Resorptions can result from maternal stress, infection, nutritional deficiencies, or toxicant exposure (Gendron et al. 1990; Johnston and Raksil 1987; Verstegen et al. 2008; White et al. 1998), and are widely used as indicators of embryonic lethality and early developmental disturbances. Fetal malformations, or structural abnormalities that arise during development, may result in physical or functional impairments. In the context of toxicant exposure, malformations are particularly informative because they can reveal both teratogenic potential and critical windows of susceptibility (Toufaily et al. 2018).

The placenta is central to fetal development, supporting nutrient and oxygen transfer, waste elimination, and endocrine regulation (Burton and Fowden 2015). Placental weight is commonly evaluated as an individual metric, but it is also interpreted relative to fetal weight as placental efficiency, typically expressed as the fetal‐to‐placental weight ratio and used as a common proxy for placental functional capacity (Hayward et al. 2016). Considering placental weight relative to fetal weight is therefore important for assessing whether placental growth appropriately supports fetal development. Placental efficiency reflects how effectively the placenta transfers nutrients to the fetus: a small placenta supporting a large fetus is considered efficient, whereas a large placenta supporting a small fetus may suggest placental insufficiency. While high placental efficiency is often interpreted as beneficial (Fowden et al. 2006), it may also represent compensatory adaptations to placental stress following toxicant exposure (Fowden et al. 2009). In addition to these growth metrics, placental morphology provides insight into structural or functional impairments caused by toxicant exposure. Alterations in placental structure can compromise its functional ability to sustain normal fetal growth and development throughout gestation.

Across these outcomes, intralitter variability, driven by factors such as litter size, uterine location, fetal sex, and developmental timing, can influence how individual fetuses respond to toxicant exposure, which is the topic of this review and will be discussed in more detail below. Accounting for this variability ensures that measurements of fetal weight, mortality, and malformations, as well as placental weight and morphology, accurately reflect the true impact of toxicants, strengthening the biological relevance, translatability, and reliability of developmental toxicology studies.

Influence of Litter Size

Litter size, defined as the total number of implantation sites (including resorptions, live, and dead fetuses) and live litter size (all living fetuses at birth or dissection), is an important factor influencing both fetal and placental development, and a critical variable in developmental toxicology studies.

Larger litters increase intrauterine competition for maternal resources, such as nutrients, uterine space, and oxygen, naturally limiting fetal growth, while smaller litters provide more favorable conditions. Across species, a consistent inverse relationship exists between litter size and fetal weight. In rodents, Tanaka (2004) demonstrated that increasing litter size reduces fetal weight in mice, and Wurtman and Miller (1976) observed similar significant effects in male rats from 16‐pup litters compared to litters of 2, 4, 8, or 12. Epstein (1978) confirmed this trend by comparing mouse litters of 2, 4, 6, and 12 pups, noting that fetal weight decreased as litter size increased, except in disproportionately small 2‐pup litters. Romero et al. (1992) quantified this effect in Sprague–Dawley rats, reporting a strong negative correlation (r = −0.677, p = 0.002). This pattern extends to goats (Mellado et al. 2011) and sheep (Gootwine et al. 2007), where larger litters also result in lower birth weights due to increased intrauterine competition. Intrauterine competition is also reflected in uterine blood flow per fetus, which decreases as litter size increases. Père and Etienne (2000) reported a decline ranging from 0.72 L/min/fetus in small litters (2–3 fetuses) to 0.43 L/min/fetus in large litters (6–8 fetuses) in sows, directly correlating with reduced fetal weight. This finding suggests that as litter size increases, individual fetuses receive less uterine blood flow, limiting nutrient and oxygen supply and contributing to growth restriction. Together, these findings show that larger litters naturally limit the resources available to each fetus, resulting in lower fetal weights under normal conditions.

Placental weight and function also mediate the effect of litter size on fetal development. Placental weight generally decreases as litter size increases (Gootwine et al. 2007; Norman and Bruce 1979), but weight alone does not indicate function. Instead, placental efficiency, defined as the fetal weight‐to‐placental weight ratio, provides a more meaningful measure of how effectively the placenta supports growth (Fowden et al. 2009, 2006; Wilson and Ford 2001). A small placenta can sustain normal development if it efficiently transfers nutrients and oxygen to meet fetal demands (Figure 1A). However, when placental capacity fails to keep pace with fetal requirements, placental insufficiency occurs, characterized by inadequate nutrient and oxygen delivery that results in growth restriction or even fetal loss (Figure 1B; He et al. 2007). Gestational timing also influences this dynamic. In mice, placental growth primarily regulates fetal weight in mid‐gestation, while litter size becomes the dominant factor in late gestation (Ishikawa et al. 2006). These relationships underscore that litter size, placental development, and gestational timing together shape fetal growth trajectories and must be considered when interpreting toxicological outcomes.

Toxicant exposure can modify these natural dynamics in two distinct ways. First, litter‐size reductions redistribute maternal resources to surviving fetuses, which can mask underlying developmental toxicity. Cavieres et al. (2002) exposed pregnant mice to a herbicide mixture containing 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid, mecoprop, dicamba, and other inert ingredients during pre‐implantation and organogenesis (GD 0–15) or organogenesis alone (GD 6–15). All doses reduced the number of live‐born pups by approximately 20%, primarily due to fewer implantation sites, suggesting impaired blastocyst development or implantation. Pups from smaller litters weighed more and had longer crown‐rump lengths than pups from larger litters, creating the impression of enhanced growth. Similarly, Brown et al. (2025) reported that a single exposure to 600 mg/kg valproic acid on GD 9 increased post‐implantation losses (resorptions and fetal deaths), reducing the number of live fetuses per litter. At GD 18, non‐malformed surviving fetuses weighed the same as saline controls. Together, these findings demonstrate that toxicant‐induced litter‐size reduction can make surviving fetuses appear normal, or even healthier, at birth or dissection, obscuring developmental toxicity. Importantly, both example studies ended exposure before the fetal growth spurt (which occurs after GD 15), a period when litter size exerts a stronger influence on fetal growth (Ishikawa et al. 2006), highlighting that exposure timing further complicates growth interpretation.

Second, some toxicants directly impair fetal growth without reducing litter size, allowing weight changes to be more confidently attributed to the toxicant rather than to shifts in intrauterine competition. Loch‐Caruso et al. (2019) exposed pregnant Wistar rats to trichloroethylene (an organic solvent) from GD 6–16 and observed a 10% reduction in fetal body weight on GD 16 with no changes in litter size. Similarly, Huang et al. (2022) exposed pregnant Kunming mice to polystyrene nanoplastics (doses ranging from 0.1 to 10 mg/L) through drinking water from GD 0 (plug identification) to postnatal day 21. They observed that exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics reduced offspring body weight at birth by 12%–15% across doses, while litter size remained unaffected. Brown et al. (2025) found that 400 mg/kg of valproic acid on GD 9 increased fetal weight at GD 18 without altering litter size. Shafique and Winn (2021) reported that after exposure to 400 mg/kg valproic acid on GD 9, fetal weights were significantly lower than saline controls on GD 13, 15, and 17, yet by GD 19 the difference had resolved, again without any significant differences in litter size at any of the timepoints. Together, these findings demonstrate that when litter size remains unchanged, observed changes in fetal growth can be more directly attributed to toxicant exposure, rather than to shifts in resource competition. Moreover, the benzene and valproic acid findings highlight that growth effects may be dynamic, with fetuses capable of compensatory responses over gestation.

The interpretation of fetal growth outcomes becomes even more complex when a toxicant induces malformations. Malformed fetuses typically weigh less than their non‐malformed littermates (Ryan et al. 1991), and including them in a single litter average can artificially skew the mean downward, masking normal growth in unaffected fetuses. Brown et al. (2025) demonstrated this with valproic acid: non‐malformed fetuses exposed to 400 mg/kg weighed more than controls, and the 600 mg/kg exposed group showed similar non‐malformed weights to controls, whereas malformed fetuses weighed significantly less. If only average fetal weight per litter were reported, the higher proportion of malformations in the 600 mg/kg group would create the appearance of uniform growth restriction, obscuring the true pattern of toxicity.

Together, these findings demonstrate that litter size profoundly shapes fetal growth under normal and toxicant‐exposed conditions, making it a critical variable to consider in developmental toxicology studies. If not properly considered, litter size effects, particularly when malformations are present, can mask true toxicant‐induced growth changes or exaggerate growth restriction. More accurate assessments require statistical approaches that account for litter size, separate malformed and non‐malformed fetuses, and attention to exposure timing. Incorporating these factors strengthens the biological relevance of study findings and improves their value for risk assessment and translation to human health.

Uterine Implantation Location

Litter‐bearing animal species possess two uterine horns (left and right) to allow for multiple implantation sites along the length of the uterus. Depending on the species, this anatomy can be classified as either bicornuate or duplex. In a bicornuate uterus, the two uterine horns extend from the ovaries and merge into a single cervix. In contrast, a duplex uterus consists of two largely separate uterine horns that connect to two distinct cervices. Pigs and sheep have a bicornuate uterus (Abd‐Elkareem et al. 2025; Lorenzen et al. 2015) and rabbits have a duplex uterus (Fischer et al. 2012). However, there is an ongoing debate in the literature regarding whether the uterus of mice and rats should be described as bicornuate or duplex (Elvis‐Offiah et al. 2022; Bertram et al. 2019; Boyd et al. 2018). A detailed analysis by Bertram et al. (2019) demonstrated that both species possess two distinct cervical canals, supporting classification of their reproductive tract as a duplex uterus rather than a bicornuate uterus. Regardless of terminology, the presence of two uterine horns introduces another layer of biological variability in litter‐bearing species. The specific site of implantation introduces another layer of variability, shaped by the number of implantations in a horn (and thus the space available for growth), the position along the horn's length, and the sex and viability of adjacent fetuses that may influence development. Implantation location can, in turn, affect fetal and placental growth and development, though these effects appear specific due to anatomical differences in uterine structure.

In this section, we discuss how implantation location contributes to intralitter variability. We define uterine implantation location in three ways: interuterine horn position (IeUP), the horn (right or left) containing the implantation (Figure 2A); intrauterine horn position (IaUP), placement along the horn relative to the ovary and cervix (Figure 2B); and relative intrauterine horn position (R‐IaUP), the influence of adjacent implantations based on their sex and viability (Figure 2C). These positional factors, shaped by maternal physiology, uterine vascular anatomy, and the local intrauterine environment, can alter blood flow, nutrient delivery, hormonal signaling, and toxicant exposure, ultimately affecting fetal outcomes.

(B) Intra‐uterine position (IaUP) refers to the position of a fetus along a single uterine horn, categorized as ovarian end (OE), middle (M), or cervical end (CE). (C) Relative intra‐uterine position (R‐IaUP) describes the position of a fetus relative to its immediate neighboring fetuses, with potential influences from hormones, resource competition, and immune signaling.

Interuterine Horn Position (IeUP)

IeUP refers to whether an implantation is in the right or left uterine horn of a bicornate uterus. Although often assumed to be functionally equivalent, accumulating evidence suggests that horn‐specific physiological differences can influence fetal and placental development and outcomes. These asymmetries can introduce baseline variability and, under toxicant exposure, may interact with exposures in complex, horn‐specific manners, complicating interpretation in developmental toxicology studies.

Even under normal conditions, uterine horns differ in size and blood supply. Measures such as uterine horn size (the number of implantations along a horn) and live uterine horn size (the number of live fetuses within a horn) are rarely symmetrical between the two horns of the same uterus, introducing natural variability. In Swiss‐Webster mice, Wiebold and Becker (1987) reported that the mean ovulation rate (right: 5.8, left: 5.4) and number of fetuses (right: 4.9, left: 4.4) were significantly higher in the right horn, though mean fetal weight per horn did not significantly differ. Similarly, Falconer et al. (1961) examined ovulation across multiple mouse strains and found that the right ovary yielded 51%–65% of all eggs or corpora lutea; however, this difference was not statistically significant, and the authors emphasized that equal contribution from each ovary should be assumed. Barr et al. (1970) found that, in litters from 103 Wistar rats, there were significantly more fetuses on the right horn than on the left, and as horn size increased, mean fetal weight decreased. These studies highlight that the number of fetuses is often unequal between uterine horns, raising important questions about how differences in horn size and blood supply may influence fetal growth and development.

Like overall litter size, uterine horn size has also been shown to negatively correlate with fetal weight (Barr et al. 1970; McLaurin and Mactutus 2015). Although no studies have directly tested this, it is plausible that if the uterine blood flow to each conceptus decreases with increasing litter size (Père and Etienne 2000), the same may hold true within each horn. Thus, blood supply could vary between uterine horns depending on their size, with implications for toxicant exposure and fetal susceptibility. Anatomical differences in vascular supply between the right and left horns have been reported. In rodents, each horn is supplied by a single main loop artery running in parallel (Even et al. 1994); however, the sources of these arteries are variable between horns. For example, Even et al. (1994) observed that the artery feeding the ovarian end of the right uterine horn arose from the descending aorta, whereas the artery feeding the ovarian end of the left uterine horn arose from the renal artery in Sprague–Dawley rats. This variability in source may contribute to differences in blood supply to each uterine horn. Similarly, Vom Saal and Dhar (1992) found that in CF‐1 mice, the left uterine horn was supplied by the renal artery in 39 of 80 cases (49%), whereas the right horn was supplied by the descending aorta in 78 of 80 cases (98%). Notably, in approximately half of the pregnancies, the left uterine horn was supplied by the renal artery, indicating that the blood supply source to the left uterine horn is more variable compared to the right, in which almost all cases were supplied from the same source.

It has been suggested that the right uterine horn may provide a more favorable in utero environment, as there are reports of higher ovulation rates from the right ovary (Falconer et al. 1961; Wiebold and Becker 1987) and a more consistent blood supply origin (Even et al. 1994; Vom Saal and Dhar 1992), with a greater number of fetuses occupying the right uterine horn (Wiebold and Becker 1987). Supporting this idea, Even et al. (1994) also observed that uterine blood flow to the right uterine horn exceeded that of the left, regardless of the number of implantations within each horn. However, more evidence is needed to support this right horn superiority hypothesis. Such consistent asymmetries in horn structure and perfusion suggest fetuses developing in different uterine horns may experience systematically different growth conditions and exposures (as depicted in Figure 3), with important implications for studies of developmental toxicity.

Several toxicology studies provide direct evidence that uterine horn asymmetry can shape fetal susceptibility to toxicants. In Sprague–Dawley rats, D'Errico et al. (2021) reported that both single (on GD 17) and repeated nano–titanium dioxide inhalation significantly decreased fetal weight in the left uterine horn. However, in the same study, a single exposure on GD 4 produced the opposite pattern, with fetuses in the right horn being significantly smaller than those in the left (D'Errico et al. 2021). It is important to note that this study did not report uterine horn sizes, making it unclear whether structural asymmetry contributed to these horn‐dependent responses, which could explain these contrasting results. Notably, these exposure‐related differences were limited to fetal weight; no significant horn‐specific effects were observed for placental weight or placental efficiency.

Horn‐specific effects were also evident in benzene‐exposed CD‐1 mice. Cull et al. (2025) found that at GD 19, fetal weight was significantly increased in the left horn of benzene‐exposed litters compared with both left‐horn controls and their benzene‐exposed right‐horn counterparts. This left‐horn–specific increase was mirrored across multiple metrics, including fetal heart weight, head–rump length, abdominal circumference, placental weight, placental diameter, and placental efficiency. Similarly, Brown et al. (2025) demonstrated that uterine horn asymmetry influenced susceptibility to valproic acid. In the 600 mg/kg valproic acid group, fetal and placental weights were significantly lower in the left horn compared with the right. In contrast, among controls, the left horn exhibited significantly higher fetal and placental weights than the right horn. Interestingly, placental efficiency in controls was higher in the right horn, whereas no horn‐specific differences in placental efficiency were observed in the 600 mg/kg valproic acid group. These studies reinforce that uterine horn asymmetry can meaningfully alter fetal responses to toxicant exposure. However, because all studies normalized fetal weights to maternal and litter‐level factors (Brown et al. 2025; Cull et al. 2025; D'Errico et al. 2021), the values represent adjusted rather than raw measures and should be interpreted within that context.

Intrauterine Horn Position (IaUP)

IaUP refers to the absolute position of a fetus along a uterine horn, typically classified as ovarian end, middle, or cervical end positions. These positions are defined relative to fixed anatomical landmarks (ovary and cervix) and shaped by the uterine vasculature, which determines how maternal blood enters each horn. More than half a century ago, Eckstein and McKeown (1955) proposed a hemodynamic theory to explain natural variations in fetal weight across these positions. Their theory posits that fetal growth is strongly influenced by the pressure at which maternal blood reaches each placenta via the uterine arteries and that this pressure progressively declines as more fetuses (each acting as an arteriovenous shunt) are interposed between the blood supply and downstream implantation sites. Because the uterine vasculature varies across mammalian species, the magnitude and even the direction of these IaUP‐associated growth patterns differ accordingly.

In mice, uterine blood flow is bidirectional, entering from both the ovarian and cervical ends and converging toward the middle of the uterine horn (Vom Saal and Dhar 1992). Under normal conditions, fetuses located near the ovarian end of the horn are heavier at birth than those in the middle (Louton et al. 1988; McLaren 1965). This pattern aligns with the hemodynamic theory: fetal‐placental units closest to arterial entry points (ovary and cervix) experience the highest perfusion pressure, whereas conceptuses in the center receive blood last, after it has passed multiple upstream placentas.

Although pigs have a uterine vascular arrangement that differs markedly from mice, they display a similar positional pattern. Multiple studies have reported that pig fetuses grow larger in the ovarian and cervical regions than in the middle of the horn, with the ovarian end supporting the greatest fetal growth (Jang et al. 2014; Perry and Rowell 1969; Rohde Parfet et al. 1990; Wise et al. 1997). Perry and Rowell (1969) also noted that this effect was most prominent in uterine horns containing more than five fetuses, aligning with the hemodynamic theory in that fetal weight decreases as the number of fetuses (and therefore the number of arteriovenous shunts) between the blood supply and mid‐horn region increases.

Comparable position effects have also been observed in rabbits. Argente et al. (2003) found that the lightest live fetuses were located in the middle of the uterine horn, approximately 7% lighter than those nearest the ovary and cervix, and that the heaviest fetuses and placentas were located near the ovary. This is likely due to greater uterine space per fetus and greater blood flow in this region. Several other studies have reported similar findings (Bautista et al. 2015; Duncan 1969; Poigner et al. 2000; Rosahn and Greene 1936; Stuckhardt et al. 1981).

In contrast, rats exhibit the opposite pattern from mice, pigs, and rabbits. Rats display an inverted U‐shaped relationship, with the heaviest fetuses occupying middle horn positions and the lightest fetuses located at the ovarian and cervical ends (Barr and Brent 1970; Barr et al. 1969; Barr et al. 1970; Cornwall et al. 1984; McLaurin and Mactutus 2015; Norman and Bruce 1979). However, this pattern does not align with predictions of the hemodynamic theory based on the available evidence regarding uterine blood flow in rats. Even et al. (1994) found that fetuses in the right uterine horn were largest at the ovarian and cervical ends and smallest in the middle, supporting the hypothesis that blood flows into the right uterine horn from both the ovarian and cervical ends, with the lowest flow rate being in the middle of the horn, consistent with mice (Vom Saal and Dhar 1992). In the left uterine horn, however, placental blood flow and fetal body mass were highest at the cervical end and lowest at the ovarian end (Even et al. 1994). This pattern is consistent with findings from Lipton et al. (2002), who reported a gradient of decreasing blood flow toward the ovarian ends of the uterus in pregnant rats. Meisel and Ward (1981) also found that blood in the uterine loop artery flows unidirectionally from the cervical end toward the ovarian end of the uterus. Importantly, Gorodeski et al. (1995) found that, during early pregnancy, blood flow was bidirectional, entering the parametrial artery from both the ovarian and cervical ends, but as pregnancy progressed, flow increasingly favored the cervical‐to‐ovarian direction. They demonstrated this via faster flow velocities toward the ovary, a collision point that shifted toward the ovarian end of the artery, widening of the cervical portion of the vessel that facilitated greater flow in this direction, and hormone gradients that aligned with cervical‐to‐ovarian perfusion (Gorodeski et al. 1995). Taken together, there is strong evidence to suggest that uterine arterial supply in the pregnant rat predominantly moves from the cervical end toward the ovarian end of the uterine horn. However, based on the hemodynamic theory from Eckstein and McKeown (1955), the largest fetuses should occupy cervical‐end positions, which does not align with previous literature reporting the largest fetuses in the middle positions.

Preferential blood flow to specific IaUPs has implications for fetal susceptibility to toxicant exposure. Under normal conditions, fetuses at high‐perfusion positions may experience a growth advantage; however, the same hemodynamic pattern could also expose them to higher concentrations of circulating toxicants. If maternal blood carrying a toxicant preferentially perfuses certain regions of the uterine horn, fetuses at these positions may be disproportionately affected, experiencing higher rates of growth restriction, malformation, or death than their littermates.

Several studies support this hypothesis. Woollam and Millen (1962) found that cervical‐end fetuses in mice were more prone to death and deformity under anoxic conditions. Eluma et al. (1984) reported that CD‐1 mouse fetuses at the cervical ends had higher frequencies of malformations following in utero exposure to carbamazepine, valproic acid, and diphenylhydantoin. Brown et al. (2025) noted the highest incidence of neural tube defects at the right cervical end with 600 mg/kg valproic acid exposure, though this was not significantly different from other positions. Conversely, Trasler (1960) reported more cleft palate at the ovarian ends in A/Jax mice, and Beck (1967) found higher mortality in ovarian‐end positions after trypan blue exposure across multiple mouse strains. Overall, mouse studies support the premise that IaUP can influence malformation risk in a toxicant exposure model, although the specific position of greatest susceptibility varies with strain and toxicant.

Evidence for IaUP influencing susceptibility to toxicant‐induced growth restriction is mixed. Cornwall et al. (1984) administered diethylstilbestrol, zeranol, 3,4,3′,4′‐tetrachlorobiphenyl, and cadmium to pregnant Sprague–Dawley rats from GD 6–18. In control litters, ovarian‐end fetuses were significantly lighter than those in the middle (Cornwall et al. 1984), consistent with some rat IaUP studies. However, chemical exposure uniformly decreased fetal weight across all positions, indicating that mid‐horn fetuses were not disproportionately affected.

A more direct way to test IaUP‐dependent susceptibility is to measure fetal tissue concentrations. Lipton et al. (1998) administered cocaine to pregnant Sprague–Dawley rats and found that fetal brain cocaine and dopamine levels varied by IaUP, with ovarian‐end fetuses exhibiting lower concentrations than fetuses closer to the cervix. In contrast, Withey et al. (1992) observed higher [14C]‐labeled pyrene and metabolite concentrations in fetuses on the right side of the uterus compared to the left, but no consistent relationship between IaUP and fetal concentrations within each horn. A subsequent study similarly found no IaUP‐specific differences in benzo[a]pyrene distribution (Withey et al. 1993). Research in mice has also reported no IaUP‐associated differences in fetal weight, placental weight, or placental efficiency following benzene, titanium dioxide, and valproic acid exposure (Brown et al. 2025; Cull et al. 2025; D'Errico et al. 2021).

These findings suggest that IaUP may influence vulnerability to toxicant‐induced malformations or differential fetal exposure, but the evidence is inconsistent and depends heavily on both species and toxicant. To clarify whether IaUP meaningfully modifies fetal exposure, additional studies are needed that directly quantify toxicant concentrations in fetuses occupying specific positions along the horn and compare whether exposure is evenly or unevenly distributed across conceptuses within a litter.

Relative Intrauterine Position (R‐IaUP)

R‐IaUP introduces additional complexity into analyses of intralitter variability by accounting for factors determined by the characteristics of adjacent fetuses. Unlike IaUP, which considers the fetus' absolute location relative to anatomical landmarks (e.g., ovary and cervix), R‐IaUP captures the local uterine microenvironment created by neighboring littermates. This neighbor‐dependent microenvironment can substantially influence fetal development. This section focuses on two distinct R‐IaUP effects that reflect different aspects of neighbor‐dependent development. The first concerns the potential influence of fetal viability, such as proximity to resorbed or non‐viable fetuses. The second is related to variation in fetal exposure to steroid hormones arising from the sex of adjacent fetuses.

The influence of fetal viability on the development of neighboring fetuses is not well understood. Only one study has directly addressed this: Barr et al. (1970) examined fetal growth adjacent to resorbed fetuses in rats and reported no significant differences in birth weight. As such, the impact of proximity to resorption under normal physiological conditions remains unclear. This finding highlights a substantial research gap regarding how non‐viable neighboring fetuses may shape the uterine environment and influence developmental outcomes. Addressing this research gap is critical for understanding sources of developmental variability in litters, as non‐viable fetuses may alter nutrient availability, inflammatory signaling, hormonal gradients, or placental function, creating microenvironments that shape growth and developmental trajectories. This information would improve interpretation of reproductive and developmental toxicology studies and help to refine broader models of fetal programming and in utero environment influences.

Another example of R‐IaUP‐dependent development involves sex steroid hormones in litter‐bearing species. This effect has been seen in gerbils (Clark et al. 1993; Clark et al. 1992; Clark et al. 1998; Jones et al. 1997; Sherry et al. 1996), rabbits (Bánszegi et al. 2009; García‐Fernández et al. 2024), and rats (Hernández‐Tristán et al. 1999; Houtsmuller et al. 1994; Meisel and Ward 1981), but has been most extensively studied in mice (Kinsley, Miele, et al. 1986; Vandenbergh and Huggett 1995; Vom Saal and Bronson 1978, 1980; Wechman Jr et al. 1985). Due to the trans‐amniotic transfer of sex steroid hormones, local fetal concentrations of testosterone and estradiol vary with the sex of adjacent fetuses. Specifically, fetuses positioned between two males (2 M) are exposed to higher local concentrations of testosterone than fetuses of the same sex positioned between two females (0 M), which experience relatively higher estradiol exposure [reviewed in Vom Saal 1989 and Ryan and Vandenbergh 2002]. This exposure influences behavior patterns later in life. For example, both male and female 2 M fetuses tend to exhibit more masculinized traits later in life, including increased body weight and heightened aggression, whereas 0 M littermates generally display more feminized characteristics (Kinsley, Konen, et al. 1986; Vom Saal and Bronson 1980). Extending this concept, Hernández‐Tristán et al. (1999) demonstrated in rats that the neighboring male effect is further influenced by IaUP relative to uterine blood flow. Specifically, regardless of fetal sex, fetuses with a male neighbor located caudally (toward the cervix, where uterine blood flow originates and proceeds toward the ovary) were larger by gestational day 21, indicating that both the sex of adjacent fetuses and their position within the uterine horn contribute to variability in developmental outcomes.

R‐IaUP is particularly critical to consider for developmental toxicology studies assessing endocrine disrupting chemicals as they can interact with the natural hormone microenvironments created by neighboring fetuses. In a series of studies, Howdeshell et al. (1999) and Howdeshell and vom Saal (2000) demonstrated that in utero exposure to bisphenol A, which is an environmental chemical with estrogenic and androgenic activity that is linked to reproductive toxicity (Cull et al. 2025), produced markedly different outcomes depending on R‐IaUP. Specifically, bisphenol A increased postnatal body weight and advanced female puberty most strongly in 0 M fetuses, who naturally experience the highest endogenous estradiol levels, while 2 M fetuses (exposed to higher testosterone) were comparatively unaffected. These findings show that identical chemical exposures can produce different developmental responses based on the hormonal environment created by neighboring fetuses. As a result, failing to account for R‐IaUP in developmental toxicology studies risks masking true effects and overlooking susceptible subpopulations within litters.

Translatability of Implantation Location to Humans

Although most human pregnancies involve a single fetus, findings from litter‐bearing animal models demonstrate that implantation location can shape fetal development. Animal evidence shows that differences in uterine blood flow, to either the right or left uterine horn and to different sections of the same uterine horn, can influence fetal growth and potentially susceptibility to adverse outcomes. Importantly, these patterns are observed in human pregnancy. The right and left ovaries are supplied by separate blood vessels and differ in ovarian activity, with several studies reporting higher ovulation frequency or stronger responses to stimulation on the right side (Fukuda et al. 2000; Lan et al. 2010), though other studies report no consistent difference in pregnancy outcomes between ovaries (Lass et al. 1997; Thomson et al. 2001). Beyond ovarian differences, implantation site within the uterus itself has been linked to pregnancy success. Implantation in the lower uterine cavity is associated with increased risk of miscarriage (Nancarrow et al. 2023), indicating that local uterine conditions can influence early developmental viability. Similarly, placental position within the uterus has been associated with fetal growth and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Specifically, a retrospective analysis of 474 pregnancies found that anterior placental implantation has been associated with increased risks of pregnancy‐induced hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus, placenta abruption, intrauterine growth restriction, and intrauterine fetal death (Zia 2013). These human studies parallel animal findings by demonstrating that even within a singleton pregnancy, differences in implantation location can influence fetal outcomes.

Sex

Sex represents a unique source of intralitter variability because, within a single litter, male and female fetuses and placentas develop side by side but follow different biological trajectories. These inherent differences influence fetal growth, placental development, and susceptibility to adversity, including toxicant exposure. Importantly, sex is not only a biological variable at the individual level, but also at the litter level, since variation in sex ratio (i.e., the proportion of males to females within a litter) can alter overall litter dynamics. For these reasons, sex must be considered an important intralitter variable in developmental toxicology studies. Although both hormonal and genetic factors shape sex differences, we will be focusing on genetic factors in this section, as hormonal differences were discussed above. This section integrates these influences to summarize baseline sex‐dependent differences in fetal and placental growth and outcomes, and to highlight how these differences contribute to variability in susceptibility to in utero toxicant exposure.

Even in the absence of toxicant exposure, male and female fetuses follow distinct developmental strategies in utero. Sex‐based growth disparities are well documented: male fetuses typically grow faster, metabolize nutrients more efficiently, and achieve greater body weights than females (Baines and West 2023; Broere‐Brown et al. 2016; Chahoud and Paumgartten 2005; Eriksson et al. 2010; Galjaard et al. 2019; Jang et al. 2014; Redifer et al. 2021; Roland et al. 2014; Wise and Christenson 1992). This pattern reflects a fundamental trade‐off, whereby males prioritize rapid growth, while females adopt a more conservative and adaptable developmental strategy, particularly under conditions of maternal stress or pregnancy complications. Such differences may help explain the greater vulnerability of male infants to adverse outcomes, including a higher prevalence of certain birth defects (Lary and Paulozzi 2001; Zhang et al. 2023). At the litter level, sex ratio can further influence outcomes, as litters with a higher proportion of males tend to have heavier mean fetal weights (Owusu et al. 2010).

These sex‐specific growth strategies are closely linked to baseline differences in placental biology. Because trophoblast cells originate from the embryo, the placenta reflects the genetic sex of the fetus (XX and XY), resulting in inherent differences in placental biochemistry, signaling, and function. In female conceptuses, one X chromosome is typically inactivated to achieve dosage compensation; however, the pattern of X inactivation in the placenta differs between species. In rodents, the paternal X chromosome is preferentially inactivated (Takagi and Sasaki 1975; Wang et al. 2001), whereas in humans, X inactivation occurs randomly (Looijenga et al. 1999; Moreira de Mello et al. 2010). Notably, X inactivation in the placenta is relatively relaxed compared with somatic tissues, allowing partial reactivation or shifts toward non‐random inactivation in response to the intrauterine environment (Migeon et al. 2005). This plasticity may enhance the adaptive capacity of female placentas, as the presence of a second X chromosome permits upregulation of protective X‐linked genes, conferring greater resilience to adverse conditions compared with male placentas, which lack this genetic redundancy.

Consistent with these genetic differences, sex‐specific distinctions are evident in placental structure and function. Male placentas are generally heavier and more efficient at nutrient transfer but are less adaptable to maternal stressors, whereas female placentas invest more in maintaining homeostasis and flexibility, even at the expense of slower fetal growth (Ishikawa et al. 2003; Roland et al. 2014; Wise and Christenson 1992). These functional differences extend to vascular development, immune regulation, and endocrine signaling, where female placentas demonstrate a greater capacity to adjust under suboptimal conditions. Consequently, male fetuses often thrive when conditions are favorable but are disproportionately affected when the intrauterine environment is compromised.

At the molecular level, the placenta exhibits extensive sex‐biased gene expression that likely contributes to differences in disease susceptibility (Buckberry et al. 2014). Female placentas display higher expression of genes involved in transport, immune regulation, growth, and development (Goodman et al. 2023), whereas male placentas are more prone to oxidative stress under conditions such as suboptimal maternal nutrient supply (Minghetti et al. 2013). Together, these findings demonstrate that sex‐specific placental biology is a key determinant of fetal growth trajectories and vulnerability to adverse pregnancy conditions.

Sex as a Biological Variable in Fetal Responses to Toxicant Exposure

Although sex differences in fetal growth and developmental outcomes are well recognized, the biological mechanisms that confer sex‐specific vulnerability or protection following in utero toxicant exposure remain incompletely understood. This knowledge gap is partly attributable to the historical underrepresentation of sex‐stratified analyses in developmental studies (DiPietro and Voegtline 2017). Sex‐specific differences in baseline fetal growth trajectories and placental function directly shape how male and female conceptuses respond to toxicant stress. Consequently, toxicant‐induced changes in fetal and placental outcomes frequently diverge by sex, contributing substantially to intralitter variability.

Emerging evidence indicates that fetal sex modulates responses to maternal nutrition, stress, infection, and environmental toxicants, influencing not only placental growth, fetal growth, and malformation susceptibility, but also long‐term cardiac, metabolic, and neurodevelopmental outcomes (Alves et al. 2020; Baines and West 2023; Benevenuto et al. 2017; Mukhopadhyay et al. 2018; Yochum et al. 2014). These observations underscore the importance of explicitly incorporating sex as a biological variable in developmental toxicology research to identify mechanisms of susceptibility and resilience and to improve the translational relevance of animal models to human pregnancy outcomes.

Rodent studies provide clear examples of sexually dimorphic malformation risk. High‐dose valproic acid exposure in mice resulted in significantly higher rates of neural tube defects in female fetuses compared with males, indicating enhanced female susceptibility under conditions of severe teratogenic stress (Brown et al. 2025). Similarly, arsenic exposure during neurulation produced sex‐specific malformation profiles, with exencephaly occurring primarily in females and spina bifida aperta predominating in males (Machado et al. 1999). These findings suggest that fetal sex influences not only the likelihood of malformation, but also the nature of structural defects that arise following disruption of critical developmental windows.

Sex differences also extend to fetal growth and organ‐specific outcomes. In a sheep model, prenatal betamethasone exposure increased oxidative stress in male kidneys, whereas female offspring were protected due to higher superoxide dismutase activity, highlighting sex‐dependent differences in antioxidant capacity (Bi et al. 2014). In murine models of in utero benzene exposure, Cull et al. (2025) observed sex‐specific effects on fetal cardiac growth, with female fetuses exhibiting increased heart weights relative to males at late gestation. These fetal differences occurred despite minimal changes in overall placental efficiency at the litter level, suggesting that sex‐specific placental adaptations may redirect toxicant effects toward particular organ systems rather than uniformly impairing growth.

Sex‐dependent effects of toxicant exposure are not limited to prenatal outcomes but can extend into postnatal life and disease susceptibility. In a murine model of in utero benzene exposure, male offspring were more susceptible to liver tumor development, whereas females exhibited increased risk of hematological malignancies, indicating long‐term, sex‐specific programming of disease risk (Badham et al. 2010). Similar sex‐dependent trajectories have been reported following prenatal exposure to a range of environmental stressors, including endocrine disruptors, alcohol, and air pollutants, affecting growth, metabolic health, and neurodevelopment across the lifespan (Cull and Winn 2025; O'Shaughnessy et al. 2011; Reddy and Oliver 2021; Signes‐Pastor et al. 2019).

Sex‐dependent neurodevelopmental outcomes further illustrate how fetal sex modifies toxicant susceptibility. In mice exposed to cigarette smoke during gestation, male offspring exhibited increased aggression, hyperactivity, anxiety‐like behavior, cognitive impairment, and somatosensory deficits (Yochum et al. 2014). These behavioral alterations were accompanied by reductions in striatal and cortical dopamine and serotonin levels, indicating heightened male vulnerability of developing neural circuits to prenatal toxicant exposure. While these outcomes extend beyond fetal growth, they underscore the broader and lasting consequences of sex‐specific developmental programming initiated in utero.

Sex as a Biological Variable in Placental Responses to Toxicant Exposure

At the placental level, numerous studies demonstrate that fetal sex modifies toxicant‐induced changes in placental weight, efficiency, and function. Cadmium exposure in humans preferentially reduced placental weight in females (Punshon et al. 2019), while maternal dexamethasone exposure in mice selectively reduced female placental weight without affecting males (Cuffe et al. 2011). In contrast, high‐dose valproic acid exposure preserved baseline sex differences in placental efficiency, with female placentas maintaining higher efficiency than males (Brown et al. 2025). Similarly, benzene exposure in mice did not significantly alter placental efficiency overall, yet female placentas consistently exhibited higher efficiency than males, suggesting enhanced compensatory capacity under toxicant stress (Cull et al. 2025). Prenatal cadmium exposure in rodents impaired fetal and placental growth in males but not females, even though female placentas accumulated substantially more cadmium (Kozlosky et al. 2023). This sex‐specific protection in females was associated with preserved placental vascularization and glucose transporter expression, highlighting placental mechanisms underlying differential susceptibility (Kozlosky et al. 2023). These findings illustrate how sex‐specific placental adaptations can influence toxicant burden from fetal growth outcomes and contribute to intralitter variability.

Sex‐specific placental effects have also been reported following prenatal exposure to marijuana smoke. In pregnant mice exposed to daily low‐dose marijuana smoke from mid‐ to late gestation, placental weight was increased, and fetal sex modified placental efficiency outcomes (Benevenuto et al. 2017). Male fetuses exhibited a significantly reduced fetal‐to‐placental weight ratio, indicating compromised placental efficiency and an increased placental burden required to support fetal growth. These findings suggest that inhaled toxicants may impair placental function in a sex‐dependent manner, with male fetuses displaying heightened vulnerability to inefficiencies in placental support.

Mechanistic insight into these sex‐specific placental responses has been provided by transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. Maxwell et al. (2023) demonstrated that benzene exposure elicits a sexually dimorphic placental transcriptional response in mice, with male placentas exhibiting nearly threefold more affected biological processes than females. This pattern suggests that male placentas may mount broader, but potentially less targeted, responses to toxicant stress, whereas female placentas engage more restrained and adaptive transcriptional programs. Importantly, placental sexual dimorphism was also identified in first‐trimester human placentas, supporting the translational relevance of sex‐dependent placental responses observed in animal models (Maxwell et al. 2023).

Conclusion

Most animal models used in developmental toxicology involve litter‐bearing species, introducing biological complexity that is absent in most human pregnancies. Here, we have highlighted a substantial body of evidence that demonstrates intralitter variability exists and meaningfully influences fetal and placental susceptibility to toxicant exposure, with factors such as litter size, uterine location, and biological sex contributing to differential outcomes within the same pregnancy. Failure to account for intralitter variability can mask true toxicant effects, exaggerate variability, or lead to inaccurate conclusions regarding dose–response relationships. In addition to accounting for these sources of variability, careful evaluation of individual‐level data and an understanding of the background or spontaneous incidence of defects in the species and strain used are also essential for placing findings into proper biological context during hazard assessment. These considerations help distinguish treatment‐related effects from normal biological variation and improve interpretation of developmental toxicity studies, while improving the ability to identify mechanisms of susceptibility and resilience and strengthening the interpretability and translational relevance of experimental findings. Incorporating these variables into analyses in developmental toxicology studies is therefore essential for accurately identifying mechanisms of susceptibility and resilience and for improving the interpretability and translational relevance of experimental findings.

Best practices include maintaining consistent and transparently reported sampling strategies across experimental groups. We recommend that investigators avoid randomly sampling fetal and placental tissues and instead adopt standardized approaches such as collecting samples from predefined uterine regions (e.g., ovarian, middle, or cervical positions) or consistently sampling from the same uterine horn across all litters. Reporting these strategies is critical for transparency and reproducibility. Additionally, biological sex should be routinely determined to allow for sex‐specific analyses. Analytical approaches should also move beyond litter‐level averaging and instead employ more advanced statistics such as mixed effects models that can account for both within‐ and between‐litter sources of variation while incorporating multiple biological determinants of development. Together, these practices will improve scientific rigor, reproducibility, and interpretability in developmental toxicology studies, enhance the findings from litter‐bearing species to human health, and align with the principles of the 3Rs by maximizing information gained from each pregnancy and reducing unnecessary animal use.
