Characterization of mechanical and temperature effects on delamination and basic strength behaviors of PVC coated PUR foam substrate used in bus dashboard
Authors: Niyazi Oral, Okan Otuz, Rafet Kemal Kocabıyık, Mustafa Akoğlu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on the delamination and characterization of different loadings and temperature conditions of polyvinyl chloride (PVC)-coated polyurethane (PUR) foam component layers commonly used in bus dashboards. The method was developed through mechanical and thermal tests.The study involves the examination of primer and no primer PVC-coated PUR components using SEM (Scanning Electron Microscope), tensile-compression, three-point bending, primer characterization, peel, PUR material thermal expansion, and thermal tests. Physical and material tests were conducted to determine the mechanical properties of PVC and PUR foam and the material properties of the primer. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was also conducted to identify the functional groups present in the PVC, PUR, and primer materials, helping to understand the material interactions.Some tests produced the expected results, while others did not. The primer characterization and three-point bending tests were unsuccessful due to sample foam breakage. However, tensile-compression tests, peel tests, thermal expansion tests, and thermal tests yielded the desired outcomes. During thermal testing of primed samples, it was observed that the samples shortened and bent as the primer material melted and began to separate from the PVC and PUR layers. This bending and swelling were attributed to the difference in thermal expansion coefficients between the melting primer material and the PVC and PUR layers. The results demonstrate that PVC-coated PUR components can be effectively separated, and they also highlight opportunities for design modifications to improve the durability of bus dashboards.Keywords: PVC, PUR foam, Material characterization, Bus dashboard, Physical tests, Material properties, Material nonlinearity, Stress strain curve

Introduction

The dashboard is a crucial component in vehicles, located directly in front of the driver. It displays essential indicators and controls needed for vehicle operation. Additionally, the dashboard protects the vehicle's internal mechanisms from moisture, impact, wear and tear, excessive heat, and harmful UV rays. Given that dashboards must endure high temperatures (up to 120 °C) and have high impact resistance standards, they play a vital role in ensuring vehicle durability [1]. In buses, the dashboard is often designed as a modular panel, forming an enclosed area around the driver.

When it comes to molding technology and materials, passenger vehicle dashboards are divided into three categories: injection-molded dashboards, foam-molded dashboards, and fiberglass-reinforced plastic dashboards [2]. Their basic features are as follows:

Injection-molded dashboards are made from materials like polycarbonate (PC)/acrylonitrile butadiene styrene polypropylene (ABSPP), styrene maleic anhydride (SMA), and polyphenylene oxide (PPO). These dashboards are mainly found in small to medium-sized passenger cars, which tend to have larger bodies and greater stability. The benefits of using these materials include a high-quality product quality and increased production efficiency.

Foam-molded dashboards typically feature a three-layer structure consisting of a skin layer, a foam layer, and a frame layer. The skin layer, made of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) film through vacuum molding, serves as the dashboard's outer surface. The frame layer, composed of glass fiber-reinforced plastic, forms the inner layer, providing structural support. The intermediate foam layer is primarily made of polyurethane (PUR) foam. It is created by mixing PUR, polyether, and isocyanate, which are then injected between the layers to form the foam core.

FRP dashboards are used in special passenger cars with small batches and complex situations. The advantages include being lightweight and customizable, with easy paint removal.

In a broader classification, dashboards are divided into two main types [3]:

Hard dashboards (uncoated) are composed of a rigid structure made primarily from polypropylene, along with various components crafted from acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS)-based materials. The selection of materials is determined by the component's geometry as well as its technical, mechanical, and thermal properties.

Coated dashboards can be of two different types. The first type consists of a rigid support made of polycarbonate/ABS and a leather-like coating made of foamed PVC. The second type consists of a rigid frame made of ABS reinforced with fiberglass, a foam PUR sandwich, and a PVC-coated ABS final coating.

In recent years, plastics have increasingly played a more significant role in the performance and design of these panels. (ABS), polycarbonate alloys, polycarbonate, polyester, and polypropylene are the most common materials concerning these important applications [4].

The use of PUR materials extends not only to the construction sector, interior spaces of buildings, and furniture but also dominates the transportation sector, including passenger cars, trucks, and buses. Despite 15 different polymer types being used in vehicles, the most preferred polymers are polypropylene (PP), PUR, PVC, polyethylene (PE), ABS, and nylon (PA). Around 75 % of polymer and composite applications in vehicles are used for interior spaces, 14 % for exterior parts, and 11 % for electrical and under-hood components [5]. PVC is one of the most affordable plastics available and is widely used on a large scale. As a thermoplastic, it offers excellent resistance to alkalis, salts, and highly polar solvents. Additionally, due to the chlorine in its structure, it is flame-retardant [1].

Currently, the four leading foam materials in the market are polyolefin, PUR, polystyrene, and PVC. Polymer foams, defined as materials containing gas voids enclosed by a dense polymer matrix, have gained significant attention from researchers due to their broad range of applications. PUR typically makes up about 10 %–15 % of a vehicle, depending on the production technology used. These materials contribute directly to reducing fuel consumption, either by enabling lightweight designs in traditional vehicle models or through innovative hybrid metal or plastic designs. The applications for PUR are broad, including seat cushions, steering wheels, sound and energy-absorbing components, roofs, and dashboards.

The use of plastic materials in automotive applications is on the rise, prompting industries to explore solutions that minimize their environmental impact at the end of their service life. In this regard, one of the key goals for car manufacturers is to fully recycle the materials used in their vehicles [6].

Attention has shifted to plastic assemblies, as metal components currently account for 75 % of vehicles. Plastic assemblies, which now make up 12.5 % of these components, are expected to increase further in the near future. Another critical concern is the recycling and reuse of PUR foam in vehicles [7].

The dashboard examined in this study mainly consists of three components: PVC coating, primering, and PUR foam. PVC coating provides panels with a durable and aesthetic appearance, while PUR foam provides flexibility and durability. Dashboard can be exposed to various physical and chemical factors, including scratches, impacts, breakage, burning, moisture and mold, and external factors, which can cause damage to the surface and internal structure of the dashboard. Damages such as keying, object impacts, fractures, burning, moisture, and mold can affect the function and appearance of the dashboard, potentially endanger its safety. Therefore, it is important to separate damaged PVC coatings from PUR foam and subsequently recycle them. The primary objectives of the study are to characterize the material, observe material delamination, and minimize waste during the recycling process. The delamination of these components is quite complex due to the structural elements needed for testing, the time involved, and the often incompatible polymer materials used in their construction. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a suitable method for delamination and, more importantly, to characterize carefully by assessing the mutual compatibility of structural materials. In line with these objectives, the properties of PVC-coated PUR foam components have been analyzed in detail using mechanical and thermal characterization methods.

Kesavan A. et al. investigated the mechanical and thermal properties of PUR and PVC foam composites widely used in structural applications in the aviation and automotive industries due to their low weight and high rigidity. Sandwich composites were produced using PUR, PVC foams and carbon fibers as the leather material through hand lay-up. The mechanical tests conducted included tension, bending, impact, double shear, laminar interface peeling, and hardness tests. Additionally, thermogravimetric tests (TGA) were conducted to analyze the temperature properties of sandwich composites. The internal structures of the tested samples were analyzed by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The results show that PVC foam composites have higher tensile, bending, impact, thermal, and laminar interface peeling resistance compared to other composites. The literature review of this study indicates that foams such as PVC, PUR, balsa wood, and wax are widely used [8].

Ragosta, G. et al. extensively researched the recycling of a multi-layered polypropylene-based automotive interior component, the dashboard. Thermal, spectroscopic, mechanical, and morphological analyses were performed on this polyolefin-based component. The recycled material showed significant deterioration in mechanical and impact properties due to thermo-oxidative and thermo-mechanical degradation processes during processing. Limited improvement in mechanical behavior was achieved by adding an appropriate antioxidant and stabilizer during recycling. A more significant improvement in fracture deformation, impact strength, and overall morphology was achieved by adding ethylene-propylene copolymer and pure polypropylene during the recycling process. This method resulted in materials with suitable properties that could be reused for the same or similar applications in automobiles [3].

S. Milojević et al. addressed the question of how the dashboard material of buses could be replaced with an environmentally friendly material and how dismantling, reuse, and recycling requirements could be considered in the design of new buses. It is important to consider the recycling requirements of vehicles during production to optimize the reusability of materials at the end of their service life. The aim of the study is to optimize vehicles for recycling requirements. The dashboard material of a city bus powered by natural gas was converted from plastic and PUR foam to an ecological material and carbon-reinforced material. Dismantling, reuse, and recycling requirements were integrated into the design of new buses [4].

The polyurethane foams analyzed by Petru and Novak for mechanical behavior demonstrated high non-linear viscoelastic responses, particularly in the automotive and medical sectors. The material has been studied using mathematical descriptions that are subsequently verified through experimentation and measurements. Factors influencing the response of the polymer foam include the structure, shape, and dimensions of the foam cells, fill volume, and the type of polymer used. The study demonstrated that polyurethane foam exhibits a non-linear character when undergoing viscoelastic deformation, relaxation, and structural recovery. The author established that low-density polyurethane foams possess good mechanical properties, making them suitable for use in the automotive sector. Methods of application such as vertical layering offer energy-saving and vibration-isolating improvement. However, they have never produced the anticipated synergetic effect. In this context, low-density foams exhibit higher overall stiffness and a lower damping coefficient. The mechanical properties under rapid compression are influenced by both the air content within the cells and the breathability of the porous structure [9].

Experimental

Specimen geometry and preparation

The processes involved in preparing the test specimens, which consist of polyurethane foam, primer, and PVC film, as investigated in this study, are illustrated in Fig. 1. The production process includes several steps: preparation of materials, preparation of molds, placement of materials into molds, compression of materials, and removal of products from the molds. First, a PVC mold cut to specific dimensions is laid out, and a primer material is applied evenly over its surface to ensure consistent coverage and the formation of a uniform structure. Next, liquid polyurethane foam is poured onto the primed PVC, followed by the application of compression within the mold to create a tight bond between the materials. The molds hold the polyurethane foam, primer, and PVC film in place during this process. Once the materials are securely compressed to achieve the desired shape, the molds are emptied, and any necessary cutting or correction processes are performed on the products. Following these procedures, test specimens consisting of polyurethane foam, primer, and PVC film are created. The PVC film provides additional protection by wrapping around the outer layer of the plate, enhancing the overall durability of the material and strengthening it against various external factors. The plate is made of 12 mm thick PUR foam and 1.2 mm thick PVC material, resulting in a total thickness of 13.2 mm, with dimensions of 210 mm × 297 mm for width and length, respectively.

Tensile test

The specimen dimensions for tensile testing were determined according to ASTM D638 standard, gauge length is 25 mm, distance between grips is 65m, inner radius is 14 mm etc. According to the dimensions obtained from the standard, the PVC plate was cut using a water jet at 0 and 90° with respect to the rolling direction [10].

Based on the dimensions obtained from the standard, the PVC plate was cut using a water jet at angles of 0 and 90° relative to the rolling direction. Following the same sample dimensions, cutting with a water jet was also performed for PUR and PUR + PVC materials. Since the thickness of the PUR material is 12 mm and the thickness of the PUR + PVC material is 13.2 mm, the thicknesses were reduced to 3 mm to comply with the standard. The initial thickness comprised a ratio of 9 % PVC and 91 % PUR foam. The final sample thickness was reduced to 3 mm in accordance with the type-1 sample thickness specified in the test standard, while maintaining this ratio. The PUR foam thickness is 2.73 mm, and the PVC thickness is 0.27 mm.

These differences arise from the fiber orientations of the materials, as previously mentioned. Since the fibers in the 0-degree material are aligned with the direction of the tensile force, this material experiences greater plastic deformation, exhibiting higher ductility and toughness. On the other hand, the fibers of the material at 90° are perpendicular to the tensile force. Therefore, the material at 90° undergoes less plastic deformation and shows lower tensile strength.

Also the tensile strength of the regular PVC specimen is higher than that of the heated PVC specimen, but it exhibits less toughness and is more brittle. On the other hand, the heated specimen shows higher toughness and ductility. The 0-degree samples tested at 80 °C were observed to plasticize at 9.25 MPa, similar to the 90-degree samples tested at 25 °C. The molecular structure of heated PVC is more relaxed compared to normal PVC. A relaxed molecular structure makes the material more flexible and more prone to plastic deformation. However, a relaxed molecular structure also reduces the material's durability and tensile strength. The similarity in yield strength between the 0-degree samples at 80 °C and the 90-degree samples at 25 °C can be explained by the increased molecular mobility due to the elevated temperature and the influence of orientation on mechanical properties. When the 0-degree direction is heated, it exhibits a yield strength similar to the mechanical response of the material in the 90-degree direction at 25 °C.

Single Lap Joint test

SLJ tests are conducted to determine the adhesive shear stress. Samples with adhesive bonding in a single region are prepared and subjected to tension in order to induce adhesive shear. It is expected that during the test, adhesive shear will occur at the interface often resulting in failure.

SLJ test samples were prepared using ASTM 7616/D7616M standard [14]. When preparing the samples, the dimensions were not directly used; instead, they were scaled down according to the proportions given in the standard. This was done to ensure higher force application to the desired region where separation is expected during the test, as the material is brittle.

The plate thickness is not compliant with the standard as it totals 13.2 mm. Therefore, the PUR portion of the sample was cut using a saw, and the sample was thinned down to approximately 2.7 mm to meet the standard requirements. Separate samples were produced for materials with and without adhesive.

Discussion

The discussion section of this research paper will primarily focus on analyzing the findings and their implications. It will cover the results of physical and thermal tests conducted on PVC-coated PUR foam substrates under various loading and temperature conditions. These test results offer valuable insights into the durability and thermal stability of the substrates, as well as potential areas for improvement to enhance material performance. Additionally, the findings will help explain how the material behaves under high-temperature and mechanical loading conditions, providing a clearer understanding of its performance characteristics.

SEM images taken from the samples before testing did not show the desired difference in adhesion between primed and unprimed samples. The PUR material is thought to absorb the primer during curing and this may need to be reconsidered. This indicates that the primer application may need to be reevaluated. Further research is also needed on how the absorption property of PUR material affects liner performance.

FTIR spectral analysis provided important information about the chemical structures and possible interactions of PVC, PUR and primer samples. The strong absorptions at 2920 and 2850 cm-1 indicating CH2 and CH stretching in the PVC sample are specific to the main structure of PVC. The C=O stretches at 1728, 1512 and 1454 cm-1 indicate ketonic or esteric groups that may originate from additives or degradation products. Peaks at lower wave numbers indicate the addition of bio-based plasticizers or stabilizers. The PUR sample exhibits distinct peaks at 1705 cm-1 with C=O stretching indicating the presence of polyurethane, especially urethane groups. Broad bands at 1617 and 1597 cm-1 indicate hydrogen bonded -OH groups, which are critical for thermal and mechanical stability. In the primer sample, prominent peaks of -OH and C=O groups at 1724 and 1631 cm-1 indicate a complex formulation designed to enhance adhesion with PVC and PUR.

Some tests yielded expected results, while others did not. These are presented in the convergent and non-convergent test sections. Primer characterization tests and three-point bending tests failed to achieve their objectives due to the sample fracturing from the PUR foam. Desired findings were obtained in tensile-compression tests, peel tests, thermal expansion tests, and thermal tests.

In convergent tests, regarding tensile tests, the PVC material exhibited anisotropic behavior, showing different mechanical properties depending on its orientation. The PUR material showed lower strength and higher sensitivity compared to PVC. The tensile properties of the PUR + PVC material were found to lie between those of PUR and PVC. During the tensile test, when the temperature raised to 80 °C, a decrease in the tensile strength was observed as well as in the literature of Marsavina et al. (2008) [16].

In compression tests, the PUR material experienced plastic deformation as expected due to its cellular structure after compression. The compressive strength of the PUR material was found to be 4950 N. The primed PUR + PVC material exhibited 49,5 % lower strength when compared to the unprimed sample. During the peel test, PUR foam particles were found on the PVC surface, indicating excellent adhesion between the two surfaces. Looking at the thermal expansion tests of PUR material, it was observed that the expansion coefficient decreased after 70 °C. This can be considered as the chemical bonds within the molecules of the PUR material are not completely broken above 70 °C and the attractive forces between the molecules increase. This reduces the expansion rate, making the PUR material less flexible and more brittle after 70 °C, potentially limiting its application. During thermal tests, in primed samples, a strong bond was formed between PVC and PUR materials due to the primer. However, when heat was applied, the effect of the primer decreased, causing the bond between the materials to break and separation to occur. In unprimed samples, without such an adhesive bond, the behavior of each material was observed independently. Thermal effects were more distinct in thinner samples as thickness increased the impact of the effects. The different thermal expansion coefficients of PVC and PUR led to bending in the examined cases. However, while this resulted in foaming in unprimed samples, separation occurred in primed samples. As a result, the presence of the primer significantly affected the thermal behavior of the specimen and the thickness affected the effect even worse.

In non-convergent tests, in primer characterization tests, SLJ, and DCB tests, difficulties were encountered in achieving the desired separation intended by fracturing from the PUR foam. Hence, the intended calculation of the primer shear stress in the SLJ test and the intended calculation of the fracture energy in the DCB test could not be performed. Three-point bending tests revealed some behavioral differences depending on the position of the PUR material. When the PUR material was on top, displacement increased compared to when it was at the bottom, but the sample still exhibited quite brittle behavior.

Conclusion

In this study, the physical and thermal properties of PVC-coated PUR foam used in bus dashboards, as shown in Fig. 21, were thoroughly examined. Various material tests and thermal analyses were conducted under different loading and temperature conditions, providing valuable insights into the properties and thermal stability of these materials. This information enhanced the understanding of the physical and thermal behavior of PVC-coated PUR foam, contributing to the prevention of delamination.

Mercedes-Benz dashboard.

The inferences made from SEM images and the varying effects of priming on strength across different test types suggested further research to understand how the absorption properties of PUR affect primer performance and the impact of primer material on strength behavior. Primer characterization tests showed challenges in achieving the desired separation from PUR foam, indicating the need for further improvements in test techniques. The variation in behavior depending on the location of the PUR material highlighted the need for further research into material properties and interactions with primers. The decrease in the expansion coefficient of PUR showed that the thermal expansion behavior of PUR should be considered in applications with temperature fluctuations. In thinner samples, thermal effects were more visible and the importance of thickness in material behavior was observed. This effect should be considered in potential applications. This study provided a comprehensive analysis of the performance and durability of PVC-coated PUR foam in industrial applications. The results offered insights for improving long-term performance in production and post-delamination processes, emphasizing the recycling potential of the components of PVC-coated PUR foam, thus contributing to sustainability efforts and laying the groundwork for further research in this area. Comprehensive understanding of material behavior under different conditions not only facilitates improvements but also reveals potential areas for future research. The results showed the effective separation potential of PVC-coated PUR components and suggested design changes that would enhance the durability of bus dashboard.
