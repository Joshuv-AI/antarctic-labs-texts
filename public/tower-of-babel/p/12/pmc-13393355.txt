Large-Scale Numerical Simulation of Fracture-Fluid–Rock Interactions Controlled by the Strike Slip Fault System in Shunbei Area of Tarim Basin, China
Authors: Qian Ding, Donghua You, Leilei Yang, Shu Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the interactions among fractures, hydrothermal fluids, and formation rocks in six Cambrian-Ordovician strata of the Shunbei area, Tarim Basin, based on actual geological settings and numerical simulation methods. By construction of a large-scale 3D geological model and simulation of the dynamic processes of hydrothermal migration and reactions along fractures, the following key findings are revealed: (1) fluid–rock reaction mechanisms: hydrothermal fluids migrate upward along high-permeability fractures, leading to the dissolution of minerals such as calcite and kaolinite and the precipitation of quartz. Acidic fluids, obstructed by caprocks at fracture tops, form reflux zones that concentrate H+ and enhance the localized dissolution. (2) Mineral distribution patterns: calcite dissolution dominates within fracture zones, while calcite precipitation occurs along fracture margins due to Ca2+ saturation. Geothermal gradients further regulate the spatial differentiation of the mineral reactions. (3) Fracture-controlled reservoir effects: branching fractures divert flow, reducing the velocity in main fractures and prolonging the reaction time. Nonpenetrating fractures induce lateral fluid seepage, creating zones of superimposed dissolution and precipitation. This study provides a theoretical foundation for predicting high-quality carbonate reservoirs in deep formations, highlighting how fracture architecture and thermobaric conditions jointly control the reservoir storage space evolution. The findings offer significant guidance for the hydrocarbon exploration.

Introduction

Deep and ultra-deep layers contain abundant oil and gas resources, serving as crucial target formations for hydrocarbon exploration in major petroliferous basins worldwide. In China, significant breakthroughs have been made in both western and eastern basins, including the Tahe and Lunnan karst fracture-cave reservoirs in the Tarim Basin, the Shunbei and Fuman ultradeep fault-controlled fracture-cave reservoirs, tight sandstone gas fields in the northern and southern Tianshan regions, the Puguang and Yuanba reef-shoal gas fields in the Sichuan Basin, as well as large-scale Sinian-Cambrian platform-margin mound-shoal gas fields such as Moxi and Anyue.

Among these, the most distinctive is the Shunbei Oil and Gas Field in the Tarim Basin, which consists of a unique “fault-controlled reservoir”, exhibiting multilayer three-dimensional hydrocarbon exploration characteristics. The reservoirs in Shunbei are primarily composed of middle-lower Ordovician carbonate rocks, which were fractured by multiphase strike-slip faulting and subsequently modified by fluid dissolution, forming a distinctive fracture-cave system with strong spatial heterogeneity. − The deepest well in Shunbei exceeds 9,000 m, with an average reservoir burial depth of 7,512 m and an oil column height surpassing 600 m.

The strike–slip faults serve as migration pathways for geological fluids, which permeate through fractures, faults, and cavities while reacting with the surrounding rocksdissolving minerals or precipitating new ones. These interactions alter the geometry of the flow pathways, modify the physical properties of the strata, and even change the composition of the fluids themselves. If we consider the rock–fluid–fault system in the Shunbei region as a vast reactive system, it spans an enormous spatial scaleover 7,000 m in depth and more than 10,000 m horizontallyas well as a temporal scale extending across tens of millions of years. Taking the multilayered strata, kilometer-scale cross-cutting faults, and formation fluids as the research subject, what fluid–rock interaction processes occurred to form the unique “fault-controlled fracture-cavity” reservoirs in the Shunbei Oil and Gas Field? Addressing this key scientific question will help clarify the genetic mechanisms of high-quality, large-scale reservoirs in deep and ultra-deep formations. It will also guide hydrocarbon exploration by enabling the prediction of favorable carbonate reservoirs, ultimately laying a theoretical foundation for discovering the next “Shunbei oil and gas field”.

Previous researchers have conducted substantial work on the tectonic setting, reservoir characteristics, fracture-cave systems, and distribution patterns of oil and gas reservoirs in the Shunbei Oil and Gas Field. Comparisons have also been made with the “weathering crust” karst fracture-cave reservoir spaces in the Tahe Oil Field in the northern Tarim Basin. − Alternatively, from the perspective of fluid–rock interactions, a series of experiments have been carried out to investigate mineral dissolution–precipitation processes and the modification of reservoir spaces by fluids. These studies, combined with numerical simulations, have summarized geological models of the Shunbei region across different dimensions. , Fluid–rock interactions span scales from pore-sized to basin-sized, reflecting progress from mechanistic research to geological applications. Through numerical simulations and core CT characterization, upgrading pore-scale simulations to field or even basin scales represents a future research trend. − However, numerical simulations targeting multilayer, cross-stratigraphic fault-geological fluid systems, spanning spatial scales of several kilometers and temporal scales of millions of years, remain virtually unexplored.

Therefore, we propose taking the key stratigraphic units in the Tarim Basin study area as the primary research target. By integrating actual drilling data and employing numerical simulation techniques, we aim to model the dynamic processes of geological fluids migrating along cross-stratigraphic faults and their subsequent alteration of the surrounding rocks. The study will quantitatively assess changes in the physical properties of host rocks induced by fluid migration and alteration with the objective of establishing the spatiotemporal correlation between high-quality reservoir distribution and fault systems. This research is expected to provide more robust guidance for future exploration of strategies.

Numerical Simulation

Numerical Model

With the advancement of earth sciences, there is an increasing demand across both environmental and energy sectors for the detailed characterization of various geological processes. These include fluid flow during diagenesis, mineral transformation, and elemental migration. Accurately describing such complex processes requires a comprehensive methodology applicable to diverse geological bodies that integrates fluid dynamics, mass transport, and chemical reactions, enabling both qualitative and quantitative analyses of these phenomena. Subsurface multicomponent reactive transport modeling has emerged as a vital tool for interpreting numerous coupled processes in Earth systems, encompassing geochemical, microbial, and physical interactions. Currently, several prominent reactive transport modeling software packages are widely utilized in the field, including EQ3/6, PHREEQC, NETPATH, WATERQ4F, PFLOTRAN, and TOUGHREACT.

TOUGHREACT software employed in this study is a reactive transport modeling tool developed based on TOUGH, specifically designed for simulating nonisothermal multiphase flow and reactive transport in variably saturated geological media. Internationally recognized for its applications, it has been extensively utilized in nuclear waste geological disposal, CO2 geological sequestration, and geothermal development research.

TOUGHREACT demonstrates exceptional capability in coupling water–rock chemical interactions with the transport of reaction products. This enables comprehensive simulation of various geological processes and parameters including: rock cementation and replacement, dissolution processes, pore space destruction/preservation, and composition of pore-filling materials. , Notably, TOUGHREACT offers unique advantages for this particular study: multiscale simulation capacity (mm to km scale), capacity to model physicochemical changes over million-year time scales, optimal compatibility with our research objectives regarding fault-controlled reservoir formation mechanisms. Software’s ability to handle both the spatial (multikilometer fault systems) and temporal (multimillion-year diagenesis) dimensions of our study makes it the most suitable computational tool for this investigation.

This study focuses on the Ordovician strata fault–fluid system in the Shunbei Oil and Gas Field of the Tarim Basin to characterize fluid migration processes along faults while quantifying the spatiotemporal relationships of various strata–fault–fluid configurations (Figures and ). The research aims to establish theoretical frameworks and practical methodologies for predicting the spatial distribution patterns of high-quality carbonate reservoirs in this region.

Numerical Simulation Process

To address these challenges, this study follows a systematic research approach beginning with the construction of fundamental temperature, pressure, fluid, and chemical field models through four key steps: developing a two-dimensional stratigraphic framework based on actual formation thickness and depth distributions with appropriate computational grid determinations; establishing heterogeneous temperature and pressure field models by calculating formation-specific thermal and pressure gradients to account for nonuniform distributions; defining fluid composition parameters for simulation based on formation water characteristics obtained from drilling data and literature review; and characterizing the mineralogical composition (including calcite, dolomite, quartz, etc.) and abundance through comprehensive analysis of regional geological data, thereby establishing a robust foundation for subsequent numerical simulations.

Establish Geological Model in Three-Dimensional Profiles with ∼km Scale

The study area’s stratigraphic sequence comprises six formations vertically stacked from bottom to top, corresponding to the Ordovician-Cambrian strata in the Shunbei area of Tarim Basin. The 6 layers were lined up as ε1, ε2, ε3, O3p, O2y, O1–2yj like the real formation distribution character (Figure a). A three-dimensional cubic model measuring 200,000 m (length) × 1,000 m (width) × 5,500 m (height) was established by importing the top and bottom coordinate data of the six formations from fundamental geological data into the stratigraphic modeling process. The coordinate selection followed specific rules: sampling points were taken every 5,000 m along the X-axis, while five fixed Y-coordinates (Y = 0, 3,000, 5,000, 7,000, and 10,000 m) were selected along the Y-axis, resulting in 205 corresponding points for each formation surface and requiring the setup of seven stratigraphic surfaces for comprehensive modeling.

Establish a Geological Profile of Heterogeneous Temperature and Pressure Fields

The temperature and pressure values at varying depths were determined using the geothermal–geopressure equilibrium method. Based on reference data from the study area, the top formation was set at 75 °C and 32.5 MPa, while the bottom formation was established at 235 °C and 112.5 MPa. Through interpolation, a precise depth-dependent correlation was derived for the temperature–pressure relationship throughout the stratigraphic column.

The establishment of anisotropic heterogeneous temperature and pressure fields requires preliminary analysis of existing data within the study area to determine temperature values at various spatial locations and depths, which are then mapped onto the stratigraphic grid. Multiple temperature interpolation operations were performed on the target area to ultimately derive temperature parameters for all 64,000 grid cells (Figure a). Similarly, the pressure data obtained through the thermobaric equilibrium process were imported into the model’s grid partition file to determine the corresponding pressures at various depths and locations. These computational procedures successfully established both the temperature and pressure fields for the study area (Figure b).

Results and Discussion

The Temporal–Spatial Evolution of Ion Concentrations on 2D Cross Sections

The hydrothermal fluid injection along fractures has induced significant ion concentration changes in the reservoir, as illustrated in Figure . Simulation results demonstrate that hydrothermal flow and subsequent reactions have substantially altered the distribution of the major ions within the reservoir system. Over a 100,000 year period, the primary affected zone extends several tens to hundreds of meters around the fracture network.

Spatiotemporal distribution of different cation concentrations.

The hydrothermal fluid flows from the bottom to the top along the fault. H+ in the hydrothermal fluid also flows and diffuses, reacting with the surrounding rock minerals. According to the simulation calculations (Figure ), the H+ concentration of the fluid inside the fracture increases, and the pH decreases. At the same time, H+ diffuses into the strata on both sides of the fault, and the fluid pH in the stratum decreases. Because of the difference in porosity and permeability of different formations, the fluid velocity is different, resulting in a difference in the rate of pH change. In addition to the physical process of diffusion, the H+ in the fluid reacts with the minerals such as calcite. The calcite dissolves and releases Ca2+, which increases the concentration of Ca2+ in the formation fluid. At the same time, H+ in the fluid is consumed, and the formation fluid pH increases.

Similarly, the concentrations of metal cations, such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and Na+, also showed an increasing trend within the faults. On both sides of the fault, if the porosity and permeability of the stratum are better, the fluid flows further and the influence range is wider. In the opposite, the fluid flowing distance is closer, and the influence range is narrower. It was also noted that the concentration of Ca2+ showed a decreasing trend at some locations. This is due to the reaction of H+ and calcite around the faults and the calcite dissolving to form Ca2+. The reaction consumes H+, the fluid pH turns into alkaline, and the concentrations of Ca2+ and CO3 2– are higher, which then combine to form calcite precipitate and reduce the concentration of Ca2+ in the fluid.

The Distribution of the Typical Mineral Content on the Two-Dimensional Profile Evolved with Time

As can be seen from Figure , the dissolution/precipitation of different minerals occurred in the study area, calcite, kaolinite, and illite minerals were dissolved, quartz was precipitated, and the dissolution of calcite in the fault was greater than that of quartz, kaolinite, and illite. Because of the acidic nature of hydrothermal fluids, the most reactive calcite minerals are dissolved first. The solubility of quartz increases with the increase of the pH of the solution and decreases with the decrease of temperature. The hydrothermal fluid reacts along the fault, reacts with the surrounding rock to consume acid, and the fluid temperature decreases. Compared with the two opposite processes, the influence of the temperature on the solubility of quartz is much greater than that of the pH value of the fluid. Therefore, by combining these two processes, quartz tends to precipitate from the solution. Calculated results indicate quartz precipitation in the Ordovician, while actual observations in cores from the Ordovician of wells SN401 and SN4 contained euhedral prismatic quartz, chert, quartz veins, etc. For example, at a depth of 7,900 m in well Shunbei 42, microscopic thin sections showed calcite precipitation, which is consistent in depth and location with the calcite precipitation near the main fault of zone 4 according to the calculation results. The dissolution and precipitation of different minerals occur simultaneously in the fault, among which calcite dissolves and quartz precipitates. On the whole, the precipitation at the fracture is less than the dissolved amount and the porosity increases.

Spatiotemporal distribution of different mineral contents in geological strata.

In the spatiotemporal distribution of calcite, the calcite in the fault has strong dissolution, while in the porous strata on both sides of the fault, a small amount of calcite precipitation is generated on both sides of the fault due to the increase in the Ca2+ concentration and the decrease in the fluid flow velocity. In the study of this area, previous research had pointed out that the silica-bearing hydrothermal fluid through the deep clastic rocks goes up the deep fault and enters the shallow Ordovician limestone layer, dissolving calcite and precipitating quartz to form a special “silicified carbonate” reservoir. This is also consistent with the calculation results of this study. Therefore, in deep and large fault zones, calcite is mainly dissolved. In the sampling near the fault zone, there are calcite veins in the rock sample fractures, indicating that calcite also precipitates.

In the spatiotemporal distribution map of the quartz content, it can be seen that the overall quartz precipitation is dominated and there is a partial dissolution phenomenon in the Yurtus Formation at the deepest part of the strata. According to the solubility–temperature relationship of quartz, the solubility of quartz increases with temperature, so cooling is an effective mechanism for quartz precipitation. ,, The concentration of silicon in the deep hydrothermal fluid is at a high level, and as the hydrothermal fluid moves upward, the formation temperature and pressure gradually decrease, so the silicate ions in the fluid precipitate into quartz, filling the fractures.

From the comparison of calcite-quartz spatiotemporal distribution, calcite dissolution is mainly found in fault zones no. 5 to no. 10, and deep dissolution and shallow filling are present in fault zones from no. 12 to 20. The area with a shallow burial depth in the southeast direction of zone no. 12 shows extensive quartz precipitation. The difference in the dissolution–precipitation trend of calcite and quartz in this profile may be closely related to the change of the geothermal field in this area. Previous studies have revealed that the deep temperature field in the study area gradually increases from northwest to southeast, that is, the area in the southeast direction of the no. The no. 12 fault zones have a higher formation temperature under the same buried depth conditions. At the same time, the characteristics of the geothermal field have a significant control effect on the distribution of oil and gas reservoir types, and condensate gas reservoirs and dry gas reservoirs are developed in the study area from northwest to southeast.

In the spatiotemporal distribution of the kaolinite content, kaolinite dissolution is the main reason, which is due to the reaction of H+ in the acidic solution with kaolinite, thereby dissolving the kaolinite. The pH range of kaolinite precipitates is usually between 8 and 11. Very few alkaline environments are present in the formation; therefore, it is difficult to observe kaolinite precipitation.

It can be observed in the spatiotemporal distribution of the illite content that the H+ in the hydrothermal fluid reacts with illite due to the acidic hydrothermal fluid inside the fault. However, at the upper end of the fault, the fluid migration speed is slow, and due to the increase in the concentration of sulfate ions and metal ions produced by illite dissolution in the fluid, it gradually precipitates into illite, and the red part of illite precipitation can be seen in the image. After that, due to the continuous expansion of the fluid migration range, new H+ is continuously replenished, resulting in the illite precipitate being dissolved again and producing a new illite precipitate at the new fluid migration edge. This also forms a phenomenon where the dissolution front continues to expand, and the precipitation site also migrates with the direction of fluid migration.

The Control of Fluid Migration and Reaction by Different Fracture Characteristics

There are many different types of faults in the study area, including simple faults and complex multiforked fault morphology, main faults and secondary faults, interfault areas and intrafault areas, and through-stratigraphic faults and nonpenetrating faults.

Hydrothermal fluids migrating along fractures into the reservoir exhibit intensified fluid–rock interactions at both the top and bottom of the fracture zones. With the upward flow of the hydrothermal fluid, the reaction of the top and the bottom of the fracture is the strongest, mainly due to the upward migration of the hydrothermal liquid from the bottom. The H+ concentration at the bottom increases rapidly, and a large amount of the reactant H+ promotes the dissolution of CaCO3. When the hydrothermal fluid flows to the top, the top is covered by marl reservoirs with low porosity and permeability, which hinder the fluid movement, causing the fluid to accumulate at the top and then flow downward, which increases the H+ concentration in the fluid and strengthens the reaction between the fluid and the rock.

Different fracture geometries have different influences on the migration of fluids, with the main fracture being the main channel for the fluid migration. In the process of hydrothermal fluid flowing, it moves from the bottom to the top along the main fracture while seeping into the porous strata on both sides and reacting with minerals. At the same time, part of the fluid migrates along the branched fault. Each bifurcation reduces the main fracture flow velocity. The area between faults is also affected by fluid migration due to the lateral seepage of fluids, in which fluids from different faults may seep and converge at two close fractures, and the effects of mineral dissolution and precipitation may be superimposed. Because the top of the nonpenetrating fault is blocked by the overlying strata, the fluid will seep back into the strata on both sides of the fault after moving upward to the top.

Hydrothermal fluid first enters the reservoir along the main fault, and heat diffusion causes the reservoir temperature to increase near the fault. In addition to the influence of hydrothermal fluid on the reservoir temperature, it also brings a large number of metal ions such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and SiO3 2– to the reservoir. The increase of temperature increases the rate of fluid–rock chemical reaction, accelerates the dissolution/precipitation of minerals, and forms new storage space, or precipitates minerals, which in turn affects the physical properties of the reservoir (Table ).

Change of Formation Porosity, Permeability, and Pressure with Time

From the spatiotemporal distribution of the porosity, it can be seen that the porosity of the formation at the fault increases significantly. In the Yijianfang Formation, the porosity seeps and extends horizontally along the stratum and the porosity increases. At the same time, the fluid moves along the interface between the Yijianfang Formation and the Yingshan Formation, resulting in the increase of the porosity of the formation interface. Similarly, this phenomenon also exists at other stratigraphic interfaces between other formations.

The change of formation permeability mainly occurs at the top of the fault, which is due to the fact that after the fluid moves to the upper end of the fault, it cannot continue to move upward because of the dense caprock. The fluid will accumulate here, causing a more obvious fluid–rock reaction, which increases the permeability of the formation. The permeability at the fault bifurcation is significantly increased, which is due to the convergence of the fluid and the increase of the H+ concentration.

The formation pressure changes caused by hydrothermal fluid charging the reservoir along the fault are shown in Figure . The pressure change shows that in 0–50,000 years, the hydrothermal fluid will first enter the formation along the main fault driven by pressure, and the pressure at the bottom of the fault will first begin to increase, and then a series of pressure transfer changes occur from the inside of the fault to the surrounding fracture, resulting in the increase of the formation pressure near the fault, which can also affect the dissolution and precipitation process of minerals, as well as the further rupture or extension of the fault.
