A case report: false hCG trail: dysgerminoma imitates gestational trophoblastic disease
Authors: Anna Babkova, Helena Robova, Hana Malikova, Jana Drozenova, Borek Sehnal, Nikola Janovska, Michael J. Halaska, Lukas Rob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian malignant germ cell tumors (MOGCTs) represent approximately 5% of all ovarian malignancies. However, in women aged 10 to 30, they account for up to 70% of ovarian neoplasms and may occasionally occur bilaterally. Roughly one-third of MOGCTs are dysgerminomas. These tumors may also be hormonally active. Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) is a spectrum of rare tumors arising from abnormal trophoblast proliferation, involving invasive mole, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT) and epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT) – characterized by elevated levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). This case report presents a young patient with a hormonally active dysgerminoma initially misdiagnosed as a GTN due to elevated and progressively rising hCG levels, along with clinical symptoms. After the misdiagnosis, the patient was initially treated with monochemotherapy and after the completion of the therapy, hCG levels did not reach negativity. The patient presented with recurrence of hCG levels elevation, and for suspected GTN the chemotherapy was further escalated. The correct diagnosis was only established after thoracotomy excision of the pulmonary lesion, which turned out to be a pulmonary metastasis of dysgerminoma. Thereafter, the patient was promptly treated according to the recommended guidelines and is currently in remission. It is necessary to maintain a broad differential diagnosis in patients with low but persistent hCG levels. GTN is often the first suspected entity, but we must consider the possibility of hormone-producing ovarian tumors, including dysgerminomas.

Introduction

Dysgerminoma is a malignant ovarian germ cell tumor originating from primordial germ cells, histologically analogous to testicular seminoma. It accounts for about 33% of malignant ovarian germ cell tumors. Approximately 75% of cases occur in girls or women under 30, among whom dysgerminoma represents around one-third of all ovarian malignancies.1 More than 70% of patients are diagnosed at stage I, with staging following the same FIGO criteria as for epithelial ovarian cancers. Bilateral ovarian involvement occurs in up to 15% of cases. The most common presenting symptoms include abdominal enlargement (tumor mass), abdominal pain (due to cyst rupture, hemoperitoneum, or adnexal torsion), and, less frequently, menstrual irregularities or precocious puberty in hormonally active tumors. hCG production is reported in about 5-15% of dysgerminomas (due to syncytiotrophoblastic components), but unlike choriocarcinoma, cytotrophoblasts are absent. Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels are more frequently seen and correlate with tumor burden. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) elevation is not typical for dysgerminoma and suggests a yolk sac tumor instead. Diagnosis relies on ultrasound imaging and tumor markers (hCG, LDH), but definitive diagnosis requires histopathological analysis post-surgery. Immunohistochemistry further supports the diagnosis.2,3 Due to the tumor’s chemosensitivity, early-stage detection, and young patient population, a fertility-sparing approach (typically unilateral adnexectomy) is preferred. However, management is guided by disease stage, patient age, reproductive plans, and other clinical factors. Standard systemic therapy involves platinum-based chemotherapy, most commonly BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin). Three cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy are considered sufficient after R0 resection. Lifelong follow-up is necessary.4

GTN is a spectrum of rare but highly treatable pregnancy-related tumors arising from abnormal trophoblast proliferation, involving invasive mole, choriocarcinoma (these clinical entities are characterized by high levels of hCG) and placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT) and epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT) – characterized by low levels of hCG.

Preinvasive molar disease is linked to aberrant genomic imprinting and paternal genetic contribution. Complete hydatidiform moles are typically diploid and androgenetic in origin, arising from fertilization of an enucleated ovum by one or two spermatozoa, resulting in exclusively paternal genetic material. Partial moles are usually triploid, with an excess of paternal genome. In addition, genotyping can assist in distinguishing gestational from non-gestational trophoblastic tumors and in identifying the causative pregnancy.5

Most cases of GTN follow hydatidiform mole - circa 50% of the cases, 24% of GTN cases develop after a spontaneous abortion, 24% after term pregnancy and relatively rarely, in 1% to 2%, after extrauterine pregnancy (1%-2%).6 Diagnosis relies on persistently elevated hCG levels and imaging, since biopsy is often not necessary for the final diagnosis. The International FIGO/WHO scoring system stratifies patients into low-risk (score ≤ 6) and high-risk (>6) and ultra-high risk (>12), guiding treatment decisions. Low-risk GTN is managed with single-agent chemotherapy: methotrexate (first-line due to lower toxicity) or actinomycin-D. High-risk or metastatic GTN requires multi-agent regimens, notably EMA-CO (etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycinD, cyclophosphamide, vincristine), achieving complete remission in approximately 93% cases. For resistant cases, salvage platinum-based chemotherapy combined with surgery is effective. Overall cure rates approach 100% in low-risk and 75% to 95% even in metastatic high-risk patients. Long-term outcomes are excellent, with fertility preservation possible, and disease recurrence is rare with vigilant hCG followup.7,8

In this case report, we present a patient with an ovarian dysgerminoma secreting hCG, which was initially misdiagnosed as GTN. The administered chemotherapy, which had a partial effect, further masked the growth of the dysgerminoma, which remained undetectable on imaging. The correct diagnosis was established only later, specifically after thoracothomic resection of a pulmonary metastasis performed based on positive PET/CT findings.

Case report

In this case report, we present a 27-year-old otherwise healthy woman who first visited her registering gynecologist in April 2020 due to a two-month history of amenorrhea. She had a history of hormonally induced menses and no previous pregnancy. A pregnancy test was positive, but no intrauterine gestational sac was visualized on ultrasound, prompting a referral to a different gynecology clinic for suspicion of an ectopic pregnancy. In the referral clinic, the serum hCG was confirmed positive, but no pathology was visualized on imaging. After one week of outpatient observation, the patient was admitted and underwent diagnostic laparoscopy, but no dilatation and curettage (D&C) was performed. No ectopic pregnancy was found; only a small left ovarian cyst was resected. Final histopathology was benign. Despite surgery, hCG levels continued to rise, raising suspicion of GTN.

Due to the continuously rising hCG trend and no histopathological confirmation being necessary in presumed GTN, chemotherapy with Actinomycin D in monotherapy was initiated. While initially hCG levels partially declined after the start of the treatment, after four cycles they began to rise again (details of hCG levels in Graph 1). Due to elevation, hCG chemotherapy was escalated to EMA-CO (etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide, vincristine). This regimen led to a steep hCG decline to undetectable levels and the chemotherapy treatment was ended in October 2020 (Graph 1). hCG levels stayed negative, however, only temporarily. After 12 weeks hCG levels started to rise again. At that time, the patient was referred to our center.

We performed complete workup of the patient. MRI of the liver and pelvis was unremarkable – both ovaries with functional changes. As hCG continued to rise (Graph 1), chemotherapy was switched to cisplatin and etoposide (EMA-PE). Due to persistent pulmonary findings and after consultation with thoracic surgeons, thoracoscopic resection was performed in March 2021. In August 2021, expert transvaginal ultrasound showed only a 24 mm hemorrhagic cyst in the left ovary, and no clear pathology. Surgery was performed two days later via midline laparotomy. Given the stage IV disease and bilateral ovarian involvement, bilateral adnexectomy, infracolic omentectomy, and para-aortic lymphadenectomy were performed. Complete (R0) resection was achieved. Intraoperative histopathological findings (frozen section) confirmed bilateral ovarian involvement. The procedure was without any surgical complications.

Final pathology confirmed bilateral dysgerminoma with syncytiotrophoblastic elements, with no other germ cell tumor components. hCG levels dropped sharply postoperatively. Given the diagnosis, the patient received adjuvant BEP chemotherapy. Three cycles were completed in December 2021, with consistently low hCG levels throughout. Given the patient's age and reproductive plans, combined hormone replacement therapy was initiated in January 2022 with estrogen and progesterone.

The patient remains under surveillance and in complete remission. The hormone replacement therapy is ongoing. As of June 2025, her hCG level was negative, CT scan is without any pathology, and the patient has reproductive plans via assisted reproduction with donor oocyte.

Discussion

In GTN, histological confirmation is not always required to initiate chemotherapy, and hCG trends alone may justify treatment. However, in this case, reliance on biochemical markers led to a misdiagnosis. All administered chemotherapies (partially containing etoposide and cisplatin) temporarily lowered hCG levels due to the chemosensitivity of dysgerminoma. Paradoxically, this hindered proper diagnosis, as imaging failed to detect viable tumor tissue. Also, given that the patient received two cycles of EMA-PE chemotherapy prior the thoracoscopic lung resection in March 2021, this resulted in regression of the lesion, and the histopathological examination revealed only regressive tissue changes rather than the metastasis of dysgerminoma. Six months later, after PET/CT-guided thoracotomy, definitive histopathological material was obtained, leading to a radical change in management. Subsequent surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy resulted in a rapid and sustained decline in hCG levels.

When the histological examination of the lung resection specimen obtained during open thoracotomy revealed a metastasis of dysgerminoma, it marked a turning point that required a complete reassessment of the therapeutic strategy. At the same time, we requested the original ovarian resection specimen from the referring clinic for review.

At the referring institution, the initial histological diagnosis was interpreted as benign, since immunohistochemical analysis was not available at that time. However, immunohistochemistry is essential for establishing the diagnosis of dysgerminoma.9 On the second review, the slides revealed ovarian stroma with an infiltrative growth pattern of individual tumor cells, which raised suspicion for a germ cell neoplasm and prompted further immunohistochemical characterization.

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for SALL4, OCT3/4, and PLAP, all of which are general markers of germ cell tumors. Dysgerminoma is further characterized by CD117 expression; in some cases, D2-40 may also be useful,10 although it was not assessed in this case. CD30 negativity helped to distinguish the tumor from embryonal carcinoma, which also shows more pronounced cytological atypia. The absence of glypican-3 expression argued against a yolk sac tumor. Cytokeratin expression was negative or only focally positive, consistent with dysgerminoma and helpful in the differential diagnosis with other germ cell tumors.11 In addition, numerous syncytiotrophoblastic elements were observed, which likely accounted for the elevated hCG levels; however, most of these appeared degenerated or crushed (histopathological photo – Figure 4).Figure 4Dysgerminoma – interface between tumor and ovarian stroma with dissociation of tumor cells, H&E, 100 × and dysgerminoma - immunohistochemically positive for SALL4; border between solid tumor and infiltration into the surrounding ovarian stroma (00 ×)Figure 4 dummy alt textBabkova. The histological examination following definitive gynecological surgery confirmed the previous histopathological findings. Dysgerminoma was present bilaterally in the ovaries, containing elements of syncytiotrophoblast, with solid growth patterns and infiltration of the adjacent cortical ovarian stroma (Figure 4). Lymph nodes were free of metastasis, and both the omentum and the left fallopian tube were without evidence of tumor involvement.

About 5% to 15% of dysgerminomas contain syncytiotrophoblastic elements and produce hCG12,13; in such cases, hCG can serve as a reliable tumor marker during follow-up. The patient continues to be monitored. Given the histology, R0 resection, and completed adjuvant therapy, her prognosis is very favorable.

Given the young age of the patient, fertility preservation remains an important consideration in management. However, the safety of ovarian stimulation in patients with active malignancy, particularly in cases with bilateral ovarian involvement, remains uncertain. Although ovarian stimulation followed by oocyte or embryo cryopreservation is considered an established strategy in selected oncologic patients, its oncological safety has not been sufficiently established across all tumor types and clinical scenarios. Therefore, such approaches should be carefully individualized and discussed within a multidisciplinary team, also taking into account the psychological impact.14, 15, 16

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients with low but persistent hCG levels. Although gestational trophoblastic neoplasia is often the first suspected entity in such scenarios, clinicians must also consider the possibility of hormone-secreting ovarian tumors, including dysgerminomas with syncytiotrophoblastic elements. In our patient, the incorrect initial diagnosis led to a cascade of inappropriate therapeutic decisions and significant diagnostic delay. Partial responses to GTN-directed chemotherapy further masked the true nature of the disease. Ultimately, histopathological confirmation from a resected metastatic lesion shifted the clinical course toward appropriate surgical and adjuvant management. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of relying solely on biochemical markers in the absence of histological confirmation and emphasizes the necessity of revisiting the diagnosis when clinical evolution deviates from expectations.
