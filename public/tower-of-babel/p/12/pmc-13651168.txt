Contemporary outcomes of en bloc stapling of the pulmonary artery, bronchus, and lymph nodes during lobectomy for non-small cell lung cancer: a retrospective study
Authors: Keiji Yamanashi, Ryo Miyata, Takeshi Kawaguchi, Masatsugu Hamaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the safety and postoperative outcomes of en bloc stapling of the pulmonary artery branches, bronchial branches, and lymph nodes using an auto-stapler during lobectomy for non-small cell lung cancer.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted to identify consecutive patients who underwent lobectomy for non-small cell lung cancer with en bloc stapling of the pulmonary artery branches, bronchial branches, and lymph nodes using an auto-stapler between 2019 and 2024. Background

Thoracic surgeons occasionally encounter dense adhesions of non-metastatic lymph nodes (LNs) involving the pulmonary artery (PA) and bronchus during lung cancer surgery [1, 2]. These adhesions are thought to result from chronic inflammation and fibrotic tissue formation around the hilar structures, making dissection difficult [3]. Pulmonary arterioplasty and/or bronchoplasty, and staged bronchial closure [4] may be required to achieve complete resection; however, these procedures can be technically challenging depending on the anatomical location of the LNs and tumor [5–8].

In these scenarios, en bloc stapling of the, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler has traditionally been employed [1, 2, 9]. However, to our knowledge, only one institution has reported on the safety and favorable long-term postoperative outcomes of this approach for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with most available data now considered outdated [9–11].

On the other hand, patients with NSCLC and hilar metastatic LNs may face a similar challenge. When complete nodal dissection from the PA and bronchus is not feasible, pneumonectomy may be required despite its substantial morbidity and mortality [12, 13]. Pulmonary arterioplasty and/or bronchoplasty for complete resection in such cases are technically demanding, and their oncological benefit remains uncertain because only a limited number of cases have been reported [5, 6, 14]. At our institution, lobectomy with en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler followed by additional therapy, is occasionally performed in patients who are unable to tolerate pneumonectomy. This strategy appears to follow the international guidelines for managing R1–2 resections [15, 16]. However, data regarding this strategy remain limited.

This study aimed to evaluate the safety and postoperative outcomes of en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler during lobectomy for NSCLC, in light of recent advancements in medical technology and lung cancer treatment.

Methods

Patients and data collection

A retrospective chart review was conducted to identify all consecutive patients who underwent surgical resection for NSCLC at Nara Medical University Hospital between January 2019 and December 2024. Patients were eligible if they underwent lobectomy with en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler. En bloc stapling was adopted only when separate hilar dissection and division were considered technically difficult or potentially hazardous based on intraoperative findings, at the discretion of the operating surgeon. Clinical staging was based on radiological findings at initial presentation, typically assessed using whole-body contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging, and 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET). If mediastinal LNs exhibited a short-axis diameter > 1 cm on CT or 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on PET-CT, endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration was the preferred method, with video-assisted mediastinoscopy as the second choice, following the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines [16]. During surgery, lobe-specific mediastinal LN dissection or sampling was routinely performed. Pathological staging was based on the 9th edition of the TNM classification [17]. Postoperative management was generally based on contemporary international guidelines [15, 16]. For patients who underwent R0 resection, adjuvant systemic therapy was administered when indicated and tolerated. For patients with R1–2 resection, postoperative radiotherapy was additionally considered in combination with systemic therapy. Treatment decisions were individualized based on pathological findings and patient condition.

The following clinical data were collected: age, sex, performance status, smoking history, dust exposure history, body mass index, pulmonary function at the time of the initial pulmonary resection, Charlson comorbidity index [18], details of pulmonary resection, histological and pathological stage of lung cancer, treatment for recurrence, and follow-up information. Postoperative surveillance typically included chest CT every 3–6 months, with brain magnetic resonance imaging performed only in symptomatic patients, in line with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and ESMO guidelines [15, 16]. No additional surveillance specifically for bronchovascular fistula was conducted. Patients who experienced recurrence and received treatment were followed up at similar 3–6-month intervals. The follow-up data were censored on December 10, 2025.

This study was approved by the Nara Medical University Institutional Review Board (March 31, 2025; reference number: 3945). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test, whereas continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The median follow-up time was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method for potential follow-up [19]. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause or to the last known date the patient was alive. OS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between survival curves were assessed using a two-tailed log-rank test. Data were analyzed using the R software (version 3.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All P-values are two-sided, with values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and survival outcomes

In this study, 808 consecutive patients who underwent surgical resection for NSCLC were reviewed, and 18 (2.2%) who underwent lobectomy with en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler were included in the analysis (Table 1). No bronchoscopic pigmentation suggestive of calcified or dense hilar LNs was documented in the medical records of the patients. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Tables S1, S2, and S3. None of the patients received any neoadjuvant therapy. Of the 18 patients, 13 achieved R0 resection, one patient underwent lobectomy with R1 resection of metastatic LNs unexpectedly, and four patients underwent lobectomy with R2 resection due to invasive metastatic LNs involving the PA and bronchus. Among patients who underwent R2 resection, pneumonectomy was deemed unfeasible owing to the following reasons: low forced expiratory volume in 1 s (< 2.0 L) in two patients according to international guidelines [20, 21], poor cardiac function in one patient, and multiple comorbidities in one patient. Four patients had driver gene alterations, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exon 19 deletion (n = 1) and EGFR L858R mutation (n = 3). Among patients with pathological stage ≥ IIB, adjuvant therapy was administered to four; however, it was withheld in one patient owing to disease progression and in four patients following a comprehensive assessment of their condition, performance status, and social circumstances.

A representative case

A 78-year-old woman was referred to our department after a chest CT scan incidentally revealed a 7-mm part-solid nodule in the left upper lobe. Over three years, the nodule size increased to 12 mm, with a consolidation-to-tumor ratio > 0.5 (Fig. 3a). The patient declined PET-CT due to claustrophobia. Because the lesion was suspected to be a stage IA1 NSCLC, a left upper lobectomy was planned, considering the location of the nodule and the patient’s preoperative cardiopulmonary function assessment.

Intraoperatively, multiple LNs were found to be firmly adherent to the left upper division PA branches and bronchus. Because dissection of these structures carried a high risk of vascular injury, conversion to thoracotomy was performed. Temporary occlusion of the left main PA resulted in severe hypotension, indicating intolerance to unilateral PA occlusion. Therefore, the left upper division PA branches, bronchus (B1–3), and adherent LNs were simultaneously stapled en bloc using ECHELON FLEX™ GST staplers (Ethicon, Inc.), as shown in Supplementary Video S1. The staple lines were intact, with no evidence of bleeding (Fig. 3b). Finally, the stump was covered with free pericardial fat pad. The operation lasted 3 h and 50 min, with an estimated blood loss of less than 10 mL.

Histopathological examination revealed adenocarcinoma in situ without LN metastasis. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 6. No evidence of fistula formation between the PA and the bronchial stump was observed during the follow-up period. Although the bronchial stump remained stapled, the left upper divisions of the PA branches were separated from it at both the 6- and 12-month follow-ups (Fig. 3c and d).

Discussion

In the late 1990s, Lewis et al. first reported the safety and long-term postoperative outcomes of lobectomy for NSCLC using en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs with an auto-stapler [9]. Since then, evidence regarding this technique has remained limited, with only two small case series reporting short-term outcomes [2, 22]. Table 3 summarizes the findings of these studies, none of which reported procedure-related complications such as bronchovascular fistula. In addition, previous studies have recommended the use of green or black cartridges for en bloc stapling because of the substantial tissue thickness involved [2, 22]. Consistent with these reports, black cartridges were used in all patients in the present study (Supplementary Tables S2).

In a previous swine model study, the PA and bronchial stumps created by en bloc stapling were observed to separate six weeks after surgery [1]. Notably, while the bronchial stump remained stapled, the stapled tissue of the PA disappeared, and a new vascular stump was formed, which histologically resembled the PA stump after individual ligation [23]. This process may promote separation of the PA and bronchial stumps, potentially lowering the risk of bronchovascular fistula. In our representative case, this separation was confirmed by contrast-enhanced CT. As in previous studies [1, 2, 9], no patient in our study experienced procedure-related complications, suggesting that en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler may be safe in selected patients, likely because the PA and bronchial stumps separate during the postoperative healing process.

Several international guidelines provide specific recommendations for managing R1–2 resections, suggesting reoperation if the residual disease is resectable, postoperative radiotherapy, or adjuvant chemotherapy/chemoradiotherapy regardless of nodal status [15, 16]. In this study, four patients with metastatic hilar LNs were deemed unsuitable candidates for pneumonectomy and complete resection of these LNs was technically challenging. As a result, lobectomy with en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs was performed, followed by postoperative non-surgical treatment based on a comprehensive assessment of each patient’s clinical condition. This strategy appears to follow the international guidelines for managing incomplete resection [15, 16]. Although the five-year OS rate of 50.0% in patients with incomplete resection appeared higher than rates reported in National Cancer Database studies from the 2010s (28.8–33.8%) [24, 25], possibly reflecting recent advances in lung cancer treatment, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [26] and immune checkpoint inhibitors [27], these findings should be interpreted with caution. The apparently favorable survival observed in the R1–2 subgroup compared with the R0 subgroup may reflect the small sample size and potential selection bias and should therefore be regarded as exploratory.

The increasing use of neoadjuvant targeted therapy and immunotherapy [28–30] may further influence the role of en bloc stapling in thoracic surgery. Although these treatments may improve resectability and reduce the need for incomplete resection in selected patients, treatment-related fibrosis and hilar adhesions may increase the technical complexity of bronchovascular dissection. Because no patients in the present study received neoadjuvant therapy, the impact of modern perioperative treatments on the feasibility and safety of en bloc stapling could not be evaluated and warrants further investigation.

One potential benefit of R1–2 resection is the acquisition of sufficient histological material, which enables accurate genetic profiling to guide targeted therapy. Specifically, the adequacy of surgical specimens for assessing EGFR mutations, anaplastic lymphoma kinase rearrangement, ROS1 rearrangement, and PD-L1 expression (98.2%, 100%, 95.7%, and 97.8%, respectively) was higher than that of small biopsy specimens (87.5%, 94.7%, 81.8%, and 92.2%, respectively) [31]. Another possible benefit is tumor volume reduction (debulking surgery), which has long been the standard of care for selected patients with ovarian cancer [32, 33], renal cell carcinoma [34, 35], and thymic epithelial tumors [36–38]; however, only a few studies have shown promising outcomes in NSCLC [39, 40]. Recently, a randomized phase III study was conducted to compare systemic therapy with and without debulking surgery (primary tumor resection) in patients with advanced NSCLC [41], and its long-term results are awaited. Although the small number of patients in this study precludes drawing definitive conclusions regarding lobectomy with incomplete resection of metastatic LNs around the PA and bronchus, two patients receiving multidisciplinary treatment, including EGFR-TKIs, are alive without signs of disease progression. These findings suggest that surgery may contribute to the management of NSCLC with adherent metastatic LNs in selected patients.

Importantly, the present study should not be interpreted as advocating routine en bloc stapling of hilar structures during lobectomy. Separate dissection and division of the pulmonary vessels and bronchus remain the standard surgical approach whenever technically feasible. In our practice, en bloc stapling was reserved for situations in which separate hilar dissection was considered difficult or potentially hazardous based on intraoperative findings. Therefore, our findings support en bloc stapling as a feasible bailout option in selected circumstances rather than a routine surgical strategy. Similar technical challenges may arise during completion lobectomy, where dense hilar adhesions can complicate separate bronchovascular dissection [42]. Although completion lobectomy was not evaluated in the present study, en bloc stapling may have potential applicability in such situations.

This study has some limitations. First, the study design was retrospective and non-randomized. Second, we were unable to identify an appropriate control group because en bloc stapling was performed only in highly selected patients as a bailout technique, and five of the 18 patients underwent incomplete resection. Therefore, direct comparison with standard lobectomy, sleeve resection, or pneumonectomy was not feasible. Third, the decision to perform en bloc stapling was based on intraoperative findings and surgeon judgment, which may limit the reproducibility of patient selection across institutions. Despite these limitations, this study reports contemporary outcomes of en bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs during lobectomy for NSCLC in a real-world setting, which may be difficult to obtain through clinical trials.

Conclusions

En bloc stapling of the PA branches, bronchial branches, and LNs using an auto-stapler during lobectomy for NSCLC may represent a feasible bailout option in selected patients in whom separate hilar dissection and division are technically difficult or potentially hazardous. Even in cases involving LN metastases near the stapling site, multimodal therapy guided by genetic profiling of surgical specimens may contribute to acceptable long-term outcomes in carefully selected patients.
