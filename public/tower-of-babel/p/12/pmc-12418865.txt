AutoML identification of microRNA biomarkers in high-risk pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Ioannis Kyriakidis, Zacharias Papadovasilakis, Georgios Papoutsoglou, Iordanis Pelagiadis, Helen A Papadaki, Charalampos Pontikoglou, Eftichia Stiakaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite significant advancements in overall survival rates for childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), relapse continues to pose a major challenge. MicroRNAs have proven valuable for improving diagnosis, treatment, and survival outcomes, establishing themselves as key biomarkers. Using RNA-seq data from 123 ALL patients and employing predictive modeling via automated machine learning (AutoML) alongside causal-inspired biomarker discovery, we identified highly predictive microRNA signatures linked to high-risk strata and clinical features in unfavorable cases. We further identified predictive signatures for each genetic subtype of childhood ALL, highlighting shared miRNAs throughout the study. A thorough literature review of the relationships between miRNA differential expression and key high-risk features in childhood ALL [immunophenotype, elevated white blood cell counts at diagnosis, central nervous system involvement, measurable residual disease (MRD), and chemoresistance] confirmed the signatures generated in this study. Our results revealed a highly predictive signature distinguishing B- and T-ALL, associated with apoptosis, confirming the reported difference between the two immunophenotypes. Additionally, miR-223 emerged as crucial for high-risk stratification and chemoresistant MRD-positive cases. These findings demonstrate the potential of AutoML tools to reveal novel biological insights in pediatric ALL, driving future advancements.Keywords: microRNA, Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Childhood, Adolescence, Prognosis, risk assessment

Introduction

Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most prevalent cancer in the pediatric population, and advancements in treatment protocols during the last decades have significantly improved overall survival, with current rates exceeding 90 %. This success is mainly due to the improved risk stratification, which tailors treatment intensity based on the patient's specific risk factors, thus optimizing outcomes while minimizing toxicity. Despite these advancements, relapse remains a critical issue, affecting about 15–20 % of patients and often linked with poorer prognosis. Moreover, survivors of childhood ALL frequently encounter long-term health issues resulting from both the disease and its treatment, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular problems, cognitive impairment, and second malignant neoplasms -among others. In the same context, health-related complications, along with the psychological and social impacts on these young patients, further underscore the need for comprehensive, multidisciplinary care strategies to address the immediate and long-term challenges associated with childhood ALL [1,2].

MicroRNAs (or miRNAs or miRs) are small non-coding RNA molecules of approximately 22 nucleotides in length that play a crucial role in regulating gene expression by targeting messenger RNAs (mRNAs) for degradation or translational repression [3]. In normal hematopoiesis, miRNAs are essential for maintaining the balance between self-renewal and differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells, thus ensuring the proper development of the blood cell lineages. Specific miRNAs are involved in the expression regulation of critical genes for the proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis of hematopoietic cells, ensuring the orderly production of platelets and red and white blood cells [4,5]. In childhood ALL, miRNAs have been found to play significant roles in the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and progression of the disease. Aberrant expression of specific miRNAs can disrupt normal hemopoietic processes, leading to leukemogenesis. For instance, miRNAs can act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors, and their dysregulation can contribute to the uncontrolled proliferation and survival of leukemic cells [[6], [7], [8]]. Polymorphisms in miRNA genes and miRNA-processing genes have also been associated with susceptibility to childhood ALL [[8], [9], [10]]. In diagnosis, specific miRNA expression profiles can serve as biomarkers for ALL, helping to distinguish between different subtypes and predict patient outcomes [8]. Moreover, miRNA signatures have been linked to relapse, as changes in their expression can reflect the presence of measurable residual disease (MRD) or the emergence of treatment-resistant leukemic clones [7]. MiRNAs are being explored as potential therapeutic targets and prognostic tools in childhood ALL, offering insights into drug resistance mechanisms and novel treatment approaches. Their study holds promise for enhancing diagnosis, treatment, and survival outcomes, making them key players in personalized medicine for ALL [11,12].

Risk stratification in childhood ALL corresponds to relapse risk and, consequently, to outcome. It involves several patient- and leukemia-related features that have been established as significant predictors. These commonly refer to age, white blood cell (WBC) count, and central nervous system (CNS) involvement at diagnosis, immunophenotype (B- vs. T-cell), cytogenetics, ploidy, molecular defects, and MRD at the end of the induction regimen. Altogether, these variables offer risk-based therapy, improving survival rates and preventing excessive toxicities [2,13]. Notably, modern treatment protocols present a decreased ALL-associated excess risk of death, and survivors display a mortality rate at 6 years after diagnosis similar to that of the general population [14].

This study aims to investigate the microRNA signatures in childhood ALL cases with unfavorable genetic traits and to identify miRNAs implicated in high-risk strata and clinical features. In addition, a review of all published studies on the interplay between microRNA levels and high-risk traits explores the responsible underlying mechanisms.

Methods

The data

The study investigated bone marrow RNA-seq data from 123 children and adolescents with ALL. The Data Access Committee of St. Jude Children's Research Hospital granted access to the EGAD00001007530 dataset, namely St. Jude Total XVI (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT00549848). Raw sequencing data (RNA reads in “.bam” format) and their supplementary information on samples used were acquired from EGA. BAM files were sorted with “samtools”, and raw reads were counted using “htseq-count,” while mapping to known RNA molecules was performed with version "v44lift37" of the Ensembl GTF file. Subsequently, all miRNA information (gene names, IDs, etc.) was retrieved from the HGNC (HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee at the University of Cambridge; https://www.genenames.org/; accessed on July 1, 2025) and mapped to the measured quantities.

Preprocessing

In this study, we performed four different predictive analyses. Each analysis employs the same predictor (independent) variables, i.e., the raw miRNA measured quantities, which were preprocessed (normalized) using the trimmed-mean normalization method. Regarding the outcome variable (target or dependent variable) that we wish to build the predictive model on, four different approaches were followed.

AutoML pipeline

To produce robust diagnostic models, we employed automated machine-learning techniques to analyze the predictive-analytic scenarios mentioned above. JADBio (https://jadbio.com/; accessed July 1, 2025) [17] is an autoML framework that operates as follows: a rule-based decision support system defines and applies all suitable algorithms based on the size, type, and characteristics of the data at hand, alongside user-defined preferences. Next, depending on the sample size and the distribution of the target variable, an appropriate partitioning protocol for evaluation (e.g., k-fold cross-validation, holdout) is decided. Then, JADBio explores the space of potential machine learning pipelines through static and dynamic strategies (such as grid search and Bayesian optimization), evaluating hundreds of combinations involving preprocessing, imputation of missing data, feature selection, and modeling algorithms. For feature selection, advanced methods like LASSO and proprietary techniques such as the Statistically Equivalent Signature (SES) and gamma-Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (γ-OMP) are employed [[18], [19], [20]].

Standard practices in bioinformatics for selecting and filtering associated biomarkers -such as differential expression analysis in RNA-seq datasets, which identifies key genes among tens of thousands, or summary statistics in genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which associate single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with phenotypes across millions of loci-typically rely on the computation of p-values or similar ranking scores to evaluate the importance of each biomarker independently in a univariate framework [21,22]. While computationally efficient, these approaches overlook potential interactions and correlations between biomarkers, often missing complex biological relationships. In contrast, multivariate methods, such as those employed by SES, assess the importance of biomarkers collectively, facilitating the detection of intricate mechanisms and the identification of biomarkers that jointly influence phenotypic variation. Although multivariate approaches generally require larger sample sizes than univariate methods, SES has demonstrated notable performance even with sample sizes as small as 100 patients [17]. Specifically, the SES algorithm is ideal for biomarker discovery because it employs statistical techniques directly linked to the underlying causal relationships (Markov blanket identification) between the data and the target variable, thereby identifying a minimal set of features that optimally describe the target feature's distribution. SES, in each iteration, selects the next feature to add in signature by conditioning on the previously chosen features. This process ensures no redundancy in the final set, meaning each feature provides unique information for distinguishing between two states. Additionally, SES can produce multiple signatures by identifying statistically equivalent features, i.e., signatures with the same predictive performance but with alternative feature sets [19]. The process described above provides additional insights into features that are interchangeable: either by reducing costs through the use of a more favorable biomarker or by raising new questions about why these features have the same predictive power for a given problem (e.g., common gene targets). Depending on the type of the target variable (categorical or continuous), different algorithms are used, including classification or regression models such as ridge regression, Random Forests, Decision Trees, and Support Vector Machines. Methods such as Cox models or tree-based approaches (penalized Cox regression, survival decision trees, and random forests) are applied in survival analysis tasks. To avoid overestimating the final model's predictive performance due to selection bias, known as the "winner's curse", JADBio incorporates a proprietary Bootstrap Bias Correction CV (BBC-CV) algorithm [23], ensuring that predictive performance is not inflated, even for small sample sizes (<100), as demonstrated through extensive computational experiments across hundreds of datasets [24]. A well-estimated model performance, along with the corresponding confidence intervals (CIs), ensures trust in a model's true capabilities while quantifying uncertainty. Through BBC-CV, performance is slightly underestimated, and the model avoids optimistic bias caused by overfitting, thereby promoting better generalization. This is particularly important for establishing confidence in the performance of the final model when applied to new, unseen data, which is a critical aspect in the development of clinical predictive models. Ultimately, this strategy balances accuracy with reliability, resulting in more dependable and deployable machine learning solutions [23,24]. This procedure offers a clinician an estimate of the true distribution of the model's performance across, in principle, all comparable datasets. It enables the prediction and regulation of specific operational points of the model based on reported metrics such as Sensitivity, Specificity, and Precision –particularly when the objective is to minimize false negatives and avoid false “healthy” reports. An overview of the JADBio pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.

JADBio initially analyzes the dataset meta-features (characteristics of the data, such as sample size) and user preferences. Based on them, it selects the appropriate combinations of algorithms and hyperparameter values to try, to try, which form the configuration space to explore. It also determines the partitioning protocol (cross-validation) for estimating the performance of each configuration. Based on these decisions, JADBio prepares the list of all configurations (ML pipelines) to be evaluated. Next, it identifies the winning configurations using the determined partitioning protocol, i.e., by repeatedly applying each configuration on different partitions of the data (“Train” boxes) to train a model and estimating performance on the remaining data (out-of-sample predictions, “Validation” boxes). Finally, JADBio generates the final model and evaluates its performance. It combines all out-of-sample predictions made during cross-validation and feeds them into the BBC-CV algorithm. The final model is then built using all available data with the winning configuration [17].

Other resources

The DIANA-miTED Tissue Expression Database (http://www.microrna.gr/mited; accessed July 1, 2025) was utilized to investigate the expression of mature miRNAs in healthy tissues [25]. DIANA-miRPath v4.0 web server (http://www.microrna.gr/miRPathv4; accessed July 1, 2025) employs TarBase to select targets and was used to identify Gene Ontology (GO) biological process domains for selected Homo sapiens miRNAs (miRBase-v22.1 annotation) with merged p-values and FDR [26]. TarBase v9.0, a reference database dedicated to producing, curating, and delivering high-quality experimentally-supported miRNA targets on protein-coding transcripts (https://dianalab.e-ce.uth.gr/tarbasev9; accessed July 1, 2025) was used to identify combinatorial targeting with interactive graphs [27]. The validation and prediction of miRNAs associated with drug resistance and miRNAs targeted by drugs was conducted employing the ncRNADrug database (http://www.jianglab.cn/ncRNADrug/; accessed July 1, 2025) [28].

Results

Predictive performance

The final model's predictive performance was evaluated by recruiting the Area under the Receiving Operating Curve (AUC) metric. This metric is ideal for identifying the optimal predictive model, as it is threshold-independent and can produce multiple operating points when considering other threshold-dependent performance metrics such as precision or recall. For a binary classification problem, e.g., predicting whether a sample belongs to class Case or Control, AUC can be interpreted as the probability of correctly assigning the classes to a randomly selected Case-Control pair of samples: an AUC of 50 % suggests that the model is no better than a random assignment of classes. JADBio creates numerous configurations combining different algorithms in the steps of an autoML pipeline [preprocessing-feature selection (FS)-modeling] along with their varying hyper-parameters to identify the optimal predictive model. We opted for an extensive configuration space to be searched (∼3500 different configurations per analysis). The predictive performance of each analysis for two model families that JADBio offers are summarized as (i) the overall best-performing model and (ii) the best-performing model, given that a causal-based FS algorithm was used.

Identified signatures

Despite JADBio recruiting numerous algorithms for performing FS, like LASSO, univariate, or the JADBio-developed SES, we suggest reporting the identified signatures from each analysis only from configurations that employ the SES algorithm. SES belongs to a special FS algorithm family, where it tries to identify a minimal-sized feature subset while maximizing the predictive performance. As previously elaborated, the signatures returned by SES are highly associated with the data generation mechanisms, i.e., the miRNA targets that directly or indirectly affect the outcome of choice [19]. Furthermore, parsimonious models (those with minimal feature sets) will have a significant economic impact on developing affordable diagnostic kits, provided that the expected performance drop remains within a reasonable range. Discussion

Several studies have substantiated the theory that B- and T-ALL in childhood display distinct microRNA profiles. Twenty years after the first relevant report, there are limited data on the discrimination of T- and B-cell ALL based on miR levels, but there are plentiful publications on the signatures of each immunophenotype [11,29,30]. Literature review on the differential expression analysis of miRNAs in T- and B-ALL designated miR-196b as the top upregulated miRNA (followed by miR-542) and miR-708 as the top downregulated miRNA (followed by miR-151a, miR-425, and miR-497) in T-ALL cases [8]. Of note, miR-708 is a key negative regulator of CD47. This transmembrane protein inhibits phagocytosis in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and may serve as an attractive candidate for immunotherapy of T-ALL [31]. Supplementary Table 1 provides an updated and comprehensive listing of all miRNAs found significantly up- and down-regulated in childhood T-ALL cases compared to B-ALL cases. Our results support the prominent role of miR-548i-2 hairpin in discriminating T-from B-ALL cases in childhood and adolescence (92.8 % predictive performance), while miR-548i upregulation in T-ALL has been previously elaborated by two more studies [32,33]. Levels of miR-548i have been associated with early-stage colorectal cancer, basal cell carcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, but there are no studies on its role in lymphoid malignancies [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Moreover, the rs7683574 polymorphism in MIR548I2 has been implicated in lung cancer pathogenesis by a recent large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) [21]. Beyond cancer, the role of miR-548i has been explored in the context of immune response processes. One study showed that miRNA-548 regulates host antiviral response by targeting the type III interferon IFN-λ1 directly, while a preceding study found a compensatory elevated miR-548 expression resulting from an overactivated immune system response associated with dramatically increased IL-29 levels in persistent measles infection [38,39].

According to the miTED database, hsa-miR-548i expression in white blood cells obtained from healthy individuals is extremely low (0.080 RPM compared to 140256.140 RPM of hsa-miR-26a-5p), suggesting that its upregulation involves pathologic processes. The top-5 miRNAs that were capable of discriminating T-from B-ALL samples (hsa-miR-548i, hsa-miR-4752, hsa-miR-637, hsa-miR-631, and hsa-miR-1260b) were analyzed as regards their GO biological process domains (pathways union) and the GO terms “RNA splicing” and “mRNA processing” were found with the most significant contribution, followed by “viral process” and “hippo signaling”. Of interest, miR-1260b seemed to play a pivotal role in GO terms (Supplementary Table 2). Combinatorial targeting analysis of these top-5 miRNAs revealed significantly strong relationships with BCL2 and BNIP2 (encoding BCL2 Interacting Protein 2), both regulators of apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The role of apoptosis in childhood ALL has been long known and implicated in relapse and glucocorticoid resistance [40,41]. Moreover, the combinatorial analysis of miR-548i (ranked first in our study) and miR-708 (ranked first in the literature) pointed out a significant relationship with HIF1A (Supplementary Fig. 1B). HIF1A is a key regulator of the homeostatic response to hypoxia, resulting in the activation of transcription of genes involved in energy metabolism, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and other genes whose protein products increase oxygen delivery or facilitate metabolic adaptation to hypoxia, while it holds a significant role in chemoresistant ALL [42].

Numerous attempts have been made to identify high-risk miRNA signatures in childhood ALL [22]. Risk strata in our dataset were defined according to NCI criteria, age, and WBC upon diagnosis. Of course, additional risk factors differ among the available treatment protocols (MRD, molecular cytogenetics, CNS involvement, and immunophenotype), which will be discussed later in the text. Supplementary Table 3 refers to all relevant studies on dysregulated miRNAs in the pediatric population diagnosed with ALL and WBC ≥50,000/μL at diagnosis. The miRNA signature for HR cases displayed the lowest AUC with causal FS in this study compared with other analyses (Table 2; Fig. 2B). Among the 18 miRNAs comprising the signature of HR ALL, we will focus on the top-5: miR-4749, miR-4265, miR-4417, miR-298, and miR-1260b. The only recorded miRNA-gene interaction in TarBase for hsa-miR-4749-3p is with HIC1, encoding the Hypermethylated In Cancer Zinc Finger And BTB Domain-Containing Transcriptional Repressor 1. Notably, HIC1 hypermethylation is considered a late event in ALL (53 % at diagnosis and 100 % at relapse), suggesting that other inhibitory mechanisms, such as miRNAs, might lower HIC1 expression [43]. There are no registered interactions for miR-4265 and miR-4417. Regarding miR-298, the highest microT interaction score was recorded for DYRK1A (0.96) in TarBase, followed by CDKN1A (0.83), which encodes a regulator of cell cycle progression at G1 tightly controlled by p53. DYRK1A regulates B-ALL through phosphorylation of FOXO1 and STAT3, while its inhibition exhibited promising results in Down syndrome ALL [44,45]. Beyond its prominent role in pediatric T-ALL miRNA signature, miR-1260b also seems to play a significant role in discriminating HR cases according to the NCI criteria alone and through its role in RNA splicing and mRNA processing (Supplementary Table 4). In addition, the expression of MIR181A1HG is included in the HR signature, although its contribution is limited and causes a minimal percentage drop in predictive performance when removed from the model (Supplementary Table 5). An integrated sequencing analysis of 1300 pediatric T-ALL cases recently delineated the significance of MIR181A1HG in the disease [46]. Focal deletions in MIR181A1HG are also prevalent among Ph+ and Ph-like subtypes, and they may affect lineage fate as this gene's expression decreases during myeloid differentiation in hematopoietic progenitor cells [16]. In addition, the role of miR-181a in childhood ALL involves escaping from immune surveillance, activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and constitution of an amplifying loop linking inflammation to cancer [8,47].

An integrative analysis of miRNA-mediated gene signatures and pathways modulating WBC in childhood ALL highlighted the significance of PI3/AKT, JAK/STAT, IL-17, TGF-β, apoptosis, IL-15, STAT3, IGF-1, FGF, mTOR, VEGF, NF-kB, and P53 signaling pathways [48]. Based on miRPath analysis of our results' generated 18-miRNA signature of HR cases, the prevailing GO terms referred to the positive regulation of transcription and the negative regulation of apoptosis processes (Supplementary Table 4). The literature review revealed several deregulated miRNAs in pediatric ALL cases with high WBC at diagnosis (Supplementary Table 3). Downregulation of miR-100 and miR-708 were the miRNAs with the most citations in this review to significantly associate with WBC at presentation. Hsa-miR-100 belongs to the miR-10 family (comprising eight members: miR-10a, miR-10b, miR-99a, miR-99b, miR-100, miR-125a, miR-125b-1, and miR-125b-2) and has been linked with the inhibition of cell proliferation and increased dexamethasone-induced cell apoptosis in ALL patients [49]. Downregulation of miR-100 seems to be a hallmark of ALL development in children, and its miRNA family not only influences glucocorticoid response but also affects the resistance to vincristine and daunorubicin [8,[49], [50], [51], [52]]. The role of miR-708 in T-cell ALL has been discussed above, but its role in HR pediatric ALL also involves predicting glucocorticoid response and relapse. Of note, FOXO3, a forehead transcription factor critical for hematopoietic stem cell self-renewal, which mediates the initial apoptotic response, contains conserved miR-708 response elements in its 3′ untranslated terminal region [53].

Only a few studies have aimed at recognizing specific miRNA signatures among patients with sentinel favorable and unfavorable risk genetics (FRG and URG, respectively). Our study adhered to the genetic risk classification cited in the manuscript from which the EGAD00001007530 dataset originated [16]. Apart from St. Jude Children's Research Hospital's classification of cytogenetics, we also tried to classify our dataset genetic subgroups according to a recent Children's Oncology Group (COG) report, but the results were less conclusive, presumably due to reduced sample size (n = 50 vs. 123 in our dataset) [13]. The signature of URG consisted of miR-4508, miR-320d-2, miR-637, miR-635, and miR-3159 (Fig. 2C). Combinatorial targeting analysis of these miRNAs in TarBase revealed a significant interaction with MYC proto-oncogene (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the same context, mounting evidence showed the pivotal role of the NOTCH1-MYC regulatory highway toward the initiation and progression of T-cell leukemogenesis [54]. Moreover, integrated analysis of miRNA-seq and mRNA-seq data in relapsed pediatric B-ALL cases acknowledged the central role of MYC [22]. Of interest is that miR-637 has also been implicated in the T-cell immunophenotype, while miR-320d-2 and miR-3159 have been linked with HR cases in this analysis. The role of miR-4508 in leukemia is largely unknown but has been associated with HR breast and bladder cancer in recent reports [55,56]. Various cancers have been associated with dysregulated miR-320d levels, but its role in leukemia warrants further investigation. It is known that myelodysplastic syndromes display increased miR-320d levels, exhibiting an independent prognostic value for the overall survival rates [57]. The expression of miR-320d was also associated with poor prognosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma via CDK6 (Cyclin Dependent Kinase 6), while upregulation of this miRNA seems to accurately discriminate acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cases from controls [58,59]. Notably, miR-320d seems to regulate chemoresistance in osteosarcoma by interacting with MCL1 apoptosis regulator (Myeloid Cell Leukemia Sequence 1 BCL2-Related) [60]. Regarding miRNA-637, two axes were found to play significant roles in chemoresistance: circ 0051886/miR-637/ABL1 and miR-637/LIF/STAT3, involving a well-described fusion partner in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and Leukemia Inhibitory Factor, respectively [61,62]. Imatinib-resistant CML has also been linked with miR-635 sponging, which results in upregulation of glutamine synthetase and mTOR signaling pathway [63]. Intriguingly, and although reports on miR-3159 in cancer are lacking, one recent study reported that both this miRNA and miR-635 exert regulatory effects on osteosarcoma cell proliferation and migration [64]. Furthermore, it is known that miR-3159 targets MYC, a driver of leukemogenesis, as described above [65]. The GO terms analysis of this URG miRNA signature indicated the regulation of transcription and apoptosis as the predominantly affected biological processes, followed by a marginally significant association with cellular response to DNA damage (Supplementary Table 6). Since URG demonstrate the highest hazard ratio among all the prognostic variables of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (age, WBC, and CNS status at diagnosis, and MRD), this 5-miRNA URG signature could result in a significant reduction of costs, if replicated [13].

We further aimed to identify miRNA signatures for each genetic subtype of childhood ALL (Fig. 2D). Multiclass classification with JADBio generated a signature of 40 miRNAs, which displayed high-performance metrics (Fig. 3). Of course, there was a significant overlap of key miRNAs in this analysis with the previous analyses due to overlapping features (immunophenotype and URG), but common miRNAs with HR cases were also noted (Fig. 4). The top-5 miRNA signature revealed significant contributions for miR-151b, miR-223, miR-548f-3, miR-133, and miR-5685. Upregulation of hsa-miR-151b has demonstrated a prognostic significance in AML, while its downregulation seems to discriminate T-from B-ALL cases in the literature [66,67]. Within the same miRNA family, high miR-151a levels have been linked with B-ALL, displaying higher levels in ETV6::RUNX1 and B-other cases but lower in KMT2A-r cases [8,68,69]. In the case of miR-223, case-control studies documented both upregulated and downregulated levels in childhood ALL samples [[70], [71], [72], [73]], but reports on its levels in specific childhood ALL subtypes have been more enlightening: (i) upregulated in infantile T-ALL compared to childhood T-ALL [74]; (ii) upregulated in KMT2A-r, TLX1, TLX3, STIL::TAL1, PICALM::MLLT10 alias CALM::AF10, and inv(7) cases compared to controls [70]; (iii) upregulated in hyperdiploid cases compared to other B-cell precursor (BCP) ALL cases [50,75]; and significantly downregulated in TCF3-r among BCP-ALL cases [75]. Moreover, miR-223 is actively involved in the proliferation of leukemic cells, affecting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and the miR-223-3p/FBXW7 axis –among others [[76], [77], [78]]. The literature does not substantiate the role of miR-548f-3 hairpin in leukemogenesis, but there are known interactions with TGF-β and IKZF1 [79]. As mentioned above, miR-548i can discriminate T-from B-ALL cases, while high miR-548am levels seem to distinguish KMT2A-r cases from B-ALL [80]. The miR-548 family members originate from primate-specific short miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements (MITEs) that form almost perfect palindromes. The evolutionary pattern of these miRNAs has been implicated in various cancers primarily due to dynamic expression profiles and through plentiful regulatory networks [81]. Notably, the contribution of miR-548f-3 hairpin in this signature was interchangeably equal to that of miR-185 (Table 1). The latter miRNA seems involved in both glucocorticoid-resistant ALL, infantile T-ALL, and BCR::ABL1-positive leukemia [74,82,83]. A pan-cancer analysis indicated a key role in miR-133a-1 hairpin downregulation, and whereas several reports supported a role in AML, publications on ALL are lacking [84]. Only one study outlined the significant overexpression of miR-133a in KMT2A-r cases compared to other BCP-ALL specimens [50]. According to miTED, miR-5685 levels in white blood cells are nearly indetectable, but its expression seems to differentiate T-from B-ALL cases –as shown above. The role of miR-5685 in ALL has been only supported by a bioinformatic report, while this miRNA has been correlated with microsatellite-unstable, CpG island methylator phenotype, and non-TP53-mutated colorectal cancer [85,86]. Other notable miRNAs included in the signature for subtype classification that exhibited high predictive performance percentage drop (>0.5 %) are miR-4265 and miR-1260, which have been implicated in HR cases and in discriminating T-from B-ALL cases, respectively.

Common miRNAs between the analyses of this study were anticipated mainly due to overlapping features for the immunophenotype, molecular subtype, and URG but not for the HR classification in this study, which was based solely on the NCI criteria (Fig. 3). Common miRNA signatures of HR, T-cell immunophenotype, and subtype were miR-1260b, a T-ALL discriminator, and miR-4437, which controls endothelial integrity through CXCR2 expression and triggers the migration of granulocytes and natural-killer cells [87]. As mentioned above in the text, miR-320d-2 and miR-3159 were common between HR and URG cases.

Our dataset did not incorporate data for CNS involvement and MRD, which confer high-risk stratification. CNS infiltration is associated with relapse and major therapeutic challenges, and the literature review indicated miR-181a as the most credible biomarker of CNS disease to date (Supplementary Table 7) [88]. On the contrary, miRNAs’ association with MRD has been extensively investigated (Supplementary Table 8). Due to the variability of the corresponding study designs, several reproducibility issues occurred. After combining this information with the three available Small RNA-seq studies in the literature, we have indications that MRD in childhood ALL seems to be associated with miR-21, miR-125a, miR-128, miR-143, miR-146a, miR-182, miR-210, miR-221, miR-223, miR-326, miR-335, miR-574, miR-708, miR-6734, and miR-6735 [22,71,89]. Five miRNAs were common in both B- and T-ALL studies: miR-21, miR-143, miR-210, miR-223, and miR-574. According to ncRNADrug, several miRNAs have been implicated in conferring chemoresistance in childhood ALL (Supplementary Table 9). Strikingly, miR-21 has been linked with prednisone, daunorubicin, and cyclophosphamide resistance, while miR-223 was associated with vincristine-resistant cases. The respective data point out the prominent contribution of many miR-10 family members (miR-99a, miR-100, miR-125a, miR-125b, and the clustered let-7c) -among others-in multidrug-resistant childhood ALL (Supplementary Fig. 3). Not surprisingly, most miRNAs associated with positive MRD have also been associated with resistance to several chemotherapy agents. MRD and HR miRNA signatures seem to bear significant differences, but conversely, miR-223 was the only common miRNA between the MRD-positive and the high-risk-stratification signatures, constituting a well-established miRNA in hematopoiesis, inflammation, and ALL [78].

Among the strengths of the present study are the high-performance status across all analyses (>80 %) and nearly 100 % maximum performance using the few most important features (in cases with 10+ identified features). Another strength trait of the study is the shared miRNA signature across different analyses, which mostly consists of well-described miRNAs. Of interest, a miRNA-seq study has also recently reported the enrichment of MYC targets and lower DICER1 expression in high-risk cases [9,22].

It is important to note that the identified signatures and the corresponding predictive models were generated in a pure data-driven manner: providing results from an unbiased, biology-agnostic methodology for the field expert to assess and validate. Specifically for the feature selection procedure (SES algorithm), it facilitates the development of faster and cheaper diagnostic kits through truly associated and minimal-sized identified miRNA targets, without sacrificing the prognostic value demanded by the clinician, while its causal-based feature selection can interpret and find application in the actual biological pathways.

A significant challenge in designing miRNA profiling studies for childhood leukemia is the risk of overrepresenting certain molecular subtypes, which may bias the expression signatures. This concern is particularly relevant given that the underlying biology of leukemic blasts exerts a strong influence on miRNA expression patterns [90]. A notable strength of the EGAD00001007530 dataset is its representation of molecular subtypes in proportions that closely mirror their real-world epidemiology in pediatric ALL (Table 2). Despite their regulatory significance, small RNAs such as microRNAs are often underrepresented in RNA-seq studies –either due to library preparation protocols that omit them by design or analytical workflows that deprioritize their quantification. To date, only four miRNA sequencing studies have been published in the context of pediatric ALL: three in T-cell ALL [89,91,92]and one in B-cell precursor ALL [22]. The present study is the first to analyze miRNA expression using RNA-seq data across the full molecular spectrum of childhood ALL and to systematically review the literature on miRNA signatures in distinct clinical and biological contexts, including immunophenotype, high initial white blood cell count, CNS involvement, and MRD positivity (Supplementary Tables 1, 3, 7, and 8). Previously published work from our laboratory has reported subtype-specific miRNA signatures in childhood ALL [8].

AutoML models can sometimes function as “black boxes,” offering limited interpretability of how specific miRNA signatures are linked to disease mechanisms –an outcome which was not applicable in our study. A limitation of the study is the small sample size in rare pediatric ALL subtypes (with <3 samples) and the inability to provide performance metrics for these specific classes. Of course, the readership should know that the dataset's quality and size can significantly impact the model's performance, as small or imbalanced datasets might result in overfitting or biased results. Other minor technical limitations are discussed in a relevant publication on the principles of JADBio [17].

A key limitation of the present study is the absence of survival endpoints in the analyzed dataset (EGAD00001007530), which was provided by St. Jude Children's Research Hospital. Although the accompanying publication integrates overall and relapse-free survival data [16], such outcomes were not included in the publicly deposited files, likely due to ethical or institutional data-sharing policies. This constraint precluded direct survival modeling; however, in childhood ALL, relapse risk serves as the primary determinant of adverse outcomes, given the comparatively high overall survival rates in contemporary treatment settings. Moreover, the occurrence of relapse imposes a significant clinical and psychosocial burden, rendering its prediction a clinically actionable endpoint in its own right, and justifying the focus on relapse-associated high-risk features [93,94].

Another limitation is the lack of an independent external validation cohort within the current analysis. This study serves as a pre-analytical phase for the ongoing MICRO-CALLS study (MicroRNA levels in Childhood Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia and Survival; https://osf.io/w6zsf/; accessed July 1, 2025), wherein a targeted panel of candidate miRNAs is being quantified at multiple treatment milestones—including diagnosis, end of induction, remission, and relapse—across a clinically annotated pediatric and adolescent ALL cohort. Preliminary findings from this longitudinal investigation appear to be consistent with the signatures identified herein, providing emerging validation while comprehensive reporting is still forthcoming. This analysis thus provides a biologically grounded, data-informed rationale for constructing a clinically translatable miRNA panel, thereby strengthening its eventual prognostic and diagnostic utility.

This research highlights the need for further validation to ensure that the findings can be applied in modern clinical settings, particularly with the introduction of immunotherapy for relapsed childhood ALL. The limited availability of miRNA-seq data in pediatric patients indicates a need for large-scale prospective studies to enhance prognostic models, which could help guide personalized treatment strategies. Moreover, the discovery of new genetic abnormalities through whole-genome sequencing suggests that combining transcriptome and genomic data could lead to more comprehensive diagnostic tools [95], ultimately improving patient outcomes and guiding more tailored therapies in children and adolescents with ALL.

Conclusion

JADBio provides useful automation and functionality for translational research, predictive modeling, and corresponding decision support. In this study, we identified a 14-miRNA signature that can predict T-cell immunophenotype with a nearly 100 % performance, along with other reliable signatures for URG, NCI HR stratum, and subtype classification. Expression of miR-223 seems to play a pivotal role in both high-risk stratification and MRD-positive/chemoresistant pediatric ALL cases.
