Choose Wisely: Parameter Choice is Key for Ensuring Consistent Estimates of Photosynthetic Capacity From A–C i Response Curves
Authors: Josef C Garen, Kristine Y Crous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary statementWe compared PhotoGEA, a new software tool for analysing gas exchange data, to a previous, widely used software tool. The two approaches had excellent agreement in values of photosynthetic capacity. Care and transparency are needed in parameter selection for gas exchange analysis.

Leaf‐level gas exchange measurements provide vital data for parameterising land surface models and predicting photosynthesis in a dynamic environment, including the effects of anthropogenic change on vegetation. Of particular interest are assimilation‐CO2 (A–C i ) responses because they quantify a plant's capacity to respond to a changing environment. Assimilation‐CO2 responses describe the effect of acute changes in CO2 concentration on instantaneous rates of photosynthesis. A–C i data are typically used to parameterise the Farquhar‐von Caemmerer‐Berry (FvCB) model of C3 net CO2 assimilation (Farquhar et al. 1980), a biochemical model widely used in process‐based models of vegetation function (Rogers et al. 2017). Practitioners are often interested in fitting the FvCB model to gas exchange data to extract parameters representing key rate‐limiting processes: Rubisco (ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) carboxylation capacity (V cmax), electron‐transport capacity (J max), and triose‐phosphate utilisation rate (T p ). In practice, however, fitting the FvCB model to A–C i data is computationally difficult. Many distinct approaches employing differing statistical techniques have been developed for this purpose, including Excel spreadsheets, R packages, SAS scripts, and online tools (e.g., Duursma 2015; Miao et al. 2009; Sharkey et al. 2007).

Recently, Lochocki et al. (2025) released PhotoGEA, a powerful new software tool which addresses many of the practical and theoretical challenges associated with analysing gas exchange data. This package offers many useful features, including native handling of raw data exported from gas exchange instruments, a critical—and previously absent—piece of data infrastructure. PhotoGEA differs from previous tools in its fitting approach, treating error in a non‐parametric fashion which improves estimates of confidence intervals, as well as improving the functional representation of T p relative to prior approaches. These are powerful tools, but with the introduction of a new package such as this, it is appropriate to ask whether photosynthetic capacity metrics estimated by this new software will be comparable to those obtained using previous approaches. In the paper, Lochocki et al. (2025) offer a comparison between PhotoGEA and several other software packages for estimating photosynthetic capacity metrics. While the reported photosynthetic capacity estimates appeared similar between the software packages tested, this comparison was performed on only a relatively small sample of greenhouse‐grown tobacco. Here, we aimed for a more comprehensive evaluation using a wide range of species and environmental conditions to closely examine the methodology and performance of two packages for A–C i curve fitting.

We compared the performance of PhotoGEA (v. 1.3.2) to a previous, widely used package, plantecophys (v. 1.4‐6; Duursma 2015), for fitting the FvCB model to A–C i data. We used a recent dataset of 520 A–C i curves collected using LI‐6800 instruments (LI‐COR Biosciences; Lincoln, NE, USA) on 22 species of Eucalyptus and Corymbia grown in controlled environments, at leaf temperatures spanning 15°C–40°C. Curves were collected using the dynamic assimilation technique (Saathoff and Welles 2021), with reference CO2 spanning from 50 to at least 1800 ppm at a saturating light intensity of 1800 µmol m−2 s−1 and airflow rate of 600 µmol s−1. We fit the FvCB model to each A–C i curve with each package and extracted V cmax, J max, and T p at measured leaf temperature; fitting details and parameters are given in Table S1. Estimates were compared using ordinary least‐squares linear regression. When plantecophys fails to estimate J max, it may report unreasonably large values (i.e., J max > 10 000 µmol m−2 s−1). These were excluded from the regression analysis (n = 29) but interpreted as failure to fit J max (and therefore included) in the comparison of limiting rates below.

Our comparison yielded excellent agreement between the two packages in two of the three metrics (Figure 1). For V cmax, relating estimates made with each package yielded a linear relationship with nearly perfect correlation and minimal bias between methods (Figure 1A; r 2 = 0.994, slope indistinguishable from 1, p = 0.83; intercept = 0.84 µmol m−2 s−1, p = 0.049). T p similarly exhibited nearly perfect correlation (Figure 1B; r 2 = 0.989). This relationship exhibited a slope of 1.02 (p = 10−5) and an intercept of −0.50 µmol m−2 s−1 (p < 10−5), suggesting a small bias between the methods. Comparing estimates of J max revealed a nonlinear relationship, in which PhotoGEA underestimates J max relative to plantecophys especially at high values of J max (Figure 1C). At the highest values of J max, estimates differed by as much as 275 µmol m−2 s−1 (43%).

In (C), α = 0.2930375 and Θ = 0.97875 for PhotoGEA (at 25°C) and α = 0.24 and Θ = 0.85 for plantecophys. In (D), α = 0.24 and Θ = 0.85 for both packages. Points indicate photosynthetic capacity estimates for individual A–C i curves (n = 520). Dotted lines are 1:1; solid lines are ordinary least squares regressions.

This difference can be understood by noting that J max is not derived directly from fitting the FvCB model to the A–C i curve. Rather, J (rate of electron transport) is fit directly to the data, and J max is then calculated from J using assumptions about the shape of the light response curve. This is typically assumed to be a non‐rectangular hyperbola with parameters α and Θ, which describe the initial slope and convexity of the light response curve, respectively (von Caemmerer 2000). PhotoGEA and plantecophys by default use different parameter sets (for PhotoGEA, α = 0.2930375, Θ = 0.97875 at 25°C; for plantecophys, α = 0.24, Θ = 0.85), resulting in different estimates of J max, despite nearly identical estimates for J (data not shown). When the plantecophys parameters are assumed, the highest values of J observed are entering the asymptotic portion of the light response curve; in contrast, when using the PhotoGEA parameters, those same J values remain in the linear portion of the light response curve. This difference causes the nonlinear deviation between the two estimates (Figure 1C). However, by manually setting these parameters to the same values (α = 0.24, Θ = 0.85) in each package, the discrepancy between J max estimates is resolved (Figure 1D). With the common set of parameters, the correlation between these estimates is again nearly perfect, and only a slight bias remains (r 2 = 0.993; slope = 1.03, p < 10−5; intercept = −2.3 µmol m−2 s−1, p = 0.001). Finally, we examined whether the packages agreed on which limitations are present in each A–C i curve. In more than 99% of curves, both packages identified a V cmax (PhotoGEA did not find V cmax in four curves). For J max, there was 94% agreement (J max was estimated by both packages in 482 curves and by neither package in six curves). Finally, for T p , the packages agreed in 96% of cases (T p was found in 440 curves by both packages, and by neither package in 59 curves). Overall agreement in both identification of limiting rates and capacity metrics was excellent; slight deviations between the two packages were observed, which may be due to differences in the representation of reverse CO2 sensitivity and T p , which differs substantially between the two packages (Duursma 2015; Lochocki et al. 2025).

When properly compared, there is excellent agreement between PhotoGEA and plantecophys in both determination of which limiting rates are present in a given A–C i curve and in the estimates of photosynthetic capacity metrics. However, a critical caveat is that the consistency of these estimates depends on parameter selection, especially for J max. In this comparison, the two packages initially differed in their default values of α and Θ, resulting in differences in J max. However, other parameters are also required to fit the FvCB model to gas exchange data. For example, estimating V cmax requires Michaelis‐Menten constants for CO2 and O2 (K c and K o ) and the CO2 compensation point in the absence of non‐photorespiratory CO2 release (Γ*), which are typically—but not necessarily—computed from the temperature response functions reported by Bernacchi et al. (2001). For greater transparency and repeatability in gas exchange analysis, we suggest that practitioners should report the parameter sets used when estimating photosynthetic capacity metrics. Simply reporting the package used or that “default” parameter sets were employed may be insufficient to ensure replicability of analyses in the future, as new software versions may change default parameter sets or fitting routines. This is increasingly important as premade software packages become widespread tools used by physiologists to help manage the demands of data analysis. Practitioners should always exercise due caution before adopting new tools, and maintaining transparency and comparability with legacy datasets should be a priority to facilitate future comparisons and data syntheses. Lochocki et al. (2025) have added an impressive new software package to the toolkit of plant physiologists; further validation of this software and fitting method using steady‐state gas exchange data and data collected from a wider range of species would be a valuable future contribution.
