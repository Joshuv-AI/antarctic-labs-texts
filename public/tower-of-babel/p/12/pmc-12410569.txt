ABSTRACT
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have become a critical focus in robotics research, particularly in the development of autonomous navigation and target-tracking systems. This journal article provides an overview of a multi-year IEEE-hosted drone competition designed to advance UAV autonomy in complex environments. The competition consisted of two primary challenges. The first competition hosted was the Rover Chase Challenge. In this competition a UAV was tasked with autonomously tracking and following a ground rover as it maneuvers through an obstacle-filled environment. The drone relied on onboard sensors such as cameras and LiDAR to estimate the rover’s trajectory and adjust its flight path accordingly. The second competition hosted was the Maze Navigation Challenge. In this challenge, the UAV navigated through a structured maze using LiDAR-based environment mapping and obstacle avoidance, without relying on external positioning systems such as GPS. Developing robust autonomous drone algorithms for such tasks requires extensive data collection, simulation, and testing, which can be costly and time-intensive. To address this, competitors completed this competition using a PX4-Gazebo based simulator. This dataset includes sensor data recorded in rosbag format, comprising LiDAR, IMU, GPS, and other telemetry readings. This dataset enables researchers to benchmark algorithms, conduct reproducible experiments, and develop robust UAV autonomy, perception, and GPS-denied navigation systems in both simulated and real-world contexts.

Background

Recently, datasets have emerged to facilitate the development and evaluation of drone autonomy, focusing on tasks such as target tracking, obstacle avoidance, and environment mapping. Publicly available datasets, including the TUM RGB-D dataset [1], the EuRoC MAV dataset [2], and various open-source SLAM benchmarks, have provided high-quality sensor data and ground truth for tasks ranging from visual-inertial odometry to object detection and trajectory estimation [[3], [4], [5]]. In contrast to the aforementioned datasets, the UAS Competition Dataset captures more realistic and challenging conditions. Notably, the Rover Chase scenario introduces real-time adaptation to dynamic ground targets in cluttered scenes—an aspect largely absent from prior datasets. Similarly, the Maze Navigation challenge enforces reliance on LiDAR-only sensing in constrained GPS-denied environments, pushing the boundaries of SLAM and obstacle-avoidance algorithms. These elements provide novel benchmarking opportunities and complement existing SLAM and tracking datasets by introducing a level of operational complexity and sensor usage not previously captured. These have played a pivotal role in advancing perception algorithms, offering scenarios with varying complexity and sensor modalities [[6], [7], [8]]. However, most of these datasets address static or open-space environments, limiting their applicability to more complex, dynamic, and constrained tasks [9,10]. In contrast, the IEEE Rover Chase dataset highlights specialized conditions that are not fully captured by existing public datasets. While previous datasets and benchmarks often focus on stable targets and unobstructed navigation paths, the Rover Chase Challenge introduces a dynamic ground-based target and cluttered surroundings, requiring drones to continuously adapt their tracking strategies [11,12]. Despite the existence of tracking-oriented datasets, few match the operational complexity and sensor alignment needs of the Rover Chase scenario. By addressing these gaps, the datasets stemming from the Rover Chase and Maze Navigation challenges complement and extend the landscape of drone autonomy resources [[13], [14], [15]].

Experimental design, materials and methods

Maze navigation challenge structure

• Dimensions: Both the simulated and real-world mazes shared the same dimensions of 9′ x 9′, ensuring participants could design their algorithms with scalability in mind.

• Structure: Each maze wall was constructed using PVC pipes and packing paper arranged vertically, with 15–20 cm gaps between each section. This design added complexity to LiDAR-based navigation by creating intermittent surfaces and gaps. These design elements were incorporated in the simulation maze design to encourage the development of robust navigation algorithms capable of generalizing to physical environments

• Simulator Access: The complete simulator codebase, including all software dependencies, configuration files, and launch instructions for the Maze Navigation Challenge, is available on GitHub at https://github.com/ksommerkohrt/ieee-2024-px4-gz-docker

Maze Navigation Challenge Simulation Software Architecture

To ensure modularity and extensibility, the simulation system was built around a modular controller architecture integrated with external components (see Fig. 1). Limitations

While synthetic noise was introduced to the Gazebo LiDAR model, a gap remains between the simulated sensor behavior and that of real-world LiDAR hardware. Real sensors are affected by factors such as temperature variation, multi-path reflections, and material-dependent reflectivity, which are not fully replicated in simulation. As a result, algorithms developed using this dataset may require additional calibration and validation when transferred to physical platforms. To mitigate potential biases stemming from overfitting to the Gazebo physics engine, future work will focus on introducing greater variability in simulation parameters, including randomized textures, lighting conditions, and more complex environmental dynamics that better reflect real-world uncertainties. This will encourage the development of algorithms that are more generalizable and less reliant on specific simulation characteristics.

Future iterations of this dataset will aim to incorporate real-world LiDAR data to bridge the simulation-to-reality gap. This will involve deploying drones equipped with high-fidelity LiDAR sensors in physical maze and tracking environments. Additionally, exploring the integration of other sensor modalities to capture motion capture data or radar data could further enrich the dataset, providing more diverse types of data for robust autonomy algorithm development in challenging conditions such as low visibility or high dynamic range scenarios.
