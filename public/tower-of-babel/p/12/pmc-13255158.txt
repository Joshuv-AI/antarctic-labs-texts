High‐Content Immunofluorescence Quantification of Transcription Factors: Application to pSMADs for BMP/TGFβ Signaling Pathways
Authors: Valia Khodr, Paul Machillot, Catherine Picart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh‐throughput immunofluorescence (IF) detection has become an essential tool in drug discovery for analyzing multiple markers and providing insights into cellular pathways. While IF techniques have been established for decades, high‐content IF protocols vary among laboratories, highlighting the need for standardized and quantitative workflows. In addition, recent technological advances allow single‐cell IF detection, which often offers a detailed perspective on cell population distribution within the sample. This protocol presents an optimized IF method for detecting and quantifying nuclear pSMAD signaling in adherent cells cultured on biomaterial substrates. Notable optimizations include fixation buffers, antibody concentrations, and an automated image analysis workflow. This process significantly reduces assay time, enables multiplex biomarker detection, and describes a method for overcoming the optical artifact observed with biomaterials. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Immunofluorescence (IF) has been a useful tool since its development in the mid‐20th century to detect intracellular organelles and proteins at a single‐cell level. The technique employs fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies to visualize molecules with precise location and to quantify them within cells (Ortiz Hidalgo, 2021). However, applying IF on biomaterials is often met with a series of challenges such as autofluorescence and reproducibility issues due to the variability in biomaterial composition (van de Lest et al., 1995).

Therefore, standardized and automated protocols allowing the performance of multiplex quantitative IF on biomaterials are necessary. Such protocols would optimize fixation, permeabilization, antibody concentration, and washing steps to enhance penetration and reduce non‐specific background. This protocol thus provides an optimized reproducible IF process designed to overcome biomaterial‐specific issues and to perform high‐throughput detection and quantification of nuclear pSMAD proteins on biomaterials.

IMMUNOFLUORESCENCE STAINING OF NUCLEAR pSMAD SIGNALING ON BIOMATERIALS AND ON GLASS SUBSTRATES

The aim of this technique is to perform high‐content detection of nuclear pSMAD signaling. The IF protocol has been specifically optimized for detecting nuclear biomarkers in adherent cells cultured on biomimetic films with varying stiffness. The preparation of biomimetic films has been previously described (Crouzier et al., 2009; Machillot et al., 2018). The films were loaded with bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). BMP‐responsive cells such as C2C12 mouse skeletal myoblasts and human periosteum–derived stem cells (hPDSCs) were used in this protocol (Sales et al., 2022). Immunofluorescence imaging of pSMAD and the nuclei was performed in cells cultured in 96‐well microplates, followed by quantitative image analysis. Cell adhesion and cell differentiation were then quantified as a function of the BMP type and concentration. This methodology allowed us to conduct simultaneous testing under a multitude of experimental conditions, using the same protocol. By doing so, we could make direct comparisons between the conditions. A schematic overview of the workflow is presented in Figure 1.

Schematic illustration of the immunofluorescence assay workflow done for cells cultured on biomaterials. The biomaterial is first prepared by depositing the layer‐by‐layer films directly at the bottom of the cell culture microplate (in this example, a 96‐well microplate). The cells are then seeded using common protocols and incubated for 24 h (or any other time frame). The cells are then imaged using a microscope, preferably a high‐content microscope. Each single well is imaged at different positions within the well, so that a high number of cells, typically more than 10,000 for a given experimental condition, can be analyzed in an automated manner. Commentary

Background Information

This protocol was developed to address the practical challenges of performing high‐throughput quantitative analysis of SMAD signaling under multiple experimental conditions. Our goal was to establish a workflow that allows comparison of pSMAD 1/5/9 and pSMAD 2/3 signaling pathways, on both biomaterials with varying stiffness and on glass, after stimulation with BMP and TGF‐β1, and exposure to drugs. Thus, we combined automated image acquisition with single‐cell analysis, which allowed us to quantify and process thousands of cells per experiment.

Reproducibility, standardization, and adaptability of the method

This standardized method is reproducible, as it was already applied in six research studies focused on three different cell types: C2C12 skeletal myoblasts, human periosteum–derived stem cells, mesenchymal stem cells, on endothelial cells, on various cancer cells, and on different supporting matrices (either layer‐by‐layer films or streptavidin‐based platforms) presenting different glycosaminoglycans, different matrix‐bound BMPs, and the ECM‐derived adhesive peptide RGD. Notably, pSMAS1/5/9 and pSMAD2/3 as well as other transcription factors are already studied and quantified.

Sales et al. (2022) used this method to assess pSMADs in C2C12 skeletal myoblasts cultured on PLL/HA films of controlled stiffness with matrix‐bound BMPs (BMP‐2, 4, 7, and 9). Owing to automation, Sefkow‐Werner et al. (2022) performed a proof of concept to study the effect of heparan sulfate on the bioactivity of four BMPs on myoblast cells (BMP‐2, 4, 6, and 7) in parallel, at five increasing concentrations. Rengaraj et al. (2022) studied the behaviors of two human pancreatic cell lines PANC1 (immortalized) and PAN092 (patient‐derived cell line) in response to film stiffness, presence of matrix‐bound fibronectin, and matrix‐bound BMPs (BMP‐2 and 4). Guevara Garcia et al. (2022) used it to analyze pSMAD using immuno‐fluorescence after receptor silencing.

Valat et al. (2023) used it to study the individual effect of each receptor (integrins and cadherins), using silencing RNA, on osteogenic transcription factors (pSMAD1,5,9, RunX2, and osterix). Furthermore, this protocol was used to screen drug effects on BMP and TGF‐β signaling pathways on biomaterials (Khodr et al., 2024). Regarding other cell types, Al Tabosh et al. (2024) used it to quantify pSMAD in endothelial cells from hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia and pulmonary arterial hypertension donors with ALK1 mutation. Arnaldos Perez et al. (2026) used it to assess pSMADs in mesenchymal stem cells derived from healthy or leukemic donors.

This method has also been cited in two recent review articles written by our team: Le Pennec et al. (2024) about glycosaminoglycans and Borges et al. (2024) regarding automation of biomaterial fabrication and of cell analysis on layer‐by‐layer films.

As this method relies on standard multichannel fluorescent imaging and open‐source analysis tools, it is adaptable at laboratories that can access a high‐content microscope, typically on any common microscopy service or platform. Although initially optimized for studying pSMAD signaling pathways, this approach of optimizing fixation, imaging, and automated single‐cell quantification can be further applied to other types of biomaterials, such as self‐assembled streptavidin platforms (Sefkow‐Werner et al., 2022), other cell types, such as cancer cells (Rengaraj et al., 2022) or different types of stem cells, and signaling proteins such as fibronectin (Rengaraj et al., 2022) or various BMPs. It could be applied to any other signaling pathways induced by matrix‐bound growth factors. In general, it provides a ready, flexible, and scalable method for performing reproducible, high‐throughput, single‐cell IF analysis.

Critical Parameters

Fixation buffer

In the present protocol, 4% formaldehyde was used as a fixation buffer. Although formaldehyde is a commonly used buffer in IF because it crosslinks cellular structures and preserves morphology, it is also known to cause autofluorescence, and its performance can vary depending on the readout (Zhang et al., 2022).

To ensure that our fixation step was compatible with the kinetic IF assay as previously described (Khodr et al., 2024), we evaluated alternative fixation buffers and compared their performance with formaldehyde. The tested solutions were 96% methanol and a citrate/acetone mixture (40 mM citrate with 60% acetone).

As fixation must be performed repeatedly at different time points, we considered the possibility of doing it on the same plate, considering compatibility with quantification, permeabilization necessity, and suitability for alkaline phosphatase staining, which is used as an osteogenesis marker. Overall, our results showed that PFA fixation on the same plate induced toxicity, limiting its usage for kinetic studies. The citrate/acetone mixture was incompatible with pSMAD staining, as it led to reduced signal intensities. In contrast, methanol fixation preserved pSMAD signaling and did not require a permeabilization step, although it impaired actin cytoskeleton staining. These optimizations indicate that both PFA and methanol can be used for pSmad analysis depending on the experimental goals.

Antibody incubation temperature and concentration

To ensure strong signal quality and minimize background noise, we compared two primary antibody incubation strategies: a short 1‐h incubation at room temperature and a longer incubation (overnight) at 4°C. Although both approaches produced reliable staining, the room‐temperature incubation generated a slightly higher nonspecific signal, suggesting that incubating overnight at 4°C provides improved specificity when time allows.

We also previously considered a range of primary and secondary antibody concentrations to identify conditions that maximize the signal‐to‐noise ratio. Adjusting the secondary antibody concentration had little effect on the staining, while the primary antibody concentration had a more noticeable influence on signal intensity. Through this optimization, we identified the ideal concentrations of both primary and secondary antibodies that consistently provided robust staining. These observations highlight the importance of optimizing antibody conditions in your experimental setup and not relying only on general manufacturer guidelines.

Understanding Results

Following image acquisition and single‐cell quantification, pSMAD fluorescence intensities are displayed for each individual cell. This shows distributions that could reflect the heterogeneity of cell populations, as seen in the example shown in Figure 2. In this example, we can see some dispersion in SMAD signaling after stimulation with matrix‐bound BMP‐2. This representation of results could help identify heterogeneities, changes in the dynamics of the signaling pathway, or the presence of distinct cell populations in experimental contexts. All these complementary analyses are beyond the scope of this study but may well be studied in future works.

Each sample should be analyzed at the single‐cell level, and data should be taken from the different fields of view for the given well. Technical replicates showing acquisition artifacts and segmentation errors should be excluded prior to final analysis. Signals can be summarized per cell, per field, or per well, depending on the objective of the analysis.

The distribution of cells with lower and higher intensities of pSMAD indicates that the assay is sensitive enough to distinguish differences in signaling activation in a given sample. Shifts in the proportion of these intensities can therefore be used to quantify the effects of growth factors or pharmacological treatments on SMAD and on the activation of pathways characterized by specific nuclear markers.

Time Considerations

This protocol can be completed within 2 days, depending on the incubation strategy for the primary antibody, the number of samples to be imaged, and the incubation time of your drug compounds. Expansion of cells before seeding generally requires about 24 h, although overnight culture (about 12 h) is typically sufficient. Seeding on biomaterials or glass substrates should be planned to allow 24 h of adhesion prior to fixation, especially on biomaterials where adhesion takes longer.

The fixation step, followed by three washes, takes approximately 15 min. Blocking typically requires 1 h. Antibody and nucleus staining could require 2 h (if done at room temperature) or extended overnight (if done at 4°C). The image acquisition time depends on the microscope's technical characteristics, the number of wells, and the number of fields per well. With our experimental setup (a high‐content microscope, two different channels, and a 96‐well microplate), typical durations range from 15 min to up to 2 h for a full 96‐well plate. Data analysis using the Fiji macros generally requires 1–3 h depending on the number of images and computational speed (related to the technical characteristics of the computer station used for data analysis).
