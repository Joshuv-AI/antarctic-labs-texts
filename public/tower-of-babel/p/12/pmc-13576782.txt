Psychological Outcomes of Family Members Related to a Loved One's Resuscitation in the Emergency Department: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ira Rahmawati, Tamara Page, Lisa Conlon, Anggun Setyarini, Frank Donnelly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aimed to assess the psychological outcomes of family members of patients who were resuscitated in the Emergency Department (ED) and analyse factors associated with these outcomes.DesignThis study utilised a cross‐sectional design.MethodsData were collected using a self‐reported questionnaire sent to family members of patients who had undergone resuscitation in the ED from February 2024 to January 2025. Instruments for data collection included The Impact of Event Scale‐Revised (IES‐R), the short version of The Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale–21 items (DASS‐21), the Multicultural Quality of Life Index (MQLI) and questions related to demographic variables and the resuscitation event.ResultsA total of 106 family members completed the questionnaire. Of this, 64.2% (n = 68) reported witnessing the resuscitation attempt, and 35.8% (n = 38) did not witness the event. Family members who witnessed the resuscitation displayed more symptoms of post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), measured by the IES‐R, compared to those who did not witness the event. A statistically significant negative correlation was found between the IES‐R and the MQLI scores, indicating that higher PTSD symptoms correlate with lower quality of life (QoL) ratings.ConclusionThe findings of this study indicated that witnessing the resuscitation of a loved one in the ED is associated with increased PTSD symptoms.Implications for Health Professionals and Patient CarePatients' and family members' cultural and religious needs should be acknowledged by the health care providers. Study findings indicate that family members prefer to be with the patient during the patient's resuscitation. However, without adequate support from hospital staff, this experience may cause adverse psychological effects. Strategies to support family members during and after resuscitation should be developed and integrated into the management of in‐hospital resuscitation.Reporting MethodThis study followed the STROBE guidelines.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Keywords: cardiopulmonary resuscitation, family perceptions, family presence, family‐witnessed resuscitation, post‐traumatic stress disorder, quality of life

Introduction

Despite advancements in cardiac emergency care, the survival rate of an intra‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) remains low, with reported rates between 15% and 34% (Penketh and Nolan 2022). The incidence of IHCA varies across institutions and countries, ranging from about 1.2 to 10 cases per 1000 hospital admissions (Penketh and Nolan 2022). No studies or national registries have reported the incidence of cardiac arrest in Indonesia. However, data from a single‐centre study in Indonesia revealed that the average survival rate for IHCA between 2017 and 2023 was 17.7% (Giwangkancana et al. 2025). Due to the uncertain outcomes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), family members often express their wish to be with the patient during resuscitation attempts (Toronto and LaRocco 2019), a wish acknowledged by numerous professional organisations (Australian Resuscitation Council 2025; Raffay et al. 2025).

Family presence during resuscitation (FPDR), also known as family‐witnessed resuscitation, refers to the presence of family members in a patient care area, allowing them to maintain physical or visual contact with the patient during CPR (Halm et al. 2024). International Resuscitation Guidelines have endorsed FPDR (Australian Resuscitation Council 2025; Raffay et al. 2025), but the implementation of FPDR remains uncommon and is often influenced by cultural context (Abualruz et al. 2025). Notably, only 32% of United States (US) hospitals have a formal FPDR policy (Halm et al. 2024). Specifically, FPDR guidelines have been adopted by only 5% of critical care units in the US, 8% in Canada, and 7% in Europe (Akman and Koyuncu 2024).

Although Health Care Professionals' (HCPs) attitudes toward FPDR vary across different countries, the literature indicates growing support for this practice (Abualruz et al. 2025; Waldemar and Thylén 2025). However, concerns remain regarding the potential negative consequences of family presence. Recent studies note that clinicians express concern that FPDR may increase stress on medical staff and that the family members may interfere with the CPR process (Tai et al. 2025; Waldemar and Thylén 2025). Literature also notes concerns about the emotional trauma family members might experience from witnessing a loved one's resuscitation (Waldemar and Thylén 2025). These concerns highlight the need for more research on the potential consequences of FPDR in clinical settings, particularly to assess the psychological impact on family members who witnessed a loved one's resuscitation attempt (Rahmawati et al. 2025), especially in an emergency department (ED), where resuscitation procedures are frequently initiated.

Background

FPDR has been a topic of debate for the past three decades (Meghani 2021). Benefits of FPDR for family members, patients and the HCPs involved in the resuscitation attempt have been highlighted in the literature (Cornell and Powers 2022; Meghani 2021). For family members, FPDR facilitates their need to be emotionally and physically close to their loved one, allowing them to support and comfort the patient (Meghani 2021; Waldemar et al. 2023) in addition to facilitating their wishes to be involved in the patient's care and decision‐making (Meghani 2021). Witnessing the resuscitation attempt may assure the family that everything possible has been done for the patient. Therefore, in the context of an unsuccessful attempt, this could facilitate closure for the family and prevent prolonged or complicated grief (Cornell and Powers 2022; Meghani 2021). However, it has also been reported that family members who stayed with the patient during the attempt may experience psychological consequences, such as symptoms of post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Erogul et al. 2020).

Currently, there are limited studies evaluating patients' experiences after a resuscitation event. One study involving patients who had survived CPR or had been admitted to the ED for invasive procedures, and another study involving patients treated in the ED, both suggested that the presence of a family member during critical interventions benefits the patient (Barreto et al. 2019; Fernández et al. 2021). These patients reported feeling comforted and less anxious when their family members were present and prayed or spoke to them during the event, as this provided them with peace of mind and a sense of security (Barreto et al. 2019; Fernández et al. 2021). While many patients desired having family members present, they also acknowledged that not all family members may be prepared to witness resuscitation procedures (Fernández et al. 2021).

Health care providers vary in their perspectives and experiences relating to FPDR. Allowing family members to witness the resuscitation has been described as important, as it can enhance trust in the team caring for their loved one (Waldemar and Thylen 2019). Some HCPs also indicated that the presence of family members enhanced communication and facilitated rapid history taking (Abualruz et al. 2025; Rubin et al. 2024). A small number of HCPs believed that the presence of a family member could negatively affect the resuscitation team's performance (Magowan and Melby 2019). Nevertheless, FPDR is considered consistent with the patient–family‐centred care (PFCC) model, a holistic approach to patient care (Powers et al. 2023).

Witnessing the CPR process of a loved one in an ED may cause psychological trauma that could lead to long‐term stress responses, and include symptoms associated with PTSD, as shown in a study conducted by Erogul et al. (2020). PTSD, a mental disorder triggered by traumatic experiences, can cause chronic impairments that could lead to reduced quality of life (QoL), increased economic burden and increased risk of suicide (Mann et al. 2025). People with PTSD may also experience feelings of exhaustion, rage and depression (Erogul et al. 2020). These individuals may frequently relive the traumatic event in their daily lives and experience excessive nervous system responses, including physical tension and sleep difficulties (Mann et al. 2025). In addition, individuals with PTSD are more likely to experience impaired social and occupational functioning (Mann et al. 2025).

However, a systematic review that examined the effect of FPDR on PTSD‐related symptoms in family members found some indication that FPDR might reduce symptoms of PTSD, anxiety and depression. The studies reviewed were very limited, making the overall effect unclear (Afzali Rubin et al. 2023). This highlights the need for further research on the effects of witnessing ED resuscitation across different geographical and cultural contexts. Moreover, there is a lack of literature evaluating how witnessing a loved one's resuscitation affects family members in an Asian setting, including Indonesia, which emphasises the need for research in this context.

The Study

Aims

This study aimed to assess the psychological outcomes of family members of patients who underwent resuscitation in the ED in Indonesia, focusing on symptoms of PTSD, depression, stress and anxiety, as well as overall QoL. It also examined the relationship between these psychological outcomes and factors such as whether the respondents had witnessed the resuscitation process and their demographic characteristics. In addition, the study identified respondents' perceptions and preferences regarding FPDR.

Study Design and Setting

This study utilised a cross‐sectional design to assess the psychological outcomes of family members of patients resuscitated in the ED. The respondents were recruited from a tertiary referral hospital in Malang, East Java Province, Indonesia. The hospital is an 868‐bed hospital with more than 22,000 ED presentations annually. The hospital does not have a formal policy on FPDR in the ED or other clinical areas. However, there have been situations where family members have witnessed the resuscitation attempt in the ED. The decision to allow family members to be present during a loved one's resuscitation relied on the personal judgement of the resuscitation team, particularly the medical officer in charge. In addition, no staff member in this department is formally assigned to support the family member during a patient's resuscitation.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) at Adelaide University (H‐2024‐229) and the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Dr. Saiful Anwar Hospital Malang (400/061/K.3/102.7/2024). Given the possibility that completing the study questionnaires might trigger unpleasant memories or previously repressed emotions, respondents were informed that they could contact the research team for support. An Indonesian psychiatrist was available to provide initial counselling if needed. To ensure participants could access assistance in case of psychological distress, the contact details of a research team member were included on the last page of each questionnaire.

Subjects

The respondents in this study were family members of patients who received CPR in the ED from February 2024 to January 2025. The hospital authority provided the details of these patients. The inclusion criteria for the patients were age 18 years or above and had received CPR in the ED, regardless of the CPR outcome. CPR is a set of life‐saving procedures intended to restore circulation and breathing in individuals experiencing cardiac arrest, including chest compression, airway management, rescue breathing and other interventions such as cardiac defibrillation (Meghani 2021). The inclusion criteria for family members were aged 18 years or above, physically present in the hospital during the resuscitation, either in the resuscitation room or elsewhere in the hospital, and able to speak and understand Indonesian. Family members in this study included individuals bonded to the patient by biological, legal, social, spiritual or emotional relationships, who support the patient's health and well‐being (McAndrew et al. 2020). The exclusion criteria for the respondents included family members under 18 years of age, not present at the hospital when the resuscitation occurred, and family members of non‐adult patients.

Instruments

This study utilised a set of four questionnaires: demographic data and information related to the patient's resuscitation event; the Impact of Event Scale‐Revised (IES‐R); the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale–21 (DASS‐21); and the Multicultural Quality of Life Index (MQLI) (Creamer et al. 2003; Lovibond and Lovibond 1995; Mezzich et al. 2011). The demographic questionnaire included questions related to the loved one's resuscitation, including open‐ended questions that asked respondents for their perceptions and preferences regarding FPDR. For example, one question asked: ‘Do you think it is important for the family to be present during the patient's resuscitation attempt? Yes/No/Unsure. Please provide reasons for your answer’. The complete questionnaires are available in Appendix S1. All questionnaires were administered in Indonesian and were available both online (REDCap) and in paper formats.

The research team initially developed a questionnaire in English to collect demographic data and information regarding patients' resuscitation events. This demographic and the MQLI questionnaires were then translated into Indonesian using a five‐step method developed by Cheung et al. (2020). The first step of the translation process was recruiting a translation team. The initial translators comprised an English language lecturer who also runs a translation agency in Indonesia and a PhD‐qualified nurse with over 5 years of experience working in an English‐speaking country, who conducted the forward translation. Following initial translation work, backward translation was conducted by another team that had no prior knowledge of the English version. This team included a lecturer with over 15 years of experience teaching English to university students and a health professional experienced in conducting research in medical sciences. After completing the translations, the research team compared the back‐translated versions with the original instrument to evaluate conceptual consistency. In addition, the research team found no significant semantic discrepancies, and minor final amendments were made before finalising the translated instrument. The forward‐translated questionnaire was then administered to seven native Indonesian speakers to assess its readability and cultural appropriateness. This face validity technique evaluated whether the questions were well understood and ensured that the language used was relevant and appropriate to the prospective research subject (Cheung et al. 2020).

Symptoms of PTSD were measured using the 22 items of the IES‐R, a validated scale that measures post‐traumatic distress symptoms and correlates closely with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐IV (DSM‐IV) criteria for PTSD (Creamer et al. 2003; Erogul et al. 2020). The IES‐R is a tool for detecting symptoms indicating the risk of PTSD and is not intended for diagnostic purposes. Each item in this scale is rated on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). The IES‐R consisted of three subscales: intrusion, avoidance and hyperarousal, and has a score range from 0 to 88. A score of 33 or above indicates a likely presence of PTSD. The IES‐R has demonstrated high internal consistency with a Cronbach's α of 0.96 (Creamer et al. 2003).

Warsini et al. (2015) translated and validated the Indonesian version of IES‐R and gave permission for its use in this study. The Indonesian version of IES‐R shows good internal consistency with Cronbach's α for the total IES‐R score reported as 0.90 and 0.92 on retest (Warsini et al. 2015). The authors also conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess the construct validity of the Indonesian version and identified three underlying factors: intrusion, avoidance and hyperarousal, which align with the original IES‐R structure and support the validity of this instrument (Warsini et al. 2015).

The DASS‐21, developed by Lovibond and Lovibond (1995), is a brief measure of symptoms commonly associated with psychological distress. The instrument consists of three subscales (Depression, Anxiety and Stress). Each subscale consists of seven items and is scored on a 4‐point scale ranging from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much or most of the time). Because the DASS‐21 is a short version of the original 42‐item DASS‐42, the sum of each subscale is to be multiplied by two; higher scores indicate more frequent negative symptoms (Lovibond and Lovibond 1995). The original DASS‐21 demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.93 for the total scale, and alphas of 0.88, 0.82 and 0.90 for the depression, anxiety and stress subscales, respectively (Henry and Crawford 2005). This current study used the Indonesian version of DASS‐21, adopted and validated by Kinanthi et al. (2020). The Cronbach's α values for the Indonesian version of DASS‐21 were 0.91 for the total scale, 0.85 for the depression scale, 0.77 for the anxiety scale and 0.90 for the stress scale (Kinanthi et al. 2020). Additionally, the Indonesian version of DASS‐21 was evaluated by Indonesian clinical psychologists, who found it to be suitable for assessing symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress (Kinanthi et al. 2020).

The MQLI, developed by (Mezzich et al. 2011), is a brief, culturally informed questionnaire that measures health‐related QoL. It is comprised of 10 items (from physical well‐being to global perception of QoL) and was constructed based on a critical review of international literature (Mezzich et al. 2011). The MQLI has high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.92), and each item is rated on a 10‐point scale ranging from 1 (poor QoL) to 10 (excellent). The MQLI questionnaire was translated into Indonesian through the steps outlined above and tested for reliability using Cronbach's α coefficient, which showed high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.95; Appendix S2). Permission to use MQLI was obtained from the original author (Mezzich et al. 2011).

Data Collection and Analysis

Data collection was carried out from December 2024 to March 2025. Research assistants, who are nursing college graduates, initially contacted potential respondents via phone calls and text messages, inviting them to participate in this study. These potential respondents were contacted at least 1 month after the resuscitation date of their loved one. This timeframe was chosen to allow sufficient time to identify whether any PTSD symptoms were present (Jones et al. 2024), and to avoid approaching individuals during their grieving period or during an acute stress response (Jones et al. 2024). Prior to data collection, several meetings had been conducted with the research assistants to ensure that the surveys were delivered in the same way. Potential respondents who expressed interest had the option to complete the questionnaires either online or on paper. For respondents who chose the paper format, the research team coordinated home visits or arranged for the questionnaires to be delivered and collected by a courier, while the online questionnaire was distributed via a REDCap link sent through text message. Each REDCap link was individually coded, making it valid for only one respondent. The research assistants made three attempts to contact each potential respondent.

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics Software version 29. Demographic information and respondents' perceptions of the patient's resuscitation event were analysed using descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation and percentages). In the questionnaire, respondents were asked whether they had witnessed the resuscitation event. Based on their responses, they were categorised into two groups for the analysis: those who witnessed the resuscitation (FWR) and those who did not (non‐FWR). To evaluate the differences in mean scores of the IES‐R, DASS‐21, MQLI and other continuous variables between the FWR and non‐FWR groups, an independent sample t‐test was performed for normally distributed data and a Mann–Whitney U for non‐normally distributed data. A Chi‐Square test was performed to determine the association between two categorical variables. Additionally, a linear regression model was used to examine the association between the IES‐R scores and whether the respondents witnessed the resuscitation event. Witnessing the resuscitation was treated as the primary exposure variable. The model was adjusted for prespecified covariates, including age, gender, resuscitation outcomes and the time elapsed since the event. All statistical tests were two‐tailed. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical advice was provided by the Statistical Service at the College of Health, Adelaide University.

The respondents' answers to the open‐ended questions were analysed using inductive content analysis (Berkovic 2023). The responses from each respondent were recorded in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (version 2506). These responses were read several times to identify patterns, keywords or ideas, which were then assigned initial codes. Codes with similar meanings or nuances were grouped into broader categories, with each category named based on its content. The frequency of responses within each category was counted. The data were analysed for percentages using SPSS software (version 29), and the results were visualised accordingly. Finally, the frequency of the respondents' preferences or opinions was recorded. For example, the number of respondents who answered ‘Yes’, ‘No’ or ‘Unsure’ to the question about whether they believe it is important for family members to be present during a patient's resuscitation was documented.

Results

Family members of 139 patients were contacted for this study. A total of 64 patients had at least one family member who agreed to participate in this study, resulting in a 46% response rate. This study did not limit the number of family members per patient. All eligible family members of included patients were invited to participate. A total of 123 family members agreed to participate, but only 106 completed the survey and were included in data analysis. The results of this cross‐sectional study suggest associations, not causality, between the presence of families during resuscitation events and PTSD.

Characteristics of the Family Members, Patients and Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

The mean age of the family members who participated in this study was 36.59, with 54.7% being female. The education level of the respondents ranged from primary school to graduate school. More than half of the respondents were married (51.9%), and two‐thirds (31.1%) were self‐employed.

Most respondents reported they were Muslims (97.2%). A total of 95.3% of the respondents were Javanese, an ethnic group native to East Java, Indonesia. Half of the respondents were the patient's children, and only 3.8% of the respondents had experience or education in the medical sciences, such as nurses, midwives and doctors.

Of the 106 respondents who completed the survey, 64.2% (n = 68) reported that they had witnessed the patient's resuscitation, while 35.8% (n = 38) reported that they had not. Respondents were categorised into the FWR and non‐FWR groups based solely on their self‐reported experiences. This cross‐sectional survey did not collect data on the specific reasons why respondents witnessed or did not witness the resuscitation, such as personal choice or decisions made by the medical team. However, the respondents were asked whether they were given the option to be present during their loved one's resuscitation. Among those who witnessed the event, 80.9% reported being offered the option to be present, 7.4% were unsure whether the option was offered, and 11.8% reported that they were not given the option. In contrast, among the respondents who did not witness the resuscitation, 44.7% stated they were offered the option to be present, 28.9% were unsure and 26.3% reported that they were not given the option. In addition, the correlation between the IES‐R and MQLI scores was analysed using the Spearman rho correlation test. The results showed a coefficient correlation of r s = −0.244, with p = 0.01, indicating a weak but statistically significant negative correlation. This means that as the score of one variable increases, the score of the other variable tends to decrease. An independent sample t‐test was also conducted to compare the mean IES‐R score based on the patient survival status. This showed no significant difference in the IES‐R scores in family members of patients who underwent unsuccessful resuscitation (M = 36.19, SD = 16.83), compared to family members of patients who survived the resuscitation (M = 33.62, SD = 17.02; t(104) = 0.62, p = 0.53, two‐tailed). The magnitude of the differences in the means (mean difference = 2.57, 95% Cl [−5.59–10.73]) was small (Cohen's d = 0.15).

Family Perceptions on Being Present During a Loved One's Resuscitation

A total of 90.57% of the respondents in this study felt that it was important for family members to be present during a loved one's resuscitation. Additionally, 85.8% of respondents felt that they had the right to choose to be with the patient during resuscitation attempts, and 67.9% of the respondents stated that they were offered the choice to be with the patient during the resuscitation. The respondents were also asked why being with the patient during the resuscitation attempt was important, with approximately 30% reporting that they wanted to see what was being done to their loved ones. Other reasons described by the respondents included providing spiritual and emotional support for the patient, staying informed about the patient's condition, showing respect and love for the patient, and fulfilling the family's need to be with or close to the patient (Figure 1).

Family members' perceptions of the importance of being present during a loved one's resuscitation.

The two most common reasons respondents felt that FPDR was important were their desire to see what is being done to the patient and to see the patient's current condition and progress. Some respondents reported that they felt frightened while witnessing the CPR, but they still chose to stay with the patient. Additionally, some respondents expressed that they needed to have a sense of whether the patient would survive or die, as they wanted to be prepared for what to do next. If the resuscitation attempt became prolonged, they wanted the opportunity to request that the resuscitation team cease the attempt. It was reported that knowing that everything possible has been done for the patient would help the family members accept the loss and avoid misunderstandings with the staff. Some of the quotes from the respondents representing these two reasons are as follows:

So that family members know the interventions being performed to save the patient. (Male, 34 years old)

For me, it is important (to witness the resuscitation). I felt scared about my son's condition, but I wanted to know what was being done by the doctor to my son. (Female, 57 years old)

(witnessing the resuscitation) is important for knowing the patient's progress. (Female, 36 years old)

The family members in this study believed that the patient needed emotional support and wanted the family member to be with them. Being with the patient during resuscitation was also considered a way to show respect and love for the patient. Additionally, some respondents felt that staying with the patient during a critical time such as the resuscitation is an obligation they should fulfil as a family.

The patient needed to be with their family to feel calm. (Male, 54 years old)

(witnessing the resuscitation) provides emotional support to the patient and other family members. (Female, 31 years old)

My desire to be with my father during resuscitation, to see his condition and also as a way to show my respect and love for him. (Male, 36 years old)

Another important reason for family members to stay by the patient's side during the resuscitation was to pray and recite the shahada, which is one of the fundamental practices of Islam. In Islam, it is the religious duty of a family member to be with the patient to guide the critical patient to recite the shahadah by saying ‘La Ilaha Illa'llah’, which means there is no God but Allah (Othman et al. 2020). Indeed, some respondents in this study wrote that it was their obligation to stay with the patient to ensure the patient was either saying or hearing the shahada. Additionally, some family members indicated that it was important to pray beside the patient, as it allowed the patient to hear these sacred words. Praying in Islam usually includes reciting some part of the Al‐Quran (Othman et al. 2020), which is in Arabic. The family believed that these religious rituals would have comforted the patient, and if the resuscitation was unsuccessful, it would help ease the patient's transition into the afterlife, known as ‘akhirah’.

Praying for the patient so that the patient would have an easy and peaceful “sakaratul maut” (Agony of death). (Female, 40 years old)

To guide the patient reciting shahadah so that he could have an easy “sakaratul maut.” (Female, 37 years old)

Cheering up the patient and praying in their ear. (Female, 59 years old)

Some family members stated they needed to be with the patient during their seemingly last moments. As the resuscitation attempt may or may not be successful, the presence of family members should be allowed. One respondent wrote:

Especially for biological children, they needed to witness the resuscitation of their parents, regardless of the outcome of the attempt. Like myself, I really wanted to be with my mother during the last moment, but I was not allowed to. (Female, 26 years old)

A total of 15% of respondents in this study did not feel or were unsure that witnessing the resuscitation of a loved one is important for family members. Some of the reasons stated by the respondents include concerns that witnessing the patient's resuscitation procedures might cause prolonged sadness and frightening memories that they wanted to avoid. Also, the doctor should focus on resuscitating the patient and should not be distracted by the presence of family members.

(Witnessing the resuscitation is) unimportant because it can prolong sadness and prevent the image of it from appearing in the mind. (Male, 24 years old)

Family members may disturb the resuscitation procedure. (Female, 39 years old)

I think the doctors should focus on the resuscitation procedures and not be distracted. (Male, 23 years old)

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the psychological outcomes associated with witnessing a loved one's resuscitation and to quantitatively assess family perceptions of FPDR in Indonesia. In this study, witnessing a loved one's resuscitation was associated with higher levels of PTSD symptoms in family members compared with those who did not witness the attempt. Despite this, most family members expressed a desire and need to be present during resuscitation and viewed having the choice to attend as a right, consistent with prior literature showing strong support for FPDR among family members (Karaçay and Dogu 2020; Rahmawati et al. 2025; Toronto and LaRocco 2019).

The findings of this study support previous research conducted in a U.S. ED by Erogul et al. (2020), suggesting that witnessing a loved one's resuscitation may be associated with an increased risk of developing PTSD symptoms among family members (Erogul et al. 2020). However, similar to the present study, the study by Erogul et al. (2020) categorised respondents into the FWR and Non‐FWR groups based on their self‐reports, and the respondents received no specific support during the resuscitation event. The broader literature presents mixed findings. Some studies indicated that family members who were present during a loved one's resuscitation reported experiencing fewer symptoms of PTSD, anxiety and depression (Considine et al. 2022; Karaçay and Dogu 2020), while other studies have found no significant differences in psychological outcomes based on whether family members witnessed the resuscitation (Considine et al. 2022). The risk of adverse psychological outcomes appeared to be higher for family members who witnessed a loved one's resuscitation without professional support or in the absence of formally implemented FPDR guidelines. Research has suggested that support provided by healthcare staff during resuscitation events could reduce levels of depression, anxiety and PTSD symptoms in family members in the months following the event (Soleimanpour et al. 2017).

Support for family members who are present during a loved one's resuscitation is usually provided by a designated staff member, often referred to as a family support person (FSP) (Powers et al. 2023). The role of the FSP, which can be fulfilled by a nurse or other trained professionals, involves preparing family members for what they will see, explaining the patient's condition, being with the family members throughout the resuscitation and explaining the activities of the resuscitation team (Powers et al. 2023). The FSP will also address the informational, emotional and physical needs of family members witnessing the event (Powers et al. 2023). Indeed, the presence of a designated family liaison is essential when family members are present during in‐hospital resuscitation, and family members may not be formally invited for FPDR if the FSP is not available (Powers et al. 2023). Other strategies, such as post‐resuscitation debriefing, may also be implemented to help families process the experience and receive emotional or spiritual support, thereby improving the intended benefits of FPDR.

Most family members in this study felt that family presence during a patient's resuscitation attempt was important. They expressed that witnessing the resuscitation would help them achieve a sense of closure, knowing that every possible medical intervention had been done to save the patient. Some family members added that being allowed to be with the patient during the attempt could prevent misunderstandings with the staff involved in the resuscitation. These findings are consistent with previous studies that revealed family members wanted to ensure that the resuscitation team was doing their job to provide care for the patient, and see what was happening with the patient (Halm et al. 2024). Excluding family members from witnessing resuscitation may cause the family to think that something is being hidden from them, which could result in misunderstandings and conflicts between families and the health care team (Celik et al. 2019). The present study has shown the potential for psychological distress associated with witnessing the attempt. Therefore, hospital authorities should consider developing a policy on FPDR that includes designating an FSP to help facilitate the process.

Another noteworthy finding from this study is that there was a significant negative correlation between the IES‐R and MQLI scores, which indicates that the more severe symptoms of PTSD the respondents experienced, the lower level of QoL they reported. This finding supports existing literature suggesting that individuals with PTSD tend to express lower QoL than those without trauma exposure or those remitted from PTSD (Presciutti et al. 2021). Individuals with PTSD may also experience disruption in social functioning due to symptoms such as diminished interest, avoidance and detachment from others (Presciutti et al. 2021). QoL is an important measure of health care outcomes, and the findings of this study suggest that a patient's resuscitation attempt can have a lasting impact on family members' QoL, even months after the event. This further highlights the need for developing interventions that support family members during resuscitation events and establishing structured follow‐up processes to identify surviving patients and family members in need of additional support after a cardiac arrest event.

This study, conducted mostly with Muslim respondents, adds to the existing literature by showing that in some cultures, being present during a loved one's resuscitation is viewed not only as a religious need but also as a religious obligation. Comparable results have been noted in other studies with predominantly Muslim populations (Akman and Koyuncu 2024; Saifan et al. 2023). Family members felt a sense of duty to support and protect the patient during critical moments (Saifan et al. 2023), ensuring that the patient received care aligned with their beliefs (Akman and Koyuncu 2024). Family presence is especially important when the chance of successful resuscitation is low, as families aim to facilitate appropriate religious and cultural practices aligned with the patient's values (Akman and Koyuncu 2024; Othman et al. 2020). These findings highlight the need for culturally responsive policies within critical care settings.

Religious needs have not appeared as primary themes in studies involving family members from Western countries (Considine et al. 2022; Waldemar et al. 2023). Nonetheless, established guidelines for FPDR in Western healthcare systems also recommend that institutional policies be developed with sensitivity to the local context (Australian Resuscitation Council 2025; Raffay et al. 2025). When offering family members the option of being present during resuscitation, cultural, religious and other sociological factors should be considered and accommodated (Australian Resuscitation Council 2025).

Cultural and religious values can influence how individuals perceive and respond to medical events. In Indonesia, where this current study was conducted, family members often play a central role in a patient's healthcare decision‐making (Martina et al. 2022). Additionally, religious beliefs are integral to Indonesians' daily lives (Martina et al. 2022) and become even more important during difficult times. More than half of the family members surveyed reported being offered the option to be with the patient during resuscitation, indicating that HCPs in this setting acknowledged the cultural and spiritual needs of the patient and family members, despite the absence of a formal institutional policy on FPDR. Additionally, the number of family members who witnessed the resuscitation process in this study was nearly double that of those who did not.

However, the lack of a formal policy resulted in inconsistencies, as the decision to allow family members present during a loved one's resuscitation relied on the personal judgement of the resuscitation team, particularly the medical officer in charge. When spiritual or cultural needs are unmet, the patients and family members may experience anxiety and be dissatisfied with the care they receive (Swihart et al. 2023). Therefore, clinical institutions should consider developing a local policy regarding family presence and ensure that HCPs are adequately equipped with knowledge, training and resources to deliver culturally competent care for patients and their families, including during resuscitation attempts (Australian Resuscitation Council 2025; Swihart et al. 2023).

Limitations

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting the findings of this study. First, this study was conducted in a single centre, with almost all the respondents being Javanese and Muslim. Considering that there are many other ethnicities and religions in Indonesia alone, the findings derived from this study cannot be generalised to all populations. Additionally, respondents were categorised into FWR and non‐FWR groups based solely on self‐reported data. Those who witnessed the resuscitation may have done so because of closer emotional bonds with the patient and may therefore have been more likely to develop symptoms of PTSD, potentially introducing selection bias. Self‐report measures, like those collected in this study, have inherent limitations, such as memory bias, social desirability bias and response bias. Furthermore, this study only conducted a single evaluation of the psychological outcomes. Symptoms of PTSD and other measures can fluctuate over several months after the event and reassessing psychological outcomes at a second point in time after the event may provide further insights into the respondents' psychological well‐being. Finally, since this study used a cross‐sectional design, it is not possible to establish a true cause‐and‐effect relationship between variables.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicated that witnessing the resuscitation event of a loved one in the ED is associated with increased PTSD symptoms. Family members who experienced more PTSD symptoms reported a lower QoL. Most family members in this study expressed a desire to be present during resuscitation, which is consistent with many previous studies. Further research is needed to better understand factors shaping family members' experiences and perceptions regarding FPDR across different settings. Future research may consider evaluating factors such as the availability of hospital policy or the presence of a family support person. In addition, using longitudinal or mixed‐method designs may be beneficial, as individuals' psychological responses can fluctuate over time and may be shaped by contextual factors that are best examined through both quantitative and qualitative methods.
