Hydroxytyrosol attenuates obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis by inhibiting NLRP3/CYLD/NOX4 mediated oxidative stress
Authors: Tao Li, Bing Shao, Xiao Li, Ziyi Wu, Dongxin Yi, Yuji Xiao, Xue Zhang, Yixue Deng, Tao Yang, Jiaxin Zhu, Tiecheng Huang, Ningning Wang, Rongfeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydroxytyrosol (HT), the most bioactive polyphenol in virgin olive oil, has been shown to have cardiometabolic benefits in obese rats, but the underlying mechanism has not been fully elucidated. The aim of this study was to explore the potential driving mechanism in the initiation node of obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis through in vivo and in vitro experiments, and the role of HT in obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis was investigated based on the above mechanisms. The results demonstrated that HT reduced cardiomyocyte oxidative stress and ultimately ameliorated myocardial injury and fibrosis in obese mice by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis, which in turn downregulated the deubiquitinating enzyme cylindromatosis (CYLD), thereby reducing NADPH oxidase (NOX)4 deubiquitination and promoting its degradation. Subject terms: Cardiology, Cell biology, Diseases, Molecular biology

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) survey, high BMI caused 4 million deaths in 2015, of which more than two-thirds were caused by cardiovascular diseases (CVDs)1. Obesity is considered an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease, and can also increase susceptibility to arrhythmias2–4, as well as abnormalities in cardiac structure and function5. Multiple mechanisms involved in obesity-related myocardial injury, remodeling, and dysfunction have been explored, including iron-dependent ferroptosis6 and NOD-like receptor family 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis5. As a pro-inflammatory programmed cell death (PCD) mode, pyroptosis has significantly contributed to obesity-related CVDs7,8, especially the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. It’s morphologically characterized by the formation of micropores in the membrane structure and the destruction of the mitochondrial ultrastructure, which is manifested as the formation of micropores in the mitochondrial membrane, mitochondrial shrinkage and rounding, whirled, or absence of mitochondrial cristae and decreased number of mitochondria9–11. In addition, ferroptosis has also been found to be involved in obesity-related cardiomyopathy6, which is caused by the iron overload, excessive accumulation of membrane lipid peroxides and depletion of the amino acid antioxidant system. Therefore, both cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and ferroptosis seem to be involved in obesity-related myocardial remodeling, but it is still unclear which PCD drives the initiation of myocardial remodeling in obesity.

Cylindromatosis (CYLD), a deubiquitinase initially identified in familial cylindromatosis, is involved in the regulation of a variety of physiological processes, including cell cycle processes, immune response, spermatogenesis and osteoclasterogenesis, and also plays critical roles in the pathogenesis of cancer, pulmonary fibrosis and inflammatory bowel disease12. Recent studies have found that CYLD also plays a mediating role in cardiac maladaptive remodeling and dysfunction12–14. In adriamycin-stimulated cardiomyocytes, CYLD deubiquitinated nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) to enhance ferritin autophagy, thereby leading to ferroptosis15. CYLD has also been found to be involved in regulating the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome and provocation of pyroptosis. Specifically, in the macrophages and in an animal model of peritonitis, CYLD inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by deubiquitinating polo-like kinase 4 (PLK4)16. In hypoxic cancer cells, decreased CYLD levels led to NF-κB activation and induction of the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis17. Taken together, CYLD is most likely involved in myocardial remodeling and dysfunction through its regulation of cell ferroptosis or pyroptosis.

NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) is a key oxidase involved in the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cardiomyocytes, and excessive oxidative stress mediated by NOX4 is found to participate in the process of myocardial remodeling18–20. The level of NOX4 is mainly affected by deubiquitinating enzymes. In adventitial fibroblasts, the deubiquitinating effect of CYLD on NOX4 promoted oxidative stress-triggered vascular remodeling21. However, whether CYLD plays a role in NOX4-mediated oxidative stress in obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis remains unclear.

Hydroxytyrosol (HT) is the most bioactive small-molecule polyphenol in virgin olive oil, and its health benefits for cardiovascular metabolism have been confirmed by a large number of studies due to its outstanding anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties22–25. In a randomized controlled trial, vascular function and left ventricular diastolic function were significantly improved after HT administration in patients with chronic coronary syndrome, with concomitant reductions in inflammation, oxidative stress and triglyceride levels26. It has been reported that HT improves the pathological cardiac hypertrophy induced by strenuous exercise in rats by regulating mitochondrial homeostasis27. HT could also alleviate adriamycin-induced cardiotoxicity by reducing oxidative stress and cardiomyocyte apoptosis28. In the rat model of myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI), HT reduced oxidative stress, cardiomyocyte apoptosis and myocardial infarct size by activating the PI3K/AKT/GSK3β pathway, thereby protecting cardiac function29. In addition, its protective effect on the heart of high fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mice has been preliminarily explored30, but the potential mechanism needs to be further elaborated.

With combing in vitro and in vivo experiments, this study aims to investigate the underlying mechanism at the initiation point of obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis and the possible protective effect of HT. We found that cardiomyocyte pyroptosis plays a driving role at the onset of myocardial remodeling in HFD-induced obese mice. Mechanistically, NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis leads to an increase in the CYLD protein level and the deubiquitination of NOX4, which induces excessive oxidative stress in the heart, eventually leading to myocardial injury and fibrosis. Meanwhile, HT ameliorates myocardial fibrosis in obese mice through the aforementioned mechanisms.

Results

HT improved myocardial injury, oxidative stress and myocardial remodeling in obese mice

In this study, an HFD with 60% fat for energy was used to establish an obesity model in mice, as shown in Fig. 1A, which were divided into the CON group, the HFD group, and the HFD + HT group. The HFD mice weighed significantly more than the CON mice from 8 weeks. By 12 and 16 weeks, the HFD group not only weighed more than the CON group (Fig. 1B), but also exhibited higher heart weight (Figs. 1C and S1A) and volume (Fig. 1D). In addition, the FBG level was slightly increased in the HFD group at 12 weeks (Fig. S1B), while by 16 weeks, it was significantly higher than that of the CON group (Fig. 1E). All the above results indicated the successful modeling of obesity after 16-week feeding of HFD. Meanwhile, the detection of myocardial injury-related indicators CK-MB (Figs. 1F and S1C) and LDH (Figs. 1G and S1D), as well as oxidative stress-related indicators MDA (Figs. 1H and S1E) and NOX4 (Figs. 1I and S1F) revealed that HFD feeding for 16 weeks caused significant myocardial injury and oxidative stress, rather than 12 weeks. Similarly, Masson and H&E staining (Figs. 1J and S1G) also showed that compared with the CON group, no significant changes were found in the myocardial tissue of the HFD mice at 12 weeks, but obvious collagen fiber deposition and myocardial structural disorder were observed at 16 weeks. Changes in the expression levels of the fibrosis-related proteins Collagen I, Collagen III and α-SMA were consistent with the pathomorphological findings (Figs. 1K and S1H). Notably, the above obesity-associated cardiac injuries were significantly mitigated by 16 weeks of HT intervention. In addition, echocardiography at 16 weeks (Fig. S2A) showed a significant thickening of the LVAW (Fig. S2B, C), as well as an increasing trend of LVPW (Fig. S2D, E) and LV mass (Fig. S2F) in the HFD group compared with the CON group. HT tended to alleviate the HFD-induced increase in LV wall thickness and mass, but not in a statistically significant way. In conclusion, 16 weeks of HFD feeding might be the initial point of myocardial fibrosis in obese mice, accompanied by myocardial injury and oxidative stress imbalance, which could be alleviated by HT gavage.

Pyroptosis and CYLD activation were involved in the initiation of HFD-induced myocardial fibrosis, which was inhibited by HT

Then, we further explored whether it was ferroptosis or pyroptosis that played a driving role in the initiation of HFD-induced myocardial fibrosis. There was no hallmark sign of ferroptosis in the myocardium of mice at 12 weeks of HFD, according to the protein level of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) (Fig. S3A), and the lipid peroxidation product MDA (Fig. S1E), as well as cardiac and serum iron (Fig. S3B). After 16 weeks of HFD feeding, accumulation of cardiac MDA (Fig. 1H), serum and tissue iron (Fig. S3D) was observed, but there were no significant changes in the expression of ACSL4 (membrane lipid peroxidation substrate), and COX2 (lipid peroxidation and inflammatory marker). In addition, the amino acid antioxidase GPX4 remained at a high level (Fig. S3C). These results indicated that although an iron metabolism disorder developed in the myocardial tissue of mice at 16 weeks of HFD, the compensatory activation of the antioxidant system restrained the onset of cardiomyocyte ferroptosis. Based on the detection of pyroptosis-related proteins, including NLRP3, caspase 1, adapter apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC), cleaved-caspase 1, N-terminal gasdermin D (N-GSDMD), matured IL-1β, and IL-18, no pyroptosis was observed in the myocardium of 12-week HFD mice (Fig. S4A, B). However, at 16 weeks, the above pyroptosis-related proteins were significantly up-regulated (Fig. 2A), as well as the serum IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. 2B), which were all significantly inhibited by HT supplement. Besides, TEM showed ultrastructural damage of mitochondria in the cardiac tissue of HFD mice, which was in line with the characteristics of pyroptosis (Fig. 2C). These alterations, including shrunk and rounded shape, loss of cristae, and membrane defects, were also alleviated by HT replenish. Critically, the expression of cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. S5A) and the appearance of TUNEL staining (Fig. S5B) confirmed the absence of apoptosis in cardiac tissue of mice after 16 weeks on the HFD, supporting the notion that the initial stage of myocardial fibrosis induced by HFD exposure was accompanied by the development of pyroptosis. Furthermore, IF co-localization of NLRP3, F4/80 (macrophage marker), and cTNT (cardiomyocyte marker) revealed that HFD feeding triggered pyroptosis events predominantly in cardiomyocytes, rather than resident macrophages (Fig. 2D).

As mentioned earlier, CYLD plays a potential role in mediating cardiac maladaptive remodeling and is involved in the regulation of ferroptosis and pyroptosis. Therefore, we examined CYLD expression in mouse models and found that CYLD was not significantly changed in the hearts of 12-week HFD mice (Fig. S4A) but was significantly aggravated in the hearts of 16-week HFD group (Fig. 2A), which was also attenuated by HT treatment. The above results indicated that CYLD was involved in the initiation stage of obesity-associated myocardial fibrosis.

In conclusion, the initial stage of HFD-induced myocardial fibrosis was accompanied by the occurrence of cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, as well as the elevated CYLD protein, which were effectively alleviated by oral administration of HT.

HT ameliorated PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury, oxidative stress and cardiac fibroblast activation

It is well known that myocardial enzymes are primarily released following cardiomyocyte injury, and the activation of cardiac fibroblasts is required for the development of myocardial fibrosis. Moreover, we identified that pyroptosis during the initiation stage of HFD-induced myocardial fibrosis took place mainly in cardiomyocytes. Therefore, we subsequently focused on elucidating the relationship between cardiomyocyte injury and cardiac fibroblast activation under a high-fat condition. H9C2 cells and RCFs were co-cultured in transwell plates (Fig. 3A) and treated with PA to mimic the high lipid environment. We first explored whether PA-intervened H9C2 cells could cause activation of RCFs and the exact time point of activation. H9C2 cells were treated with PA for 12 h and 24 h, respectively, followed by cultivation in PA-free medium. Subsequently, these cells were co-cultured with RCFs for 24 h, then the expression of collagen I (Fig. 3B), collagen III (Fig. 3C) and α-SMA (Fig. 3D) in RCFs was assessed by IF. It was found that after PA treatment of H9C2 cells for 24 h, the RCFs activation marker proteins were significantly increased. Furthermore, conditioned medium collected from H9C2 cells treated with PA for different durations were applied to RCFs, as shown in Fig. 3E. RCFs activation-related proteins were detected by WB (Fig. 3F), which yielded consistent results with those obtained from the transwell co-culture. These results indicated that treatment of H9C2 cells with PA (100 μM) for 24 h could indirectly activate RCFs. Therefore, H9C2 cells were treated with PA for 24 h in all subsequent experiments.

The aforementioned in vivo results revealed that HT mitigated HFD-induced myocardial injury. Subsequently, the optimal concentration of HT that can alleviate injury of H9C2 cells and the activation of RCF under PA stimulation in vitro needs to be determined. First, H9C2 cells were treated with different concentrations of HT (5 μg/mL, 10 μg/mL, 15 μg/mL, and 20 μg/mL), and cell viability was detected by CCK8 assay, and it was found that 20 μg/mL concentration of HT decreased H9C2 cells viability (Fig. 4A). Therefore, H9C2 cells were further co-treated with 5 μg/mL, 10 μg/mL and 15 μg/mL of HT and PA, and it was found that 10 μg/mL of HT could improve cell survival under PA (Fig. 4B), and then 10 μg/mL of HT was applied in all subsequent experiments. H9C2 cells were treated with PA and PA + HT followed by co-culture with RCFs. Consistent with the in vivo findings, HT mitigated PA-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 4C–F) and cellular damage (Fig. 4G) in H9C2 cells and suppressed the activation of RCFs (Fig. 4H–K). Moreover, consistent results were obtained in AC16 cells and HCFs (Fig. S6A–H). In conclusion, HT could indirectly inhibit the activation of cardiac fibroblasts by ameliorating cell damage and oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes under PA stimulation.

HT alleviated PA-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and associated increase in CYLD protein

After treating cardiomyocytes (H9C2 and AC16 cells) with PA and PA + HT for 24 h, we measured markers of pyroptosis (Fig. 5A, B for H9C2, Fig. S7A, B for AC16) and ferroptosis (Fig. S8A, B for H9C2, Fig. S7C, D for AC16), as well as CYLD protein (Figs. 5A and S7A) levels. Consistent with in vivo findings, PA stimulation induced cardiomyocytes pyroptosis and elevated CYLD protein, which were mitigated by HT. Although the lipid peroxidation product MDA (Figs. 4F and S6C) were also elevated in cardiomyocytes treated with PA for 24 h, as well as iron levels in the cells and medium (Figs. S7D and S8B), no more characteristic signs of ferroptosis were observed (Figs. S7C and S8A). Additionally, no evidence of apoptosis was detected by TUNEL staining and cleaved caspase-3 protein determination (Fig. S7E, F for AC16, Fig. S8C, D for H9C2). The results are consistent with in vivo experiments, indicating that pyroptosis plays a dominant role in the initiation stage of myocardial fibrosis. Further, neutralizing antibodies against IL-1β and IL-18 were utilized in the conditioned medium of cardiomyocytes, which could partially attenuate myocardial fibrosis, suggesting that inflammatory mediators released during cardiomyocytes pyroptosis activate myocardial fibroblasts (Fig. S9). Taken together, HT alleviated PA-induced cardiomyocytes pyroptosis and elevated CYLD protein, which occurred at the initial point of cardiac fibroblast activation.

Downregulation of NLRP3 could abate PA-induced pyroptosis, CYLD activation and oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes, as well as cardiac fibroblast activation

Next, the cascade of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, CYLD upregulation, cellular oxidative stress and injury in the process of obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis was explored. Knockdown of NLRP3 in H9C2 cells with small interfering RNA (si-RNA) (Fig. 6A) significantly suppressed PA-induced pyroptosis and elevated CYLD protein levels (Fig. 6B, C), as well as PA-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 6D–F) and cardiomyocyte injury (Fig. 6G). These results suggested that PA stimulation first activated NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in H9C2 cells, which in turn enhanced CYLD expression, oxidative stress, and cardiomyocytes injury. RCFs were then intervened with conditioned medium from H9C2 cells treated with si-RNA and PA (Fig. 6H), and it was found that NLRP3 knockdown markedly alleviated the activation of RCFs (Fig. 6I). Alternatively, the H9C2 cells treated with si-RNA and PA were co-cultured with RCFs in the manner shown in Fig. 5J. The levels of collagen I, collagen III and α-SMA were detected by IF (Fig. 5K–M), and the same results were obtained. Furthermore, upon additional treatment of AC16 cells with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 and repetition of the aforementioned experiments, identical results were obtained (Fig. S10A–D). Collectively, PA stimulation caused the onset of NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in cardiomyocytes, which led to increased CYLD protein level, cellular oxidative stress and injury, as well as activation of cardiac fibroblast.

Knockdown of CYLD alleviated PA-induced H9C2 cells damage, oxidative stress and RCFs activation

The causal relationship between CYLD upregulation, cellular oxidative stress and injury in cardiomyocytes induced by PA was further explored by knockdown of CYLD in H9C2 cells with si-RNA (Fig. 7A). Obviously, knockdown of CYLD didn’t affect PA-induced upregulation of NLRP3 (Fig. 7B), which further validated that pyroptosis was the upstream event of CYLD activation in PA-stimulated H9C2 cells. However, PA-induced cardiomyocytes oxidative stress (Fig. 7C–E) and damage (Fig. 7F) were relieved after CYLD knockdown. RCFs were again treated with conditioned medium from H9C2 cells (Fig. 7G) or co-cultured with H9C2 cells after si-CYLD and PA treatment (Fig. 7H–J). Similarly, knockdown of CYLD efficaciously restrained the RCFs activation indirectly induced by PA stimulation. In conclusion, PA triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation-dependent pyroptosis in H9C2 cells, resulting in an increase in CYLD protein level, which in turn led to cardiomyocyte damage and oxidative stress, as well as the activation of RCFs.

Knockdown of NOX4 inhibited RCFs activation indirectly induced by PA-stimulated H9C2 cells injury

The relationship between oxidative stress in H9C2 cells and RCFs activation was further investigated by knockdown of NOX4 in H9C2 cells using siRNA (Fig. 8A). By treating RCFs with conditioned medium from H9C2 cells following si-NOX4 and PA treatment (Fig. 8B), or by co-culturing RCFs with H9C2 cells after si-NOX4 and PA treatment (Fig. 8C), it was found that NOX4 knockdown also effectively suppressed PA‑indirectly-induced RCFs activation. In summary, PA triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation, leading to pyroptosis. This process increased CYLD protein level, induced NOX4-mediated oxidative stress in H9C2 cells, and ultimately activated RCFs.

HT ameliorated myocardial fibrosis in obese mice through restraining oxidative stress induced by the activation of NLRP3/CYLD/NOX4

As previously reported, CYLD acted as a deubiquitinating enzyme in vascular adventitial fibroblasts, promoting oxidative stress-induced vascular remodeling by deubiquitinating NOX4 in adventitial fibroblasts. Furthermore, it has been discovered that CYLD can directly bind to NOX4 and exert a direct deubiquitinating effect on it21. Therefore, we hypothesized that CYLD might function as a deubiquitinating enzyme to up-regulate NOX4 protein level in the pathogenesis of HFD- and PA-induced cardiomyocytes injury and oxidative stress, as well as activation of cardiac fibroblast. As shown in the Co-IP result (Fig. 9A, B), compared with the CON group, the mutual binding level of CYLD and NOX4 was distinctly up-regulated, and the ubiquitination level of NOX4 was apparently reduced in the HFD and PA groups, thus the protein level of NOX4 was enhanced. However, under the action of HT, the inhibition of CYLD protein brought down its interaction with NOX4, which in turn contributed to ubiquitination and degradation of NOX4 protein. Moreover, the application of proteasome inhibitor MG132 eliminated the difference in NOX4 expression between the PA and PA + HT groups (Fig. 9C), confirming the involvement of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway in NOX4 degradation. Therefore, in conjunction with the findings of Yu et al.21, CYLD also promoted oxidative stress by deubiquitinating NOX4, ultimately leading to myocardial injury and fibrosis under high-fat exposure. Nevertheless, HT increased the ubiquitination level of NOX4 by suppressing the deubiquitinase CYLD, thereby alleviating NOX4-mediated oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, to verify whether HT achieved its protective effect on the heart through the NLRP3/CYLD/NOX4 pathway, the plasmid overexpressing NLRP3 and CYLD was utilized in H9C2 cells (Fig. 9D, F). It was found that NLRP3 overexpression abolished the effect of HT on the expression of NLRP3, CYLD and NOX4 protein under the circumstance of PA (Fig. 9E). Following overexpression of CYLD, HT still mitigated PA’s effects on NLRP3 but failed to alleviate PA-induced alterations in NOX4 levels (Fig. 9G). Furthermore, molecular docking revealed that HT and NLRP3 could directly interact with a moderate binding affinity (binding energy: −5.9 Kcal/mol, Fig. 8H), involving two hydrogen bonds. In conclusion, HT ameliorated myocardial remodeling in obese mice by inhibiting NLRP3/CYLD/NOX4-induced oxidative stress.

Discussion

Cardiometabolic conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, and obesity, are significant risk factors for CVDs. Myocardial fibrosis, an irreversible and progressive pathological change, is a complication and final common pathway of these conditions, potentially leading to heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden death31. Therefore, preventing myocardial fibrosis is the key to reducing the burden of CVDs. It has been found that chronic inflammation mediated by macrophage Doublecortin-like kinase 1 (DCLK1) promotes cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis in HFD-induced obese mice32. In obese diabetic mice, TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling specific to cardiac fibroblasts mediated ventricular fibrosis, hypertrophy, and dysfunction33. In addition, pyroptosis mediated by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and ferroptosis dependent on iron overload in myocardial fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction under the state of obesity have been preliminarily explored by a number of studies34,35. However, in the initial stage of obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis, the driving cytotoxic pattern and its underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. In order to determine the time point at which myocardial fibrosis occurs in obese mice fed with HFD, blood and heart tissue samples were collected at 12 and 16 weeks of dietary intervention to detect markers of myocardial damage, oxidative stress, and fibrosis. At 12 weeks of HFD feeding, no obvious myocardial injury, oxidative stress and myocardial fibrosis were observed. By the 16th week of HFD, evaluation of histopathology and molecular markers suggested that the heart of obese mice began to develop fibrosis, accompanied by myocardial injury and oxidative stress, as well as a significant increase in LVAW and an increasing trend in LVPW and LV mass examined by ultrasound. Taken together, the 16th week of HFD was the starting point of myocardial fibrosis in obese mice. Consistent with the assessment of myocardial morphology and molecular markers, there were no significant changes in either pyroptosis- or ferroptosis-related markers in the cardiac tissue of mice after 12 weeks of HFD. At 16 weeks of intervention, the serum/heart iron level, cardiac lipid peroxide MDA level, and antioxidant enzyme GPX4 level were all increased in HFD mice, while the ferroptotic marker proteins ACSL4 and COX2 remained unchanged. Among them, ACSL4 is the key enzyme to insert polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) into the cell membrane36, and COX2 is an important protein mediating PUFA peroxidation37, while the most characteristic sign of ferroptosis refers to cell death caused by membrane PUFA peroxidation dependent on iron accumulation, and the depletion of GPX4 antioxidant system38. Based on the test of the above markers, we believed that there might be disorders of iron metabolism in the cardiac tissue at 16 weeks of HFD, while the antioxidant system was still in a compensatory activation stage to counteract oxidative stress, thus the cell membrane had not yet suffered from lipid peroxidation injury, and no substantial ferroptosis occurred in the cardiomyocyte. However, at this time point, all the pyroptosis-related markers were significantly up-regulated in cardiac tissue, which strongly confirmed the occurrence of pyroptosis. Additionally, we examined the apoptosis markers, cleaved caspase-3 and TUNEL staining, in the myocardium of mice after 16 weeks of HFD, with no significant changes observed. Therefore, we suggested that pyroptosis was prevalently involved in the initiation of obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis. As is well known, NLRP3 inflammasome activation involves two steps: priming and assembly. During the priming step, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs, e.g., lipopolysaccharide [LPS]), damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, e.g., ATP, ROS, K⁺ efflux, crystalline substances such as cholesterol, uric acid and islet amyloid polypeptide [IAPP]) and excess nutrients (e.g., glucose, non-esterified fatty acid [NEFAs]) trigger toll-like receptor (TLR) and IL-1 signaling, initiating expression of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory genes, including IL-1β, IL-18, NLRP3, and others. This subsequently leads to NLRP3 assembly with ASC and Caspase-1 to form inflammasomes, mediating pyroptosis. Consistently, obesity is characterized by systemic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance, often accompanied by nutrient excess, along with intestinal barrier damage and gut dysbiosis. Consequently, elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, IAPP, glucose, NEFAs, cholesterol, and LPS are frequently observed in obese patients or mice, as well as upregulated ROS levels. All of the above metabolic risks may activate the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in obese individuals. Our results align with findings by Wang et al. in a transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced chronic heart failure mouse model, showing that pyroptosis participates in the early stage of myocardial remodeling (the first week post-TAC surgery). Furthermore, they conducted additional studies revealing that by week 8 post-TAC (the late stage of myocardial remodeling), pyroptosis was absent while ferroptosis became prominent39. Although our study did not longitudinally track subsequent phases, given prior research indicating the involvement of ferroptosis in obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis, we supposed that both PCD pathways participated in obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis through a “sequential relay” rather than in an overlapping manner, but this hypothesis required further investigation.

Considering that myocardial enzymes are released after cardiomyocyte injury, while myocardial fibrosis mainly occurs upon activation of cardiac fibroblasts, besides, since we identified cardiomyocytes as the primary cells undergoing pyroptosis through IF colocalization, we constructed a co-culture system of H9C2 cells and RCFs in vitro. To enhance the translational relevance of our findings, we simultaneously employed a co-culture system of human-derived AC16 cells and HCFs. Saturated fatty acid PA was used to simulate a high-fat environment, which could also trigger the activation of cardiac fibroblasts through cardiomyocyte injury in vitro. At the same time, cardiomyocyte pyroptosis but not ferroptosis occurred at the time point when cardiac fibroblasts were activated. Moreover, PA-induced cardiomyocyte injury, oxidative stress and RCFs/HCFs activation were effectively reversed after inhibition of NLRP3 in H9C2 cells and AC16 cells, indicating that cardiomyocyte pyroptosis plays a driving role in myocardial remodeling induced by high-fat exposure. Furthermore, to investigate the potential mechanisms by which cardiomyocyte pyroptosis activated cardiac fibroblasts, we conducted antibody neutralization experiments, which confirmed that inflammatory mediators released during cardiomyocytes pyroptosis (IL-18, IL-1β) served as effective stimuli for the pro-fibrotic activation of cardiac fibroblasts. Therefore, through in vivo and in vitro studies, we demonstrated for the first time that the initiation node of myocardial remodeling in obesity was driven by pyroptosis of cardiomyocytes.

In addition, HFD- and PA-induced activation of cardiac fibroblasts in this study was accompanied by high expression of CYLD, which is consistent with the findings in multiple previous studies that CYLD plays a key role in myocardial injury and remodeling12,13,40. However, in hypoxic cancer cells, decreased levels of CYLD have been found to lead to NF-κB activation and induce NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis17. In the osteoporotic mouse model, overexpression of CYLD could inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome-triggered pyroptosis through deubiquitinating of WNK1 (no lysine kinase 1), thereby inducing osteogenic differentiation and alleviating osteoporosis41. In contrast to previous studies, we found that inhibiting NLRP3 significantly suppressed PA-induced CYLD upregulation, NOX4 augmentation, ROS accumulation and RCFs activation, while silence of CYLD did not affect PA-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation, but markedly suppressed NOX4 protein, ROS levels, and RCFs activation. Furthermore, we found that knockdown of NOX4 in cardiomyocytes significantly suppressed the activation of cardiac fibroblasts. Therefore, the cascade relationship between NLRP3, CYLD and NOX4 in fibrotic myocardium caused by high-fat exposure was first elucidated in the present study, and it has been revealed that a high-fat state could increase CYLD levels by activating NLRP3 inflammasoma-mediated pyroptosis in cardiomyocytes, thereby promoting NOX4-mediated oxidative stress, which eventually led to myocardial injury and fibrosis.

In the activation of vascular adventitial fibroblasts and vascular remodeling, Yu et al. have uncovered that CYLD directly binds to NOX4 and contributes to the stabilization of NOX4 via removing both K63- and K48-linked ubiquitin chains, while mutation or knockout of CYLD promoted the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of NOX4, thus leading to the conclusion that CYLD directly acts on NOX4 to exert a deubiquitinating function21. Consistent results were yielded in our study that the protein interaction between CYLD and NOX4 also existed in cardiomyocytes. Under high-fat conditions, the interaction between CYLD and NOX4 was intensified, resulting in prominent deubiquitination and more stable NOX4, thereby mediating the generation of more ROS.

As the most bioactive small molecular polyphenol in virgin olive oil, HT possesses extremely strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, and its health benefits for CVDs have been confirmed by a large number of studies. Meanwhile, as a botanical extract, HT exhibits a relatively broad effective concentration range. In diabetic mice, HT administration has shown significant efficacious across experimental doses of 5 to 100 mg/kg42. Furthermore, polar toxicity studies indicated that no toxicity was observed in rats at a dose up to 500 mg/kg by gavage43. Moreover, it has been reported that HT plays a cardioprotective role in both doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity and MI/RI models28,29. Similarly, Poudyal et al. illustrated that HT ameliorated cardiac dysfunction and myocardial remodeling in HFD-induced obese rats, but the underlying mechanism was not further elaborated30. Here, our study clarified that HT intervention could effectively counteract HFD-induced myocardial injury and fibrosis in male mice, and inhibited the accompanying cardiomyocytes pyroptosis, as well as the augmented CYLD protein level and NOX4-mediated oxidative stress. Whereas, after overexpression of NLRP3, the upstream protein of this pathway, HT could no longer exert its effect on the increase of NLRP3, CYLD, and NOX4 protein levels in H9C2 cells stimulated by PA. Mechanically, given the molecular docking analysis of HT and NLRP3, HT might curb the inflammasome assembly by binding to NLRP3. Then, this receded the binding of CYLD and NOX4, which in turn caused ubiquitination degradation of NOX4 protein, ultimately relieving myocardial oxidative stress. In a similar way, overexpression of CYLD also eliminated the inhibitory effect of HT on NOX4, but not NLRP3. Therefore, it was confirmed that HT protected against myocardial injury and myocardial fibrosis in obese mice through oxidative stress mediated by the NLRP3/CYLD/NOX4 axis.

Our study only elucidated the role of pyroptosis in initiating obesity-induced myocardial fibrosis, but did not further investigate the temporal dynamics between pyroptosis and ferroptosis. Furthermore, the exclusion of female mice limited the generalizability of our findings and warranted further exploration. However, in our previous investigation on the effect of HT in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), HT could mitigate liver injury and steatosis in MAFLD patients regardless of sex, implying there may be no gender-specific differences in the biological effects of HT44. Finally, the present study validated the role of the NLRP3/CYLD/NOX4 pathway in the beneficial effects of HT, mainly in vitro, without further investigation in genetically modified mouse models.

Methods

Animals and treatment

The animal experiment procedures in this study have been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Dalian Medical University (approval number: AEE23051). Fifty-four 4-week-old specific pathogen free (SPF) C57BL/6J male mice were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [License number: SYXK (Liao) 2024-0002], and housed in temperature- and humidity-controlled SPF facilities with a 12 h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. After 7 days of acclimatization feeding, 18 mice were given standard chow diet (10% fat for energy, XTCON50J, Jiangsu Synergistic Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd.) and gavaged with 0.1 mL/10 g of distilled water as the control (CON) group, 18 mice were fed with 60% HFD (XTHF60, Jiangsu Synergistic Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd.) and gavaged with 0.1 mL/10 g distilled water as the HFD group, 18 mice were assigned to the HFD + HT group and were given HFD feeding and HT gavage (50 mg/kg/day, ≥96%, Xi’an Kangqi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). HT was freshly prepared prior to use, and animal manipulation was performed daily from 16:00–17:00. At 12 weeks of intervention, 9 mice in each group were sacrificed, and the remaining mice continued to receive treatment for another 4 weeks (for a total of 16 weeks) and underwent echocardiography. After the intervention, mice in each group were fasted for 12 h overnight, and their body weight and fasting blood glucose (FBG) were measured the next morning. Anesthesia was performed by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), and serum and dissected cardiac tissue were collected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at -80°C.

Regarding the rationale for the dosage of HT gavage: the concentration of HT in olive oil has been reported varying from 1.55 ~ 14.42 mg/kg31, and a daily intake of approximately 50 g of olive oil in a population with a Mediterranean diet results in a maximum intake of 0.72 mg of HT32. Based on the average weight of 30 g of HFD-induced obese mice, an intragastric HT content of 25 mg/kg would ensure 0.75 mg HT consumption per day for each mouse, which was consistent with the intake of people on a Mediterranean diet. In our preliminary experiments years ago, HT of 25 mg/kg and 50 mg/kg were orally administered to HFD mice, while the high dose intake of HT exerted significant metabolism benefits33, which was also adopted in our subsequent experiments34 and other publications32,35,36.

Cell culture

The rat cardiac cell line (H9C2) was purchased from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences, rat cardiac fibroblast (RCF), human-derived cardiomyocyte cell line (AC16), and human-derived cardiac fibroblast (HCF) were purchased from Guangzhou Huatuo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). H9C2 cells, RCFs and AC16 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. HCF was cultured in fibroblast-specific medium (HTX2125M, Guangzhou Huatuo Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China). Since cardiac fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes account for about 90% of the total number of cardiac cells, among which cardiac fibroblasts account for 60% ~ 70%, in order to better simulate the co-metabolism of cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts, the H9C2/RCF and AC16/HCF co-culture model was established. In brief, H9C2 or AC16 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of 24-well transwell plates at a density of about 5000 cells per well. After 24 h of cell seeding in the upper chamber, they were treated with palmitic acid (PA, SP8060, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, China) and/or HT, followed by replacement with a new complete medium without PA and/or HT and cocultivation with the appropriate density of RCFs or HCFs in the lower chamber for another 24 h. Then, the RCFs/HCFs activation-related proteins were detected by immunofluorescence (IF).

For the configuration of PA, 20 mg of PA was dissolved in 750 μL of DMSO (D8371, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) in a 70 °C water bath. Then, 10% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA, 9048-46-8, Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) solution was configured with DMEM (DMEM:BSA = 10 mL:1 g) and placed in a 55 °C water bath. Subsequently, PA heated in a 70°C water bath was quickly added to 10% fatty acid-free BSA in a 55 °C water bath, shaken to full dissolution, prepared into a 5 mM stock solution, filtered and packaged, and stored at −20 °C.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and underwent echocardiography with a Vevo®3100 High-Resolution Preclinical Imaging System (FUJIFILM Visual Sonics, Canada). The left ventricle anterior wall thickness at end diastole (LVAW; d), LVAW thickness at end systole (LVAW; s), LV posterior wall thickness at end diastole (LVPW; d), LVPW thickness at end systole (LVPW; s) and LV mass were measured.

Histology staining

After mice were sacrificed, hearts were fixed in 10% formalin (H-80096618, Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., China), embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining were used to assess the myocardial remodeling. Observation and image capture were performed using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Myocardial tissue (1 × 1 × 1 mm3) was quickly removed from the apex and immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (111-30-8, Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., China). After the samples were dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, fixed, and stained, they were observed and photographed using a Hitachi transmission electron microscope (Japan).

Western blot (WB)

The whole protein extraction kit was purchased from Jiangsu KeyGen Biotech Co., Ltd. (KGB5303-100, China), and an appropriate amount of heart tissue or collected cells was placed in 1× RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The samples were then ground, lysed and centrifuged to obtain protein supernatants, followed by determination of protein concentrations with the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay kit (A55860, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins were separated by 8% or 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA), which were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next morning, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, then combined with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, P2300, New Saimei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), and imaged with the Bio-Rad ChemiDocTM MP (USA) and LICOR Odyssey 9120 (USA) imaging system. After that, Image J (National Institutes of Health, USA) was used to analyze and calculate the gray values of each protein band. Subsequently, normalization was performed by dividing the gray value of the target band by that of the internal control band, with the resulting ratio used for statistical analysis. The antibodies used in this experiment are shown in Table S1.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (IP) assay

The Co-IP kit was purchased from Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology CO., Ltd. (P2179M, China). In this experiment, tissue and cell proteins were extracted using procedures in WB. According to the protein concentration of heart tissue or cells determined by BCA assay, the lysate containing 2 mg of total protein was used for IP. Specific antibodies were added to the lysates of IP and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The next morning, 30 μL magnetic beads were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with rotation. After discarding the supernatant and washing the magnetic beads three times, 100 μL of 1× SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer was added, and the supernatant was collected after heating at 95 °C for 5 min and stored at −20 °C for future use as immunoblotting.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

After co-culturing with H9C2/AC16 cells, RCFs and HCFs cells seeded on slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, H-80096618, Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., China) for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-100 (T8200, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) for 10 min at room temperature, and blocked with 5% BSA (KGL2314-10, Jiangsu KeyGen Biotech Co., Ltd., China) for 1 h. Then, cells were incubated with diluted primary antibodies (Table S1) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were incubated with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies (Table S1) for 1 h at room temperature and nucleated with DAPI solution (C0065, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) for 5 min before being sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching sealant (S2100, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Finally, images were observed and captured using Nikon (Japan) and Olympus (Japan) inverted fluorescence microscopes, and the intensity of green fluorescence was analyzed using Image J.

For the IF of myocardial tissue, after being fixed with 4% PFA and dehydrated with a gradient of sucrose, the tissue was embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound and prepared for frozen sections. Then, sections were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature, incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against NLRP3, cardiac troponin I (cTnT), and F4/80 (Table S1), followed by incubation with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Table S1) at room temperature for 1 h the next day. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI solution (C0065, Beijing Sol Biotech Co., Ltd.) for 5 min, and anti-fluorescence quencher (S2100, Beijing Sol Biotech Co., Ltd.) was mounted on sections. Observation and image capture were performed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of interleukin (IL)-1β (BPE20533), IL-18 (BPE21213) and creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB) (BPE20897) were measured by ELISA kits (Shanghai Lengton Bioscience, China). The levels of IL-1β (BPE30419), IL-18 (BPE30650) and CK-MB (BPE30801) in the culture medium of H9C2 and AC16 cells were also determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Biochemistry assay

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, A020-2) content in the serum and culture medium, malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1) content in cardiac tissues and cells, iron level in tissues/cells (A039-2-1) and serum/culture medium (A039-1-1) were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). For all indicators in the heart, a small amount of heart tissue (30 mg) was homogenized in normal saline (270 μL), and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation for analysis. For all indicators in cells, 200 μL of normal saline was added to the cell precipitate of each dish for sonication and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation for analysis.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay

In order to determine the optimal concentration of HT applied in H9C2 cells stimulated by PA, H9C2 cells were first treated with HT at different concentration gradients (5, 10, 15, and 20 μg/mL), and the cell viability was detected by CCK8 kit (KGA9306-500, Jiangsu KeyGen Biotech Co., Ltd., China) to determine the HT concentration without cytotoxic effect. Then, H9C2 cells were co-treated with HT at non-cytotoxic concentrations (5, 10, and 15 μg/mL) and PA, and cell viability was detected again by CCK8 assay to determine the optimal protective concentration of HT on cardiomyocytes under PA stimulation, which was used for subsequent experiments.

Measurement of ROS

H9C2 or AC16 cells were seeded onto cell slides in 24-well plates. After treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µM of DCFH-DA (KGA7308-100, Jiangsu KeyGen Biotech Co., Ltd., China) at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing, the cells were observed and photographed under Nikon (Japan) and Olympus (Japan) inverted fluorescence microscope, and the intensity of green fluorescence was analyzed using Image J.

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS

H9C2 cells were seeded onto cell slides in 24-well plates. After treatment, cells were incubated with 200 μL of MitoSOX Red (HY-D1055, MedChemExpress, USA) at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing, the cells were observed and photographed under an Olympus (Japan) inverted fluorescence microscope, and the intensity of red fluorescence was analyzed using Image J.

Tunel staining

H9C2 or AC16 cells were seeded onto cell slides in 24-well plates. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-100 for 10 min at room temperature. After processing, TUNEL detection solution (C1089, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C in the dark for 60 min. After being washed with PBS, the slides were stained with DAPI solution for 5 min and then sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching sealant. Finally, images were observed and captured using a Olympus inverted fluorescence microscope, and the intensity of fluorescence was analyzed using Image J.

For TUNEL staining of myocardial tissue, paraffin sections from each group were prepared, and the staining was performed according to the kit instructions. Finally, samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope Nikon (Japan), and images were captured.

Transfection

si-RNA targeting rat NLRP3, CYLD and NOX4 was constructed and synthesized by General Biology Co., Ltd. (China). The specific sequences are shown in Table S2. The overexpression plasmid targeting rat NLRP3 and CYLD was constructed and synthesized by Suzhou Jima Gene Co., Ltd. (China). H9C2 or AC16 cells were subcultured to 10 cm dishes for 24 h and transfected with si-RNA or plasmid using LipofectamineTM 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen, USA) for 6 h followed by replacement of the culture medium. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with PA or PA + HT for another 24 h.

Statistical analysis

All data were described as mean ± standard error. SPSS 27.0 was used for statistical analysis, and GraphPad Prism 9.5 was used for plotting. The independent sample t-test was used to compare the statistical differences between the two groups; One-way ANOVA combined with LSD post hoc test was used to compare statistical differences among multiple groups, and Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test was applied when variances were unequal. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
