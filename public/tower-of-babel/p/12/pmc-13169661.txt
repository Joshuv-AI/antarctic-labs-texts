Parenting style and mental health issues among primary school children in rural China
Authors: Jing Tian, Lei Wang, Steven Sun Zhao, Sicong Li, Zekai Fu, Scott Rozelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health problems among young children have become a common public health issue worldwide. As the family plays a crucial role in creating a favorable environment for children’s mental health, the parenting style of caregivers is a key factor related to children’s mental health issues. This study aims to examine the associations between parenting styles of primary caregivers and the mental health of their primary school child in rural China and identifies the factors that may influence the adoption of different parenting styles.MethodsData were collected from 1,298 primary school children (aged 9–11) and their primary caregivers in northwestern China. The primary caregivers completed the Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire, and a socio-demographic questionnaire. The cognitive development of children was measured using the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence-Fourth Edition, and the non-cognitive development of children was evaluated using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire: Social-Emotional. We performed descriptive statistics, linear regression analysis, and multivariable regression analysis.ResultsOur data showed that 17% of the sampled children exhibited total difficulty problems. The results indicated that authoritative parenting style was negatively associated with child mental health problems. In contrast, authoritarian parenting style was positively associated with child mental health problems. Analysis of combined parenting styles revealed that the combinations that included relatively more intensive authoritative parenting styles was associated with reduced incidence of child mental health problems. In contrast, the combinations that were found to have relatively more authoritarian parenting styles were associated with higher rates of mental health issues among children. Demographic characteristics, such as child gender, child developmental outcomes, child attending preschools, identity of primary caregiver, and family economic status, that were associated with the adoption of different parenting styles.ConclusionsThe study indicates that an authoritative parenting style is positively associated child mental health outcomes. Likewise, when authoritative strategies are more prominent within combined parenting styles, the likelihood that a child will experience mental health problems decreases. In contrast, the more dominant authoritarian behaviors are, the greater the risk of child mental health problems. Therefore, primary caregivers should be encouraged to adopt more positive parenting styles, which can help promote better outcomes of child mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04395-8.

Introduction

Child mental health issues have become a serious public health challenge across the world in recent years [1, 2]. Research has indicated that, globally, rates of mental health problems among primary school children remain relatively high [3]. It is estimated that LMICs bear a large share of the world’s mental health problems (approximately 80%) [4]. The literature also has shown that mental health problems at an early age can persist until adulthood if left untreated and tend to have long-term negative effects on an individual’s social and emotional development in later life [5, 6]. As such, it is vital to identify and treat childhood mental health problems as early as possible.

When investigating factors that are associated with the mental health of young children, the literature has found that the parenting style of caregivers is one key that is linked with child mental health issues, since the family plays such a critical role in creating favorable environment for child mental health [7, 8] According to the literature, different styles of parenting (authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive), classified by Baumrind [9], have different effects on child mental health [10–13]. Specifically, authoritative parenting style predicts higher levels of social emotional skills and fewer problematic behaviors among young children [10, 13]. In contrast, it has been found that authoritarian parenting style is associated with child emotional maladjustment, more inattention, and delinquency [11, 12]. Children parented using a permissive style also are more likely to have mental health problems, for example, more anger, more anxiety, and under-developed self-regulation skills [14–16]. A study conducted both in Australia and Indonesia revealed that, among children aged 5 to 9 years old in both countries, higher levels of authoritative parenting were associated with better mental health outcomes. In contrast, higher levels of authoritarian parenting were associated with poorer emotional regulation and more behavioral problems [11]. These discrepancies may be attributed to the fact that authoritative parents balance emotional warmth with clear expectations, granting children appropriate autonomy alongside consistent guidance. Authoritarian parents, however, tend to prioritize obedience and control while being less responsive to their children’s emotional needs. An authoritarian parenting style can impede emotional expression and regulation, and may contribute to an increase in mental health problems [11, 17, 18].

Given that different depths of associations between different parenting styles and child mental health outcomes, it is important to identify factors that may be linked to the adoptions of different parenting styles by caregivers. The literature has shown that certain characteristics of children and families can affect the extent to which parenting style tends to be chosen [19–22]. Regarding the characteristics of children, the level of development of children or the depth of developmental delay are critical factors that influence the choice of parenting style [20, 21, 23–25]. These studies have shown that, compared with normally developed children, developmentally-delayed children have unique and differentiated characteristics which create obstacles when the caregivers try to use positive parenting styles. Beyond children’s developmental levels, prior studies have also shown that caregivers use different parenting styles with children of different genders [19, 22]. Specifically, boys are more likely to experience authoritarian parenting, whereas girls tend to receive authoritative parenting. For instance, Berkien and coauthors found that the ideas of boys received more rejection than those of girls, whereas girls experienced greater emotional warmth from their parents [19]. Similarly, a study using a sample of children from China [26] noted that boys were more likely to be subjected to parental physical aggression than girls. These differential parenting patterns are often linked to deep-seated gender stereotypes and societal expectations regarding how children of different genders “should” behave. In China, in particular, traditional masculine gender roles remain prevalent [26]—roles that emphasize traits, such as toughness and resilience. Consequently, parents in China may adopt authoritarian parenting approaches toward boys, encouraging them to tolerate pain and distress and reduce any emotional responsiveness, whereas girls are often raised with more emotional support and communicative engagement.

Regarding the characteristics of family, the literature has investigated the associations between socioeconomic status (SES) of a family and the parenting styles of caregivers [21, 27, 28]. Findings of these studies demonstrate that when the levels of education and income of a family are higher, caregivers are more likely to parent their child with a more authoritative style and less authoritarian parenting style. For example, in the study by Luo et al., parents with higher levels of SES were found to be more warm and responsive to their children, whereas those from lower SES families tended to be more controlling and restrictive to their children [28].

In China, it is estimated that over 30 million children and adolescents suffer from mental health problems [29]. Previous research has indicated that the prevalence of mental health problems among primary school children in China ranges from 10% to 30% [30, 31]. Research has demonstrated that the prevalence among rural children is notably higher than that of their urban peers [30, 31]. With approximately 110.31 million rural children [32], the mental health of rural children in China is of particular concern to the country.

The literature in China has begun to examine the sources of mental health problems among primary school children [31, 33, 34]. The empirical evidence has shown that the style of parenting used by the caregiver is one of the primary sources of mental health problems for their children [33, 34], which is consistent with findings in the literature on this topic outside of China. A study involving 469 left-behind children in primary school revealed that protective parenting practices of caregivers can mitigate the occurrence of mental health problems among left-behind children [33]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study by J. Wang et al. [34] also found that, in urban families, emotional warmth in parenting styles was negatively associated with child mental health problems. In contrast, when caregivers adopt parenting styles that are characterized by rejection, their child is more likely to have mental health problems [34].

Since the mental health problems among primary school children in rural China have become a growing concern, coupled with increasing recognition of the role that parenting styles play in child psychological outcomes, investigating the mental health challenges among rural primary school children and exploring the associations between the parenting styles of caregivers and the mental health problems of their children can guide effective interventions to bolster child mental health outcomes. Much of the existing literature focuses on Western or urban (Chinese) contexts, overlooking the unique socioeconomic conditions of rural China [35–37]. To the best of our knowledge, there is no related study has been conducted among primary school children and their families in rural China.

To fill this gap in the literature, the current study aims to examine the associations between the parenting styles used by caregivers and the mental health of primary school children in rural China, using a cross-sectional data collected by the research team. To achieve this goal, the current study has four objectives. First, the study examines different parenting styles of rural caregivers and the mental health outcomes of their primary school children. Second, the study investigates the link between parenting styles (authoritative and authoritarian) of caregivers and the mental health problems of their primary school children. Third, the study explores the associations between different combinations of the authoritative and authoritarian parenting styles and child mental health problems. Finally, the study identifies factors that may influence the adoption of parenting styles by caregivers.

Methods

Participants

The data for the current study were drawn from a longitudinal study conducted in 11 nationally designated poverty counties in a province in northwestern China. The sample selection for this longitudinal study was carried out in 2013 using a multistage cluster sampling design. First, all townships (n = 174) in the 11 study counties were included in the study. Next, we randomly selected two villages from each of the sample townships. In total, 351 villages were included. Finally, after excluding children with known diseases or disabilities, we selected all of the remaining children that were within the target age range (6–12 months) for inclusion in the longitudinal study. Following the baseline survey, the sample children and households were followed up multiple times. The two most recent follow-up surveys took place in 2017 (when the sample children were in preschool) and 2023 (when they were in primary school). All participants included in the current study completed the follow-up surveys in both 2017 and 2023. In total, 1,298 primary school children (Mean age = 10.15 years; 629 females) and their primary caregivers completed survey forms and were included in the study. The primary caregiver, the person that is mainly responsible for the child’s care during the day/evening, is typically either the child’s mother or the grandmother. All participating caregivers gave their informed consent for the involvement in the study for both themselves and their children. The data were collected both by questionnaires that were given to the primary caregivers as well as through one-on-one assessment of the cognitive development of each child.

This study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (Protocol ID 25734). The data collection for this study was performed by enumerators that were recruited from local universities. In total, there were 106 enumerators that conducted the in-the-field survey work for data collection.

Measures

Child mental health

Child mental health was measured using a 25-item caregiver-reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [38]. The scale produces a Total Difficulties score (including subscales of internalizing and externalizing problems) and a Prosocial Behavior score. Among the main subscales, Internalizing problems can be further divided into Emotional symptoms and Peer relationship problems subscales; Externalizing problems include Conduct problems and Hyperactivity problems subscales. All items were scored on a three-point Likert scale (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat true, and 2 = certainly true). The higher the Total Difficulties score, the lower the level of child mental health. In contrast, higher Prosocial Behavior scores represent better outcomes of child mental health. Each subscale is further divided into three categories: “normal,” “borderline,” and “abnormal.” The cutoffs of the three categories for each subscale can be found in Appendix Table A1. In the current study, children with scores falling in the abnormal or borderline range on the Total difficulties scale and individual subscales were classified as having mental health problems. In our sample, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subscales of the SDQ were 0.75 (Total Difficulties), 0.64 (Internalizing Problems), 0.71 (Externalizing Problems), and 0.70 (Prosocial scale). These values are consistent with prior use of these measures in China [22, 31], indicating acceptable to good reliability.

Parenting styles

Parenting styles of primary caregivers were measured using Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire-Short Version (PSDQ-Short Version; [39]). This widely used instrument assesses caregiver daily caregiver-child interaction attitudes and behavior, and typically includes three subscales: authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive parenting. Given that prior research indicating that permissive parenting is rarely used by Chinese caregivers [40–43] and that its construct has low reliability in the China’s cultural context [44–46], the permissive subscale was excluded from this study’s data collection and subsequent analysis. Instead, 27 items were adopted to measure the remaining two subscales: 15 items for authoritative parenting and 12 items for authoritarian parenting [39]. The authoritative parenting subscale can be further divided into Connection, Regulation and Autonomy granting dimensions, while the authoritarian parenting subscale includes Physical coercion, Verbal hostility and Non-reasoning / Punitive dimensions. Primary caregivers responded to each item based on their parenting practices on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Higher scores on each scale indicate a greater alignment of the parenting practice of the primary caregiver with the corresponding parenting style. The authoritative and authoritarian scales demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.87 and 0.81, respectively.

In order to investigate different combinations of parenting styles, we classified the caregivers into four different combinations of groups using the medians of scores on the authoritative and authoritarian subscales. Specifically, caregivers whose authoritative subscale ratings were higher than the median but whose authoritarian subscale ratings were lower than the median were classified as Group 1 (High authoritative, low authoritarian). Caregivers whose authoritative subscale scores were lower than the median but whose authoritarian subscale scores were higher than the median were classified as Group 2 (High authoritarian, low authoritative). Caregivers whose authoritative subscale and authoritarian subscale scores were higher than the medians were classified as Group 3 (High authoritative, high authoritarian). Caregivers whose authoritative subscale and authoritarian subscale scores fell below the medians were classified as Group 4 (Low authoritative, low authoritarian). In the analysis, we take Group 4 as the reference group.

Child cognitive development

The Chinese version of the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence-Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV; [47]) was used to assess child cognitive development during the preschool stage. Prior to the survey, permission to use the WPPSI-IV was obtained from Zhuhai King-May Psychological Assessment Technology Development Co., Ltd, the copyright owner of the Chinese version of the WPPSI in China. WPPSI-IV produces a Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ), which is a composite score that summarizes the cognitive ability of a child across a diverse set of domains. Children with a FSIQ scores lower than one standard deviation below the mean are considered to be cognitive developmentally delayed. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.80, indicating a high level of reliability.

Child non-cognitive development

The Ages and Stages Questionnaire: Social-Emotional (ASQ: SE) was used to measure the non-cognitive development of children during the preschool stage [48]. The scores for each item of the ASQ: SE, based on the primary caregiver’s responses, are aggregated to generate a composite total score. A higher total score suggests that the child may be susceptible to non-cognitive issues, whereas a lower total score indicates that the child’s social and emotional behaviors align with typical developmental expectations for their age group. Scores higher than the cutoff set for different ages are indicative of potential social-emotional challenges. The cutoffs can be found in Appendix Table A2. The Chinese version of the ASQ: SE scale demonstrates a reliability coefficient of 0.94.

Socio-demographic characteristics

Information regarding the characteristics of the sample children and households were self-reported by the primary caregiver of each child. Items in the questionnaire included child age, gender, whether the child had siblings, whether the child attended preschool, whether the child was boarding at primary school, whether any parent was an out-migrant, the identity of the primary caregiver, the age and educational level of primary caregiver, and household assets.

Statistical analysis

To examine the associations between parenting styles and child mental health outcomes, we first performed descriptive statistical analysis and then conducted linear regression analysis. We also performed linear regressions to examine the determinants of parenting styles. In the regressions for examining the associations between parenting styles and child mental health, we estimated and reported standardized coefficients. In the regressions for investigating the determinants of parenting styles, unstandardized coefficients were estimated and presented. Following previous studies [49], we also reported the Adjusted R2 for each regression to clarify the magnitude and implications of the observed effects. All analyses were adjusted for child and household characteristics, using the variables that were described above. All standard errors accounted for clustering at the village level. County and tester fixed effect also were controlled for. We used Stata version 16.0 to conduct all analyses.

Associations between parenting styles and child mental health

When looking at the data, the research shows that there are strong associations between the style of parenting and a child’s mental health outcome (Table 3). Authoritative parenting style was negatively associated with mental health problems in terms of total difficulties, internalizing and externalizing problems, while positively with the prosocial behaviors exhibited by the sample children. Specifically, a one-SD increase in the authoritative parenting style standardized score was associated with decreases of 0.18, 0.15, and 0.15 SDs in the standardized scores for Total difficulties, Internalizing problems, and Externalizing problems, respectively, as well as an increase of 0.31 SDs in the standardized score for Prosocial behaviors. All coefficients were statistically significant (all p-values < 0.001). In contrast, when primary caregivers exercised more parenting practices using an authoritarian style, children tended to have higher scores of mental health problems for the Total difficulties (β = 0.36; p < 0.001), Internalizing (β = 0.28; p < 0.001) and Externalizing (β = 0.32; p < 0.001) scales, and lower scores on the Prosocial behavior scale (β = -0.13; p < 0.001). Regarding the explanatory power of the model, the regression analysis yielded an adjusted R2 values ranging from 0.078 to 0.239. According to the benchmarks for the effect size R2 for survey-based research proposed by Wei and Hu (2019) — where 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, and 0.09 represent small, typical (medium), large, and very large effect sizes, respectively — the observed variance in this study reflects large to very large effect sizes [49]. These results suggest that the variable that measures parenting style in our study is able to explain a substantial and practically meaningful proportion of the variance in children’s mental health outcomes within our rural sample. Similar associations also were found between the subscales of each parenting styles and child mental health problems (Appendix Table A3). Appendix Table A4 presents the associations between one form of authoritative and authoritarian parenting styles and child mental health outcomes while controlling for the other. The results demonstrate findings similar to those observed in Table 3, except for small differences in the magnitudes of some coefficients.

Determinants of parenting styles

Discussion

Previous research has highlighted the increasing mental health problems among young children (especially in LMICs) and the role that the style of parenting can play in the emergence (or not) of mental health problems among children [4, 8]. As the biggest economy among the world’s LMICs, China also is challenged with mental health problems among their young children, including those in rural primary schools [30, 31]. Hence, it is highly likely that other LMICs could learn about managing mental health issues among children by examining the associations between parenting styles and child mental health in rural China. Until now, however, little information on the subject of mental health among children has been provided from rural China since few studies have focused on or analyzed these issues. To fill the gap in the literature, the purpose of this study is to examine the associations between the parenting style of primary caregivers and mental health of primary school children. The research in this paper also focuses on identifying the factors that may influence the adoption of different parenting styles by primary caregivers in rural China.

The findings of this study confirm the existing literature that indicates there is a strong link between parenting styles and child mental health [10–13]. Specifically, it showed that when parents adopt an authoritative parenting style, the primary school-aged children in these families have lower levels of mental health problems. In contrast, parenting in an authoritarian style is negatively associated with the mental health outcomes of children in China’s rural primary schools. In fact, these findings are consistent with prior studies that have looked at these issues in other countries and in urban China [10–13, 34–37, 45]. Research conducted in urban China suggests that when caregivers adopt a strict authoritarian style, children tend to exhibit less favorable developmental outcomes [37, 45]. However, unlike urban parents who often have access to diverse parenting resources and therefore tend to adopt more authoritative parenting strategies [45], rural primary caregivers are more likely to be influenced by traditional concepts and to adhere to an authoritarian parenting style [36, 37, 45]. This difference may partly explain why the level of developmental outcomes of rural children in our sample is relatively low. In addition, the associations between both authoritarian parenting styles and child mental health scores observed in our study are notably stronger (β = 0.36) than those reported in previous research (β = 0.16) [10–13], suggesting that primary caregivers need to learn to parent children with more authoritative-based (instead of authoritarian-based) parenting styles in order to improve the mental health of their young children on a larger scale.

The analysis in this study also found that, when caregivers were classified into combinations of authoritative and authoritarian parenting styles, rather than only one of the two, combinations characterized by a stronger presence of authoritative parenting were associated with lower rates of child mental health problems. In contrast, combinations with a stronger presence of authoritarian parenting were linked to higher rates of mental health issues in their young children. Although, to our knowledge, no previous study has examined the associations between child mental health and combined parenting styles, these findings suggest that using more authoritative and fewer authoritarian strategies when parenting young children may be an effective way to promote better mental health outcomes, especially in contemporary rural China. As shown in the literature, a two-dimensional assessment of parenting style (either authoritative or authoritarian), which has been used widely in the Western world [9, 39], may not fully capture the range of variation in parenting styles in the context of China’s culture [41–43, 46]. It is also possible that parents in China that endorse China’s cultural values may adopt both an authoritative and an authoritarian parenting style [42]. In this context of cultural transition, promoting combinations of parenting styles may play an important role in shaping child mental health outcomes. In addition to statistical significance, the effect sizes observed in this study highlight the practical magnitude of these associations. With substantial effect sizes indicated by R2 (explaining up to 23.9% of the variance), our models demonstrate that parenting styles are robust predictors rather than incidental factors. These findings reinforce the premise that family-based interventions are central—not merely supplemental—to fostering mental health resilience among children in rural China.

The analysis of this study on the importance of factors related to the style of parenting and the mental health of young children also identified a number of demographic characteristics that seemed to induce different rates of adoption of the different parenting styles by primary caregivers. In line with previous studies [19, 22], compared to how they parent girls (often adopting more a more authoritative parenting style), primary caregivers tend to parent boys in an authoritarian style. The literature is suggestive that this is primarily due to culturally influenced expectations and gendered perceptions of behavior. Boys are frequently subject to stricter control to enforce discipline and assertiveness, and reduce emotional responsiveness, aligning with traditional masculine norms, while girls are encouraged through supportive communication and emotional engagement, reflecting stereotypes of femininity [19, 22, 26]. In addition, as reported by other research teams in the literature [20, 21, 23], our analysis demonstrates that children with developmental delays often keep primary caregivers from adopting a more positive parenting style and instead make them more likely to resort to authoritarian parenting style. The findings of the present study also indicate that when a child exhibits delays in cognitive or non-cognitive development during preschool years, caregivers are more inclined to use an authoritarian parenting style as the child progresses to primary school age. One possible explanation is that parenting children with developmental delays is indeed more challenging and stressful than parenting normally developed children [21, 24]. The complex needs and unique characteristics of developmentally delayed children often require additional attention, patience, and resources from parents. This can lead to feelings of frustration and helplessness, in parents, prompting them to resort to authoritarian parenting as a means of establishing control and structure in their interactions with their children. Additionally, developmental delays in a child may exacerbate parental anxiety and uncertainty, further influencing the tendency of parents towards authoritarian parenting style as a response to the perceived difficulties and uncertainties of raising a child with developmental delays [25].

The current study also demonstrates that primary caregivers with higher levels of SES are more likely to use an authoritative parenting style and less likely to parent in an authoritarian way—which is consistent with previous studies [21, 27, 28]. As such, this study suggests that certain demographic appear to play important roles in helping (hurting) children to improve (harm) their mental health outcomes.

This study, we believe, also has several new contributions to the literature. While previous research has predominantly focused on urban populations and singular parenting typologies in China, our study targets an understudied population and introduces a more nuanced, combinatorial approach to understanding different types of parenting styles. First, to our knowledge, this is the first empirical study to investigate the relationship between parenting styles and mental health outcomes among primary school children in rural China. By focusing on this population, our findings provide insights relevant to low- and middle-income families in similar resource-constrained settings, shedding light on how parenting styles may either support or undermine children’s mental well-being. This paper thus offers implications for policy makers of other LMICs to carry out effective parenting training interventions to improve child mental health. Specifically, governments in other LMICs should use the results of this study to consider designing similar interventions that can incorporate training sessions or educational content that promote authoritative parenting strategies rather than authoritarian parenting practices. Second, this study explores the combinations of parenting styles (that primary caregivers often exhibit more often other than sticking into only one style of parenting), which enriches the literature by revisiting the classifications initially raised by Baumrind [9] with consideration of more novel concepts and practices that often characterize the literature in the 21st century. This combinatorial approach responds to the complex reality of contemporary parenting styles. Therefore, based on our findings, caregivers in the settings of LMICs should be encouraged to adopt parenting styles with high levels of authoritative strategies in order to help decrease the likelihood of their children having mental health problems. Third, while many studies address externalizing behaviors or academic outcomes, this research concentrates specifically on the mental health of early school-aged children—a critical developmental period that receives insufficient attention in the parenting literature, particularly in rural contexts. By identifying modifiable parenting styles, our results emphasize that embracing more authoritative strategies, while reducing authoritarian practices, can serve as an effective approach for improving psychological outcomes among young children.

Beyond its contributions, this study has a few limitations worth noting. First, caregiver-reported measures of child mental health and self-reported measures of parenting styles were used to assess the outcomes of interests of this study, which may introduce measurement error. Such errors can arise from factors such as social desirability bias, recall bias, or subjective interpretation of questionnaire items. These biases may lead respondents to overestimate or underestimate certain behavior or symptoms of their young child, thereby affecting the accuracy and validity of the findings. Consequently, the observed associations between parenting styles and child mental health outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Second, this study focused solely on the associations between parenting styles and child mental health using a cross-sectional data, which did not allow to make casual conclusions of the effect of parenting styles on child mental health. Future work might want to consider the possibility of collecting longitudinal data to help determine the actual causal links between parenting styles and the mental health of their young children.

Conclusions

Using cross-sectional survey data collected from 1,298 child-caregiver dyads in rural China, this study examined the associations between caregiver parenting styles and child mental health outcomes. The findings show that a large share of the sampled children is experiencing mental health problems (either Internalizing or Externalizing problems).

By exploring the associations between parenting styles and the mental health of primary school children in the setting of rural China, this study extends the current literature by revisiting the classifications of classical parenting styles and demonstrating the associations between classical and combined classifications of parenting styles and the mental health of primary school children in an LMIC. The study indicates that an authoritative parenting style is most predictive of positive mental health outcomes in young children. Similarly, the greater the presence of these authoritative strategies (e.g., active listening to children’s needs while setting clear, consistent boundaries, provide rational guidance rather than one-sided demands, and involve children in age-appropriate decision-making) within combined parenting styles, the lower the likelihood that a child will experience mental health problems. In contrast, the more prominent is authoritarian behavior (such as, coercive discipline or dismissing children’s opinions) in such combined styles, the higher the probability that a child will develop mental health issues. The findings shed lights on the important value of encouraging primary caregivers to invest in positive parenting styles to help children improve their mental health outcomes when their children are still fairly young.

Policy implications

Based on the findings of this study, we have several policy recommendations. First, we recommend that government organizations in China need to pay more attention to teaching rural caregivers of young children positive parenting behavior that is related to authoritative parenting style in order to alleviate mental health problems among young rural children. Specifically, the benefits of authoritative parenting and risks of highly authoritarian practices that have been identified in this manuscript should guide evidence-based parenting education programs. Second, early screening tools for family dynamics and parenting behavior could be integrated into primary healthcare and school-based psychological services. Interventions for at-risk children should include caregiver components that encourage authoritative parenting to prevent or mitigate mental health issues among their young children. Third, public health and educational authorities should design and scale up tailored parenting programs for rural communities. These programs should emphasize the benefits of adopting more authoritative practices (e.g., responsiveness, emotional support, democratic communication) while reducing authoritarian behavior (e.g., harsh discipline, psychological control). Training can be delivered through local schools, community centers, or digital platforms to improve accessibility, addressing barriers faced by rural or low-income families.
