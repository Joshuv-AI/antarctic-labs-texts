Rerouting reductant flux via protein tethering enhances biohydrogen production in Thermococcus kodakarensis
Authors: Sere A. Williams, David A. Crosby, Danielle M. Riley, Teagan P. Rockwood, Brett W. Burkhart, Meghan E. Stettler, Thomas J. Santangelo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobes that generate copious amounts of hydrogen (H2) via dark fermentation are a promising means to evolve and improve renewable biofuels. Many anaerobic hyperthermophilic archaea, such as the fast-growing, genetically tractable, heterotroph Thermococcus kodakarensis, produce generous quantities of H2 and provide an idealized platform to further optimize naturally high levels of biohydrogen reduction. Precise genetic manipulations and modifications to growth conditions have already resulted in substantial increases to H2 output but additional improvements are desired. An unexamined and potentially valuable route towards increased H2 production is to tether select electron donor and acceptor proteins together to reroute and maximize the flow of electrons towards H2 production. Such strategies have shown promise in Bacteria and Eukarya but have not yet been investigated in thermophilic Archaea. Here, we generate and evaluate twelve novel T. kodakarensis strains wherein a proteinaceous electron carrier (a ferredoxin, Fd) is physically tethered to the membrane-bound-hydrogenase (MBH), the sole H2 producing enzyme, to direct electron flux towards biohydrogen generation. Growth assessments and H2 output measurements demonstrate that strains encoding protein-fusions evolve up to ~ 40% more H2 per cell than the host strain. Eliminating H2 consumption and alternative routes of electron sinks in concert with protein tethering further increased H2 output per cell for a maximum increase of ~ 66% over the host strain. Our results demonstrate that rerouting electron flux via protein tethering coupled with the elimination of reductant sinks is a promising means towards improved biohydrogen production in T. kodakarensis.Key points Protein tethering between redox proteins can reroute electron flux in vivo.Enforced protein proximity results in ~ 40% increases in H2 production per cell.Protein-tethering provides a generalizable framework to redirect redox metabolism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13772-7.

Introduction

The efficiency of hydrogen (H2) production via dark fermentation is dictated, in large part, by how electrons are distributed among competing reductant usage pathways and ultimately, the share of electrons that get delivered to hydrogenases for H₂ evolution (Thauer et al. 1977; Maier 1996; Amend and Shock 2001; Lewalter and Müller 2006; McNeely et al. 2010; Martin and Thauer 2017). While studies focused on mesophilic derived dark fermentation have produced significant gains (Agapakis et al. 2010; Eilenberg et al. 2016; Koo et al. 2016; Mazzoli et al. 2024; Vauthier et al. 2026), anaerobic hyperthermophiles offer an attractive alternative due to their thermostable enzymes, high metabolic rates, and capacity to maintain efficient reduction–oxidation (redox) balance under extreme conditions, providing idealized systems for probing and engineering electron flux for biohydrogen generation (Rittmann and Herwig 2012; Lipscomb et al. 2014; Bae et al. 2015; Aslam et al. 2017; Simons et al. 2020). Energy conservation in anaerobic microbes depends on the orchestration of reductant flux via redox proteins such as ferredoxins (Fds), hydrogenases, and oxidoreductases which maintain redox balance and generate reduced cofactors in the absence of oxygen (Santangelo et al. 2011). Hyperthermophilic archaea must also maintain protein integrity while controlling electron transfer at elevated temperatures—near the limits of life—thus their redox-responsive proteins must direct efficient and selective electron transfers at high temperature. In response, electron flux in hyperthermophiles is mediated by small, specialized redox proteins that function near thermodynamic limits (Schäfer et al. 1999; Page et al. 1999; Mayer and Müller 2014; Nguyen et al. 2017). The specialized electron transfer pathways of hyperthermophilic archaea are thus an appealing model for understanding fundamental bioenergetics, for developing high-temperature biotechnologies, and for enhancing the production of biohydrogen.

Rerouting electron flux could provide powerful control over cellular redox pathways and energy metabolism by altering the flow of electrons between competing acceptors towards a preferred product (Page et al. 1999; Menon et al. 2009; Lipscomb et al. 2014; Atkinson et al. 2019; Truong and Silberg 2025). The genetically accessible, H2-evolving hyperthermophile, Thermococcus kodakarensis, provides an exceptional platform to reroute electron flux (Atomi et al. 2004; Fukui et al. 2005). T. kodakarensis maintains two distinct respiratory pathways that ultimately drive ATP production via a sodium ion gradient (Yu et al. 2018, 2020): depending on the availability of the preferred terminal electron acceptor, elemental sulfur (S˚), a cytoplasmic ion gradient is either generated and maintained (in the presence of S˚) by a membrane-bound sulfane reductase (MBS) or (in the absence of S˚) by a membrane-bound hydrogenase MBH (Fig. 1a) (Sapra et al. 2003; Atomi et al. 2004; Fukui et al. 2005; Kanai et al. 2005; Hachisuka et al. 2017; Atomi and Reeve 2019).Fig. 1T. kodakarensis generates hydrogen via MBH and Fd-MBH tethered strains are viable. a Oxidation of amino acids and glucose provide electrons to produce reduced small molecules, maturation of lipids, or the generation of H2 or H2S via one of three encoded Fds (Fd1, green; Fd2, pink; Fd3, blue). MBH plays a key role in biohydrogen production, providing the terminal chemistry that allows for largescale catabolic oxidation of organic substrates to ultimately reduce protons to H2. MBH activity is thus an obvious target for increased electron flux that would be predicted to immediately impact production levels of H2 (Fig S1a) (Yang et al. 2010; Yacoby et al. 2011; Eilenberg et al. 2016).

Natural and engineered metabolisms must manage the challenge of controlling electron flux which follow classical laws of diffusion but must be maintained by transiently interacting proteins (Verhaart et al. 2010; Atkinson et al. 2016). The overall efficiency of energy transduction depends on the redox potential of redox active protein partners and the specificity and frequency of these encounters, but to date, research concerning the spatial organization of electron transfer has been primarily limited to mesophiles. Fd-[FeFe]-hydrogenase (HydA) fusion in the green algae Chlamydomonas reinhardtii demonstrated a ~ 4.5-fold increase in hydrogen generation in vivo (Eilenberg et al. 2016), but it was previously unknown if restricting the physical distance between two redox-active proteins would be viable or productive in anaerobic hyperthermophiles (Björnberg et al. 2006; Jurić et al. 2009; McMillan et al. 2013; Williams et al. 2025). Electron flux is most typically controlled through the selective expression and conformation of redox proteins and their respective complexes. As T. kodakarensis finely tunes not only the expression, but the conformation of complexes in response to redox state, it follows that by restricting the spatial distance of a donor and acceptor protein could impact metabolic outputs (Jäger et al. 2014; Williams et al. 2025). Thus, if a particular donor and acceptor are physically joined and maintain function in a redox-active manner, the selected electron-donor protein no longer needs to rely on collisions to find the optimal electron-acceptor protein, as tethering would immediately bring the partners together to facilitate efficient, directed, electron transfers (Fig. 1a, hypothesis inset).

Small proteinaceous electron carriers, such as Fds, serve as ideal electron donors, and T. kodakarensis encodes three Fds. Fd1 and Fd2 are small proteins (~ 7 kDa) and essential, while Fd3 is slightly more than twice the size and dispensable (Burkhart et al. 2019). Prior research suggests that each Fd interacts with preferred electron donors and acceptors, with the strongest association observed between Fd2 and its operonically encoded partner, geranylgeranyl reductase (GGR), which is responsible for lipid maturation, a vast reductant sink (Fig. 1a) (Isobe et al. 2014; Burkhart et al. 2019; Williams et al. 2025). Here, we are enforcing the proximity between redox partners through genetically encoded protein tethering such that the encoded proteins are expressed with a fusion or tether domain linking them to one another. By linking a small Fd to the MBH, we aim to enhance biohydrogen production via engineered reductive flux (Table 1). By engineering tethered proteins, we demonstrate positive impacts to biohydrogen production that are dependent on the Fd, linker, and MBH-subunit. By deleting alternative pathways of reductant disposal, H2 production was increased, and the combination of providing a Fd-MBH tether in addition to limiting reductant loss provided the greatest increase (~ 66% H2/cell). Thus, we demonstrate that electron flux in T. kodakarensis can not only be manipulated but substantially enhanced through targeted protein fusions and reductant channeling, establishing a versatile framework for advancing dark fermentation-based bioenergy platforms. Materials and methods

Generation of protein tethered strains and deletion strains

The parental strain (typically TS559) of T. kodakarensis was anaerobically grown from 1:100 inoculums at 85 ˚C in an artificial sea water base media supplemented with 5 g/l of yeast extract, tryptone, and pyruvate, and when appropriate, 2 g/l of S˚ (Hileman and Santangelo 2012). A two-stage integration and excision strain construction process, as previously described (Scott et al. 2021), wherein individual native loci are markerlessly modified was used to generate either deletion or protein tethered strains (Table 1). For deletion strains (ΔFd3, ΔCH, and ΔAAT), the entire coding region of the gene was removed (Santangelo et al. 2010). All Fd-MBH encoding tethered strains were constructed in the ΔFd3 background and encode one of three Fds (fd1, TK1694; fd2, TK1087; or fd3, TK2012) at either the 5’ end of mbh-j (TK2089) or the 3’ end of mbh-n (TK2093) (Fig S1b, Table S1, Table S2). Fd1 and fd2 sequences are codon optimized synonymous sequences, ensuring that the sequence encoding the tether does not direct integration of the introduced construct at the respective fd locus but rather at the desired loci in the mbh operon. Each Fd is tethered to a MBH subunit using a sequence which encodes either a 5 aa (GGGGG; ggcggaggcggaggc) or 9 aa (GGSPPPSGG; ggcggaagcccgcccccgacgggcgga) sequence. All Fd-MBH encoding tethered strains additionally include codon optimized 9 amino acid hemagglutinin (HA) epitope (YPYDVPDYA; tacccatacgacgttccggactacgca) and His6 affinity (HHHHHH; catcaccatcaccatcac) tag sequences at the 5’ end in mhb-j strains or the 3’ end in mbh-n strains (Fig S1b, Table S2).

Confirmation of strains and expression of desired protein products

The genomic sequences of all modified loci were first confirmed via amplification of the modified locus by PCR (Fig S1b-c and S2a-b). As a secondary measure of genetic confirmation and to ensure our genetic manipulations did not introduce undesirable modifications anywhere within the > 2 Mbp genome, genomic DNA was prepared (Monarch Genomic DNA Purification Kit, NEB, #T3010L) or cell pellets were stabilized (DNA/RNA Shield, Zymo Research, R1100) and genomic DNA was prepared (Plasmidsaurus) and the whole genome of every strain was sequenced via in-house or commercial Oxford Nanopore Technology sequencing at minimally ~ 30X coverage and often > 1000X coverage.

Expression of tethered proteins was confirmed via Western blotting. Total cell lysate (TCL) from ~ 100 mls of culture grown to late-exponential phase was pelleted and resuspended in 25 mM Tris HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol before being sonicated on ice for 10 s every 40 s for 5 min at 50% power (VibraCell, Sonics and Materials Inc.). Protein content within the TCL was quantified by the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen) and ~ 10 µg of protein was mixed with 5X sample loading buffer (250 mM Tris HCl pH 6.8, 8% SDS, 0.1% bromophenol blue, 40% glycerol, 100 mM DTT, 50 mM EDTA) before incubation at 99 ˚C for 5 min to ensure protein denaturation. Treated TCL was separated via SDS-PAGE on a 12% Criterion TGX Stain-Free Protein Gel (BioRad, 5678044) at 150 V for 55 min before being transferred to 0.2 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane and visualized via Western blotting employing 1:10,000 anti-HA 1˚ antibody (Invitrogen, 26183-1MG) made in mouse which binds to the HA epitope encoded on tethered proteins and a 1:1000 anti-mouse conjugated alkaline phosphatase 2˚ antibody (LGC Clinical Diagnostics, 5220-0312) that was used for colorimetric detection (Fig. 1c). Confirmation of ΔFd3 using anti-Fd3 antibodies was performed with the following modifications: SDS in the sample buffer was increased to 16%, TCL containing ~ 5 µg of protein for T. kodakarensis derived samples and 1 ng of recombinant Fd3 was loaded onto a 4–20% Criterion TGX Stain-Free Protein Gel (BioRad, 5678094) and incubated with 1:10,000 anti-Fd3 1˚antibody (made in-house with Cocalico Biologicals) and a 1:5000 anti-guinea pig conjugated alkaline phosphatase 2˚ antibody (Southern Biotech, 6090-04) (Fig S1d).

Growth and hydrogen production assessments

To evaluate the impact of protein tethering on cellular physiology and electron flux we assessed the growth kinetics and H2 generation of engineered and parental strains. While H2 production per cell mass would ideally be reported, the inclusion of solid elemental sulfur restricts the ability to establish an accurate dry cell weight of the cells alone, thus optical density at 600 nm (OD600) is used in place of dry cell weight. Growth curves with concurrent H2 measurements can provide a means to assess redox flux as measured by the percent of H2 gas in the head space divided by the by OD600, providing a percent H2/per cell value (Ω) (Santangelo et al. 2011). Confirmed strains and respective parent strains were grown from 1:100 inoculums in ASW-YTP ± S˚ for ~ 9–12 h and passaged twice before being used to inoculate four replicates of test cultures. Anaerobic glass culture tubes were freshly washed and autoclaved before being filled with 10 mls of ASW-YTP and autoclaved again. OD600 measurements of starter cultures were used to calculate relative cell densities such that the 1:100 inoculation ratio could be adjusted appropriately (e.g., 100 µl of a starter culture at an OD600 of 1.0 or 200 µl of a starter culture at an OD600 of 0.5 were used to inoculate respective samples). Inoculated cultures were sealed with new, autoclaved butyl rubber stoppers (Chemglass Life Sciences, 4209-14) to minimize gas exchange, and the headspace was vacuum purged and then replaced with ultra-high purity (UHP) grade nitrogen gas (99.999%; Airgas, Ni UHP 300) three times before bringing the headspace to ~ 12–15 psi UHP N2. Cultures were then incubated in a water bath at 85 ˚C and OD600 measurements (ThermoScientific, Genesis 30) were recorded every hour for ~ 12–27 h. Two uninoculated samples (blanks) were used to zero absorbance readings on the spectrophotometer and were read throughout the 27-h incubations such that any changes to absorbance readings due to changes apart from growth were accounted for. Absorbance readings for each sample were subtracted against the average of the two blanks.

A Shimadzu Gas Chromatograph 2014 (GC-2014) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) was fitted with two columns: a Molecular Sieve 5 A 60/80, 2 m, 0.32 mm ID on the left and a HayeSep Q 80/100, 2 m, 0.32 mm ID on the right. The instrument was equilibrated for > 4 h with a 15.0 ml/min flow of UHP N2 carrier gas. H2 percentages in the headspace were assessed when cultures reached an OD600 of 0.3, 0.8, and at the end of the growth curve. Gas in the headspace was accessed via a 21-gauge × 1″ sterile needle before sample tubes were returned to incubate in the 85 ˚C water bath. Gas flowed from the headspace of the culture tube into the built-in injection valve for ~ 2 s using an automatic valve open/close action programmed into the method. A 100-μl sample gas was injected onto the left column at 110.0 ˚C. Excess gas was released via an overflow shunt during the 2 s sampling window. Columns (held at 40 ˚C) were equilibrated for 0.5 min before gasses were detected at 110.0 ˚C at 65 mA via TCD. H2 standards at 5%, 20%, and 50% H2 (Airgas, Certified Standard-Spec) were used to generate a standard curve and then estimate percent H2 from sample gasses. Cumulative % H2 was divided by the OD600 (to generate Ω values) at ~ 2 h following entry into stationary phase (approximately hours 11 and 15 for + S˚ and -S˚, respectively, for the growth curves involving all tethered Fd-MBH strains and approximately hour 13 for the -S˚ deletion growth curves involving ΔCH, ΔAAT, and 35J combinations). Replicates were assessed for outliers using the Grubb’s Test and averages for all remaining replicates were normalized to the average of H2 detected in cultures of TS559 grown in the absence of S˚ respective to each growth curve. Significance compared to TS559 (-S˚) was assessed using Dunnet’s test at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Design and construction of Fd-MBH encoding strains

To establish the impacts on biohydrogen production and electron flux resultant from protein tethering of select electron transfer proteins and MBH, we generated strains which express a Fd physically tethered to the MBH (Fig. 1c, Table 1). Strains are markerlessly modified such that each Fd is genetically encoded at one of two distinct loci in the MBH operon (Scott et al. 2021). The 14-subunit MBH complex is composed of a membrane-embedded module housing the Na+/H+ antiporter (TK2080-2088 and TK2092; MBH-A – I and M) and a four-subunit cytosolic Ni–Fe hydrogenase module (TK2089-91 and TK2093; MBH-J – K and N) (Yu et al. 2018). Each Fd is genetically encoded at either the 5’ end of MBH-J (TK2089) or the 3’ end of MBH-N (TK2093) such that the Fd may perform normal redox activity in the cytosol while being physically linked to the MBH complex. Prior work linking Fd to the [FeFe] hydrogenase in Clostridium pasteurianum demonstrated effective redox transfer with a 15 aa but not 55 aa linker (Koo et al. 2016). Here, each Fd is linked via one of two amino acid sequences such that each tethered Fd will have unique access and positioning to reduce MBH (Fig. 1b, S1a-b) (Reddy Chichili et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2013). A short and flexible five glycine (5 aa) sequence or a longer, slightly more rigid nine amino acid (9 aa) sequence composed of three prolines at the center, bracketed on either side with a hydrophilic serine and two flexible glycine residues (GGSPPPSGG) links each Fd to the respective MBH subunit, generating twelve protein tethered strains in total (Fig S1a inset, Table S1). To limit extraneous electron transfer whenever possible, the only dispensable Fd, Fd3, was deleted (Δfd3) from the Fd-MBH encoding tethered strains such that native electron transfer from Fd3 to MBH was eliminated. As Fd1 and Fd2 are essential, synonymous sequences were used to assure integration at the desired loci. Tether strains are denoted by the respective Fd (Fd1, Fd2, or Fd3), followed by the linker length (5 aa or 9 aa), and then MBH subunit (MBH-J or -N), such that each strain is denoted by a three-character code (i.e., Fd1 tethered via a 5 aa linker to MBH-J is referred to as “15J”). Modification to select loci were confirmed by PCR followed by whole genome sequencing with minimally 50X coverage to confirm all genotypes, and Western blotting allowed confirmation that Fd-MBH tethered proteins remained expressed and tethered in vivo (Fig. 1c).

H2 production (steady-state levels) in the headspace of each culture was assessed when cultures reached exponential phase (at an OD600 of ~ 0.3), stationary phase (at an OD600 of ~ 0.8), and at the end of the growth curve to generate a summative percent H2 production value per cell, (% H2/OD600nm termed Ω value) (Fig. 2a) (Santangelo et al. 2011). Ω values are normalized to TS559 grown in the absence of S˚. As expected, S˚-containing media limits the generation of H2, and as expected, all strains grown in the presence of S˚ generated ~ 20–40% less H2/cell than TS559 -S˚ (Fig. 2b) (Kanai et al. 2005). However, in S˚-limited conditions, all tethers and ΔFd3 evolved ~ 15% more H2/cell than TS559 with significant increases by 29J, 25N, 29N, 35J, 35N, and 39N. Notably, 35J generated ~ 40% more H2/cell over TS559 -S˚, while it’s 9 aa-linker counterpart, 39J, produced only slightly more than TS559, suggesting that the linker length and composition play important roles in allowing specific orientations for efficient electron transfer.

Elimination of additional reductant sinks further increases hydrogen production

T. kodakarensis metabolism is well characterized, providing a detailed framework for manipulating redox balance and enabling targeted perturbation of electron flux by deleting non-essential genes (Williams et al. 2025). T. kodakarensis can oxidize H2 via the dispensable cytosolic hydrogenase (CH), thus deletion of CH can increase H2 production (Fig. 3) (Santangelo et al. 2011; Schut et al. 2012).Fig. 3Deletion of CH or AAT may promote increased hydrogen production. Glycolysis yields electrons which can be passed to MBH to generate H2. H2 can pass the cell membrane and be oxidized to protons by CH. Pyruvate can be further metabolized via an oxidative or reductive pathway. Pyruvate may be oxidized by POR to acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA can further be oxidized by ACS to acetate, providing two additional electrons and dispensing acetate as a waste product. Further modifications to limit reductant loss are possible considering the multiple catabolic pathways for pyruvate (Fig. 3). Pyruvate oxidoreductase (POR) oxidizes pyruvate into acetyl-CoA and CO2, with acetyl-CoA further oxidized by the reversible acetyl-CoA synthase (ACS) into acetate and CoA, thus providing two additional electrons for H2 evolution (Fig. 3). Alternatively in the reductive pathway, pyruvate can be reduced to alanine which requires an amine from glutamate in the conversion to 2-oxoglutarate by alanine amino transferase (AAT) (Santangelo et al. 2011). Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) reduces glutamate with ammonia and the oxidation of NAD(P)H into NAD(P)+ (Yokooji et al. 2013). Both acetate and alanine are discarded in the media as waste products, but limiting alanine production by deleting AAT via the oxidative pathway could lead to increased generation of H2 (Fig. 3, Table 1). Both ΔCH and ΔAAT strains were generated in the TS559 and 35J backgrounds and assessed for fitness and H2 output (Table S1).

Hence, six additional strains were generated which either limited H2 consumption (ΔCH), directed pyruvate catabolism towards oxidation (ΔAAT), or combined both limiting H2 consumption and pyruvate oxidation (ΔCH; ΔAAT). The 35J tether was integrated into these genotypes generating strains encoding the tether and deletion(s): 35J; ΔCH, 35J; ΔAAT, and 35J; ΔCH; ΔAAT (Fig S2, Table S1). We then performed growth assessments and H2 measurements on deletion strains and their 35J counterparts as was performed with the tethered strains but only in S˚-limited conditions when H2 production is preferred. Initial growth trials suggested that there could be a distinct phenotype as cultures exhausted nutrient availability; thus, we extended the growth curves (to ~ 27 h) to measure late-stationary phase phenotypes.

Discussion

Microbial dark fermentation is a promising route for sustainable biofuel production, particularly for generating molecular H2 as a clean energy carrier. Efforts to enhance H2 yields have largely focused on optimizing substrate utilization and minimizing competing reductant sinks through metabolic engineering. Hyperthermophilic archaea, with their thermostable enzymes and unique redox networks, represent a largely untapped resource for advancing high-temperature biofuel production and developing robust bioprocesses for renewable energy conversion.

Here we establish that electron flux in T. kodakarensis can be rationally rerouted through genetically encoded protein tethering, establishing a new approach for controlling reductant flow during dark fermentation. We demonstrate that enforced proximity between redox partners significantly increases H2 production per cell. The efficiency of redox transfers resulting in H2 production are highly dependent on Fd, MBH-subunit, linker length, and composition. By deleting competing reductant disposal pathways, we amplified the positive impacts of protein tethering on H2 production in T. kodakarensis. While deletion of the H2-consuming cytosolic hydrogenase increases (~ 25%) H2/cell output, the additional inclusion of the protein tether 35J into ΔAAT or ΔCH; ΔAAT altered reductant flux metabolisms and further enhanced biohydrogen production upwards of ~ 66% H2/cell. Prior studies in T. kodakarensis have reported an increase in biohydrogen production from over-expressing MBH alone (~ 25% increase in specific H2 production rate) or in combination with deleting CH and AAT (~ 28% increase) (Santangelo et al. 2011; Kanai et al. 2015). Overexpression of an A1-type ATPase resulted in ~ 20% increase in H2 which could be further exploited with the addition of maltodextrin to the media, resulting in ~ 30% increase in H2 over the host strain (Simons et al. 2020). Findings reported here extend the scope of metabolic engineering in extremophiles, revealing that spatial organization of redox enzymes offers a powerful means to manipulate energy metabolism and optimize microbial H2 production under high-temperature, anaerobic conditions.

The protein tethering and resultant reductant rerouting strategies reported here could be combined with continuous cultivation methods to further increase H2 production in the Thermococcales (Kanai et al. 2005; Kim et al. 2010; Bae et al. 2015). Additionally, efforts to decrease feedstock costs, specifically towards engineering for the utilization of cellulose, are a promising venture, given the capacity of T. kodakarensis to consume a variety of energy sources (Kim et al. 2010; Aslam et al. 2017).

Together, these results demonstrate that precise control of electron flow through spatial organization can fundamentally reshape energy metabolism in anaerobic hyperthermophiles. By integrating protein engineering with targeted genetic deletions, we establish a framework for systematically redirecting reductant flux toward desired bioenergetic outputs—an approach that could be extended to optimize other redox-dependent pathways. Beyond advancing our understanding of archaeal bioenergetics, this work provides a foundation for next-generation biohydrogen production and synthetic metabolism at high temperature where tethering could be valuable. Moving forward, expanding this strategy to multi-enzyme assemblies, alternative electron carriers, and diverse redox chemistries could enable the design of robust, tunable biocatalytic systems—bridging the gap between fundamental redox biology and applied bioenergy innovation.
