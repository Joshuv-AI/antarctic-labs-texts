Two Novel Exophiala Species Isolated from Galls on the Chinese Magnolia-Vine (Schisandra Chinensis) in Korea
Authors: Seong-Keun Lim, Min-Ki Kim, Che-Yeon Park, Won-Kwon Jung, In-Kyu Kang, Seung-Yeol Lee, Hee-Young Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo novel fungal species (KNUF-24-1HaT and KNUF-24-3LaT), belonging to the genus Exophiala, were isolated from galls produced by gall-midge (Lasioptera sp.; Diptera: Cecidomyiidae) on a Chinese magnolia vine (Schisandra chinensis) collected in Mungyeong-si, Gyeongbuk province, South Korea. Molecular analyses based on combined sequences of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions and partial sequences of the large subunit (LSU), rRNA, beta-tubulin, and actin genes revealed that the strains clustered within the Exophiala genus. However, strains KNUF-24-1HaT and KNUF-24-3LaT each occupied a distinct phylogenetic position. Comparative analysis of the morphological characteristics of KNUF-24-1HaT and KNUF-24-3LaT and those of closely related species in the phylogenetic tree revealed distinct differences in the shape and size of conidia, conidiogenous cells, and yeast cells. Based on molecular and morphological evidence, strains KNUF-24-1HaT and KNUF-24-3LaT represent a novel species within the genus Exophiala, now designated as Exophiala schisandrae sp. nov. and E. exsudans sp. nov., respectively.

Introduction

The Chinese magnolia vine (Schisandra chinensis), a plant native to northeastern Asia, is known for its vibrant purple-red berries. Often known as the “five-flavor fruit,” it uniquely combines sweet, bitter, pungent, salty, and sour tastes [1]. S. chinensis is highly valued for its adaptogenic properties and has been used for centuries in traditional medicine and phytotherapy. The plant enhances the ability of the body to adapt to stress, increase energy levels, and improve physical endurance [2]. Dried fruit and extract-based formulations are widely recognized for their supportive effects on neurological, cardiovascular, and digestive health. Additionally, these extracts are used to alleviate fatigue and manage obesity. They demonstrate antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, anticancer, and hepatoprotective activities [3,4]. The genus Exophiala (order Chaetothyriales, family Herpotrichiellaceae) was established by J.W. Carmichael in 1967, with Exophiala salmonis—a fungal pathogen of fish – designated as the type species [5]. These black yeasts exhibit yeast-like budding and produce dark-pigmented mycelia. They thrive in warm, humid environments, and they have been isolated from clinical specimens, animal tissues, plants, deep-sea sediments, and hydrocarbon-contaminated sites [6,7]. As anamorphic ascomycetes, species of the genus Exophiala are classified among black yeasts or black yeast-like fungi due to their melanized mycelium, which asexually generate yeast-like budding cells and aseptate or transversely septate conidia [8,9]. Species of the genus Exophiala are widely distributed across various environments, including soil, biological crusts, rock surfaces, air, natural water bodies, rhizospheres, and plant tissues, demonstrating significant ecological adaptability [10–15]. To date, 95 species have been identified within the genus Exophiala; however, only three species, E. radicis, E. equina, and E. xenobiotica, have been reported in Korea [16]. These fungi form mutualistic relationships with plants, enhancing nutrient uptake, regulating hormonal balance, and increasing resistance to environmental stresses, such as drought, heavy metal exposure, and pathogen attack [17,18].

This study aims to identify an uncharacterized Exophiala species isolated from gall midge larvae (Lasioptera sp.; Diptera: Cecidomyiidae) and their galleries within stem galls of the Chinese magnolia vine (S. chinensis) collected in Korea. Morphological, cultural, and molecular analyses will be conducted to determine its taxonomic position.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and fungal isolation

Gall samples were collected in 2024 from Mungyeong-si, Gyeongbuk province, South Korea (36°42′55.1ʺN, 128°18′53.6ʺE). Galls were cut longitudinally, and larvae were extracted from larval chambers using sterilized pins. Larvae and chamber tissues were surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol and 1% NaClO, rinsed three times with sterile water, then ground with a hand grinder. The homogenates were suspended in 1.0 mL sterile water, vortexed, serially diluted, and 100 μL aliquots were spread on PDA (Difco, Detroit, MI) plates, incubated at 25 °C for 2–3 d. Single colonies were transferred to fresh PDA and incubated at 25 °C for 5–7 d. Strains for molecular analysis were selected based on morphological characteristics [16]. Strains KNUF-24-1Ha, KNUF-24-1Wb, KNUF-24-3La, KNUF-24-5La, KNUF-24-7L1b, KNUF-24-7L2a, KNUF-24-7L2c, KNUF-24-7L3a, KNUF-24-9L2a, KNUF-24-9L3e, KNUF-24-9Wb, and KNUF-24-10Wa were selected for further analysis.

Cultural and morphological characterization

Strains KNUF-24-1Ha, KNUF-24-1Wb, KNUF-24-3La, KNUF-24-5La, KNUF-24-7L1b, KNUF-24-7L2a, KNUF-24-7L2c, KNUF-24-7L3a, KNUF-24-9L2a, KNUF-24-9L3e, KNUF-24-9Wb, and KNUF-24-10Wa were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco, Detroit, MI) and malt extract agar (MEA; Difco, Detroit, MI) to evaluate their morphology and growth. Cultural characteristics, including colony color, shape, and size, were recorded after 14 d of incubation [19]. Morphological features were examined using a BX-50 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Genomic DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction amplification, and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fungal mycelia of strains KNUF-24-1Ha, KNUF-24-1Wb, KNUF-24-3La, KNUF-24-5La, KNUF-24-7L1b, KNUF-24-7L2a, KNUF-24-7L2c, KNUF-24-7L3a, KNUF-24-9L2a, KNUF-24-9L3e, KNUF-24-9Wb, and KNUF-24-10Wa cultured on PDA. DNA was extracted using the HiGeneTM Genomic DNA Prep Kit (BIOFACT, Daejeon, South Korea). For molecular identification, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and partial sequences of the 28S large subunit (LSU) rRNA, actin (ACT), and beta-tubulin (TUB) genes were amplified using the primer pairs ITS1F/ITS4, LR0R/LR5, ACT-1/ACT-5ra, and Bt2a/Bt2b, respectively [20–24]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were purified using the ExoSAP-IT™ Product Cleanup Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and sequenced by Macrogen (Daejeon, South Korea). The resulting sequences from the five representative strains have been deposited to the GenBank database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis

Sequences retrieved from the NCBI were used to infer phylogenetic relationships (Table 1). A neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed using concatenated sequences of the ITS regions, partial LSU, ACT, and TUB genes, as described via the Kimura two-parameter model [25,26]. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using MEGA version 11 (Philadelphia, USA), with bootstrap values calculated from 1,000 replicates [27].

Results and discussion

Taxonomic analysis of Exophiala schisandrae sp. nov.

The morphology of strains KNUF-24-1Ha, KNUF-24-1Wb, KNUF-24-5La, KNUF-24-7L1b, and KNUF-24-7L2c was distinct from that of other closely related Exophiala species. Consequently, these strains were classified as novel species. Given that all five strains exhibited identical cultural and morphological features, strain KNUF-24-1Ha was selected for detailed morphological analysis.

Molecular phylogenetic analysis of Exophiala schisandrae sp. nov.

The ITS regions, LSU, ACT, and TUB gene sequences obtained from strains KNUF-24-1Ha, KNUF-24-1Wb, KNUF-24-5La, KNUF-24-7L1b, and KNUF-24-7L2c were 599, 1240, 885, and 404 bp in length, respectively. All five strains exhibited identical sequences across these four loci. The ITS sequences of strain KNUF-24-1Ha revealed a maximum similarity of 87.6% with Exophiala dermatitidis CBS 207.35T and showed 83.7–87.2% similarities with various Exophiala strains including E. cancerae CBS 120420T, E. equina CBS 119.23T, and E. radicis P2772. The LSU gene sequences showed the highest similarity of 98.1% with E. cancerae CBS 117491T, followed by 97.7% with E. radicis P2772, and 97.2% with E. equina CBS 119.23T. For the ACT gene, the sequences exhibited the highest similarity of 83.6% with E. cancerae CBS 117491T and 83.2% similarities with both E. radicis P2772 and E. equina CBS 119.23T. The TUB gene sequences showed the 75.0–80.2% similarities with E. radicis P2772, E. equina CBS 119.23T, and E. cancerae CBS 117491T. The NJ phylogenetic tree, based on the combined dataset of the four molecular markers, the ITS regions, LSU, ACT, and TUB genes, positioned the strains KNUF-24-1Ha, KNUF-24-1Wb, KNUF-24-5La, KNUF-24-7L1b, and KNUF-24-7L2c in a distinct clade, clearly separated from other Exophiala species (Figure 3). Consequently, this novel strain is considered to represent a single, phylogenetically distinct species within the genus Exophiala.

Molecular phylogeny of Exophiala exsudans sp. nov.

The ITS regions, LSU, ACT, and TUB gene sequences obtained from strains KNUF-24-3La, KNUF-24-7L2a, KNUF-24-7L3a, KNUF-24-9Wb, KNUF-24-9L2a, KNUF-24-9L3e, and KNUF-24-10Wa were 599, 1240, 885, and 404 bp in length, respectively. Sequence analysis revealed that KNUF-24-3La, KNUF-24-9Wb, and KNUF-24-9L3e shared identical sequences across all four loci. Similarly, KNUF-24-7L2a, KNUF-24-7L3a, KNUF-24-9L2a, and KNUF-24-10Wa also exhibited identical sequences. A single base pair difference was observed between the two groups in the ITS regions and LSU gene sequences. In comparison, the ITS sequences showed 99.3% similarity to those of E. schisandrae sp. nov. Similarly, the LSU gene sequence was most similar to E. dermatitidis CBS 207.35ᵀ and E. cancerae CBS 120420T (97.8%), followed by 97.6% similarity with E. radicis P2772, 97.55% similarity with E. salmonis CBS 157.67T, and 97.1% similarity with E. equina CBS 119.23T. In contrast, the LSU sequences shared 99.5% similarity with E. schisandrae sp. nov. For the ACT gene, the highest similarity of 85.0% with E. dermatitidis CBS 207.35T and 83.2% similarities with both E. radicis P2772 and E. equina CBS 119.23T. The genus Exophiala is a ubiquitous group of fungi distributed worldwide, isolated from various environmental sources, such as soil, rhizosphere, and plant tissues, demonstrating its adaptability to diverse habitats [28]. Several endophytic Exophiala fungi enhance plant growth and stress resilience by modulating phytohormones, improving nutrient uptake, and mitigating environmental stress [29]. Exophiala sp. LHL08 enhances cucumber stress tolerance by modulating gibberellin, salicylic acid, and abscisic acid, while E. pisciphila improves maize resilience by reducing cadmium toxicity, increasing lignin biosynthesis, activating glutathione metabolism, and enhancing phosphorus uptake through root modification and phosphate transporter regulation [18,30,31]. Moreover, E. pisciphila enhances maize growth by regulating indole-3-acetic acid, thereby promoting root development and nutrient absorption [32]. Studies highlight the positive effect of Exophiala on plant growth, emphasizing the need for further research into the molecular mechanisms and potential applications of E. schisandrae sp. nov. in plant management.

Additionally, gall midges are closely associated with certain fungi, serving as vectors that facilitate fungal transmission to host plants, as reported in various cases. In 2012, Exophiala sp. was isolated from a gall induced by the gall midge Asphondylia sarcocorniae on Sarcocornia quinqueflora [33]. This suggests a close triangular relationship among gall midges, Exophiala, and host plants. However, detailed investigations into this interaction remain lacking. Further research is needed to determine whether gall midges act as vectors for Exophiala fungi.
