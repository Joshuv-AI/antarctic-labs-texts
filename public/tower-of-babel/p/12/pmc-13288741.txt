Vision Impairment, Insurance Coverage, and Out-of-Pocket Spending
Authors: Mst Sadia Sultana, Melissa McInerney, Michel Boudreaux, Fei Yu, Anne L. Coleman, Brandy Lipton
Published: 2026

Introduction

Uncorrectable vision impairment (VI) affects over 7 million people in the US.1 VI is associated with adverse health outcomes, including difficulties with activities of daily living,2 cognitive impairment,3 falls and hip fractures,4 long-term care admissions,5 and mortality.6 We examined trends in insurance coverage and out-of-pocket (OOP) medical spending among adults under age 65 years with and without VI from 2014 to 2024.

Methods

This cross-sectional study using publicly available, deidentified data from the 2014–2024 Current Population Survey (CPS) Annual Social and Economic Supplements was deemed not human participants research and was exempted from approval and informed consent by the University of California, Irvine’s institutional review board. It followed the STROBE reporting guideline. Our main outcomes were insurance coverage at the interview date (uninsured, private, Medicaid, or Medicare) and total nonpremium family OOP medical spending during the previous 12 months (any, continuous, and ≥10% of family income). The sample included adults aged 19 to 64 years with and without self-identified VI.

We estimated weighted outcome rates to examine unadjusted trends and linear yearly outcome trends with regression models adjusted for demographic and health-related covariates. Analyses, conducted using Stata, version 17, accounted for the survey’s complex design using replicate weights. Two-sided P < .05 was considered significant. The eMethods in Supplement 1 provide additional details.

Results

Among 12 166 adults with VI, 51.8% (95% CI, 46.8%–56.8%) were female and 48.2% (95% CI, 43.2%–53.2%) were male; most were aged 50 to 64 years (56.2%; 95% CI, 52.3%–60.0%) and unmarried (64.1%; 95% CI, 59.2%–68.7%), and 40.4% (95% CI, 38.3%–42.6%) had annual household income below $30 000. Among 1 074 969 adults without VI, 50.8% (95% CI, 46.7%–54.8%) were female and 49.3% (95% CI, 45.2%–53.3%) were male, 32.1% (95% CI, 29.1%–35.3%) were aged 50 to 64 years, 47.9% (95% CI, 41.0%–54.9%) were unmarried, and 14.6% (95% CI, 14.3%–14.8%) had household income below $30 000 (Table).

The Figure, A, illustrates unadjusted trends in coverage types from 2014 to 2024. Among adults with VI, the uninsured rate declined from 15.1% (95% CI, 12.3%–17.9%) to 10.1% (95% CI, 8.0%–12.3%), consistent with a significant adjusted downward trend (adjusted odds ratio [AOR], 0.94; 95% CI, 0.92–0.96). There were increases in private coverage and Medicaid coverage among adults with VI, whereas Medicare coverage declined, with the sharpest drop after 2021. However, only the adjusted trend for Medicaid coverage in adults with VI was significant (AOR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.03–1.07). Uninsured rates were similar in both groups, but private coverage was substantially lower and Medicaid and Medicare coverage were higher among adults with VI.

The Figure also shows unadjusted trends in OOP spending from 2014 to 2024. Among adults with VI, the prevalence of any OOP spending declined slightly (AOR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.92–0.97) (Figure, B). Analogously, the percentage of adults with VI whose OOP was 10% or more of family income decreased from 17.9% (95% CI, 15.1%–20.7%) to 11.1% (95% CI, 8.7%–13.5%) (AOR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–0.99), but this percentage remained more than double that among adults without VI in 2024. Mean OOP spending among adults with VI also declined, from $2907.58 (95% CI, $2568.48-$3246.69) to $2495.27 (95% CI, $2088.51-$2902.02), with a significant adjusted trend (−$101.92; 95% CI, −$141.38 to −$62.46) (Figure, C). Among adults without VI, mean OOP remained more stable over time, with a small adjusted downward trend (−$49.14; 95% CI, −$56.97 to −$41.32).

Discussion

After the Affordable Care Act’s implementation, uninsured rates fell, rates of private and Medicaid coverage increased, and Medicare coverage decreased among adults with VI. Changes in the composition of coverage sources among adults with VI may have important implications for health care access, utilization, and health, given differences in services covered, practitioner networks, and patient cost sharing across coverage types. Increased Medicaid coverage may have contributed to lower cost sharing,7 although access to practitioners may depend on the structure of state Medicaid programs and practitioner supply.

We also found reductions in OOP health care spending, with a decline in rates of OOP expenses of 10% or more of family income from nearly 18% in 2014 to about 11% in 2024. However, adults with VI still had more than double the rate of high OOP burden vs those without VI in 2024. Adults with vs without VI have been shown to have higher health care spending for health services both related and unrelated to eye care.5,8 Future research is warranted to identify targeted policy interventions that may help close gaps.

Our study has limitations. First, VI and all outcome measures were self-reported and could be subject to bias. Second, individuals with significant disabilities may be less likely than those without to participate in surveys such as CPS. Third, our measure of OOP spending was based on family-level spending, and we could not distinguish spending on different types of care (eg, eye care).
