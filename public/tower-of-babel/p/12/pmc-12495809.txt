Prognostic nutritional index (PNI) for predicting perioperative complications after tuberculous constrictive pericarditis surgery: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Qiuyi Cai, Jing Guo, Zhengkai Zhao, Lijuan Zhang, Shuangshuang Yan, Li Zhang, Shuzhen Wang, Qiuyu Xiao, Jin Gao, Feng Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerioperative complications following pericardiectomy in patients with constrictive pericarditis can significantly affect cardiac function recovery and postoperative outcomes. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), a well-established nutritional marker, has been shown to predict outcomes in various diseases. However, its role as a predictive factor in patients with tuberculous constrictive pericarditis undergoing pericardiectomy remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative PNI and adverse perioperative outcomes in this patient population.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 158 patients with tuberculous constrictive pericarditis who underwent pericardiectomy between January 2016 and June 2024. Preoperative PNI was calculated using the formula: (10 × serum albumin [g/dL]) + (0.005 × total lymphocyte count [cells/mm³]). The optimal PNI cutoff value was determined via ROC curve analysis, and patients were categorized into two groups: PNI ≥ 36.11 and PNI < 36.11. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between PNI and adverse perioperative outcomes.ResultsOf the 158 patients, 67.7% had a PNI < 36.11. Significant differences were observed between the two groups in hemoglobin levels, platelet count, C-reactive protein, indirect bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase, cholinesterase, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), D-dimer, positive ventricular septal bounce sign, pericardial calcification, pericardial effusion, and pleural effusion. Patients with PNI < 36.11 experienced longer total and postoperative hospital stays. The incidence of adverse perioperative events was 76.6% (82/107) in the PNI < 36.11 group. Both univariate logistic regression (OR = 4.324, 95% CI: 2.12–8.816) and multivariate logistic regression (OR = 3.586, 95% CI: 1.523–8.444) confirmed that lower PNI was significantly associated with increased adverse perioperative outcomes.ConclusionA lower preoperative PNI is strongly associated with a higher incidence of adverse perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing pericardiectomy for tuberculous constrictive pericarditis. As a reliable indicator of preoperative nutritional status, PNI can serve as an independent risk factor and a modifiable clinical parameter to reduce adverse outcomes.

Introduction

Tuberculous pericarditis, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, manifests clinically as pericardial effusion, exudative constrictive pericarditis, or constrictive pericarditis [1]. Studies have reported that tuberculous pericarditis accounts for up to 80% of cases requiring pericardiocentesis [2]. When the condition progresses to persistent constrictive pericarditis, it often results in heart failure, necessitating surgical intervention via pericardiectomy [3, 4]. However, pericardiectomy is associated with significant postoperative complications, including low cardiac output syndrome, bleeding, arrhythmias, renal failure, and respiratory failure [5]. Among these, low cardiac output syndrome, congestive heart failure, and ventricular arrhythmias are closely associated with increased 30-day postoperative mortality [6, 7]. Therefore, identifying reliable preoperative risk factors to predict perioperative complications is crucial for enabling timely interventions and reducing mortality rates in clinical practice.

Nutritional status is a well-established factor influencing both humoral and cellular immune responses [8]. Malnutrition significantly increases the risk of severe consequences in tuberculosis and is closely linked to disease progression [9]. The dissemination of Mycobacterium tuberculosis to the pericardium primarily occurs via rupture of adjacent lymph nodes [10], with other routes including hematogenous dissemination and, less commonly, direct spread from an infected pleura [1, 11]. The resulting chronic inflammatory process, driven by persistent infection, represents a prolonged physiological response exacerbated by malnutrition, stress, viral infections, and environmental toxins [12]. Given these factors, evaluating reliable nutritional parameters becomes essential for assessing the risk of perioperative complications in patients with tuberculous pericarditis undergoing pericardiectomy.

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), calculated from serum albumin concentration and peripheral blood lymphocyte count, serves as a simple yet effective marker of nutritional status, inflammatory activity, and overall prognosis [13]. Unlike other screening tools, PNI is readily available from routine preoperative laboratory tests. To date, PNI has been widely used to evaluate prognosis and disease progression in oncology [14, 15] as well as cardiovascular disease [16, 17]. However, most studies have focused on PNI’s role in predicting long-term outcomes such as survival or mortality, with limited exploration of its utility in predicting perioperative complications. Notably, its application in assessing perioperative risks in patients with tuberculous constrictive pericarditis undergoing surgery remains unexplored.

In this study, we hypothesize that a lower preoperative PNI is associated with a higher incidence of adverse perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing pericardiectomy for tuberculous constrictive pericarditis. The aim of this study was to validate this hypothesis and provide a reference for the preoperative PNI threshold that can predict the occurrence of adverse perioperative outcomes following pericardiectomy.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study analyzed data from 193 patients hospitalized for tuberculous pericarditis at the Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu between January 2016 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of tuberculous pericarditis based on etiological, molecular biological, or pathological methods; (2) pericardial constriction confirmed by imaging and hemodynamic assessments, as well as postoperative pathology. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Concurrent malignancies or autoimmune diseases; (2) Incomplete perioperative laboratory/imaging data. After applying these criteria, 158 eligible patients were included in the final analysis. All patients underwent pericardiectomy. Patient screening process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the study according to the CONSORT diagram

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Third People’s Hospital (Approval Number: 2023-S-183). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the anonymization of all patient data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Surgical procedures

All patients underwent surgery by either associate chief surgeons or chief surgeons with more experience. After tracheal intubation under general anesthesia, all patients underwent radical pericardiectomy via a median sternotomy, except for two critically ill patients with impaired cardiopulmonary function, for whom left-sided thoracotomy was chosen because of concerns that they would not be able to tolerate the procedure.

The method of surgical pericardial dissection: exposure of the ascending aorta - left ventricular outflow tract - left ventricular surface - apical - ventricular outflow tract and right ventricle - atrioventricular ring - right atrium - superior and inferior vena cava. The extent of pericardiectomy includes: minimum to the anterior border of the septal nerve on both sides, up to the root of the great vessels, down to the septal surface and the apex of the heart, full relaxation of the superior and inferior vena cava to the entrance of the right atrium, complete relaxation of the atrioventricular loop.

Outcome definitions

The perioperative period was defined as the time from 5 days before surgery to 7–12 days post-surgery. Adverse outcomes during this period, including low cardiac output syndrome, pulmonary infections, electrolyte imbalances, abdominal infections, hemorrhagic or septic shock, gastrointestinal bleeding, incision infections, cardiac rupture, acute liver or kidney dysfunction, and all-cause mortality, were classified as adverse prognostic events.

The optimal cut-off value for PNI was determined using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Imaging data collection

Preoperative echocardiographic assessments were conducted using echocardiography systems from Siemens, Philips, and GE. Measurements included the left atrial anterior-posterior diameter (LAD), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), and right ventricular anterior-posterior diameter (RVD) from the parasternal long-axis view, as well as the right atrial transverse diameter (RAD) and early diastolic mitral valve peak flow velocity (E) from the apical four-chamber view. Tissue Doppler imaging was used to obtain the early diastolic mitral annular peak velocity (e′), and the E/e′ ratio was subsequently calculated. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was determined using the Simpson’s method. The severity of mitral and tricuspid valve regurgitation was classified as follows: normal (< 2 cm²), mild (2–4 cm²), moderate (4–8 cm²), and severe (> 8 cm²).

Additionally, retrospective electrocardiographically gated enhanced cardiac CT and plain chest CT scans were performed using Siemens and Philips scanners. Images were acquired during breath-hold following deep inspiration, with a reconstruction slice thickness of 1.5 mm. Preoperative CT scans were analyzed on the PACS workstation to assess pericardial calcification, pericardial effusion, and bilateral pleural effusion. The severity of pericardial effusion was graded based on the maximum distance between the visceral and parietal pericardial layers during diastole: mild (10–15 mm), moderate (15–25 mm), and severe (> 25 mm). All imaging data were independently evaluated by two experienced CT diagnosticians. In cases of disagreement, a senior diagnostician provided the final judgment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on data distribution, and compared using independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n, %) and compared using chi-square (χ²) tests or Fisher’s exact tests. To assess the relationship between preoperative PNI and adverse perioperative prognostic events, PNI was treated as a categorical variable. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables showing statistical significance in the univariate analysis and clinically relevant baseline characteristics were included in the multivariate analysis. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 22.0 (IBM, Chicago, USA), with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Clinical baseline characteristics of the study population

The final cohort comprised 158 patients with surgically confirmed tuberculous constrictive pericarditis who met all eligibility criteria and underwent pericardiectomy.

To determine the optimal cut-off value of the PNI for predicting adverse perioperative outcomes, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The results identified an optimal PNI cut-off value of 36.11, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.703, a sensitivity of 61.1%, and a specificity of 77.9% (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis of PNI in tuberculous constrictive pericarditis

Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis of PNI in tuberculous constrictive pericarditis

Patients were stratified into two groups based on their PNI values: PNI < 36.11 and PNI ≥ 36.11. Patients with PNI < 36.11 were classified as malnourished, accounting for 67.7% (107/158) of the cohort.

The baseline characteristics of the two groups were compared. No statistically significant differences were observed with respect to age, gender, BMI, disease duration, cardiac functional class, or comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and atrial fibrillation). However, patients in the PNI < 36.11 group exhibited statistically significant differences in laboratory parameters compared to those with PNI ≥ 36.11: Higher levels of platelet count (P = 0.025), C-reactive protein (CRP) (P < 0.001), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (P = 0.031), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) (P < 0.001), and D-dimer (P < 0.001) were observed in the PNI < 36.11 group (P < 0.05). Lower levels of hemoglobin (P = 0.018), indirect bilirubin (P = 0.017), and cholinesterase (P < 0.001) were noted in the same group. (Table 1).

Imaging examinations revealed no significant differences in atrial and ventricular dimensions or ejection fraction (EF) between the two groups. However, patients with a PNI < 36.11 demonstrated significantly higher incidences of ventricular septal bounce (P = 0.041), pericardial effusion (P = 0.045), and pleural effusion (P < 0.001) compared to those with a PNI ≥ 36.11. Conversely, the incidence of pericardial calcification was significantly lower in the PNI < 36.11 group compared to the PNI ≥ 36.11 group (P = 0.014). (Table 1).

Adverse perioperative outcomes in pericardiectomy

The incidence of adverse perioperative outcomes was significantly higher among patients with PNI < 36.11, at 76.6% (82/107), compared to those with PNI ≥ 36.11 (P < 0.001). The most common adverse outcomes were postoperative lung infections (P = 0.007) and electrolyte disturbances (P < 0.018). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding other adverse outcomes.

Although no significant difference was observed in postoperative ICU stay between the two groups, patients with PNI < 36.11 had significantly longer overall hospital stays (P = 0.015) and postoperative hospital stays (P = 0.004) compared to patients with PNI ≥ 36.11. (Table 2).

Association between prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and adverse perioperative outcomes

To investigate the relationship between PNI and adverse perioperative outcomes, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed, with PNI treated as a categorical variable.

Univariate analysis revealed that a lower PNI (< 36.11), along with elevated neutrophil count and reduced cholinesterase, was significantly associated with an increased risk of adverse perioperative outcomes. Specifically, the odds ratio (OR) for the PNI < 36.11 group was 4.324 (95% CI: 2.12–8.816, P < 0.001), indicating a strong association between malnutrition and adverse events.

In the subsequent multivariate logistic regression analysis, factors that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included. The results demonstrated that PNI < 36.11 remained an independent predictor of adverse perioperative outcomes, with an adjusted OR of 3.586 (95% CI: 1.523–8.444, P = 0.003). In addition, neutrophils were also associated with an increased risk of adverse perioperative outcomes, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 1.288 (95% confidence interval: 1.036–1.601, P = 0.023). Discussion

It is well established that tuberculosis contributes to a decline in nutritional status through mechanisms such as inflammation-induced cachexia, anorexia, and malabsorption [18]. Tuberculosis-related malnutrition is characterized by a reduction in fat-free mass, independent of BMI, and can occur even in overweight or obese individuals [19]. Alterations in body composition or impaired gastrointestinal function resulting from malnutrition may influence the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of antituberculosis medications, potentially leading to treatment failure or increased virulence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis [20]. This, in turn, raises the risk of extrapulmonary tuberculosis infection and disease progression. Tuberculous pericarditis, a leading cause of constrictive pericarditis [3], often manifests in conjunction with various forms of malnutrition, compounding the inflammatory burden and imposing a dual challenge on the body. In the present study, based on the optimal cutoff value of the PNI, 67.7% of patients with tuberculous constrictive pericarditis were found to be malnourished.

A randomized clinical trial among hospitalized patients at nutritional risk demonstrated that individualized nutritional support during hospitalization significantly improved clinical outcomes, including survival rates, compared to standard hospital meal [21]. Various tools are available for assessing the nutritional status of hospitalized patients, including the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score [22], the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) [23], and the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI). However, the CONUT score, which incorporates serum albumin, lymphocyte count, and cholesterol levels, may be influenced by the long-term use of medications such as statins. The GNRI, on the other hand, is primarily designed for elderly populations, while tuberculosis predominantly affects younger individuals. Therefore, applying CONUT or GNRI to patients with tuberculous pericarditis may introduce bias. In contrast, the PNI offers a more appropriate and practical alternative for this patient population.

Our study demonstrates that a low PNI is an independent predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing pericardiectomy. Even after adjusting for other risk factors, patients with a low PNI experienced significantly higher rates of adverse outcomes and longer hospital stays compared to those with a high PNI. As the preoperative PNI reflects the patient’s nutritional and immune status, dynamic monitoring of PNI can be incorporated into a routine preoperative risk assessment system to explore the impact of preoperative nutritional interventions based on PNI stratification on perioperative outcomes, and to develop a standardized protocol for nutritional assessment and management for patients undergoing pericardiectomy, including the management of preoperative comorbidities, enhanced perioperative care, and individualized nutritional support. Furthermore, In the surgical setting of emergency pericardiectomy requiring immediate intervention, risk stratification based on preoperative PNI can help to immediately triage patients with poor nutritional status to targeted, specific postoperative monitoring and care pathways (Fig. 3). These measures may help reduce the risk of adverse postoperative outcomes.

The mechanisms linking the PNI to perioperative metabolic and prognostic changes remain unclear. Nevertheless, previous studies have proposed several plausible explanations. A large-scale study in the Netherlands found that preoperative and postoperative serum albumin levels were correlated with myocardial injury following cardiac surgery [24]. Other research by Dagenais et al. [25] and Schunkert et al. [26] highlights that serum albumin contributes to maintaining intravascular blood volume by preserving vascular wall integrity. Decreased serum albumin can lead to increased tissue edema and reduced circulating blood volume due to extravasation. Rapp-Kesek et al. [27] further reported that low serum albumin levels increase the risk of postoperative infections.

Consistent with these findings, our study confirmed that patients with lower PNI were more likely to develop hypoalbuminemia, lung infections, and electrolyte disturbances during the perioperative period. As serum albumin is a well-established marker of nutritional status [28, 29], its levels can be influenced by altered hepatic protein metabolism and capillary leakage in acute and chronic inflammatory conditions [30–32]. Thus, serum albumin can serve as an indicator of disease severity in inflammatory state [33, 34]. A reduced PNI, reflecting both low serum albumin and peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, is indicative of impaired immune function and potential deficiencies in protein synthesis. Such deficiencies may reduce the body’s resistance to surgical trauma and stress, contributing to severe postoperative inflammation and increased vascular permeability, ultimately raising the risk of adverse perioperative outcomes.

One severe complication following cardiac surgery is low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS), which increases postoperative mortality by 12 to 25 time [35]. While no prior studies have explored the relationship between the PNI and LCOS, we found no significant correlation between PNI and the occurrence of LCOS in our study. This may be due to the lack of statistical differences in preoperative LCOS risk factors, such as advanced age and atrial fibrillation, between the two PNI groups. Additionally, constrictive pericarditis typically causes congestive right heart failure, with preserved left ventricular function in most patient [4]. Following pericardiectomy, the reduction in afterload lowers the risk of LCOS compared to other types of cardiac surgeries.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, as a single-center retrospective cohort study with a limited sample size, this work is susceptible to selection bias and reduced statistical power. These limitations may compromise the generalizability of our findings and introduce variability in prognostic assessments. Future large-scale, multicenter studies utilizing propensity score matching are warranted to validate our observations and elucidate potential heterogeneity in the prognostic utility of the PNI across demographic strata. Second, sarcopenia and protein-losing enteropathy are also important factors affecting nutritional status, which have not been evaluated in the current study, and the study by Lee et al. suggests that there are more appropriate assessment tools for sarcopenia [36], so the value of combining these assessment tools in preoperative nutritional evaluation of patients with tuberculous pericarditis could be explored in the future. Finally, this study was unable to evaluate the impact of preoperative PNI on perioperative outcomes after the specific nutritional interventions the patients received. Future studies are warranted to address these limitations.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that a low preoperative PNI in patients with tuberculous constrictive pericarditis is significantly associated with a higher incidence of perioperative adverse outcomes, particularly postoperative lung infections and electrolyte disturbances. As an assessment tool for preoperative nutritional status, the PNI serves as an independent risk factor for adverse perioperative outcomes. Importantly, the PNI is a modifiable biomarker, and targeted nutritional and clinical interventions can help reduce the risk of adverse perioperative events following pericardiectomy.
