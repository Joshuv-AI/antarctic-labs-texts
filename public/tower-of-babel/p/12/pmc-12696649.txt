Arsenic regulates ALKBH1 abundance and substrate specificity to promote translation and tumorigenicity
Authors: Emma Wilkinson, Yan-Hong Cui, Zizhao Yang, Michelle Verghese, Seungwon Yang, Ming Sun, Houxiang Zhu, Jack Peterson, Jiangbo Wei, Yingming Zhao, Mengjie Chen, Chuan He, Yu-Ying He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe mechanisms that underlie arsenic tumorigenicity remain incompletely understood. Here, we show that arsenic regulates the abundance and the demethylase activity of ALKBH1, which, in turn, promotes arsenic-induced skin tumorigenesis. At the molecular level, knockdown of ALKBH1 in arsenic-exposed keratinocytes increases m6A enrichment in mRNA, and ALKBH1 demethylates m6A in vitro in an arsenic-dependent manner. Arsenic binds to the ALKBH1 protein at cysteine residues to regulate the m6A RNA demethylase activity of ALKBH1. m6A-immunoprecipitation (IP)-sequencing demonstrates that ALKBH1 regulates m6A demethylation on the NR2C2 transcript to inhibit NR2C2 protein translation in a YTHDF1-dependent manner. Functionally, knockdown of ALKBH1 increases NR2C2 expression, leading to decreased mTOR activation and global translation. Our work uncovers a previously unknown mechanism into arsenic tumorigenicity, adds understanding into the functional effect of arsenic binding to proteins, and implicates ALKBH1 as a potential druggable target and biomarker for arsenic tumorigenicity.

INTRODUCTION

Arsenic is a metalloid found both within the Earth’s crust and derived from anthropogenic sources, such as industry waste discharge.1 Arsenic from the Earth’s crust can leach into neighboring bodies of water and lead to contamination.1 A primary source of arsenic exposure is through ingesting contaminated drinking water. Arsenic is colorless, odorless, and tasteless, and individuals exposed to arsenic are often unaware of exposure until the onset of symptoms.2 Symptoms of arsenic exposure are dependent on concentration. Exposure to arsenic at high concentrations in the blood leads to the rapid onset of symptoms and, often times, death.3 Chronic exposure to low levels of arsenic, however, contributes to the carcinogenesis of several types of cancer, including skin cancer, lung cancer, and kidney cancer.3 Chronic arsenic exposure also leads to other pathologies, including cardiovascular diseases and diabetes.2

The most well-documented case of chronic arsenic exposure on a population-scale occurred in Bangladesh in the 1990s as populations within this region were exposed to elevated levels of arsenic in drinking water, originating from water sources that were contained in tube wells contaminated with arsenic.1,2 To this day, populations within this region serve as areas of clinical interest and evidence of the long-term clinical effects of chronic arsenic exposure. Due to the prevalence of arsenic in the environment, arsenic has remained one of the top hazards listed by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.4

A major target organ for arsenic exposure is the skin.2,3 Arsenic binds to proteins at cysteine residues.4 In the skin, arsenic binds strongly to sulfhydryl-rich keratin proteins, leading to arsenic accumulation.4 A hallmark indicator of such arsenic accumulation is the development of arsenical keratoses, which are pre-malignant lesions that can lead to the development of skin cancer.2

The field of epitranscriptomics aims to understand the role of RNA modifications in modulating gene expression. RNA modifications exist on all types of RNA species.5 To date, over 100 modifications have been identified, and current research seeks to elucidate the role of these modifications in gene expression and cellular functions.5 RNA modifications are deposited onto RNA species by writer enzymes (methyltransferases) and are removed by eraser enzymes (demethylases).5 The process of writing and erasing RNA modifications emphasizes the dynamic nature of these modifications.5

Our lab recently identified a critical role for m6A mRNA methylation and the m6A demethylase FTO in arsenic-induced skin cancer,6 establishing a critical role for epitranscriptomics in arsenic tumorigenicity. FTO, along with ALKBH5,7 belongs to the Fe2+-and α-ketoglutarate-dependent AlkB family of demethylases.8 Members of this family differ in their activity, substrate specificity, and subcellular localization.8

ALKBH1 is another member of the AlkB family. Functionally, ALKBH1 is involved in several cellular processes and is required for development.9–11 ALKBH1 has been also found to contribute to several cancers, including glioblastoma12 and lung cancer.13 However, how ALKBH1 activity is regulated remains largely unknown. ALKBH1 acts on a diverse array of substrates, including N6-methyladenine (6mA) on DNA,14,15 histone H2A,9 and m5C16,17 and m1A10 on tRNA. Although ALKBH1 has been previously found to regulate m6A mRNA methylation, such function lacks sufficient direct evidence in vitro.10,13

Here, we report a mechanistic role for ALKBH1 in arsenic-induced skin cancer. Our findings provide insights into ALKBH1’s role in arsenic-driven tumorigenesis and highlight its potential relevance in other arsenic-associated cancers. Targeting ALKBH1, either alone or alongside other arsenic-dependent pathways, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for arsenic-associated cancers.

RESULTS

Arsenic up-regulates ALKBH1 protein expression via inhibiting ALKBH1 protein degradation

Using an established model of arsenic-induced malignant transformation of skin keratinocytes, we previously identified that the m6A demethylase FTO promoted arsenic-induced skin tumorigenicity.6,18 In this model, we treated HaCaT cells, an immortalized keratinocyte cell line, with a physiologically relevant dose of arsenic (100 nM) for 28 weeks and termed these cells chronic arsenic cells (As).6,18,19 While we previously worked to characterize the mechanisms that govern arsenic tumorigenicity (after 28 weeks of chronic arsenic exposure), the cellular and molecular mechanisms that are employed throughout the arsenic-induced transformation process remain unknown.

To address this gap in knowledge, we transformed HaCaT cells with arsenic and sought to capture phenotypic changes throughout the transformation process. In parallel, we also transformed BEAS-2B lung epithelial cells to evaluate arsenic-dependent phenotypic changes across model systems. We found that 3 months of chronic arsenic exposure led to increases in proliferation in both HaCaT and BEAS-2B cells (Figures 1A–1C). These results suggest that 3 months of chronic arsenic exposure is an important inflection point within the arsenic-induced transformation process. Of note, we found that 2 months of chronic arsenic exposure in BEAS-2B cells induced increases in proliferation, which signifies potential cell-type-specific changes in arsenic transformation across models.

We next sought to determine potential drivers of these observed arsenic-induced phenotypic changes. Our lab has previously found that epitranscriptomic modifiers, specifically the m6A mRNA demethylase and AlkB family member FTO, play critical roles in promoting arsenic-induced tumorigenesis.6 Using a targeted screen of epitranscriptomic modifiers at 3 months within the transformation process, we found that the AlkB family member ALKBH1 was up-regulated, while other AlkB family members, including ALKBH5 and FTO, showed no change in expression at this time point, nor did m6A writers METTL3 or METTL14 (Figure 1D), implicating that ALKBH1 may play a role within the early stages of the response to chronic arsenic exposure.

We then traced ALKBH1 up-regulation across the arsenic transformation process and inquired whether ALKBH1 up-regulation correlated with our observed increases in proliferation in chronic arsenic-exposed HaCaT and BEAS-2B cells (Figures 1A–1C). Interestingly, we found that ALKBH1 up-regulation was indeed correlated with the increases in proliferation that we observed in these chronic arsenic-exposed cells (Figures 1E–1G). These results suggest that ALKBH1 up-regulation correlates with increased proliferation and, more broadly, correlates with the adoption of tumorigenic traits in chronic arsenic-exposed cells. These results further signify that ALKBH1 up-regulation by chronic arsenic exposure is a relatively early event within the arsenic-induced transformation process.

Next, we asked whether ALKBH1 was also up-regulated by short-term arsenic exposure, which is characterized by 24–72 h arsenic exposure. We found that the ALKBH1 protein levels were increased in HaCaT and BEAS-2B cells in response to short-term arsenic exposure (Figures 1H and 1I). Of note, we did not observe changes in ALKBH1 in response to cadmium (Figure 1J), another carcinogenic metalloid. We then asked whether treatment with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC), known to reduce cysteine residues, disrupt thiolated proteins, bind arsenic, and act as an arsenic antitoxin,20–22 could theoretically abrogate arsenic-induced ALKBH1 protein up-regulation. Indeed, we found that pre-treatment with NAC abrogated arsenic-mediated ALKBH1 up-regulation in HaCaT cells (Figure 1K). Taken together, these data suggest that ALKBH1 expression is responsive to both short-term and chronic arsenic exposure.

We next sought to determine whether ALKBH1 was up-regulated in arsenic-tumorigenic or arsenic-transformed keratinocytes. These cells are derived from As cells, which were subjected to two paths after chronic arsenic exposure: (1) the As cells were injected into nude mice and the tumorigenic cells were isolated into cell lines, which are referred to as arsenic-tumorigenic (As-T) cells.6 Concurrently, (2) the As cells also underwent soft agar cloning, and the clones were then isolated into cell lines, which are referred to as arsenic-transformed (As-Tr) cells (Figure 2A). As-Tr cells retain their tumorigenic capacity when injected into nude mice, as previously reported.23 We noted that ALKBH1 protein levels, and to a lesser extent, ALKBH1 mRNA levels, were increased in both As-T and As-Tr cells, suggesting that ALKBH1 may be dually required both during and after transformation (Figures 2B–2D). We next hypothesized that arsenic may up-regulate ALKBH1 protein expression more predominately via controlling ALKBH1 protein stability or translation. Using a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay, we found that ALKBH1 protein stability was increased in As-Tr cells as compared to HaCaT control cells (Figure 2E). We then sought to determine the mechanism by which ALKBH1 protein stability was increased. Two major regulators of protein stability are proteasome- and lysosome-mediated protein degradation.24,25 Interestingly, we found that treatment with either MG-132, a proteasome inhibitor, or Bafilomyin A1 (BafnA1), a lysosome inhibitor, increased ALKBH1 protein expression in HaCaT cells (Figure 2F), while these inhibitors had no effect on ALKBH1 protein expression in As-T or As-Tr cells. These results indicate that chronic arsenic exposure up-regulates ALKBH1 at least, in part, by inhibiting ALKBH1 protein degradation. We believe these results imply that the regulation of ALKBH1 protein stability is complex and may be mediated by more than one mechanism.

We also inquired whether our findings in chronic arsenic-exposed keratinocytes translated to human squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), which is the sub-type of skin cancer commonly found in arsenic-exposed individuals. We found that ALKBH1 was increased in human SCC samples, as compared to normal skin (Figures S1A and S1B), and in A431 SCC cells, as compared to non-tumorigenic HaCaT cells (Figure S1C), suggesting a critical role for ALKBH1 in SCC.

ALKBH1 up-regulation plays a critical role in arsenic-induced tumorigenicity

As we observed that ALKBH1 is up-regulated in response to both chronic and short-term arsenic exposure (Figures 1 and 2) and in SCC (Figure S1), we hypothesized that ALKBH1 may serve as an oncogenic factor in promoting arsenic-induced tumorigenicity. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the effect of ALKBH1 inhibition in As-Tr and As-T cells. We noted that knockdown of ALKBH1 (Figures 3A and 3B) in As-Tr and As-T cells decreased proliferation (Figure 3C), colony formation (Figures 3D and 3E), growth in soft agar (Figures 3F and 3G), and tumor growth and volume (Figures 3H and 3I). These data demonstrate that ALKBH1 is oncogenic and is a critical factor involved in arsenic-induced skin tumorigenicity.

We also sought to determine whether the tumor-promoting role of ALKBH1 is limited to arsenic-induced skin cancer. Indeed, we found that knockdown of ALKBH1 in A431 skin cancer cells also decreased tumor growth (Figures S2A–S2C), suggesting an expanded oncogenic role for ALKBH1 in SCC.

ALKBH1 acts as an m6A demethylase in an arsenic-dependent manner

Next, we sought to identify the functional substrate of ALKBH1 in chronic arsenic-exposed keratinocytes. ALKBH1 has several reported substrates and exhibits a cell-type- and context-specific substrate specificity.9,10,12–17 In our previous work, we noted that chronic arsenic-exposed cells exhibit low levels of m6A.6 We found that FTO, a known m6A demethylase, demethylated a number of critical transcripts in order to promote arsenic-induced skin cancer.6 However, while m6A levels were low in these cells, we found that knockout of FTO led to a moderate change in m6A levels (Figure S2D), suggesting that there may be other FTO-independent mechanisms that could contribute to m6A demethylation in these cells.

Due to the high expression of ALKBH1 in As-Tr cells and the variety of ALKBH1substrates that are context-specific, we asked whether ALKBH1 could regulate m6A levels in As-Tr cells. Using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) assay in polyadenylated mRNAs (Figure 4A) and mRNA dot blotting (Figure 4B), we found that knockdown of ALKBH1 in As-Tr cells increased m6A levels in mRNA. Concurrently, we identified that global m6A levels in mRNA were decreased in short-term arsenic-exposed cells and As-Tr cells, consistent with ALKBH1 up-regulation (Figure S3). We then investigated whether functional changes in ALKBH1-mediated tumorigenicity were dependent on m6A.

Using siRNA, we found that knockdown of the m6A writer METTL3 led to increased proliferation in shALKBH1 As-Tr cells (Figures 4C and 4D), suggesting that the oncogenic role of ALKBH1 is at least, in part, mediated by m6A. Knockdown of ALKBH1 also did not change the expression of other m6A machinery (Figure 4E).

We next sought to determine (1) whether the effect of ALKBH1 on m6A was a direct effect and (2) what the impact of arsenic was on ALKBH1’s demethylase specificity. To answer these questions, we performed an in vitro demethylation assay using a cell-free system. Using previously published reaction conditions10 and a previously validated m6A-decorated single-stranded oligo,26 we found that pre-incubating the purified wild-type (WT) human ALKBH1 protein with arsenic led to demethylation, as evidenced by m6A dot blot (Figure 4F), while ALKBH1 alone without arsenic had no effect, consistent with previous reports.10 We further sought to determine whether arsenic alone leads to demethylation and found that incubating the m6A oligo alone with arsenic (no ALKBH1 protein) led to no changes in m6A demethylation (Figure 4G). These data suggest that ALKBH1-mediated m6A demethylation is dependent on both arsenic and the ALKBH1 protein.

Arsenic binds to ALKBH1 to regulate ALKBH1’s m6A demethylase activity

The direct mechanism for arsenic modality remains poorly understood. Arsenic binds to proteins, which, in turn, can influence protein expression and functionality.4 As arsenic binds to proteins at cysteine residues,4 we asked whether arsenic could bind to ALKBH1 at specific cysteine residues. Using AutoDock, we identified several candidate cysteine residues on ALKBH1 that showed potential favorable binding (Figure 5A). Of note, we used the murine ALKBH1 protein for this analysis as the human ALKBH1 protein was unusable for this analysis in the Protein DataBank (PDB). Candidate cysteine residues 118 and 207 in ALKBH1 showed favorable bond lengths with arsenic (Figure 5A). The cysteine residue at position 110 is not conserved in the human ALKBH1 protein and therefore we did not pursue further mechanistic studies on this residue.

We next sought to validate our in silico docking studies and identify whether arsenic could bind to ALKBH1 in vitro and in vivo. Using a drug affinity response target stability (DARTS) assay27 (Figure 5B), we found that ALKBH1 showed binding with arsenic in HaCaT cells (Figure 5C). As a complementary approach, we also used a commercially available arsenic-biotin conjugate,28 followed by immunoprecipitation, to further determine whether arsenic could bind to ALKBH1 (Figure 5D). This biotin pull-down assay demonstrated that the arsenic-biotin conjugate did indeed bind to, and pull down, ALKBH1 in HaCaT cells (Figure 5E), suggesting that arsenic binds to ALKBH1 in vivo. We also found that ALKBH1 was pulled down in As-Tr cells, suggesting that our observed arsenic-ALKBH1 binding is conserved after transformation (Figure 5F). Furthermore, using a recombinant ALKBH1 protein incubated with the arsenic-biotin conjugate, we recapitulated our in vivo biotin pull-down data and demonstrated that arsenic binds to ALKBH1 in vitro (Figure 5G). We found no evidence of in vitro binding between arsenic and FTO, ALKBH5, or the m6A writers METTL3 and METTL14 (Figures S4A and S4B). We also assessed the possible binding of arsenic to RNA using an in vivo biotin pull-down assay after incubation with the arsenic-biotin conjugate and found that arsenic does not bind to cellular RNA in vivo (Figures S4C and S4D).

We next investigated whether we could abrogate our observed arsenic-ALKBH1 binding by mutating candidate cysteine residues 118 and 207 to alanine (hereafter referred to as C118A and C207A) using site-directed mutagenesis. Using DARTS, we found that expression of C118A or C207A mutants in shALKBH1 As-Tr cells abrogated the arsenic-ALKBH1 interaction (Figure 5H). Using an arsenic-biotin pull-down assay with recombinant WT or C118A and C207A mutant ALKBH1 proteins (Figure S4E), we also observed a loss of ALKBH1 binding to arsenic (Figure 5I). These findings demonstrate that arsenic can bind to the ALKBH1 protein and establishes that the C118 and C207 residues are candidate binding sites for arsenic.

We next evaluated the effect of C118A and C207A mutations on ALKBH1 activity or function. Using an m6A mRNA dot blot, we found that over-expression of only the WT ALKBH1 protein, but not the D233A catalytically inactive mutant, or C118A or C207A mutants, resulted in decreased m6A in mRNA (Figure 5J). Next, we investigated whether the oncogenic function of ALKBH1 is dependent on its demethylase or arsenic-binding capacity. We found that over-expression of the WT ALKBH1 protein only, and not the D233A, C118A, or C207A mutants, could rescue proliferation (Figure 5K), suggesting that the demethylase domain and arsenic-binding sites are critical to ALKBH1’s oncogenic function.

ALKBH1 exhibits nuclear localization in As-Tr cells

ALKBH1 has been found to localize to several subcellular compartments, including the nucleus,9,29 mitochondria,30,31 and mitochondrial granules32 in a cell-type-specific manner. We next sought to visualize ALKBH1 localization in HaCaT and As-Tr cells. Using confocal microscopy, we noted that ALKBH1 was mainly localized to the cytoplasm in HaCaT cells, while ALKBH1 was mainly visible in the nucleus/nucleoplasm in As-Tr cells (Figure S5). We also noted that ALKBH1 aggregated at spots within the nucleus in As-Tr cells, which resemble nuclear speckles and/or may be an indication of arsenic-induced protein aggregation.33,34

NR2C2 is a functional target of ALKBH1-mediated m6A demethylation

We next mapped the functional m6A-dependent mRNA targets of ALKBH1 in As-Tr cells with or without ALKBH1 knockdown using m6A-IP-sequencing. Our data demonstrated m6A enrichment on both mRNA and ncRNA targets upon knockdown of ALKBH1 (Figure S6A). Overall, knockdown of ALKBH1 in As-Tr cells led to an increase in targets with increased m6A enrichment (Figure 6A, red box), consistent with our UHPLC/MS-MS and m6A dot blot data (Figures 4A and 4B). Of the targets that showed increases in m6A upon ALKBH1 knockdown, we identified that the orphan receptor/transcription factor NR2C2 showed the highest increase in m6A enrichment (Figure 6A).

NR2C2 belongs to a large family of nuclear hormone receptors that also function as transcription factors.35 While the ligand for NR2C2 is unknown, several studies have established critical roles for NR2C2 in hematopoiesis,35 inflammation,36 and lipid metabolism.37,38 Pathway analyses identified ‘‘neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction’’ as one of the top functional pathways up-regulated in our data, consistent with the function of NR2C2 (Figure S6B).

Using a HOMER motif analysis, we identified that the canonical m6A motif GGACU39 was the most common motif from our m6A-immunoprecipitation (IP)-sequencing data, suggesting that ALKBH1 demethylates m6A at canonical m6A motif sites (Figure 6B). Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) visualization of our m6A-IP-sequencing data (Figure 6C) and m6A-IP-qPCR analysis (Figure 6D) validated m6A increases on the NR2C2 transcript in shALKBH1 As-Tr cells. We also established that the m6A levels on the NR2C2 transcript were decreased upon short-term arsenic exposure and in As-Tr cells (Figure 6E), consistent with ALKBH1 protein up-regulation (Figures 1E and 2D). Our m6A-IP-sequencing data identified that the NR2C2 transcript showed significant changes in m6A demethylation at two sites within the 3′ UTR region (Figure S6C). Using cross-linking immunoprecipitation (CLIP)-qPCR, we validated that ALKBH1 indeed binds to the NR2C2 transcript at those sites (Figure 6F). Mechanistically, we also found that knockdown of ALKBH1 led to an increase in NR2C2 protein expression (Figure 6G), but not mRNA expression (Figure 6H).

To further validate NR2C2 as a target of ALKBH1, we determined whether NR2C2 expression was correlated with ALKBH1 expression patterns. We found that NR2C2 expression was decreased in A431 and As-Tr cells and in response to short-term arsenic exposure (Figures 6I–6K). These patterns suggest that NR2C2 expression is negatively correlated with ALKBH1 expression. We also found that NR2C2 protein expression was decreased upon over-expression of WT ALKBH1, but not D233A, C118A, or C207A mutants (Figure 6L). Furthermore, knockdown of METTL3 and METTL14 decreased NR2C2 protein expression (Figure 6M). We also inquired whether the m6A demethylase FTO could regulate NR2C2 expression. Knockout of FTO had no effect on NR2C2 protein levels, suggesting that the regulation of NR2C2 may be specific to ALKBH1 (Figure S6D).

Next, we sought to identify the reader protein responsible for ALKBH1’s regulation of NR2C2. Reader proteins are m6A binding proteins that regulate RNA metabolism.40 Of these reader proteins, YTHDF1 promotes the translation of m6A-bound transcripts.41,42 Given that ALKBH1 regulates NR2C2 protein expression, we sought to determine whether YTHDF1 could promote NR2C2 translation. Using small interfering RNA (siRNA), we found that knockdown of YTHDF1 decreased NR2C2 protein expression, suggesting that YTHDF1 may be the reader protein for m6A-modified NR2C2 transcripts (Figure 6N) and that knockdown of other reader proteins, YTHDF2 and YTHDF3, had no effect on NR2C2 expression (Figure S6E). Using RNA-immunoprecipitation (RIP)-qPCR, we further validated that YTHDF1 binds to the NR2C2 transcript (Figure 6O). These results suggest that YTHDF1 is the reader that promotes the m6A-dependent changes in NR2C2 protein expression.

Finally, we sought to evaluate the functional role of NR2C2. NR2C2 has been previously found to have a tumor suppressive role in bladder cancer.43 Using CRISPR, we knocked out NR2C2 in shALKBH1 As-Tr cells. We noted that inhibition of NR2C2 led to a robust rescue of the effect of ALKBH1 knockdown on cell proliferation and colony formation in soft agar (Figures 6P and 6Q), indicating that NR2C2 is a critical functional target of ALKBH1. Conversely, over-expression of NR2C2 in As-Tr cells decreased cell proliferation and colony formation (Figures 6R, 6S, and S6F), mimicking the effect of ALKBH1 inhibition. These results establish NR2C2 as a functional m6A-dependent target of ALKBH1 and suggest that NR2C2 may play a tumor suppressive role in As-Tr cells.

We also over-expressed NR2C2 in A431 SCC cells and similarly found that this resulted in decreased proliferation (Figures S6G and S6H). We then further explored the role of NR2C2 in non-transformed HaCaT cells and in A431 SCC cells to examine whether NR2C2 also assumes a tumor suppressive function in the absence of arsenic. In HaCaT cells, which are non-tumorigenic, we observed no colony formation and no consistent effect on cell proliferation upon NR2C2 depletion (Figures S6I–S6K). Furthermore, we also noted no significant change in colony formation or proliferation upon knockout of NR2C2 in A431 SCC cells (Figures S6L–S6N). Interestingly, knockout of NR2C2 in As-Tr cells showed a slight increase in cell proliferation (Figure S6O). These different responses to NR2C2 depletion may highlight a complexity in the NR2C2 tumor suppressive activity. It is also possible that the effect of NR2C2 depletion may depend on arsenic or arsenic-induced transformation. Further work is needed to parse out mechanistic differences in NR2C2 functionality across A431 and As-Tr cells.

To determine whether our findings can be re-capitulated in vivo, we performed immunofluorescence on tumors from a mouse co-carcinogenesis model of UVB/chronic arsenic exposure.6 We found that ALKBH1 protein levels were increased in mouse tumors, while the levels of NR2C2 and m6A were decreased (Figures S7A–S7F). Taken together, our findings demonstrate a unique relationship among ALKBH1, NR2C2, and m6A in response to arsenic exposure.

Arsenic promotes protein translation through the ALKBH1/NR2C2 pathway

Next, we aimed to establish the functional and mechanistic role of the ALKBH1/NR2C2 axis in promoting arsenic-induced tumorigenesis. Dysregulation of protein translation machinery is a major mechanism of carcinogenesis.44 Arsenic exposure leads to increased translational output, protein misfolding, protein aggregation, and proteotoxic stress.33,34,45 However, the mechanisms that underlie this arsenic-dependent translational dysregulation remain unstudied. Using a SUnSET puromycin assay,46 we observed that chronic arsenic-exposed and As-Tr cells show increased global translation (Figure 7A). We also treated HaCaT cells with or without ALKBH1 knockdown with arsenic for 72 h and found that knockdown of ALKBH1 in HaCaT cells abrogated short-term arsenic-induced increases in global translation (Figure 7B). Using SUnSET, we also show that knockdown of ALKBH1 in As-Tr cells decreases global translation (Figure 7C), suggesting a critical role for ALKBH1 in regulating global translation in an arsenic-dependent manner.

We next tested whether NR2C2 contributes to ALKBH1-dependent regulation of translation. Indeed, we found that knockdown of NR2C2 rescued the effect of ALKBH1 knockdown on global translation (Figure 7D). To determine the mechanism by which ALKBH1/NR2C2 regulates translation, we assessed whether our observed changes in translation were due to altered activation of mTOR, which is a critical regulator for global translation, and has been previously found to be regulated by arsenic.47,48 mTOR catalyzes the phosphorylation and activation of several critical downstream targets, such as 4EBP1.47 We noted that knockdown of ALKBH1 led to decreased phosphorylation of 4EBP1 (Figure 7E), suggesting that ALKBH1 may play a role in regulating mTOR, which may contribute to the changes in translation we observed.

Next, we examined how ALKBH1/NR2C2 regulates mTOR. By analyzing our RNA-sequencing data (used as input for m6A-IP-sequencing) of As-Tr cells with ALKBH1 knockdown, we found that knockdown of ALKBH1 increased the expression of SESTRIN1 (SESN1), SESTRIN2 (SESN2), and SESTRIN3 (SESN3) genes (Figure 7F), which are negative regulators of mTOR.49–51 NR2C2 is also a transcription factor.38 We hypothesized that NR2C2 could regulate SESTRIN expression through direct binding. Using NR2C2 chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data from ENCODE and visualization using IGV, we found that NR2C2 binds to the SESN1 transcript in three cell lines (HEPG2, WTC11, and K562), binds to the SESN2 transcript in HEPG2 cells only, and did not bind to the SESN3 transcript (Figures 7G and 7I). Due to consistent binding between SESN1 and NR2C2, we focused on the regulation of SESN1 by NR2C2 and confirmed that knockdown of ALKBH1 did increase SESN1 levels (Figure 7J). Furthermore, we found that knockout of NR2C2 in shALKBH1 As-Tr cells decreased SESN1 expression and increased 4EBP1 phosphorylation (Figure 7K), consistent with the inverse correlation between SESN1 expression and mTOR activation. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the ALKBH1/NR2C2 axis regulates mTOR activation.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we report a role for the demethylase ALKBH1 in promoting arsenic-induced skin cancer. We demonstrate that arsenic activates ALKBH1’s m6A mRNA demethylase activity and increases ALKBH1 protein abundance, leading to malignant transformation and tumorigenicity. Our findings also highlight expanded roles for metals/metalloids in regulating the function of proteins. While our studies are limited to arsenic, we believe that further investigation into how metals/metalloids affect protein function is a critical, yet understudied, area of research.

ALKBH1 acts on diverse substrates, with specificity varying by species, cell type, and subcellular localization.8–10,12–15,17,29–32 ALKBH1 has the largest number of reported substrates among AlkB family members, with over six validated substrates.52 A current hypothesis postulates that ALKBH1’s structure may contribute to its wide demethylation activity.53 The ability of ALKBH1 to demethylate m6A is controversial. A seminal study previously showed that ALKBH1 in E. coli has a low affinity for m6A and that the preferred substrate for ALKBH1 is m1A on tRNA.10 However, a study in human lung cancer cells showed that knockdown of ALKBH1 increased m6A.13 These differences suggest that substrate specificity varies by cell type. Whether ALKBH1 can demethylate several substrates at once, or what mediates substrate preference, remains incompletely understood. In addition, our findings show that ALKBH1 promotes global translation, distinct from a previous report showing that ALKBH1 decreases translation in an m1A-dependent manner on tRNA.10 This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in cellular context, which may be due to arsenic-dependent or keratinocyte-specific factors.

Our findings demonstrate that ALKBH1 up-regulation as a relatively early event in arsenic-induced malignant transformation of epidermal keratinocytes (Figure 1) and establish ALKBH1 as a biomarker and a critical target for arsenic transformation and tumorigenicity. The development of diagnostic tools that screen for ALKBH1 expression could be developed for populations that are exposed to elevated levels of arsenic in their drinking water. In addition, ALKBH1 inhibitors could be deployed for therapeutic use as a preventative agent in preventing arsenic-induced skin cancer. We also establish that ALKBH1 serves as a tumor-promoting role in arsenic-induced skin tumorigenesis and that inhibiting ALKBH1 leads to a robust decrease in malignant traits (Figure 3). Therefore, an ALKBH1 inhibitor may be dually used to treat and/or prevent arsenic-induced skin cancer. We also show that ALKBH1 up-regulation is similarly an early event in chronic arsenic response of lung epithelial cells, and therefore our findings may be relevant to other arsenic-induced cancers.

In summary, our findings elucidate a role for arsenic in regulating ALKBH1’s abundance and its role as an m6A mRNA demethylase and establish ALKBH1 as a critical factor in arsenic-induced skin tumorigenesis.

Limitations of the study

Our study establishes ALKBH1 as an arsenic-binding protein. Using protein docking, we identified and validated several candidate cysteine residues for arsenic binding on the ALKBH1 protein (Figures 5A–5I). From our protein docking (Figure 5A) studies, in addition to our candidate cysteine residues C118 and C207, we also identified several other potential sites. Although we believe that the candidate residues we identified are the preferred arsenic-binding sites, it is possible that arsenic may bind to alternative residues on ALKBH1 when these primary sites are mutated. In addition, the structural details of arsenic binding before and after the C→A mutations remain to be explored. Furthermore, our study demonstrates the critical role of ALKBH1-mediated demethylation of m6A in mRNA within the arsenic stress response. As ALKBH1 has broad substrate specificity, the role of ALKBH1 in regulating DNA demethylation,14,15 tRNA modifications10,17, and histone modifications9 remains to be investigated within the arsenic damage response.

EXPERIMENTAL MODELS AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Human skin and tumor samples

All human specimens were studied after approval by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board. No human subjects were used in this study. Normal human skin samples were obtained from the archives in the tissue bank of the Section of Dermatology (Department of Medicine, University of Chicago). SCC samples were purchased from US Biomax and were prepared as previously described.54

Cell culture

HaCaT (human keratinocyte, kindly provided by Dr. Fusenig), A431 (ATCC, CRL-1555), BEAS-2B (ATCC, CRL-3588), and HEK-293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11965118) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10–437-028), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen, 15140122). Chronic As and As-T cells were developed as described previously.6 As-T and As-Tr cells were derived from Chronic As cells as described previously.6,23 As-T #1 and As-T #2 represent two distinct cell lines derived from two distinct tumors. As-Tr and As-Tr-2 represent two distinct cell lines derived from two distinct clones grown in soft agar. Cell lines were treated with Plasmocin (InvivoGen, ant-mpt) to prevent and/or eliminate mycoplasma contamination.

METHOD DETAILS

Lentiviral transfection and transduction

Briefly, lentivirus was generated by co-transfection into HEK-293T cells with lentiviral constructs together with the pCMVdeltaR8.2 packaging plasmid (Addgene, 12263) and pVSV-G envelope plasmid (Addgene,138479) using X-tremeGENE 9 (Roche, 6365787001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection into target cells was supplemented with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Santa Cruz, sc-134220). Virus-containing supernatants were collected at 24–48 h and were pooled. Pooled viral supernatants were filtered using a 45 μm filter (Millipore Sigma, SLHV033RS). shALKBH1 #1 and #2 represent the two unique shRNAs targeting ALKBH1.

Plasmids

Lentiviral vectors expressing shRNA targeting human negative control (shNC) and human ALKBH1 (shALKBH1) were purchased from Millipore Sigma. pLenti-CMV-GFP-2A-Puro-Blank Vector (LV590) and ALKBH1-HA (LV073172) were purchased from AbmGood. NR2C2 over-expressing vectors were purchased from Origene (RC219184).

CRISPR-Cas9–mediated gene knockout using the Alt-R™ system

Knockout of NR2C2 was carried out using the Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 system (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT). Two crRNAs targeting NR2C2 were synthesized by IDT, along with the universal trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA). Each crRNA and the tracrRNA were resuspended in nuclease-free water at a concentration of 100 μM, mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio, and annealed by heating at 95°C for 5 min followed by gradual cooling to room temperature to generate a 10 μM crRNA:tracrRNA duplex. Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes were formed by incubating the crRNA:tracrRNA duplex with Alt-R Cas9 Nuclease (IDT) at room temperature for 15 min. The resulting RNP complexes were then delivered into cells via electroporation using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector System, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Site-directed mutagenesis

C→A mutations were generated using the QuikChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent, 210519) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

siRNA transfection

Cells were transfected with siRNA targeting negative control (Dharmacon, D-001810–03-20), an individual gene, or the combination of METTL3 (Dharmacon, L-005170–02-0005), METTL14 (Dharmacon, L-014169–02-0005), YTHDF1 (Dharmacon, L-018095–02-0005), YTHDF2 (Dharmacon, L-021009–02-0005), YTHDF3 (Dharmacon, L-017080–01-0005), NR2C2 (Dharmacon, L-003418–00-0005), using PepMute siRNA Transfection Reagent (SignaGen, SL100566) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and as previously described.6

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 74136), following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix for PCR (Bio-Rad, 1708841), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR assays were performed using a CFX Connect real-time system (Bio-Rad) with Bio-Rad iTaQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, 1725124). The threshold cycle number (CQ) for each sample was analyzed in triplicate as previously described.6 The CQ values were normalized against GAPDH. Primers are shown in Table S1.

m6A dot blot assay

mRNA was isolated using the Dynabeads mRNA DIRECT Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 610.11) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were subjected to the protocol twice to eliminate rRNA contamination. Samples were prepared and blotted as previously described.6 In brief, mRNA was boiled for 5 min at 90°C and immediately placed on ice. Equal amounts of sample were loaded on to a Cytiva Amersham Hybond-N + Membrane (GE Healthcare, 45–000-763) and air-dried for 15 min. Samples were then crosslinked 3x with UV and blocked with 5% BSA in 1X TBST for 1 h at RT. Membranes were incubated with an m6A-antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202003) 1:3,000 overnight at 4°C. Membranes were then washed 3x with 1X TBST and incubated with Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody, HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 31460) for 1 h at RT, then washed again 3x with 1X TBST. Membranes were visualized using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34580) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Gene-specific m6A-qPCR

Gene-specific m6A-IP-qPCR was conducted as previously described.6,54 Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74136) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For input samples and m6A immunoprecipitation (IP) samples, RNA was diluted in IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 10 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 100 U RNase inhibitor) containing the m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202003). The mixture was incubated and rotated at 4°C for 2 h. Samples were then incubated with BSA-coated Dynabeads Protein A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10008D) for an additional 2 h at 4°C with rotation. After samples were washed with IP buffer, RNA was eluted using the elution buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 0.05% SDS, and 4.2 μL Proteinase K [20 mg/mL]). The eluted RNA was then concentrated with the RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 Kit (Zymo Research, R1014). Equal amounts of concentrated IP RNA and input RNA from each sample were used to construct the cDNA library. Relative m6A levels were calculated by normalizing m6A-IP levels to the corresponding gene expression (input) levels.

RNA immunoprecipitation

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) studies were performed as previously described using the EZ-Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore Sigma, 17–701).55 As-Tr cells were lysed using the RIP lysis buffer. YTHDF1 antibody and a control IgG antibody (Cell Signaling Technologies, 2729) were conjugated to Pierce Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88802) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with rotation; the beads were washed 2x with RIP washing buffer and incubated with the RIP lysate at 4°C overnight with rotation. The next day, the beads were washed 6x with the RIP washing buffer and the bound protein was digested at 55°C for 30 min while shaking. Input and co-immunoprecipitated RNAs were isolated using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596018) and analyzed by qPCR.

Immunoblotting

Protein extracts were obtained by washing cells once with cold 1X PBS and resuspending in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PI89901) supplemented with Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78442). Samples were kept on ice and sonicated 3x. Samples were then spun down at 13,500 RPM for 15 min at 4°C and the supernatant was kept. Protein concentrations were determined by Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After quantification, samples were diluted with RIPA lysis buffer, NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (4X) (Invitrogen, NP0007), and DTT. Samples were boiled for 15 min at 70°C. SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by immunoblotting was performed to examine protein abundance. Samples were blocked with 5% milk in TBST for 1 h at RT. All antibodies were diluted in 5% milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4°C with shaking. Blots were washed 3x with 1X TBST and incubated with Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody, HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 31460) or Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody, HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 31430) for 1 h at RT. Blots were washed again 3x with 1X TBST. Blots were visualized using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34580) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Protein translation assay using SUnSET

The SUnSET assay was performed as previously described.46 Puromycin (Invivogen, ant-pr-1) was diluted 1:1,000 and added 1:100 to cell culture media and then incubated for 30 min at 37°C and 5% CO2 to pulse. Cells were lysed and immunoblotting was performed using an anti-Puromycin antibody.

Protein purification

FLAG-tagged ALKBH1 was inserted into pCMV6 vector using the Gibson Assembly Kit (NEB, E5510S), following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 50 ng vector fragment and 200 ng ALKBH1 were prepared and incubated together at 50°C for 15 min. After adding the Gibson master mix, 5 μL reaction buffer was transferred into One Shot Stbl3 competent cells (Invitrogen, C737303) and incubated for 30 min on ice. Competent cells were heat-shocked for 45 s at 42°C and placed on ice for 2 min 0.5 mL of S.O.C. medium was added to competent cells and the transformation mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h with shaking at 190 RPM. The transformation mixture was placed onto agar plates containing Kanamycin and incubated overnight. All vector constructions were validated by DNA sequencing at the University of Chicago DNA Sequencing Facility (RRID: SCR_025314).

The pCMV6-ALKBH1-FLAG vector was transfected into HEK-293T cells. After being cultured for 24 h, 2×108 cells were harvested and sonicated in buffer (50 mm Tris-HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1%, 1 mM DTT and protease inhibitors). Samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000 × g. 1 mL of the supernatant was added to a fresh tube. 200 μL of Anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads (Millipore Sigma, M8823) were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Beads and samples were incubated for 4 h at 4°C, washed 3x with 1 mL buffer (50 mm Tris-HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 1 mM DTT and protease inhibitors), and eluted with 300 μL elution buffer (50 mm Tris-HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mM DTT and 100 μg 3X FLAG Peptide (Millipore Sigma, F4799–4MG) for 2 h at 4°C. The eluate was collected and dialyzed (Millipore Sigma, D6191) in buffer (50 mm Tris-HCl, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mM DTT) overnight. Sample volumes were reduced using an Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit (Millipore Sigma, UFC905008).

In vitro demethylation assay

Commercially-available purified ALKBH1 (Origene, TP306529), or in-house purified protein, as described above, was incubated in the presence or absence of arsenic for 2 h at RT. Purified proteins were then incubated with 100 ng of an m6A-probe (5′-CGUGG (m6A)CUGGCU-3′) (IDT).26 Reactions conditions used were as described previously.10 Samples were incubated for 1 h at 37°C and centrifuged for 30 min at 13,000 × g. The supernatant was then prepared for m6A dot blot analyses as described above.

UHPLC-MS/MS QQQ analysis

mRNA was isolated using the Dynabeads mRNA DIRECT Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 610.11) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. UHPLC-MS/MS was performed as previously described.54,56 UHPLC-MS/MS was run at the University of Chicago Mass Spectrometry Facility.

Molecular docking

The murine ALKBH1 protein was retrieved from the Protein DataBank (PDB ID 6KSF). Docking was performed using AutoDock.57

DARTS assay

Samples were incubated with water or arsenic for 1 h at RT and were then digested with pronase (Millipore Sigma, 10165921001), as previously described.27 After digestion, samples underwent SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by immunoblotting.

Immunoprecipitation with arsenic-biotin conjugate

For in vivo studies, HaCaT cells were incubated with 10 μM of the arsenic-biotin conjugate (Toronto Research Chemicals, TRC-B394970) or biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 29129) control for 2 h at 37°C. Cells were then washed 1x with 1X PBS and lysed using a lysis buffer (8 M Urea, 50 mM Tris-EDTA (pH 7.5), and 150 mM NaCl). Samples were kept on ice and sonicated 3x. Samples were then spun down at 13,500 RPM for 15 min at 4°C and the supernatant was kept. For in vivo studies, 500 ng of recombinant WT, C118A, or C207A protein were incubated with 10 μM of the arsenic-biotin conjugate (Toronto Research Chemicals, TRC-B394970) or biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 29129) control for 2 h at RT. For both in vitro and in vivo studies, 50 μL of Streptavidin beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88816) were washed 1x with 1X PBS and arsenic-biotin/biotin-containing samples were added to the beads and rotated overnight at 4°C. Samples were then washed 5x with lysis buffer and eluted using NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (4X) (Invitrogen, NP0007), and DTT. Samples were boiled for 15 min at 70°C. SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by immunoblotting was performed to measure protein abundance. Mouse Anti-rabbit IgG (Conformation Specific) (L27A9) mAb (HRP Conjugate) was used for secondary antibody staining (Cell Signaling, 5127S).

Mouse tumorigenesis in immunocompromised mice

Cell proliferation assay

CLIP-qPCR

Cells were harvested, lysed in NP-40 Surfact-Amps (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 85124), and digested using RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0541). m6A-IP-sequencing

100–150 μg total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596018) following the manufacturer’s protocol. mRNA was purified using a Dynabeads mRNA DIRECT Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 61012). 1 μg of mRNA was fragmented to ∼200 nt using the NEBNext Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEB, E6150S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 5% of the fragmented mRNA was saved as input. mRNA fragments containing m6A were enriched using the EpiMark N6-Methyla-denosine Enrichment Kit (NEB, E1610S) and purified using the RNA Clean and Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research, R1014). RNA libraries were prepared for both input and IP samples using TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep (Illumina, 20020594) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the University of Chicago Functional Genomics Facility (RRID:SCR_019196) on an Illumina NovaSeqX machine in paired-end mode with 50 bp per reading at around 25 M–30 M clusters/sample.

Sequencing data analysis

Sequencing data analysis was performed at the University of Chicago Bioinformatics Facility (RRID: SCR_022937). Raw fastq data of different lanes for the same sample were first merged for R1 and R2, respectively. Trim Galore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) was used to trim the adapters. FastQC (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC) was used for quality control. The trimmed reads were processed by using the easym6A pipeline (https://github.com/shunliubio/easym6A) to generate bam and bigwig files. The bam files were then used as input for RADAR (https://github.com/scottzijiezhang/RADAR) for differential methylation analysis. m6A-IP and RNA-sequencing data are deposited onto the Gene Expression Omnibus repository and is available at the following accession number: GSE285900.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were carried out using Prism 10 (GraphPad). All data are displayed as a means of at least three independent experiments and error bars indicate the SD of the means unless indicated otherwise. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
