Refractory cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia as initial presentation of gastric B cell lymphoma: a case report
Authors: Gayathri Fernando, S. A. P. N. Samarasingha, Harindra Karunatilake, Nandana Dikmadugoda, Visaka Ratnamalala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCold agglutinin disease is one of main two types of cold-antibody autoimmune hemolytic anemias, which can be either primary or, commonly, secondary to an infection or underlying lymphoproliferative disease [1]. We report a rare case of refractory cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia as an initial presentation of gastric B cell lymphoma.Case presentationA 53-year-old Sri Lankan woman with diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and hyperthyroidism presented with increased fatigue and shortness of breath on mild exertion for 1 month. She reported yellowing of eyes and passage of dark urine. Examination findings revealed pallor, icterus, and moderate splenomegaly with no hepatomegaly or lymphadenopathy. She was found to have hemoglobin of 8.3 g/dl with mean corpuscular volume 90.8 fL and mean corpuscular hemoglobin 30.4 pg. Elevated reticulocyte count (8%), lactate dehydrogenase (357 U/L), and indirect hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin 1.2 mg/dl and indirect bilirubin 0.6 mg/dl) was suggestive of hemolysis. The presence of red cell autoagglutination in the peripheral blood smear with direct antibody testing positivity for C3d (3+) specificity confirmed cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Her initial contrast enhanced computed tomography of the chest, abdomen and pelvis failed to show any enlarged lymph nodes or underlying malignancy, and a bone marrow biopsy only showed moderate erythroid hyperplasia. She had poor response to intravenous methylprednisolone pulses, mycophenolate mofetil, and intravenous rituximab. In view of refractory hemolysis, she underwent a repeat imaging, which showed a heterogeneous mass lesion arising from the greater curvature of the stomach with regional lymph node involvement. Gastroscopy revealed a submucosal mass arising from the gastric fundus with a smooth surface. Endoscopic ultrasound guided biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of high-grade B cell gastric lymphoma, which was identified as the culprit for refractory cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia.ConclusionThis case report highlights that refractory cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia can be an initial presentation of gastric B cell lymphoma. This also emphasizes the importance of reassessments and reimaging to exclude underlying secondary causes in cases of refractory cold autoimmune hemolytic anemias.

Background

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia is caused by an increased destruction of red blood cells due to the presence of autoantibodies. It is subdivided into warm, cold, and mixed/atypical types [2]. Cold agglutinin disease is one of main two types of cold-antibody autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHAs). Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) can be either primary or mainly secondary to an infection or underlying lymphoproliferative disease [1]. Especially in chronic refractory AIHA, underlying lymphoproliferative disease should be considered and excluded. We report a case of gastric B cell lymphoma that presented initially as refractory cold autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

Case report

A 53-year-old Sri Lankan woman presented to a tertiary care center in Sri Lanka with fever and productive cough for 5 days in the background of hyperthyroidism treated with carbimazole for 3 months duration. She also complained of increased fatigue, shortness of breath on mild exertion, and palpitations for the past month. She noted passing dark urine and yellowing of her eyes recently. She did not have a history of recurrent infections, easy bruising, or mucosal bleeding. She denied loss of appetite or loss of weight. She did not have alopecia, skin rashes, photosensitivity, inflammatory type joint pain, Raynaud’s phenomenon, or sicca symptoms.

Apart from hyperthyroidism, she was on treatment for diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia. Her family history is insignificant. She is a nonsmoker and teetotaler. She denied high-risk sexual behavior.

On examination, her body mass index (BMI) was 23.9 kg/m2. She was pale and icteric. There was no lymphadenopathy. She did not have skin rashes or joint deformities. An abdominal examination revealed a moderate splenomegaly with no hepatomegaly. Other system examinations were unremarkable.

On admission, her white cell count (WCC) was 3.84 × 103/µL (4.0–10.0 × 103/µL), with neutrophils 1.53 × 103/µL (2.0–7.0 × 103/µL), and lymphocytes 1.79 × 103/µL (1.0–3.0 × 103/µL). Platelets were 212 × 103/µL (150–410 × 103/µL). Her hemoglobin was 8.3 g/dL (12.0–15.0 g/dL) with mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 90.8 fL (83.0–99.0 fL) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) 30.4 pg (27.0–32.0 pg). Absolute reticulocyte count was 0.22 × 106/µL, which was 8% (0.5–2.5%). Total bilirubin was 1.2 mg/dl (0.1–1.2 mg/dL) with an indirect fraction of 0.6 mg/dL (0.1–0.8 mg/dL). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 357 U/L (135–250 U/L). Peripheral blood smear revealed severe normochromic normocytic anemia with autoagglutination and few spherocytes. Direct antibody testing was negative for anti-immunoglobulin (Ig)G and positive for C3d (3+) specificity at thermal amplitudes of 4 °C, 10 °C, 22 °C and 37 °C, confirming the cold AIHA.

Serology for mycoplasma IgM and IgG were negative. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)1 and 2 were negative. Antinuclear antibody showed 1:160 positivity in nuclear pattern but negative for anti-double-stranded DNA antibody. However, she did not meet the criteria for the diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Screening for primary immunodeficiencies could not be carried out owing to a lack of resources in our setting. Her initial contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed a hypoattenuating focal lesion in the upper pole of the moderately enlarged spleen, which is suggestive of an area of extra medullary hemopoiesis, but failed to show any enlarged lymph nodes.

For ongoing hemolysis patient was initially started on intravenous methylprednisolone pulses for 2 days followed with oral prednisolone with mycophenolate mofetil. However, as the response was poor, four doses of intravenous rituximab were given 1 week apart, and yet, hemolysis continued; she was transfusion dependent.

In view of refractory hemolysis, she underwent a repeat CECT, which showed a heterogeneous mass lesion arising from the greater curvature of the stomach with regional lymph node involvement. Gastroscopy revealed a submucosal mass arising from the gastric fundus with a smooth surface.

A bone marrow biopsy showed moderately hypercellular marrow with moderate erythroid hyperplasia compatible with AIHA, and there was no evidence of malignant infiltration of the marrow. Peripheral flow cytometry showed cluster of differentiation (CD)45-gated lymphoid cells (13%) with strong kappa-restricted CD5-negative, CD10-negative clonal B cell proliferation.

Endoscopic ultrasound guided biopsy revealed a diffuse population of medium-to-large lymphocytes with hyperchromatic nuclei with scanty cytoplasm, which showed diffuse membrane positivity for CD20 with a Ki67 proliferation index of 85%, confirming a diagnosis of high-grade B cell lymphoma. According to the CECT and bone marrow biopsy findings, the Lugano classification at the time of diagnosis was stage I.

The patient was referred to the oncology unit and started on the R-CHOP chemotherapy regimen (intravenous rituximab 600 mg day 1 (D1), cyclophosphamide 1200 mg D1, intravenous doxorubicin 80 mg D1, vincristine 2 mg D1, and prednisolone 50 mg twice daily for D1–5). With that, her hemoglobin level increased to 8.5 g/dL following the first chemotherapy cycle. On 5 months follow-up, at the end of six cycles of R-CHOP, the patient had a hemoglobin level of 10.2 g/dL, and repeat gastroscopy showed the persistence of the submucosal mass in the gastric fundus. Posttreatment whole body positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) showed marked reduction in the size of the previously noted mass in the gastric fundus, and it was minimally 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avid with a Deauveille score of 4. The size of the previously noted regional lymph node was also significantly reduced and was non-FDG avid. There was no evidence of other hypermetabolic lesions. Therefore, the patient showed partial metabolic and partial morphological response to chemotherapy and was given three more cycles of R-CHOP and radiotherapy. Discussion and conclusion

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia is caused by an increased destruction of red blood cells due to presence of autoantibodies. It has an incidence of 1–3 per 100,000 per year. AIHA is subdivided in to warm, cold, and mixed/atypical depending on the effects of autoantibodies, their immunoglobulin class (IgG, IgM, and IgA), thermal amplitude and the extent of antibody- mediated complement activation. Warm AIHA is mostly mediated by IgG. IgG bound red cells are identified by IgG-Fc receptors on macrophages and destructed in the spleen. Some IgG antibodies may also cause complement activation [2].

There are two main types of cold-antibody AIHAs: CAD and paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria. CAD has a incidence of 1 per 1,000,000 per year [3]. The definition of CAD is not consistent. It is mostly defined as chronic hemolysis with monospecific strong DAT positivity for C3d with a cold agglutinin titer of ≥ 64 at 4 °C [4]. In CAD, IgM is the main immunoglobulin involved. However, it can rarely be mediated by IgG, IgA, or light-chain restriction. At core body temperature, IgM is unbound to red blood cells (RBCs), whereas towards the peripheries, IgM transiently binds to RBCs at a much lower temperature. This reaction can overcome the natural repulsive forces between cells and causes red cell agglutination. Once IgM is bound to RBCs, a complement cascade is activated, and C3b gets bound to the RBC surface. C3b-coated RBCs are recognized by receptor-specific macrophages at liver and spleen leading to extravascular hemolysis. Some degree of intravascular hemolysis also occurs. Depending on the thermal amplitude, the severity of hemolysis varies. Once C3b-coated RBCs return to the body core temperature, IgM dissociates. This finding of agglutination without antiglobulin antisera in microtiter wells at 4 °C is termed as “cold agglutination” [3].

CAD can be either primary or secondary. Secondary CAD can be mainly due to infections (for example, mycoplasmal pneumonia, infectious mononucleosis, adenovirus, cytomegalovirus, influenza viruses, varicella zoster virus, human immunodeficiency virus, Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, and Treponema pallidum) or underlying lymphoproliferative diseases. In the index case, screening for infection was negative, and she failed to fulfill diagnostic criteria for SLE. CAD due to infections are usually transient, and a treatment-refractory chronic course would suggest an underlying lymphoproliferative disorder (for example, chronic lymphocytic leukaemias, lymphomas, and Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia), as in our patient. Solid organ malignancies such as pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma, metastatic colonic adenocarcinoma, metastatic adrenal adenocarcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, and mixed parotid tumor are also rarely associated with CAD [1].

Gastric lymphomas represent 5% of all gastric neoplasms. Most of them are either mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphomas and diffuse large B cell lymphomas (DLBCL), with a rare occurrence of mantle cell and follicular lymphomas [5]. Commonly, these patients present with abdominal pain, early satiety, nausea, vomiting, and indigestion. B symptoms such as fever, night sweats, and weight loss are rare. Patients could present with iron deficiency anemia due to occult bleeding or acute blood loss due to hematemesis, hematochezia, or melena [6]. Kotera et al. reported a MALT lymphoma in a 68-year-old woman with rare association of AIHA [7]. A patient with cold AIHA secondary to DLBCL in terminal ileum was reported in 2011 [8]. A thorough literature search failed to reveal a case in which AIHA was the initial presentation of gastric B cell lymphoma.

The association of AIHA with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) is well known. Patients with NHL who do not develop AIHA have better overall survival than those who develop AIHA [9].

In this patient, the initial imaging may have missed the evolving lymphoma, or treatment with steroids may have masked the lymphoma. Therefore, in refractory cases of hemolytic anemia, it is important to be vigilant to do reassessments and reimaging to exclude underlying secondary causes. This case report also highlights that refractory cold AIHA can be an initial presentation of gastric B cell lymphoma.
