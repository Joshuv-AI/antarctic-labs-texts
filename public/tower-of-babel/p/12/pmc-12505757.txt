The effect of different treatment methods on apical closure and treatment success in immature permanent first molars with reversible pulpitis
Authors: Muhammed ALAGOZ, Sera SIMSEK DERELIOĞLU
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to evaluate the effects of direct pulp capping and coronal pulpotomy treatments on root development (apexogenesis) and treatment success in immature mandibular first permanent molars with symptoms of reversible pulpitis.MethodsThe study included 72 immature mandibular first molars from 60 children aged between 7 and 10 years who applied to the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, Ataturk University. Pulpotomy was performed on 34 teeth and direct pulp capping on 38 teeth. In all cases, a single biomaterial (ProRoot MTA) was used, and treatments were completed in a single visit. Clinical and radiographic follow-ups were performed at 3-, 6-, 12-, and 18-month. Root development was assessed using the Moorrees classification.ResultsAsymptomatic clinical progress and regular root development were observed in both treatment groups. More than 90% of the treated teeth were successfully followed up to 18 months, and root development progressed consistently according to the Moorrees classification. No statistically significant difference was found between direct pulp capping and pulpotomy in terms of clinical or radiographic success (p >0.05).ConclusionThe data obtained indicate that in immature permanent teeth with reversible pulpitis, both direct pulp capping and pulpotomy treatments can provide similar clinical, radiographic, and physiological outcomes.Trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov) with the registration ID NCT06575062 (2024-08-28).Keywords: Direct pulp capping, Coronal pulpotomy, Immature permanent teeth, Apexogenesis, Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), Vital pulp therapy, Root development, Reversible pulpitis

Introduction

In immature permanent teeth, anatomical features such as a large pulp chamber, thin dentin walls, and an open apex make them more susceptible to early inflammatory responses following deep dentin caries. Bacterial toxins from carious lesions can penetrate dentinal tubules and initiate inflammation in the pulp even before bacteria physically reach the pulp chamber [1]. This phenomenon, termed “functional exposure” may lead to irreversible pulp inflammation [2, 3]. These anatomical characteristics, particularly in immature teeth, facilitate the early activation of pulp defense mechanisms [4].

The wide apical foramen, rich blood supply, and high cellular content of the pulp in young permanent teeth enhance their defense and reparative capacity, which can increase treatment success in immature teeth [5–7]. For immature teeth with deep dentin caries but only signs of reversible pulp inflammation, direct pulp capping and coronal pulpotomy are recommended as minimally invasive treatment options that preserve the biological integrity of the pulp-dentin complex [1, 8]. These approaches aim to maintain tissue vitality by preserving the pulp’s proprioceptive and immunological defense and its nociceptive response to dentin sensitivity [1, 9]. Keeping the radicular pulp vital allows uninterrupted physiological apexogenesis in teeth with incomplete root development and is a more biological and advantageous alternative compared to traditional calcium hydroxide apexification [10]. Additionally, these treatments have been shown to be more cost-effective than root canal treatment [11].

Direct pulp capping is one of the most conservative vital pulp therapies (VPTs) and is preferred for teeth with limited pulp injury and reversible pulpitis. The aim is to promote healing by covering the exposed pulp with a biocompatible material, and long-term success rates have notably increased with the use of calcium silicate-based materials such as MTA and Biodentine, especially in cases with small exposures and controlled bleeding controlled [9, 12]. Pulpotomy similarly preserves pulp vitality and enables long-term tooth function. Maintaining pulp vitality in immature teeth supports apexogenesis, allowing continued root development and retention of the tooth [13].

MTA has been proven effective and is widely used in clinical practice for VPTs such as pulpotomy and direct or indirect pulp capping [14–16]. ProRoot MTA, in particular, supports pulp healing by promoting dentin bridge formation and stands out for its high biocompatibility, strong sealing ability, and long-term biological stability [17].

The clinical diagnosis of reversible pulpitis is typically symptom-based, with short-term, stimulus-dependent pain being an important indicator [18]. Moreover, the success of pulpotomy in immature teeth depends on accurate diagnosis, age, apical patency, appropriate material selection, and regular follow-up [19]. A retrospective clinical study [20] in children reported a 93.1% success rate for pulpotomy in immature first permanent molars over approximately 2.5- year of follow-up.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the 3-, 6-, 12-, and 18-month clinical and radiographic success of direct pulp capping and pulpotomy procedures performed with MTA in immature mandibular first molars with symptoms of reversible pulpitis, as well as to assess the effects of these treatments on maturogenesis (root development and apical closure). The null hypothesis was that there is no significant difference between the two treatment modalities in terms of treatment success and apical closure.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This prospective clinical study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ataturk University Faculty of Medicine. Participation was voluntary, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and data were collected confidentially. Participants were enrolled between June and December 2023.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.2 software. Assuming an effect size of 0.40, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80, the minimum number of participants was determined to be 60. A 20% dropout rate was anticipated and accounted for in this calculation to ensure adequate statistical validity.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were

patients aged between 7- and 10-year, presenting symptoms of reversible pulpitis, without spontaneous or prolonged throbbing pain, and possessing a mandibular permanent molar tooth at the R¾ or Rc stage of root development according to the Moorrees classification.

Exclusion criteria included

patients with systemic diseases; clinical signs of pulp necrosis; percussion/palpation sensitivity; radiographic evidence of root resorption, periodontal ligament space widening, or radiolucency; extensive substance loss or MIH; and observable pulp hyperemia/inflammation.

In the allocation of study groups, cases diagnosed with reversible pulpitis based on clinical and radiographic findings were assigned according to the size of the pulp exposure during caries removal: cases with pulp exposures greater than 2 mm were included in the pulpotomy group, while those with exposures smaller than 2 mm were included in the direct pulp capping group.

Clinical procedures

Following clinical and radiographic examination in our study, the mucosal surface to be anesthetized was air-dried, then topical anesthesia was applied (Locanest 10% spray, Avixa, Istanbul, Türkiye), followed by mandibular anesthesia using a local anesthetic agent containing a vasoconstrictor (Ultracain D-S Ampoule, Aventis, Istanbul, Türkiye). The treated teeth were isolated with a rubber dam for better visibility. The cavity was accessed with a diamond round bur (ISO 018, Diatech, Coltene, Altstätten/Switzerland), carious tissues were removed using steel round burs (ISO 021–023, NTI-Kahla, Germany), and the cavity was cleaned with cotton pellets soaked in 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl). The treatment approach was determined based on the diameter of pulp exposure.

The direct pulp capping procedure was applied only in teeth that met the following clinical criteria: (1) the dentin surrounding the exposure site was sound and stable, (2) the exposed pulp tissue appeared vital, red, homogeneous, and healthy, with no necrotic or liquefaction zones, (3) no dislodged dentin particles were present on the exposure site, and (4) effective hemostasis was achieved within 2–3 min using a sterile cotton pellet soaked with 1% NaOCl [21].

In cases meeting these conditions and with an exposure diameter smaller than 2 mm, MTA (ProRoot MTA, Dentsply, Tulsa, USA) was placed over the exposure area, covered with RMGIC (Ionoseal, Voco, Cuxhaven, Germany). The cavity walls were etched with orthophosphoric acid (Vococid, Voco, Cuxhaven, Germany), a bonding agent (Futurabond-U, Voco, Cuxhaven, Germany) was applied, and the restoration was completed with a composite resin (Solare-X, GC, Tokyo, Japan) using the incremental layering technique.

In cases not meeting the direct pulp capping criteria or with an exposure diameter greater than 2 mm, pulpotomy was performed. The roof of the pulp chamber was removed with a bur, and coronal pulp tissue was amputated to the canal orifices using a spoon excavator. Hemostasis was achieved after 3–5 min using a cotton pellet soaked in sterile saline; otherwise, regenerative treatment was initiated. In cases where hemostasis was achieved, the cavity was disinfected with 1% NaOCl, MTA was placed on the pulpal floor, covered with RMGIC, and the restorative procedure was completed.

Evaluation and follow-up

Evaluations were conducted at baseline and during the follow-up months (3rd, 6th, 12th, and 18th). Clinical findings were recorded on patient follow-up forms. Radiographs were taken under standardized conditions using a periapical film holder (Super-Bite, Kerr Corporation, Switzerland). Radiographic assessments were performed independently in a dark environment by two observers who had reviewed 15 separate radiographs for calibration. These evaluations were repeated 2-week later. In cases of discrepancies between findings, a final decision was reached by consensus. Inter-observer agreement was analyzed using the Cohen’s Kappa statistic and found to be 0.757.

Criteria for clinical success included: the treated tooth remaining functional in the oral cavity; absence of any pathological symptoms (e.g., spontaneous pain, fistula, and swelling); no tenderness on percussion or palpation; and mobility within physiological limits. Criteria for clinical failure included: spontaneous, prolonged, or throbbing pain; sensitivity to percussion/palpation; presence of fistula or swelling; or abnormal mobility. Criteria for radiographic failure included: widening of the periodontal ligament space; interradicular or periradicular radiolucency; and internal or external root resorption.

Root development was evaluated according to the Moorrees classification (R¾, Rc, A½, Ac) (Fig. 1), and dentin bridge formation was determined by the presence of a radiopaque line beneath the pulp capping material [22, 23]. The presence of dentin bridge formation was evaluated radiographically, and representative radiographs from selected cases were included to illustrate the treatment outcomes.

Patients who missed an intermediate follow-up visit were not excluded from the study, as treatment success was evaluated separately for each time point. For those who could not attend certain radiographic controls, symptom information was obtained by phone and radiographic examinations were performed at subsequent visits. This approach was adopted to ensure transparency and to minimize data loss without affecting the validity of the results.

Statistical analysis

For the analysis of relationships between categorical variables, Pearson’s chi-square test was used when the sample size assumption (expected value > 5) was met; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was applied. For the analysis of relationships between dependent categorical variables, the marginal homogeneity test was conducted. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27.

Results

In this study, VPT was performed on 72 immature mandibular first permanent molars from 60 patients, with 38 teeth (52.8%) undergoing direct pulp capping and 34 teeth (47.2%) receiving coronal pulpotomy. Statistical analysis revealed that the mean age in the direct pulp capping group (8.96 ± 0.80 years; range: 7.76-10.73) was significantly higher than in the pulpotomy group (8.54 ± 0.81 years; range: 6.97-10.64) (p<0.05). The proportion of female patients was significantly higher in the direct pulp capping group (73.7%), while the pulpotomy group had a greater proportion of male patients (52.9%). At baseline, all 72 teeth were included in the study, with follow-up evaluations completed for 70 teeth (97.2%) at 3-month, 65 teeth (90.3%) at 6-month, and 66 teeth (93.1%) at both the 12th- and 18th- month. The root development stages of the treated teeth were evaluated according to the Moorrees classification at baseline and during follow-ups. Statistical comparisons between treatment groups at each time point showed no significant differences in root development levels (p>0.05) (Table 1).

In this study, the effects of different VPTs (direct pulp capping and pulpotomy) on root development levels were evaluated over time, and the distributions of Moorrees scores at baseline and follow-up periods were analyzed for both treatment protocols. The analyses revealed statistically significant differences between the initial and follow-up root development stages at the 3rd, 6th, 12th, and 18th-month in both the direct pulp capping and pulpotomy groups (p < 0.05). In particular, for teeth treated at the Rc stage, substantial progression to the A½ and Ac stages was observed by the 12th- and 18th-month. In contrast, teeth that began treatment at the R¾ stage showed a slower rate of development. These findings indicate that the rate of root development is influenced more by the initial root development stage at the time of treatment rather than by the type of treatment protocol applied (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

VPT includes indirect/direct pulp capping and pulpotomy to preserve pulp vitality in teeth with deep caries. While direct observation of the pulp is possible, clinical appearance alone does not reliably indicate histological status. Therefore, clinical indicators such as exposure size, dentin condition, and bleeding characteristics are essential for decision-making [21, 24, 25]. Leong and Yap [25] reported that in carious pulp exposures, pulpotomy provides more predictable outcomes than direct pulp capping. Similarly, Jakovljevic et al. [26] emphasized the critical role of clinical assessment in determining treatment success, suggesting that direct pulp capping may be appropriate when inflammation is limited, while pulpotomy remains a safer alternative in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.

In our study, only immature permanent molars exhibiting clinical signs of reversible pulpitis were included. This strict case selection helped to standardize the inflammatory status of the pulp across the study population, thereby allowing for a more objective and methodologically reliable analysis of the relationship between the applied treatment protocols and the resulting clinical and biological outcomes. Furthermore, this approach aligns with the clinical guidelines of the European Society of Endodontology (ESE), which prioritize the preservation of pulp vitality [19].

Preserving pulp vitality in immature permanent teeth is critical for ongoing root development and apical closure, leveraging the high regenerative capacity of young pulp tissue. VPT provides a biologically driven alternative to traditional endodontics, supporting root development even in technically challenging, immature teeth [1, 20, 27]. The favorable results obtained in this study highlight the influence of developmental stage and proper case selection, in addition to treatment choice.

Throughout the 3-, 6-, 12-, and 18-month follow-ups, no significant difference in clinical symptoms or root development was found between the 2 treatment groups. Our findings show that root development in immature teeth depends more on the initial developmental stage of the tooth than on the type of VPT performed. Therefore, individual tooth maturity should be prioritized in treatment planning.

Single-visit VPT with immediate restoration using MTA is effective and consistent with the literature [28–31]. This protocol is especially beneficial in pediatric patients and ensures biomaterial stability and cooperation.

Most cases remained asymptomatic, and all maintained continued root development and apical narrowing. Dentin bridge formation, a key marker of biological healing, exceeded 90% in both groups by 12–18 months, consistent with the literature on MTA and Biodentine [23, 32]. The absence of early radiographic evidence of bridge formation was not considered a failure, as radiographs may not fully capture mineralized tissue formation and artifacts can interfere [33, 34].

Percussion sensitivity was rare and, when present, did not affect treatment success or root development. This finding agrees with previous reports suggesting that such findings in isolation are not pathognomonic [35, 36]. Multifactorial assessment is therefore recommended for accurate interpretation.

Our findings are also consistent with the review by Leong and Yap [25], who concluded that pulpotomy generally provides more predictable outcomes in carious pulp exposures, although direct pulp capping may also be successful in carefully selected cases. Similarly, Jakovljevic et al. [26] emphasized the importance of clinical assessment for treatment success, which is in line with our strict case selection criteria and may explain the favorable results obtained in both groups. The high dentin bridge formation rates observed in our study also corroborate previous reports on the favorable biological responses associated with MTA and Biodentine [23, 32]. Taken together, these comparisons suggest that both pulpotomy and direct pulp capping can be reliable options for vital pulp therapy in immature permanent teeth when case selection is carefully performed.

The results of this clinical study demonstrated that both direct pulp capping and pulpotomy provided favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes in immature permanent first molars with reversible pulpitis. No significant differences were observed between the two treatment modalities in terms of treatment success or apical closure. Accordingly, the null hypothesis of the study was accepted.

Study limitations include occasional telemedicine follow-ups, inhomogeneous age distribution, and observer-dependent radiographic assessment, although calibration was performed to minimize bias. In addition, the relatively short follow-up period of 18 months does not allow for long-term conclusions regarding the prognosis of immature permanent teeth. The single-center design and limited sample size may also restrict the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, occasional missed intermediate radiographic evaluations may have introduced bias, despite subsequent follow-ups being completed. Future research should therefore involve larger, age-homogeneous samples, multicenter participation, and longer follow-up periods to enhance the validity and reliability of the results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that both direct pulp capping and coronal pulpotomy are effective VPT options for immature permanent first molars with reversible pulpitis. Both approaches resulted in high clinical and radiographic success rates, with sustained pulp vitality and continued root development throughout the follow-up period. Completing the definitive restoration in the same visit as the pulp therapy helped maintain biomaterial stability and prevented microleakage. Dentin bridge formation increased over time in both groups, and isolated percussion sensitivity during follow-up did not negatively affect root development or treatment outcomes.

Our findings highlight the importance of careful case selection and prompt restoration, and support the use of biocompatible materials in VPT for apexogenesis in immature permanent teeth. Further long-term and large-scale studies are needed to better understand the factors influencing clinical success.
