Royal jelly protein hydrolysates accelerate in vitro diabetic wound healing by enhancing keratinocytes functions and suppressing macrophage-mediated inflammation
Authors: Tianxing Lin, Lei Huang, Anqi Lin, Yuan Yuan, Rongjing Cai, Zhenyu Lin, Luying Wang, Yan Lin, Songkun Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pathophysiology of diabetic wounds is underpinned by a vicious cycle of persistent inflammation and impaired re-epithelialization, posing a substantial challenge to clinical management. Therapeutic strategies capable of simultaneously targeting these interconnected pathologies are urgently needed. Royal jelly from Castanea mollissima Bl. (CmRJ) has previously demonstrated wound healing properties, yet the activity of its protein hydrolysates against diabetic complications has not been reported. This study focused on investigating the effects and mechanisms of the protein hydrolysates from CmRJ (CmRJPH) on the in vitro diabetic wounds, particularly on the functions of keratinocyte and macrophages.MethodsCmRJPH were trypsinized and then analyzed using MALDI-TOF MS and RPLC-MS/MS. The biological activities and the relevant mode of action of CmRJPH were assessed on the advanced glycation end products (AGEs)-damaged human keratinocytes (HaCaT) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated macrophages (Raw 264.7).ResultsCmRJPH was found to not only enhance the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes, but significantly rescue the impaired migratory function of an in vitro diabetic wound model induced by AGEs. Concurrently, CmRJPH exerted potent anti-inflammatory effects on LPS activated macrophages. It markedly suppressed the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby curtailing the expression of the downstream pro-inflammatory effectors, including INOS, COX-2, IL-6, and TNF-α. Besides, CmRJPH exhibited strong antioxidant properties by scavenging DPPH and hydroxyl free radicals and reducing intracellular ROS levels in macrophages.ConclusionsThese findings suggested that CmRJPH had the potential to promote the healing of diabetic wounds by restoring the migratory capacity of impaired epithelial cells and concurrently resolving pathological inflammation. It offers new insights into the molecular mechanisms of RJ in promoting wound healing, positioning CmRJPH as a promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of intractable diabetic wounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05332-3.Keywords: Royal jelly, Hydrolysates, Anti-inflammation, Anti-oxidant, Wound healing

Introduction

Chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), are characterized by persistent inflammation, elevated oxidative stress, and impaired re-epithelialization, which contribute to delayed healing and high recurrence rates [1]. In individuals with diabetes, hyperglycemia exacerbates vascular dysfunction and neuropathy, thereby reducing the supply of oxygen and nutrients to wound sites while simultaneously suppressing immune responses and amplifying inflammatory cascades [2]. A significant contributor to these complications is the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which are formed through non-enzymatic reactions between reducing sugars and proteins, lipids, or nucleic acids [3]. AGEs disrupt normal cellular functions by cross-linking with extracellular matrix proteins, thereby increasing tissue stiffness and impairing cellular migration essential for wound repair [4]. They also interact with the receptor for AGEs (RAGE), which triggers oxidative stress and perpetuates inflammation [5]. This persistent inflammatory environment impedes the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase of healing, and oxidative stress damages extracellular matrix components and keratinocyte function, thereby further impeding re-epithelialization [6].

Food-derived peptides are typically produced through enzymatic treatment, physical extraction, chemical synthesis, recombinant expression, or microbial fermentation [7]. Enzymatic hydrolysis is widely employed in the industrial production of food-derived peptides due to its advantages over alternative techniques including high biosecurity, efficient reactions, reduced processing time, and lower costs [8, 9]. Enzymatic hydrolysis can be employed to produce peptides with diverse bioactivities and these peptides constitute the essential basis underlying the biological activity of the hydrolysates [10], including wound healing promotion, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects [11]. Trypsin is a proteolytic enzyme that specifically cleaves lysine and arginine residues (except when followed by proline), producing peptides in the range of 700-1500 Da [12]. These small peptides are ideally sized for efficient absorption by intestinal peptide transporters, such as PepT1, enabling them to be absorbed faster than intact proteins or free amino acids [13]. Several studies have utilized trypsin to obtain peptides from broccoli [14], eggplant leaf [15], quinoa [16], soy [17] and skipjack tuna dark muscles [18] for the treatment of diabetes. However, there has been no report on the activity of CmRJPH produced via trypsin digestion in the treatment of diabetes and its complications.

Royal jelly (RJ) is a nutritive substance secreted by the hypopharyngeal and mandibular glands of worker bees, which is specifically utilized to nourish the queen and young brood [19]. Recent studies have extensively demonstrated the application of RJ and its extracts in wound healing. RJ derived from C mollissima Bl. could enhance cutaneous wound healing by promoting the growth and motility of keratinocytes, modulating AQP3 expression, regulating MAPK and calcium pathways, and mediating inflammatory responses [20]. RJ enhanced the migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts through modulating NF-κB signaling pathway and suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby promoting collagen synthesis and angiogenesis [21]. Another study demonstrated that RJ-derived extracellular vesicles effectively regulated the inflammatory phase, which facilitated re-epithelialization and extracellular matrix remodeling [22]. Moreover, defensin-1 from RJ stimulated matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) secretion from epidermal keratinocytes, thereby enhancing cell migration and expediting wound closure [23]. The compounds which promote wound healing in RJ are most likely to be some proteins or peptides, while enzymatic hydrolysis technology can effectively produce active peptides and improve their bioavailability [24].

This research concentrated on the wound healing-promoting activity of enzymatic products derived from CmRJ, particularly regarding the effects on the migratory capability of keratinocytes, and the anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects on macrophages. The hydrolysis efficiency of trypsin was evaluated by MALDI-TOF MS, and the corresponding biological activities and mechanisms of action of the resultant hydrolysates were further investigated in HaCaT cells and Raw 264.7 cells. The peptide composition of CmRJPH was concurrently analyzed using RPLC-MS/MS to facilitate the identification and development of specific wound healing-promoting peptides.

Methods

Harvest of RJ samples

RJ produced by nurse bee of Apis mellifera Ligustica. was harvested during the middle blossom season of Castanea mollissima Bl. (May 1 to June 15, 2021) with permission from a private apiary of Jiangshan, Zhejiang Province (28°35’N, 118°40’E), P.R. China and stored at -20℃ in polypropylene tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) immediately after harvesting. Sample of Castanea mollissima Bl. was identified by Yan Lin and the voucher specimen was deposited in the herbarium of the College of Bee Science and Biomedicine, Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University under voucher number CmZj-20,210,630, with digital images permanently archived in the herbarium database for traceability.

Preparation of RJ protein hydrolysates

The procedures of enzymatic hydrolysis methods were carried out as previous described [25]. Briefly, 3 g RJ was dissolved in 10 ml of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 1.5% (w/v) trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for pH = 8.5 and incubated for 3-6 h at 2000 rpm and 37 °C. The reaction was terminated by incubating the mixture at 100 °C for 10 min in water bath and then cooled to 4 °C. Following filtration through 0.45 μm cellulose acetate filter disks (Agilent Technologies, Japan), the hydrolysates were desalted using a 100-500 Da molecular weight cut-off dialysis membrane (Spectrum Labs, USA) in deionized PBS for 72 h. The final product was then lyophilized (Labconco FreeZone 4.5, USA) and stored at -80 °C in airtight containers.

Protein mass fingerprinting by MALDI-TOF MS

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry was carried out on MALDI-TOF/TOF MS (Bruker, Germany) as the method of previous descripted [26]. In summary, sinapinic acid (SA) was dissolved in a 70% acetonitrile solution containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid at the concentration of 25 mg/ml as a standard MALDI matrix. 1.0 µl of matrix was spotted onto a ground steel target plate (Bruker Daltonik, Germany) and air-dried for 10-15 min at room temperature. Then 1.0 µl of sample solution dissolved in 70% acetonitrile solution (1.0 mg/ml) was spotted onto the same plate and re-dried. The MS database was obtained on MALDI-TOF/TOF MS equipped with SmartbeamTM-II laser (355 nm) at 500 Hz.

Identification peptides by RPLC-MS/MS

CmRJPH was dissolved with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and subsequently desalted utilizing ZipTip C18 pipette Tips (Millipore, USA). The RPLC-MS/MS experiment was conducted using the Thermo Scientific™ EASY-nLC™ 1200 system in conjunction with the Thermo Scientific™ Q Exactive HF Orbitrap LC-MS/MS System. LC separation was performed on Acclaim PepMap™ RSLC column (75 μm i.d. × 150 mm, nanoViper C18, 2 μm, 100 Å) [27]. 3 µl of pre-dissolved peptides in the dissolution buffer (0.1% formic acid, 5% acetonitrile (ACN)) were injected into system, with the nanoflow rate established at 400 nl min− 1. Peptides were eluted with eluent A (formic acid/water, 0.1/99.9, v/v) and eluent B (formic acid/ACN/water, 0.1/80.0/19.9, v/v/v) over 70 min with the gradient program of 0-3 min, 3% B; 3-7 min, 3%-8% B; 7-46 min, 8%-32% B; 46-51 min, 32%-44% B; 51-56 min, 44%-99% B; 56-60 min, 99% B; 60-60.1 min, 99%-3% B; 60.1-70 min, 3% B.

The mass spectrometer was configured to operate in full-scan MS data-dependent MS2 (ddMS2) mode. Full-scan spectra were obtained at a resolution of 120,000 within the m/z range of 350-1550, with an AGC target of 4e5, and a maximum ion injection time of 50 ms. From the top 20 most abundant ions, ddMS2 scans were obtained at a resolution of 30,000, with an AGC target of 1e5 and an isolation window of 2 m/z, while the dynamic exclusion list set to 10 s. The maximum injection time was 100 ms, and the normalized collision energy was 32 arbitrary units.

The peptides in the digest were identified by de novo peptide sequencing and database searching using Peaks 10 software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Canada) against the Uniport-proteome database (Apis mellifera, Castanea mollissima). Search parameters included specified enzyme: trypsin; maximum missed Cleavages per peptide: 2; precursor mass tolerance: 15 ppm; fragment mass tolerance: 0.02 Da; dynamic modifications: Oxidation(M), Acetylation(N-term).

Results

Peptides analysis of CmRJPH

Based on the MALDI-TOF MS analysis, the untreated RJ sample exhibited multiple characteristic peaks in the m/z range of 20,000-200,000, indicating a complex composition of endogenous proteins and peptides. After 3 h trypsin digestion, the high-molecular-weight peaks were markedly reduced. The original protein peaks nearly vanished when extending the digestion to 6 h (Fig. 1). It indicated that trypsin exerted a time-dependent proteolytic effect on RJ proteins, with prolonged digestion promoting more extensive degradation. CmRJPH promoted proliferation and migration of keratinocytes

The proliferation of keratinocytes is the fundamental cellular procedure involved in wound healing [29]. CmRJ was previously found to possess proliferative effects on keratinocytes [30]. Therefore, the peptides obtained from trypsin-hydrolyzed CmRJ were preliminarily accessed for their proliferative effects on HaCaT cells. As shown in Fig. 2A-B, CmRJPH significantly enhanced the proliferation of HaCaT cells at concentrations of 7.81-250 µg/ml at 48 h, and did not display any cytotoxicity at the concentration of 1000 µg/ml. In comparison, the previous research displayed that CmRJ had negative impacts on the cell viability of HaCaT at higher doses (> 1000 µg/ml) [30]. It suggested that RJ treated with trypsin could enhance biosafety and be utilized in further investigations for the potential wound healing promoting effects.

The migration of keratinocytes towards the apex of granulation tissue and the formation of new tissue are necessary steps in the epithelialization process. In this study, an in vitro scratch wound healing model was utilized to assess the migratory capacity of HaCaT cells treated with CmRJPH within its effective concentrations (7.81-125 µg/ml). Following the induction of scratch wounds, exposure to CmRJPH at concentrations ranging from 7.81 to 62.5 µg/ml significantly enhanced keratinocyte wound closure in 24 h (Fig. 2C). Notably, the highest scratch healing rate (84.07%) was observed at the concentration of 62.5 µg/ml, demonstrating a statistically significant improvement compared to the control group (47.50%).

The wound closure of HaCaT cells in the scratch wound assay was dependent on the dual regulation of cell proliferation and migration. Since CmRJPH promoted cell proliferation at 24-48 h, the cell chemotaxis assay was subsequently conducted to exclude the potential cell-growth promoting effect of CmRJPH in scratch wound model. As shown in Fig. 2D, the number of migrating HaCaT cells stained with crystal violet was significantly higher than that in the control. Especially, the migration amount of HaCaT cells stimulated by 31.25 µg/ml and 62.5 µg/ml of CmRJPH was 228.68% and 295.47% as much as control group, respectively. The identical effective concentrations of CmRJPH in promoting the scratch wound closure and chemotaxis suggested a direct chemoattractant effect of CmRJPH on keratinocytes.

CmRJPH promoted migration of keratinocytes damaged by AGEs

The excessive accumulation of AGEs in the skin has been demonstrated to induce irreversible damage to the epidermis, accelerate keratinocyte senescence, and impair cell migration and proliferation. To investigate the potential therapeutic effects of CmRJPH on chronic non-healing wounds, an in vitro wound healing model using HaCaT monolayer cells preconditioned with AGEs was established. As shown in Fig. 3A-C, the proliferation of HaCaT cells preconditioned with AGEs was significantly slower than that of untreated cells, and none concentration (7.81-500 µg/ml) of tested CmRJPH significantly enhanced the cell growth of such AGE-pretreated HaCaT cells at either 24-48 h. In contrast, the wound closure rate of AGEs-induced HaCaT cells treated with 15.63 µg/ml of CmRJPH significantly increased by approximately 11.39% and 13.09% compared to AGEs-treated group at 24 and 48 h, respectively (Fig. 3C). These data collectively indicated that CmRJPH might possess therapeutic potential to accelerate the repair of chronic non-healing wounds.

CmRJPH reduced the expression of critical inflammatory factors in LPS-induced Raw 264.7 cells

The production of nitric oxide (NO) has been widely utilized as a biomarker to evaluate the establishment of in vitro inflammatory models. Raw 264.7 macrophages were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1 µg/ml) and the NO inhibitory effect of CmRJPH was assessed for their anti-inflammatory potential, as previously reported [20]. Firstly, CCK-8 experiment was performed to access cytotoxicity of CmRJPH towards Raw 264.7 macrophages, and the result indicated no cytotoxic effects on these cells below the concentration of 1000 µg/ml (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, each concentration (15.63-250 µg/ml) of CmRJPH significantly decreased the NO production in a dose-dependent manner, in which 250 µg/ml was selected as the optimal concentration for the subsequent anti-inflammatory studies.

Nitric oxide synthase (INOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) are two essential enzymes involved in initiating the inflammatory response, and their excessive upregulation could lead to inflammatory diseases even cancer. As shown by the qPCR and Western Blot analysis in Fig. 4C-F, the mRNA and protein expression levels of INOS and COX-2 in the LPS-induced Raw 264.7 cells were significantly inhibited by CmRJPH in a dose-dependent manner.

Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are two critical pro-inflammatory cytokines that play a pivotal role in mediating the acute inflammatory reactions. Excessive secretion of these cytokines can trigger a cytokine storm, compromise the immune system, and even result in death. As shown in Fig. 4G-J, the stimulation of LPS significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of TNF-α and IL-6 as determined by qPCR and ELISA, while lower concentrations of CmRJPH (31.25-62.5 µg/ml) displayed a remarkable inhibitory effect (Fig. 4G-J).

CmRJPH suppressed the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway in the LPS-induced Raw 264.7 cells

LPS can stimulate the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and promote the transcription of inflammatory factors, ultimately eliciting an inflammatory response in the body. This research evaluated the effect of CmRJPH (250 µg/ml) on the expression of some critical molecules (IKKα/p-IKKα; IκBα/p-IκBα; p65/p-p65) involved in the LPS-induced NF-κB signaling pathway. As shown in Fig. 5A-B, CmRJPH significantly suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of IKKα and IκBα in a time-dependent manner. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, compared with LPS-stimulated Raw 264.7 cells without CmRJPH treatment, those cells treated with CmRJPH exhibited a significant suppression in the phosphorylation of p65 by 0.72, 0.70 and 0.69-fold at 0.5, 3 and 6 h. These results directly suggested that the NF-κB signaling pathway was inactivated by CmRJPH treatment.

Antioxidant activity of CmRJPH

The DPPH radical scavenging activity and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity were determined to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of CmRJPH and the original CmRJ, with ascorbic acid at an equivalent concentration serving as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 6A-B, both DPPH free radical scavenging activity and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity were increased by the treatment of CmRJPH and RJ in a dose-dependent manner over the concentration range of 0.63-20 mg/ml. The DPPH free radical scavenging activity treated by CmRJPH was significantly higher than that by RJ at concentrations of 5-20 mg/ml, and the hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was also significantly higher over the concentration of 0.63-10 mg/ml, indicating that trypsin treatment significantly enhanced the antioxidant capacity of RJ.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are released by activated immune cells under inflammatory stimulation, resulting in oxidative stress in the body [31]. The release of ROS can initiate intracellular signaling cascades and enhance pro-inflammatory gene expression by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, and excessive ROS perpetuates the inflammatory response. Therefore, this study employed the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe to detect ROS levels in Raw 264.7 cells. As shown in Fig. 6C, compared with cells without LPS stimulation, the fluorescence intensity of DCFH-DA in Raw 264.7 cells was significantly increased after being stimulated with LPS for 6 h, indicating that LPS markedly induced the release of ROS. However, 250 µg/ml of CmRJPH significantly inhibited LPS-induced ROS production.

Discussion

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is one of the most common complications of diabetes which often resulting in amputations and even mortality if not cured appropriately. Unfortunately, few approved therapeutics are available for chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers in clinical applications. Apart from slow healing speed, the long-term inflammation and persistent infection are the other ordeals for chronic wound. Recent studies have extensively investigated the therapeutic effects of RJ on cutaneous wounds; for example, CmRJ and its extractions have been demonstrated to possess remarkable wound-repairing promoting effects on both the in vitro and in vivo model as well as notable anti-inflammation activity [20, 30]. However, further research and application of RJ are still restricted due to its complex components and low utilization rate. In this study, we found that peptides hydrolyzed from CmRJ could alleviate the symptoms of diabetic wounds by promoting the migration and proliferation of AGEs-damaged keratinocytes, as well as reducing inflammatory symptoms in Raw 264.7 macrophages through suppressing NF-κB signaling pathway.

Keratinocytes, which synthesize keratin proteins, are the predominant cell type in the epidermis and play a crucial role in epithelialization during wound healing [32]. Analogous to CmRJ, CmRJPH significantly enhanced the proliferation and migration of HaCaT cells in an in vitro wound healing model (Fig. 2), substantiating that the bioactive agents responsible for the wound healing efficacy of CmRJ are attributable to its proteinaceous constituents to some extents. In comparison with natural CmRJ, CmRJPH contains peptides with lower molecular weight, which facilitate the deeper skin permeation and enable their delivery to functional sites [33]. Some disease such as diabetes can accelerate the metabolic process of the formation of AGEs, and the excessive accumulation of AGEs would induce various forms of cell death and impair wound healing [34]. Keratinocytes and fibroblasts are the primary cells involved in skin wound healing; however, in the high glucose (HG) microenvironment of diabetic patients, the functional status of these cells is impaired, leading to accelerated cellular senescence [35]. In an in vitro AGEs-induced diabetic wound model, although CmRJPH failed to restore the proliferation of AGEs-damaged HaCaT cells, it significantly enhanced the migration of the cells (Fig. 3C), demonstrating the great potential of CmRJPH for the treatment of diabetic wounds.

LPS is a major structural component of the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria. It activates the TLR4/MD2 receptor complex and triggers NF-κB activation, thereby inducing the expression of COX-2 and INOS [36]. COX-2 catalyzes the conversion of arachidonic acid (AA) to prostaglandin H2 (PGH2), which is subsequently converted to prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) by prostaglandin E synthase (mPGES-1), and then PGE2 increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by activating NADPH oxidase [37]. INOS catalyzes the oxidative deamination of L-arginine to produce NO which reacts with the superoxide anion to generate peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), inducing lipid peroxidation and protein nitration damage [38]. These processes synergistically exacerbate oxidative stress and promote the excessive release of inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α, IL-6), exacerbating tissue damage [39]. In LPS-stimulated macrophages, the mRNA and protein expression of COX-2 and INOS are significantly positively correlated, and inhibiting the activity of either enzyme can reduce the expression of the other [40]. CmRJPH dose-dependently reduced the expression of INOS and COX-2 at both the transcriptional and translational levels, and significantly decreased the release of NO in LPS-induced Raw 264.7 cells (Fig. 4B, C-F), demonstrating that CmRJPH could exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the expression of INOS and COX-2.

Macrophages produce large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines upon the stimulation of LPS, and excessive cytokines production will exacerbate the damage of inflammation. During the initial inflammatory stage, the release of TNF-α activates the cascade of cytokines and induces the production of NO [41]. Early production of IL-6 is to a large extent under the regulation by TNF-α, and the persistent induction of IL-6 will be harmful to surrounding neurons or bones, and lead to long-term chronic inflammation [42]. LPS significantly increased both the mRNA expression and protein secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 in this study; however, concurrent treatment with CmRJPH dose-dependently inhibited the production of these pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4G-J), indicating its ability to alleviate the cellular inflammatory response.

NF-κB is one of the fundamental eukaryotic transcription factors responsible for regulating the gene expression involved in cell proliferation, migration, adhesion and immune functions. NF-κB transcription factor is commonly sequestered in the cytoplasm in an inactive form bound to IκB inhibitory protein in the form of homo- or heterodimers, with p65/p50 being the most common dimerization form [43]. When cells are exposed to some external stimuli such as LPS, IκB will be activated, phosphorylated and degraded by IκB kinase (IKK), and then dissociated from NF-κB [44]. The activated NF-κB p65/p50 heterodimers translocate to the cell nucleus, where they bind to cis-acting κB elements and undergo phosphorylation of NF-κB, inducing the enhanced transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and target genes (COX-2, INOS) [45]. Additionally, proteins induced by NF-κB, such as TNF-α, can also activate NF-κB, creating a vicious cycle that amplifies the initial inflammatory response. Therefore, blocking NF-κB is one of the most important strategies against inflammatory diseases. CmRJPH effectively reversed the phosphorylation of IKKα, IκBα, and NF-κB p65 (Fig. 5), demonstrating that CmRJPH exerts anti-inflammatory effects by blocking the NF-κB pathway.

ROS are highly reactive molecules produced during normal metabolic activities of the body, including superoxide anion radicals, hydrogen peroxide, singlet oxygen, and hydroxyl radicals [31, 46]. Elevated levels of ROS are generally considered a key indicator of cell damage. Excessive ROS can induce cell death through apoptosis and necrosis mechanisms, thereby causing oxidative damage to important intracellular biomolecules such as nucleic acids, lipids, and proteins. ROS also plays a key role in the differentiation and activation of macrophages. Inflammation promotes the production of ROS, and excessive ROS can lead to lipid peroxidation, thereby severely damaging cells; moreover, excessive ROS can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway and subsequently increase the expression of cytokines [47]. Our research showed that LPS stimulation increased ROS levels in Raw 264.7 cells, whereas treatment with CmRJPH could attenuate the production of ROS. It indicates that inhibiting ROS overproduction is likely a key upstream event in the anti-inflammatory action of CmRJPH, through which it not only alleviates direct oxidative stress but also blocks the signal amplification loop that drives inflammation.

Intact proteins (typically > 50 kDa) exhibit limited diffusivity within the wound bed matrix and activate cellular receptors inefficiently due to steric hindrance [48]. The trypsinization process generated low-molecular-weight peptide fractions. These peptides possess superior solubility, mobility, and bioavailability, enabling rapid penetration of the cellular microenvironment and more pronounced effects on HaCaT cell migration during critical phases of wound closure [49]. Furthermore, the small peptides generated by trypsinization present a greater number of electron-donating side chains compared to intact proteins [50]. This confers a superior targeted antioxidant capacity, effectively neutralizing local ROS and promoting the recovery of damaged keratinocytes [51]. By mitigating the hostile oxidative microenvironment, the hydrolysates facilitate the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase of wound healing [52].

As shown in the schematic diagram (Fig. 7), our findings indicated that CmRJPH attenuated macrophage-mediated inflammation by inhibiting the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway. Such an inhibition subsequently downregulated the expression of iNOS, COX-2, TNF-α and IL-6, while reducing the production of ROS. Concurrently, CmRJPH significantly enhanced the migration of AGEs-damaged keratinocytes. These dual actions contributed to the accelerated healing of diabetic wounds.

Schematic diagram of the possible mechanisms of CmRJPH accelerating the in vitro diabetic wound healing by enhancing keratinocytes functions and suppressing macrophage-mediated inflammation

Although our findings demonstrate that CmRJPH facilitates diabetic wound healing by rescuing the migration of compromised epithelial cells while mitigating aberrant inflammation, there is a notable limitation to this study: cell lines used in this article were from different species. While HaCaT cells and Raw 264.7 cells originate from different species, the fundamental signaling pathway investigated in this study is highly conserved between humans and mice [53]. Nevertheless, the species-specific differences in receptor sensitivity or cytokine responses cannot be entirely excluded. The future studies would utilize primary human macrophages or 3D skin co-culture models to fully validate the translational potential of CmRJPH in a completely humanized system.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that CmRJPH, the protein hydrolysates of C. mollissima Bl. royal jelly, constitutes a multi-faceted therapeutic agent for diabetic wounds. Its efficacy stemmed from the concurrent rescue of keratinocytes migration from AGE-induced impairment, the resolution of macrophage-mediated inflammation through inhibition of the NF-κB pathway, and the mitigation of oxidative stress via its intrinsic antioxidant properties. It revealed the therapeutic function and mode of action of CmRJPH in the treatment of diabetic wounds. Future investigations are warranted to identify the principal bioactive peptides and evaluate their efficacy in the in vivo diabetic wound models. This research offers a clue to develop novel peptide-based therapeutics from royal jelly, and provides a promising strategy to address the significant clinical challenge posed by intractable diabetic wounds.
