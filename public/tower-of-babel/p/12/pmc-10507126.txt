Chorioamnionitis and maternal sepsis caused by AmpC β-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli infection: A case report
Authors: Ryuto Tsushima, Kaori Iino, Shuang Song, Norihiro Saito, Kanji Tanaka, Yoshihito Yokoyama
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMultidrug resistant bacteria have increasingly become a concern regarding infection treatment. The clinical course of chorioamnionitis (CAM) caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria is not well understood.Presentation of caseWe report a case of CAM caused by AmpC-type β-lactamase (AmpC)-producing Escherichia coli (E. coli), a multidrug-resistant bacterium. A 35-year-old primipara was hospitalized with preterm membrane rupture at 36 weeks of gestation and was started on oral ampicillin. On the fourth day after admission, the patient was diagnosed with CAM owing to high fever development and uterine tenderness; therefore, an emergency cesarean section was performed. AmpC-producing E. coli were detected in blood and amniotic fluid cultures. Post-operation, the patient received treatment for septic shock and was discharged on the 15th post-operative day.DiscussionThe patient initially had no symptoms of infection but later experienced fever and uterine pain. She underwent an emergency cesarean section, and both mother and baby were successfully treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics. Introduction

Chorioamnionitis (CAM) is a common ascending maternal infection caused by vaginal microbial flora. While CAM is observed in approximately 1–4 % of full-term deliveries, its incidence is notably higher in preterm births, with reports indicating its presence in 8–50 % of spontaneous preterm deliveries [1]. Neonates exposed to intrauterine infection are at risk of developing fetal inflammatory response syndrome, which can lead to serious complications such as cerebral palsy [2]. Therefore, early and accurate diagnosis of CAM is extremely important for avoiding severe outcomes.

In recent years, the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria has become a significant concern in the treatment of infections. However, the clinical course of chorioamnionitis (CAM) caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria is not well understood due to the limited availability of only three previous reports on this condition in the literature. In this case report, we present a case of CAM caused by AmpC-type β-lactamase (AmpC)-producing Escherichia coli (E. coli), a multidrug-resistant bacterium. This case report has been reported in accordance to the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) guidelines [3].

Presentation of case

A 35-year-old primigravida woman in Japan, at 36 weeks of gestation, visited our hospital with a complaint of continuous clear vaginal fluid for 1 h. There were no signs of labor initiation, and she did not exhibit any symptoms of infection such as fever or abdominal pain. Three years ago, she underwent a cervical conization procedure due to cervical intraepithelial neoplasia 3 (CIN3). Six months after the surgery, she was given permission to become pregnant. All the regular cervical cytology tests after the surgery, including those in the early stages of her pregnancy, were normal. Upon admission, serum laboratory and physical examinations showed no abnormalities. Vaginal speculum examination confirmed clear amniotic fluid leakage. Cardiotocography (CTG) performed on admission showed normal results, and ultrasound revealed a normal estimated fetal body weight with a small amount of amniotic fluid. On the fourth day of admission, the patient's C-reactive protein (CRP) level was slightly elevated (4.25 mg/dL), while her white blood cell (WBC) count was not significantly increased (12.11 × 103/μL). However, on the fifth day of admission (100 h after amniorrhexis), the patient's body temperature suddenly rose to 38.1 °C, and she complained of continuous uterine pain, chills, and headaches. Additionally, she had a pale complexion and was in a clouded state of consciousness. CTG at the time showed normal baseline values, normal variability, and no deceleration or tachycardia (Fig. 2). At this time, the patient's WBC and CRP levels had not increased significantly (WBC, 14.62 × 103/μL; CRP, 4.63 mg/dL). The patient was initially administered oral antibiotics (amoxicillin) upon admission, but on the fourth day, the treatment was switched to intravenous ampicillin.

An emergency cesarean section was performed due to the diagnosis of CAM. A male neonate weighing 2886 g was delivered with Apgar scores of 6 at the first minute and 9 at the fifth minute after birth. Fortunately, the baby did not develop an infection, and their clinical course was uneventful. Intrauterine amniotic fluid was severely stained and had a foul odor.

Immediately after the surgery, the patient's blood pressure and white blood cell (WBC) counts dropped dramatically. Additionally, she experienced altered consciousness, raising a strong suspicion of septic shock. Therefore, broad-spectrum antibiotics (meropenem 3 g/day and metronidazole 1.5 g/day) were administered. On the second day following the surgery, the results of the blood and amniotic fluid cultures confirmed the presence of AmpC-producing E. coli. Based on the recommendations of the infectious disease team, the administration of meropenem and metronidazole was continued.

The pathological examination of the placenta also confirmed the presence of chorioamnionitis. On the second day after the surgery, the patient developed paralytic ileus, which required treatment. After a two-day period of fasting, she gradually resumed eating and recovered. As she recovered, the white blood cell count and CRP levels gradually decreased. Meropenem and metronidazole were administered for nine days, followed by a continued administration of meropenem alone for an additional seven days. The patient and her baby were discharged on the 15th day after the surgery.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of chorioamnionitis (CAM) caused by AmpC-producing bacteria. The patient's clinical course highlighted several crucial clinical considerations. CAM associated with multidrug-resistant bacteria poses challenges in diagnosis due to its occurrence in the unique environment of pregnancy and the uterus, compared to other infections. Furthermore, it can exacerbate during pregnancy due to compromised immunity. In the presented case, the patient was at 36 weeks of gestation when membrane rupture occurred. Therefore, we administered antibiotics and aimed to continue the pregnancy until 37 weeks. However, we could not prevent the onset of CAM. Although the CTG readings remained within normal limits, the patient exhibited pallor and diminished alertness. In light of these clinical observations and considering the increased likelihood of severe CAM, we opted for a cesarean section over labor induction to ensure prompt conclusion of the pregnancy This suggests that the conventional diagnostic criteria for CAM may not be applicable when multidrug-resistant bacteria are the causative agents.

The recent spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria has become a significant concern in infection control. Patients infected with multidrug-resistant bacteria are confronted with limited options for antibiotic treatment. Chorioamnionitis (CAM) presents particular challenges, including the difficulty of achieving an accurate diagnosis and the risk of fetal infection, which further underscores the gravity of multidrug-resistant bacteria. To date, three cases of CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria have been reported [[4], [5], [6]]. One case was attributed to Escherichia coli producing extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL), while the other two cases were caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).

AmpC is a beta-lactamase produced by specific bacteria such as Enterobacter species, Citrobacter species, and Serratia species. It has been observed that the administration of cephalosporin antibiotics can induce the activation of AmpC genes, leading to excessive production of AmpC. Infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria producing AmpC are generally not susceptible to most antibiotics, except for carbapenems and cefepime [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]].

This case represents the first reported occurrence of chorioamnionitis (CAM) caused by AmpC-producing bacteria. However, there is a potential for an increased number of patients in the obstetrics field, where prophylactic antibiotic administration is common. Additionally, pregnancy is generally associated with decreased immune function, which increases the risk of severe infections even in otherwise healthy pregnant women. In fact, among the three previously reported cases of CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria mentioned earlier, two cases, as well as our patient, did not exhibit any risk factors for infection. Furthermore, all four cases exhibited a significantly severe course of infection, underscoring the challenges associated with the diagnosis and treatment of CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria.

Clinical CAM is diagnosed based on the presence of the following symptoms: maternal fever, maternal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, foul smell, fetal tachycardia, and maternal leukocytosis [12]. However, prior reports have indicated that these criteria are not highly accurate [13,14]. CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria can rapidly progress and become severe, making accurate and timely diagnosis crucial. Therefore, conventional diagnostic criteria are not effective. In fact, in two prior cases and the case presented here, most diagnostic criteria were not met at the time of CAM diagnosis [[4], [5], [6]]. Development of effective biomarkers for early CAM diagnosis and establishment of new diagnostic criteria are desired in the future. In addition, all of them developed CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria during their hospitalization [[4], [5], [6]]. This indicates the potential for acquiring multidrug-resistant bacteria through healthcare workers or other sources within the hospital environment. If this is indeed the case, it may be possible to mitigate the risk of CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria by rigorously implementing standard precautions.

Conclusion

CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria during pregnancy is not only difficult to diagnose but also carries the risk of sudden onset and severe exacerbation. Infection spreading to the fetus can result in extremely grave outcomes. Therefore, in cases where severe CAM is suspected, prompt administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics is necessary to prevent severe outcomes in both mother and child. Despite being underreported, CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria may increase in incidence in the future. To prevent serious consequences of CAM caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria, appropriate antibiotic use and the establishment of more effective diagnostic criteria for CAM are warranted.
