Mpox in people living with HIV in Brazil: spatial distribution, clinical features, and outcomes from a nationwide surveillance study
Authors: Silvia Maria da Silva Sant'Ana Rodrigues, Thialla Andrade Carvalho, Francy Waltilia Cruz Araújo, Danilo Santos de Sousa, Paulo Ricardo Martins-Filho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBrazil has emerged as one of the global epicenters of the Mpox outbreak and accounts for the highest HIV prevalence in Latin America, particularly among men who have sex with men (MSM). Despite this overlapping epidemiological burden, population-based evidence on the intersection between Mpox and HIV remains limited. This study provides the first national epidemiological characterization of Mpox/HIV coinfection in Brazil, focusing on spatial distribution, clinical features, and outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide, registry-based, retrospective, analytical cross-sectional study using individual-level data from Brazil's national surveillance system, encompassing all confirmed and probable Mpox cases reported between May 2022 and April 2025. Extracted variables included sociodemographic data, gender identity, sexual behavior, probable transmission route, STI coinfections, clinical presentation, hospitalization, and mortality. We calculated municipality-level cumulative case-notification rates of Mpox/HIV coinfection per 100,000 inhabitants using 2022 IBGE population estimates, mapped spatial distribution, and applied empirical Bayes smoothing. Group comparisons by HIV status used the Mann–Whitney and two-proportion Z tests (p < 0.05), with effect sizes estimated using rank-biserial correlation and Cohen's h, respectively. Analyses were conducted in R.FindingsAmong 14,340 Mpox cases, 5031 (35.1%) occurred in people living with HIV (PLHIV), with disproportionate clustering in large urban centers and touristic municipalities. Although certain symptoms, such as fever, fatigue, lymphadenopathy, myalgia, and mucocutaneous lesions, were more common among PLHIV, effect sizes were small, limiting their clinical discriminative value. Despite modest differences, PLHIV had also higher rates of antiviral therapy use against Mpox, hospitalization and ICU admission, and accounted for 93.3% of Mpox-related deaths.InterpretationThese findings support the integration of HIV status into Mpox surveillance and clinical protocols, especially in contexts marked by structural and social vulnerability.FundingNone.Keywords: Monkeypox virus, Mpox, HIV

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The global reemergence of Mpox has disproportionately affected key populations, particularly men who have sex with men (MSM) and people living with HIV (PLHIV), raising concerns about differential clinical presentation, disease progression, and outcomes in immunocompromised individuals. Although several case series and multicenter reports from high-income countries have described Mpox/HIV coinfection, evidence from low- and middle-income countries in Latin America remains scarce. To identify relevant population-based studies in the region, we conducted a systematic PubMed search between March 20 and March 23, 2025, without language restrictions, using the following combination of terms: ("mpox" OR "monkeypox") AND ("Human immunodeficiency virus" OR "HIV" OR "PLHIV" OR "people living with HIV") AND ("clinical characteristics" OR "clinical presentation" OR "symptoms" OR "manifestation") AND ("Latin America" OR "South America" OR "Brazil" OR "Argentina" OR "Colombia" OR "Peru" OR "Chile" OR "Mexico" OR "Uruguay" OR "Paraguay" OR "Bolivia" OR "Ecuador" OR "Venezuela"). Among the retrieved studies, only one Peruvian investigation conducted stratified clinical comparisons of Mpox cases according to HIV status. A prior Brazilian study, limited to early outbreak data, provided descriptive statistics but lacked comparative analyses or spatial assessments. To our knowledge, no other publications have integrated clinical, epidemiological, and geographic data on Mpox/HIV coinfection in Brazil using nationwide surveillance data.

Added value of this study

This study provides a comprehensive national analysis of Mpox/HIV coinfection in Brazil based on over 14,000 confirmed and probable cases reported between 2022 and 2025. It offers disaggregated comparisons between individuals with and without HIV regarding sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behavior, STI coinfections, clinical features, hospitalization, and mortality. The study also incorporates a spatial dimension by estimating and smoothing cumulative notification rates of Mpox/HIV coinfection at the municipal level. Approximately one-third of Mpox cases occurred in PLHIV—predominantly cisgender MSM reporting sexual contact as the likely route of infection—and were frequently accompanied by other STIs, particularly syphilis. These cases were more frequent in large urban centers and touristic municipalities, reflecting underlying sociodemographic and spatial vulnerabilities. Moreover, individuals with Mpox/HIV coinfection experienced worse outcomes, including higher rates of hospitalization and mortality.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings underscore the importance of integrating HIV status into Mpox surveillance, clinical triage, and public health decision-making, particularly in countries like Brazil where HIV prevalence is high among key populations. Tailored interventions, including risk stratification, targeted vaccination, and enhanced monitoring in high-burden areas can optimize resource allocation and improve outcomes. Furthermore, the integration of emerging infectious disease surveillance with existing STI and HIV programs may strengthen health system responsiveness in settings marked by social and structural vulnerabilities.

Introduction

Since May 2022, the re-emergence of Mpox (formerly Monkeypox), a zoonotic infection caused by the Monkeypox virus (MPXV), has evolved into a global public health concern, with sustained transmission chains observed in non-endemic regions. Distinct from previous outbreaks characterized by animal-to-human transmission and household clustering, the current epidemic is driven predominantly by human-to-human spread in urban settings and disproportionately affects sexual and gender minority populations. In particular, cisgender men who have sex with men (MSM) represent the majority of confirmed cases, many of whom report multiple sexual partners and a recent history of other sexually transmitted infections (STIs).1,2

Clinically, Mpox has demonstrated considerable variability in its presentation. While classic manifestations include fever, lymphadenopathy, and vesiculopustular exanthema, the recent outbreak has shown a predominance of localized lesions in the genital and perianal regions, which may resemble other STIs and pose diagnostic challenges in clinical practice.3, 4, 5 Coinfections with Treponema pallidum, Chlamydia trachomatis, and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are common, and a substantial proportion of affected individuals are people living with HIV (PLHIV). Current evidence suggests that approximately 40% of Mpox cases occur in PLHIV,6, 7, 8 raising important considerations regarding immunological status, disease severity, and clinical management.

Emerging data indicate that PLHIV—particularly those with low CD4+ T-cell counts or unsuppressed viral loads—may be at increased risk of hospitalization, severe disease, and death.9 However, evidence regarding clinical manifestations remains inconsistent, likely reflecting variations in study design, population profiles, and health system contexts.4,10 Importantly, most available studies are based on convenience samples from referral centers, limiting generalizability and introducing potential selection bias. Furthermore, there is a paucity of population-based studies directly comparing population-based comparisons between PLHIV and HIV-negative individuals remain scarce in low- and middle-income countries, including in Latin America, where structural disparities, delayed diagnosis, and differences in care-seeking behavior play a crucial role in shaping disease burden and progression.

Brazil has emerged as one of the epicenters of the current Mpox outbreak, reporting one of the highest cumulative case counts globally. Simultaneously, the country has the highest HIV prevalence in Latin America, particularly among MSM and young adults. Despite the overlapping epidemiological burden, no previous study has systematically examined the interaction between Mpox and HIV at a national level in Brazil. Existing national data are limited to descriptive reports and do not offer comparative analyses or spatial assessments based on HIV status.11 Using individual-level data from the Brazilian Ministry of Health, we examined spatial distribution, sociodemographic characteristics, gender identity, sexual practices, probable transmission routes, STI coinfections, clinical manifestations, and key outcomes such as hospitalization and mortality. By integrating spatial epidemiology and comparative clinical analyses, our findings offer a robust characterization of the Mpox/HIV interface in a middle-income country setting and contribute to the global understanding of syndemic interactions among sexually transmissible infections.

Methods

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement12 to ensure methodological rigor, transparency, and reproducibility (Supplementary file). The analysis was based on secondary data obtained from a publicly accessible national surveillance database maintained by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. All records were fully anonymized, precluding the identification of individuals. In accordance with Brazilian regulations for research involving human subjects (CNS Resolution No. 510/2016), ethical approval was not required for studies using de-identified, publicly available data.

Study design

We conducted a nationwide, registry-based, retrospective, analytical cross-sectional study using anonymized individual-level data from the national Mpox surveillance system (e-SUS SINAN, June 2022–April 2025) (http://plataforma.saude.gov.br/esussinan/), comparing characteristics of cases with vs. without HIV (case–case comparison). Additionally, we performed a spatial ecological analysis at the municipality level, estimating cumulative notification rates and assessing local spatial association. The dataset was provided by the General Coordination of Epidemiological Information and Analysis, Department of Epidemiological Analysis and Noncommunicable Diseases Surveillance, Secretariat for Health Surveillance and Environment, Ministry of Health, Brazil. The e-SUS SINAN is a web-based platform used for the compulsory notification of diseases and health conditions listed in Brazil's national list of notifiable diseases. It serves as a critical tool for public health planning, prioritization of interventions, and evaluation of health policies and surveillance performance.

Mpox case Definition

We included all reported cases of Mpox classified as probable or confirmed. A probable case was defined as a clinically compatible individual with a history of exposure within 21 days prior to symptom onset—such as close, prolonged, and unprotected respiratory contact; intimate or sexual contact with a probable or confirmed case; contact with contaminated materials; or occupational exposure without appropriate personal protective equipment—and without laboratory confirmation or an alternative diagnosis. A confirmed case was defined as a clinically compatible individual with laboratory confirmation of Mpox virus infection by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and/or genomic sequencing.

HIV Coinfection

Mpox/HIV coinfection was defined as a probable or confirmed Mpox case in individuals with a previous diagnosis of HIV or who were newly diagnosed with HIV during clinical care related to Mpox.

Variables of interest

The following variables were extracted from the database: state and municipality of residence, date of notification, age, self-reported race/skin color, occupation, gender identity, sexual behavior, history of smallpox vaccination, probable route of Mpox transmission, coinfection with other STIs, clinical manifestations, antiviral therapy against Mpox, hospitalization, and death.

Cumulative case-notification rates of mpox by municipality and spatial analysis

Cumulative case-notification rates (CNRs) of Mpox/HIV coinfection were calculated by dividing the total number of individuals with Mpox who were living with HIV by the resident population of each municipality, based on 2022 Census estimates from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), and expressed per 100,000 inhabitants. The same approach was applied to estimate CNRs of Mpox among individuals without HIV, using the same population denominator.

To address potential random fluctuations in CNRs, particularly in municipalities with small populations, empirical Bayesian smoothing (EBS) was applied using GeoDa software (version 1.22, University of Chicago). This statistical technique adjusts unstable crude rates by borrowing strength from neighboring areas, producing more robust local estimates. The EBS method operates by combining the observed rate in each geographic unit with an overall reference rate (e.g., the mean across all areas), with weights inversely proportional to the local population size. As a result, estimates from sparsely populated municipalities are pulled more strongly toward the global mean, while those from populous areas remain closer to their observed values. This approach reduces the influence of random variability in low-count areas, yielding more stable and interpretable spatial patterns of disease risk for small-area epidemiological analysis. Spatial analyses of both crude and smoothed CNRs of Mpox, stratified by HIV status, were performed in QGIS software (version 3.34). The municipality of residence served as the unit of analysis, and all cartographic layers were projected using the SIRGAS 2000 geodetic reference system.

To explore potential syndemic interactions and shared risk environments, an additional spatial analysis was conducted to identify patterns of geographic association between overall cumulative Mpox notification rates and those specifically observed among cisgender MSM living with HIV and/or other STIs. After confirming the presence of significant global spatial autocorrelation, the bivariate Local Moran's I (LISA) index was employed to assess local associations between these rates across neighboring municipalities. This approach enabled identification of clusters where high Mpox rates co-occur with high rates in the MSM/HIV/STI subpopulation in adjacent areas (high–high pattern), and conversely, low–low clusters, as well as transitional zones (high–low or low–high patterns). Spatial contiguity was defined using a first-order queen contiguity matrix, and statistical significance was assessed via 999 Monte Carlo permutations (α = 0.05). All bivariate spatial analyses were performed in GeoDa, and resulting maps were generated using QGIS (version 3.40).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3). The median age of individuals with Mpox living with HIV and those without HIV was compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, given the non-parametric distribution of data. For all other variables—including sociodemographic characteristics, gender identity, sexual behavior, history of smallpox vaccination, probable transmission route, STI coinfections, clinical manifestations, hospitalization, and death—group comparisons were performed using two-proportion Z tests (two-sided, α = 0.05). Events with very low frequency (<0.1%), particularly rare clinical signs and symptoms, were summarized descriptively and excluded from group comparisons due to the low number of observations and limited statistical power to detect meaningful differences.

To enhance the interpretability of differences between groups, we calculated the absolute difference in proportions (Δ pp) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Newcombe method without continuity correction.13 In addition, we computed the standardized effect size for proportions, Cohen's h, defined as the difference between arcsine-transformed proportions: ℎ = 2 × [arcsin(√p1)—arcsin(√p2)]. Following Cohen's classification (1977),14 we interpret effect sizes as trivial (0–0.19), small (0.20–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.79), and large (≥0.80). For the comparison of median age between individuals with Mpox living with and without HIV, we calculated the rank-biserial correlation (r) as a nonparametric measure of effect size. According to common thresholds, effect sizes were interpreted as: small (r < 0.30), medium (0.30–0.49), and large (r ≥ 0.50).15

We treated “missing” and “unknown” as distinct response categories recorded in the e-SUS/SINAN system (e.g., “unknown” transmission route vs. absent field entry). For all variables, denominators reflected the total number of confirmed Mpox cases, regardless of data completeness. Missing data were reported to ensure transparency but were not interpreted clinically, and no imputation was performed. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Spatiotemporal dynamics of Mpox/HIV Coinfection in Brazil

Between June 2022 and April 2025, Brazil notified a total of 14,340 confirmed and probable cases of Mpox. Temporally, the epidemic exhibited a biphasic pattern, with a sharp peak between August and September 2022, followed by a sustained decline through the first quarter of 2023. A second, less intense wave of transmission was observed between August and December 2024. Throughout the study period, the proportion of Mpox cases involving PLHIV remained consistently high, suggesting persistent transmission in disproportionately affected subpopulations and reinforcing the syndemic nature of Mpox and HIV in Brazil (Fig. 1).

Monthly distribution of Mpox cases in Brazil, June 2022–April 2025, by HIV status. Bars represent monthly confirmed and probable Mpox cases among people living with HIV (orange) and those without HIV (blue); the line indicates the total number of cases reported per month.

Geographically, information on municipality of residence was available for 14,332 cases, which were distributed across 759 municipalities. Among these, 5031 cases (35.1%) occurred in PLHIV, with coinfections reported in 317 municipalities. In 59 of these, all reported Mpox cases occurred exclusively among PLHIV, indicating localized outbreaks restricted to this key population. The highest absolute numbers of Mpox/HIV coinfection were observed in major urban centers, including São Paulo (SP) (n = 1336), Rio de Janeiro (RJ) (n = 597), Manaus (AM) (n = 254), Goiânia (GO) (n = 230), Curitiba (PR) (n = 174), Fortaleza (CE) (n = 154), Brasília (DF) (n = 149), Belo Horizonte (MG) (n = 147), and Recife (PE) (n = 103). In contrast, when considering crude cumulative CNRs per 100,000 inhabitants, the highest values were observed in small and mid-sized municipalities, such as Arenópolis (GO) (33.9), Fernando de Noronha (PE) (31.6), Aurilândia (GO) (30.4), Balneário Camboriú (SC) (23.0), Nazaré (TO) (22.1), Caturaí (GO) (19.2), and Florianópolis (SC) (18.0). After applying EBS, the highest smoothed CNRs were recorded in Balneário Camboriú (SC) (19.3), Florianópolis (SC) (17.0), Goiânia (GO) (15.7), Manaus (AM) (12.2), São Paulo (SP) (11.6), Curitiba (PR) (9.7), and Rio de Janeiro (RJ) (9.6) (Fig. 2).

To identify geographic areas with overlapping transmission dynamics, we examined the spatial intersection between overall Mpox rates and those observed among MSM living with HIV and/or other STIs. A total of 144 municipalities exhibited High–High clustering patterns. These were primarily concentrated in major metropolitan regions and densely populated urban centers, including the metropolitan areas of São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro, as well as municipalities within the metropolitan region of Porto Alegre, urban poles in the North such as Manaus and Belém, and scattered coastal capitals across the Northeast Region. These spatial clusters suggest a convergence of Mpox and HIV/STI transmission networks in urban settings marked by high population density, intense mobility, and established sexual health vulnerabilities (Fig. 3).

High–High clusters (red) indicate municipalities with elevated overall Mpox rates surrounded by neighbors with elevated MSM/HIV/STI-specific rates, denoting overlapping syndemic hotspots.

Sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics

Among the 14,340 Mpox cases reported in Brazil between June 2022 and April 2025, the majority were young adult males from the Southeast region. The median age was 32 years (IQR: 27–38), and 91.6% of all cases occurred in men. Cisgender identity was reported by nearly 80% of individuals, and over half (52.3%) identified as MSM. Most cases were notified in 2022 (76.2%), with lower frequencies in subsequent years. Self-reported race/ethnicity was predominantly Brown or Black (42.2%) or White (42.0%). Only 1.0% of patients reported a history of smallpox vaccination. Sexual contact was the main reported route of transmission (36.6%), although a substantial proportion of cases had unknown exposure routes (30.0%) or missing data (28.6%).

Individuals with Mpox/HIV coinfection differed from those without HIV across several sociodemographic and behavioral variables. Nonetheless, most of these differences, including geographic region, race/ethnicity, gender identity, occupation in healthcare, and smallpox vaccination history, presented trivial effect sizes and limited clinical relevance. Differences with greater magnitude were observed for age, sex, sexual behavior, and probable transmission route. PLHIV were older (median: 34 vs. 31 years; p < 0.001; rank-biserial r ≈ 0.19: small effect size), and the vast majority were male (99.2% vs. 87.5%; p < 0.001; Cohen's h ≈ 0.54: moderate effect size). The proportion of individuals identifying as MSM was also higher among PLHIV (68.7% vs. 43.5%; p < 0.001; Cohen's h ≈ 0.51: moderate effect size), and sexual transmission was more frequently reported in this group (45.4% vs. 31.8%; p < 0.001; Cohen's h ≈ 0.28: small effect size). Additionally, completeness differed by HIV status. Compared with HIV-negative individuals, PLHIV had lower missingness for gender identity (13.7% vs. 20.8%), sexual behavior (21.0% vs. 30.3%), smallpox vaccination (53.4% vs. 58.6%), and transmission route (unknown: 24.2% vs. 33.1%; missing: 26.5% vs. 29.8%) (Table 1).

STI coinfections, clinical presentation, and outcomes of mpox cases

Among the 14,340 Mpox cases, coinfection with at least one other STI was documented in 10.5% of patients, with syphilis being the most common (8.7%), followed by gonorrhea (0.9%) and genital herpes (0.5%). Other STIs such as genital herpes, chlamydia, lymphogranuloma venereum, and chancroid were less frequently reported. The most common clinical manifestations were skin rash (72.1%), fever (57.9%), asthenia/fatigue (57.5%), anogenital lesions (47.4%), and lymphadenopathy (46.9%). Other symptoms such as headache (37.9%), myalgia (33.2%), back pain (16.9%), sore throat (12.9%), and chills (11.9%) were also frequent. Clinical findings reported in fewer than 0.1% of patients included bleeding (anal, gingival), genitourinary symptoms (urethritis, urinary tract infection, dyspareunia), respiratory and oropharyngeal conditions (nasal congestion, pharyngotonsillitis, dysphonia), neurological signs (vertigo, convulsion, delirium, mental confusion), and sensory symptoms (ageusia). Regarding severity, hospitalization was required in 5.3% of cases, with 0.4% admitted to intensive care units. Antiviral therapy was reported in 97 patients (0.7%), including tecovirimat (n = 51), cidofovir (n = 6), brincidofovir (n = 2), and unspecified antivirals (n = 38), with higher frequency among hospitalized individuals (3.2% vs. 0.5% in non-hospitalized; p < 0.001). A total of 15 deaths (0.1%) were directly attributed to Mpox, and an additional 21 deaths were reported from other causes (Table 2).

When stratified by HIV status, differences emerged in the clinical characteristics of Mpox cases. Individuals with Mpox/HIV coinfection exhibited a significantly higher frequency of STIs, particularly syphilis (15.4% vs. 5.0%; p < 0.001; Cohen's h ≈ 0.30: small effect size). Other STIs, such as condyloma acuminatum, gonorrhea, and genital herpes were also more frequently reported among PLHIV, although the effect sizes were negligible. Regarding clinical presentation, asthenia/fatigue (62.2% vs. 54.7%; p < 0.001; Cohen's h ≈ 0.25) and lymphadenopathy (52.8% vs. 43.8%; p < 0.001; Cohen's h ≈ 0.23) were more frequently reported among PLHIV, with small effect sizes. Other clinical manifestations, including fever, headache, myalgia, anogenital lesions, and oral mucosal lesions, were also more prevalent in PLHIV; however, the effect sizes were trivial. Although the magnitude of between-group differences was very small, individuals with Mpox/HIV coinfection had higher rates of hospitalization (6.5% vs. 4.6%; p < 0.001) and intensive care unit admission (0.5% vs. 0.3%; p = 0.032). Moreover, antiviral use was slightly more frequent among PLHIV (0.9%) than among HIV-negative individuals (0.6%) (p = 0.019), although the absolute difference was also trivial. However, 14 out of the 15 deaths directly attributed to Mpox occurred among PLHIV (93.3%; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Mpox/HIV Coinfection in other vulnerable populations

An exploratory analysis was conducted to identify potential cases of Mpox/HIV coinfection among pregnant women. Among the 27 pregnant women diagnosed with Mpox in the national surveillance database, one was a 37-year-old cisgender woman in the third trimester of pregnancy, living with HIV and coinfected with syphilis. The patient presented with rash, anogenital lesions, headache, lymphadenopathy, and myalgia, but no complications such as hospitalization or death were reported. Additionally, there was no record of fetal demise associated with this case.

Discussion

Despite the growing body of literature on the global reemergence of Mpox, population-level analyses that disaggregate clinical and epidemiological data by HIV status remain scarce in Latin America.11,16, 17, 18 To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study in Brazil to assess Mpox/HIV coinfection using surveillance data with individual-level resolution. Our findings offer robust epidemiological evidence of distinct patterns in geographic distribution, clinical manifestations, and disease outcomes among people living with and without HIV. These results advance the understanding of how Mpox disproportionately affects PLHIV in a middle-income setting characterized by intersecting social, geographic, and epidemiological vulnerabilities.

The spatial concentration of Mpox/HIV coinfection in large urban centers and tourist destinations, such as Balneário Camboriú, Florianópolis, Goiânia, Manaus, São Paulo, Curitiba, and Rio de Janeiro, underscores the complex and multifactorial nature of transmission dynamics in these settings. In addition to high levels of human mobility and regional connectivity, these municipalities are characterized by active nightlife scenes, significant LGBTQIA + visibility, and recurring events that attract large crowds and facilitate casual sexual interactions. Moreover, dense sexual networks, frequent use of geolocation-based dating applications, relative anonymity in urban environments, and travel-associated sexual behaviors—often involving multiple anonymous partners and inconsistent condom use—may further facilitate viral transmission.19, 20, 21 Despite these social and behavioral determinants, these cities are recognized as regional healthcare hubs, which may concentrate case notifications due to enhanced access to diagnostic resources and specialized care, thereby introducing a potential ascertainment bias. In addition, the observed biphasic epidemic pattern, with a sharp peak of Mpox cases between August and September 2022 followed by a smaller resurgence from August to December 2024, reinforces the importance for sustained epidemiological vigilance. These temporal fluctuations, possibly influenced by population mobility, large-scale events, and residual vulnerability in key groups, call for continuous diagnostic readiness and prevention efforts aligned with the sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics of affected populations in higher-burden settings.

In Brazil, more than one-third of all Mpox cases occurred in PLHIV, predominantly among young cisgender MSM, with sexual contact identified as the primary transmission route. This pattern reflects the convergence of overlapping sexual networks, high background prevalence of STIs, and persistent barriers to integrate sexual health services among gender and sexual minorities. Despite the formal existence of national STI control programs, stigma, discrimination, and healthcare exclusion continue to undermine early diagnosis and timely management.22,23 These structural inequities also hinder partner notification, compromise treatment adherence, and limit the dissemination of targeted health education, particularly regarding emerging infectious diseases such as Mpox, where sexual exposure constitute the predominant transmission route, thereby sustaining chains of transmission.24, 25, 26, 27 In this context, health departments in high-incidence municipalities may adopt operational strategies, such as outbreak-responsive testing campaigns, extended clinic hours, mobile outreach teams during mass events, and community-based linkage initiatives, to reduce access barriers, mitigate stigma, and strengthen syndromic surveillance.

The disproportionate burden of Mpox among PLHIV and MSM also heightened the visibility of the outbreak in public discourse, including digital platforms. A global infodemiological study28 analyzing YouTube search trends reported increased interest in STIs during the early stages of the outbreak, particularly following official communications that emphasized sexual transmission and the high prevalence of HIV among Mpox cases. While such search behavior does not directly reflect individual actions, it serves as a proxy for heightened public concern and risk perception. This visibility, however, also contributed to the social framing of Mpox as a disease associated with sexual minorities, which may reinforce existing stigma. In this context, infodemiological indicators can support the development of evidence-based risk communication strategies aimed at addressing misinformation while minimizing stigma and structural inequities.

From a surveillance perspective, the elevated frequency of syphilis among individuals with Mpox/HIV coinfection may serve as a sentinel indicator of sustained sexual risk and gaps in the STI prevention cascade. This finding may reflect limited access to periodic screening, insufficient partner management, and missed opportunities for timely treatment—components that are central to syndromic control approaches.29, 30, 31 Although other coinfections, including condyloma acuminatum, gonorrhea, and genital herpes, were also more frequent in this group, the corresponding effect sizes were modest and may partly reflect enhanced diagnostic scrutiny in PLHIV. These disparities point to the importance of aligning Mpox surveillance with broader STI control policies. Service integration can be operationalized by offering same-day Mpox assessment and vaccination during ART/PrEP visits, and by embedding rapid syphilis testing and treatment in Mpox encounters, including facilitated partner services. Incorporating such protocols into HIV care settings can improve case detection and reduce missed prevention opportunities in populations affected by overlapping sexual health burdens. In a recent study, we emphasized the importance of expanding Mpox vaccination strategies in Brazil to include key populations at increased risk, such as PLHIV, sex workers, and individuals with recent STI diagnoses or on HIV PrEP, in with proactive outreach through ART/PrEP clinics, STI clinics, and community organizations.32

Additionally, we observed statistically significant differences in several clinical signs and symptoms between individuals with and without Mpox/HIV coinfection, including fever, headache, asthenia/fatigue, lymphadenopathy, myalgia, arthralgia, anogenital lesions, penile edema, proctitis, and oral mucosal lesions. Although these manifestations may reflect early systemic immune activation and broader viral dissemination in PLHIV, the small magnitudes indicate that symptom profiles alone are unlikely to discriminate cases by HIV status in routine care, particularly when immunological data are unavailable. This interpretation is consistent with prior syntheses showing that symptom profiles in PLHIV with adequate viro-immunologic control are not consistently distinct, whereas excess severity clusters among those with advanced immunosuppression (e.g., low CD4 counts or unsuppressed viral load).33

Beyond differences in symptom frequency, more severe clinical outcomes were disproportionately observed among PLHIV, who exhibited increased rates of hospitalization and ICU admission and accounted for 93.3% of Mpox-related deaths. These findings align with national surveillance data from Peru, where Mpox/HIV coinfection was associated with increased clinical severity, particularly among individuals lacking antiretroviral treatment or presenting with high-risk sexual exposures.16 Immunocompromised status, especially in patients with CD4+ T-cell counts <200 cells/mm3 or detectable viral load, has been linked to persistent viremia, necrotizing lesions, and complications such as secondary bacterial infections.34,35 Although immunological markers such as CD4+ count and viral load were not available, the observed mortality concentration among PLHIV suggests that uncontrolled HIV replication and delayed care may have contributed to clinical deterioration in a subset of patients. Nonetheless, given the absence of viro-immunological data, we refrained from inferring direct causality between HIV status and severe outcomes, and interpreted these findings as hypothesis-generating.

While our analysis focused primarily on differences in severity by HIV status, one exploratory observation involving a pregnant woman deserves mention. Among the 27 pregnant individuals with confirmed Mpox in the national database, a single case of triple infection with Mpox, HIV, and syphilis was identified in a 37-year-old cisgender woman in her third trimester. The patient exhibited typical Mpox symptoms, but did not experience complications such as hospitalization, maternal death, or fetal loss. Although pregnancy was not the central focus of this investigation, the co-occurrence of Mpox, HIV, and syphilis in this case raises concerns regarding the integration of antenatal care and STI surveillance. Further studies are needed to assess the maternal and perinatal consequences of Mpox infection, especially in syndemic contexts involving HIV and other STIs.

In our national dataset, antiviral therapy was infrequently prescribed for Mpox, with tecovirimat being the most commonly reported agent. Its use was significantly more frequent among hospitalized patients and slightly higher among PLHIV, albeit with a trivial absolute difference. These findings suggest that antiviral administration was likely reserved for patients with more severe disease courses or greater clinical risk. Tecovirimat, a viral envelope formation inhibitor targeting the p37 protein, has demonstrated in vitro and animal model activity against orthopoxviruses,36 but clinical evidence remains limited.37 Real-world data from U.S. cohorts, including PLHIV, indicate that tecovirimat is generally well tolerated, though serious adverse events may occur in severely immunocompromised individuals, particularly when multiple treatment cycles are used or intravenous administration is required.38 Moreover, observational studies have not consistently shown accelerated healing or viral clearance among hospitalized patients treated with tecovirimat.39 These findings support the careful use of antivirals in clinical practice, particularly in populations at greater risk, such as PLHIV.

This study is subject to inherent limitations associated with secondary surveillance data. First, under-ascertainment is likely, particularly during the early outbreak phase and in smaller municipalities, due to heterogeneous access to testing, changes in clinical awareness, and reporting delays. Second, differences in data completeness by HIV status (with more complete records among PLHIV) may introduce detection bias in clinical features and coinfection reporting. This differential completeness may reflect greater diagnostic scrutiny or more structured clinical follow-up among PLHIV, potentially inflating the observed burden of symptoms or comorbidities in this group. Moreover, disparities in access to testing, health-seeking behavior, and reporting practices may further amplify ascertainment bias, especially in healthcare hubs where PLHIV are more likely to be engaged in specialized care. Third, the concentration of cases in large urban centers, where laboratory capacity and referral services are clustered, may reflect both true transmission dynamics and notification artifacts, complicating spatial inference. Fourth, several key variables (e.g., sexual exposure, gender identity) are self-reported and subject to misclassification or social desirability bias; the high proportion of missing data on transmission route reinforces this concern. Fifth, municipal rates were calculated using 2022 IBGE population denominators and based on municipality of residence rather than probable exposure, which may lead to geographic misattribution in the context of population mobility. Sixth, while empirical Bayesian smoothing improves rate stability in small areas, it shrinks outlier values toward the mean, potentially masking true hotspots. The use of municipalities as the unit of spatial analysis may obscure intra-urban heterogeneity, particularly in large and socioeconomically diverse cities, where transmission patterns and access to care can vary substantially across neighborhoods. This spatial aggregation introduces the potential for ecological bias and limits the resolution of our findings. Future studies using finer spatial units, such as census tracts or ZIP codes, may allow for more granular identification of high-risk microenvironments. Finally, the lack of immunological data (e.g., CD4+ count, viral load) precluded stratified analyses by HIV disease stage, which may have refined interpretations of clinical severity.

In conclusion, this population-based study provides comprehensive epidemiological evidence of significant differences in the spatial distribution, clinical presentation, and outcomes of Mpox according to HIV status in Brazil. People living with HIV accounted for a substantial proportion of cases and exhibited more severe clinical manifestations, higher rates of coinfections with other STIs, and elevated risk of hospitalization and death. The observed patterns reflect the intersection of biological vulnerability and persistent structural barriers to prevention and care. These findings demonstrate the importance of incorporating HIV status into Mpox surveillance systems and reinforce the need for specific clinical management protocols, as well as future research addressing immunological determinants and long-term outcomes in high-risk populations.
