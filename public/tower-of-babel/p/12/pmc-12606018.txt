Safety and Effectiveness of Cholecystectomy and Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography in Biliary Pancreatitis During Pregnancy: BORN Study
Authors: Dorottya Tarján, Eszter Ágnes Szalai, Bálint Erőss, Péter Jenő Hegyi, Vasile Liviu Drug, Serge Chooklin, Michael Hirth, Gabriel Sandblom, Vanessa Sandblom, Åsa Edergren, Ahmed Tlili, Sami Fendri, Simon Sirtl, Daniel de la Iglesia Gracía, Floreta Kurti, Dong Wu, Adriana Gherbon, Łukasz Nawacki, Alexandru Constantinescu, Natalia V Shirinskaya, Alexander N Zolotov, Sanjay Pandanaboyana, Tsukasa Ikeura, Tiago Cúrdia Gonçalves, Louise Rasmussen, Bodil Andersson, Ahmed Bouzid, Ahmed Saidani, Nándor Ács, Zoltán Sipos, Nelli Farkas, Balázs Tihanyi, Brigitta Teutsch, Johan Nilsson, Alexandra Mikó, Péter Hegyi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBiliary acute pancreatitis (AP) during pregnancy is a challenging situation, and current guidelines for AP, pregnancy care, and surgery do not specifically address its management. This study investigated the safety and effectiveness of cholecystectomy and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) during pregnancy in AP.MethodsThis international retrospective multicenter cohort study encompassed questions related to demographic information, clinical presentation, management strategies, timing of cholecystectomy, approaches to the procedure, complications, and outcomes. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons used Welch's t‐test, Pearson's chi‐squared, or Fisher's exact tests.ResultsA total of 101 cases from 14 countries and 19 centers were enrolled. Cholecystectomy after mild AP during pregnancy had a lower rate of readmission due to recurrent AP or other gallstone‐related complications compared with those who did not undergo surgery after a mild AP during pregnancy (0% vs. 24%; n = 0/17 vs. n = 12/49, p = 0.027). Cholecystectomy performed during pregnancy was associated with a low surgical complication rate, identical to that seen in postpartum procedures (12% vs. 10%; n = 2/17 vs. n = 3/30; p > 0.999). Preterm birth occurred in 7.1% (n = 1/14) of patients with cholecystectomy versus 11% (n = 5/45) without. Fetal loss after surgery occurred only in the first trimester (n = 3/17 vs. n = 1/49). No difference was seen in readmission (5%, n = 1/21 vs. 27%, n = 4/15; p = 0.138), fetal loss (5%, n = 1/21 vs. 27%, n = 4/15; p = 0.138) and preterm birth (6%, n = 1/17 vs. 8%, n = 1/12; p > 0.999) between the surgical and ERCP groups. The fetal loss (9.1%, n = 2/22 vs. 5.4%, n = 4/74; p = 0.618) and preterm birth rates (5.9%, n = 1/17 vs. 12%, n = 8/65; p = 0.677) did not significantly differ between patients with and without ERCP during pregnancy.ConclusionCholecystectomy is effective and safe in pregnant patients during the second or third trimester in cases of mild biliary pancreatitis. ERCP is safe in any trimester.

Introduction

Acute biliary pancreatitis in pregnant patients is a rare condition; however, it poses a challenge for clinicians [1]. Pregnancy is a risk factor for gallstone formation due to elevated levels of circulating estrogen and progesterone, which contribute to cholestasis and the supersaturation of bile with cholesterol. Symptomatic gallstone disease during pregnancy is a leading cause of non‐obstetric hospitalizations and surgical interventions, and is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth [2, 3]. The incidence of acute pancreatitis (AP) during pregnancy ranges from 1 in 1000 to 1 in 4000 pregnancies [4]. Gallstone disease is the leading cause of AP during pregnancy, accounting for over 65% of cases, with most instances occurring in the third trimester or postpartum period [5].

Advancements in maternal and neonatal care, as well as early diagnosis, have contributed to a reduction in maternal and fetal mortality rates reported in recent studies [6]. A recent meta‐analysis showed that the overall maternal mortality rate was 2.8%, comparable to the general population; the pooled fetal mortality rate was 12.3% [7]. In another study, the risk of preterm labor was 3.5 times higher in women with AP [7, 8]. Compared to other surgical emergencies (e.g., appendicitis, cholecystitis) during pregnancy, AP still has one of the highest rates of fetal loss [7].

Early cholecystectomy is recommended in nonpregnant patients after mild biliary pancreatitis to prevent recurrence and reduce other complications associated with gallstone [9]. Pregnancy has been identified as an independent risk factor for 30‐day readmission following acute pancreatitis, with readmission rates twice as high as in nonpregnant women and a recurrence rate of up to 50% reported during the first trimester [10, 11]. Guidelines recommend laparoscopic cholecystectomy for symptomatic biliary disease during pregnancy, irrespective of trimester, and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for urgent indications such as choledocholithiasis or cholangitis, preferably in the second trimester with fetal radiation minimized. Despite these recommendations, pregnant women with gallstone‐related AP are less likely to undergo cholecystectomy or ERCP compared to nonpregnant counterparts. Although cholecystectomy is considered safe and may reduce readmissions, its optimal timing remains uncertain, and clinicians often hesitate to perform cholecystectomy during pregnancy. The aim of the study was to analyze current international practices and outcomes of managing biliary AP in pregnant patients, focusing on the role of cholecystectomy.

Methods

International Cohort

The research project was conducted under the Systems Education education‐research model, coordinated by the Centre of Translational Medicine at Semmelweis University and the Hungarian Pancreatic Study Group [10, 11]. The study aimed to assess data from multiple centers on the safety and effectiveness of cholecystectomy during pregnancy, following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist (Table S1) [12]. We sent an invitation letter to members of the International Association of Pancreatology and the European Pancreatic Club in March 2023. A total of 19 centers from 14 countries participated in this retrospective cohort study. The National Center for Public Health approved the study under the number 17787‐8/2020/EÜIG.

The study population consisted of pregnant patients diagnosed with acute biliary pancreatitis who had been treated in participating centers since January 2011. To ensure comprehensive data capture and minimize selection bias, centers were instructed to consecutively enroll all patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the specified timeframe. Inclusion criteria were based on the Atlanta criteria, and a biliary cause was suspected if gallstones, biliary sludge, or a dilated common bile duct were identified via imaging, or biochemical signs of cholestasis were present [13]. Exclusion criteria were chronic pancreatitis, postpartum pancreatitis, and pancreatitis of non‐biliary etiology.

Maternal complications recorded in the survey included maternal mortality, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome, placental abruption, antepartum hemorrhage, and eclampsia. Fetal outcomes were evaluated based on fetal loss, preterm birth (defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation), the need for admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and fetal complications such as reduced movement, abnormal heart rate or rhythm, structural abnormalities, and acid‐base imbalances. Intraoperative complications considered during the study were trocar site bleeding, liver bed injury, bile leakage, bleeding, spilled gallstones, and common bile duct injury. Postoperative complications tracked included wound infection, bleeding, and bile leaks.

The data collection sheet included questions on demographic information, clinical presentation, management strategies, timing of cholecystectomy, procedural approaches (ERCP), complications, and outcomes such as 1‐year readmission rates due to recurrent AP, other gallstone‐related complications, and maternal and fetal outcomes. The severity of AP was classified according to the modified Atlanta classification [14]. The detailed questionnaire is available in the supplementary documents (Table S2). Additional methodological details are provided in Supporting Information S1.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistical summaries of our data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) values for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. To compare our data based on each grouping, we used the Welch Two Sample t‐test for continuous variables, and Pearson's Chi‐squared test for categorical variables if expected frequencies were sufficiently high; otherwise, Fisher's exact test was applied. All results were considered significant only if our p‐value was < 0.05. All analyses were performed in the R statistical environment (R version 4.4.2, R Core Team (2024)). Missing data were systematically cataloged in a data quality table (Table S3).

Results

Basic Characteristics of AP

A total of 101 cases were analyzed from 14 countries and 19 centers. The basic characteristics are shown in Figure 1, Tables S4, S5, and Figure S1.

The median age of the patients was 31 years (Q25–75 27–35). Nearly half of the patients were pregnant for the first time (44%). The median gestational age was 25 weeks (Q25–75 14–31). In terms of pancreatitis severity, 86% (n = 87/101) had mild AP, 9% (n = 9/101) had moderate AP, and 5% (n = 5/101) had severe forms. Besides pancreatitis, 14% (n = 14/101) of the patients also had cholecystitis, 7% (n = 7/101) had cholangitis, 3% (n = 3/101) had both conditions, and 1% (n = 1/101) had peritonitis. The median length of hospitalization (LOH) was 6 days (Q25–75 4–9.5) (Figure 2), with a readmission rate of 17% (n = 16/95) within 1 year. A total of 29 cholecystectomies were performed during pregnancy, and 49 postpartum, most of which were scheduled within the first 3 months after delivery (Figure S2). The timing of each procedure to hospital admission is detailed in Table S6 and Figure S2. See Supporting Information S1 for more clinical details of the analyzed population (Figure S3).

Acute pancreatitis characteristics. Details on the clinical course of acute pancreatitis during pregnancy, including severity classification (a), length of hospital stay (b), occurrence of local complications (c), and concomitant diagnoses (d). AP, acute pancreatitis.

Of the patients, 37% (37/100) had a prior history of symptomatic cholelithiasis, including 11 cases (34%) reporting an episode 6 months before their pancreatitis (Figure S4).

Among the patients, 23% (23/97) had choledocholithiasis, and 67% had multiple gallbladder stones (Figure S5). Gallstones or sludge were identified in 101 patients in total—97 (96%) via imaging, 2 (2%) through ERCP, and 2 (2%) during surgical procedures. ERCP was performed in 22 cases, 16 patients of which (73%) had choledocholithiasis and 9 (41%) cholangitis (Figure S6). Fetal mortality was reported in 6.2% (n = 6/97), with five cases occurring before or during the 20th week of gestation and one postnatally (Table S6). Additionally, 12% (n = 10/83) of births were preterm. There were four cases of premature membrane rupture (n = 4/83), no instances of amniotic infection (n = 0/83), and no cases of HELLP syndrome (n = 0/100). There was no maternal mortality (n = 0/101).

Effectiveness and Safety of Cholecystectomy in Mild Biliary AP

During pregnancy, 17 patients with mild biliary pancreatitis underwent cholecystectomy, whereas 50 patients were managed conservatively. Patients who underwent cholecystectomy during pregnancy had no readmissions for biliary complications within 1 year after AP (0%, n = 0/17), whereas 24% (n = 12/49; p = 0.027) of patients in the non‐cholecystectomy group were readmitted, most commonly due to recurrent AP (n = 8/48) or due to cholecystitis or cholangitis (Figure 3).

Readmission rates after mild biliary pancreatitis within 1 year (e), recurrent pancreatitis (f), cholecystitis (g), and cholangitis (h) in women with cholecystectomy during pregnancy. Statistical analysis was performed using Fisher's exact test for readmission within 1 year (p = 0.027) and for recurrent acute pancreatitis (p = 0.094).

There was no difference in preterm birth rate between the cholecystectomy and non‐cholecystectomy groups (7.1%, n = 1/14 vs. 11%, n = 5/45; p > 0.999). The cholecystectomy group had a slightly lower rate of neonatal intensive care admission (0%, n = 0/13 vs. 2.5%, n = 1/42; p > 0.999). Although the cholecystectomy group showed a marginally higher rate of fetal loss (18%; n = 3/17 vs. 2%, n = 1/49; p = 0.050), all fetal losses occurred when cholecystectomy was performed in the first trimester (Figure 4). The median age was similar in the two groups (33 [31; 34] vs. 30 [26; 33] years; p = 0.154), while the median gestational age at the time of index admission was significantly lower among patients who had a cholecystectomy (15 [Q25–75 8; 26] vs. 26 [Q25–75 17; 31]; p = 0.008). Length of hospitalization was comparable between the groups; the median LOH was (6 [Q25–75 4; 8] and 5 [Q25–75 3; 6] days; p = 0.341).

Preterm birth (a), NICU admission (b), and fetal mortality (c) in women after mild biliary pancreatitis, without cholecystectomy during pregnancy. Preterm birth (d), NICU admission (e), and fetal mortality (f) in women after mild biliary pancreatitis, with cholecystectomy during pregnancy. Statistical analysis was performed using Fisher's exact test for preterm birth (p > 0.999), for NICU admission (p > 0.999), and for fetal loss (p = 0.050). NICU, neonatal intensive care unit.

In patients with mild biliary pancreatitis, the rates of intraoperative complications were similar, regardless of whether cholecystectomy was performed during pregnancy or after pregnancy (12%, n = 2/17 and vs. 10%, n = 3/30; p > 0.999); the rates of postoperative complications were also similar between the two groups (0%, n = 0/17 vs. 0%, n = 0/30; p > 0.999) (Figure S7). These findings show that cholecystectomy is a safe and effective strategy to prevent biliary complications in patients with mild biliary AP when performed in the second or third trimester. However, its safety during the first trimester remains uncertain, as all observed fetal losses occurred in this period.

ERCP in Patients With Biliary Pancreatitis

ERCP was performed in 22 cases of biliary pancreatitis; nine patients (40.9%, n = 9/22) had choledocholithiasis alone, eight (36.4%, n = 8/22) had both choledocholithiasis and cholangitis, and one (4.5%, n = 1/22) had cholangitis alone. See Supporting Information S1 for more details. The readmission rates (23%, n = 5/22 vs. 15%, n = 11/72; p = 0.517), fetal loss rates (9.1%, n = 2/22 vs. 5.4%, n = 4/74; p = 0.618) and preterm birth rates (5.9%, n = 1/17 vs. 12%, n = 8/65; p = 0.677) did not differ significantly between patients who had ERCP during pregnancy compared with those who did not (Figures S8–S10). In the ERCP group, one fetal loss occurred in a patient with severe AP, complicated by abdominal compartment syndrome; a few days after discharge, the patient was readmitted due to complications of the index episode; the other fetal loss occurred in the first trimester after discharge, with no apparent link to AP or ERCP (Table S6). The median gestational age was slightly lower in patients who had ERCP (19 [Q25–75 11, 27] vs. 25 [Q25–75 14, 31] weeks; p = 0.221). Post‐ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) was diagnosed only in one case (n = 1/22); there was no other complication, such as bleeding or perforation, observed (n = 0/22). These findings suggest that ERCP can be safely performed during pregnancy when clinically indicated.

Effectiveness and Safety of Cholecystectomy Compared to ERCP

In the subgroup analysis comparing ERCP and cholecystectomy performed during pregnancy, there were no significant differences in readmission (5%, n = 1/21 vs. 27%, n = 4/15; p = 0.138), preterm birth (6%, n = 1/17 vs. 8%, n = 1/12; p > 0.999), or fetal mortality rates (14%, n = 3/22 vs. 7%, n = 1/15; p = 0.633) did not differ significantly (Figure 5, Figure S10). The median gestational age at admission did not differ between the two interventions (15 [8; 22] vs. 17 [11; 29] years; p = 0.287). Cholecystectomy during pregnancy was performed in seven of the 22 patients who underwent ERCP, and these cases were excluded from the subgroup analysis to prevent overlap between treatment groups.

Discussion

In this retrospective multicenter cohort study, we found that pregnant patients who underwent cholecystectomy after a mild biliary AP during pregnancy had lower readmission rates within 1 year. Almost all readmissions were due to symptomatic gallbladder disease, and the most common cause was recurrent AP. There was no significant difference in the rate of preterm birth; however, a slightly increased incidence of fetal loss was noted in patients who underwent surgery during pregnancy.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies that have shown that conservative management of symptomatic gallbladder disease is associated with an increased risk of hospital readmissions and adverse pregnancy outcomes [15, 16]. Luthra et al. reported that ERCP lowered the odds of early readmission by 60%, and that same‐admission cholecystectomy further reduced it by 85% [16]. Hedström et al. reported that 56% of pregnant patients treated conservatively for gallbladder disease required surgical intervention within 2 years of delivery [17]. Furthermore, Jorge et al. noted that patients with uncomplicated symptomatic cholelithiasis who do not undergo operative management during pregnancy frequently require postpartum cholecystectomy, often following at least one hospitalization for recurrent or persistent symptoms [2]. As 43.48% of the patients had a prior episode of symptomatic cholelithiasis, with 21 patients having an episode 6 months before their hospitalization due to AP, this highlights the importance of elective cholecystectomy in women with symptomatic cholelithiasis before pregnancy to prevent recurrent episodes during pregnancy. Multiparity, a recognized risk factor for cholelithiasis, was observed in 57.14% of patients [5].

Most cases of AP occur during the second and third trimesters, potentially due to physiological changes that contribute to gallstone formation, which significantly increases after the first trimester. Elevated abdominal pressure may also contribute to this condition [18]. Additionally, multiple small gallstones are recognized as a risk factor for AP [19]. The severity distribution and clinical course of AP were similar to those in the general population, implying that pregnancy itself does not significantly affect the outcome of AP. In our study, 30.3% of patients received antibiotics, most commonly justified by coexisting diagnoses (24.75%), which is consistent with the wide international variability (31%–82%) in antibiotic use for acute pancreatitis [20]. Mortality associated with AP has declined in the general population in the last decades, with 80% of cases remaining mild [21]. In a recent meta‐analysis, Hughes et al. reported a similar mortality rate among pregnant patients and in the general population with AP; however, the fetal mortality rate, only slightly improved, was still 12.6% [7]. By comparison, the fetal mortality rate in the European Union in 2021 was 2.3 per 1000 births in the general population [22].

Our study found no statistically significant difference in the rate of fetal loss between pregnant patients undergoing cholecystectomy versus non‐operative management. However, although not statistically significant, the observed trend toward higher fetal loss in the first trimester may reflect a risk that our study was underpowered to detect due to the limited sample size. One other explanation for these findings is that previous studies have indicated that AP in earlier gestational weeks is associated with higher fetal mortality [23, 24]. A recent meta‐analysis reported a fetal death rate of 20.9% when AP occurred in the first trimester, compared to 12.4% in the second trimester and 12.0% in the third trimester [7]. It is also important to note that miscarriage rates in the first trimester are relatively high in the general population, occurring in approximately 12%–15% of recognized pregnancies by 20 weeks of gestation [25].

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is considered safe for the treatment of symptomatic gallbladder disease in any trimester; the second trimester is regarded as the safest time for cholecystectomy, as organogenesis is complete, and the uterus is not yet large enough to obstruct visibility [26, 27].

Cholecystectomy in the third trimester has been associated with higher rates of fetal loss and preterm birth in some studies, whereas other studies show comparable preterm birth rates when comparing conservative and surgical approaches [17, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Hantouli et al. also noted that, when considering pregnancy trimesters and non‐operative management, cholecystectomy during pregnancy was associated with reduced rates of fetal loss and preterm birth [32].

ERCP, considered safe during pregnancy, is best performed by a multidisciplinary team at high‐volume tertiary care facilities [33]. In our study, ERCP was most often performed for clear indications (cholangitis or choledocholithiasis), and nearly all patients underwent sphincterotomy; however, it did not significantly reduce readmissions or recurrent pancreatitis compared with surgery. In line with the APEC trial, Schepers et al. demonstrated that urgent ERCP did not improve the outcome of patients with gallstone pancreatitis without concomitant cholangitis, supporting a restrictive approach to ERCP during pregnancy [34]. Although pregnancy is an independent risk factor for PEP, there are some controversies [33, 35]. Studies conducted before 2010 reported a higher incidence of PEP than more recent studies; Tang et al. reported an incidence of 16%, while the nationwide register‐based study by Hedström et al. found no cases [36, 37]. In our study, there was only one case of PEP.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides critical multidisciplinary data on managing biliary AP during pregnancy. However, because of the retrospective design of this study, potential confounders, such as comorbidities, socioeconomic status, nutrition, anesthesia techniques, and obstetric management strategies, may have been missed. Given the multicenter international design, variations in surgical timing, ERCP protocols, and supportive obstetric care may have influenced outcomes. The study only reports short‐term neonatal outcomes, long‐term developmental outcomes, and health status of infants born to these patients remain unknown. Additionally, although this cohort includes a relatively larger number of cases, the limited sample size reduces the statistical power and the applicability of the findings. While a randomized controlled trial could theoretically provide higher‐level evidence, conducting such a study in pregnant patients would be nearly impossible due to significant ethical concerns and practical challenges in obtaining informed consent in this vulnerable population.

Implications for Practice

Translating new scientific results into daily practice is pivotal [38, 39]. Based on the findings of this retrospective international multicenter cohort study, (1) in cases of mild biliary AP during pregnancy, cholecystectomy can be performed in the second and third trimesters, and (2) ERCP can be safely performed during any trimester.

Implications for Research

At least two important areas for future research have been identified: (1) Prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to better define the risk of fetal loss explicitly associated with first‐trimester surgery, controlling for gestational age and disease severity. (2) Longitudinal studies should assess long‐term maternal and neonatal outcomes following intra‐gestational cholecystectomy.

Conclusion

Cholecystectomy during pregnancy is a safe and effective intervention for preventing recurrent biliary events in patients with mild biliary pancreatitis when performed in the second or third trimester. In contrast, its safety in the first trimester remains uncertain due to a potentially higher risk of fetal loss and limited data. ERCP is safe during all trimesters but does not appear to reduce readmission rates.
