Organisational responses to patient complaints in healthcare service delivery in low- and middle-income countries: a scoping review
Authors: Suleyman Mohammed Arage, Mirkuzie Woldie, Adane Kebede, Lake Yazachew, Asmamaw Atnafu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatient complaints reveal gaps in quality, safety and patient-centred care, yet many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) health systems respond inadequately, resulting in dissatisfaction, disengagement and ongoing service problems.ObjectivesThis scoping review maps the causes of patient complaints, evaluates existing organisational response mechanisms and policies and identifies gaps in the current evidence.MethodsThe study was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute scoping review methodology. It focused on the causes of complaints, organisational response mechanisms, existing policies and frameworks, limitations and recommendations identified in the literature. A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Advanced Google Scholar and other grey literature sources. Study selection and screening were performed using Rayyan software, with two independent reviewers applying predefined eligibility criteria. Data were extracted using a standardised table and the findings were presented through tables, graphs and narrative synthesis. Reporting adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.ResultsThe review includes 37 studies from various regions. These studies found that the main cause of complaint was categorised into five main domains, mainly management, followed by relationship, clinical, environmental, patient rights and others. Moreover, regarding response mechanisms, the review highlights complaint response mechanisms in healthcare that operate across three interlinked levels: national, institutional and frontline care, each with distinct approaches. Furthermore, regarding policy, the review showed that there are a few national-level and institutional-level policies on complaint handling. However, there needs to be greater focus on methodological diversity research in LMIC, as suggested by the literature on limitations and recommendations.ConclusionsThis scoping review identified gaps in evidence on patient complaints, organisational responses and policies, providing a foundation for research on healthcare complaints in LMICs.

Introduction

Unsafe care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) causes about 134 million adverse events and 2.6 million deaths each year, with a social cost of US$1–2 trillion.1 Patients often respond through complaints, defined as expressions of dissatisfaction with healthcare services (eg, staff behaviour, communication, treatment or access) submitted verbally, in writing or via other channels and requiring a response. A grievance is a formal written or unresolved verbal complaint about care, abuse, neglect or hospital services.2

Patient complaints are mainly directed at private practitioners or hospitals with the aim of both patient-centred responses and system-level improvements,3 4 providing valuable insights that identify risks, high-risk areas (‘hot spots’) and overlooked areas (‘blind spots’) to inform targeted quality and safety improvements.5

Complaint management is an essential part of healthcare, as complaints provide insights for improving standards.6 Effective complaint management involves collecting, analysing and resolving concerns, which enhances satisfaction and loyalty.7 Though often stressful, complaints are valuable when used as learning opportunities.8 Health facilities are expected to provide timely, safe and compassionate care; when these expectations are unmet, patients often raise justified complaints that highlight areas for improvement.9 Addressing such complaints systematically through clear policies and structures enhances both organisational performance and quality of care.10

Even though complaints are increasingly recognised as a potentially valuable source of information for improving healthcare quality, costs, waiting times, patient safety and employee satisfaction,11 health systems often fail to meet patient and staff expectations in responding to concerns and complaints, and poor responses have been linked to organisational failures. Understanding why health systems struggle to address concerns and why complainants remain dissatisfied is essential.12

Although patient complaints are increasing, they offer valuable opportunities for improvement; yet major gaps persist, illustrated by the contrast between managers’ high care ratings (80%) and patients’ very low satisfaction (8%).13 Prioritising complaints that signal quality or safety risks helps organisations enhance patient safety, guide service improvements, reduce legal risks and strengthen patient–provider collaboration, ultimately improving satisfaction and outcomes.14 The review seeks to synthesise findings from diverse studies to identify key themes, strategies and outcomes related to patient complaint handling.

Review question

What is the existing evidence on the causes and types of patient complaints and the organisational responses and policies addressing these complaints within health systems of LMICs?

Methods and materials

The following stages were (1) identification of the research question, (2) identification of relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) data charting, (5) collation, summarisation and reporting of results and (6) consultation with key stakeholders to communicate and discuss the findings from the review.15 This scoping review followed Joanna Briggs Institute’s guidelines for scoping reviews, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used for reporting,16 17 and it was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF). (https://osf.io/9df2t/). The selection criteria were guided by the Population, Concept and Context (PCC) framework.18

Eligibility criteria

This study used the PCC framework in conducting the scoping review, which allowed us to clearly define the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study population in this review consisted of patients and healthcare users regardless of their disease or sociodemographic factors (population), as well as studies that explored the causes and types of patient complaints, organisational response mechanisms and related policies (concept). Regarding context, this review included studies conducted in any health system of LMICs (primary, secondary and tertiary healthcare systems), as well as studies that employed different approaches, including qualitative and/or quantitative methods and other study designs, whether primary or secondary. Moreover, this study included publications in any language between 1 January 2015 and 2 August 2025. We extract data from those with other languages from their abstract section since they put their abstract section in English. Only research involving human subjects was considered.

Search strategy

Articles published between 1 August 2015 and 2 August 2025 were retrieved from major databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science (WOS) and Research4Life using a standard search strategy. In addition, grey literature published in Google and Google Scholar was considered. In the advanced database searches, the search strategy was built based on the terms mentioned above, using “Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)” and “All fields,” and combining terms with the Boolean operators “*,” “AND” and “OR” as appropriate

Study selection

First, articles accessed from databases and electronic search engines were downloaded into the EndNote library and exported to the Rayyan software, where duplicates were removed. The records were then screened by title and abstract by two reviewers. Unrelated topics and irrelevant articles were excluded. Finally, the remaining studies’ abstracts and full texts were reviewed in their entirety to identify the articles included in the final review.

Data extraction

Once eligible studies were identified, the full texts of these potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility by two reviewers (SMA and LY). Any disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion. Relevant data were then extracted using a prepared template in Microsoft Excel. The summary table contained a list of items on study characteristics, including the primary author’s name, year of publication, country, study setting, study design, number and types of complaints, results, limitations and recommendations (online supplemental file 1).

Data synthesis

The findings of this scoping review were presented using narratives, tables and figures. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive numerical summaries, while qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis techniques with an inductive approach.

Results

The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (figure 1) summarises the study selection process. A total of 1651 records were identified from databases and 131 from other sources. After removing 70 duplicates, 1581 records were screened, of which 132 were excluded at the title and abstract level. Full texts of 238 articles were assessed, with 201 excluded for predefined reasons. Finally, 37 studies were included in the review.

Cause of patient complaint in healthcare service delivery

Based on the complaint domain

Among the 37 articles included, 21 (56.76%) identified the cause of the patient’s complaint. Across the included studies, complaints were categorised into five main domains with varying magnitudes and emphasis. Clinical care-related complaints were reported in 10 studies, ranging from 3% to 38%.910 14 2224 28 29 45 48 Clinical care complaints mainly involved inadequate treatment,9 10 14 22 28 29 45 patient safety errors,102224 service process issues and unauthorised practices.10 48

Management issues represented the broadest domain documented in 14 studies, with a magnitude range of 7% to 70%.81019 Management complaints mainly stemmed from bureaucratic issues,8 19 20 23 24 29 38 48 resource shortages,1921 access and scheduling problems,20 43 financial disputes10 20 and weak complaint-handling systems.8 24 33

Relationship and communication problems appeared in 10 studies, with magnitudes ranging between 6% and 54%.19 20 22 25 26 28 29 37 38 45 Relationship and communication complaints mainly involved provider rudeness,20 25 45 uncaring behaviour,28 45 miscommunication19 20 22 38 and insufficient information.26 38

Environmental and infrastructural issues were identified in 4 studies, consistently ranging between 19% and 46%.19 21 23 43 Environmental complaints primarily involved poor facility conditions26 38 and inadequate infrastructure maintenance.19 43

Patient rights and equity concerns were less frequently documented, appearing in only 5 studies with magnitudes of 3% to 27%.20 26 28 29 37 Patient rights complaints mainly involved disrespect of dignity and autonomy,28 29 inadequate information and consent and occasional discrimination.20

Finally, barriers to complaints were discussed in two studies, though only qualitatively.8 43 These included structural barriers, such as limited channels and weak monitoring systems, and cultural or social barriers, such as low awareness, power imbalances and unresponsiveness from providers or institutions.8 43

Based on level of care, ownership and focus area (facility and system-related issue)

Of the 37 studies reviewed, 21 (56.8%) identified the origins of patient complaints. In tertiary hospitals, which are predominantly public institutions, complaints were mainly linked to the facility and focused on clinical care aspects, including insufficient treatment, patient safety incidents and issues with service delivery processes.9 10 14 22 45 In secondary hospitals, also largely public, complaints were facility-related and included management and patient-provider communication concerns that extended beyond clinical care.78 19 2427 29 32 37 39 41 Studies that included a mix of hospital levels reported similar facility-related management and communication complaints.33

At the primary care and health centre level, complaints were mainly facility-related, focusing on management deficiencies, relationship and communication problems and, in some cases, environmental issues such as poor infrastructure and inadequate maintenance.20 34 43 49 Private hospitals reported complaints related to relationship and communication issues, as well as occasional patient rights concerns.19

At the administrative or system level, complaints were system-related, covering broader management and governance issues, such as bureaucratic barriers, resource shortages, weak complaint-handling systems, patient rights concerns and structural and cultural barriers to lodging complaints.21 31 42 47 Across all levels, common complaint domains included clinical care deficiencies, management problems, provider–patient communication gaps, environmental and infrastructural shortcomings and patient rights violations. Structural and social barriers, such as limited channels for complaint submission, low awareness and provider unresponsiveness, were highlighted in two studies.8 47

Based on the organisational response mechanism to patient complaints

Organisational responses to patient complaints operate across system-level and facility-level structures, with variations observed by level of care and ownership. At the administrative or system level, which is predominantly public and system-related, response mechanisms are largely driven by national and regulatory frameworks. These include formal actions such as providing explanations, issuing resolutions or refusals and referring cases to appropriate authorities, alongside legal and judicial pathways. System-level responses also involve regulatory oversight, national quality assurance frameworks and centralised complaint offices or monitoring systems that coordinate grievance handling across the health system.1 14 23 46 48 These mechanisms reflect a structured and policy-driven approach, often addressing broader governance, accountability and patient rights issues.

At the facility level, particularly in hospital settings (secondary and tertiary care), which are mostly public, response mechanisms are primarily facility-related and institutional. These include disciplinary measures such as warnings, establishment of communication centres to improve doctor–patient interaction and operational adjustments like modifying physicians’ schedules to enhance service responsiveness. Hospitals also implement routine monitoring and evaluation systems, staff training programmes and adherence to standard operating procedures (SOPs). In addition, multiple complaint channels such as telephone, email and social media are widely used, and some institutions integrate complaint handling into total quality management systems for continuous improvement.19 20 22 27 35 In mixed or private hospital settings, similar facility-based mechanisms are observed, often with additional emphasis on service quality improvement and patient satisfaction.19 33

At the primary care and health centre level, which is largely public and facility-based, organisational responses focus on strengthening accessibility and responsiveness through simpler and more decentralised mechanisms. These include establishing complaint systems within primary healthcare networks, improving communication channels and enhancing service delivery processes through monitoring and feedback systems.20 35 36 43 However, these settings may face limitations related to resource constraints and weaker institutional capacity compared with hospitals.

At the front-line care level, across all facility types, responses are more immediate, interpersonal and facility-related. Common approaches include providing explanations or apologies, resolving complaints directly, often within short timeframes, referring cases to relevant departments and using mediation or therapeutic communication strategies. Despite these efforts, gaps remain, as approximately one-quarter of complaints receive no formal action or follow-up, highlighting weaknesses in implementation and accountability at the point of care.14 20 21 37 Generally, while system-level responses emphasise governance, regulation and oversight, facility-level responses across hospitals and primary care focus more on operational, managerial and interpersonal mechanisms. The findings suggest that effective complaint management requires stronger integration between system-level policies and frontline implementation to ensure consistent, timely and patient-centred responses across all levels of care.

Existing policy and regulatory framework

At the national level, Vietnam demonstrates strong legal backing, where citizens have a constitutional right to lodge grievances, formalised in the Law on Grievances (2011). This framework stipulates specific timelines, with some sources noting a maximum of 90 days for resolution while others highlight a shorter, 30-working-day deadline.42 These provisions indicate that Vietnam has clear legal timelines for complaint handling, reflecting formal recognition of patients’ rights. In Tanzania, a National Guide for Complaint Management in Health Facilities has been developed, outlining structured procedures at the facility level.46

At the institutional/hospital level, evidence from India and Indonesia illustrates how facilities integrate grievance policies into operational management. In India, hospital policies emphasise core grievance redressal principles such as accountability, transparency, promptness, fairness, trustworthiness and credibility.47 In Indonesia, hospitals adopt formal complaint policies, including SOPs, staff assignments and periodic policy updates every 5 years.27 Additional operational measures, such as reward/punishment systems, staff scheduling adjustments, budgeting and training, demonstrate an effort to embed complaint handling into hospital governance and staff management structures.19

Discussion

This review mapped evidence on the causes, types of patient complaints, the organisational responses and policies addressing these complaints within health systems of LMICs. The findings highlight important geographical, methodological and system-level gaps in the current literature. Evidence is heavily skewed towards Asian and upper-middle-income countries,710 14 1923 27 28 33 with very limited representation from low-income settings and notably no studies identified in the Ethiopian context. This limits the generalisability of findings and underscores the need for context-specific research, particularly in underrepresented health systems.

In terms of study design and setting, the review demonstrates a strong dominance of facility-based studies, particularly hospital settings, with 22 of the 37 studies conducted in hospitals, compared with only a small proportion focusing on health centres (primary care) and national or system-level settings. Furthermore, only a limited number of studies employed mixed methods, experimental designs or policy/legal analyses,194145 49 indicating a reliance on descriptive and retrospective approaches. This imbalance suggests that current evidence is largely micro-level and facility-focused, with insufficient attention to system-level dynamics, governance and policy implementation. The review also revealed that overall, there is a noticeable increase in publications (nine)22 24 29 31 32 34 35 41 42 during 2024, suggesting a growing interest in this area of research; however, the depth and diversity of methodologies remain limited.

Regarding data sources, the evidence is predominantly patient-centred and record-based, with only a few studies incorporating perspectives from providers, managers or governance actors.1 27 31 41 42 44 46 This narrow focus restricts a comprehensive understanding of complaint management systems, particularly in relation to organisational responses and accountability mechanisms. Given that complaint handling is inherently a multi-level process involving patients, providers and institutions, future research should adopt more integrated and multistakeholder approaches.

The findings further reveal that the causes of patient complaints are largely facility-related, particularly within hospital settings. Management-related issues were the most frequently reported, especially bureaucratic inefficiencies, administrative delays and weak complaint-handling systems.8 19 20 23 24 29 38 48 Clinical care problems, primarily inadequate treatment and patient safety concerns, were also prominent, particularly in secondary and tertiary care settings.9 10 14 22 28 29 45 Additionally, provider behaviour and communication issues, such as disrespect and poor interaction, were consistently reported across both hospital and primary care levels.20 25 45 However, relatively less attention is given to system-level causes, such as structural barriers, governance failures and cultural factors influencing complaint behaviour. Issues such as limited complaint channels, low awareness and power imbalances are rarely quantified, indicating a critical gap in understanding the broader determinants of patient complaints.

In terms of organisational response mechanisms, the review highlights a clear distinction between system-level and facility-level responses. At the national or system level, responses are predominantly policy-driven and regulatory, including explanations, resolutions, referrals and legal pathways, supported by regulatory frameworks and centralised complaint systems.1 14 23 46 48 These mechanisms are essential for ensuring accountability and standardisation, but often remain detached from frontline realities. At the institutional (facility) level, particularly in hospitals, responses are more operational and managerial, including disciplinary actions, staff training, communication channels and integration of complaint management into quality improvement systems.20 22 At the frontline level, responses are more interpersonal and immediate, such as apologies, explanations and direct resolutions.20 21 However, a significant proportion of complaints receive no formal follow-up, indicating gaps in implementation and monitoring.

Regarding the existing policy and regulatory framework, a few studies examine the existing policies and regulatory framework. The evidence revealed that most studies lack evaluation of barriers and enablers of policy implementation and limited research on whether these operational measures improve patient satisfaction. Overall, the review suggests that complaint management systems in LMICs are fragmented, with stronger emphasis on facility-level responses and weaker integration with system-level governance and policy frameworks. The lack of comprehensive evaluation, particularly regarding effectiveness, patient satisfaction and system-level impact, limits the potential of complaint data to inform quality improvement.

The limitations and recommendations given by the literature are also included in this review, even though some of them do not include their limitations. Key limitations across studies include limited evidence across settings and populations, data and methodological gaps, narrow perspectives, weak policy and system-level evaluation.1 22 38 42 Based on their limitations the recommend different stakeholders, basically for future researchers to conduct multicentre and mixed-methods studies to examine demographics, system-level factors, complaint drivers and patient satisfaction to improve complaint handling and examine macro-level policies, governance and legal frameworks to understand their impact on complaint management.719 2224 30 33

Identified gaps and implications of the review

The review findings offer valuable insights that can guide researchers and practitioners in shaping their approach to complaints and responses to those complaints to improve the quality of healthcare service. Several key gaps were identified, which can inform future research and practice. A major gap relates to the geographical distribution of evidence, which is heavily concentrated in Asian and upper-middle-income countries, with very limited representation from low-income settings and no studies identified in the Ethiopian context. This limits contextual understanding of complaint systems in resource-constrained environments. There is a clear need for more research in low-income countries focusing on context-specific causes of complaints, response mechanisms and policy implementation to address unique systemic challenges.

The evidence base is also predominantly facility-based and hospital-centred, particularly at secondary and tertiary levels of care of public hospitals, with limited attention to private hospitals, primary healthcare facilities such as health centres and to administrative or system-level settings. Considering that primary care serves as the main entry point to health systems in many LMICs, this imbalance creates a critical knowledge gap. Future research should adopt multilevel and multicentre approaches to better capture variations across different levels of care and health system structures.

Another important gap lies in the type of data and perspectives used. Most studies rely on patient records and patient-reported data sources, with minimal inclusion of providers, managers and policymakers. This narrow perspective limits a comprehensive understanding of complaint management processes.

Although various organisational response mechanisms are documented across national, institutional and frontline levels, there is limited evidence on their effectiveness. Existing studies tend to describe response processes such as explanations, disciplinary actions and communication channels without evaluating whether these responses lead to improved patient satisfaction or service utilisation. This highlights the need for incorporating patient-centred outcome measures, particularly satisfaction with complaint handling, to better assess the impact of these mechanisms.

In addition, only a few studies explore existing policy and regulatory frameworks related to patient complaints. Evidence suggests that some countries have formal systems with defined procedures and institutional guidelines; however, there is limited assessment of how these policies are implemented in practice, including the barriers and facilitators influencing their effectiveness. This points to a gap between policy design and implementation, especially in resource-constrained settings, and underscores the importance of examining governance, accountability and system responsiveness.

Overall, the review indicates that complaint management systems in LMICs remain fragmented and largely facility-focused, with weak integration between system-level governance and frontline practice. Strengthening healthcare quality requires the development of integrated, multilevel complaint systems that ensure accessible reporting mechanisms, effective response processes and accountability across all levels of care.

Strengths and limitations of the review

This review had several strengths, including a comprehensive and systematic approach using multiple databases and grey literature to capture a wide range of studies from LMICs. Adherence to a preregistered protocol, the use of predefined inclusion criteria and independent screening by two reviewers enhanced transparency and reduced selection bias. The review also provides a multidimensional synthesis of complaint causes, response mechanisms and policy frameworks across different levels of care. However, some limitations exist. Restricted access to certain sources, such as government websites, may have led to missing relevant studies. The exclusion of non-English publications may have introduced language bias, and no critical appraisal of included studies was conducted, which may affect the interpretation of findings.

Conclusions

This scoping review identified key evidence gaps and highlights underexplored areas and supports the development of an evidence-informed study design. The findings also have important implications for policy and practice by informing the development of more effective, integrated and patient-centred complaint management systems. Overall, the review enhances understanding of patient complaints and will guide context-specific research and improvements.
