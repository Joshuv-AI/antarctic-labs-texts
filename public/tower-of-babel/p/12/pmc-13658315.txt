High-throughput monitoring of in vitro enzymatic reactions using probe electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry
Authors: Nobuyuki Okahashi, Takeo Taniguchi, Takuma Suzuki, Takanari Hattori, Hidenori Takahashi, Kohsuke Honda, Fumio Matsuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Probe electrospray ionization-mass spectrometry (PESI-MS) is an ambient ionization technique that enables rapid and simple mass spectrometry with minimal sample preparation. In this study, we evaluated the applicability of PESI-MS for the quantitative analysis of metabolites in in vitro enzyme reaction mixtures, which often contain high concentrations of proteins, salts, and buffers that are not suitable for conventional MS. Using glycolytic metabolites as a model, we optimized the type of organic solvents for mixing, data processing method, and investigated the effects of additives and reaction components on ionization efficiency and signal stability. Ethanol and 2-propanol were identified as suitable solvents for the detection of sugar phosphates, organic acids, and nucleotides. The quantitative reproducibility was improved by employing a total peak-height-based data processing method. The presence of proteins had a minimal influence on ionization. Although high concentrations of MgCl2 and buffers suppressed the signal intensity, metabolite signals were still observable at 10 and 50 mM, respectively. The linearity of the calibration curves and the reproducibility of the analysis of glycolytic metabolites in the mock enzyme reaction solution were confirmed. Finally, PESI-MS was successfully used to monitor the time course of intermediates in an in vitro synthetic non-oxidative glycolysis system. The data trend obtained by PESI-MS was roughly consistent with the results of conventional LC–MS analysis. These findings demonstrate that PESI-MS is a simple, rapid, and robust method for analyzing in vitro enzymatic reactions.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00216-026-06656-2.

Introduction

Probe electrospray ionization (PESI) is an ambient ionization technique for mass spectrometry (MS) in which metabolites are ionized by applying a high voltage to a needle probe carrying a small volume of the sample solution (Fig. 1) [1, 2]. PESI was developed to allow rapid analysis with relatively simple sample preparation. Since the volume of the sample introduced into the mass spectrometer via PESI is extremely small, typically on the order of picoliters [3, 4], PESI minimizes the risk of ion source contamination, even when analyzing unprocessed crude samples containing salts or surfactants [5, 6]. This technique has been applied to a wide range of samples, including biological fluids, such as blood and urine, food, and plants, and for organ tissue imaging [4, 7–11].Fig. 1Schematic representation of probe electrospray ionization and the obtained chronogram. These characteristics of PESI-MS are suitable for the quantitative analysis of metabolites in in vitro enzymatic reactions and reconstituted metabolic pathways, which often contain high concentrations of enzymes, inorganic salts, and buffers [4, 12]. An in vitro enzyme reaction is a basic method used for the kinetic analysis of enzyme activity in biochemical studies. Recently, multiple enzymatic reactions have been coupled to construct metabolic pathways in vitro for the bioproduction of valuable compounds [13–15]. Such in vitro reconstituted pathways can be used for direct application in biomanufacturing and for the rapid prototyping of synthetic pathways before they are introduced into living cells [16, 17]. Time-course measurements of the substrate, product, and intermediate levels in enzyme reaction solutions can reveal pathway bottlenecks, enabling the optimization of enzyme levels to improve productivity. However, these reaction solutions contain large amounts of nonvolatile substances and require extensive sample pretreatment for typical mass spectrometry analyses. PESI-MS is expected to minimize the pretreatment steps and allow the high-throughput analysis of enzyme reaction mixtures. Although the potential of PESI-MS for monitoring reactions has been suggested, as demonstrated in protein denaturation [18], hydrogen/deuterium exchange [18], Schiff base formation [18], and protease reactions [19], the applicability of PESI-MS to the in vitro reactions of water-soluble metabolites commonly used in biochemical and metabolic studies has not yet been evaluated.

In this study, the feasibility of using PESI-MS to quantify metabolic intermediates in an in vitro metabolic pathway consisting of multiple enzymatic reactions was investigated. Glycolysis, one of the most central pathways in bioproduction, was selected as the model. Glycolysis involves a wide range of intermediates and cofactors with different physicochemical properties, including sugar phosphates, glycerol phosphates, carboxylates, and nucleotides. Suitable organic solvents for PESI-MS analysis, data processing methods, and the effects of additives, such as volatile salts, were investigated. In addition, the effects of proteins, inorganic ions, and buffers commonly present in enzyme reaction mixtures on analytical sensitivity and system robustness were evaluated. Finally, PESI-MS was used to analyze the metabolites of the in vitro non-oxidative glycolysis pathway, which consists of multiple enzymatic reactions.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

All reagents were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan) unless otherwise noted. Glycolytic intermediate standards (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) were dissolved in Milli-Q water to 100 mM and further diluted as described in the main text. Formic acid, acetic acid, an ammonia solution (Merck), ammonium formate, and ammonium acetate were added as necessary to achieve the concentrations indicated in the text. The mock enzyme reaction mixture was prepared by adding bovine serum albumin (BSA, Merck), MgCl2, HEPES (DOJINDO, Kumamoto, Japan), Tris (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), MOPS (DOJINDO), and phosphate to the standard solution as necessary to achieve the concentrations indicated in the text. The solutions were mixed with an equal volume of ethanol containing 100 µM D-camphor sulfonic acid and centrifuged at 15,000 × g and 4 °C for 20 min. Methanol, 2-propanol, 1-butanol, acetone, acetonitrile, dimethyl sulfoxide, tetrahydrofuran, and N, N-dimethylformamide were also evaluated as organic solvents for mixing. The supernatant was transferred to an analysis vial and used for PESI-MS analysis.

PESI-MS analysis

A quadrupole time-of-flight/mass spectrometer (QTOF/MS; LCMS-9030, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a PESI unit (DPiMS™ QT; Shimadzu) was used. A 10 µL aliquot of the sample solution was applied to the liquid sample plate (Shimadzu). The ionization and MS parameters were as follows: sampling time, 30 ms; ionization time, 160 ms; m/z scan range, 40–990; scan rate, 10 ms; polarity, negative ion mode; desolvation line (DL) temperature, 150 °C; heat block temperature, 50 °C.

In vitro enzyme reaction of non-oxidative glycolysis

Twelve non-oxidative glycolytic enzymes were co-expressed, and GlyK was individually expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta2 (DE3) pLysS adk-P87S, according to a previous report [20]. Cell lysates, which were prepared from a cell suspension at a concentration of 200 mg-wet-cells mL⁻1, were heated at 70 °C for 30 min to remove thermolabile proteins. The resulting heat-treated supernatants were used as enzyme preparations at 200 mg-wet-cells mL⁻1. A reaction mixture containing 10 mM MgCl₂, 0.5 mM MnCl₂, 1 mM thiamine pyrophosphate, 0.1 mM adenosine diphosphate (ADP), 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 50 mM HEPES–NaOH buffer (pH 7.0), 50 mM glycerol, and 10 mg-wet-cells mL⁻1 GlyK was pre-incubated at 60 °C for 2 min. The reaction was initiated by adding the preparation of 12 enzymes to a final concentration of 10 mg-wet-cells mL⁻1. The reaction was quenched by immediately transferring the collected samples onto ice. The progress of the reaction was confirmed by measuring the amount of acetic acid produced using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Shimadzu) equipped with an Aminex HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and a refractive index detector (Shimadzu) [21].

LC–MS analysis

The same samples analyzed by PESI-MS were used for liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole MS (LC-tripleQ/MS) and LC-QTOF/MS. Hexose 6-phosphate (total intensity of glucose 6-phosphate (G6P), fructose 6-phosphate, and glucose 1-phosphate), pentose 5-phosphate (total intensity of ribose 5-phosphate, ribulose 5-phosphate, and xylulose 5-phosphate), sedoheptulose 7-phosphate, ADP, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) were measured using a Nexera X2 equipped with a mastro2 C18 column (150 mm, 2.1 mm, 3 µm; Shimadzu GLC) coupled to an LCMS-8060NX instrument (Shimadzu) as previously described [22]. To analyze the total intensity of the isomers, multiple reaction monitoring channels were modified by choosing identical precursor and product ions. Erythrose 4-phosphate and triose 3-phosphate (total intensity of dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde) were measured using a Nexera X2 equipped with a GL-HilicAex column (5.0 μm, 2.1 mm internal diameter, 150 mm; Resonac Techno Service Corporation, Ibaraki, Japan) coupled to an LCMS-9030 as previously described [23], because an inaccurate peak was observed in the LC-tripleQ/MS analysis. The QTOF/MS parameters were as follows: m/z scan range, 70–990; scan rate, 250 ms; polarity, negative ion mode; ionization, electrospray ionization; heating gas flow, 10 L/min; interface temperature, 300 °C; drying gas flow, 15 L/min; DL temperature, 250 °C; heat block temperature, 400 °C.

Results and discussion

Organic solvents suitable for PESI-MS analysis of glycolysis metabolites

In PESI-MS, the mass spectrometer continuously acquires data in scan mode throughout the measurement period. The analyte solution is sampled and ionized by up- and down-motion of a probe in 30 ms and 160 ms, respectively (Fig. 1A). Each up–down cycle of the probe takes approximately 200 ms and produces a mass spectrum of the analyte. This process is repeated for 30 s in one analysis (Fig. 1B). The time-course data obtained from the ion intensity chart are herein referred to as chronograms. In general, the sum of all the peak areas obtained within a given time period is used as the data for a single sample. Metabolites were annotated based on the exact m/z that matched theoretical values ± 5 ppm.

Since organic solvents are required to facilitate ionization in PESI-MS, solvents suitable for the analysis of glycolytic metabolites were evaluated. Glycolysis involves a variety of compounds, including sugar phosphates, phosphoglycerates, carboxylic acids, and nucleotides; therefore, G6P, 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG), pyruvate, and ATP were selected as representative analytes. Previous studies have employed methanol [18] and ethanol [8, 24]; however, the effect of the solvent has not been systematically investigated, especially for the analysis of phosphate-containing compounds. A standard mixture of these compounds at 100 µM was mixed with each organic solvent at a 1:1 ratio and analyzed in negative ion mode via PESI-MS. The chronograms obtained using methanol, ethanol, 2-propanol, and 1-butanol showed peaks corresponding to glycolytic metabolites (Fig. 2). Only a weak signal was observed for methanol (Fig. 2A), whereas the signals for ethanol and 2-propanol were strong and consistent (Fig. 2B, C). Analysis with 1-butanol yielded slightly higher peaks for G6P and 3PG than those with ethanol or 2-propanol (Fig. 2D); however, the use of 1-butanol was not favorable because of its noticeable odor and the requirement for adequate laboratory ventilation. No ions were detected when acetone, acetonitrile, dimethyl sulfoxide, tetrahydrofuran, or N, N-dimethylformamide was used. Although organic solvents are volatile, no detectable evaporation of the sample was observed during the 1-min analysis period when the sample was placed in the ion source without analysis. This is likely because the atmosphere of ion source was maintained at approximately room temperature (31 °C). These results indicated that ethanol and 2-propanol are suitable solvents for the analysis of glycolytic intermediates using PESI-MS. Examination of stable and quantitative data analysis

Next, the stability of the signal intensity during consecutive analyses using the same probe was examined. Since peak intensities varied with each ionization event by PESI-MS, metabolites were repeatedly ionized for 0.5 min during a single analysis. Analysis of the standard mixture of glycolytic metabolites was repeated nine times using the same probe. The representative peak shapes of the G6P chronogram at the first, fifth, and ninth runs gradually broadened (Fig. 3A–C). ATP levels showed a similar trend (Fig. 3D–F). Peak broadening was observed for all metabolites. This peak broadening is not attributable to carryover, as no metabolite peaks were detected in a blank sample analyzed without needle washing after a standard sample (data not shown). Bordag et al. showed that the needle surface changes during analysis [25], which may result in changes in peak shape. Since conventional data processing of chromatography-MS uses the peak area for quantification, the sum of the peak areas observed between 0.1 and 0.4 min was calculated. The total peak area of G6P and ATP (0.1–0.4 min) gradually increased, even though the same standard solution was analyzed repeatedly (Fig. 3G, H). The relative standard deviations (RSDs) of the nine peak areas were 16% and 25% for G6P and ATP, respectively. To overcome this problem, the total peak height was employed in data processing as the height of the peaks remained relatively constant (Fig. 3G, H). This approach improved the data stability and reduced the RSDs for G6P and ATP by 3.8% and 9.4%, respectively. Effect of additive on PESI-MS analysis

Since the addition of salts and acids to the samples influences the ionization efficiency [1], their effects on the PESI-MS analysis of glycolytic intermediates were investigated. Common volatile additives used in MS, such as formic acid, acetic acid, ammonia, ammonium formate, and ammonium acetate, were added to the glycolytic metabolite standards, which were then mixed with ethanol at a 1:1 ratio and analyzed in negative ion mode. Because in vitro enzyme reactions are typically conducted at relatively high substrate concentrations in the millimolar range, the investigation was performed over a concentration range that includes higher concentrations. The signal intensities of G6P [M − H]− and 3PG [M − H]− decreased with increasing concentrations of additives (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, pyruvate [M − H]− showed little sensitivity to the additives, and the addition of 100 mM ammonium formate resulted in a two-fold increase in the signal intensity (Fig. 4C). The signal intensity of ATP [M − H]− was enhanced by all the additives tested, but the optimal signal intensity varied depending on the additive (Fig. 4D). Under conditions where 10 and 100 mM formate and acetate were added, the intensity increased by 1.3–1.5-fold. The addition of ammonia had a minor effect. While 10 mM ammonium formate and ammonium acetate enhanced ATP [M − H]− signal intensity, 100 mM ammonium formate and acetate reduced it. No single additive was found to simultaneously improve the signal intensities of any of the four metabolites. The intensities of [M + HCOO]− and [M + CH3COO]− were relatively minor for the tested metabolites except for pyruvate (Fig. S1). Since all the analytes could be detected even without additives, subsequent experiments were conducted without additives.Fig. 4Effect of additives on the signal intensities of glycolytic intermediates measured using PESI-MS. Effects of proteins, inorganic salts, and buffers

The ionization of metabolites in enzyme reaction mixtures can be affected by the presence of large amounts of enzymes, inorganic salts, and buffers. These effects were assessed using a mock enzyme reaction mixture by individually adding BSA, MgCl2, and buffers to a 100 µM metabolite standard solution. BSA was used as a representative enzyme substitute since enzyme metabolizes intermediates and alters metabolite levels in the mixture. Each solution was mixed in a 1:1 ratio with ethanol, and the precipitates were removed using centrifugation before analyzing the supernatants via PESI-MS. Increasing the BSA concentration to 111 µg/mL did not significantly affect the peak intensities of any metabolites (Fig. 5). At 333 and 1000 µg/mL BSA, slight decreases in the signal intensities of G6P and ATP were observed. These results indicated that increasing protein levels did not substantially affect the analysis of glycolytic metabolites by PESI-MS, likely since most proteins precipitated upon mixing with ethanol.Fig. 5Effect of protein, inorganic ion, and buffers on PESI-MS analysis. Since the ATP regeneration and consumption reactions in glycolysis require inorganic ions, such as Mg2⁺, the effect of MgCl₂ on ionization was examined. Increasing the Mg2⁺ concentration inhibited ionization in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5). Compared to samples without MgCl2 added, the signal intensities of G6P, 3PG, pyruvate, and ATP in samples with 10 mM MgCl2 were reduced to 8.7%, 9.8%, 39.1%, and 4.3%, respectively. Since peaks were still detectable, it was concluded that MgCl2 concentrations should ideally be kept below the order of several tens of millimolar.

Buffers were added to the enzyme reaction mixtures to stabilize the pH during the reactions, and their effects on ionization were evaluated using HEPES, Tris, MOPS, and phosphate buffers. The signal intensity of G6P decreased in a concentration-dependent manner, regardless of the buffer type, reaching a minimum of 4.4–13.3% (Fig. 5A). The 3PG signal intensity slightly decreased below 5.56 mM in HEPES and MOPS (Fig. 5B), suggesting that the suppressive effect of HEPES and MOPS on 3PG was relatively mild. The ionization suppression of pyruvate was also attenuated when HEPES was employed (Fig. 5C). ATP ionization was not significantly inhibited by any of the buffers at concentrations up to 5.6 mM (Fig. 5D). In some cases, such as with HEPES, the ATP intensity was enhanced. These findings suggested that HEPES and MOPS are preferable buffers for in vitro enzymatic reactions, as analyzed using PESI-MS.

Examination of linearity range and reproducibility

To evaluate the quantification performance of PESI-MS for metabolites in the enzymatic reaction mixture, a mock reaction mixture was prepared containing BSA (110 µg/mL), MgCl₂ (1.1 mM), and HEPES buffer (5.6 mM). Calibration curves were constructed by spiking G6P, 3PG, pyruvate, and ATP at concentrations ranging from 5 to 800 µM. The signal intensity of each metabolite was normalized to that of an internal standard (D-campher sulfonic acid). All analytes exhibited excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99) (Fig. 6A). The limit of detection for G6P, 3PG, and pyruvate was 5 µM, while that of ATP was 10 µM. The reproducibility of PESI-MS using the same needle probe was examined with the mock reaction mixture containing 200 µM standards using the height-based data analysis evaluated above (Fig. 3). The relative standard deviations of the intensities in 50 analyses were 10.3%, 11.5%, 13.6%, and 28.6% for G6P, 3PG, Pyr, and ATP, respectively (Fig. 6B), indicating that stable and quantitative analyses were feasible, even in the presence of proteins, salts, and buffer components using the same needle probe. The durability of the same needle probe was assessed over 500 analyses. PESI-MS analysis of intermediates in an in vitro non-oxidative glycolysis reaction

Finally, PESI-MS was used to analyze the time course of metabolite levels in in vitro multiple enzyme reactions. The synthetic non-oxidative glycolysis (NOG) pathway, which is expected to be applicable to bioproduction, was chosen as the model [20]. Unlike the conventional glycolysis pathway, this pathway degrades starch to acetate in a non-oxidative manner without decarboxylation, while regenerating ATP (Fig. S3). Twelve heat-tolerant enzymes constituting the NOG pathway derived from thermophilic bacteria were expressed in E. coli. Thermophilic enzymes were purified by removing E. coli-derived proteins through heat inactivation. The purified enzymes were then mixed with the substrate for in vitro enzymatic reactions (see Experimental Section). Reactions were performed at 60 °C with or without glycerol kinase (+ GlyK or −GlyK, respectively) for recycling ATP to ADP (Fig. S3). The reaction was confirmed by measuring the amount of acetate produced using HPLC. As expected, the + GlyK system produced more acetate than the −GlyK system, reaching a two-fold increase after 60 min (Fig. S4). Subsequently, the reaction mixtures were collected over time, mixed with ethanol, centrifuged, and analyzed using PESI-MS to monitor the time-dependent changes in sugar phosphate, ADP, and ATP levels. The measurement of a single sample using PESI-MS, including polarity switching between positive and negative ion modes, was completed within 1.0 min. The sample application procedure required approximately 0.2 min, resulting in a total analysis time of 1.2 min per sample in PESI-MS analysis. The same samples were also analyzed using LC–MS to validate the PESI-MS results. In LC–MS analysis, each run required 20 min including column equilibration (5 min). As sample injection required an additional 0.2 min, the total analysis time per run was approximately 20.2 min in LC–MS. Compared with LC–MS analysis, the analysis time required for PESI–MS was reduced to 5.9%, corresponding to a 17-fold increase in throughput. The overall trends obtained using PESI-MS were roughly consistent with those obtained using LC–MS (Fig. 7). The average R2 value between normalized intensities of each metabolite obtained by using PESI-MS and LC–MS was 0.89 (Fig. S5), indicating strong agreement between the two methods. Although the PESI-MS data for ADP under + GlyK conditions appear to show a slight increase after 30 min whereas the LC–MS data show a slight decrease, the magnitude of these changes is small and within the experimental variability. In the −GlyK system, ADP was depleted within 20 min, whereas ATP levels remained high (Fig. 7A). Meanwhile, sugar phosphates accumulated markedly after 20 min, suggesting that the ADP-dependent Ack reaction was a bottleneck because of ADP depletion. However, gradual accumulation of acetate was observed during the initial 60-min reaction (Fig. S4), indicating that the AcK-catalyzed reaction proceeded slowly but steadily during this period. The ADP required for the AcK reaction may be supplied by the activity of intrinsic ATP-decomposing enzyme(s), as reported in a previous study [26]. In contrast, in the + GlyK system, ATP levels remained low, whereas ADP levels were not depleted, indicating that ATP was continuously converted back to ADP by GlyK, enhancing the overall reaction (Fig. 7B). These results demonstrated that PESI-MS can reasonably monitor the intermediate levels in multiple in vitro enzymatic reactions. Accumulation of sugar phosphates was also observed in the + GlyK system, suggesting that the Ack step may still be a rate-limiting reaction.
