Gamma-glutamyltransferase testing in paediatric inflammatory bowel disease to screen for primary sclerosing cholangitis: a diagnostic study based on routinely collected electronic healthcare data
Authors: Besrat Berhane, Wouter Sjoerd van de Put, Patrick Ferry van Rheenen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate, among children with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and elevated liver enzymes, what threshold of gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) best distinguishes those with and without primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC).MethodDelayed-type diagnostic study with a paired design. Children with IBD were regularly screened with GGT (index test). Confirmation of PSC was based on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and/or liver histology (preferred reference standard). Children at low risk of PSC continued regular GGT testing for latent PSC to become visible (alternative reference test). The primary outcome was the negative predictive value (NPV) using three predefined test thresholds, respectively, 1, 2 and 5× the upper limit of normal (ULN). The secondary outcome was the GGT threshold based on receiver operating characteristic analysis.Results132 of 469 children (28.1%) had elevated GGT levels at their first colonoscopy or during follow-up. Eventually, 34 children (7.2%) were diagnosed with PSC. Median GGT (IQR) for children with and without PSC was 227 (127–345) and 77 (59–138) U/L, respectively. Of the predefined GGT thresholds, 2× ULN (ie, 100 U/L) had the best test characteristics, including an NPV of 98% and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.04 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.31). The area under the curve was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75 to 0.90) and the optimal GGT threshold was 103.5 U/L.ConclusionIn children with IBD who have GGT elevations less than 2× ULN, the likelihood of PSC is extremely low. In such cases, MRCP and liver biopsy can be omitted. Regular GGT monitoring is advised, as PSC may develop over time.

Introduction

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a rare chronic cholestatic liver disease characterised by inflammation, destruction, stricturing and fibrosis of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts.1 2 The cause of PSC is unknown, but it has a strong association with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).35

Childhood-onset PSC typically presents with asymptomatic liver test abnormalities, with disproportionate elevations of gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) compared with alanine aminotransferase (ALT).6 The diagnosis of PSC is confirmed when characteristic irregular bile ducts are found on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or when periductal onion skin fibrosis is seen in a liver biopsy specimen.7 Patients with abnormalities on MRCP are classified as having ‘large duct disease’, whereas those with fibrosing cholangiopathy on histology, but without MRCP abnormalities, are classified as having ‘small duct disease’.

Children with both PSC and IBD are at higher risk for colorectal neoplasia compared with those with isolated IBD8 9 and should be included in a surveillance colonoscopy programme. The European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) recommends regular GGT and ALT testing in children with IBD to screen for subclinical PSC,51012 but it is unknown when to expose children with liver test abnormalities to MRCP and/or liver biopsy. We set out to investigate, among children with IBD and liver test abnormalities, what GGT threshold best distinguishes those with and without PSC.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-centre delayed-type diagnostic accuracy study with a paired design was performed at the University Medical Centre Groningen, a tertiary care hospital in the Netherlands. Children with IBD were regularly screened with GGT (index test). Confirmation of PSC was based on MRCP, liver histology or both (preferred reference standard). Children at low risk of PSC continued regular GGT testing to witness biochemical normalisation or for possible latent PSC to become visible (alternative reference test).

Participants

We used routinely collected electronic healthcare data. Eligible patients were identified through an automated search in Clinical Assistant (RVC BV, Amersfoort, The Netherlands), a digital storage system of endoscopy images and reports. Patients diagnosed with IBD before the age of 18 years who were periodically screened with a basic liver test panel including GGT were eligible for inclusion. Those who had a GGT level above 50 U/L (ie, above the upper limit of normal (ULN)) at the time of their first colonoscopy or any time during follow-up were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if PSC or any other cholestatic disorder was diagnosed prior to IBD diagnosis or when extensive data was missing in the medical records. Online supplemental figure 1 shows a graphical depiction of the patient recruitment.

Data collection

Medical data of eligible patients was entered in the REDCap electronic data capture tool hosted at the UMCG.13 14 Baseline was defined as the date of the first colonoscopy, which was between February 2007 and February 2023. Patients were followed until October 2023. Extracted variables included age at the time of IBD diagnosis, IBD phenotype (ulcerative colitis (UC), Crohn’s disease, UC or IBD-unclassified (IBD-U)) and results of ALT and total serum bilirubin at first GGT elevation. For patients with consecutive elevated GGT values, we used the value that led to the decision to proceed with the execution of additional testing (which was not necessarily the first abnormal GGT value).

Reference test

Confirmation of PSC was based on MRCP and/or liver histology and serology. Online supplemental figure 2 shows the deterministic predefined rule that was used to classify large duct PSC, small duct PSC and overlap syndrome with autoimmune hepatitis (AIH). The decision to perform any other diagnostic tests (including virology, thiopurine metabolite testing and liver Doppler ultrasound) was left to the paediatrician’s discretion.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the negative predictive value (NPV) of GGT for PSC using a multilevel approach with three predefined thresholds, respectively 1, 2 and 5× ULN. The secondary outcome was the GGT threshold based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, V.29.0.1.0). Diagnostic test characteristics (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and NPV) were calculated for children with and without PSC using the earlier mentioned predefined thresholds, as well as optimal thresholds (defined as the most upper left data point in the (ROC) curve).

Human subjects protection

This study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The data were collected and recorded by the investigators in such a manner that subjects could not be identified, directly or through identifiers linked to the subjects.

Results

We identified 481 children with IBD in our electronic healthcare database, of which 469 were eligible for inclusion. A total of 132 children (28.1%) had elevated GGT levels at their first colonoscopy or at any time during follow-up. The median (IQR) duration of follow-up was 6.5 (3.9–11.3) years. Forty-three children were exposed to the preferred reference standard, whereas 89 were exposed to the alternative reference standard. Eventually, 34 of 132 children (7.2%) with IBD and elevated GGT levels were diagnosed with PSC (figure 1).

The children with IBD and PSC were significantly younger than those without PSC and had their GGT elevation shortly after the first-time colonoscopy. The distribution of IBD phenotypes was significantly different between groups, with 74% of the patients with PSC having ulcerative colitis compared with 39% in the non-PSC group (p<0.001). Optimal (ROC-based) threshold

The ROC curve depicted in figure 2 shows that the ideal GGT threshold to predict PSC gives an area under the curve of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75 to 0.90). This threshold corresponds with a GGT value of 103.5 U/L.

Diagnostic work-up and final diagnosis

Online supplemental table 1 shows eight different patterns of diagnostic work-up among the 132 children with IBD and elevated GGT values. In children ultimately diagnosed with PSC, the combination of MRCP, liver biopsy and immunology testing was the most frequently observed pattern. A total of 74 patients did not undergo any form of additional diagnostic testing, and repeat GGT and ALT measurements showed quick normalisation.

Most study participants (n=80) experienced a short-term GGT elevation with a median (IQR) time to normalisation of 1.8 months (1.2–3.7). No explanatory diagnosis could be established for this group. Six of 34 children who were eventually found to have PSC also exhibited features of AIH. Additionally, two children had isolated AIH. Viral hepatitis (including Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus) was diagnosed in 10 children (table 3).

Discussion

We showed that GGT abnormalities frequently occur in children with IBD. This usually involves transient mild elevations of undefined aetiology. About a fourth of the children with asymptomatic GGT elevations had bile duct abnormalities compatible with PSC. Children who were ultimately diagnosed with PSC had significantly higher GGT values compared with those without PSC. The optimal GGT cut-off point to decide whether to expose a child with IBD to MRCP and liver biopsy was 100 U/L (or 2× ULN). The probability of missing a case with PSC when the GGT value is below 100 U/L is extremely low.

Comparison with existing literature

We searched PubMed and Embase for studies that assessed the diagnostic accuracy of elevated GGT in children with IBD and identified two of acceptable quality. A Canadian single-centre study by Valentino and colleagues15 retrospectively collected data from 300 children with IBD, of whom 18 (6%) were ultimately diagnosed with PSC (with or without features of AIH). Ninety-three per cent had persistent GGT elevations above the threshold of 2× ULN. The optimal GGT threshold, based on ROC curve analysis, was 252 U/L (or 5× ULN). A second Canadian, registry-based study by Chandrakumar and colleagues5 focused on a subgroup of 95 children with UC, of whom 9 (9%) developed PSC. Eight of them had elevated GGT values (>1× ULN) at the time of the initial colonoscopy. Without ROC curve analysis or multi-level testing, the authors postulated that every child with UC and mild elevation of serum GGT, especially at baseline, may develop PSC.

PSC prevalence in our study cohort was comparable with those reported by Valentino et al and Chandrakumar et al. However, the difference in optimal GGT thresholds differed. In our study, the emphasis was on ‘ruling out’: lowering the probability of PSC to provide reassurance, adopting a ‘watchful waiting’ strategy and minimise unnecessary invasive diagnostics. Both multilevel testing and ROC curve analysis led us to a GGT threshold of 100 U/L, equivalent to 2× the ULN.

A study by Goyal and colleagues,16 although not a diagnostic study, highlights another interesting aspect. They used data from a retrospective registry in which 27 American and Canadian IBD centres participated. After a median follow-up of 3.4 years, 27 of 1569 patients (1.7%) were diagnosed as having PSC (with or without features of AIH). Approximately three-fourths of them were diagnosed within the first 3 months of the IBD diagnosis. Early GGT elevations were also observed by Valentino et al, Chandrakumar et al5 15 and by ourselves. In our study cohort, 26 out of 34 (76%) children with PSC experienced their first GGT elevation within 3 months following their initial colonoscopy.

Strengths and limitations

This study used a non-randomised healthcare database which requires that several of our design choices be considered. For this purpose, we used a standardised graphical representation as advised by Schneeweiss et al17 to visualise patient recruitment (online supplemental figure 1). We used a delayed-type cross-sectional design, in which a GGT measurement (the index test) was related to MRCP and/or liver biopsy (the preferred reference standard). Children at low risk of PSC (ie, with only mild GGT elevations) continued regular GGT testing to witness biochemical normalisation or further deterioration of liver enzymes (the alternative reference test). With this, we introduced a work-up bias, which is a common problem in screening studies where only subjects with a positive index test result proceed to the more invasive reference test. We reduced this bias by following the low-risk patients for a median (IQR) duration of 6.4 years (3.6–11.1) for possible latent PSC to become visible. One might argue that this observation period was not sufficiently long, but we are confident that most cases with PSC become apparent within this time, as is discussed in the previous section. We regard the single-centre design as a strength of this study. It allowed us to follow patients even after their transfer to adult care, and we did not have to contend with variations in laboratory assays between different centres.

Clinical implications

Early recognition of PSC in children with IBD is essential to control long-term complications, including biliary obstruction and the development of colorectal carcinoma. Periodically measuring liver enzymes, including GGT, is a suitable screening method to evaluate the presence of underlying PSC. We showed that at a threshold of 100 U/L GGT has high sensitivity (97%) and a very low negative likelihood ratio (0.05). These are excellent test characteristics to rule out a condition with a low pre-test probability such as PSC. We recommend liver enzyme testing, including GGT, in the treatment-naive child with suspected IBD and then repeated periodically throughout the follow-up. Mildly elevated GGT levels up to 100 U/L justify a watchful waiting approach, with repeat blood tests recommended 1 month later. Moderately to markedly elevated GGT levels (>100 U/L) prompt further diagnostic work-up. Transabdominal ultrasound is recommended as the initial imaging study and may reveal bile duct wall thickening and focal bile duct dilatations. However, formal imaging of the biliary tree with MRCP is essential for the diagnosis and classification of PSC.12

Conclusion

Children with IBD who have GGT elevations <100 U/L (<2× ULN) have a very low probability of underlying PSC. In such cases, MRCP and liver biopsy can be omitted. Periodic measurement of GGT is recommended, as PSC may develop over time.
