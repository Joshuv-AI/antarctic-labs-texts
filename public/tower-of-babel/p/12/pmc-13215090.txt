Duration of vaccine-induced immunity following MMR2 vaccination in a measles and rubella-eliminated country
Authors: June Young Chun, Ah-Ra Kim, Hwichang Jeong, You-Jin Kim, Hae Ji Kang, Hye-Sook Jeong, Gi-Eun Rhie, Youngmee Jee, Yongdai Kim, Junewoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine is generally considered to provide lifelong immunity against measles and rubella. However, occasional measles outbreaks in countries with high vaccine coverage have raised concerns about waning immunity. In this study, we aimed to estimate the duration of vaccine-induced immunity following MMR2 vaccination in settings where measles and rubella have been eliminated.MethodsUsing 2019 national serosurvey data, this study estimated antibody waning (decay) rates for each antigen after MMR2 vaccination, with age as a proxy for time since vaccination. In South Korea, MMR2 coverage has exceeded 95% from the 1995 birth cohort, and no significant measles or rubella outbreaks have occurred since 2001, allowing for homogeneous cohorts. Additionally, the time required for the geometric mean titers of each antibody to reach the seronegative cutoff levels was calculated.ResultsThe average antibody decay rates for MMR were 8.71%, 12.02%, and 5.48% per year, respectively, after MMR2 vaccination. The duration of vaccine-induced immunity was estimated to be 19 years for measles, 9.71 years for mumps, and 31.57 years for rubella when equivocal results were considered negative. If equivocal results were considered positive, the duration would be 27.32 years for measles, 15.25 years for mumps, and 39.77 years for rubella.ConclusionHere, we found that individuals who have not experienced natural infection exhibited age-dependent waning of vaccine-induced immunity. While vaccination remains the most effective way to prevent disease, continued vigilance is necessary even in countries with high vaccine coverage.

Introduction

In South Korea, the measles-containing vaccine (MCV) was first introduced in 1965, followed by the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in 1980. Subsequently, the Korean Society of Paediatrics recommended two doses of the MMR vaccine, with the first dose at 12–15 months of age and the second dose (MMR2) at 4–6 years of age, starting in 1997 [1]. The annual incidence of measles decreased significantly from 30 792 cases (11.61 cases per million) in 1962 to 2 cases (0.00 cases per million) after vaccination efforts intensified in 1997 [2].

However, a measles outbreak occurred in 2000–1, which recorded an annual incidence of 5.81 cases per million [3]. This outbreak prompted the South Korean government to conduct a serosurvey in 2000 among school-aged children with high attack rates (birth cohorts 1982–93), which revealed the relatively low seroprevalence in elementary and middle-school students (as low as 84.6% of immunity in birth cohort 1990). In addition, the coverage of second dose MCV (MCV2) was at most 43.4% in the serosurvey study [4].

Based on this serological study, the South Korean government announced a “Five-Year Measles Elimination Program” in 2001 that included (i) a catch-up measles-rubella (MR) vaccination targeting the school-aged children (birth cohorts 1985–94, 5.8 million) and (ii) a keep-up program that required all children entering elementary school to present evidence of MMR2 vaccination [5]. These efforts reduced the incidence of measles to less than one per million, and South Korea became the first country in the Western Pacific to declare the elimination of measles in 2006, which was verified by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2014 [6, 7].

After nearly 15 years of measles-free status, there were outbreaks, albeit small, in 2014 (n = 442) and 2019 (n = 194), primarily affecting adolescents and young adults in their 20s (Fig. 1) [5]. These individuals belonged to the birth cohorts with an MMR2 coverage that exceeded 95%, given the history outlined above (Table 1). The individual vaccination records of these patients might not accurately reflect reality, since the Immunization Registry Information System (IRIS) was launched in 2002 and has only compiled reliable annual statistics since 2010 [8].

In contrast to measles, mumps cases have continued to occur among children and adolescents, with the annual incidence rising sharply since 2013 and peaking at 25 286 cases (4.94 cases per million) in 2014 [9]. In the case of rubella, the annual incidence has remained below one per million since the implementation of the “catch-up vaccination” campaign in 2001. South Korea was verified by the WHO as having achieved rubella elimination in 2017 [10].

Despite high vaccine coverage, the measles and mumps outbreaks suggest the possibility of vaccine failure. Indeed, a nationwide seroprevalence study conducted in 2014 by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) showed a decreasing proportion of the measles seropositive population with age (time since vaccination), implying secondary vaccine failure, namely, the waning of vaccine-induced immunity [11–13]. Moreover, recent studies have shown that healthcare workers who were initially seronegative for measles immunoglobulin G (IgG) developed measles-specific IgG with consistently high avidity after MMR vaccination. Given their birth cohorts, these findings suggest prior immunological exposure, most likely through previous MMR vaccination [14, 15].

So far, the MMR vaccine is considered to provide lifelong immunity against measles and rubella, while immunity against mumps may wane over time [16–19]. However, occasional measles outbreaks in countries with high vaccine coverage are inconsistent with that understanding. Here, we introduce a nationwide seroprevalence study of MMR conducted in 2019, which is a 5-year follow-up study to those conducted in 2014 [11]. Furthermore, we explored the seroconversion and antibody decay rates for each antigen using detailed antibody titer data. Finally, we attempted to estimate the duration of vaccine-induced immunity following MMR2 in a measles and rubella-eliminated setting.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We used publicly available data from the Infectious Disease Surveillance Yearbook for MMR incidence in South Korea [2, 3]. These are based on the universal surveillance system for national notifiable infectious diseases which, for example, mandates reporting an acute febrile rash illness with laboratory confirmation (detection of measles virus–specific nucleic acid using real-time reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction or a positive serologic test for measles immunoglobulin M antibody) or direct linkage to a laboratory-confirmed measles case. For vaccine coverage data, we used the national immunization surveys conducted in 2000 and 2009 and the IRIS which compiles annual statistics on national vaccine coverage by the KDCA [4, 8, 20].

Study population

A total of 3050 residual serum samples were primarily supplied by the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) (n = 2050; for ages 10–50 years, birth cohorts 1969–2010) and a private diagnostic laboratory (n = 1000; for ages 0–9 years, birth cohorts 2011–19), conforming to quotas for age, sex, and geographical location in South Korea [21, 22].

The KNHANES is an ongoing annual surveillance that assesses the health and nutritional status of Koreans, based on Article 16 of the National Health Promotion Act proclaimed in 1995. The KNHANES employs a multi-stage clustered probability sampling design based on enumeration districts and households to recruit nationally representative samples. The survey consists of three components: a health interview, a health examination including laboratory tests, and a nutrition survey. Blood samples through this survey are collected from participants aged 10 years and older and stored in the national biobank for future use. Each year, approximately 10 000 individuals participate in the study, with a target of securing 3000 serum samples annually [22]. Among the collected samples, 50 samples were randomly selected for each age group.

Samples from participants aged <10 years were obtained from one of the major private diagnostic institutions in South Korea, Eone Laboratories. Blood samples were referred to the laboratory for medical diagnosis and health screening. Those tested for MMR or human immunodeficiency virus were excluded from the analysis. For individuals over 1 year old, 50 samples per age year were collected; for infants under 12 months, 50 samples per month were collected. From 23 September 2019 (the institutional review board approval date), the initial 50 quota-matched samples collected for each age and month-of-age group were included in the analysis. A seroprevalence study in 2014 was conducted in a similar manner [11].

Enzyme immunoassays

Serological tests for MMR virus-specific IgG were performed using an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) kit for each antigen (Enzygnost®, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, GmbH Marburg, Germany). The results were categorized into three groups: positive, equivocal, or negative based on the optical density (OD) values: OD >0.2 (positive), 0.1–0.2 (equivocal), and <0.1 (negative). Serum samples yielding equivocal results were retested in duplicate and classified according to the majority of the results. The OD values were converted to international units using the α-method, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Values with an OD <0.1 (cutoff) are recommended to be excluded from the calculation. The quantitative cutoff for seropositivity was set at ≥320 mIU/ml for measles virus IgG, a titer of ≥470 for mumps virus IgG, and ≥6.5 IU/ml for rubella virus IgG, as established by the manufacturer. The cutoffs for seronegativity were <150 mIU/ml, a titer of <230, and <4 IU/ml [23].

Statistical analyses

Antibody decay rates

The individual antibody titers for MMR were log-transformed prior to analysis. Here, vaccine-induced antibody titers are assumed to experience an exponential decay, as shown below, where GMT denotes the geometric mean titer, w is the antibody decay rate, and a represents age [18, 24]. Under a fixed vaccination schedule among birth cohorts 1995–2019, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the age of individuals and the time since the last vaccination. Data from individuals aged ≥5 years were used in this analysis, as approximately 80% of children completed the MMR2 vaccination by the age of 5 (Supplementary Table S1). A sensitivity analysis was also conducted using data from individuals aged ≥6 years.

To account for values below the lower limit of quantification (i.e. the cutoffs for seronegativity), we employed Tobit linear regression to obtain the maximum likelihood estimates of the average decay rates for MMR, respectively [25]. As each antibody declined at different rates, the model was fitted until the age with the lowest GMT levels for each antigen. Detailed methodology is described in antibody decay rates in the Supplementary File.

There is a caveat interpreting the MMR seroprevalence of males in South Korea, where all men aged 19 and above are required to serve in the military for 2 years (Supplementary Table S2) [26]. Owing to frequent mumps outbreaks in the military, MMR vaccination has been obligatory since 2012. Consequently, males born in 1993 or later have received a third dose of MMR (MMR3) from 2012 onward. To mitigate the impact of MMR3 vaccination in males when estimating antibody decay rates, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis using only female data.

Duration of vaccine-induced immunity

Utilizing the same regression model to estimate the antibody decay rates, we calculated the time required for GMTs at 5 years of age to reach the seronegative cutoff values of each antigen. The results are presented in two ways, where equivocal results are regarded as positive or negative.

Seroconversion

To explore the seroconversion rates of the MMR2 vaccination, we estimated the seroprevalence at 5 years of age, assuming that every individual had been vaccinated, given that the South Korean vaccine coverage in the studied birth cohorts outweighed 95% (Table 1). Following a previous study by Zibolenová et al., we calculated the proportion of individuals whose antibody titers were above the threshold of each antigen at 5 years of age under normal distribution [27]. Detailed methods are available in seroconversion in the Supplementary File. The results are also presented in two ways, where equivocal results are regarded as positive or negative.

All analyses were conducted using the R statistical software version 4.3.1. Code and data to reproduce the analyses are available at https://github.com/Hwichang/Duration-of-Vaccine-induced-Immunity-Following-MMR2-Vaccination.

Results

Seroprevalence

Among the 3050 participants enrolled in 2019, 1526 (50.03%) were female, 1479 (48.49%) were male, and 45 (1.48%) were unknown. The overall proportions of the population that were seropositive for MMR were 72.98% [95% confidence interval (CI): 71.41%–74.56%], 60.86% (95% CI: 59.15%–62.62%), and 77.67% (95% CI: 76.19%–79.15%), respectively, when equivocal results were regarded as negative. The age-specific serostatus distribution of the population for each antigen is shown in Fig. 2A and Supplementary Figs S1–S3.

The proportions of the population that were seropositive against the three antigens exhibited a gradual age-related decline after 5 years of age across birth cohorts born between 1995 and 2019, consistent with increasing time since vaccination under a uniform two-dose schedule. Birth cohorts who received catch-up MR vaccination (born between 1985 and 1994, 25–34-year-olds in 2019) or were born during the endemic era exhibited consistently high seroprevalence.

In the case of mumps, unlike measles and rubella, the proportion of the population that was seropositive fluctuated owing to sporadic outbreaks. The trend in the age-specific seroprevalence of the three antigens in 2014 was generally similar to that in 2019. For reference, previous serosurvey results and vaccine coverage (%) are summarized in Table 1.

Antibody decay rates

The age-specific GMTs of MMR measured in 2019 are shown in Fig. 2B and Supplementary Figs S4–S6. Similarly to the seroprevalence results, the GMT levels declined from 5 years of age after receiving MMR2, and this trend was also maintained within birth cohorts with the routine MMR2 vaccination schedule.

The fitted regression models indicated that the GMTs of MMR declined by 8.71% (95% CI: 7.09%–10.3%), 12.02% (95% CI: 9.18%–14.78%), and 5.48% (95% CI: 4.72%–6.23%) per year, respectively, after MMR2 vaccination (Table 2). The models were fitted until the ages of 19, 13, and 24 years for MMR, respectively, with the lowest GMT levels measured in 2019 (Fig. 3).

In the sensitivity analysis using only female data, the antibody decay rates for measles and rubella were 8.55% (95% CI: 6.99%–10.09%) and 5.97% (95% CI: 4.84%–7.08%) per year, respectively (Table 2 and Supplementary Figs S7–S9). The models were fitted until the age of 23 years for measles, 24 years for rubella, and mumps were not analyzed owing to the span of the fitted age ending earlier than the military entrance.

When the analysis was initiated from age 6, the estimated antibody decay rates were 8.36% (95% CI: 6.52%–10.16%) for measles, 11.85% (95% CI: 8.43%–15.15%) for mumps, and 5.16% (95% CI: 4.34%–5.97%) for rubella.

Duration of vaccine-induced immunity

The mean duration of vaccine-induced immunity was estimated to be 19 years (95% CI: 16.53–21.47 years) for measles, 9.71 years (95% CI: 8.15–11.27 years) for mumps, and 31.57 years (95% CI: 28.34–34.79 years) for rubella after MMR2 vaccination when equivocal results were considered negative. If equivocal results were considered positive, the mean duration would be 27.32 years (95% CI: 23.3–31.33 years) for measles, 15.25 years (95% CI: 12.38–18.13 years) for mumps, and 39.77 years (95% CI: 35.41–44.12 years) for rubella (Table 3).

Seroconversion

The estimated proportions of seropositive individuals at 5 years of age (seroconversion after MMR2) were 95.04% (95% CI: 93.48%–96.29%), 92.03% (95% CI: 89.17%–94.27%), and 99.27% (95% CI: 98.99%–99.49%) for MMR, respectively, when equivocal results were regarded as negative (Table 2). If we regard the equivocal results as positive, the seroconversion rates would be 99.11% (95% CI: 98.72%–99.39%), 98.65% (95% CI: 97.93%–99.14%), and 99.89% (95% CI: 99.84%–99.93%), respectively.

Discussion

In a country that has eliminated measles and rubella, such as South Korea, individuals who have not contracted these diseases exhibit an age-dependent loss of immunity following routine MMR2 vaccination. Age-specific GMT levels displayed declining trends, and the fitted regression analysis indicated the most rapid decay rates in mumps (12.02% per year), followed by measles (8.71% per year) and rubella (5.48% per year).

If those waning rates persist, the mean duration of vaccine-induced immunity was estimated to be 19 years for measles, 9.71 years for mumps, and 31.57 years for rubella after MMR2 vaccination, when equivocal results were considered negative. These findings align with the ongoing pattern of frequent mumps outbreaks among adolescents, sporadic measles outbreaks from imported cases in young adults, and the near eradication of rubella in South Korea. The mumps epidemic may be attributed not only to rapid antibody degradation but also to the initially low seroconversion rates for mumps. Furthermore, a recent study from Austria reported that the median age of individuals infected with measles despite previous vaccination was 25.4 years, which corresponds to our finding of an estimated 19-year duration of immunity following MMR2 vaccination at age 5 [28].

In a cohort follow-up study of children in the US state of Wisconsin, the antibody decay rates of MMR were 9.7%, 9.2%, and 5.9% per year, respectively [29]. Those results are consistent with our findings. In a Slovakian study, the antibody decay rate of measles after MMR1 was 9.7% per year, and the decay rate after MMR2 was 4.8% per year [27]. In Slovakia, the second dose of MMR was administered at 11 years of age, and this difference might lead to the results that are discordant with ours. In an Australian study, the seroconversion rates after MMR2 vaccination were 100% (86%–100%), 98% (74%–100%), and 100% (89%–100%), respectively [30]. Our estimates also appeared consistent with these findings.

It is also worth considering that if the estimated antibody decay rates are accurate, we would expect to observe significantly more cases of measles than we currently do. One possible explanation is that the seronegative cutoff used in commercial EIA kits may be conservative—designed to be more specific but less sensitive to attest immunity. Moreover, the plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) is regarded as the gold standard for determining measles immunity, and a PRN titer of 120 is known to confer protection against natural infection [31]. Although an excellent correlation between Enzygnost EIA and PRNT methods has been reported, the exact cutoff of EIA to attest protective immunity is yet to be elucidated [11, 32].

This study had several limitations. First, it was a cross-sectional study, not a longitudinal follow-up study. Although large-scale cross-sectional serosurvey allowed us to measure the population-level loss of immunity over time, there might be a cohort effect that we could not take into consideration. Second, the loss of antibody concentration should not be directly translated into the absence of protection against the infection. Without measuring the cellular immunity levels, we could only assess the part of humoral immunity. Nonetheless, the measles outbreaks in 2019 predominantly affected young adults in their 20s, who had a low seroprevalence of measles, implying the nonnegligible effect of humoral immunity. Lastly, we assumed that every individual born in 1995 and thereafter was vaccinated with MMR2 in the study, given that vaccine coverage exceeded 95% among those cohorts.

To date, the duration of humoral immunity in the absence of re-exposure to wild-type measles and rubella remains unclear, since most of the representative studies have been conducted during the measles-endemic era [16, 18]. In South Korea, however, MMR2 coverage has exceeded 95% from the 1995 birth cohort, and no significant measles or rubella outbreaks have occurred since 2001, allowing us to explore solely vaccine-induced immunity among homogeneous cohorts. It is particularly important in anticipating future challenges for measles and rubella elimination.

In conclusion, detailed nationwide antibody data allowed us to evaluate age-specific immunity levels after MMR vaccination, especially when there is solely vaccine-induced immunity. Our findings indicate the possibility of secondary vaccine failure (i.e. waning immunity) of MMR at different rates for each antigen. Undoubtedly, vaccination is by far the most effective way to prevent infectious disease, as seen in South Korean incidence data after the introduction of the MMR vaccine. Nonetheless, continued vigilance is necessary even in countries with high vaccine coverage.
