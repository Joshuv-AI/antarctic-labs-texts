Voice Outcomes of Patients Intubated for Critical COVID Illness: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Francesco F Caruana, Alexander Gelbard, David Francis, Michael J Pitman, Elena P Kennedy, Joel Blumin, Jonathan Bock, Matthew Clary, Quinlyn Axelson, Laura Matrka, Patrick McGarey, James Daniero, Phillip Weissbrod, Yin Yiu, Daniel Gorelik, Robert J Morrison, Shady Soliman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCharacterize the long‐term vocal outcomes of patients who required ICU care for COVID‐19 infection.Study DesignProspective cohort study.SettingMulti‐institutional North American Airway Collaborative Study.MethodsPatients with a COVID‐19 diagnosis requiring ICU admission were identified via ICD‐10 codes and recruited after discharge to complete patient‐reported outcome measures (PROM) in voice (Voice Handicap Index: VHI‐10), communication (Communicative Participation Item Bank: CPIB), and breathing (Clinical COPD Questionnaire: CCQ). Multivariate analysis investigated the association between clinical variables and PROMs.Results308 patients enrolled; 271 were admitted with COVID to the ICU. 221 were intubated (81.5%); 50 patients admitted to the ICU did not require intubation (18.5%). Mean follow‐up was 489 days after discharge (95% CI: 452‐526). Mean intubation duration was 17 days (95% CI: 15‐19). Median endotracheal tube (ETT) size was 7.5. Intubation was associated with higher VHI‐10 score (15 vs 10; P = .01) and lower CPIB score (27 vs 30; P < .01). When controlling for ETT size, multivariate analysis did not show a relationship between intubation duration and VHI‐10 or CPIB scores but was associated with worse CCQ score (P = .04).ConclusionSurvivors of COVID infection requiring ICU admission and intubation have persistent functional impairments in voicing and breathing more than 1 year after their hospitalization. While COVID survivorship and “long COVID” have centered on neuropsychiatric and metabolic outcomes, this study highlights the unappreciated negative impact of endotracheal intubation on voice and communication related to this illness and reinforces the relationship between duration of intubation and subjective dyspnea after surviving critical illness.Keywords: CCQ, COVID‐19, CPIB, ICU, intubation, VHI‐10, vocal outcomes, voice

In the United States, 55,000 patients receive care in more than 6000 intensive care units (ICUs) daily. 1 , 2 Emerging evidence demonstrates that patients who spend time in an ICU are often left with physiologic impairments that affect health and overall quality of life. 3 , 4 Laryngeal functional impairment after critical illness and laryngeal intubation trauma are underrecognized. Recent studies have shown that 50% of ICU patients intubated for >12 hours had acute laryngeal injury (ALgI) 5 and 7% had unilateral vocal fold immobility (UVFI). 6 These injuries can permanently compromise vocal function, create glottic incompetence, and cause posterior glottic stenosis (PGS). All have major morbidity and quality of life sequelae. Laryngeal intubation injuries are mitigated by transitioning to tracheostomy within 1 week. 7

ICU survivors of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus‐2 (COVID) were at higher‐than‐normal risk of laryngeal injuries due to higher‐than‐normal rates of intubation. 5 , 7 In all, 5% to 10% of the 1.7 million patients infected in the United States (85,000‐170,000) required intubation for ventilation in an ICU. 8 , 9 Their laryngeal injury risk was increased because early tracheostomy was discouraged in COVID patients due to concerns of aerosolizing the virus and exposing caregivers, health care workers, and other patients.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 The majority of mechanically ventilated COVID patients experienced prolonged intubation (mean 21 days) with large ETTs due to concerns for clearance of airway secretions. 5 , 10 Both prolonged intubation and placement of large ETTs are factors that are strongly associated with the development of ALgI, UVFI, and PGS. 5 , 10 These injuries can have dramatic effects on voice and laryngeal health. Vocal impairments may profoundly affect a patient's quality of life. Voice is a key aspect of identity and communication, and any degradation in voice quality can lead to reduced self‐esteem, social isolation, and a diminished quality of life. 14 , 15

Improving survivorship for affected patients requires that we understand the burden of laryngeal injuries and understand the magnitude of vocal communication dysfunction resulting from prolonged intubation after COVID‐19. In the COVID‐survivorship literature, there are myriad studies on its short‐ and long‐term sequelae related to the many organ systems that the virus effected; however, dysphonia is often overlooked. It is anticipated that patients who experienced prolonged intubation during treatment of their COVID respiratory failure had significant and detrimental voice outcomes. The focus on COVID survivorship and post‐COVID‐related syndromes has led to the defining of a new condition, “Long COVID,” which consists of mainly neuropsychiatric, metabolic symptoms and fatigue, without a focus on voice and communication.16, 17, 18 In fact, voice and communication‐related issues are not even listed on the CDC webpage dedicated to “Long COVID” complications. 19 A lack of awareness of communicative disabilities may resulted in their underdiagnosis and undertreatment. Accordingly, this study aims to test the hypothesis that patients admitted to the ICU with COVID‐19 with or without intubation experience significant long‐term voice and communication disabilities.

Methods

This is a multi‐institutional, prospective cohort study using data from 13 tertiary care centers in the United States. Data were collected on all patients admitted to the ICU with COVID‐19 between 2020 and 2021 and eligible patients underwent longitudinally administered patient‐reported outcomes measures (PROMs) to evaluate voice, communication, and breathing outcomes following hospital discharge (Table 1). The study was approved by a central independent review board carried out by WGC in Princeton, New Jersey. Patient clinical data were collected retrospectively, and PROM data collected prospectively in 2022. The tertiary care institutions involved were chosen to ensure geographic representativeness.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To identify eligible patients for the study, each institution performed an electronic query into their respective electronic medical record (EMR) systems to identify patients who (a) tested positive by PCR to COVID‐19, and were (b) admitted to the ICU in 2020 and 2021, (c) age 18 years and older, and (d) not deceased. Patient data and contact information were collected. Data from each institution was entered into a standardized and secure excel database. Research personnel at each institution manually reviewed each list of intubated and non‐intubated patients to confirm true eligibility via EMR review. Any patients that met the above criteria but were not relevant to the study were excluded (e.g., admitted to the ICU for a separate disease process).

Clinical Data Extraction

Once a patient's eligibility had been confirmed, manual chart review was performed to collect retrospective clinical data, which was entered into a REDCap database. Information extracted included date of COVID PCR positivity, date of admission, intubation (y/n, date), tracheostomy placement (y/n, date), extubation or decannulation (y/n, date), date of discharge from hospital, medical comorbidities, need for prone positioning (y/n), need for ECMO (y/n), ETT size used, and tracheostomy tube size, if applicable.

Patient Questionnaires

Within 1 week of confirmed eligibility, patients were prospectively contacted via a templated recruitment email and phone call. After verbal informed consent, participants were sent an electronic link to baseline REDCap questionnaires. Questionnaires included the Voice Handicap Index (VHI‐10), The Communicative Participation Item Bank (CPIB), and Clinical COPD Questionnaire (CCQ). These questionnaires were specifically selected for their assessment of different aspects of patients' functional outcomes as they pertain to voice and communication.

The CPIB 10‐item short form is designed to assess an individual's ability to engage in communication in daily life situations by measuring the extent to which speech or voice disorders impact their ability to participate in various activities, like having conversations, making phone calls, or participating in meetings. 20 Examples of questions on the CPIB are, “does your condition interfere with talking with people you know,” and “does your condition interfere with getting your turn in a fast‐moving conversation.” Each item is scored from 3 (not at all) to 0 (very much), with a summary score that ranges from 0 to 30. These are converted to T‐scores. Higher scores represent better communication. No MCID has been established for the CPIB; thus 0.5 standard deviation (5 T‐points on CPIB) was used to determine whether a change is clinically meaningful. 21

The CCQ is a 10‐item questionnaire designed to assess symptom severity in the last 7 days and health‐related quality of life in individuals with breathing difficulties. 22 Patients who with a history of COVID and intubation have a higher risk of persistent breathing difficulties. CCQ scores range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating a worse health status. Lower scores indicate better health, and higher scores signify increased impairment. The minimally clinically important difference for CCQ is a change of 0.4 points. 22

Finally, the VHI‐10 was selected to assess how voice‐related issues impact the patient's daily life, whether in terms of functional limitations (eg, difficulty speaking), emotional distress, or physical discomfort. On the VHI‐10, higher scores (up to a maximum of 40) demonstrate handicap. For our study purposes, VHI‐10 scores less than 11 were considered normal, and a score of 11 and greater suggested a clinically significant voice problem. 23 The minimally clinically important difference for VHI‐10 is a change of 4 points. 24

Patients were asked to complete questionnaires within 1 to 3 days of receiving it. All survey data were de‐identified and recorded in the REDCap database. Spanish versions of the templated emails, phone calls, and surveys were available for any Spanish‐speaking patients. Patients that did not answer their phone were left a templated voicemail and called again within 3 to 5 days. The REDCap system was checked regularly for participant completion of the surveys, and follow‐up phone calls were made to encourage participation as needed.

Statistical Analysis

Univariate analyses were performed using chi‐square tests, Student t‐tests, and analysis of variance (ANOVA), where appropriate. Regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between length of intubation (days) and PROM scores (ie, VHI‐10, CPIB, and CCQ), controlling for ETT size. These covariates were chosen because they have been shown to be highly associated with acute laryngeal injury. 8 , 9 , 25 , 26 STATA was used for all analyses.

Results

A total of 308 patients enrolled and 271 (94.1%) had previously been admitted to the ICU for COVID‐related respiratory failure between 2020 and 2021 (Figure 1). In all, 221 (81.5%) required intubation for mechanical ventilation. The average duration of intubation was 17 days (95% CI: 15‐19 days) with a 7.5 median ETT size. Mean time from hospital discharge to completing the REDCap questionnaires was 489 days (95% confidence interval [CI]: 452‐526 days).

Voice and Breathing After Intubation

In regression analysis controlling for ETT size, we found no association between days of intubation and either VHI‐10 or CPIB score. In contrast, regression analysis did show that days intubated was associated with CCQ score. Specifically, each additional day intubated there was a 0.25 increase in CCQ score (coefficient 0.25, 95% CI 0.14‐0.49; P = .04). Patients who had been intubated reported worse VHI‐10 scores than those who were not (15 vs 10; P = .01). Intubated patients also had lower CPIB scores were lower in the intubated group, with a mean score of 27 compared to 30 in the nonintubated group (P < .01), reflecting reduced communicative participation in the intubated cohort (Figure 2, Table 1). In secondary analyses, we repeated each regression analyses after dichotomizing ETT size into those who had a 7 ETT and those who had large ETT. Findings did not differ from the primary analyses.

Voice and Breathing After Tracheostomy

Among those intubated, 83 (37.6%) patients ultimately underwent tracheostomy. In fact, 15 (18%) still had their tracheostomy a mean 473 days (SD 170) after discharge, suggesting ongoing airway management needs or prolonged recovery. There were no differences in VHI‐10, CCQ, or CPIB scores between participants who did or did not undergo tracheostomy (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides evidence that intubation is associated with significant and long‐lasting communication impairments in patients who were critically ill with COVID‐19. The data also demonstrate an association between the length of intubation and worsened subjective dyspnea (measured via CCQ). The finding that longer intubation duration correlates with worse CCQ scores underscores the broader health implications of extended mechanical ventilation and the severity of disease, including the potential for increased mortality and diminished overall health status.

The analysis of vocal outcomes was conducted at an average of 489 days postdischarge. The results show the deleterious effects of critical illness and intubation on the voice persisting for more than a year after the initial recovery period. Intubated patients reported higher VHI‐10 scores, with a mean of 15, compared to nonintubated patients with a mean of 10. This finding suggests these patients continue to experience a perceived handicap in their voice, affecting their daily communication and overall quality of life. 23 , 27 , 28 These scores parallel those of patients with severe voice disorders. Additionally, the CPIB scores were notably lower in the intubated group, which would indicate decreased ability to participate in daily communicative tasks. The intubated group had a mean score of 27, compared to 30 in the nonintubated group, further highlighting the long‐term impact on communicative participation and social interaction. It is important to recognize that being intubated may be a proxy for more severe COVID illness and may portend worse pulmonary function upon discharge that may persist and contribute to poor breath support and dysphonia.

The overall tracheostomy rate among intubated patients in our study was 37.6%, as compared to the 10% to 15% rate typically cited rate among intubated ICU patients who are suffering from non‐COVID‐related illnesses. 16 This confirms a unique aspect of the COVID‐19 pandemic, in which a significant proportion of patients required extended respiratory support, eventually getting a tracheostomy. Importantly, there were not statistically significant differences between PROM scores (VHI‐10, CCQ, CPIB) when comparing intubated patients who had a tracheostomy and intubated patients who did not have a tracheostomy, indicating that either the severity of the critical illness itself or perhaps intubation were primary drivers of the long‐term functional communication impairment rather than the subsequent tracheostomy. While other studies have compared rates of dysphonia and long‐term voice outcomes in patients who had COVID, 29 this is the only study that looks specifically at voice and communication outcomes in the critically ill COVID population needing ICU‐level care. Several studies comment on the effects of prolonged intubation on swallowing, breathing, and voicing in both COVID and non‐COVID populations, demonstrating a relationship between prolonged intubation and increased incidence of these issues, especially in the COVID‐19 population.2, 3, 4, 25, 26 However, this study is the only one to look at these issues more than 1 year after intubation and infection occurred. While we were unable to evaluate the direct effects of COVID on voice and breathing, this study takes advantage of a natural experiment that led to prolonged intubation in the ICU—namely the COVID pandemic, further associating these known effects.

Study limitations deserve note. First, while many patients were identified through EMR screening as being eligible for our study, only a subset met inclusion criteria, were contacted and consented for the study. Nonresponse bias and volunteer bias may affect results. The comparatively low number of patients that could be recruited from a larger pool may also signify the profound effect that surviving ICU‐level care in the setting of COVID‐19 infection had on patients. They were either too ill to be able to participate in a survey study several years later or had succumbed to their illness. Second, the study design obviated the ability to evaluate the vocal folds of effected patients. Thus, we cannot comment on mechanism of long‐term dysphonia documented on the PROMs. While each PROM has associated weaknesses, those chosen are among the most commonly used and rigorously validated instruments available. Additionally, the study design involved patient completed questionnaires. Not all questions were completed, which resulted in some missing data. Missingness effects statistical power and could have impacted study results. Moreover, the study was not designed to disentangle the effects of prolonged intubation and the underlying pulmonary consequences related to COVID infection. Both intubation effects and the pulmonary disease resulting from COVID could have impacts on voice and communication. Finally, this study did not compare the long‐term voice sequelae from intubation between COVID and non‐COVID populations, which, in future studies, may allow us to isolate the effects of COVID on voice among the critically ill.

Conclusions

These data demonstrate that survivors of COVID‐19 infection requiring ICU admission and intubation suffer from persistent functional impairments in voicing and breathing. This study highlights the unappreciated negative impact of critical illness and intubation on voice and communication. Communication sequelae need to be considered in management among COVID survivors who were critically ill. While COVID survivorship has centered on neuropsychiatric and metabolic outcomes, the data from this research support the inclusion of voice and communication in active longitudinal study of the sequalae of COVID.
