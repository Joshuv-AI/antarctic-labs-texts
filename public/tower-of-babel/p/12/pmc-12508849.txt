Short‑term effects of ambient air pollution exposure on hospital emergency room visits for atrial fibrillation: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Yu-Shan Huang, Li-Wei Lo, Tsung-Ying Tsai, Hsin-Bang Leu, Shih-Ann Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough air pollutants are linked to cardiopulmonary mortality, their impact on cardiac arrhythmias is not well understood. This study examines the short-term effects of air pollution on emergency admissions for acute atrial fibrillation (AF) in Taiwan.MethodsThis study used Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database, including 16,778,374 participants aged 20 and older, residing in the same districts during 10-year follow-ups from 2008 to 2017. Hourly air pollutant exposure data were obtained from the Taiwan Environmental Protection Administration Database. Records of patients with ICD-9 code 427.31 (AF) as the primary diagnosis from emergency departments were extracted. Emergency visits for AF were compared across exposures to pollutants such as particulate matter PM2.5, PM10, Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2), Nitrogen Oxide (NO), Nitrogen Oxides (NOX), Sulphur Dioxide (SO2), Carbon monoxide (CO) and Ozone (O3).ResultsIn our study cohort of 16,778,374 patients, 129,595 (0.77 %) were admitted to emergency departments for initial AF episodes. Significant associations were found between AF visits and PM2.5 (1.01 %; CI: 1.00–1.02 %; P = 0.003), PM10 (1.01 %; CI: 1.00–1.01 %; P = 0.001), NO2 (1.02 %; CI: 1.00–1.03 %; P = 0.001), NO (1.02 %; CI: 1.00–1.04 %; P = 0.016), NOx (1.01 %; CI: 1.00–1.01 %; P = 0.002), CO (1.05 %; CI: 1.00–1.11 %; P < 0.0001), with exposure levels on the event day compared to the previous 5 days. Except for O3, patients without comorbidities like coronary artery disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and thyroid disease were more susceptible to air pollution.ConclusionsHigh concentrations of ambient air pollutants with short-term exposure are linked to an increased number of emergency room visits for acute AF attacks.

Introduction

Air pollution is increasingly recognized as one of the main risk factors for morbidity, and premature death. Epidemiological research has shown that short- and long- term exposure to an increased level of air pollution is associated with the development of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1].

Among the most frequent cardiovascular diseases in the general population, atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, which can lead to increasing risks of stroke, being responsible for increasing healthcare costs and hospitalizations [2,3]. Several risk factors yield to a substrate for AF, including advanced age, male sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), obesity, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, and valvular heart disease [4]. In addition to the traditional risk factors contributing to AF, the increasing prevalence of this arrhythmia in conjunction with the global increase in air pollution levels, especially PM2.5, could potentially have significant inter-relationships.

Recent studies have shown that PM2.5 exposure may contribute to AF [5]. Possible reasons include the adverse effects on cardiac autonomic regulation [6,7]; inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered atrial pressure caused by PM2.5 [8,9].

Over the past few years, numerous research groups have explored the link between both short and long-term exposure of PM2.5 and the occurrence of AF in the general population. However, most of the studies on the impact of short-term exposure of PM2.5 were conducted in North America or Europe [10,11]. Also, relatively few studies simultaneously investigated the relationship between the different air pollution beyond PM2.5.

Thus, we focused on the short-term effects of ambient air pollutants on acute AF attack with emergency room visits, and aimed to evaluate the association between new-onset AF and the exposure to ambient air including particulate matter (PM), carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O3), nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) in Taiwan.

Methods

Study population

This study used the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) in Taiwan and comprised 16,778,374 participants over 20 years old staying in the same districts during 10-year follow- ups between 2008 and 2017 in Taiwan.

Anonymous records for patients, with a new diagnostic record of ICD-9 code 427.31 (AF) as the primary diagnosis from emergency departments have been extracted from the administrative database recording in National Health Insurance Research Database. The repetitive admission of emergency department, or known history of AF were excluded. The baseline characteristics, including age and sex, as well as their comorbidities such as ischemic heart disease (ICD-9: 410–422), heart failure (ICD-9: 428), chronic kidney disease (ICD-9: 585), ischemic stroke (ICD-9: 434), asthma (ICD-9: 493), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (ICD-9: 490–492, 496), thyroid disease (ICD-9: 240–246), other cardiac arrhythmias including paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) (ICD-9: 427.0) and ventricular tachycardia (VT) (ICD-9: 427.1), and medication history, were recorded for each enrolled patient. The study was approved by the National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University Institutional Review Board (IRB) with the reference number YM111051E before commencing enrollment.

Environmental data

Hourly concentrations of air pollutants including PM, CO, O3, NO2 and SO2 were obtained from Taiwan Environmental Protection Administration Database at different districts from 2008 to 2017.

Because air pollution circulation exhibits watershed characteristics and crosses county boundaries, the Environmental Protection Administration divides the Taiwan region into seven air quality zones (AQ zones) based on topography, climate, wind direction, and pollution dispersion conditions. These zones are respectively the North, ChuMia, Central, YunChiaNan, KaoPing, Yilan, and Eastern regions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Only monitors with equal to or more than 75 % yearly data coverage were considered eligible, and data referred to hourly measurements and passed the quality control of the agency. Exposure was determined as the average of daily mean concentrations measured by three designated and suitable monitoring stations.

The short-term association between exposure to air pollutants and acute episodes of AF was estimated through lag analyses. We collected environmental data on the concentration of each air pollutant for patients who met the criteria for emergency visits due to AF. Daily concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, CO, O3, NO2, and SO2 were recorded on the day of the emergency visit and up to 21 days prior to the visit. The definition of lags is as follows: day 0 represents air pollution exposure on the same day as the emergency hospital visit, lag 1 to 21 represents air pollution exposure from 1 day before (lag 1) up to 21 days before (lag 21) the emergency hospital visit.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables and as mean ± 95 % confidence interval (CI) for categorical variables. The analysis of the association between daily exposure to air pollution and emergency hospital visits for AF was conducted separately for five lag days. Conditional logistic regression was utilized to calculate adjusted odds ratios (OR), and 95 % CI were determined for every unit increase in each pollutant within a 24-hour concentration compared with different lag periods. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted to assess possible effect modifications for sex, age category (<75 and > 75 years old), and pre-existing medical conditions including hypertension, diabetes, stroke, coronary artery disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and thyroid disorders individually.

Results

Enrollment and analysis population

During the period from 2008 to 2017, 90,295 (0.54 %) of 16,778,374 individuals (47,963 males, 53.1 %) were admitted to the emergency departments due to a first- time AF attack. The age of the study participants who experienced attacks ranged from 20 to 106 years, with a mean age of 69.1 ± 13.8 years. Of the total emergency room visits for AF attacks, 60.4 % of individuals had a pre-existing medical condition of hypertension, and 62.1 % had prior medication with beta-blockers. The effect on emergency visits for AF

Basic characteristics of air pollutants

The distribution of PM2.5 in different Air Quality zones is illustrated as an example in Supplementary Fig. 2, and the air pollutant monitoring data provided by the relevant monitoring agencies in each Air Quality zone all show similar Gaussian distributions. There are differences in the distribution of PM2.5 concentration among various zones. Yilan and Eastern zones, located along the Pacific coast, demonstrated left-shifted distributions with sharp peaks at values <15 μg/m3, consistent with cleaner and more stable air quality due to prevailing Pacific winds, lower population density, and the absence of heavy industry. In contrast, the KaoPing and YunChiaNan zones exhibited right-shifted distributions with higher mean concentrations, peaks between 30 and 50 μg/m3, and long-tailed values extending beyond 100 μg/m3, confirming these regions as Taiwan’s most polluted areas. These differences reflect regional emission sources and dispersion patterns, particularly the concentration of industrial activity on the southern and central west coasts.

Discussion

Main findings of the study

The most significant finding of this study was the notable relationships between acute exposure to air pollutants within one week and the rapid onset of symptomatic AF, with varying impacts observed on different days involved. We found that high concentrations of air pollution may trigger AF emergency visits in the short term. Moreover, this effect appeared to be more pronounced among female patients, those under 75 years old, and individuals without pre-existing underlying diseases.

The characteristics for air pollutants

Daily air pollutant concentrations were obtained from the Taiwan Environmental Protection Administration Database recorded in specific districts. The data were collected from the event day with AF admission occurring within the previous 3 weeks. The highest concentrations were recorded on the event day for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NO, NOX, SO2 and CO, except for O3, which had its highest level on Lag 2. Recognizing that air pollution can cause harm at much lower levels than previously believed, the World Health Organization (WHO) released revised air quality guidelines in 2021 with more stringent limits compared to the 2005 version [12].

The minimum 24-hour average concentration of PM2.5, PM10, and NO2 within the three weeks preceding the event day was 23.71 µg/m3, 47.32 µg/m3, and 14.94 ppb, respectively. These values fall below the Taiwanese Environmental Protection Agency's daily average standard [13,14] of 35 µg/m3 for PM2.5, 100 µg/m3 for PM10, and 30 ppb for NO2. However, the thresholds in Taiwan significantly exceed double the standard of 15 µg/m3 for PM2.5, 45 µg/m3 for PM10, and 25 µg/m3 (13.29 ppb) for NO2 published in the WHO 2021 Air Quality Guidelines. For SO2, CO, and O3, the highest values measured within the specific period are all below the air quality standards announced in Taiwan and the WHO guidelines, even though the standard in Taiwan is higher than the standard set by the WHO.

Importantly, regional variation in pollutant distribution suggests that Air Quality zones may act as a contextual modifier of exposure risk. While our nationwide design allowed comprehensive assessment, differences between clean-air regions such as Yilan and Eastern zones and highly polluted zones such as KaoPing and YunChiaNan imply that the health impact of PM2.5 and other pollutants may be more pronounced in heavily burdened areas. These findings align with the geographic concentration of industrial emissions and higher population density on Taiwan’s western coast, contrasting with the cleaner Pacific coast. Future studies incorporating zonal stratification or interaction analysis will be valuable to disentangle whether air quality zones serve as confounders or effect modifiers in the relationship between air pollutants and AF onset.

The new air quality guidelines are ambitious and restrictive, and local conditions should be taken into consideration by respective governments. These results also reflect the significant impact that air pollution has on global health [15].

Association between air pollutants and AF emergency visits

Short-term exposure to air pollution can precipitate various cardiac arrhythmias, with our study focusing on AF. Presently, air quality is one of the most pressing concerns, and numerous studies have indicated that air pollutants may induce cardiac autonomic dysregulation [[16], [17], [18]], potentially resulting in alterations in atrial electrophysiology. Furthermore, inflammation and oxidative stress may also influence atrial pressure, contributing to episodes of AF [[19], [20], [21]].

To date, more studies have focused on epidemiological evidence regarding the effects of air pollution on the incidence and occurrence of AF [[22], [23], [24]]. However, the majority of these studies have primarily addressed long-term exposure rather than short-term exposure. Additionally, the reported issues have predominantly focused on particulate matter, including PM2.5 and PM10 only [25,26].

These studies have suggested an increased risk of AF emergency visits associated with exposure to both PM2.5 and PM10. Consistent with these findings, we observed that the risk of acute AF episodes abruptly increased with higher concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10. However, we also identified a hysteresis effect, wherein the concentrations significantly increased on the previous days before the event day [27]. In this research, we observed that CO and SO2 exposure exhibited a similar hysteresis effect as fine particulate matter, leading to an increased risk of daily emergency visits due to AF attacks. The highest concentrations were found on the same day as the emergency visits. This finding might provide evidence that the arrhythmogenic effects of CO and SO2 are similar to those of PM2.5 mentioned above.

Numerous epidemiological studies have furnished substantial evidence indicating that elevated levels of ambient NOx, frequently used as a surrogate for traffic-related air pollutants, are associated with cardiovascular mortalities [28,29]. Solveig et al. demonstrated that short-term increases in NO2 concentrations were associated with heart diseases, specifically with AF in Reykjavik, Iceland. The strongest results were observed at day 0 and lag 1 [30]. Our results also establish an association between an increased risk of AF emergency visits and elevated NOx levels. In contrast to previous findings, our results showed an effect only on the event day, with both NO and NO2 concentrations abruptly increasing. Moreover, these effects were stronger than those observed with PM2.5.

An epidemiological study found that O3 exposure was associated with an increased risk of emergency room visits due to arrhythmias [31], although there was limited evidence showing that O3 exposure can trigger the onset of cardiac arrhythmias [32,33]. Notably, our investigation has revealed the delayed impact of O3 exposure specifically on AF attacks, with the most substantial effects observed following exposure in the two days preceding the event. The 24-hour average O3 concentration reached its peak on the lag2 day (29.42 ppb), demonstrating a lag effect characterized by a 0.51 % increase in O3 (p = 0.029). These findings underscore the critical significance of ongoing O3 monitoring, as it may contribute to the occurrence of subsequent AF attacks in the following days.

In Fig. 2, the consistent small upward trends observed across pollutants in the days preceding D0 are likely attributable to averaging across multiple monitoring stations nationwide. Such aggregation smooths out natural day-to-day fluctuations that would be apparent in raw single-station data, and therefore limits the direct applicability of these curves to actionable air quality management. Instead, they provide epidemiological evidence of short-term pollutant accumulation preceding AF onset on a population level.

Emerging evidence further supports mechanistic pathways between air pollution, blood pressure elevation, and arrhythmogenesis. A recent randomized crossover trial demonstrated that high-efficiency particulate arrestance (HEPA) filtration reduced blood pressure compared with sham filtration[34]. Given that acute increases in blood pressure can precipitate atrial stretch and trigger AF, it is plausible that pollutant-induced vascular stress may act as an intermediate pathway. Although our current dataset cannot directly evaluate the efficacy of HEPA filtration on AF prevention, these findings highlight the potential of targeted indoor interventions to mitigate cardiovascular risk during periods of elevated ambient pollution. Future interventional studies should clarify whether reducing pollutant exposure through HEPA or similar strategies can effectively lower AF incidence.

Effect of pre-existing medical conditions

Baseline comorbidities play a crucial role in shaping the susceptibility to AF under environmental stressors. Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and structural heart disease are well-recognized contributors to atrial remodeling, autonomic imbalance, and systemic inflammation, all of which predispose to AF onset [4,35]. Prior studies have demonstrated that patients with multimorbidity are at heightened risk for arrhythmia-related hospitalizations and adverse outcomes [36]. Moreover, systemic inflammation and oxidative stress—common in conditions such as heart failure, ischemic heart disease, and CKD can synergize with pollutant-induced pathways, thereby amplifying arrhythmogenic vulnerability [9].

Upon PM2.5 exposure, subgroup analysis revealed that individuals aged > 65 years may be more likely to develop AF compared to those < 65 years old. Additionally, patients with comorbidities were reported to be more sensitive to PM2.5 [37]. Contrary to the majority of the results, our subgroup analysis indicated that younger individuals aged less than 75 years old or those without comorbidities are more susceptible to stimulation by exposure to PM2.5.

Apart from PM2.5, the modification of the effect of other ambient air pollutants on AF emergency visits was heterogenous [27,28,38]. However, there is more consensus that older age, male gender, and hypertension were clear modifiers [27]. In our study, exposure to PM10, NO2, NO, SO2, and CO exhibited significant effects across different age groups, genders, and among patients with or without hypertension, with a similar degree of impact. Similarly to PM2.5, there was a more pronounced significance observed in patients without comorbidities when exposed to other air pollutants.

Our findings showed that individuals without such comorbidities demonstrated relatively greater susceptibility to pollution-related AF, which may reflect fewer competing risk factors and highlight air pollution as an independent and potent arrhythmic trigger. This paradox has also been observed in previous epidemiological and meta-analytic studies, where differential susceptibility across comorbidity subgroups underscores the complexity of interaction between environmental exposures and clinical risk factors [37,38]. Moreover, our analysis confirmed the consistent strong influence of AF with traditional major risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, diseases of the heart such as history of heart failure, and coronary heart disease. Consequently, these observations suggest that while patients with established comorbidities remain vulnerable due to their intrinsic atrial substrate, individuals in relatively healthy groups may experience air pollution as a leading environmental precipitant of symptomatic acute AF attacks.

Limitations

As with many other studies utilizing the NHIRD, several crucial limitations need to be addressed. First, our study aimed to assess the association between AF and air pollution by enrolling patients with a primary diagnosis of AF, excluding those with prior diagnoses based on the ICD-10 code, suspecting it to be their first-time attack. However, we may have missed some patients with acute-onset AF who were not recognized with a primary diagnosis of AF in emergency records, and we may have misclassified patients as experiencing their first AF attack due to the loss of clearly recognized prior diagnoses of AF in outpatient records. Secondly, the dates of emergency visits should be taken into account, as they may occur hours or days after the onset of AF, potentially leading to exposure misclassification. Thirdly, concerning outpatient conditions or underlying comorbidities, additional information was limited, such as socioeconomic status, lifestyle, health behavior, or unhealthy habits, which could also influence the incidence of AF or the likelihood of admission or outpatient follow-up.

Finally, our study did not include laboratory data, such as serum electrolyte levels. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia, are well-recognized precipitants of AF, as they influence atrial refractoriness, conduction velocity, and susceptibility to triggered activity [39,40]. Future studies incorporating biochemical parameters will be essential to clarify whether electrolyte disturbances interact with air pollution exposure in predisposing individuals to acute AF onset.

Conclusions

Our comprehensive 10-year population-based nationwide cohort study has revealed a compelling association between short-term exposure to ambient PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, CO and O3 and an elevated incidence of AF. Notably, our investigation has elucidated a clear link between acute exposure to PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, and CO within 24 h and the delayed impact of O3 exposure specifically on AF attacks.

Additionally, changes in air pollutant concentrations exceeding a mean of 1 % are associated with increased emergency department visits for initial symptomatic AF attacks. This emphasizes the importance of ongoing efforts to reduce individual air pollutant levels and implement prompt protective measures, especially for susceptible populations during periods of elevated pollution.
