Reduced dynamicity and increased high-order protein assemblies in dense fibrillar component of the nucleolus under cellular senescence
Authors: Minjeong Jo, Soomin Kim, Jeongeun Park, Young-Tae Chang, Youngdae Gwon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cellular senescence, which is triggered by various stressors, manifests as irreversible cell cycle arrest, resulting in the disruption of multiple nuclear condensates. One of the affected structures is the nucleolus, whose tripartite layout, separated into distinct liquid phases, allows for the stepwise progression of ribosome biogenesis. The dynamic properties of dense fibrillar components, a sub-nucleolar phase, are crucial for mediating pre-rRNA processing. However, the mechanistic link between the material properties of dense fibrillar components and cellular senescence remains unclear. We established a significant association between cellular senescence and alterations in nucleolar materiality and characteristics, including the number, size, and sphericity of individual subphases of the nucleolus. Senescent cells exhibit reduced fibrillarin dynamics, aberrant accumulation of high-order protein assemblies, such as oligomers and fibrils, and increased dense fibrillar component density. Intriguingly, the addition of RNA-interacting entities mirrored the diminished diffusion of fibrillarin in the nucleolus during cellular senescence. Thus, our findings contribute to a broader understanding of the intricate changes in the materiality of the nucleolus associated with cellular senescence and shed light on nucleolar dynamics in the context of aging and cellular stress.

Introduction

Biomolecular condensates, or briefly condensates, are subcellular compartments with different phase properties from their surroundings and are built upon the process of liquid-liquid phase separation through weak but multivalent interactions of biomolecular constituents [1]. Intermolecular interactions, which often reflect stoichiometric changes, intramolecular rearrangements, and chemical modifications of biomolecules as a consequence of cellular responses to physiological changes, collectively manifest as the formation, dissolution, and redistribution of condensates [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. The mitotic process, which entails cell division and significant remodeling of subcellular structures, is a representative event that leads to alterations in biomolecular condensates [6]. Several nuclear compartments, such as Cajal bodies and nuclear speckles, undergo a sequence of breakdown and buildup during mitosis [[6], [7], [8]]. The nucleolus, recognized as a key site for ribosome biogenesis, is another example of a nuclear condensate whose assembly status varies across mitotic stages [9,10]. Being conspicuous under phase-contrast microscopy because of its dense material states in interphase, the nucleolus dissolves during prophase with the cessation of rDNA transcription by RNA polymerase I [10,11]. As the cell cycle proceeds, it begins to condense from the nucleolar organizer region with multiple copies of rDNA, thereby acting as hubs for nucleolar assembly at late prophase [12]. This nucleolar condensation is a phase separation event of nucleolar proteomes and transcriptomes from the surrounding nucleoplasm [13,14].

The nucleolus is a multilayer biomolecular condensate composed of three coexisting but phase-separated substructures known as the fibrillar center (FC), dense fibrillar component (DFC), and granular component (GC) [11,14]. Because the individual phases contain functionally linked profiles of nucleic acids and proteins, their layered architectures, resembling Matryoshka dolls, enable the stepwise progression of ribosomal biogenesis [15]. At the interface between the FC and the DFC, rDNA transcription generates pre-rRNA [11]. Subsequently, pre-rRNA undergoes chemical modification and cleavage in DFC [15]. In GC, mature rRNA species assemble with ribosomal proteins to form the 40S and 60S ribosome subunits, which are then exported to the cytoplasm [16,17]. Recent studies suggest a strong association between the material properties of sub-nucleolar phases and their functional roles. For instance, poly(Pro-Arg) dipeptide repeats, produced by the unconventional translation of G4C2 hexanucleotide repeats in the C9ORF72, alter the material state of the nucleolus [18]. This alteration is accompanied with the exclusion of NPM1 from the GC phase, resulting in defective ribosomal biogenesis [18,19]. In addition, optogenetic intervention to increase the homotypic interactions of NPM1 led to alterations in rRNA processing [20]. These findings demonstrate the causative role of nucleolar material in defining its function.

The aging of condensates is an emerging concept for understanding material transitions. The phase-separated droplets behave like Maxwell fluids with altered rheological features over time after they are initially formed [21,22]. This material shift is achieved by increasing the fraction of stable and long-lived interactions between the constituents of the condensate over transient and short-lived ones [23]. However, most studies on condensate aging have been limited to in vitro homogeneous droplets composed of few components [21,22]. Meanwhile, investigations into temporal changes in the materiality of cellular condensates over an extended period to elucidate the condensate aging phenomena was poorly conducted. Considering this gap, we hypothesized that the long-lived nucleolus, whose breakdown/buildup cycle ceases under prolonged mitotic arrest, could be an intriguing system to broaden the understanding of condensate aging at the cellular level [12].

In this study, we examined nucleolar changes in response to cellular senescence, in which cell cycle arrest is triggered by cellular stresses, including DNA damage, epigenetic disruption, and oxidative stress. We used multiple optical approaches, including FRAP, quantitative phase imaging, and immunodetection of high-order protein assemblies, to measure the physical properties of the nucleolus. We demonstrate that nucleolar characteristics such as quantity, size, sphericity, and spatial layout between sub-nucleolar phases are altered by cellular senescence. These changes were accompanied by diminished dynamics of the DFC scaffold protein fibrillarin (FIB), increased assembly of high-order proteins in the nucleoli, and an elevated mass density of DFC. Nucleolar changes during cellular senescence differ from those during acute stress because of irreversible modifications in protein insolubility. In summary, we show that altered nucleolar dynamics that propel aberrant protein aggregation are a novel phenotype of cellular senescence.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human osteosarcoma cell line U2OS, lung cancer cell line A549, liver cancer cell line HepG2, and normal human fetal lung fibroblast cell line IMR90 were used in this study. U2OS, A549, and HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning, USA). IMR90 cells were cultured in MEM (GenDepot, USA). Media were supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and 1 % GlutaMAX (Gibco, USA). U2OS cells were transfected with GFP-NPM1, FIB-GFP, and an RNA-binding domain containing FIB-GFP (FIB-RBD-GFP) using viaFect (Promega, USA) and selected with G418 (InvivoGen, USA) for 7 d. GFP-positive cells were sorted using FACSAria Fusion (BD Bioscience, USA) to generate U2OS GFP-NPM1, FIB-GFP, and FIB-RBD-GFP stable cells. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2.

Senescence assays

Senescence is induced by three mechanisms: DNA-damaged damage, oxidative stress, and replicative stress. Senescent cells were identified by a senescence-associated β-galactosidase kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with the fixative solution provided by the manufacturer for 15 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS again, and followed by acidic β-gal detection using a pH 6.0 staining solution overnight at 37 °C. SA-β-gal activity was detected by bright field images. For DNA-damaged stress-induced senescence, U2OS and HepG2 cells were treated with 2 μM etoposide for 72 and 96 h or with 5 μM decitabine for 96 h. For oxidative stress-induced senescence, U2OS and A549 cells were treated with 200 μM H2O2 for 2 h and cultured in normal medium for another 5 d. For replicative senescence, IMR90 cells at passages 17 and 29 were used.

Image acquisition and analysis

Confocal fluorescence microscopy images were acquired on a ZEISS (Jena, Germany) LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a C-Apochromat 40x/1.20 W Korr M27 objective and a Plan-Apochromat 100x/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective. When the fluorescence intensities were directly compared, the acquisition settings remained unchanged, and the resulting images were treated identically. Zen (Zeiss Zen Black edition) was used to prepare the maximum intensity z-projection, and images were analyzed using the ImageJ software (Rasband, W.S., National Institutes of Health, USA). Pearson's correlation coefficient was used to characterize the colocalization between nucleolar proteins and high-order protein assemblies using the ImageJ plugin, JACoP, on images collected from the experiments. Reconstructed 3-D images of the nucleoli were acquired using a commercial holotomography system (HT-2H, Tomocube, Inc., Republic of Korea), and the sphericity of the nucleolar subphases was analyzed using commercial software (AutoQuant X3, Media Cybernetics, USA). The nucleolar area and number of nucleoli in the nucleus were acquired using a ZOE fluorescent cell imager (Bio-Rad, USA) and quantified manually using ImageJ. Nucleolar eccentricities, including circularity, aspect ratio (AR), solidity, and roundness, were automatically analyzed using ImageJ software. Plasmid construction

To generate FIB-RBD, FIB fragments and RNA-binding domain fragments were inserted into pEGFP-N1 using the In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara Bio, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. FIB deletion mutants, FIB Δmethyltransferase (MT) and FIB ΔGly-Arg rich (GAR), were inserted into the KpnI and BglII sites of pmCherry-C1. These plasmid constructs were cloned using the Power-Pfu version 2.0 kit (Nanohelix, Republic of Korea) and transfected into U2OS cells with the viaFect transfection reagent (Promega, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. All constructs were fully sequenced to confirm the absence of unwanted substitutions.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis

Cells expressing FIB-GFP, FIB-RBD-GFP, or FIB ΔMT-GFP and cells transfected with FIB-mCherry, FIB ΔMT-mCherry or FIB ΔGAR-mCherry were grown in glass-bottom dishes (Cellvis, USA) and treated with inducers of cellular senescence. For every recovery, time-lapse imaging was performed using a ZEISS (Jena, Germany) LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100x/1.40 Oil DIC M27 objective. Circular regions of a constant size in each nucleolar component were photobleached and monitored for fluorescence recovery over time. The intensity recovery curves were normalized and corrected to set the pre-bleaching value to one and the postbleaching value to zero. The FRAP curves were fitted with one-phase exponential equations, and an exponential fit was used to extract the mobile fraction of fluorescence recovery. For FRAP analysis of RNA, live cells were stained with F22 dye, a specific dye for RNA, for 20 min after treatment with etoposide for 5 d. Data were obtained from at least seven different cells for each condition.

Correlative holotomography and fluorescence imaging

Holotomography and correlative fluorescence images of cells with subcellular organelles were obtained using a commercial holotomography system (HT-2H, Tomocube, Inc., Republic of Korea) based on Mach-Zehnder interferometry equipped with a digital micromirror device (DMD). A coherent monochromatic laser beam (532 nm wavelength) was split into two paths, one for illuminating the cells and the other serving as a reference beam, using a 2 × 2 single-mode fiber coupler. A 3-D refractive index (RI) tomogram was reconstructed from multiple 2-D holographic images acquired under 49 illumination conditions. These conditions included normal incidence and 48 azimuthally symmetric directions with a 64.5-degree polar angle. A DMD was used to control the angle of the illumination beam on the sample [24]. The diffracted beam from the sample was collected using a high-numerical-aperture (NA) objective lens (NA = 1.2, UPLSAP 60XW, Olympus, Japan). An off-axis hologram was recorded using a CMOS image sensor (FL3-U3-13Y3MC; FLIR Systems, USA). For holotomography and correlative fluorescence imaging, the cells were seeded onto a TomoDish (Tomocube, Inc., Republic of Korea). FIB and NPM1 were fluorescently stained to classify DFC and GC, respectively, whereas the nuclei were stained with Hoechst. After cell preparation, cellular senescence was induced by etoposide, H2O2, and replicative stress. The deconvolution of the reconstructed 3-D fluorescence images was performed using a commercial software (AutoQuant X3, Media Cybernetics, USA). Visualization of the 3-D RI distribution and its correlative 3-D fluorescence signal with a red pseudo-color was conducted using commercial software (TomoStudio, Tomocube, Inc., Republic of Korea).

Congo red staining

The cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.2 % Triton X-100 for 5 min at RT. The cells were stained with 1 % Congo red (Thermo Scientific, USA) in 50 % EtOH for 5 min in the dark and then incubated with 0.01 % KOH in 50 % EtOH for 1 min to stain the Congo red. A 1 % Congo red solution was filtrated through a 0.2-μm syringe filter (Hyundai Micro, Republic of Korea) immediately prior to use. The cells were washed three times with PBS and mounted with 50 % glycerol for visualization.

Western blot assay

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed at 4 °C with protein lysis buffer (pH 7.5 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 % Triton X-100, and 2 mM EDTA) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Quartett, Germany) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (GenDepot, USA). The cell lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min, sonicated for 30 s and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. For the soluble fraction, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Biomax, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Proteins were mixed with 4X sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample loading buffer. For the insoluble fraction, the pellet was collected and mixed with 1.2X SDS sample loading buffer. Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) for 120 min and transferred to a PVDF membrane (GVS Filter Technology, Italy) for 90 min. The membrane was blocked with 3 % (w/v) bovine serum albumin (GenDepot, USA) in 0.1 % Tween-20 in 1X Tris-buffered saline (1X TBS-T) on a rotating shaker for 60 min at RT. The blocked membrane was incubated with optimal concentrations of primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: rabbit polyclonal anti-FIB (Proteintech, 1:2000 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-p53 (Proteintech, 1:2000), rabbit polyclonal anti-p21 (Proteintech, 1:2000 dilution), mouse monoclonal anti-VHL (Santa Cruz, 1:1000 dilution), and mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz, 1:2000 dilution). The membranes were washed three times with 1X TBS-T for 5 min and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) at RT for 1 h. Immunolabeled proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Biomax, Republic of Korea).

Antibodies and dyes for immunofluorescence

Primary antibodies used in this study were rabbit polyclonal anti-protein oligomers (A11, Invitrogen, 1:500 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-amyloid fibrils (OC, Merk Millipore, 1:1000 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-FIB (Proteintech, 1:1000 dilution), mouse monoclonal anti-VHL (Santacruz, 1:300 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-CDK1 (Proteintecn, 1:500 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-DDX39B (Proteintech, 1:500 dilution), rabbit polyclonal anti-p53 (Proteintech, 1:500), and rabbit polyclonal anti-TDP43 (Proteintech, 1:500 dilution). Secondary antibodies specific to rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, 1:500 dilution), rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa 555 (Invitrogen, 1:500 dilution), and mouse IgG conjugated to Alexa 555 (Invitrogen, 1:500 dilution) were used. 5 μM F22 dye was used to stain RNA.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were grown on coverslips (Marienfeld, Germany). For immunostaining, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS (Thermo Scientific, USA) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by extensive washing with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized for 5 min with Triton X-100 0.2 % (v/v) in PBS at room temperature, before blocking with PBS containing 3 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) and Triton X-100 0.1 % (V/V) at room temperature for 60 min. Primary antibodies were applied in PBS supplemented with 1 % BSA and 0.1 % Triton X-100 and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied at a dilution of 1:500 after washing with PBS supplemented with 1 % bovine serum albumin for 60 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst stain in PBS for 10 min and washed again before mounting the samples in 70 % glycerol (v/v) in PBS.

Heat shock and sodium arsenite treatments

Cells were transferred to a 43 °C humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 for 30 min for heat shock. After heat shock exposure, cells were restored to a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 for 1 h to examine recovery. For treatment with sodium arsenite (Fisher Scientific, USA), sodium arsenite was diluted to 0.5 mM in media, and then cells were grown in the media containing 0.5 mM sodium arsenite for 30 min. After incubation for 30 min, the cells were kept in fresh medium for 1 h after the washout of sodium arsenite to establish the effect of recovery.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software. The data were examined using the D'Agostino-Pearson test for normal distribution and passed the normality test. Comparisons between two means were performed using an unpaired t-test. Comparisons between multiple means over two were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's test. ANOVA with an FDR test was used to compare the quantification of protein solubility in the western blot assay. The specific details are provided in the relevant figure legends. A statistical p-value of <0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Altered size, shape, and number per cell of nucleoli and sub-nucleolar architecture under cellular senescence

To evaluate the changes in nucleolar characteristics resulting from cell cycle arrest in senescent cells, we exposed U2OS cells to various stressors that induce genotoxic stress (etoposide), abnormal DNA methylation (decitabine), and oxidative stress (H2O2). All three stressors induced cellular senescence, as examined by senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA β-gal) staining and the expression of p53 and p21 (Fig. S1A-D). Senescent U2OS cells expressing GFP-NPM1 to visualize nucleoli showed a half-decline in the number of nucleoli and an approximately five-fold enlargement in nucleolar size compared to non-senescent cells (Fig. 1A and B, Fig. S1E). In addition, to estimate the nucleolar morphology under replicative senescence, we compared IMR90 fibroblasts at passage (P)17 with P29, as examined by SA β-gal staining (Fig. S1F). Similar to U2OS cells, IMR90 fibroblasts undergoing replicative senescence exhibited decreased nucleolar numbers and increased nucleolar sizes (Fig. S1G and H). To explore the three-dimensional shape of the nucleolus, we measured its sphericity using a holotomographic microscope and found that the nucleoli of senescent cells were more spherical than those of non-senescent cells (Fig. 1C, Video S1-4). Consistent with these observations, IMR90 cells undergoing replicative senescence showed similar changes in nucleolar shape (Fig. S2E-HFigs. S2E–H). Next, we applied the same approach to measure the size, number per cell, and morphology of DFC, a discrete sub-nucleolar phase embraced by GC, during cellular senescence using U2OS cells expressing GFP-tagged FIB [15]. In contrast to the prior results, senescent cells showed an approximately five-fold increase in DFC number, while the size of the DFC was reduced by approximately two-thirds compared to non-senescent cells (Fig. 1D and E). DFC in senescent cells exhibited a sphere-like morphology compared to those in non-senescent cells (Fig. 1F and Fig. S2I-L). Notably, more DFCs were localized near the boundary of the nucleoli in senescent cells (Fig. 1G–I). The ratio of the nucleolar radius to the distance from the center of the nucleolus to the DFC was significantly increased by the senescence stimuli (Fig. 1G and H). In conclusion, we observed significant alterations in the nucleolar characteristics, including number, shape, and sub-nucleolar architecture, in senescent cells.

Dynamical arrest of FIB and increased mass density of DFC under cellular senescence

To interpret our findings of altered nucleolar features (fewer but larger GCs and more but smaller DFCs) in senescent cells, we distinguished between GC and DFC in terms of coalescence. Although the fusion ability of GCs was retained, that of DFCs was largely impaired, considering their size and quantity, during prolonged cellular senescence. This contrasts with a prior observation of promoted nucleolar fusion in X. laevis oocytes, which showed that DFCs that were initially located in separate nucleoli fused together within a few hours of the coalescence of two nucleoli [14]. Subsequently, the number of DFCs per nucleolus rose rapidly and then gradually declined until it reached a level comparable to that before coalescence in X. laevis oocytes [14]. However, our observation on the increase in the number of DFCs per nucleolus in senescent cells may indicate that fusion events between DFCs are significantly inhibited (Fig. 1, Fig. 2A). Based on this, we postulated that DFCs in senescent cells are aged condensates with reduced dynamicity and performed FRAP in U2OS cells expressing FIB-GFP during cellular senescence induced by etoposide, decitabine, and hydrogen peroxide (Fig. 2A–D, Fig. S3A-C, Video S5–S10) [22]. FIB-GFP showed less fluorescence recovery in the bleached area and a decreased mobile fraction in senescent U2OS cells than in non-senescent cells (Fig. 2E–G). A widely accepted explanation for the reduced dynamics of biomolecular condensates is the constrained movement of individual molecules due to the conversion of intermolecular interactions from transient to stable [25,26]. This transition often leads to a compact arrangement of the constituent molecules and a subsequent increase in density [27]. Quantitative phase imaging was used to measure the mass density of the nucleolar subphases. This method yields the RI, which is reconstructed three-dimensionally based on the degree of light scattering around the specimen and is proportional to the mass density [28,29]. Senescent cells induced by etoposide, decitabine, and H2O2 exhibited an elevated RI measured from FIB-positive DFC and NPM1-positive GC compared to non-senescent cells (Fig. 2H–J). IMR90 cells undergoing replicative senescence with high passage numbers and senescent A549 cells induced by oxidative stress also showed an increased DFC RI compared to non-senescent IMR90 and A549 cells (Fig. S3G and H). Furthermore, we observed a significant difference between the RIs of the DFC and GC in the NPM1-positive area, reflecting the altered mass distribution inside the nucleolus during cellular senescence (Fig. 2H–J and Fig. S3G, H). Thus, DFCs in senescent cells may be analogous to aged condensates, considering the decreased diffusivity of FIB and increased mass density.

Elevated high-order protein assemblies in the nucleolus under cellular senescence

When biomolecular condensates undergo aberrant phase transitions to less dynamic states, the formation of insoluble protein aggregates is often accelerated [[30], [31], [32]]. As a result, protein oligomers, protofibrils, and fibrils, which are the intermediates or final products of protein aggregation, are readily found in condensates with arrested dynamics [33,34]. To visualize these high-order protein assemblies in the nucleolar subphases, we used A11 and OC antibodies to detect protein oligomers and fibrils, respectively, by immunofluorescence. By analyzing the colocalized immunoreactivities of either A11 or OC with NPM1 and FIB scaffolds for each nucleolar subphase, we obtained spatiotemporal information on the distribution of protein oligomers and fibrils within the nucleolus as cellular senescence progressed. The degree of colocalization between protein oligomers stained with the A11 antibody and GFP-NPM1 labeling GCs only increased after 3 days of etoposide treatment and remained unchanged after 5 days of treatment compared to that in non-senescent cells (Fig. 3A–C). This phasic colocalization pattern was also present in the nucleoplasm, implying that the fate of protein oligomers in the GC and nucleoplasm, to some extent, might be dictated by shared regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 3A) [35]. In contrast, FIB-GFP-positive DFCs showed a progressive increase in proteins with oligomeric conformations over time following etoposide treatment (Fig. 3D–F). Meanwhile, the localization of insoluble protein fibrils stained with the OC antibody in both GCs and DFCs showed a similar continuous increase throughout the process of cellular senescence, unlike in the case of protein oligomers (Fig. 3G–L). Moreover, we found that the fluorescence of Congo red, a dye for amyloid fibrils, was intensified in the nucleoli of multiple types of senescent cells, including replicative, oxidative stress-induced, and DNA damage stress-induced cellular senescence (Fig. S4A-D). We also discovered that the amount of fibrillar protein positively correlated with the density of nucleolar subphases in a comparative analysis of OC immunoreactivity and RI values (Fig. 2H and 3M) [36]. Comparison of nuclear condensation phenomena in acute stress and cellular senescence

A group of proteins is sequestered in the nucleolus under several cellular stresses, such as acidosis and heat shock. Most of these proteins contain arginine/proline-rich regions and are initially recruited to the nucleolus through charge-based interactions with certain non-coding RNAs emanating from the rIGSRNA, such as rIGS16RNA, rIGS22RNA, and rIGS28RNA, which are specifically expressed in stressed cells [[38], [39], [40]]. This rIGSRNA-protein network, named amyloid body (A-body), has evolved from the nucleolus and has physical features similar to those of the nucleolus of senescent cells that we identified, including low molecular mobility explored by FRAP and positive staining with OC antibodies [41]. Furthermore, we observed that nucleolar staining by the amyloidophilic dye Congo Red, also known as A-bodies, was intensified in senescent cells (Fig. 4A and Fig. S4A-C). To confirm that protein aggregation in the nucleolus of senescent cells occurs through a process similar to A-body formation, we first examined the subcellular localization of three proteins detected in the A-body proteome: CDK1, VHL, and DDX39B [42]. Unlike the A-bodies, CDK1 in both normal and senescent cells was mainly diffused throughout the nucleus and cytoplasm (Fig. 4B). Both VHL and DDX39B condensed into nuclear foci, but they differed significantly in size and number per cell (Fig. 4C and D). VHL foci were approximately four times larger in area than DDX39B foci. However, senescent cells contained fewer VHL foci than DDX39B foci, indicating that they did not form identical condensates (Fig. 4C and D). In addition, the VHL and DDX39B foci were located in the nucleoplasm rather than sequestered into the nucleolus (Fig. 4E). The A-body is believed to promote cellular transformation into a dormant state by acting as a shelter for its constituents during cellular stress [43]. Once the stresses are terminated, the A-body is disassembled, and its components return to their native soluble folding state and resume normal function [42]. We show that VHL became insoluble under heat stress, which induced A-body formation and returned to normal upon termination of heat stress. This finding aligns with previous reports (Fig. 4F and H). In contrast, the amount of insoluble VHL upon cellular senescence remained high even 3 days after etoposide washout, showing the irreversible transition of VHL into insoluble states, which is different from the case of A-body formation (Fig. 4G and I). While the insolubility of FIB was not altered by the addition and subsequent removal of heat stress, it was enhanced in senescent cells and continued to increase even when the senescent stimulus, etoposide, was removed (Fig. 4F–I).

To determine whether our findings are associated with the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, we compared the nuclear condensation of disease-relevant proteins under acute cellular stress and cellular senescence. TDP-43 is primarily expressed in the nucleus and contributes to the pathogenesis of multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Although abnormal cytoplasmic aggregates of TDP-43 are thought to be the main cause of this disease, reversible nuclear TDP-43 aggregates are generated in response to heat stress and sodium arsenite [44,45]. While TDP-43 foci formed upon arsenic stress were resident in the nucleoplasm rather than the nucleolus and were reversible, most TDP-43 proteins found in the nucleolus under cellular senescence were trapped after the removal of senescence-inducing stimuli, suggesting that high-order protein assemblies in senescent cells have lost their reversibility (Fig. 4J and K). In summary, we conclude that molecular condensation events, in terms of spatial configuration in the nucleus and reversibility, occur in different ways during cellular senescence and acute cellular stress.

The MT domain of FIB, when assembled with NOP56, NOP58, SNU13, and box C/D snoRNAs, catalyzes the 2′-O-methylation of rRNA. This process is crucial for maturation and the formation of C/D box small nucleolar ribonucleoprotein particles [47,48]. Previous in vitro studies have shown that binding to rRNA in this domain modulates the properties of FIB droplets in several ways [14]. Deleting the MT domain affects the rheological properties of FIB droplets containing rRNA. It shortens the relaxation time during coalescence and restores dynamics that are typically arrested in aged FIB droplets [14]. In addition, the MT domain allows the FIB to reside in the immiscible phase from the GC, which is consistent with the FIB-induced DFC assembly (Fig. S6A and B) [14,46]. Deleting the MT domain restored the reduced diffusivity of FIB in senescent cells. This suggests that interactions between the MT domain and other molecules, such as proteins and RNA, are crucial for the phase alterations of FIB observed in these (Fig. 5B, D and E). In a related context, FRAP analysis of RNA using F22 dye revealed that nucleolus-resident RNA had very low kinetics of diffusion compared to nucleolar proteins such as FIB, and it became lower during cellular senescence (Fig. 5F and G, Fig. S7A) ([49] about F22). This suggests that the diffusive properties of FIB may be associated with its interactions with RNA. Therefore, we hypothesized that the decreased FIB mobility and accumulated high-order protein assemblies in DFC during cellular senescence could be modified by modulating the interaction between RNA and FIB. A widely used approach to regulating the underlying interactions within condensates is to vary the number of interactions with RNA [5]. To leverage this, we generated a chimeric FIB-RBD-GFP construct containing an additional RNA-binding entity and performed FRAP (Fig. 5A). In both non-senescent and senescent cells, FIB-RBD-GFP chimeric proteins showed very low fluorescence recovery. This indicates that increased RNA interaction with FIB could contribute to the loss of its diffusive properties in senescent cells (Fig. 5H and I, Fig. S7B). We next investigated the role of the increased interaction between FIB and RNA in promoting the aggregation of proteins during senescence using an immunofluorescence assay with an OC antibody. We observed that the protein fibrils generated during cellular senescence were more pronounced in the DFC of FIB-RBD chimera-expressing cells than in WT FIB-expressing cells (Fig. 5J and K). Thus, these results suggest that the material properties and aberrant accumulation of protein fibrils are facilitated by increasing the number of RNA-interacting entities in the FIB.

Discussion

By affecting regulatory mechanisms such as gene expression, posttranslational modification, or proteostasis, cellular stress exerts both qualitative and quantitative effects on the constituents of biomolecular condensates, subsequently modulating their interactions [31,50]. This is supported by multiple studies on the nucleolus. In addition to its role in ribosome production, the nucleolus is a well-known organelle that contributes to stress sensing and fitness [51]. It reacts sensitively to changes in the cellular environment, such as hypoxia, acidosis, and heat shock, and participates in processes such as protein sequestration, local translation, and maintenance of nuclear folding capacity [51,52]. The spatial exchange of proteomes between the nucleolus and other cellular compartments is induced by cellular stress, leading to the formation of structures such as nucleolar aggresomes or A-bodies with unique physical properties [41,53]. This suggests that the functions of nucleolar components can be diversified depending on the phase properties of the environment in which they are located. Similarly, gelation of the GC subphase by optogenetic modulation of the interacting valency of the NPM1 scaffold alters the rate of ribosome biogenesis [15,20]. Thus, the material properties of the nucleolus and its biological role are closely related. However, most studies identified to date have focused on the rapid response of the nucleolus to acute stress, occurring within minutes to hours, and therefore have limited application to universal cellular stress situations. To address this, we utilized a model of cellular senescence induced by chronic stress to observe material changes in the nucleolus that occurred over tens of hours to days. Here, we identified both the nucleolar phase transition, especially in the DFC, into a more condensed state and increased high-order protein assemblies as novel phenotypes of cellular senescence induced by genotoxic, oxidative, and replicative stresses.

Although FIB provides a molecular framework for the DFC phase, the biological contexts that influence FIB's interactions with other molecules—and thus affect the DFC's phase properties—remain unclear. Notably, FIB expression changes under various age-related conditions. While increased FIB expression was found in oocytes from reproductively old mice and fibroblasts from Wiedenmann-Rautenstrauch progeria syndrome, decreased levels of FIB were detected after several genetic interventions to extend the lifespan of C. elegans [[54], [55], [56]]. In our system, cellular senescence—triggered by genotoxic stress, oxidative stress, and DNA methylation inhibition—led to an increased, yet less soluble, expression of FIB. These changes demonstrate quantitative and qualitative alterations of FIB (Fig. 4G and Fig. S8A-C). Moreover, FIB serves as an important regulatory factor for longevity because reduced FIB expression prolongs the lifespan of C. elegans [56]. Consistent with changes in FIB, the expression of its cooperative nucleolar constituents also varies with age-related contexts [57]. For example, NOP56 and NOP58, which co-assemble into C/D box snoRNPs with FIB, are unstable during Ras-induced senescence [58]. Changes in the expression profile of snoRNAs and the nucleolar content of pre-rRNA species are also affected by cellular senescence [[59], [60], [61]]. These changes are particularly interesting given the importance of our findings regarding the pivotal role of RNA in dictating FIB dynamics. Collectively, we propose that compositional remodeling in the nucleolus, particularly in the DFC phase, is associated with changes in materiality. Further exploration of the molecular interactions of FIB in the context of cellular senescence is required. This would also provide a better understanding of the condensate aging phenomena.

Recent studies using either C. elegans genetic systems or cellular systems from patients with Hutchinson-Gilford progeria have discovered that the magnitude of global protein synthesis is largely tied to the lifespan of organisms [56,62]. These findings address the potential role of the nucleolus as a site for ribosome assembly. Similarly, multiple aspects of ribosome biogenesis, the most notable function of the nucleolus, are affected by cellular senescence. Although the transcription of rDNA by RNA polymerase I, the initial step of ribosome biogenesis, is attenuated, the levels of pre-rRNA species and ribosomal proteins are elevated under several circumstances of cellular senescence [60,61]. In addition, C. elegans lifespan was effectively enhanced by modulating the ribosome biogenesis pathway, indicating a causal relationship [63]. Since material alterations of condensates are strongly linked to functional characteristics, it would be intriguing to study the linkage between these nucleolar abnormalities during senescence progression and aberrant nucleolar materiality [64,65]. In addition, abnormal high-order protein assemblies in the nucleoli of senescent cells, particularly during the DFC phase, may play an essential role in ribosome biogenesis.

Altered rheological properties of aged condensates have attracted attention in the field of condensate biology; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Applying the findings from in vitro-aged droplets to actual condensates in living cells has been challenging owing to the intricate interactions among multiple elements, variations in gene expression, enzymatic activity, and environmental changes [21,66]. We believe that senescence-associated changes in the DFC provide a cellular-level paradigm for studying the condensate aging phenomena by providing a potential explanation for the alteration of the nucleolar phenotype and features as aspects of material properties during cellular senescence. Finally, our findings serve as a foundation to gain integrative insight into both physical phenomena (condensate aging) and life phenomena (biological aging).
