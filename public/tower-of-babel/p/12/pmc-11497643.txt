Psychosocial implications of learning amyloid PET results in an observational cohort
Authors: Lindsay R Clark, Claire M Erickson, Nathaniel A Chin, Kristin E Basche, Fred B Ketchum, Hannah L Rosario, Amanda J Peterson, Marcella L Eveler, Sterling C Johnson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONInformation on the psychosocial impact of Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarker testing in adults at risk of AD is needed to inform best practices for communicating biomarker results.METHODSNinety‐nine cognitively unimpaired older adults learned amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) results (mean age = 72.0 ± 4.8, 95% White, 28% elevated amyloid). Linear mixed‐effects regression models were used to test the main effects and interaction of PET result × time on psychosocial outcomes up to 6 months after learning results.RESULTSA significant interaction of PET result × time was observed for concern about AD (β = 0.28, p = 0.02) and intrusive thoughts and avoidance (β = ‐0.82, p < 0.001). A main effect of PET result was observed for AD test‐related distress (β = 12.09, p < 0.001).DISCUSSIONCognitively unimpaired adults learning elevated‐amyloid PET results reported mildly intrusive thoughts/avoidance initially following disclosure, but these symptoms decreased over time. Concern about AD dementia and AD biomarker test‐related distress remained higher in elevated‐amyloid compared to non‐elevated‐amyloid participants.Highlights Longitudinal assessment of psychosocial reactions after amyloid PET disclosure was conducted.Transient highly intrusive thoughts or avoidance after learning elevated amyloid results.Persistent test result‐related distress after receiving elevated‐amyloid results.There is increased concern about AD dementia after receiving elevated‐amyloid results.Happiness and relief are experienced after receiving non‐elevated‐amyloid results. Keywords: advanced planning, Alzheimer's, amyloid, biomarker disclosure, cognition, dementia, perceived risk, psychological, psychosocial, risk communication, test‐related distress

BACKGROUND

Pathophysiological changes associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD), such as the accumulation of amyloid beta, begin 10 to 20 years or more before the emergence of clinical symptoms. 1 , 2 This “preclinical” period of AD may offer a key window for successful intervention and secondary prevention of cognitive impairment. Studies communicating AD biomarker information to cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults are needed to inform best practices for testing, return of results, and follow‐up to best support those with early AD brain changes.

Mounting evidence demonstrates that CU adults do not report increased depression, anxiety, or suicidality after learning amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) results. 3 Prior studies also assessed symptoms of intrusive thoughts or avoidance after disclosing amyloid PET results, using scales developed to measure subjective distress associated with a specific event, such as the Impact of Event Scale (IES). 4 CU participants who received elevated‐amyloid PET results as part of screening into the A4 anti‐amyloid clinical trial exhibited higher subjective distress (mean IES = 10.2 [SD = 10.8]; 35% mild and 10% moderate/severe) than non‐elevated‐amyloid participants (mean IES = 5.9 [SD = 7.6]; 23% mild and 3% moderate/severe distress) within 1 to 3 days of learning their results 5 ; similar results were replicated in a geographically diverse cohort providing elevated‐amyloid results from both PET and cerebrospinal fluid assays. 6 In the A4 trial, a common emotional reaction to learning a non‐elevated‐amyloid result was relief, as evidenced by reduced concerns about AD and increased expected future time reported by this group 5 and described in prior qualitative studies. 7 , 8 Similar results were reported in patients with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) at 1 to 3 days, 9 although studies that assessed test‐related distress at longer time intervals (eg, 6 weeks or 9 to 18 months after result disclosure) reported no differences in distress between elevated‐amyloid and non‐elevated‐amyloid groups. 10 , 11 These prior studies all only included cross‐sectional IES data; however, one study of participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) assessed distress using the IES at multiple time points. This study reported higher distress in elevated‐amyloid patients compared to non‐elevated‐amyloid patients at 4 weeks; distress ratings for both groups decreased over time, although between‐group differences persisted up to 1 year after disclosure. 12 Some studies also included a measure initially designed to evaluate reactions to learning genetic risk for AD (Impact of Genetic Testing in AD [IGT‐AD] scale) 13 and adapted for amyloid PET. CU participants with elevated amyloid (n = 70) exhibited higher test‐related distress as measured by the IGT‐AD scale compared to non‐elevated‐amyloid adults (n = 31) at 6 weeks and 6 months after disclosure. 14 In the MCI study described earlier, elevated‐amyloid MCI patients reported higher distress on the IGT‐AD scale, with the greatest difference at 4 weeks after disclosure and persistent differences through 1 year. 12

Overall, these prior studies indicate (1) a higher avoidance and intrusive thoughts at 1 to 3 days in CU adults learning elevated‐amyloid PET results but similar levels at 6 weeks to 18 months after disclosure, as evaluated cross‐sectionally by different samples and (2) higher distress related to learning that risk for AD in elevated‐amyloid participants up to 6 months after disclosure in CU participants and up to 52 weeks in MCI participants. In a prior publication, we corroborated prior findings of no increased depression, anxiety, or suicidality in CU adults learning amyloid PET results. 15 In this study, we aimed to fill current gaps in the literature around longitudinal follow‐up with CU participants using measures of distress (IES, IGT‐AD scale), concern about dementia, subjective cognitive symptoms, and future time perspective.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Wisconsin Registry for Alzheimer's Prevention (WRAP) cohort, 16 a longitudinal observational study of initially CU middle‐aged participants enriched for AD risk (75% family history of AD dementia). Eligibility criteria for the WRAP Amyloid Disclosure Study included aged 65 or older, completion of a research amyloid PET scan within the past 18 months, no research or clinical diagnosis of MCI or dementia, and no active/untreated diagnosis of depression, anxiety, or other mental health disorder. WRAP participants who met these criteria were mailed recruitment letters and contacted by the study team to complete additional screening to determine study eligibility. If interested, participants provided informed consent to receive amyloid PET results and participate in this study. Screening measures for depression (Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 [PHQ‐9]), anxiety (Geriatric Anxiety Scale – 10‐item form [GAS‐10]), or suicidality (Columbia‐Suicide Severity Rating Scale Screen Version [CSSRS]) were completed at the first visit, and participants were excluded from study participation if thresholds were exceeded (eg, PHQ‐9 > 9, GAS‐10 > 9, report of suicidal ideation within past month) or they reported a history of suicide attempts. Throughout the remainder of the study, psychological screening measures were administered at each time point; if thresholds were exceeded, a study clinician followed up with the participant to check in and conduct an additional risk assessment. Study participation was voluntary, and participants could decide not to learn of their results and withdraw at any time. This study was reviewed and approved by the University of Wisconsin‐Madison Institutional Review Board.

Amyloid PET scan

Participants completed a 70‐min dynamic [C‐11] Pittsburgh compound B (PiB)‐PET scan to evaluate amyloid beta burden, and distribution volume ratio (DVR) images were created using the cerebellar cortex as a reference region of negligible binding. Amyloid PET result was determined by visual reads using a published rating system. 17 The visual read was performed on the native space DVR images that were all scaled uniformly from 0.0 to 2.5 and displayed in MRIcron using the ACTC (activation color map). Scans were read as not elevated if (1) there was only non‐specific white matter uptake or non‐significant patchy or diffuse cortical gray matter binding not resembling an AD pattern (eg, basal ganglia uptake) or (2) gray matter binding was present but less intense than an overtly elevated scan. Scans were read as elevated if there was unambiguous positive amyloid binding in the cortex. Each amyloid PET scan was read independently by three raters (two neuroradiologists, one imaging neuroscientist). If there was a disagreement among raters, the three raters discussed the case and came to a consensus on the final read result.

Study design

The WRAP Amyloid Disclosure Study was a 9‐month study involving three core study visits comprising (1) informed consent, psychological screening, and education about AD and amyloid PET results (Week 0); (2) disclosure of amyloid PET scan results (Week 2); and (3) dementia risk‐reduction counseling (Week 6) (see Erickson et al., 2023 15 for more details). Amyloid PET disclosure visits were completed using Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA)‐compliant televideo software with participants located either in their home (65%) or from a separate room in the research center (35%). The informed consent/education and dementia risk‐reduction counseling visits were completed remotely using HIPAA‐compliant televideo software or telephone. During Visit 1, following consent, screening, and baseline data collection, trained study staff guided participants through a 15‐min interactive presentation using a slide deck and script regarding causes of dementia, AD brain changes, risk factors for AD, meaning of amyloid PET results, and potential psychosocial outcomes after learning results. 18 Participants completed a knowledge quiz, and the teach‐back method was used to address any inaccurate responses. During Visit 2, biomarker result disclosure processes were conducted following published recommendations. 19 , 20 Prior to communicating the result, the study clinician asked about participant experiences with AD, how prepared the participant felt about learning their amyloid PET results and if they had any questions or concerns to address before confirming their readiness to learn their results. The study clinician communicated the amyloid PET result verbally using standardized language and provided participants with a result summary sheet (see Table S1 for the language used) and confirmed participant understanding of the result and responded to additional questions/concerns. A telephone check‐in was completed 1 to 3 days after the amyloid PET disclosure visits. During Visit 3, participants were provided general and personalized information on modifiable dementia risk factors and discussed a brain health‐related goal. Additional telephone visits to gather follow‐up data were completed at 1 month (Week 10) and 6 months (Week 36) after the core study visits.

Measures

Test result‐related distress

Distress related to learning about amyloid PET results was assessed at four time points after the disclosure visit: 1 to 3 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months. Result‐related distress was measured using validated questionnaires including the IES 4 and a modified version of the IGT‐AD scale. 13 The IES is a 15‐item self‐report questionnaire developed to measure subjective distress, specifically experiences of avoidance (eg, “I tried to remove it from my memory,” “I stayed away from reminders”) and intrusion (eg, “I thought about it when I didn't mean to,” “I had trouble falling asleep because of pictures or thoughts about it that came to my mind”) related to a specific event. Participants are asked to rate the frequency with which each item has occurred over the past 1 to 3 days on a 4‐point scale (not at all [0]; rarely [1]; sometimes [3]; often [5]). The IES is scored as a sum of 15 items, with a possible range from 0 to 75 (0 to 35 for intrusion, 0 to 40 for avoidance). A score of 9 to 25 suggests mild distress, greater than 26 corresponds to moderate distress, and greater than 44 corresponds to severe distress. 21 If participants reported more than mild distress on the IES, a study clinician followed up with the participant to check in and conduct an additional risk assessment.

The IGT‐AD is a 16‐item scale developed and validated to assess the psychological impact of AD genetic susceptibility testing and is composed of distress and positive subscales. 13 The term “genetic test” was substituted with the term “test result,” as has been reported in prior AD imaging‐focused studies. 12 As in the IES, participants are asked to rate each item on a 4‐point scale according to how often each has occurred in the past week (not at all [0]; rarely [1]; sometimes [3]; often [5]). The IGT‐AD scale is scored as a sum of 16 items, with a possible range of 0 to 80. Positive subscale items are reverse coded, such that a higher total score corresponds to a greater degree of distress.

Concern about AD dementia

Concern about AD dementia was measured at three time points: before disclosure, 1 month after disclosure, and 6 months after disclosure. Four items regarding participants’ concern about developing AD dementia were adapted from prior measures. 22 , 23 Items included “I am concerned that I will develop AD dementia,” “I would like to know if I am going to develop Alzheimer's at some point later in my life,” “Alzheimer's is the worst disease I can think of,” and “My concern about developing Alzheimer's is greater than my concern about other medical problems.” Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with each item using a 5‐point Likert‐scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Item responses were summed, such that higher total scores referred to greater concern about AD dementia, with a possible total score range of 4 to 20.

Subjective cognitive decline

Subjective memory function was measured at three time points: before disclosure, 1 month after disclosure, and 6 months after disclosure. A single item from the Memory Functioning Questionnaire was used 24 : “Overall, how would you rate your memory in terms of the kinds of problems that you have?” The item was ranked on a 7‐point Likert‐scale such that 1 = major problems, 4 = some minor problems, and 7 = no problems. Item responses were reverse‐coded such that higher scores referred to more subjective cognitive complaints, with a total score range of 1 to 7.

Future planning

Future time perspective was measured at three time points: before disclosure, 1 month after disclosure, and 6 months after disclosure. Four items from the 10‐item Future Time Perspective Scale were used, 5 including “I expect that I will set many new goals in the future,” “My future is filled with possibilities,” “There is plenty of time left in my life to make new plans,” and “As I get older, I begin to experience time as limited.” Agreement with perceptions of one's future was assessed using a 7‐point Likert‐scale (1 = very untrue, 4 = neutral, 7 = very true). The final item was reverse‐coded, and items were summed (possible range = 4 to 28), such that a lower total score represented less anticipated time in one's future. Knowledge of long‐term care services was assessed using seven Likert‐scale items (1 = not at all true of me; 5 = completely true of me), including items such as “I am well informed about the federal programs for seniors, including Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid.” 25 Item responses were summed (possible range = 7 to 35), such that higher total scores represented greater knowledge of long‐term care services.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics and group comparisons (t tests, chi‐squared) were used to describe overall sample characteristics and compare participants who received elevated‐amyloid and non‐elevated‐amyloid PET results. Linear mixed‐effects regression models were conducted to test hypotheses of changes in distress and other psychosocial measures over time. Separate linear mixed‐effects regression models were conducted for outcome measures of IES, IGT‐AD (total score, positive subscale, distress subscale), concern about AD dementia, SCD, future time perspective, and knowledge of long‐term care services. Each model included predictor variables of amyloid PET result (0 = not elevated, 1 = elevated), visit number, and the interaction of PET result × visit number. Models also included covariates of age (centered, for easier interpretation), self‐reported sex, and parental history of AD dementia, as well as random effects of intercept and slope. For models with statistically significant interaction effects, simple slopes and 95% confidence intervals were also reported. Mann‐Whitney U tests were conducted to examine item‐level differences between elevated and non‐elevated groups on the individual IGT‐AD items at each time point. To account for multiple tests being performed (IGT‐AD items at four time points = 64 total tests) and reduce the likelihood of type I errors, a Bonferroni correction was used (alpha of 0.05 resulted in a corrected p value of 0.0008 as the adjusted threshold for significance). Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.1) using the lme4 package 26 to test the linear mixed‐effects models.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

One hundred five participants consented to the study, and six withdrew before the amyloid PET disclosure visit (two did not meet inclusion criteria due to mental health thresholds, one did not meet inclusion criteria due to a diagnosis of MCI or dementia, three withdrew as a result of changing their mind about learning their PET results). Ninety‐nine participants received their amyloid PET results (mean age = 72.0 ± 4.8, Table 1). The sample was composed mostly of female (67.0%), college‐educated (69% ≥ 16 years education), and White (94.9%) participants enriched for a parental history of AD dementia (64.6%). There were no significant demographic differences between participants with elevated‐amyloid PET results (n = 28) and non‐elevated‐amyloid PET results (n = 71). Ninety‐seven participants completed all study visits. There were no significant demographic differences between those who withdrew after disclosure (n = 2) and those who completed the study (n = 97).

Test result‐related distress

Statistical parameters from the linear mixed‐effects regression models are depicted in Table 2. For the IES measure, there was a significant interaction of visit × amyloid result (β = −0.82, p < 0.001), such that participants who received elevated‐amyloid results demonstrated decreased distress over time (β = −1.05, 95% confidence interval [CI] = −1.43, −0.66) compared to participants with non‐elevated amyloid who remained relatively stable over time (β = −0.23, 95% CI = −0.47, 0.01). As illustrated in Figure 1, elevated‐amyloid participants reported significantly greater distress at the initial post‐disclosure time point (1 to 3 days) compared to non‐elevated participants (mean [SD] elevated amyloid = 6.84 (9.4), mean [SD] non‐elevated amyloid = 1.72 [2.7], t = −2.68, p = 0.01); however, scores on this measure decreased over time, resulting in minimal group differences at 6 months after disclosure (mean [SD] elevated amyloid = 2.25 [3.8], mean [SD] non‐elevated amyloid = 0.83 [2.3], t = −1.84, p = 0.07). Notably, only 2 (2%) participants exceeded moderate thresholds (elevated participants at 1 to 3 days and 3‐month time points), and no participants exceeded severe thresholds. For the amyloid‐elevated group, 24% (n = 6) reported mild symptoms at 1 to 3 days, which decreased to 7% (n = 2) at the 6‐month time point. For the non‐elevated‐amyloid group, only 3% (n = 2) reported mild symptoms at 1 to 3 days, which remained stable across the follow‐up visits (Table 3).

For the IGT‐AD measure, the interaction of visit × amyloid result did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.59, p = 0.10). However, the main effect of amyloid result was statistically significant (β = 12.09, p < 0.001), such that participants who learned of elevated‐amyloid results reported higher test result‐related distress than participants who received non‐elevated results at all visits. The main effect of visit number (β = 0.32, p = 0.10) was non‐significant. As illustrated in Figure 1, participants who received elevated‐amyloid PET results experienced a sustained level of test result‐related distress that was higher than that of non‐elevated participants across the duration of the study. A similar main effect of amyloid results was observed when examining the IGT‐AD distress subscale (β = 5.35, p < 0.001), indicating greater frequency of distress in the amyloid‐elevated compared to non‐elevated group (Table S2 and Figure 1). For the IGT‐positive subscale, the main effects of both amyloid result (β = 6.79, p < 0.001) and visit number (β = 0.38, p = 0.02) were observed. Because this subscale is reverse‐coded, these results mean that participants who received non‐elevated‐amyloid results reported more frequent positive reactions, such as feeling relieved or happy about the results, than amyloid‐elevated participants. The main effect of visit number indicates that participants reported less frequent positive reactions to the result over time. Examination of the mean scores suggests the most frequent positive reactions were observed at the 1‐ to 3‐day time point (mean = 5.76), and fewer positive reactions were reported at the 1‐month (mean = 8.09), 3‐month (mean = 7.67), and 6‐month (mean = 7.67) time points (Table 4; lower scores = more frequent positive reactions). Demographic covariates exhibited non‐significant relationships with both measures (p’s > 0.16). Examination of individual items demonstrated consistent differences between amyloid‐elevated and non‐elevated groups at all time points for items related to feeling sad or upset about the test results (higher in amyloid‐elevated group) and items related to feeling happy or relieved about the test results (higher in non‐amyloid‐elevated group) (Table S3). Group differences were also observed at multiple visits on items related to feeling anxious or nervous about the results and having difficulty talking about results with family members and at one visit about feeling a loss of control or uncertainty about what results mean about risk for AD dementia. No differences were observed between groups at any time point for items related to worrying about risk of AD dementia, having problems enjoying life because of the results, uncertainty about what results mean for family member AD risk, frustration about the lack of prevention guidelines, concerns about impact on insurance status, communication and support from family around results, or feeling regret about receiving the results.

Subjective cognitive decline

Although not statistically significant, the interaction between visit number × amyloid result exhibited a trend toward increased SCD over time in amyloid‐elevated participants compared to non‐elevated participants (β = 0.09, p = 0.10). The main effects of visit number (β = −0.01, p = 0.79) or amyloid result (β = −0.098, p = 0.10) were non‐significant. Demographic covariates exhibited non‐significant relationships with subjective cognitive function (p’s > 0.06).

Advanced planning

There was no significant interaction between visit number × amyloid result, or main effects of visit number or amyloid result on future time perspective (p’s > 0.15). Age was significantly associated with future time perspective (β = −0.20, p < 0.001), such that older age was associated with less anticipated time in one's future. There was no significant interaction or main effect of amyloid result on knowledge of long‐term care services (p’s > 0.08). However, the main effect of visit number was significant (β = −0.33, p = 0.002), such that participants reported less knowledge of long‐term care services over the course of the study. There was also a main effect of self‐reported sex, such that women reported higher knowledge of long‐term care services than men (β = 2.26, p = 0.04).

DISCUSSION

Prior studies, including our own, 15 demonstrated no increases in anxiety, depression, or suicidality after communication of biomarker results with research participants without dementia. 3 In the current study, we observed higher test result‐related distress and concern about AD dementia in CU older adults enrolled in an observational research study who received elevated amyloid PET results compared to non‐elevated participants.

Test result‐related distress was assessed at four time points (1 to 3 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months after disclosure). Similarly to larger cohorts of CU adults screening for clinical trial eligibility, 5 , 6 elevated‐amyloid participants reported more intrusive thoughts and avoidance at 1 to 3 days after disclosure than non‐elevated‐amyloid participants; importantly, distress scores were on average mild as only two participants reported clinically significant (ie, IES > 25) symptoms. Furthermore, amyloid‐elevated participants exhibited decreased distress over time, such that by 6 months after disclosure, there were no group differences. This is similar to prior cross‐sectional studies reporting no group differences in intrusive or avoidance symptoms at 6 weeks or 9 months after disclosure 10 , 11 and is the first study to document longitudinal symptom reduction in this population. However, this finding is inconsistent with a study of MCI participants, which reported that amyloid group differences in intrusion/avoidance persisted up to 1 year after disclosure, indicating that reactions to learning results can differ depending on clinical stage. 12 Overall, findings show consistently higher avoidance/intrusion at 1 to 3 days after disclosure in CU older adults learning elevated biomarker results, although these are below clinical thresholds (eg, mild range) and tend to decrease over time.

On a separate measure of the psychological impact of AD susceptibility testing, we observed sustained distress in elevated‐amyloid participants over 6 months compared with non‐elevated‐amyloid participants. On the distress subscale results were similar to those on the total scale. On the positive subscale, non‐elevated‐amyloid participants reported more frequent positive reactions than elevated‐amyloid participants. Most frequent positive reactions were reported 1 to 3 days after disclosure, and fewer positive reactions were reported at 1‐, 3‐, and 6‐month follow‐ups; this decreased positive reaction was driven by the non‐elevated‐amyloid group, as the elevated‐amyloid group means were consistent across all time points. There were no statistically significant changes over time on the distress subscale. These findings are consistent with a prior study of 97 CU adults that reported higher IGT‐AD distress subscale scores in elevated‐amyloid compared to non‐elevated‐amyloid participants at 6 weeks and 6 months after disclosure. 14 Our findings at 1 and 6 months are also consistent with prior findings in MCI participants, whereby higher distress and fewer positive reactions were observed in elevated‐amyloid compared to non‐elevated‐amyloid participants, and decreased positive reactions were observed in non‐elevated‐amyloid participants over time. 12 Examination of individual IGT‐AD items demonstrated that elevated‐amyloid participants reported more frequently feeling upset and sad about their results, and non‐elevated‐amyloid participants reported more frequently feeling relieved and happy about their results, at all time points. Group differences were also observed at some visits on items related to feeling anxious or nervous about the results, having difficulty talking about results with family members, feeling a loss of control, or uncertainty about what results meant about risk for AD dementia.

We also observed differences in concern about AD dementia over time, whereby increased concern over time was reported by elevated‐amyloid participants compared to stable concern reported by non‐elevated‐amyloid participants. Increased concern was also reported in prior amyloid PET disclosure studies in clinical trial settings. 5 , 6 This result is not surprising but provides additional evidence of accurate interpretation of the presence of increased AD dementia risk associated with an elevated result. Additionally, family history of dementia was associated with higher concern; future studies in a larger sample could further investigate whether concern is higher in those who receive elevated‐amyloid results and also have a family history of AD. These results may have important implications for the motivation to take action based on results. For example, in a prior study, we observed that greater concern about AD dementia was associated with an anticipated higher likelihood of making health‐related behavior changes, such as lifestyle changes to reduce dementia risk, enrolling in a clinical trial, or seeking clinical evaluation or treatment. 27

We did not observe significantly increased cognitive symptoms in those receiving elevated‐amyloid results, unlike one qualitative study that reported increased awareness and worry about memory issues in a third of clinical trial CU participants after being told about their amyloid results. 8 Differences in assessment method between this study (ie, quantitative rating of a single subjective memory item) and the prior study (ie, qualitative interview asking open‐ended questions) may have contributed to these conflicting results. Additionally, although not statistically significant, our results suggest a trend toward increased subjective decline in the elevated‐amyloid group, and future studies could investigate this further in a larger sample size. Prior literature suggests that learning about the risk of AD may impact one's interpretation of one's their cognitive function or objective cognitive performance related to the stereotypical threat, 28 and studies are needed to examine whether there is an impact of receiving one's biomarker results on objective cognitive performance, as well as subjective symptoms.

Lastly, we did not observe differences based on amyloid results in future time perspectives or knowledge of long‐term care services. This is not consistent with a larger sample of CU clinical trial enrollees in the A4 trial, during which small increases were observed in non‐elevated‐amyloid participants in future time perspective, 5 but it is consistent with findings in a separate CU clinical trial. 6 Interestingly, we observed less knowledge of long‐term care services over time, possibly because participants actively thought more about long‐term care after receiving results and recognized limitations in their knowledge. Further studies are needed to clarify this result and to determine whether participants engage in more advanced planning activities after learning their biomarker results.

Importantly, this study did not include participants experiencing active depression, anxiety, or other mental health disorders; therefore, our results may not replicate in samples that include those experiencing mental health symptoms. Given this selection bias, the psychological impact of receiving AD biomarker results in those with active psychiatric diagnoses remains unclear. Future studies that include larger, more heterogeneous samples of those with mental health and other medical comorbidities are needed, as well as studies that examine whether mental health symptoms predict post‐disclosure reactions. Additionally, frequent administration of the same questionnaires requiring repeated reflection on one's emotional response to learning results could affect participants’ reactions. Studies supplementing traditional fixed items with qualitative items or using novel assessment methods such as ecological momentary assessment 29 to capture more real‐time reactions may provide more accurate and detailed psychosocial responses.

These results should be considered in the context of the study's limitations. There was a selection bias as individuals eligible for this study were already enrolled in an observational AD‐related study and undergoing PET scans. This cohort is not reflective of the general population, as these participants were more knowledgeable about AD dementia, but it is reflective of likely early adopters of AD biomarker tests and enrollees in anti‐amyloid and other clinical trials. This cohort is also more highly educated and predominately White and includes more women than men; our center is currently in the process of increasing access to biomarker testing for racially minoritized participants, and we plan to evaluate biomarker disclosure processes and outcomes in a more diverse and representative sample. It is possible that the educational information provided before and during the PET disclosure visit affected psychosocial reactions; future studies evaluating the relationship between pre‐testing education and post‐disclosure reactions are needed. Lastly, outcomes were only assessed 6 months after amyloid PET disclosure, and outcomes may differ with longer follow‐up. As participants continue to be followed by the larger observational study, we plan to examine longer‐term outcomes in the future.

In conclusion, most participants with elevated‐amyloid PET results experienced mildly intrusive thoughts or avoidance that decreased over time and mild distress that persisted over 6 months and expressed increased concern about getting AD dementia and a slight increase in memory concerns. Those with non‐elevated‐amyloid results initially felt happy or relieved, but these positive reactions decreased over time. Both groups reported decreased knowledge about long‐term care services over time and denied changes in future time perspectives. These findings provide insight into typical reactions from older CU research participants learning biomarker results in the context of a research setting that provided education and support. These results may help identify participants who could benefit from post‐disclosure support and counseling to address elevated distress and inform the development of personalized post‐biomarker testing counseling to optimize psychosocial reactions. What remains to be known is how reactions vary across the AD clinical spectrum, in less homogeneous samples including individuals with mental health symptoms and those from marginalized communities and in less resource‐intensive settings. More information is needed about additional psychosocial measures (ie, stigma, coping self‐efficacy) and the impact of learning biomarker information on the interpretation of risk and diagnosis, health behavior, and dementia prevention or treatment strategies. These and future results could inform best practices in biomarker result communication in the direction of reducing barriers to learning biomarker‐informed dementia risk and diagnosis.
