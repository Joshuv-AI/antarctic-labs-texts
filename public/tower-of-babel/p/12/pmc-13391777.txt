Stephanoside B modulates metabolic gene expression and lengthens the circadian bmal1 oscillation period in differentiated myotubes revealed by real-time bioluminescence
Authors: Papawee Saiki, Tatsunosuke Tomita, Saori Yamamoto, Fatikah Luebaeteh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGymnema (G.) inodorum is a medicinal plant with anti-diabetic, anti-obesity, and anti-inflammatory properties traditionally consumed as tea in Southeast Asia. While bioactive stephanosides and gymnemic acids suppress adipocyte differentiation, their effects on skeletal muscle metabolism and circadian regulation remain unclear. This study investigated the effects of gymnemic acid (GiA-7) and stephanosides B and C in differentiated C2C12 myotubes.Methods and ResultsDifferentiated C2C12 myotubes were treated with GiA-7 and stephanosides B and C, and gene expression was analyzed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. Stephanoside B selectively and concentration-dependently increased the expression of Pparg and Ppargc1a under basal conditions and after dexamethasone-induced circadian synchronization. It also increased the expression of the core clock genes Nr1d1, Per2, and Cry1, while decreasing Bmal1, consistent with known PPARγ–circadian interactions. To directly monitor circadian rhythmicity, we established a real-time Bmal1-luciferase reporter gene assay in differentiated myotubes. Continuous bioluminescence tracking over several days revealed that stephanoside B specifically lengthened the circadian period, demonstrating an effect on molecular clock function.ConclusionsStephanoside B is associated with coordinated changes in metabolic gene expression and circadian parameters in skeletal muscle cells. These findings suggest a potential role of G. inodorum–derived compounds in modulating muscle circadian regulation at the cellular level. This study also provides a practical real-time analytical platform for evaluating dietary compounds that influence skeletal muscle circadian dynamics.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-026-12382-w.

Introduction

Gymnema (G.) inodorum is a traditional edible leafy plant consumed as a vegetable and later developed into a tea in Southeast Asia. It has been reported to exhibit anti-diabetic, anti-obesity, and anti-inflammatory effects [1–3]. We previously found that bioactive gymnemic acid (GiA-7), as well as stephanosides B, and C in G. inodorum suppress adipocyte differentiation in 3T3-L1 cells by inhibiting expression of the peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ (PPARγ)-dependent gene and modulating downstream peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ co-activator 1 α (Ppargc1a) [4]. Following oral ingestion, bioactive compounds derived from G. inodorum have been reported to exert systemic biological effects, suggesting their absorption and distribution to peripheral tissues [5]. Although skeletal muscle expresses less PPARγ than adipose tissue, it is a key organ in systemic energy metabolism [6] and it functions as a heterodimer with the retinoid X receptor (RXR) [7]. Together with cofactors such as PGC-1α, PPARγ regulates transcription of metabolic genes involved in lipid oxidation and mitochondrial function [8, 9].

Skeletal muscle also harbors an intrinsic circadian clock, consisting of transcriptional–translational feedback loops involving Brain and muscle ARNT-Like 1 (Bmal1), Clock Circadian Regulator (Clock), Periods (Pers), Cryptochromes (Crys), nuclear receptor subfamily 1, group d, member 1 (Nr1d1) and RAR-related orphan receptor α (Rorα) [10]. This clock orchestrates daily rhythms of gene expression and metabolism. Notably, NR1D1 (REV-ERBα) contributes to mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative capacity, and its loss impairs mitochondrial content and exercise performance [11].

Emerging evidence has suggested reciprocal regulatory interactions between PPARγ signaling and circadian clock components. For example, PPARγ directly regulates Nr1d1 and aligns metabolic gene expression with circadian cues [12]. In contrast, PGC-1α induces Bmal1 expression through ROR/REV-ERB-response element (RORE) activation and integrates metabolic and circadian regulatory networks [13]. These findings together suggest that G. inodorum-derived compounds could influence skeletal muscle metabolism and circadian regulation by modulating PPARγ, PGC-1α, and related pathways.

However, the effects of G. inodorum-derived compounds on skeletal muscle circadian mechanisms remain largely unexplored, and their real-time influence on muscle clock gene oscillations in C2C12 myotube models has not been fully characterized. Therefore, we first assessed the effects of three compounds derived from G. inodorum on the expression of Pparg and Ppargc1a in differentiated C2C12 myotubes. We further analyzed a compound that upregulated both genes. Since circadian rhythms influence Pparg and Ppargc1a expression, we investigated the effects of these compounds on Pparg and Ppargc1a in C2C12 myotubes after circadian phase resetting. Thereafter, we evaluated the concentration-dependent effects of the compounds on the expression of the clock genes Bmal1, Per2, Nr1d1 and Cry1 under the same conditions.

Finally, we established a Bmal1-luciferase reporter gene assay in differentiated C2C12 myotubes to directly monitor real-time circadian oscillations. This experimental system enabled continuous measurement of Bmal1 promoter activity over several days, providing a useful and accessible analytical approach to investigate compounds that modulate molecular circadian regulation in skeletal muscle. This integrative approach provided new insights into the potential of G. inodorum-derived triterpenoids, particularly stephanoside B, to modulate metabolic and circadian processes in muscle cells.

Materials and methods

Compounds and Reagents

Harmine hydrochloride was obtained from Tokyo Kasei (TCI), Tokyo, Japan [14], whereas GiA-7, stephanoside B, and stephanoside C were isolated from G. inodorum tea and purified to > 98% purity by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Their chemical structures were confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy as previously described [4]. GiA-7, stephanoside B, and stephanoside C were dissolved in ethanol. The final concentration of the vehicle in the culture medium was adjusted to be constant across all experimental conditions and did not exceed 0.1% (v/v). Vehicle control samples were treated with the same final concentration of ethanol as the highest compound concentration. Dexamethasone-Water Soluble (DEX) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Corp. (St. Louis, MO, USA) and diluted in serum-free DMEM for synchronization.

Cell culture

We cultured C2C12 mouse myoblasts (RIKEN BioResource Research Center, Tsukuba, Japan) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium High Glucose with L-glutamine, phenol red and sodium pyruvate (DMEM; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich Corp.), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). The C2C12 myoblasts were differentiated into myotubes using 2% horse serum (HS; Sigma-Aldrich Corp.) in DMEM [12, 13] which was confirmed by the elongated, cylindrical, multinucleated morphological features of myotubes [14].

Cell viability assay

We evaluated cell viability using the CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay, commonly referred to as the MTS assay, a colorimetric assay of mitochondrial enzyme activity, which reflects the number of viable, metabolically active cells. We seeded 2 × 103 C2C12 myoblasts/well into 96-well flat-bottom microplates (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) in DMEM and incubated them at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere (standard conditions) until they reached approximately 80–90% confluence. The medium was then replaced with DMEM containing 2% HS to induce myogenic differentiation and refreshed every two days for a total of four days. After confirming myotube formation, the cells were incubated with or without 5, 10, 25, 50, or 100 µM compounds for 24 h. Subsequently, CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution reagent (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA) was added directly to the culture medium as described by the manufacturer, then the cells were incubated for 30 min under standard conditions. Absorbance at 495 nm was determined in four technical replicates (wells per condition) using a Model 550 Microplate Reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) to determine mitochondrial enzyme activity [15]. Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to that of the vehicle.

Gene expression evaluation by real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

We seeded C2C12 myoblasts (1 × 105/well) into 12-well plates (Iwaki Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) in DMEM containing 10% FBS and incubated them at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere until they reached approximately 80–90% confluence. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 2% HS to induce myogenic differentiation and refreshed every two days for a total of four days. After confirming myotube formation, circadian gene expression was synchronized by treating C2C12 myotubes with 100 nM DEX in serum-free DMEM for 2 h. Following synchronization, the cells were incubated with G. inodorum-derived compounds in DMEM containing 2% HS for 24 h. For time-course analysis, cells were harvested at multiple time points after treatment (0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42 and 48 h) to evaluate temporal changes in gene expression. The cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan) immediately prior to RNA extraction to remove residual medium.

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio Inc.) as described by the manufacturer. A total of 500 ng RNA was then reverse transcribed using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc). Complementary (c) DNA was amplified by qRT-PCR using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara Bio Inc.) on a StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) and the primers (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) listed in Table S1 [19, 20]. The cycling conditions for target sequences comprised initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 10 s. All experiments included three technical replicates (wells per condition) and qRT-PCR measurements were performed in technical triplicate for each RNA sample to ensure the reproducibility of gene expression. Relative gene expression was analyzed using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to that of beta-actin (Actb) [21] and expressed as fold change relative to the vehicle (control), which was set to 1. Statistical analyses were conducted using EZR software [22]. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons. Results are expressed as mean ± SD, with significance indicated by p-values.

Real-time bioluminescence monitoring of Bmal1 promoter-driven luciferase activity in C2C12 myotubes

We seeded 2 × 105 C2C12 myoblasts per well into 35 mm culture dishes (Iwaki Co., Ltd.) and maintained under standard culture conditions. Confluent cells (approximately 80–90% confluence) were transfected with a Bmal1-Luc reporter plasmid (containing the − 197 to + 27 bp region upstream of the transcription start site [16]) using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) as described by the manufacturer. On the following day, the cells were incubated in differentiation medium, for 3 days to form myotubes. Because a transient transfection approach was used, bioluminescence signals reflect a mixed population of transfected cells. As circadian period estimation is based on oscillatory timing rather than signal intensity, variability in transfection efficiency is not expected to affect the primary outcome. We synchronized circadian gene expression by incubating the transfected myotubes with either 100 nM water-soluble DEX or 50% HS in serum-free DMEM for 2 h [17]. After synchronization, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 2% HS, 0.1 mM D-luciferin potassium salt (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Industries), and 25 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) with or without G. inodorum-derived compounds. Real-time bioluminescence was measured at 37 °C for 1 min [18] every 10 min for 7 consecutive days using a Kronos AB-2500 luminometer (ATTO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Raw bioluminescence data were detrended using a moving average method. The trend component was calculated as a 24 h moving average (using data from 12 h before and 12 h after each time point), and detrended values were obtained by subtracting the trend from the raw data. Due to this processing, data points within the first and last 12 h were excluded. Data were visualized either as normalized or raw-scale traces depending on the figure, as described in our previous studies [19, 20]. The circadian period was estimated as a parameter derived from nonlinear regression fitting of the bioluminescence time-series data. Each condition was measured once per experiment, and experiments were independently repeated. Error bars represent the asymptotic standard error of the fitted parameter. Due to the nature of the fitting procedure and experimental design, statistical comparisons of period values between conditions were not performed, and results are presented descriptively.

Real-time bioluminescence monitoring of stable Bmal1-Luc NIH3T3 reporter cells

A stable NIH3T3 cell line expressing a luciferase reporter under the control of the Bmal1 promoter was established to monitor circadian transcriptional activity [21]. We incubated 5 × 105 NIH3T3 cells seeded in 35 mm dishes (Iwaki Co., Ltd.) containing DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere for one day. Rhythmic Bmal1 expression was initiated and synchronized by incubating the cells with 100 nM DEX in serum-free DMEM for 2 h. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% FBS, 0.1 mM D-luciferin potassium salt and 25 mM HEPES, with or without G. inodorum-derived compounds. Real-time bioluminescence intensity was evaluated using the Kronos AB-2500 luminometer and circadian period length was estimated using nonlinear regression fitting of the time-series data. Raw bioluminescence data were detrended using a moving average method. The trend component was calculated as a 24 h moving average (using data from 12 h before and 12 h after each time point), and detrended values were obtained by subtracting the trend from the raw data. Due to this processing, data points within the first and last 12 h were excluded. Data were visualized either as normalized or raw-scale traces depending on the figure, as described in our previous studies [19, 20].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test to compare treatment groups with vehicle control. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For real-time bioluminescence experiments, circadian period was estimated by nonlinear regression fitting of time-series data. Error bars represent the asymptotic standard error of the fitted parameter. Due to the experimental design and the nature of the fitting procedure, statistical comparisons of period values between conditions were not performed, and these results are presented descriptively.

Results

Stephanoside B changed Pparg and Ppargc1a expression in differentiated C2C12 myotubes

We previously found that GiA-7, and the stephanosides B and C isolated from G. inodorum suppress adipocyte differentiation without significant toxicity to 3T3-L1 cells. We used 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulphophenyl)-2 H-tetrazolium (MTS) assays to determine the cytotoxicity of the compounds in myotubes after 24 h. Figure S1 shows that GiA-7 at 50 and 100 µM and stephanoside C at 100 µM reduced cell viability to ~ 60%, whereas stephanoside B showed a more moderate reduction in viability to ~ 70% at higher concentrations. Therefore, 25 µM was the maximal concentration of these compounds in subsequent experiments to ensure minimal cytotoxicity while assessing cellular responses.

We analyzed the effects of 25 µM compounds for 24 h on the Pparg and Ppargc1a genes that express proteins with metabolic functions in differentiated C2C12 myotubes. Total RNA was isolated, reverse-transcribed, then Pparg and Ppargc1a expression was assessed using qRT-PCR. Figure 1 shows that stephanoside B significantly upregulated the expression of both genes (p < 0.001), while stephanoside C also significantly increased Pparg (p = 0.026) and Ppargc1a (p < 0.001) expression. GiA-7 did not significantly change the expression of either gene. These results indicate that the compounds exert differential effects on gene expression in skeletal muscle cells. Although stephanoside C also induced a modest but significant increase, stephanoside B exhibited a significantly greater effect than stephanoside C (Tukey’s post hoc test, p < 0.01) and was therefore selected for further analysis.

Stephanoside B also upregulated Pparg and Ppargc1a expression in differentiated C2C12 myotubes stimulated with DEX

To reduce variability associated with cellular state, we synchronized the circadian clock in differentiated C2C12 myotubes using DEX. This approach was used to align the circadian phase of the cells prior to treatment and to ensure consistency with subsequent real-time circadian analyses. The cells were then incubated with 10 or 25 µM stephanoside B for 24 h, and the expression of Pparg and Ppargc1a was evaluated.

The expression of both Pparg and Ppargc1a in cells incubated with stephanoside B concentration-dependently increased compared with the vehicle (ethanol). Specifically, 10 µM (p = 0.015) and 25 µM (p = 0.0005) stephanoside B increased Pparg expression 1.5- and 1.9-fold relative to the vehicle respectively. Similarly, stephanoside B increased Ppargc1a expression by 1.9- and 2.2-fold at 10 and 25 µM respectively (Fig. 2). These results indicated that stephanoside B consistently and concentration-dependently upregulated Pparg and Ppargc1a after resetting cellular circadian rhythms.

To further evaluate the effects of stephanoside B on PPAR family members, we additionally examined the expression of Ppara and Ppard under the same experimental conditions. Stephanoside B did not significantly alter Ppara expression, which is reportedly expressed at low levels in skeletal muscle [22] and is regulated by clock genes in liver cells [23]. In contrast, Ppard, the predominant PPAR isoform expressed in skeletal muscle [22], was significantly upregulated by stephanoside B in a concentration-dependent manner at both 10 µM (p = 0.0072) and 25 µM (p = 0.0011) (Supplementary Figure S2). This expression pattern appeared to parallel those observed for Pparg and Ppargc1a.

Stephanoside B changed clock gene expression in differentiated C2C12 myotubes with reset circadian clocks

We examined expression of the core clock genes, Bmal1, Per2, Cry1 and Nr1d1 in differentiated C2C12 myotubes with DEX-induced reset circadian rhythms. Figure 3 shows the qRT-PCR results of cDNA reverse transcribed from the RNA samples described above. A peroxisome proliferator response element (PPRE) in the promoter region of Nr1d1 is transcriptionally regulated by PPARγ combined with RXR [24]. Consistent with this, stephanoside B concentration-dependently increased Nr1d1 expression in DEX-stimulated cells compared with controls 24 h after DEX stimulation (10 µM: p = 0.0019; 25 µM: p = 0.0002). The expression of Per2 was significantly increased at 10 µM (p = 0.022). Similarly, Cry1 expression was also significantly increased at 10 µM (p = 0.014). In contrast, the expression of Bmal1, which is repressed by NR1D1 via retinoic acid-related orphan receptor response elements (ROREs), was significantly decreased at both 10 µM (p = 0.0047) and 25 µM (p = 0.0061), although this decrease was not concentration-dependent. To further investigate whether stephanoside B influenced the temporal dynamics of circadian gene expression, we monitored the expression of Bmal1, Nr1d1, Cry1, and Per2 for 48 h following DEX synchronization. All four genes exhibited oscillatory expression patterns, and stephanoside B altered their temporal profiles compared with vehicle-treated controls (Supplementary Figure S3). Notably, the expression pattern observed at 24 h was consistent with the results shown in Fig. 3, with increased Nr1d1, Cry1, and Per2 expression accompanied by reduced Bmal1 expression. These findings support the notion that stephanoside B modulates circadian clock gene regulation in differentiated C2C12 myotubes.

Stephanoside B elongated Bmal1 period length in differentiated C2C12 myotubes and NIH3T3 fibroblasts

Analyses of clock gene expression at one time point (24 h) suggested that stephanoside B should influence the rhythmic expression of the circadian clock. We considered that the transcriptional changes induced by stephanoside B might be associated with changes in the oscillatory rhythms of clock gene expression. Consistent with this hypothesis, time-course qPCR analysis over 48 h revealed altered temporal expression patterns of Bmal1, Nr1d1, Cry1, and Per2 in stephanoside B-treated myotubes (Supplementary Figure S3). Because qPCR analysis at 6-h intervals provides only discrete snapshots of gene expression, we next established a real-time Bmal1–luciferase reporter assay to continuously monitor circadian oscillations and evaluate potential effects of stephanoside B on circadian period and rhythm characteristics. We optimized the previous transfection strategy because Bmal1 expression was more stable when the Bmal1 reporter plasmid was introduced before, than after differentiation (data not shown). Harmine can elongate the period in other cells [20, 21] or tissues [25] and we found similar effects on C2C12 myotubes (Fig. 4). We then compared synchronization using DEX or 50% HS [26] and found that Bmal1 rhythms were more stable using DEX (Supplementary Figure S4).

C2C12 myoblasts were transfected with the Bmal1–luciferase (Bmal1–Luc) reporter plasmid and subsequently differentiated into myotubes. The differentiated myotubes were then synchronized by incubation with DEX for 2 h, after which the culture fluid was replaced with medium containing luciferin supplemented with compounds. Bioluminescence was then monitored in real time (Fig. 4). Representative raw bioluminescence recordings obtained from C2C12 myotubes and NIH3T3 cells are shown in Supplementary Figure S6. Using this transient expression system, circadian oscillations of clock gene reporter activity were robust for several days. Stephanoside B caused little to no change in the circadian period at 10 µM (24.4 h vs. 24.8 h in vehicle control), whereas a period lengthening was observed at 25 µM (25.6 h). Stephanoside B (25 µM) increased the circadian period relative to vehicle control in both differentiated C2C12 myotubes and NIH3T3 cells, indicating that this effect is not restricted to a single cell type (Fig. 4; Supplementary Figure S5). In addition, a reduction in oscillation amplitude was observed at higher concentrations, which may indicate dampening of circadian rhythm robustness. Together, these results indicate that the real-time Bmal1 monitoring system can be used to evaluate the effects of nutrient-derived compounds on muscle circadian properties.

Discussion

Here, we investigated the effects of GiA-7, and stephanosides B and C isolated from G. inodorum on skeletal muscle cells, with a particular focus on energy metabolism and the molecular circadian clock. Among the compounds tested, stephanoside B upregulated Pparg expression in differentiated C2C12 myotubes. Stephanoside C also induced a modest but significant increase in Pparg and Ppargc1a expression, whereas GiA-7 had no significant effects. Notably, this response contrasts with our previous observations in adipocytes [4], suggesting cell type–dependent differences in the biological actions of G. inodorum–derived compounds. However, this comparison should be interpreted with caution due to differences in experimental conditions, including adipocyte differentiation versus mature myotube models, treatment duration (8–10 days vs. 24 h), and compound concentration. The mechanistic basis for these cell type–specific responses awaits elucidation. The expression of PPARγ is abundant in adipose tissue and the liver [27], but lower in skeletal muscle [28, 29]. Nevertheless, muscle PPARγ might function in the regulation of energy metabolism [30]. The transcription of Nr1d1 can be directly upregulated via the PPRE element by PPARγ [31]. NR1D1 not only functions in the regulation of cellular redox states but also acts as a transcription factor that directly suppresses Bmal1 expression via the RORE [32, 33].

Our results are consistent with these findings because stephanoside B induced concentration-dependent increases in Pparg and Ppargc1a expression together with upregulated Nr1d1 and a corresponding decrease in Bmal1 expression. Interestingly, Per2 and Cry1 expression were increased despite the reduction in Bmal1 expression, suggesting that regulation of these genes may involve additional pathways beyond BMAL1-dependent transcription. This observation highlights the complexity of circadian regulatory networks, in which multiple feedback loops and regulatory mechanisms contribute to gene expression dynamics.

Together, PPARγ and PGC-1α regulate the expression of many genes and their upregulation should result in upregulated downstream genes. In skeletal muscle, increased expression of PPARγ and PGC-1α is associated with enhanced lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and insulin sensitivity [34, 35]. Therefore, the upregulation of these genes by stephanoside B may contribute to improved metabolic regulation in muscle, although further functional studies are required to confirm these effects. Although the present study demonstrates changes in Pparg and Ppargc1a transcript levels, corresponding protein expression was not assessed. Future studies evaluating PPARγ and PGC-1α protein levels will be important to confirm whether these transcriptional changes translate to functional protein regulation. We speculate that the mechanisms through which stephanoside B regulates Pparg differ from those in adipocytes, which warrants further mechanistic investigation. One possible mechanism is that stephanoside B may influence upstream signaling pathways that regulate PPARγ and PGC-1α transcription, such as AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) or other metabolic sensors [36]. In addition, stephanoside B may modulate nuclear receptor activity or cofactor recruitment, thereby enhancing transcriptional activation of Pparg and Ppargc1a [34]. Although our findings are consistent with the involvement of PPARγ/PGC-1α-related pathways, the present study does not establish direct causality. Future studies using pharmacological modulators (e.g., agonists or antagonists) or genetic approaches, as well as protein-level analyses, will be necessary to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms. However, the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Circadian genes expressed in skeletal muscle regulate the expression of GLUT4 and other proteins involved in glucose metabolism, which contributes to the maintenance of physiological homeostasis [37]. These considerations highlight the value of experimental systems that can monitor clock gene expression in muscle.

While real-time circadian monitoring in C2C12 myotubes has previously been established using PER2-based reporters as described by Peek et al. [38], our study employs a Bmal1-luciferase reporter system under optimized conditions for long-term monitoring in differentiated C2C12 myotubes. Both PER2- and BMAL1-based reporters are well-established tools for investigating circadian dynamics, as they reflect interconnected components of the molecular clock. BMAL1 functions as a core positive regulator of the circadian clock, directly controlling E-box–dependent transcription and playing an essential role in rhythm generation [39], whereas PER2 is a key component of the negative feedback loop [40]. In this study, the use of a Bmal1-luciferase reporter enabled assessment of circadian regulation from a complementary phase of this feedback system. Our contribution lies in establishing stable and reproducible real-time monitoring in differentiated C2C12 myotubes, a system known to present technical challenges for long-term bioluminescence recording.

One limitation of the present study is that although time-course analyses were performed, some gene expression comparisons relied on a single time point (24 h) following circadian synchronization. While this approach allows standardized comparison across conditions and is consistent with previous studies investigating metabolic and circadian regulation [25, 26], it may not fully distinguish between changes in expression amplitude and potential shifts in circadian phase. Importantly, the DEX synchronization in this study was used to align cellular circadian phase prior to treatment and to support downstream clock-focused analyses, rather than to infer rhythmic expression of Pparg from a single measurement. In addition, we examined the expression of other PPAR isoforms, including Ppara and Ppard. While Ppara expression was not significantly altered, Ppard exhibited a concentration-dependent increase similar to those of Pparg and Ppargc1a following stephanoside B treatment (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figure S2). Although Ppard is the predominant PPAR isoform expressed in skeletal muscle, its role in the regulation of circadian rhythmicity remains incompletely understood [41, 42]. Furthermore, the present study was limited to transcript-level analyses and therefore does not establish whether PPARγ, PPARδ, or their downstream signaling pathways directly mediate the effects of stephanoside B. Further studies employing isoform-selective pharmacological modulators, genetic approaches, and protein-level analyses will be required to clarify the functional contribution of each PPAR isoform. To address this limitation, real-time bioluminescence monitoring of Bmal1 promoter activity was performed, enabling continuous evaluation of circadian oscillations. Notably, sustained bioluminescence oscillations were observed over several days, indicating that cells remained viable under the experimental conditions. However, further high-resolution temporal sampling or continuous monitoring will be necessary to more precisely resolve phase-dependent effects.

In this study, we successfully established the real-time Bmal1-luciferase monitoring system in differentiated C2C12 myotubes. While real-time circadian reporter systems have been previously reported in primary human skeletal muscle cells [43–46], our study establishes and applies this approach in a differentiated C2C12 myotube model. This system provides a robust and experimentally accessible platform for tracking circadian promoter activity in skeletal muscle cells. The system allowed for the continuous observation of circadian oscillations and period changes induced by compounds in a differentiated muscle model relevant to physiology.

Taken together with our previous findings that G. inodorum-derived compounds suppress adipocyte differentiation [4], the present study suggests that stephanoside B is associated with modulation of the circadian period in skeletal muscle, potentially via PPARγ-related regulation of clock gene expression. Stephanoside B had little to no effect at lower concentrations, while higher concentrations lengthened the circadian period. In addition, the observed reduction in amplitude at higher concentrations may indicate dampening of circadian rhythm robustness, and the physiological significance of these changes remains unclear. G. inodorum has long been consumed as a food plant and tea and is associated with metabolic health benefits. From a nutritional biochemistry perspective, modulation of skeletal muscle circadian timing by plant-derived triterpenoids may represent a potential mechanism through which dietary components influence metabolic homeostasis. Overall, our findings highlight a potential link between G. inodorum–derived compounds and the regulation of circadian pathways in skeletal muscle cells. Further analysis of downstream metabolic and circadian output genes, as well as protein expression, will be required to determine the functional consequences of these regulatory changes. However, these findings are based on an in vitro model, and their physiological relevance requires further validation in vivo.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that stephanoside B modulates metabolic gene expression and circadian rhythms in differentiated C2C12 myotubes, as indicated by increased Pparg and Ppargc1a expression and altered Bmal1-driven oscillations. These findings suggest a potential link between G. inodorum–derived compounds and the regulation of skeletal muscle circadian function. In addition, the real-time Bmal1–luciferase monitoring system in differentiated C2C12 myotubes provides a practical platform for evaluating the effects of dietary compounds on muscle circadian regulation.
