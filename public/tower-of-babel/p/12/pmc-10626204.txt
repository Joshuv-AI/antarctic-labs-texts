Fast clonal family inference from large-scale B cell repertoire sequencing data
Authors: Kaixuan Wang, Xihao Hu, Jian Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryAdvances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have facilitated the large-scale characterization of B cell receptor (BCR) repertoires. However, the vast amount and high diversity of the BCR sequences pose challenges for efficient and biologically meaningful analysis. Here, we introduce fastBCR, an efficient computational approach for inferring B cell clonal families from massive BCR heavy chain sequences. We demonstrate that fastBCR substantially reduces the running time while ensuring high accuracy on simulated datasets with diverse numbers of B cell lineages and varying mutation rates. We apply fastBCR to real BCR sequencing data from peripheral blood samples of COVID-19 patients, showing that the inferred clonal families display disease-associated features, as well as corresponding antigen-binding specificity and affinity. Overall, our results demonstrate the advantages of fastBCR for analyzing BCR repertoire data, which will facilitate the identification of disease-associated antibodies and improve our understanding of the B cell immune response.

Introduction

B cells are the central component of the adaptive immune system and are responsible for mediating the production of antibodies in humoral immune responses.1,2 Antibodies are capable of neutralizing pathogens by specifically binding to antigens and triggering a broader immune response, which protects against various infections such as influenza virus3 and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).4 Dysregulation of the antibody response can lead to exacerbated infection or even autoimmune disease.5 B cell receptors (BCRs) are the membrane-bound antibodies of B cells, and they generate an enormous amount of diversity through V(D)J rearrangement of immunoglobulin (Ig) gene segments during their development to efficiently recognize foreign antigens.6 Moreover, upon encounter with antigens, B cells also undergo BCR affinity maturation through somatic hypermutation (SHM)7 and class switch recombination (CSR),8 which further increases BCR diversity and enhances antigen-binding specificity and activity. The identification of developmental pathways for HIV-1 and influenza-specific antibodies, along with the characterization of the co-evolution of B cells and viruses in patients, has informed our understanding of antibody development.9,10,11,12 Therefore, further in-depth characterizing of the antibody maturation process is of great significance to understand B cell immune response in diseases. The adaptive immune receptor repertoire sequencing technology (AIRR-seq)13,14 has revolutionized the large-scale characterization of antibody repertoires. In addition, recent advancements in BCR reconstruction methods from RNA-seq data have further facilitated the identification of antibody repertoires.15,16,17 These innovative technologies have played a pivotal role in providing insights into the diversity and dynamics of B cell immunity in both normal individuals18 and patients.19,20,21,22,23,24,25

A critical step in BCR repertoire analysis is the identification of B cell clonal families arising from affinity maturation. The problem can be formulated as identifying clusters of similar sequences from a collection of highly diverse BCR sequences. Several computational methods have been proposed for clonal family inference from BCR heavy chain sequences. VJCDR326 identifies BCRs with the same Ig heavy chain V (IGHV) and J (IGHJ) gene, the same complementarity determining region 3 (CDR3) length, and sequence similarity above a certain threshold (usually 0.85) as clonal families. It is widely used in practice but faces the challenge of the use of a fixed threshold for partitioning sequences into distinct clonal families. To address this issue, more recent methods, such as SCOPer27 and MobiLLe,28 consider using clustering models with adaptive thresholds. SCOPer uses the sequence similarity (defined by the Hamming distance) of the BCR junction region together with the spectral clustering algorithm to identify clonally related BCR groups, while MobiLLe uses a combination of the Levenshtein (edit) distance and the similarity of the IGHV and IGHJ genes to measure clustering distance. Additionally, it optimizes two clustering objective functions simultaneously, with the aim of minimizing intra-clonal diversity while maximizing inter-clonal differences. Different from the above methods, Partis29 is a typical probability model that utilizes the multi-hidden Markov model (multi-HMM)30 to calculate the likelihood that the BCR sequences share the same ancestor for clonal family inference.

Despite all the progress, the existing methods still face challenges in effectively analyzing large datasets, as the computation time for all pairwise comparisons increases quadratically with the number of sequences. This makes it infeasible for these methods to handle datasets consisting of millions of sequences. In addition, in order to minimize the cost of sequence similarity calculations, most of the distance-based methods assume that BCR sequences within a clonal family have identical junction lengths, resulting in the neglect of clones produced by indels during SHM.31 Although indels occur at a lower frequency (∼2 x 10−5 mutation rate per base pair per cell division) compared to substitutions (∼10−3 mutation rate per base pair per cell division) during SHM process,31,32,33,34,35 they play a significant role in antibody repertoire diversity and immune responses. Studies have demonstrated the importance of indels in enhancing immune responses against viral and bacterial pathogens such as HIV-1 and influenza,36,37,38,39 as well as in response to vaccination.40 Hence, there is a demand for computational methods that can effectively account for sequence indels while concurrently mitigating the computational overhead.

In this work, we present fastBCR for inferencing clonal families from large-scale AIRR-seq data. Differing from the aforementioned methods, the primary innovation of fastBCR lies in its utilization of multiple k-mers for indexing diverse BCR sequences, thereby circumventing the need for pairwise sequence distance calculations. We have demonstrated that fastBCR achieves a significant increase in running speed while maintaining high accuracy on simulated datasets with diverse numbers of B cell lineages and varying sequence mutation rates. We have applied fastBCR to AIRR-seq data from peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples of COVID-19 patients, showing that the clonal families inferred by fastBCR display disease-related features as well as corresponding antigen-binding specificity and affinity. Furthermore, to facilitate the post-clustering analysis, we have provided a series of functional modules in fastBCR, including phylogenetic tree construction, SHM, and CSR analysis, which can be useful for BCR repertoire analysis and antibody discovery.

Results

Overview of fastBCR workflow

fastBCR is a heuristic method for fast BCR clonal family inference, comprising two major steps: a fast k-mer-based pre-clustering step and an optimized clustering step (Figure 1, see implementation of fastBCR in STAR Methods). In the pre-clustering step, fastBCR initiates by partitioning BCR heavy chain sequences with the same V and J gene annotation into VJ groups. Within each VJ group, sequences are further divided into groups of different lengths. Subsequently, fastBCR selects three 5-mers from the beginning (the starting positions are the 3rd, 4th, and 5th amino acids, respectively) and the middle (the starting positions are the [l/2 – 1]th, [l/2]th, and [l/2 + 1]th amino acids, respectively, where l is the junction length) of each junction amino acid sequence as seeds. Consequently, each sequence is indexed by six 5-mer seeds, and sequences with the same seed can be rapidly grouped into clusters. To account for the possibility of indels occurring during SHM, fastBCR further merges k-mer clusters that originate from the same seed and have similar junction lengths and seed locations. By taking advantage of this step, fastBCR can efficiently group BCR sequences into pre-clusters without the need for computationally intensive distance calculations.Figure 1Overview of fastBCR workflowfastBCR is a heuristic method designed for efficient BCR clonal family inference, encompassing two primary steps: a fast k-mer-based pre-clustering step and an optimized clustering step. Overview of fastBCR workflow

fastBCR is a heuristic method designed for efficient BCR clonal family inference, encompassing two primary steps: a fast k-mer-based pre-clustering step and an optimized clustering step. In the pre-clustering step, sequences are initially partitioned into VJ groups and further divided into VJl groups of different lengths. Within each VJl group, fastBCR selects six 5-mers in the junction amino acid sequence as seeds and rapidly clusters sequences with the same seed into seed-clusters. Fully considering the possibility of indel, fastBCR merges clusters that originate from the same seed and have similar junction lengths and seed locations. In the optimized clustering step, fastBCR further combines pre-clusters from different seed groups into candidates using a dynamic programming method. Finally, the consensus scores of each candidate are calculated, and candidates with low consensus scores are filtered out to obtain the final clonal families. Further details are in the STAR Methods section.

Following the first step, each pre-cluster represents a set of sequences sharing a specific seed. However, as a set of sequences may be clustered into different pre-clusters based on the sharing of different seeds, it can result in the splitting of the original clonal family. To address this issue, fastBCR further combines pre-clusters from different seed groups during the optimized clustering step, ensuring a more accurate and cohesive representation of the true clonal relationships within the dataset. To accomplish this, all the pre-clusters are first sorted in descending order of size, and the largest is set as the initial candidate clonal family. fastBCR then calculates the overlap coefficient score based on the proportion of the intersection of each pre-cluster with the candidate, and if this score meets a specified threshold (default is 0.1), the pre-cluster is merged with the candidate. The above calculation process is repeated until no merging occurs, and the second-largest pre-cluster becomes the new candidate. To reduce the running time, the dynamic programming algorithm is used to calculate the entire process until all the pre-clusters are evaluated. In the final step, fastBCR assesses the homology of each candidate cluster through a rigorous process of multiple sequence alignment (MSA). This process involves filtering out candidates with lower sequence similarity, measured by consensus scores, which ensures that only the most reliable and accurate clonal families are retained. Ultimately, the remaining candidates that meet the stringent criteria constitute the final clonal families, providing a comprehensive and robust understanding of the underlying genetic architecture.

fastBCR achieves a substantial increase in speed while maintaining high accuracy on simulation data

To evaluate fastBCR’s performance, we compared it with state-of-the-art (SOTA) BCR clonal family inferencing methods on simulated datasets. We drew inspiration from the concepts and methodology employed in AbSim41 and developed our simulation approach (Figure S1). Specifically, we began by generating an ancestor cell through V(D)J random recombinant and simulated the process of antigen activation that led to multiple rounds of expansion, mutation in the junction region and selection, ultimately resulting in the formation of a B cell clonal lineage. We set a baseline mutation rate of approximately 10−3 mutations per base pair per cell division, as reported in previous studies.33,34,35 To account for the impact of low cell capture efficiency and potential sequencing errors, we further set three higher mutation rates (0.002, 0.005, and 0.01) in the simulation process. Moreover, we introduced a 40% dropout rate in each proliferation round to mimic the selection process of antigen-specific B cells. To investigate the performance of different methods across varying clonal family densities and mutation rates, we employed the above framework to generate simulated datasets comprising 10, 30, 50, and 100 clonal families (Table S1).

We began by using the previously defined pairwise and closeness precision, recall, and F-measure (FM)28 to evaluate the ability of different methods to accurately infer clonal families from simulation data (see evaluation metrics in STAR Methods). The pairwise procedure addresses the binary clustering task and examines the relationship between each pair of sequences. On the other hand, the closeness procedure evaluates clonal compositions and assesses the structure of the repertoire. As shown in Figures 2A and 2B, all the methods achieved a precision close to 1, exhibiting a low number of false positives across various numbers of B cell lineages and sequence mutation rates. However, VJCDR3 and Partis exhibited a tendency to over-split clonal lineages, leading to the detection of numerous false negatives. While the above evaluations are commonly utilized, it is important to note that these metrics may not comprehensively reflect the algorithm’s performance on real repertoire data. This limitation arises from their inability to accurately handle data containing singleton sequences. In real-world data, it is essential to consider the presence of not only expanded B cell clones but also singleton B cells, which collectively form the entire repertoire. Therefore, we introduced 20,000 randomly simulated singleton sequences in each dataset as noise. This augmentation accounts for the presence of singletons and enhances the realism of the simulated data. To further evaluate the performance of different methods on the datasets with noise sequences, we classified the inferred clusters (containing at least three different amino acid sequences) into three types: true-inferred clusters (TIC), mix clusters, and noise clusters. We defined TICs as the inferred clusters that had the largest number of sequences from a given simulated cluster and reconstructed at least 80% of its family members. In contrast, noise clusters were composed solely of noise sequences, while any remaining clusters were categorized as mix clusters.

TICs represent the inferred clusters that correspond to the simulated clonal families and therefore are the most suitable for evaluating the quality of different methods. Our analysis revealed that the number of TICs inferred by all the methods, except VJCDR3, closely matches the number of the simulated clonal lineages (Figure 2C). We employed TIC purity and TIC retention (see evaluation metrics in STAR Methods) to further evaluate the ability of different methods to infer clonal families accurately at the sequence level. fastBCR, MobiLLe, and Partis exhibited high TIC purity across all simulated datasets, whereas SCOPer and VJCDR3 exhibited lower TIC purity (Figure 2C), indicating that these two methods tend to over-cluster clonal lineage with noise sequences. Additionally, fastBCR, MobiLLe, and SCOPer displayed high TIC retention (Figure 2C), suggesting that they were able to cluster most of the sequences in the original clonal lineages. However, it is worth noting that TICs accounted for only a small fraction of the total inferred clusters by all the methods except fastBCR. Figure 2D illustrates that VJCDR3 primarily inferred mix clusters, while the majority of clusters identified by other methods consisted solely of noise sequences. These results demonstrate that the performance of fastBCR was less affected by the presence of noise sequences. To further confirm this observation, we chose one simulation dataset and combined it with different amounts of noise sequences. We observed a significantly positive correlation between the number of noise sequences and noise clusters, except fastBCR (Figure S2). Overall, our results demonstrate that fastBCR exhibits high accuracy, particularly in the presence of noise background, when compared to other SOTA methods.

To evaluate the performance of different methods on processing indel data, we conducted further simulations of B cell clonal lineages with varying indel levels. As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, all methods achieved a precision of 1, indicating low false positives. fastBCR and MobiLLe exhibited the highest performance on recall across different indel levels. However, as the indel rate increased, both recall and FM decreased, suggesting that the methods tend to over-split the B cell lineage, leading to reduced sensitivity and overall performance as the indel rate rises. When handling data containing noise sequences, fastBCR achieve the best performance in terms of TIC fraction, purity, and retention (Figures 3C and 3D), demonstrating that fastBCR has advantages in inferring clonal families from data with a high indel rate. Finally, we evaluated the algorithm’s efficiency by measuring the running time on six additional datasets with different sequence depths. The fast version of Partis was also included in this comparison. As shown in Figure 3F, fastBCR was consistently the fastest method at both conventional (5K–50K) and large-scale (50K–500K) sequencing depths. Specifically, fastBCR was approximately ∼1,000 times faster than Partis, ∼100 times faster than MobiLLe, ∼45 times faster than the fast version of Partis, and ∼10 times faster than SCOPer and VJCDR3. It is worth noting that on the 500K dataset, both fast and traditional versions of Partis were not able to complete the analysis due to memory limitations. Nevertheless, fastBCR maintained its speed advantage and was approximately 3,700 times faster than MobiLLe on the 500K dataset. These results demonstrate that fastBCR offers a significant speed advantage in processing large-scale data.

Comparison of the inferred B cell clonal families between fastBCR and other methods in real AIRR-seq data

To understand the differences in clonal families inferred by fastBCR and other methods, we employed the measurements defined in MobiLLe28 to quantify the differences between clonal distributions inferred by two different methods (M1 and M2) on the same set of BCR sequences. The measurements are divided into five events. Specifically, the cluster inferred by M2 is labeled as “Identical” if M1 finds it identically. The cluster inferred by M2 is labeled as “Join” if it merges clusters inferred by M1 and as “Split” if it is the separation of a cluster inferred by M1. The “Mix” event is a mixture of “Join” and “Split,” while “Not Found” means the cluster inferred by M2 was not found by M1 (Figure S3A).

We randomly selected two AIRR-seq samples of memory B cells42 sorted from individuals following SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination to investigate the inferred cluster overlaps between different methods. We did not consider VJCDR3 in this analysis due to its suboptimal performance on the simulated data. The findings showed that the clonal distribution was dominated by “Identical” when comparing fastBCR with MobiLLe or Partis (Figures 4A and 4C), suggesting that the inference results of fastBCR were similar to these two methods. However, when comparing fastBCR with SCOPer, we found that less than 10% of the clusters inferred by SCOPer were identified as “Identical” in fastBCR (Figures 4A and 4C). We then further examined the consensus scores of the not “Identical” clusters and found that they were significantly lower than those in the “Identical” clusters across all the comparison methods (Figures 4B and 4C). These results confirmed that the clonal families inferred by fastBCR had higher intra-cluster similarities. fastBCR detected significant B cell activation in PBMC samples of COVID-19 patients

To explore the applicability of fastBCR for biologically meaningful analysis of real experimental data, we applied it to AIRR-seq data obtained from PBMC samples of 19 COVID-19 patients43 and 40 healthy individuals.44 Our analysis revealed that COVID-19 patients had a significant increase in the number and size of the inferred clonal families compared to the healthy individuals (Figures 5A and 5B). To mitigate the impact of variable sequencing depth on the analysis, we have introduced a normalization metric, the Tcf20 score, which represents the proportion of sequences attributed to the top 20 clonal families out of the total number of BCR sequences. We identified a significant increase in Tcf20 score within the COVID-19 group (Figure 5C), confirming that antibody immune response was activated in COVID-19 patients. Subsequently, we analyzed the sequence length, SHM rate, and CSR of the inferred clonal families. We found that the CDR3 length and SHM ratio were significantly higher in COVID-19 patients, providing further evidence of widespread B cell clonal expansion in patients (Figures 5D and 5E). Furthermore, comparisons of the SHM ratio among different isotype subclasses indicated a consistent increase in the COVID-19 group (Figure 5F). The visualization of immunoglobulin isotype co-occurrence within the inferred clonal families in the COVID-19 group revealed frequent IGHM to IGHG1 or IGHA1 CSR events, indicating a robust humoral immune response to produce specific antibodies (Figure 5G). We have leveraged CoV-AbDab,45 a database that catalogs molecular information on publicly available neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) to SARS-CoV-2 antigens, to examine the activation of B cells expressing NAb sequences. To quantify this, we define the NAb ratio as the fraction of the number of NAb sequences within clonal families to the total number of NAb sequences present in each sample (see NAb ratio analysis in STAR Methods). Our analysis revealed a significant increase in the NAb ratio in the COVID-19 group, implying that SARS-CoV-2 antigens elicited a specific B cell immune response in infected patients (Figure 5H). fastBCR identified antigen-specific B cell clonal families from AIRR-seq data of peripheral blood memory B cells from COVID-19 patients

In this section, we demonstrated the versatility of fastBCR in analyzing functionally identical B cell populations at high resolution. Specifically, we applied fastBCR to AIRR-seq data of naive and sorted memory B cells from eight healthy SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccinees (Vaccinee) and four donors vaccinated after recovering from infection (Recovered).42 The memory B cells sorted for analysis were categorized into three distinct groups based on their antigen-binding specificities to wild-type and B.1.351 RBD antigens. These groups were denoted as follows: Spike+ RBD–, WT RBD+, and RBD++ reactive B cells. Using fastBCR, we systematically investigated the differences in inferred clonal families between different cell subpopulations.

The Tcf20 scores varied widely, ranging from less than 0.01 for naive B cells to 0.2 for the antigen-binding memory populations (Figure 6A). Notably, RBD++ samples had the highest Tcf20 scores, indicating that they were highly expanded compared to the other populations (Figure 6A). Discussion

A clonal family is a group of B cells that share the same V(D)J recombinant event and evolve into different clades through proliferation and SHM. The inference of clonal families is essential in BCR repertoire analysis, as it can help understand B cell activation and contribute to antibody-related research. However, clonally related sequences can become highly diverse, making it challenging to cluster them accurately and quickly. Previous studies have explored the benefits of incorporating sequence similarity calculations with traditional clustering methods. However, it is important to highlight that BCR clustering presents distinct challenges, setting it apart from traditional clustering problems. In BCR repertoire data, a comprehensive investigation involves not only multiple expanded clonal clusters but also a substantial number of singletons originating from naive or other types of B cells. As a result, the output of BCR clustering is characterized by a large number of clusters with small cluster sizes and numerous outliers.

In light of this, we propose that omitting the calculation of sequence distances involving singletons can lead to enhanced speed without compromising the inferencing accuracy. Following this hypothesis, we developed fastBCR, which is a heuristic method for fast BCR clonal family inference. Unlike traditional clustering methods, fastBCR uses multiple k-mers to index diverse BCR sequences, thereby circumventing the need for pairwise sequence distance calculations. By grouping sequences with the same k-mer, fastBCR is able to reduce the time required for the distance calculation of a large number of singletons. Through comparisons with SOTA methods on simulated datasets with diverse numbers of B cell lineages and varying sequence mutation rates, we have demonstrated that fastBCR achieves a significant increase in running speed while maintaining high accuracy.

Moreover, our study highlights the potential of fastBCR as a powerful tool for the efficient and accurate analysis of B cell repertoire sequencing data, with broad implications for the study of various diseases and vaccine responses. To showcase the practical applications of fastBCR, we applied it to two independent datasets and performed comparative analyses between COVID-19 and healthy BCR repertoires. The results reveal that fastBCR can identify significant B cell activation in PBMC samples from COVID-19 patients, as evidenced by differences in clonality, level of affinity maturation (characterized by SHM and CSR), and NAb ratio. The NAb ratio was defined by the fraction of the number of NAb sequences within expanded clonal families (consisting of three or more sequences) to the total number of NAb sequences present in each sample, which has the potential to facilitate the diagnosis of COVID-19 (Figures 5H and 5I). The inclusion of the NAb ratio as an evaluation indicator is motivated by several important considerations. Firstly, it is essential to acknowledge that NAbs can naturally occur in healthy individuals as part of their immune response. Thus, when distinguishing between patients and uninfected individuals, accurately measuring the level of NAb activation becomes a critical and feasible approach. By quantifying the NAb ratio, we gain insights into the extent of neutralizing antibody activity, which is particularly relevant in the context of infectious diseases or vaccination studies. Monitoring the presence and level of NAbs can help researchers and healthcare professionals assess the effectiveness of vaccines, therapeutic interventions, and the overall immune response to specific pathogens.

In addition, our analyses of sequenced memory B cell samples after mRNA vaccination confirm that clonal families inferred by fastBCR have higher antigen-binding specificity and affinity. These findings offer valuable insights into the interpretation of repertoire sequencing datasets, which can facilitate downstream tasks such as antibody discovery and disease diagnosis. Future work may include combining clonal family inferences with phylogenetics to explore the development and evolution of BCR repertoires. Additionally, our findings indicate that the computational approach utilized by fastBCR exhibits promise in its ability to be employed for large-scale T cell receptor clustering analysis.

Limitations of the study

We have demonstrated that fastBCR can accurately and efficiently infer clonal families from simulated BCR repertoire data. However, the lack of ground truth experimental data makes it challenging to systematically evaluate the performance of the clonal families inferring methods. The accumulation of SHM events can obscure the boundaries between clonal families, making it difficult to accurately identify them. To address this issue, future research should focus on elucidating the formation mechanism of BCR clonal families, and advanced experimental methods could be useful in this regard. Additionally, constructing standardized artificial datasets for method testing and comparison would be valuable, although different simulations and metrics may yield different results. In addition, fastBCR only considers the heavy chain (IGH) of the BCR as it is typically sequenced separately from the light chain. We chose to focus on the IGH since previous studies have demonstrated its higher diversity in comparison to the light chain,50 and functional antibodies have been shown to exhibit coherence with the light chain.51 However, recent advancements in single-cell-based methods now allow us to retain information on heavy and light chain pairing. Therefore, incorporating this pairing information into our clustering method could enhance the quality of our clustering results.

Method details

Input format

fastBCR requires the input data to be in an AIRR-compatible format, which must contain at least IGHV, IGHJ, and junction region details. The junction region is defined as the CDR3 plus the two flanking conserved cysteine and tryptophan/phenylalanine residues. As of now, fastBCR exclusively considers productive sequences in its analysis, with the primary focus on studying functional BCRs. Additionally, fastBCR restricts its analysis to junction amino acid lengths ranging from 9 to 26. Junctions that are shorter or longer than this range are filtered out due to their low frequencies. This ensures that fastBCR efficiently handles relevant and representative sequences, optimizing the accuracy and speed of clonal family inference. Users can use the command data.pro(rawdata) integrated in the fastBCR R package to process raw data for clustering input requirements.

Fast k-mer pre-clustering

VJ partition

The first step of pre-clustering is partitioning the BCR sequences into VJ groups, defined as subsets of sequences with the same V and J gene usage.

K-mer clustering

In our approach, we leverage the concept of k-mers, which are short fragments of "k" consecutive amino acids (or nucleotides) in a protein (or DNA) sequence, to serve as compact "fingerprints" of the sequence. Drawing inspiration from the use of k-mers in DNA sequence alignment, we extend this idea to the task of inferring B-cell clonal families from massive BCR sequences. Specifically, we select six 5-mers from each junction amino acid sequence as seeds for indexing diverse BCRs. These 5-mers are chosen from both the beginning (starting positions: 3rd, 4th, and 5th amino acids) and the middle (starting positions: (l/2-1)th, (l/2)th, and (l/2 + 1)th amino acids, where "l" is the junction length) of the sequence. By using multiple k-mers as seeds, fastBCR efficiently captures essential information about the sequences, avoiding the need for computationally expensive pairwise sequence distance calculations.

To minimize the number of seeds while maximizing the information they represent, the choice of k-mer extraction in fastBCR is carefully considered. The highly diverse BCR CDR3 region consists of conserved motifs at the beginning and end of the junction in sequences with the same V and J genes. To prevent the k-mer extraction from being affected by the use of the same V gene, fastBCR excludes the first two amino acids (which may represent the conserved motif of the V gene) and starts extracting k-mers from the 3rd amino acid in the front section of the junction (i.e., the V-D connection region). Similarly, to avoid interference from the same J gene, fastBCR does not extract k-mers from the rear segment of the junction (i.e., the D-J connection region) but chooses the (l/2 - 1) segment in the middle of the junction (i.e., the D gene segment) to begin k-mer extraction.

Additionally, the selection of six (2∗3) 5-mers from the BCR junction region was based on a systematic search of a wide range of parameters. The goal of clustering optimization is to increase the recall of true clusters while minimizing the number of wrongly clustered pairs. To assess these metrics, we performed simulations by randomly generating 50 B-cell clonal lineages and then clustering all the sequences. We computed both pairwise and closeness precision, recall, and FM to evaluate the clustering performance. In this process, we explored various combinations of k-mer sizes, ranging from 2 to 8, and the number of selected k-mers, ranging from 1 to 5, extracted both from the beginning and middle of the BCR junction region. Among all the tested configurations, the optimal solution was found to be clustering three 5-mers from the two starting positions (Figure S6). This particular configuration demonstrated the highest precision and recall values, both approaching 1, indicating its superior performance in accurately inferring B-cell clonal families.

After the k-mer selection step, each sequence is assigned to six 5-mer indices. These unique 5-mers are called "seeds," and sequences sharing the same seed with the same length are first grouped into seed-clusters. However, it is essential to filter out sequences that may not fit well within these clusters. To accomplish this, fastBCR employs a strategy based on the positions of the seeds within the sequences. The recommendation is to compare the positions of the seeds among sequences within each seed-cluster. If the seed positions are too far apart from the average position, with a maximum distance of one and a half amino acids, then those sequences should be removed from the seed-cluster. Optimized clustering

Further combine pre-clusters

In the first step, each pre-cluster in fastBCR represents a set of sequences sharing a specific seed. However, a set of sequences may be clustered into different pre-clusters based on the sharing of different seeds, leading to the splitting of the original clonal family. To overcome this challenge, fastBCR employs an optimized clustering step that combines pre-clusters from different seed groups, ensuring a more accurate and cohesive representation of the true clonal relationships within the dataset.

In this step, all pre-clusters are sorted in descending order of size, and the largest one is initially considered as the candidate clonal family. fastBCR then calculates the overlap coefficient score, which measures the proportion of intersection between each pre-cluster and the candidate. This score is related to the Jaccard index and is defined as the size of the intersection divided by the smaller of the size of the two sets:overlap(X,Y)=|X∩Y|min⁡(|X|,|Y|)

If the overlap coefficient score meets a specified threshold (default is 0.1), the pre-cluster is merged with the candidate clonal family. This merging process continues iteratively until no further merging occurs, and the second-largest pre-cluster becomes the new candidate. This process is repeated using dynamic programming to evaluate all possible combinations until all pre-clusters have been considered. By combining pre-clusters based on their overlap with a candidate clonal family, fastBCR ensures that sequences with similar characteristics and evolutionary trajectories are grouped together, leading to a more accurate and comprehensive representation of the clonal relationships in the dataset. Generation of simulated datasets

To assess the performance of fastBCR, we generated simulated datasets by varying the number of B-cell lineages and sequence mutation rates. We drew inspiration from the concepts and methodology employed in AbSim,41 which is a time-resolved antibody repertoire simulation framework. While AbSim is a valuable tool for modeling antibody repertoires, it primarily focuses on simulating the evolution of antibody sequences and lacks a dedicated module for simulating indels during the SHM process. Therefore, we did not directly utilize AbSim for simulating B cell clonal families. Instead, we developed our simulation approach. Specifically, we began by generating an ancestor cell through V(D)J random recombinant and simulated the process of antigen activation that led to multiple rounds of expansion, mutation and selection, ultimately resulting in the formation of a B cell clonal family (Figure S1).

The detailed generation process involved several steps (Figure S1). Firstly, an unmutated germline V-, D-, and J-gene were randomly selected from the IMGT database, followed by V(D)J recombination which connect them and randomly introduce 1–3 Indels in D-J junction (defined as the second half of the nucleotide sequence in the D gene fragment to the sequence of the first 15 nucleotides in the J gene fragment) and V-DJ junction (defined as the last 15 nucleotides in the V gene fragment to the sequence of the first 15 nucleotides in the D-J rearranged DNA) regions in turn. Subsequently, to ensure the comparable size of the clonal family, the B cell proliferation process started from the replication of the ancestor cell for 3 times in the first round. The replication of the following round was set up as 3–5 randomly per round of each B cell, while a 40% dropout rate was introduced in each proliferation round to mimic the selection process of resistant B cells. The junction region was defined as the sequence of the last 15 nucleotides in the V gene fragment to the first 15 nucleotides of the J gene fragment.

The occurrence of SHM in the junction region was concentrated upon and simulated through the introduction of different point mutation rates (0.001, 0.002, 0.005, and 0.01), which included three possible forms of SHM-induced point mutations (insertion, deletion, and substitution) fully considered. Based on statistical findings from previous studies on SHM process,31,32 we determined the probability ratios of insertions, deletions, and substitutions as 4:5:500, respectively. The data-driven approach60 was employed for substitution, which allows for different substitution rates for transitions (Ptransition = 0.7) and transversions (Ptransversion = 0.15), correlating with antibody evolution. The proliferation, mutation and selection process was repeated six times, with each round generating and preserving a random number of new sequences, serving as the starting sequence for the next round to achieve the required family size. Finally, the new remaining sequences generated in each round, including ancestor cell sequences, were combined to generate the final simulated clonal families.

To accurately determine the precise junction positions and filter out non-productive sequences, we employed IgBlast61 as a convenient tool. It is important to note that our usage of IgBlast was not intended for gene annotation purposes, but rather as a valuable resource to assist us in obtaining reliable information regarding the junction positions. By leveraging the capabilities of IgBlast in this manner, we were able to effectively filter out non-productive sequences and ensure the integrity of our dataset.

Using the above process, we generated a series of datasets containing 10, 30, 50, and 100 clonal lineages for the purpose of evaluating the performance of the methods. In addition, recognizing the significance of both activated B cell clones and singleton B cells in shaping the entire repertoire, we integrated 20,000 irrelevant ancestors (naive) singleton sequences as noises in each dataset. The simulated datasets provided an opportunity to assess the performance of various clonal family inference methods under different conditions, considering both the presence and absence of singleton sequences (Figure 2).

To assess fastBCR’s efficiency in handling indels, we designed a validation using simulated datasets with varying Indel mutation rates. In the regular simulation process, we determined the probability ratios of insertions, deletions, and substitutions as 4:5:500, respectively. To systematically increase the incidence of Indels, we set the mutation rate of Indels to 1, 2, 5, and 10 times that of the basal mutation rate. This allows us to evaluate fastBCR and other methods under different Indel conditions, providing a comprehensive assessment of their performance. For this validation, we generated datasets containing 50 clonal families with varying Indel mutation level (Figure 3 and Table S2). The point mutation rate is 0.001. To ensure a more accurate estimation of algorithm performance on real data, we also included a considerable number of irrelevant ancestor (naive) singleton sequences (noises) in each dataset.

Collection of real AIRR-seq datasets

To assess the practical utility of fastBCR, we gathered empirical data from various studies. Kim-202152: This dataset comprises PBMC samples from 17 patients infected with SARS-CoV-2, which together yield an AIRR-seq library of over 3 million IgH sequences. To gauge the efficiency of different methods (Figure 3F), we randomly sampled 5K, 10K, 20K, 50K, 100K, and 500K sequences from the library for comparison. Galson-202043 & Ghraichy-202044: These two datasets, one experimental and the other a control group, were published by the same research team. Galson-2020 performed BCR heavy chain sequencing on 31 patients (19 are publicly available) with SARS-CoV-2, while Ghraichy-2020 assembled a BCR sequence dataset of 40 healthy participants aged 10 years and older. The two datasets were used to characterize the B-cell response to COVID-19 and compare it to the healthy population (Figure 5). Goel-202142: This study investigated the immune memory response following vaccination against SARS-CoV-2. The researchers designed a sorting panel to distinguish three populations of memory B cells with distinct antigen-binding specificities: (i) those binding to full-length spike but not RBD, (ii) those binding to full-length spike and wild-type RBD but not the B.1.351 variant RBD, and (iii) those binding to full-length spike and cross-binding both WT and B.1.351 variant RBD. Naive B cells were also included as the control. Evaluation metrics

Absence of singleton sequences in simulated data

As clonal families are known in our simulated datasets, we can quantitatively evaluate the performance of algorithms in identifying clonally-related sequences (Figures 2A and 2B). To achieve this, we employ closeness and pairwise performance measures defined in the previous study.28

The pairwise measure is specifically designed for the binary clustering task and focuses on the pairwise relationships between individual sequences. In the context of pairwise performance measures, a pair of sequences is considered true positive (TP) if the sequences are correctly clustered together in the inferred clusters. Conversely, the pair is counted as false positive (FP) if they are clustered together in the inferred cluster but separated in the original simulated cluster, and as false negative (FN) if they are clustered together in the original simulated cluster but separated in the inferred cluster. While pairwise measure provides valuable insights into pairwise similarities, it falls short in evaluating the clonal composition and the overall repertoire structure.

On the other hand, the closeness measure takes into account both clonal compositions and repertoire structure, but its computation necessitates the identification of the optimal correspondence between inferred and original clusters. In this case, the best correspondence between inferred clusters and simulated cluster should be determined first. For brevity, we denoted the simulated cluster as SCi (i = 1 … N) and inferred clusters as ICj (j = 1 … M). Since the clonal relationships were known in the simulation data, sequences in SCi can be assigned with the corresponding true label LSCi. Furthermore, we assumed that ICj can be labeled by its most frequent label LICj. For each ICj, the best correspondence refers to SCi having the same label with it (i.e., LSCi==LICj). So far, three disjoint sets can be computed as TPj=|ICj∩SCi(LSCi==LICj)|, FPj=|ICj|−TPj and FNj=|SCi(LSCi==LICj)|−TPj. By taking the union of all ICj, we obtain TP=⋃j=1MTPj, FP=⋃j=1MFPj, and FN=⋃j=1MFNj.

When TP, FP and FN are determined, precision and recall are then calculated. Running time evaluation

Running time is the direct metric for evaluating the speed of a clonal family inference method and is inversely proportional to the method’s efficiency. In this study, we compared the running time of five methods at different scales of datasets, including fastBCR, VJCDR3,26 SCOPer,27 MobiLLe,28 and Partis29 (Figure 3F). Among them, fastBCR, VJCDR3, and SCOPer were written by the R programming language, thus running times were measured using the R command system.time(). MobiLLe and Partis were tested on Ubuntu 18.04.3 and their running times were outputted after the command was completed. It’s worth mentioning that we encountered difficulties in disabling the annotation process or obtaining the running time of the clustering step alone of Partis. We thus further added a fast version of Partis ('Partis-fast') to the running time evaluation. In our test fastBCR used the command BCR.cluster(input). SCOPer used the command spectralClones(input, method = "novj"). MobiLLe used the command bash run_Mobille.sh [input_repertoire_name] [output_repertoire_name]. Partis used the command partis partition --infname test/example.fa --outfname _output/example.yaml. Partis-fast used the command partis partition --infname test/example.fa --outfname _output/example.yaml --fast. As VJCDR3 was not available as an open-source method, we developed it in-house and tested its running time as well. All tests were conducted on a server with two 16-core, 64-thread Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 5218 CPUs (2.30GHz) and 256G RAM.

NAb ratio analysis

The NAb ratio was established as an indicator of the proportional prevalence of neutralizing antibody sequences within expanded clonotypes in each sample. It was defined as the ratio derived from dividing the tally of NAb sequences encompassed by clonal families by the aggregate count of NAb sequences present within each sample. In this context, an NAb sequence is delineated as a sequence that manifests an exact match of V gene, J gene, and CDR3 amino acid sequence alignment with any of the annotated antibody sequences documented in the CoV-AbDab. In Figure 5I, we applied ROC curve to evaluate the performance of NAb ratio in distinguishing COVID-19 and healthy samples. The ROC curve is a graphical representation commonly used to evaluate the performance of binary classification models or diagnostic tests. It plots the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1-specificity) for different thresholds used to classify the data, which could visualize how well a model can discriminate between the two classes across varying decision thresholds. The area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC) quantifies the overall performance of the model, where an AUC value of 0.5 indicates random classification, while a value of 1.0 signifies perfect classification.

SHM and CSR analysis

SHM is the important signal of B cell activation after antigen binding, leading to junction regions' high diversification. To avoid overestimating the SHM rate due to the aggregated mutations during B cell clonal expansion, we consider only mutations for pairs of sequences with only one nucleotide mismatch in each BCR cluster. The total mutation count is then divided by the CDR3 length and cluster size as the final estimation of cluster SHM (Figures 5E and 5F). IGH CDR3 calls with unique isotype annotation were used in the isotype fraction and CSR analyses (Figure 5G). Cooccurrences of unambiguously assigned different Ig classes or subclasses in the same IGH CDR3 cluster were considered as CSR. The number of CSR events was normalized by the total number of IGH clusters in each group.

Antigen binding evaluation using Absolut!

Absolut!46 is an in silico framework for simulating antibody-antigen binding. In this study, we employed Absolut! as a black-box function to evaluate all possible binding information of an arbitrary CDR3 sequence to any antigen of interest and return the optimal binding information in silico. Specifically, Absolut! first takes PDB antigens and converts them into lattice representation with integer positions, and then takes a list of CDR3s sequences as input and returns the best binding structure of each 11-mer of each CDR3 to the antigen. Here, we took two PDB antigens, 7LM847 (Crystal structure of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein RBD in complex with two cross-neutralizing antibodies CV38-142 and COVA1-16 Fabs isolated from COVID-19 patients) and 7BWJ48 (crystal structure of SARS-CoV-2 antibody with RBD) as potential antigens corresponding to the WT RBD+ memory B cells (Figure 7, S5, and S6). The binding energies of a list of CDR3s provided in ListCDR3s.txt with the given antigens were calculated using the command Absolut repertoire Antigen_ID ListCDR3s.txt. For each CDR3 sequence, the binding affinity was calculated as the average binding energies of all possible 11-mers to the antigen. Only BCR sequences with lengths in the range of 11–13 were considered here, since Absolut! requires at least an 11-mer sequence and NAb lengths enriched at 13 and below (Figure S7).
