A Short Case Series of Common Variable Immunodeficiency From a Tertiary Care Hospital in South India
Authors: Jenson G Varghese, Mathew Thomas, Gopika Gokul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is the most prevalent clinically significant primary antibody deficiency, characterised by markedly reduced serum immunoglobulin levels and impaired vaccine responses. It typically presents with recurrent sinopulmonary infections and may be associated with autoimmune phenomena, granulomatous disease, and malignancy. Despite its clinical impact, CVID remains substantially underdiagnosed, with a considerable delay between symptom onset and diagnosis. We present three cases managed at a tertiary care centre in Kerala, India, illustrating the diverse clinical presentations of CVID across different age groups. The first was a 32-year-old woman with long-standing bilateral bronchiectasis, prior lung surgery, and recurrent infections attributed to severely reduced IgG and IgA levels. The second was a 14-year-old girl with recurrent respiratory and ear infections since childhood, bronchiectasis, clubbing, and lymphadenopathy, in whom marked hypogammaglobulinemia supported the diagnosis of CVID after exclusion of secondary causes. The third was a 50-year-old man presenting with consolidation, maxillary sinusitis, and significant weight loss, in whom pan-hypogammaglobulinaemia involving IgG, IgA, and IgM supported the diagnosis. These cases underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for primary immunodeficiency in patients across all age groups presenting with recurrent sinopulmonary infections. Early immunological evaluation, including serum immunoglobulin quantification, is essential for timely diagnosis, and immunoglobulin replacement therapy remains the cornerstone of management.

Introduction

Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is a heterogeneous immune disorder and the most common clinically significant primary antibody deficiency [1]. It is defined by markedly reduced serum concentrations of IgG in combination with low levels of IgA and/or IgM, accompanied by poor or absent antibody responses to immunization, and the exclusion of other defined immunodeficiency states [1,2].

A key immunologic defect in many patients with CVID is impaired B-cell differentiation into antibody-secreting plasma cells, with impaired immunoglobulin secretion, and many patients demonstrate a reduced percentage of isotype-switched memory B cells [2]. The resulting immune dysregulation predisposes affected individuals not only to recurrent infections but also to autoimmune phenomena, granulomatous infiltration of multiple organs, lymphoproliferation, and lymphoid malignancies [3].

Despite its prevalence, CVID is frequently diagnosed late, often years to decades after the onset of recurrent infections. This diagnostic delay results in avoidable end-organ damage, particularly chronic structural lung disease in the form of bronchiectasis, which affects 30-50% of patients and carries significant morbidity and mortality [3,4]. The estimated prevalence of CVID is approximately one in 25,000-50,000 individuals, with equal sex distribution and a bimodal age of onset, typically in the first and third to fourth decades of life [1].

Here we report three cases of CVID from a tertiary care hospital in Thiruvananthapuram, Kerala, diagnosed in patients aged 14 to 50 years, each presenting with a prolonged history of sinopulmonary infections that had been managed without a unifying diagnosis for several years. These cases underscore the need for awareness of primary immunodeficiency disorders (PIDs) among clinicians in India.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 32-year-old woman presented with a three-day history of high-grade fever, cough with purulent sputum, and rapidly worsening dyspnea (from modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) grade 2 to grade 4), orthopnea, and weight loss. She had been admitted to a local hospital 15 days prior and treated empirically with intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam and oseltamivir, with incomplete recovery.

Her past medical history was remarkable for recurrent respiratory infections beginning in childhood, a cleft palate repair, and allergic rhinitis. She had been diagnosed with bilateral bronchiectasis and had undergone a left lower lobectomy and left upper lobe posterior segmentectomy in 2012 due to severe bronchiectasis. Despite multiple hospitalizations at various centres over the years, no underlying immunological diagnosis had been established. A family history of asthma was noted.

On examination, she appeared moderately built and ill-nourished with a BMI of 16.5Kg/m2. Vital signs revealed tachycardia (pulse rate (PR) 98/min), hypotension (blood pressure (BP) 100/70 mmHg), tachypnea (respiratory rate (RR) 35/min) with accessory muscle use and intercostal retractions, and a temperature of 100°F. Respiratory examination demonstrated nasal flaring, drooping of the left shoulder, decreased left-sided respiratory movements, and absence of breath sounds over the left mammary, infra-axillary, and infrascapular areas, with coarse crepitations over the left axillary and interscapular regions, and diffuse rhonchi bilaterally.

Blood investigations revealed a leukocytosis of 18,000/μL (4,500-11,000/μL) with neutrophilia of 92% (50-70%) and lymphocytes of 6% (20-40%), an elevated CRP of 42.6 mg/L (<5.0 mg/L), a platelet count of 335,000/μL (150,000-400,000/μL), and a hemoglobin of 13 g/dL (12.0-16.0 g/dL). Chest X-ray demonstrated mediastinal shift to the left, compensatory hyperinflation of the right lung, and a non-homogeneous opacity in the left middle zone as shown in Figure 1. High-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest showed bilateral bronchiectasis as shown in Figure 2.

Sputum acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear and GeneXpert (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) were negative.

Crucially, serum immunoglobulin estimation revealed IgG < 75 mg/dL (reference 700-1600 mg/dL) and IgA < 10 mg/dL (reference 70-400 mg/dL). A diagnosis of CVID was established on the basis of profoundly depressed IgG and IgA, in the context of recurrent infections beginning in childhood. IgM was not tested. The patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) replacement therapy at a standard dose of 500 mg/kg per infusion, administered at monthly intervals. Infusions were administered under supervised clinical conditions, and no significant infusion-related adverse reactions were recorded throughout the treatment course.

Case 2

A 14-year-old girl, born to non-consanguineous parents, was admitted with a one-week history of fever, productive cough, and purulent discharge from her left ear. She was born at term without neonatal complications. There was no developmental delay, and her height and weight were appropriate for age.

From the age of nine years, she had recurrent bouts of respiratory illness, fever, and ear discharge. In 2015, she had been admitted for prolonged cough with expectoration; at that time, tuberculosis had been excluded and IgG was low, although IgE and IgM were normal. In 2019, an axillary lymph node biopsy was performed and demonstrated reactive changes only. She was using inhalers for reactive airway disease. Immunization was up to date. Her father had been treated for abdominal tuberculosis. Notably, a first cousin had died at 14 years of age following a severe respiratory infection, without having been investigated for immunodeficiency.

On examination, the child was alert and moderately built. She was febrile (101.5°F) with tachycardia (PR 98/min) and tachypnea (RR 30/min). Significant findings included digital clubbing, bilateral axillary lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and a perforated left tympanic membrane. Chest auscultation revealed scattered crepitations and occasional rhonchi.

Investigations revealed leukocytosis with total count of 13,000/μL (4,500-11,000/μL), neutrophils 72% (50-70%), lymphocytes 20% (20-40%), CRP 62.8 mg/L (<5.0 mg/L), and a platelet count of 156,000/μL (150,000-400,000/μL). Chest X-ray demonstrated right lower zone consolidation as shown in Figure 3. Evidence of sinusitis was seen in a paranasal sinuses (PNS) X-ray (Figure 4). HRCT chest confirmed cylindrical and cystic bronchiectasis of the right middle lobe as shown in Figure 5.

Serum immunoglobulin levels confirmed the diagnosis: IgG was profoundly reduced at < 75 mg/dL (reference 700-1600 mg/dL), with IgA < 10 mg/dL (reference 70-400 mg/dL). CVID was diagnosed. In addition to treatment of the active infection, the first dose of IVIG was administered, with plans for monthly continuation.

Case 3

A 50-year-old man presented with a history of cough with mucoid expectoration for five weeks, associated with intermittent fever and evening rise of temperature. He had a weight loss of 4.5 kg over three months. There was no haemoptysis. He had a background history of recurrent chest infections and asthma.

On examination, his vital signs were pulse 96/min, BP 130/70 mmHg, RR 19/min, and SpO₂ 96% on room air. Chest auscultation revealed bilateral polyphonic rhonchi and bilateral interscapular coarse crepitations. ECG showed sinus tachycardia (Figure 7).

Blood investigations demonstrated a leukocyte count of 13,400/μL (4,500-11,000/μL), neutrophils 74% (50-70%), lymphocytes 19% (20-40%), hemoglobin 15.4 g/dL (13.5-17.5 g/dL), and platelet count 100,000/μL (150,000-400,000/μL), consistent with active infection. X-ray PNS demonstrated maxillary sinusitis as in Figure 8. HRCT chest revealed mild bronchiectatic changes (Figure 9).

Investigations for fungal and mycobacterial infection were negative. Aspergillus IgM and IgG were negative, and Aspergillus fumigatus was not detected. Sputum smear showed no AFB, MTB was not detected on GeneXpert, and serum IgE was 116 IU/ml ( reference range <150 IU/ml).

Serum immunoglobulin quantification revealed panhypogammaglobulinaemia: IgG < 75 mg/dL (reference 700-1600 mg/dL), IgA < 10 mg/dL (reference 70-400 mg/dL), and IgM < 20 mg/dL (reference 40-230 mg/dL).

His HIV serology was negative, excluding HIV-associated hypogammaglobulinemia. Serum total protein and albumin were 5.8 g/dL and 3.6 g/dL, respectively, with a normal albumin-to-globulin ratio, effectively excluding significant protein-losing states as a contributory mechanism. Urinary protein loss was assessed by spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio which was 24 mg/g, excluding nephrotic syndrome as a cause of immunoglobulin depletion. A thorough medication review revealed no exposure to rituximab, corticosteroids, anticonvulsants, or other recognized causes of secondary hypogammaglobulinemia. Given the thrombocytopenia and weight loss in a 50-year-old, hematologic malignancy was actively considered; peripheral blood smear showed moderate thrombocytopenia, and no atypical cells were seen. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 240 IU/L. These findings, combined with the clinical history of recurrent sinopulmonary infections, confirmed the diagnosis of CVID.

The patient received IVIG replacement therapy at a standard dose of 500 mg/kg per infusion, administered at monthly intervals. Infusions were administered under supervised clinical conditions, and no significant infusion-related adverse reactions were recorded throughout the treatment course. Treatment response was assessed on clinical grounds, with the patient demonstrating a notable reduction in the frequency and severity of recurrent infections following initiation of IVIG replacement.

Discussion

CVID is the most prevalent symptomatic primary antibody deficiency. It shows equal sex distribution and a bimodal age of presentation - in early childhood and again in the third to fourth decades [1,3]. All three cases in this series reflect this spectrum: a 14-year-old girl, a 32-year-old woman, and a 50-year-old man, illustrating that CVID has a bimodal age of onset.

The most striking feature in all three patients was the substantial diagnostic delay. In Case 1, bilateral bronchiectasis severe enough to require a left lower lobectomy had developed before CVID was recognised. In Case 2, recurrent infections had been documented since age nine, five years before the diagnosis. This pattern mirrors international data, where the mean delay between symptom onset and diagnosis can exceed eight years [5]. Delayed immunoglobulin replacement may contribute to progressive and often irreversible bronchiectatic lung damage.

The diagnostic criteria applied in these cases follow established guidelines. CVID requires (a) a suggestive clinical history, (b) markedly reduced total serum IgG (most patients < 400 mg/dL at diagnosis; all three patients in our case series had IgG levels below the assay’s lower reporting threshold of 75 mg/dL) combined with low IgA and/or IgM, and (c) exclusion of other primary and secondary causes of hypogammaglobulinaemia including haematological malignancies [2,4]. In Case 3, panhypogammaglobulinaemia (IgG, IgA, and IgM all significantly reduced) further reinforced the diagnosis, which is seen in a subset of CVID patients and is generally associated with a more severe phenotype [3].

Beyond recurrent infections, CVID is associated with a spectrum of non-infectious complications. Chronic lung disease, particularly bronchiectasis, is seen in 30-50% of patients and represents a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [3]. Gastrointestinal disease occurs in approximately 20% of patients, including chronic infections with Giardia, cytomegalovirus, and Cryptosporidium. One-quarter of CVID patients develop autoimmune conditions, most commonly immune thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, and rheumatoid arthritis. Granulomatous disease affects 8-20%, and patients carry an increased susceptibility to non-Hodgkin lymphoma [3,4]. The lymphadenopathy observed in Case 2, which had prompted an axillary lymph node biopsy (showing only reactive changes) and a PET scan (negative for lymphoma), illustrates this diagnostic complexity.

A limitation of this case report is the absence of formal vaccine antibody response testing, which could not be performed due to non-availability of serotype-specific pneumococcal IgG assays. This reflects the resource constraints faced by clinicians diagnosing PID in India. Future efforts toward establishing standardized PID diagnostic pathways in resource-limited settings are warranted.

The family history in Case 2 - a first cousin who died at age 14 following a respiratory infection without immunological investigation - is clinically significant. CVID is largely considered a polygenic or multifactorial disorder, but familial clustering is documented, and family members of CVID patients warrant screening for immunoglobulin deficiency [2,5].

Treatment of CVID is centred on immunoglobulin replacement therapy. IVIG is typically administered at a dose of 400-600 mg/kg every three to four weeks, with dose adjustments to maintain trough IgG levels sufficient to prevent infections [4]. Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG) is an increasingly used alternative, particularly for home-based administration. In all three patients, IVIG was initiated and regular follow-up planned. Immunisation practices require modification: live-attenuated vaccines are contraindicated; only inactivated or subunit vaccines should be used, and annual inactivated influenza vaccine is recommended [4].

The publication of CVID case series from India is of particular importance. Awareness of PIDs among clinicians in low- and middle-income countries remains suboptimal. Patients with recurrent infections are far more commonly evaluated for tuberculosis, fungal infections, or anatomical abnormalities than for underlying immunodeficiency [6]. In this series, all three patients had undergone multiple investigations for infective etiologies before the consideration of primary antibody deficiency. Increasing PID awareness, expanding access to immunoglobulin quantification, and facilitating affordable IVIG therapy are critical unmet needs in the Indian healthcare system.

Conclusions

This case series demonstrates that CVID can affect patients across a wide age range and often remains undiagnosed for years despite characteristic clinical features. A high index of suspicion for primary antibody deficiency should be maintained in any patient presenting with recurrent sinopulmonary infections, regardless of age, particularly when infections are unusually frequent, severe, or associated with structural lung disease. Serum immunoglobulin quantification is a relatively simple and increasingly accessible investigation that could be performed early in the diagnostic workup of recurrent infections. Timely diagnosis and immunoglobulin replacement therapy may reduce infection frequency and help prevent further progression of lung damage. A limitation of this case report is the absence of formal vaccine antibody response testing, which could not be performed due to limited resources. Clinician education in combination with access to immunoglobulin testing, immunology referral, vaccine response assessment, immunoglobulin availability, and family screening for primary immunodeficiency disorders is an urgent need in the Indian clinical setting.
