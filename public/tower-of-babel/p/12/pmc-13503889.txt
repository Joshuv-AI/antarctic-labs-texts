Reinforcement learning from human feedback improves automatic speech recognition models for ancient Thai language
Authors: Jettasic Popun, Wilaiporn Lee, Kanabadee Srisomboon, Luepol Pipanmekaporn, Akara Prayote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thailand’s ancient medicinal manuscripts preserve centuries of therapeutic wisdom but are threatened by physical deterioration. Conventional digitization via Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is largely ineffective due to faded ink and archaic orthography on palm leaves. Consequently, oral recitation by expert practitioners remains the most reliable method for preserving this content. This study proposes an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) framework enhanced with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to transcribe ancient Thai Traditional Medicine (TTM) recitations. We developed a domain-specific corpus and applied rigorous data augmentation techniques (pitch shifting and time stretching) to expand the dataset from 2,000 to 14,000 utterances, recorded from ten certified practitioners. Two state-of-the-art architectures, Whisper-small and Wav2Vec2, were evaluated before and after optimization using a reward model trained on expert linguistic judgments of phonetic, tonal, and semantic fidelity. Experimental results demonstrate that RLHF, combined with the augmented dataset, substantially improves transcription quality. Whisper-small achieved a 42.2% reduction in Word Error Rate (WER) and demonstrated superior tonal preservation compared to supervised baselines. These findings highlight the effectiveness of human-aligned ASR for low-resource tonal languages and support the scalable digitization of endangered medical heritage.

Introduction

Thailand’s ancient medicinal manuscripts, typically recorded on palm leaves or inscribed on stone tablets, preserve centuries of therapeutic wisdom and cultural heritage. However, these artifacts are rapidly deteriorating due to physical degradation, exhibiting blurred ink, glyph deformation, and irregular spacing. As illustrated in the left panel of Fig. 1, the physical condition of these sources makes direct text extraction via Optical Character Recognition (OCR) extremely difficult, often failing to address disconnected character boundaries and non-standard script forms. Given these limitations, oral recitation by certified Thai Traditional Medicine (TTM) practitioners remains the most reliable medium for capturing the semantic and tonal fidelity of these texts, serving as a vital link for intergenerational knowledge transfer1. Consequently, Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) offers a scalable method for digitization, yet Thai presents unique challenges due to its five-tone phonemic system and historical pronunciation drift2. While state-of-the-art ASR architectures such as Wav2Vec24–6 and Whisper7,8 have demonstrated strong multilingual generalization, they often struggle with the specific nuances of ancient TTM recitations. Recent studies in Scientific Reports have highlighted similar challenges in cross-language transfer for low-resource dialects9, while others have emphasized the need for richer representation learning from raw speech waveforms10,11. Furthermore, standard benchmarks in Thai ASR12 and specific studies on TTM digitization13,14 highlight persistent issues with tonal confusion and rare vocabulary. Although Transformer-based models15 capture long-range dependencies, the lack of explicit tone information in standard training objectives limits their precision in this specialized domain16. Optimization techniques such as fine-tuning with specialized constraints have shown promise in addressing these limitations17,18.

Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has recently emerged as a powerful technique for aligning machine outputs with human expectations19–21. Recent advancements in reinforcement learning have demonstrated robust optimization capabilities in complex environments, such as dynamic AI optimization for sustainable systems22. However, its application to ASR-especially for tonal, low-resource, and heritage languages-remains limited23–25. To the best of our knowledge, there are no existing studies applying ASR enhanced with RLHF specifically in the context of ancient Thai language preservation. The integration of expert judgments regarding tone correctness and semantic fidelity into a reward function is crucial for preserving the integrity of ancient Thai.

This study investigates whether RLHF can significantly improve ASR transcription accuracy for ancient Thai recitations. We adopted a two-phase evaluation approach: first, establishing a performance benchmark across traditional (Kaldi) and modern architectures (Wav2Vec2, Whisper) to identify the most suitable baselines; and second, implementing a novel RLHF framework (as shown in the right panel of Fig. 1). This framework integrates a DistilBERT-based reward model26 trained on multi-dimensional expert judgments with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)27,28 to fine-tune the selected ASR systems. By utilizing the newly augmented Thai Ancient Medical Speech Corpus (TAMSC) and applying rigorous preprocessing–including spectral gating29 and phoneme alignment via the Montreal Forced Aligner30–this work aims to reduce common errors such as tonal confusion and lexeme substitution.

Results

The evaluation of the proposed framework consists of two phases: first, benchmarking state-of-the-art ASR architectures to establish a strong baseline for ancient Thai medical speech on the held-out test set; and second, assessing the impact of Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) on transcription accuracy and tonal correctness. To ensure robustness against speaker bias, all evaluations were validated using a leave-one-speaker-out cross-validation strategy.

As shown in Table 1, the traditional Kaldi system struggled significantly with the archaic corpus. In contrast, deep learning-based models demonstrated superior baseline performance. A critical component of our framework is the DistilBERT-based reward model, which was trained to proxy expert judgments. To ensure the reliability of the human feedback signal and assess potential biases, we calculated the inter-annotator agreement among the three TTM experts using Fleiss’ Kappa. The experts achieved a score of κ = 0.82, indicating “almost perfect agreement” in ranking the transcript pairs based on tonal and semantic fidelity.

As shown in Fig. 4, the model’s predictions strongly correlate with human expert scores, achieving a Pearson correlation coefficient of r=0.87 for Whisper outputs. Discussion

This study demonstrates the efficacy of integrating Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) into Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) pipelines for digitizing endangered languages, specifically ancient Thai medicinal manuscripts. Our results confirm that while state-of-the-art foundation models like Whisper and Wav2Vec2 possess strong generalization capabilities4,7, they are insufficient for specialized domains characterized by archaic vocabulary and complex tonal structures without targeted alignment. The introduction of PPO-based fine-tuning significantly reduced the Word Error Rate (WER) by over 42.2% in the Whisper-small model, yielding a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.4) and validating our hypothesis that human-aligned reward signals can effectively bridge the gap between acoustic representation and semantic correctness19,23.

A critical challenge in Thai ASR is the handling of homophones with different tones, where a slight pitch variation drastically alters the meaning. Regarding the ambiguity of these tonal homophones, the base transformer architectures inherently leverage surrounding contextual sequences to differentiate meanings. However, supervised fine-tuning alone is often insufficient. Our framework addresses this by explicitly penalizing tonal and semantic inaccuracies through the RLHF reward model. This provides a targeted, automated mechanism during PPO training to resolve these ambiguities more effectively than standard cross-entropy loss, as evidenced by the sharp drop in Tonal Confusion errors (Table 3).

Furthermore, the integration of extensive data augmentation techniques (pitch shifting and time stretching) during the initial dataset preparation proved instrumental in preventing model overfitting. By exposing the acoustic encoder to 14,000 augmented variations of historical recitations, the models developed a highly robust representation of the Thai tonal space. This robustness was successfully preserved and refined during the RLHF phase, allowing the policy network to generalize better to subtle inflection shifts.

A key finding from our comparative analysis is the architectural divergence in performance between the encoder-decoder model (Whisper) and the CTC-based model (Wav2Vec2). As observed in the qualitative analysis (Table 4), Whisper-small exhibited superior capability in maintaining long-range semantic dependencies and correct word ordering. This is likely attributed to its autoregressive decoding mechanism, which leverages context from preceding tokens to resolve ambiguities in ancient texts where homophones are prevalent8,15. In contrast, Wav2Vec2, despite its strong acoustic feature extraction, occasionally suffered from “lexical dropout” or inversion errors. This limitation underscores the challenge of CTC-based systems in implicitly modeling language structure without an external language model, particularly when dealing with the non-standard syntax of historical dialects5,6.

The significant reduction in tonal confusion errors highlights the critical role of expert feedback in preserving the phonological integrity of Thai Traditional Medicine (TTM) recitations. Since tonal inflection in Thai dictates lexical meaning2, standard loss functions (like Cross-Entropy or CTC loss) often fail to penalize subtle pitch errors that do not disrupt phonetic sequences but drastically alter semantic interpretation. By incorporating expert judgments into the reward model26, our system effectively “learned” to prioritize tonal accuracy, addressing a persistent bottleneck in Thai ASR12,16.

Computational Complexity and Scalability: While RLHF provides significant accuracy gains, it introduces computational overhead. Training the Reward Model required approximately 12 hours on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, and the PPO fine-tuning phase added an additional 24 hours of training time compared to standard supervised fine-tuning. However, inference latency remains unchanged since the reward model and value network are discarded after training, leaving only the optimized policy model. This makes the proposed framework highly scalable for deployment in digital libraries or mobile applications for TTM practitioners, where runtime efficiency is paramount.

Limitations and Future Work: While the leave-one-speaker-out cross-validation confirms baseline robustness across our specific participant pool, we explicitly acknowledge that our evaluation of generalizability remains strictly bounded by the scope of the current TAMSC dataset (10 speakers). Therefore, any claims regarding the model’s robustness and generalization should be interpreted strictly within the context of this specific demographic and experimental evidence. Generalizing to entirely unseen speakers, different regional dialects, or a broader population of practitioners outside of central TTM recitations remains a critical limitation due to the extreme scarcity of certified practitioners. Furthermore, the persistence of errors related to Ancient Orthography (15.6%) indicates a “modernization bias,” where acoustic adaptation transcribes heard speech into Modern Thai rather than the historical script3. Addressing this requires a specialized orthographic normalization layer or a larger corpus of paired ancient text-speech data, which is currently scarce.

In conclusion, this research presents a pioneering framework for the digital preservation of tangible cultural heritage through AI. The integration of ASR with RLHF has proven to be better than traditional methods by actively incorporating expert linguistic knowledge into the model’s optimization loop. This approach effectively mitigated the high error rates typically associated with tonal and archaic languages, achieving a 42.2% reduction in WER. By creating an augmented, curated dataset of ancient TTM recitations and demonstrating the viability of RLHF for low-resource ASR, we provide a scalable solution that transcends the limitations of traditional OCR for degraded manuscripts. This approach not only facilitates the accessibility of ancient medical wisdom but also serves as a methodological blueprint for safeguarding other endangered languages, aligning with UNESCO’s vision for multimodal heritage preservation1,31.

Methods

The proposed framework integrates baseline Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) training with a Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) loop. The methodology comprises data collection, preprocessing, dataset augmentation, baseline model training, reward model construction, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) fine-tuning.Fig. 5The end-to-end RLHF fine-tuning pipeline. The workflow is divided into three main stages: (1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Baseline ASR models (Whisper/Wav2Vec2) are trained on the curated TAMSC dataset to establish initial transcription capabilities. (2) Reward Model Training: A DistilBERT-based reward model is trained on a pairwise comparison dataset, where TTM experts rank transcripts based on tonal and semantic accuracy. (3) Reinforcement Learning (RL): The SFT model is optimized using the PPO algorithm, which updates the policy to maximize the reward signal while minimizing the KL divergence from the original baseline.

The workflow is divided into three main stages: (1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Baseline ASR models (Whisper/Wav2Vec2) are trained on the curated TAMSC dataset to establish initial transcription capabilities. (2) Reward Model Training: A DistilBERT-based reward model is trained on a pairwise comparison dataset, where TTM experts rank transcripts based on tonal and semantic accuracy. (3) Reinforcement Learning (RL): The SFT model is optimized using the PPO algorithm, which updates the policy to maximize the reward signal while minimizing the KL divergence from the original baseline.

The overall pipeline, as illustrated in Fig. 5, demonstrates the hierarchical integration of these components. First, raw TTM speech is preprocessed, augmented, and used to fine-tune the baseline models (SFT). Next, the model generates multiple candidate transcripts for a subset of data, which are ranked by human experts to create a preference dataset for training the Reward Model. Finally, in the RL phase, the model generates transcripts that are scored by the frozen Reward Model. These scores serve as feedback signals for the PPO algorithm to adjust the model’s parameters, specifically targeting the reduction of tonal confusion and orthographic errors that standard supervised loss functions fail to capture effectively.

Participant Demographics and Recording Protocol: Ten certified Thai Traditional Medicine (TTM) practitioners (5 male, 5 female, age range 25–60 years) were recruited for the recording sessions. All participants held valid licenses from the Thai Traditional Medical Council and possessed at least 5 years of experience in reciting ancient scriptures. Recordings were conducted in a sound-treated studio environment (background noise <30 dBA) using a condenser microphone (Audio-Technica AT2020) at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz, later downsampled to 16 kHz for model compatibility.

Data Augmentation Strategy: To enhance the robustness of the acoustic models and mitigate the risk of overfitting on the low-resource dataset, we applied extensive data augmentation techniques to the baseline corpus. For each of the 2,000 original utterances, we generated six additional variants using time stretching and pitch shifting via the Librosa library. Specifically, time stretching was applied to alter the audio speed by factors of 0.90, 0.95, 1.05, and 1.10, simulating fine-grained variations in recitation pacing. Pitch shifting was applied to raise and lower the pitch by 2 semitones (± 2), simulating different vocal tract sizes and registers without distorting the linguistic tonal structure. This systematic augmentation process expanded the dataset from 2,000 to a robust 14,000 utterances.

RLHF formulation

We frame the ASR refinement as a sequential decision-making process formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

Constrained action space

To address the complexity of ancient Thai orthography, we define a pruned action space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathscr {A}}_t$$\end{document} at each decoding step t. Instead of allowing any vocabulary token, the valid actions are constrained by phonotactic rules to prevent invalid tone-syllable combinations. The action set is defined as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {A}}_t = \left\{ y_t \in {\mathscr {V}} \mid y_t \in \text {TopK}(p_\theta (\cdot )), \Phi _{phon}(y_t)=1, \Phi _{orth}(y_t)=1 \right\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathscr {V}}$$\end{document} is the vocabulary, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {TopK}$$\end{document} filters the most probable tokens from the policy network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_\theta$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Phi _{phon}, \Phi _{orth}$$\end{document} are binary constraint functions ensuring phonetic and orthographic validity, respectively.

Reward model construction and training

A critical innovation of this work is the domain-specific reward function. We utilize a DistilBERT-based regression model trained on expert preferences.

Human Feedback Collection Protocol: To train the reward model, we generated a dataset of 10,000 paired transcripts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(y_w, y_l)$$\end{document} from the baseline ASR models, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_w$$\end{document} represents the “winning” (better) transcript and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_l$$\end{document} represents the “losing” transcript. Three TTM experts evaluated these pairs based on a hierarchical criteria: 1) Tonal Correctness, 2) Semantic Meaning, and 3) Spelling Accuracy. If a transcript had correct tones but wrong spelling, it was preferred over one with correct spelling but wrong tones, reflecting the priority of oral transmission fidelity.

The Reward Model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_\phi (x, y)$$\end{document} was trained to maximize the likelihood of the preferred transcript using a pairwise ranking loss:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\mathscr {L}}_{rank}(\phi ) = - \mathbb {E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim {\mathscr {D}}} \left[ \log \left( \sigma (r_\phi (x, y_w) - r_\phi (x, y_l)) \right) \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where σ is the sigmoid function and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathscr {D}}$$\end{document} is the dataset of human comparisons. The final reward output R(y) is a scalar value representing the linguistic quality of the transcript.

The total reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{total}(y)$$\end{document} for a transcript y is computed as a weighted sum of normalized scores across four linguistic dimensions:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R_{total}(y) = \sum _{i \in \{tone, lex, sem, orth\}} \alpha _i \cdot f_{i\_norm}(y) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _i$$\end{document} represents the weighting coefficient for tonal accuracy (tone), lexical fidelity (lex), semantic coherence (sem), and orthographic correctness (orth), calibrated via expert feedback.

Optimization via PPO

We fine-tune the ASR policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _\theta$$\end{document} using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The objective is to maximize the expected reward while maintaining a trust region to prevent catastrophic forgetting of the pre-trained knowledge.

To ensure stability, we incorporated a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty into the reward function to constrain the updated policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _\theta$$\end{document} from deviating excessively from the initial supervised policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi _{ref}$$\end{document}:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R_{ppo}(x,y) = R_{total}(y) - \beta \log \left( \frac{\pi _\theta (y|x)}{\pi _{ref}(y|x)} \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where β is the adaptive KL penalty coefficient.

The clipped surrogate objective function used for the policy update is given by:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L^{CLIP}(\theta ) = \hat{\mathbb {E}}_t \left[ \min (r_t(\theta ){\hat{A}}_t, \text {clip}(r_t(\theta ), 1-\epsilon , 1+\epsilon ){\hat{A}}_t) \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_t(\theta )$$\end{document} is the probability ratio between the new and old policies, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{A}}_t$$\end{document} is the estimated advantage function computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), and ε =0.2 is the clipping hyperparameter. This ensures stable updates even in the high-variance domain of speech processing.
