Risk of Cardiac Disease in a Population-Based Cohort of Myotonic Dystrophy Type 1 and Type 2 in the United States
Authors: Nicholas E. Johnson, Vinay Bhandaru, Jennifer G. Andrews, Treeva Jaff, Y. Swamy Venkatesh, Frank J. Raucci, Aida Soim, Emma Ciafaloni, Madeline Murguia Rice, as the Muscular Dystrophy Surveillance, Tracking, and Research Network (MD STARnet)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesMyotonic dystrophy type 1 and type 2 (DM1/DM2) are multisystemic disorders that may affect heart function in addition to the progressive skeletal muscle weakness and myotonia that occur. There are limited population-based studies on the prevalence and risks associated with cardiac symptoms in DM1 and DM2.MethodsCardiac data were abstracted from the medical records of 429 individuals with DM1 and DM2 with a health care encounter between 2008 and 2019. The frequencies of cardiac arrhythmias and of cardiomyopathy were assessed along with relationships to demographic data and age at onset.ResultsA total of 429 individuals from MD STARnet were included in the analyses, with 82.3% having DM1. Overall, cardiac arrhythmias were observed in 44.5% and cardiomyopathy in 9.1%. The risk of developing a cardiac arrhythmia was much higher in DM1 than in DM2. Repeat length was not a risk factor for the development of a cardiac arrhythmia. Developing DM1 in childhood or adulthood as opposed to congenital onset was a risk factor of both cardiac arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy in multivariable analysis.DiscussionThis study confirms the increased risk of cardiac arrhythmias in individuals with DM and DM2 and identifies the development of cardiomyopathy as a low-frequency event. Notably, the risk of cardiac arrhythmias is particularly elevated in DM1. The study reaffirms the need for serial cardiac monitoring in individuals with DM1/DM2.

Introduction

Myotonic dystrophy type 1 and type 2 are related disorders due to a toxic RNA repeat expansion. In myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), a CUG repeat expansion in the 3′ untranslated region of the dystrophia myotonia protein kinase gene leads to distal weakness, myotonia (e.g., delayed muscle relaxation), and early-onset cataracts.1 In myotonic dystrophy type 2 (DM2), a CCTG repeat in intron 1 of the CCHC-type zinc finger nucleic acid binding protein gene leads to proximal weakness, myotonia, and early-onset cataracts.1 Both disorders are multisystemic with symptoms across nearly every organ system studied. Both disorders are progressive in nature and may involve the cardiac system.2,3

Prior studies have demonstrated an increased risk of cardiac arrhythmias with increasing disease severity among individuals with DM1.4-7 Related studies have demonstrated that there is increased mortality in patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction in DM1.4 A prior study using International Classification of Diseases 9 or 10 (ICD-9, ICD-10) codes to ascertain a population-based sample in Utah found that individuals with DM1 and DM2 had 55 times greater risk of cardiac arrhythmias compared with the general population.8 This prior population study was limited by the use of the ICD code that combined DM1 and DM2 (ICD-10 code for G71.11). Indeed, for individuals with DM2, there is limited information on the risk of cardiac disease outside small case cohorts.9-11

The Myotonic Dystrophy Foundation has provided consensus-based care guidelines for management of cardiac disease in myotonic DM1.12 These care guidelines recommend annual ECGs and cardiac imaging (e.g., MRI or echocardiogram [ECHO]) every 1–5 years. Based on these screening tests, more testing, including Holter monitoring or electrophysiologic studies, may be recommended. Primary or secondary prevention pacemaker or implantable cardiac defibrillator (ICD) is indicated for individuals living with DM1 who either are deemed to be at high risk or have symptomatic arrhythmias. It is not clear whether these monitoring or treatment strategies are similarly applicable to DM2.

In this study, we aim to describe the cardiac disease in a population-based sample of recently diagnosed individuals living with DM1 and DM2.

Methods

The Muscular Dystrophy Surveillance, Tracking, and Research Network (MD STARnet) retrospectively collects data on individuals living with muscular dystrophy and has been described elsewhere.13 Abstractors identify surveillance-eligible individuals using clinical records that may include hospitals, clinics, hospital discharge databases, and administrative billing sets based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes. These codes include those for myotonic muscular dystrophy (359.21; G711.11), myotonic disorders (G71.1), and muscular dystrophy (G71.0).

Abstractors trained in the electronic health record and the clinical features associated with myotonic dystrophy reviewed medical records for diagnostic information, including signs and symptoms, family history, muscle biopsy, electrodiagnostic testing, and genetic reports (eMethods). This information was used by a committee of clinicians to assign a case status to every person with a myotonic dystrophy diagnosis. The cardiac history and features were similarly captured, including ECGs, Holter reports, and echocardiograms.

Cardiac arrhythmias were defined as the presence of bradycardia, tachycardia, atrial ventricular block, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, junctional rhythm, conduction delay, and/or supraventricular rhythm, as noted on ECGs or Holter monitors. Sinus arrhythmia was excluded. Cardiomyopathies (also referred to as heart failure) were defined as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) less than 55% or shortening fraction less than 28% if LVEF is not available.14

Study Sample Inclusion Criteria

There were 482 individuals in our data who had a health care encounter between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2016 (Figure). Individuals were excluded from the analysis if they could not be identified as having either DM1 or DM2 (myotonic dystrophy not otherwise specified) or were asymptomatic. They also had to be a resident of one of the surveillance locations. Individuals were excluded if they had no symptoms of myotonic dystrophy.

Data were collected from the following MD STARnet surveillance locations: CO, 21 counties in Western NY, 33 counties in NC's Piedmont region, South Carolina, IA, and UT. This study was approved or exempted by the institutional review boards or ethics committees at each study site. Five sites also had public health authority (CO, IA, NC, NY, and SC).

Statistical Analyses

For each binary outcome, i.e., arrhythmia (yes/no) or cardiomyopathy (yes/no), individual characteristics were compared between outcome groups using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. Generalized linear models for binary outcomes were used to estimate the adjusted relative risk and 95% CI. Log-binomial or modified Poisson regression models were developed for each outcome (other than arrhythmia and cardiomyopathy) using backward elimination, retaining variables that remained significant at p < 0.05. Variables eligible for multivariable analysis included age at first symptom onset, sex (male/female), DM type (DM1, DM2, or combined DM1 and DM2) and CTG repeat length categorical variable (50–250, 251–500, 501–750, 751–1,000, >1,001), and history of private insurance (yes/no). Statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4. All tests were 2-tailed, and p < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons.

Data Availability

Owing to privacy concerns, data from the MD STARnet are not publicly available. Researchers interested in MD STARnet data can contact MDSTARnet@cdc.gov. Data used for this analysis are maintained at Virginia Commonwealth University.

Results

There were 367 individuals with DM1 in the cohort, but 14 individuals were excluded for being asymptomatic. There were 77 individuals with DM2 in the cohort, but 1 individual was excluded for being asymptomatic. Among all eligible individuals (DM1 and DM2) in the cohort (n = 426), 191 (44.5%) had a cardiac arrhythmia and 39 (9.1%) had cardiomyopathy. Among the 426 participants with DM1 or DM2, 13 (3.1%) had only Holter monitoring, 237 (55.6%) had only ECG, 79 (18.5%) had both Holter and ECG, and 97 (22.8%) had neither. Among the 178 individuals identified with arrhythmia, 8 (4.5%) had arrhythmia identified by Holter only, 140 (78.7%) by ECG only, and 30 (16.9%) by both ECG and Holter.

Among the 426 participants, 234 (54.9%) had ECHO only, 5 (1.2%) had ECHO and cardiac MRI, and 187 (43.9%) had neither. Among the 39 individuals identified with cardiomyopathy, 38 (97.4%) were identified by ECHO only and 1 (2.6%) by both cardiac MRI and ECHO. In the cohort, 45.1% of individuals with DM1 had cardiac arrhythmias and 26.3% of individuals with DM2 had cardiac arrhythmias (Table 1). When evaluating cardiomyopathies, 10.0% of individuals with DM1 and 5.3% of individuals with DM2 met the criteria. Most of the cohort had DM1 (82.3%) compared with DM2. 45.5% were men. The mean age at first DNA or EMG test was 36.1 years for men and 36.3 for women. Of the cohort, 77 individuals had no Holter monitor, ECG, echocardiogram, or cardiac MRI reported in the medical record. By the end of data collection, 7.7% of women were deceased and 10.8% of men were deceased.

Overall, the frequency of cardiomyopathy was low (Table 4). There was no difference between DM1 and DM2. Men were significantly more likely to have a cardiomyopathy. In addition, those noted to have adult-onset DM1 were more likely to have a cardiomyopathy compared with those with congenital/childhood-onset DM1. Among 350 patients with DM1, the frequency of any cardiac arrhythmia was higher in those with cardiomyopathy compared with those without cardiomyopathy, as was the frequency of malignant cardiac arrhythmia (Table 5, Table 6). Compared with the population frequency of cardiomyopathy, estimated to be around 0.04%, approximately 9.1% of our cohort had cardiomyopathy.

In a risk model, the risk of developing cardiac arrhythmia was associated with having DM1 as opposed to DM2 (Table 7). Childhood/adult-onset DM1 was also a risk factor of cardiac arrhythmias compared with congenital-onset DM1. There were no significant group differences in the time from DM1 symptom onset to the end of follow-up among the 3 groups (congenital, childhood, adult) based on the Kruskal-Wallis test (p = 0.4573). The end of follow-up was defined as death or the end of 2016, and among DM1 cases, there were 26 deaths before the end of 2016. When looking specifically at individuals with DM1 and excluding those without a reported CTG repeat length, there was no association between cardiac arrhythmias and the degree of repeat expansion. For cardiomyopathies, adult-onset DM1 (compared with childhood or congenital onset) was a risk factor, as was being a male.

This study identified the frequency of cardiac disease in a population of individuals living with DM1 and DM2. Similar to prior studies, cardiac arrhythmia is a risk factor for those living with myotonic dystrophy and likely contributes to the morbidity and mortality seen with this condition. Notably, the study also identified that cardiomyopathy was more prevalent than in the general population and was linked to the presence of underlying arrhythmias.

This study found that the risk of arrhythmia is significantly higher in individuals living with DM1 compared with the general population. The risk of cardiac arrhythmias was lower in those with DM2 than in those with DM1, albeit still elevated compared with the general population. Regarding the DM2 risk frequency, it is possible that these individuals are not ascertained as well as those with DM1. It is also possible that the older age of those living with DM2 may confound these results. Within the DM1 cohort, there was no difference in risk with the increasing CTG repeat expansion.15 This is different from prior work that identified increasing risk with more progressed disease or a larger CTG repeat expansions. It is possible that, given the relatively recent observation period of the cohort, we identified more newly diagnosed individuals. However, we would note that, in our risk model, congenital onset was associated with a reduced risk compared with a later age at onset. Given the short duration of follow-up, it is possible this is a proxy for age. Further observation is required.

Cardiomyopathy is more common in patients with DM1 and DM2 compared with the general population.16 Similar to the general population, men are at a higher risk. It was unexpected that those with adult-onset DM1 were at a higher risk than those with congenital/childhood-onset disease. However, this higher prevalence was significantly associated with the presence of arrhythmias, particularly malignant arrhythmias. This would suggest clinicians should aggressively manage these arrhythmias.

These findings affirm the current care recommendations that identify the need for at least annual cardiac surveillance in DM1 and DM2. The findings also indicate a generally reduced risk in those individuals living with DM2 compared with DM1. Given the low frequency of cardiomyopathy, future screening guidelines may be able to tailor these recommendations to those with other known risk factors for the development of cardiomyopathy.

There are several limitations to this study. There was a relatively short observation period. There was a relatively large proportion of not otherwise specified cases that could not be classified. Similarly, many individuals were missing repeat lengths. These may have led to bias in the risk models. However, the population-based ascertainment methodology provides several benefits over prior studies that required individuals to be recruited into the study. Finally, the ECG data were abstracted from standard clinical encounters and were not re-reviewed by a central rater. It is possible that this may have led to regional variation in interpretation of the test results.

Overall, this study supports the prior observations that cardiac arrhythmias are more frequent in DM1 than in DM2. Contributory risk factors relate to the age at onset but not to the repeat length itself.
