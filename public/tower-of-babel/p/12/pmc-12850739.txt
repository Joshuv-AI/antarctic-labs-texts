Enhancing peritoneal visualization in peroral endoscopic myotomy with fundoplication using novel robotic peritoneal pressure optimization
Authors: Ayah Matar, Farimah Fayyaz, Preethi Jagannath, Jose Antonio Almario, Mouen Khashab
Published: 2024

One key concern in natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery is creating and maintaining pneumoperitoneum intra-abdominally and intraluminally. Traditionally, this is achieved using a Veress needle to control capnoperitoneum and manual CO2 insufflation through the endoscope. Close monitoring of the peak airway and intraperitoneal pressures2 is necessary throughout the procedure. A recent endoscopic procedure requiring peritoneal entry is peroral endoscopic myotomy with fundoplication (POEM-F). GERD is a known adverse event after POEM for achalasia.1 Endoscopic fundoplication after POEM is an antireflux procedure involving transmural dissection to access the peritoneum, where a loop ligating device is anchored to the stomach fundus and the myotomy edge to create a wrap.2 During fundoplication, generating and maintaining a low but stable intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) is necessary for optimal visualization and to prevent cardiopulmonary adverse events and tension capnoperitoneum.3

Endoscopic technique

In the POEM part of the procedure (Fig. 1, A-D), a submucosal injection created a mucosal bleb (Fig. 1, A). An incision was made (Fig. 1, B) at the anterior esophageal wall, followed by submucosal dissection and tunneling (Fig. 1, C). Finally, the esophageal and gastric myotomy was performed (Fig. 1, D).

Peroral endoscopic myotomy with fundoplication. A-D, Peroral endoscopic myotomy part of the procedure. E-H, Fundoplication part of the procedure.

The fundoplication part of the procedure (Fig. 1, E-H) involved dissecting the perigastric fat and serosa overlying the gastric myotomy to access the peritoneal cavity. The fundus was grasped and retracted into the tunnel to simulate the wrap (Fig. 1, E). The wrap was viewed from within the gastric lumen using an ultraslim additional scope. The fundic serosa was marked using cautery (Fig. 1, F). The endoloop was fixed to the gastric fundus, and the proximal end of the endoloop was attached to the distal extent of the myotomy (Fig. 1, G). The endoloop was tightened, and the loop tail was trimmed (Fig. 1, H).

Traditionally, Veress needle decompression through the anterior abdomen manages the capnoperitoneum generated by CO2 insufflation through the gastroscope, maintaining peak airway and intraperitoneal pressures.2 This requires inspecting the peritoneal cavity to ensure no vascular or visceral injuries have occurred. However, for this POEM-F procedure, we used a robotic insufflator (EVA15; Palliare, Inc, Oceanside, Calif, USA) during fundoplication instead of the traditional needle decompression technique. Initially, the robotic insufflator was used through the scope, but this was unsuitable because of a flow limitation imposed by the scope channel, restricting insufflation within a space as large as the peritoneal cavity. As a result, we shifted to the use of a Veress needle, which has far less flow restriction, in conjunction with the robotic insufflator connected through the anterior abdominal wall (Fig. 2). This approach provided consistent pressure maintenance during the fundoplication procedure (Video 1, available online at www.igiejournal.org). An insufflation pressure of 12 to 14 mm Hg was found to be optimal, maintaining a peak airway pressure of 20 to 21 cm H2O. This system also allowed for quicker deflation, particularly during endoloop tightening.

Discussion

The traditional method for regulating capnoperitoneum involves needle decompression, wherein a Veress needle is inserted through the anterior abdominal wall.4 However, this technique is associated with frequent fluctuation in IAP, necessitating continuous monitoring and optimization of peak inspiratory pressure.5 We introduced a novel method using a robotic insufflator, a pressure control system that precisely administers and maintains targeted pressure within the peritoneum. The robotic system measures IAP in the CO2 delivery tube and automatically adjusts intracavity pressure by insufflating additional flow or active evacuation in response to fluctuations. The benefits include consistent pressure maintenance and the elimination of frequent decompression needle adjustments. These effects mitigate tension capnoperitoneum, a concern during CO2 insufflation that can compromise cardiovascular and respiratory functions. Using the robotic insufflator connected to a Veress needle through the abdominal wall provided a reliable method to maintain stable IAP throughout the endoscopic fundoplication.

In conclusion, using a robotic insufflator alongside a peritoneal decompression needle enhanced intraperitoneal visibility, maintained a consistent IAP, eliminated the need for manual adjustment of the decompression needle, allowed for quicker deflation, and reduced the risk of tension capnoperitoneum. Beyond POEM-F, robotic insufflators can be beneficial in other advanced endoscopic procedures, including full-thickness resection and natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery.
