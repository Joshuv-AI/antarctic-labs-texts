Multicomponent Ganoderma lucidum spore-based solid self-nanoemulsifying system of paclitaxel for improved lymphatic absorption and immune synergistic antitumor strategy
Authors: Wenyou Fang, Mingchao Xu, Huiru Li, Lujun Wang, Wenjie Lu, Xia Liu, Shengqi Chen, Song Gao, Qing Zhang, Rongfeng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn colorectal cancer, the cancer-immunity cycle is often disrupted by the lack of effective tumor-associated antigens, impaired activation of antigen-presenting cells, and insufficient T cell infiltration, leading to tumor immune escape and disease progression. Therefore, we developed PTX-GLSO SEDDS@GLS (PGS@GLS), a multicomponent solid self-emulsifying drug delivery system (SEDDS) incorporating paclitaxel (PTX) and Ganoderma lucidum spore oil (GLSO), with processed Ganoderma lucidum spores (GLS) as the solid carrier. The intestinal lymphatic absorption of PTX-GLSO SEDDS was significantly enhanced after GLS solidification, facilitating the induction of immune responses. Additionally, the oil phase of GLSO enhanced PTX solubility through self-emulsification into nanoemulsion. In tumors and the mesenteric lymphatic system, PGS@GLS induced robust immunogenic cell death and triggered the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, thereby initiating the cancer-immunity cycle. Furthermore, GLSO and GLS synergistically promoted dendritic cell maturation and cytotoxic T cell activation, thereby potentiating anti-tumor immune responses. Tumor cells were killed by the accumulated cytotoxic T cells, and the resulting dead tumor cells released additional tumor-associated antigens, which were subsequently presented by dendritic cells, creating a positive feedback loop to amplify the cancer-immunity cycle. Notably, PGS@GLS maintained potent antitumor efficacy while mitigated PTX-related systemic toxicity, representing a promising oral chemoimmunotherapy platform that restores antitumor immunity through lymphatic transport-mediated cancer-immunity cycle reinforcement.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04177-9.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Ganoderma lucidum spores, Ganoderma lucidum spore oil, Intestinal lymphatic transport, Cancer-immunity cycle

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignancy globally and is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. In recent years, the concept of the Cancer-Immunity Cycle (CIC) has emerged as a pivotal framework for understanding the body’s immune responses to tumors. The CIC delineates a continuous process that begins with the release of tumor antigens, followed by their capture and presentation by antigen-presenting cells, the activation and infiltration of T lymphocytes, and their exertion of cytotoxic effects on tumor cells [2, 3]. However, CRC cells often impede the antigen capture and presentation capabilities of dendritic cells (DCs) due to insufficient antigen release or weak immunogenicity, thereby obstructing the initiation of the CIC [4–6]. Furthermore, the maturation and function of DCs within the tumor microenvironment are frequently suppressed in CRC, leading to inadequate activation and infiltration of T lymphocytes [7, 8]. These factors collectively hinder the continuous initiation and progression of the CIC, ultimately resulting in tumor immune escape and disease progression. Consequently, addressing these challenges from multiple angles is crucial for enhancing the sustained and effective functioning of the immune response in CRC chemoimmunotherapy.

Paclitaxel (PTX), a widely used chemotherapeutic agent, exerts its anti-cancer effects by stabilizing microtubule structures and inhibiting cell mitosis [9, 10]. Recent studies have revealed that PTX can also induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby enhancing the antigen-presenting capabilities of DCs and activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which in turn initiates the CIC [11–13]. Despite its therapeutic potential, the clinical application of PTX is hampered by several limitations, including low solubility leading to poor bioavailability, severe allergic reactions caused by polyoxyethylene castor oil in traditional formulations, and myelosuppression resulting in neutropenia and thrombocytopenia [14, 15]. These challenges restrict the efficient use of PTX in cancer treatment, particularly in CRC.

Self-emulsifying drug delivery systems (SEDDS) represent an advanced approach that significantly improves the solubility and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs [16, 17]. Notably, by selecting an appropriate oil phase, oral SEDDS can considerably enhance the intestinal lymphatic absorption of hydrophilic compounds [18, 19]. Additionally, the abundant lymphatic tissues in the intestine, including lymphatic vessels and lymph nodes, serve as both primary pathways for tumor cell dissemination and key sites for the generation, transport, and anti-tumor immune functions of immune cells [20, 21]. Therefore, drugs that are transported through the intestinal lymphatic system can exert anti-tumor effects through both direct cytotoxic action and modulation of the immune microenvironment [22, 23]. However, oral SEDDS formulations face practical challenges such as poor storage and oral stability [24, 25]. These issues impede their effective delivery to intestinal lymph tissues and, consequently, limit their therapeutic efficacy in CRC.

Ganoderma lucidum spores (GLS), the reproductive cells of the edible fungus Ganoderma lucidum, exhibit a uniform oval shape with consistent dimensions of approximately 5 μm by 8 μm [26]. As a widely recognized and accepted health supplement, GLS demonstrate a high level of safety. Natural GLS possess a large cavity, and their outer surface features a robust, double-layered wall structure rich in Ganoderma β-glucan, known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [27, 28]. The PAMPs can be selectively recognized and specifically bound by the dectin-1 receptor expressed on the surface of immune cells, thereby stimulating adaptive immune responses [29, 30]. Through specific pretreatments, the spore wall can be modified to expose a rich microporous structure enriched with Ganoderma β-glucan. This modification renders the spore an ideal solid carrier for liquid SEDDS, thereby enhancing the stability and uniform distribution of SEDDS within the intestinal environment. Moreover, to further enhance the efficacy of PTX in CRC treatment, selecting an appropriate oil phase that not only promotes the lymphatic absorption of SEDDS but also exerts therapeutic adjuvant effects is crucial [31, 32]. Ganoderma lucidum spore oil (GLSO), a pale yellow, clear, and transparent oil extracted from the spores of Ganoderma lucidum, is rich in unsaturated fatty acids, sterols, and triterpenoids. GLSO exhibits pharmacological activities such as immune regulation [33], anti-tumor [34], and has demonstrated the ability to mitigate the side effects of chemotherapy [35]. However, similar to other oil-based natural products and active ingredients, the poor water solubility of GLSO is a significant obstacle to its further development and application in the fields of health and wellness.

Based on the preceding analysis, we propose the development of a mutilcomponent solid SEDDS formulation, PTX-GLSO SEDDS@GLS (PGS@GLS). This formulation incorporates PTX as the chemotherapy drug and combines it with GLSO as the oil phase to create PTX-GLSO SEDDS (PGS). Subsequently, PGS is encapsulated with processed GLS as the solid carrier to facilitate intestinal lymphatic delivery. The encapsulation with GLS protects PGS from immediate emulsification and enhances its distribution within the intestinal environment, thereby facilitating its transport and absorption via the intestinal lymphatic pathway. Once emulsified, PGS enhances PTX’s ability to induce ICD, which in turn promotes the maturation of DCs and the infiltration of effector T cells. This cascade of events stimulates a potent anti-tumor immune response, ultimately leading to the effective inhibition of further CRC development and metastasis (Scheme 1). Moreover, the PGS@GLS formulation has demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy compared to commercial paclitaxel injections and is associated with reduced systemic toxicity. Thus, our study introduces an innovative multicomponent solid-SEDDS approach to inhibit colorectal cancer progression by promoting the cancer-immunity cycle via the intestinal lymphatic pathway.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ganoderma lucidum spore oil (GLSO) and Ganoderma lucidum spores (GLS) were obtained from Yunle Lingzhi (Xuancheng, China), Paclitaxel (PTX) was purchased from Fujian South Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Sanming, China), Kolliphor® RH 40 (RH40) was provided by BASF (China) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lauroglycol 90 (L90) was provided by Gattefosse (China) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). FITC labeled PTX (FITC-PTX) was purchased from Qiyue Biology (Q-0264778, Xi’an, China). D-Luciferin potassium salt and DiR’(DiIC18(7)) were purchased from Aladdin Scientific Corp. (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). HMGB1 rabbit pAb, CRT rabbit pAb, goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L were purchased from ZEN Bioscience (Chengdu, China). ATP assay kit, mouse TNF-α kit and mouse IFN-γ ELISA kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). ELISA kit was purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology (Wuhan, China).

Preparation of PTX-GLSO self-nanoemulsion (PGS)

First, based on extensive preliminary experiments, we selected RH40 as the emulsifier and L90 as the co-emulsifier. Then, by drawing pseudo-ternary phase diagrams, we determined the proportion range of each component in the self-microemulsion formulation. Finally, we used a simplex lattice method to conduct a set of response surface design experiments with 3 factors, ultimately determining the optimal formulation. For the preparation of PGS: First, weigh the formulated amounts of GLSO, RH40, and L90 in sequence, and stir at room temperature until a uniform and transparent oily liquid is formed. Then, add the prescribed amount of PTX and continue stirring in the dark until it is completely dissolved to obtain the product. FITC-PGS was prepared using the same method except that PTX was replaced with FITC-labelled PTX.

Preparation of processed GLS

In order to make GLS capable of encapsulation, phosphoric acid and potassium hydroxide were chosen to pre-treat GLS. First, GLS are dispersed in an acetone solution and stirred under reflux at 65 °C for 15 h, then filtered and air-dried. Next, the defatted GLS are dispersed in a 12% KOH solution and stirred under reflux in a water bath at 80 °C for 2 h. The precipitate is collected by centrifugation and washed three times with distilled water at 60 °C. Subsequently, the KOH-treated GLS are dispersed in 85% phosphoric acid and stirred under reflux in a water bath at 40 °C for 2 h, followed by centrifugation to collect the precipitate. Finally, the KOH and acid-treated GLS are washed with hot water until nearly neutral, rinsed with anhydrous ethanol, and dried. The effects of KOH (concentration, temperature, duration) and phosphoric acid (temperature, duration) on GLS morphology and drug loading capacity were evaluated to optimize the method. In summary, raw GLS underwent sequential defatting, KOH reflux, phosphoric acid reflux, water/ethanol washing, and drying to obtain the final processed GLS microcapsules.

Preparation of PGS@GLS

PGS and processed GLS were weighed in a mass ratio of 5:1, mixed uniformly by vortex, and then placed in a vacuum drying chamber for reduced pressure loading for 4 h. Afterward, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation, then the precipitate was rinsed with ethanol, and finally dried to obtain PGS@GLS.

Size, zeta potential, and morphology of PGS

The emulsion size and zeta potential of PGS emulsion were determined using a Nanoparticle Size and Zeta Potential Analyzer (ZS90, Malvern, UK). Following emulsification, the droplets were placed on a copper grid and negatively stained with phosphotungstic acid before further observation of their morphology and particle size using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan).

The stability of PGS

The stability of PGS was preliminarily evaluated by examining its state at different temperatures and the emulsion size in various solutions.

Morphological and structural characterization of processed GLS

Morphological characterization of processed GLS was performed using TEM and SEM, respectively. The pore structure characteristics of GLS were evaluated by measuring N₂ adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K using a surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics, ASAP 2460, USA). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum of processed GLS in the 500–4000 cm− 1 range were recorded using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Encapsulation characteristic and drug loading of PGS

To ascertain the encapsulation of PGS by GLS, PGS was stained with Nile Red fluorescent dye and observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (LSM900, ZEISS, Germany). The content of PTX in the PGS@GLS formulation was calculated using the following equation: CPTX = mPTX / m0, where m0 denotes the total mass of PGS@GLS, and mPTX represents the total amount of PTX in the PGS@GLS. Specifically, a known mass (m0) of PGS@GLS was dispersed in anhydrous ethanol and subjected to ultrasonication for a series of three cycles, each 2 min in duration. The ethanol solution containing PTX was then filtered through a microporous membrane, and the filtrate (supernatant) was analyzed by HPLC to determine the total amount of PTX (mPTX) in the PGS@GLS.

In vitro release characteristics of PGS@GLS

The in vitro release characteristics of PGS@GLS were evaluated and compared with those of PTX@GLS using the dialysis bag method. PTX@GLS was prepared by dissolving PTX in anhydrous ethanol and then mixing the solution with processed GLS at the same PTX-to-GLS mass ratio as that used in PGS@GLS. The mixture was homogenized by vortexing and was then followed by vacuum drying for 4 h. For the release study, each formulation was sequentially immersed in hydrochloric acid solution at pH 1.2 (0–2 h), phosphate buffer at pH 6.8 (3–5 h), and phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 (6–24 h). The cumulative release profiles were then plotted based on these experiments.

In vitro cytotoxicity and cellular uptake studies

CT26.WT cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10%(v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% carbon dioxide. The CCK-8 assay was employed to assess the cytotoxic effects of free PTX, PGS, and PTX-free GLSO self-microemulsion (GLSO SEDDS, GS) on CT26 cells. Briefly, 100 µL of CT-26 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 per well and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the original medium was removed and replaced with drug-containing medium of varying concentrations for co-incubation with the CT26 cells for an additional 24 h. The concentrations of free PTX and PGS were equivalent to PTX concentrations ranging from 0.005 to 0.5 µg/mL and GLSO concentrations from 0.625 to 62.5 µg/mL, respectively. Free PTX was initially solubilized with a minimal amount of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and subsequently diluted to the desired concentration with blank medium. PGS was emulsified with a small amount of deionized water and then stepwise diluted to the appropriate concentration with blank medium. GS was prepared and diluted in a ratio identical to that of PGS. For cellular uptake studies, CT26 cells were incubated with FITC-labeled PGS (at a PTX concentration of 0.5 µg/mL) for 2 h. The cellular uptake was quantified by flow cytometry, and intracellular fluorescence was visualized by CLSM. To investigate the internalization mechanism, cellular uptake was assessed under energy-depleted conditions (4 °C incubation); furthermore, the effects of specific endocytosis inhibitors (chlorpromazine, methyl-β-cyclodextrin (M-β-CD), amiloride, and cytochalasin D) were evaluated.

In vitro ICD induced by PGS@GLS

The expression of calreticulin (CRT), high mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), was evaluated at the cellular level to determine the induction of ICD by PGS. CT26 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in a 96-well cell imaging plate and cultured until they adhered well. Then, blank medium, free PTX, and PGS (final PTX concentration 0.125 µg/mL, diluted with blank medium) were added and incubated for 6 h. After incubation, supernatants were harvested from parallel sets of cell cultures. ATP levels were determined with an ATP assay kit, and secreted HMGB1 was quantified using an ELISA kit. The exposure levels of CRT and HMGB1 within the cells were examined using immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, the cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then washed twice with TBST buffer. Subsequently, a blocking solution containing 5% BSA in TBST was added and incubated for 30 min (for HMGB1 detection, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min before blocking). After that, corresponding primary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 2 h, followed by two washes with TBST. Then, secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Finally, DAPI was applied for 10 min, and after washing with TBST, the cells were observed using CLSM.

Biodistribution studies

To investigate the in vivo distribution of PGS@GLS following oral administration, mice were gavaged with free DiR, DiR-labeled PGS, and DiR-labeled PGS@GLS (at a dose equivalent to 50 µg/kg DiR). DiR-labeled PGS was prepared analogously to the unlabeled PGS by adding a DiR stock solution in anhydrous ethanol to the pre-concentrate, followed by continuous stirring until ethanol evaporation. This DiR-labeled PGS was then used to prepare DiR-labeled PGS@GLS following the same solidification method for PGS@GLS. Subsequent fluorescence imaging and quantitative analyses were conducted on major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and mesenteric lymph nodes at various time points post-administration.

In vivo intestinal lymphatic transport

To elucidate the intestinal lymphatic absorption of PGS after oral administration, mice were gavaged with FITC-labelled PGS or FITC-labelled PGS@GLS. Intestinal tissues were harvested at 2 h and 6 h post-administration, and frozen sections were prepared. These sections were incubated with a rabbit polyclonal antibody against LYVE-1 (GB113499, Servicebio), followed by a Cy5-coupled secondary antibody (GB27303, Servicebio) to label lymphatic vessels. The co-localization of drugs with lymphatic vessels was subsequently assessed using CLSM to determine the relationship between the administered drugs and intestinal tissue or lymphatic vessels.

In vivo anti-tumor efficacy

CT26-Luc cells were purchased from Cellverse Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). CT26luc cells are stably transfected with the luc gene that luminesces in vivo in reaction with D-Luciferin. 7- to 8-week-old male Balb/c mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).

The CT26-luc cells in the proliferative phase were prepared as a single-cell suspension and centrifuged to collect the precipitate. The collected cells were then thoroughly mixed with matrix gel on ice. Subsequently, approximately 2 × 107 CT26-luc cells were injected into the cecum wall of anaesthetized mice using a 30G needle.

Semi-quantitative analysis of bioluminescence intensity in the abdomen of mice after cell inoculation was performed using a living imaging system (IVIS spectrum, Perkinelmer, USA) to monitor tumor development. When the bioluminescence intensity reaches (0.5-1) × 106 p/sec/cm2/sr, the mice were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 5) the treatment process started. The control group were not treated, and the PTX (i.v.) group was injected via tail vein with commercial paclitaxel injection (Taxol®) at a dose of 25 mg/kg on day 1 and day 6, respectively. In the GLS, GS@GLS, PGS and PGS@GLS groups, the corresponding preparations were administered daily by gavage for 5 days, and the treatment was repeated for 2 cycles, with an interval of 5 days between the two dosing periods. Then, from the first day of administration, IVIS was performed every 5 days to monitor the in vivo bioluminescence of the mice and to determine the in vivo proliferation of CT26 cells. The mice were weighed every 2 days during the 21-day experimental period and a body condition score (BCS score) was used to make a comprehensive judgement on the state of the mice.

Assessment of in vivo ICD induction

After obtaining the tumor tissue sections on day 21, microwave heating was first used for antigen retrieval. For the assessment of HMGB1, sections were subjected to permeabilization using 1% Triton X-100 for 30 min, succeeded by a 30-minute incubation with 5% BSA to mitigate non-specific binding. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with the primary HMGB1 antibody overnight at 4 °C in the dark. This was followed by a 60-minute incubation with a Cy5-conjugated fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature under light-protected conditions. Ultimately, DAPI staining was applied, and the sections were incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark prior to examination by CLSM. Other immunofluorescence staining in this section were performed similarly, except that permeabilization with 1% Triton X-100 was omitted before incubation with 5% BSA.

Flow cytometry

Freshly collected tumor tissues and mesenteric lymph node samples were minced using scissors and then treated with collagenase IV solution. Following a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C, the samples were filtered through a 200-mesh cell sieve to obtain single-cell suspensions. Erythrocyte lysing solution was added, and after centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed with PBS and blocked by incubating with 1% BSA for 15 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, different fluorescein-conjugated antibody solutions were added and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Finally, the samples were resuspended in PBS within flow cytometry tubes and analyzed using flow cytometry. The levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in tumor tissue were measured by ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histological staining

The tumor tissue was stripped from the intestines, weighed, and subsequently placed in paraformaldehyde for fixation. Paraffin embedding and tissue sectioning were then performed, and finally the tissue sections were then subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, Ki67 immunofluorescence staining, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) immunofluorescence staining, respectively, and further analysed under the microscope.

Safety evaluation

After 21 days of treatment, venous blood was collected from the mice. The blood routine was determined using an automatic hematology analyzer (BC-2800Vet, Mindray, China) and liver and kidney function indices in their serum were determined using a biochemical analyzer (Vetube 30, Mindray, China). The mice were then euthanized, and the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys from each group were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis by HE staining.

Statistical analysis

All results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between treatment groups were evaluated with unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. The p-value ˂ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of PGS@GLS

The preparation process of PGS@GLS was depicted in Fig. 1. As GLSO has not been previously reported for its application in self-emulsifying drug delivery systems, a comprehensive screening and optimization of the formulation has been undertaken. First, to achieve the best emulsification and drug-loading effects, we pre-screened and optimized the formulation composition of PGS. As shown in Fig. S1, when the mass ratio of RH40 to L90 is 1:1, the self-microemulsion region is the largest, with GLSO, RH40, and L90 proportions ranging from 10% to 40%, 27%-72%, and 14%-60%, respectively. Subsequently, a simplex lattice design with three factors was conducted (Table S1) [36]. We fitted the obtained data with a polynomial model (Fig. S2, Table S2), aiming for the smallest emulsification droplets size and the largest drug-loading amount, thereby calculating the optimal formulation composition of the self-nanoemulsifying system. Dynamic light scattering results showed that the emulsion size of PGS was 26.16 ± 0.73 nm (Fig. 2A), presenting as spherical droplets (Fig. 2B), with a zeta potential of -2.75 ± 0.74 mV. The prepared PGS was a stable, clear, and transparent oily liquid at room and high temperatures, which could gradually transition to a translucent semi-solid upon cooling and could be restored upon heating (Fig. S3). The PGS could rapidly emulsify into uniformly sized nanoemulsions in buffer media of different pH, with no significant change in particle size (Table S3).

Acid-alkali treatment can remove lignin, cellulose, protein and other substances from the interior of the spores, retaining the basic cystic deconstruction of the spores and increasing the surface pores, increasing the specific surface area, and rendering the spores naturally porous microcapsules for subsequent loading of drugs [37, 38]. During the process screening stage, we used methylene blue as a model drug [39], and screened the pretreatment process of GLS based on the final spore yield and drug adsorption efficiency (Fig. S4). Compared with the unprocessed GLS (Fig. 2C and E), the processed spores exhibit an open state of germination pores, a significant increase in surface pores, and a marked enhancement in spore wall permeability (Fig. 2D and F).

The N₂ adsorption-desorption isotherms of processed Ganoderma lucidum spore (GLS) microparticles were measured at 77 K using a surface area and porosity analyzer, as shown in Fig. S5. The isotherms exhibited Type I/IV characteristics, typical of mesoporous materials. At lower relative pressures (P/P₀), the curve rose with increasing pressure, indicating monolayer adsorption. As pressure continued to increase, multilayer adsorption characteristics appeared. Further pressure elevation led to increased adsorption capacity due to capillary condensation. When P/P₀ approached 1, the rapid rise in adsorption quantity suggested possible macropore adsorption. Notably, the processed GLS microparticles showed a gradual adsorption increase at P/P₀=0.4–0.8 with a distinct H4-type hysteresis loop, reflecting the presence of narrow mesoporous slits. Pore size distribution analysis revealed an average pore diameter of 10.9 nm. Combined with the calculated BET specific surface area (5.3 m²/g), these results classify the material as a “low-surface-area mesoporous material”. Combined with TEM/SEM observations revealing densely distributed “short and straight” pore channels on the surface, these structural characteristics provide the fundamental basis for its drug adsorption and release properties.

The FT-IR spectrum of GLS carriers is showed in Fig. 2G. The broad and intense absorption peak at 3430 cm− 1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of O-H bonds, which was the general characteristic absorption peak of polysaccharide. Peaks at 2923 cm− 1 was caused by stretching vibrations of the C-H bond of the methyl (-CH3) or hypomethyl (-CH2) group in the polysaccharide. The absorptions peaks at 1634 cm− 1 possibly due to vibrational uptake of bound water in polysaccharides. Peaks at 1076 cm− 1 and 1155 cm− 1 could assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of the D-pyranose ring, while 1076 cm− 1 could be attributed to the C-O stretching vibration of polysaccharide C-O-C and C-O-H bonds. The peak at 895 cm− 1 was a characteristic absorption of β-type glycosidic bonds, which due to a C-H variant vibration of differential isomerism at the β-terminus of the pyran ring [40].

In the prescription of PGS, the amount of PTX and GLSO was in a fixed ratio of 1:125 (w/w). To verify the effective loading of PGS by GLS, we employed Nile Red, a lipophilic red fluorescent dye, to label PGS. We then prepared the PGS@GLS complex and examined it using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Given that plant spores naturally emit green fluorescence, the fluorescence colocalization results (Fig. 2H) visually confirmed the successful loading of PGS onto GLS. Subsequently, we chose PTX as the index ingredient to evaluate the drug loading and in vitro release characteristics of PGS@GLS. It was determined that the PTX content within PGS@GLS was 10.91 mg/g. The in vitro release experiments provided further evidence that PTX is continuously released from PGS@GLS. Notably, the cumulative release reached 59.3% at 5 h, surpassed 80% at 8 h, and achieved 91.4% by 12 h, indicating near-complete release. In contrast, PTX@GLS showed incomplete release, with a cumulative release of only 46.4% at 12 h (Fig. 2I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the processed GLS not only efficiently encapsulates PGS but also facilitates the emulsification and release of the encapsulated PGS, providing a fundamental basis for the therapeutic efficacy of PGS@GLS.

Cellular uptake and ICD induction by PGS in vitro

We carried out a viability experiment to investigate the cytotoxic effects of free PTX, PGS and GS on CT-26 cell. After 24 h of treatment, both free PTX and PGS exhibited concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, whereas GS showed minimal effects (Fig. 3A). At a PTX concentration of 0.25 µg/mL, the average cell viabilities were 57.8% for free PTX and 22.5% for PGS, indicating a significant difference between the two formulations. This difference became even more pronounced at 0.5 µg/mL, with viabilities dropping to 40.0% for free PTX and 2.2% for PGS. Interestingly, GS displayed a biphasic effect. At very low concentrations (PTX equivalent < 0.125 µg/mL; GLSO < 15.625 µg/mL), GS slightly promoted CT26 cell proliferation. However, at a higher concentration (PTX equivalent 0.5 µg/mL; GLSO 62.5 µg/mL), it began to inhibit cell growth, an effect potentially attributable to the increased GLSO concentration. Notably, a non-monotonic trend was observed for free PTX, where cell viability at 0.25 and 0.125 µg/mL was slightly higher than at adjacent lower concentrations. While this phenomenon warrants further investigation, it may be tentatively explained by adaptive cellular responses such as microtubule dynamic remodeling, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, or nuclear envelope alterations in CT26 cells [41–43].

We further investigated the cellular uptake of PGS by CT26 cells using confocal microscopy. Compared to the faint pericellular fluorescence observed with free FITC-PTX (Fig. 3B and Fig. S6A), cells treated with FITC-PGS displayed intense and uniform intracellular fluorescence (Fig. 3B), with a mean fluorescence intensity that was 7.8 times higher than that of FITC-PTX (Fig. 3C). The enhanced uptake can be attributed to the formulation’s properties. Upon aqueous emulsification, PGS forms oil-in-water nano-droplets, which drastically improve PTX solubility and cellular affinity [44]. Moreover, the GLSO core of these droplets may facilitate uptake by serving as a source of fatty acids, which are in high demand by tumor cells for membrane synthesis [45]. Mechanistically, the internalization of FITC-PGS was found to be an energy-dependent process involving multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin- and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and actin-dependent processes (Fig. S6B). In summary, the significantly increased cellular uptake of PTX delivered via PGS, mediated by its favorable nano-emulsion properties and multi-pathway endocytosis, likely underlies its enhanced cytotoxic efficacy against CT26 cells.

PTX is a broad-spectrum chemotherapeutic drug that can induce ICD [11, 12]. When tumor cells undergo immunogenic cell death (ICD), they release a series of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), mainly including: (i) calreticulin (CRT), which translocates from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cell membrane; (ii) high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), secreted from the nucleus; and (iii) ATP, which is extruded into the extracellular space [46, 47]. These signaling molecules, when exposed on the cell surface, emit an “eat me” signal, which can promote the recruitment and maturation of dendritic cells and the activation of T cells, thereby activating the cancer-immunity cycle [48]. Next, we investigated the effect of PGS in inducing ICD in CT26 cells. We treated CT26 cells with free PTX and PGS, respectively. The results showed that the content of ATP in the supernatant of the cells in the PGS group was significantly increased compared with free PTX (Fig. 3D), as well as the exposure of CRT near the cell membrane in the PGS group (Fig. 3C and G). Regarding HMGB1, the amount of HMGB1 in the nucleus of PGS-treated cells was significantly reduced compared to the control group and free PTX group (Fig. 3D and H), while it was significantly increased in the culture medium (Fig. 3I), which indicated a marked increase in HMGB1 release after PGS treatment. Together, these results suggest that PGS can induce significant immunogenic cell death in CT26 cells.

In vivo biodistribution and lymphatic transport of PGS@GLS

The fluorescent distribution of a formulation in various tissues over time following oral administration can vividly reflect its absorption pathway. Lymphatic fluid in the intestinal tract is a unidirectional flow that originates from the intestinal lymphatic capillaries located in the basal layer of the intestinal wall. These capillaries progressively converge as they pass through the mesentery, eventually forming the main intestinal lymphatic vessel. From there, the intestinal lymphatic vessel continues its course through the cisterna chyli before it joins the thoracic duct (thoracic lymphatic vessel). Ultimately, the lymphatic fluid within the thoracic duct drains into the bloodstream through the left subclavian vein [49]. Therefore, drugs absorbed via the intestinal lymphatic system first accumulate in the central lymphatic chain of the mesentery (mesenteric lymph nodes, MLNs) along the mesenteric lymphatic capillaries before entering the systemic circulation, and subsequently flow through the heart and lungs prior to distribution throughout the body [20, 50]. As depicted in Fig. 4A and C, the PGS and PGS@GLS groups reached peak fluorescence intensity at 2 h and 6 h, respectively, and were significantly higher than the free DiR group. Regarding the fluorescence distribution in major organs, compared to the formulation groups, free DiR group showed significant distribution in the liver and kidneys after oral administration, with an accumulation that increased over time. At 24 h post-administration, the fluorescence intensity of free DiR in the liver (Fig. 4B, Fig.S7B) and kidneys (Fig. S7D) reached its peak and was significantly higher than the other two groups. In contrast to the free DiR group, both PGS and PGS@GLS showed significantly higher fluorescence in the MLNs. Their pulmonary distribution profiles further revealed distinct patterns: the PGS group reached its peak in the lungs at 2 h, whereas PGS@GLS peaked at 6 h (Fig. 4D)—a temporal sequence that mirrored their respective fluorescence changes in the MLNs. The notable lung accumulation observed for both formulations aligns with the established physiological pathway, wherein the lungs serve as the first capillary bed filtering lymph-derived chylomicrons that enter systemic circulation via the thoracic duct [51]. The delayed fluorescence distribution in the lymph nodes of the PGS@GLS group compared to the PGS group may be attributed to the relative slow release of PGS during the first 2 h caused by the encapsulation with GLS (Fig. 2H).

To further validate the intestinal lymphatic absorption of PGS@GLS after oral administration, we observed the co-localization of drug fluorescence with the intestinal wall’s basal lymphatic capillaries in PGS@GLS and PGS groups 6 h post-administration. Figure 4E illustrates the anatomy of the intestinal cross-section and the approximate positional relationship between the intestinal wall’s basal capillaries, lymphatic capillaries, and intestinal villi. The immunofluorescence images of intestinal cross-sections (Fig. 4G) show that at 2 h post-gavage, there are numerous green fluorescent spots near the villi and crypts close to the Peyer’s patches, indicating that GLS loaded with PGS has reached the intestine after oral administration. At 6 h post-gavage, the PGS@GLS group showed distinct green fluorescence of the drug within the lymphatic capillaries, which can be attributed to the PTX release and lymphatic transport (Fig. 4H). In contrast, the PGS group exhibited faint fluorescence at 6 h post-gavage, indicating the difference in release between PGS and PGS@GLS (Fig. S8). The encapsulation of PGS by GLS facilitates the transition of SEDDS from a liquid to a solid state, thereby helping to protect PGS from premature emulsification and release in the stomach. This also enhances the dispersion and emulsification properties of PGS in the intestine, thereby facilitating the intestinal lymphatic absorption and transport of PTX. These outcomes are consistent with the previous organ distribution results, further confirming the characteristic of intestinal lymphatic absorption for PGS@GLS after oral administration.

PGS@GLS inhibited the orthotopic colorectal cancer and prolonged survival

The therapeutic efficacy of PGS@GLS was assessed using an orthotopic colorectal cancer model in mice. Following the sequential treatment (Fig. 5A), there was a marked inhibition of tumor growth observed in the PGS@GLS group (Fig. 5B). At day 21, the tumors of mice in the control group were significantly enlarged, and the average fluorescence intensity was 126.7 times higher than that at day 1, whereas the growth of tumors of mice in the PTX intravenous injection (PTX i.v.) group, the GLS and the GS@GLS groups were marginally inhibited, and the average fluorescence intensities were 44.3, 37.4, and 32.6 times higher than that at day 1, respectively. In contrast, the rate of fluorescence enhancement in the PGS and PGS@GLS groups was significantly lower than that of the other groups, with the average fluorescence intensity being 17.0 and 11.9 times that of day 1, respectively (Fig. 5C). It’s worth noting that the GLS group showed slight inhibition of tumor growth, which may be attributed to the active ingredient, Ganoderma β-glucan, in GLS. This ingredient can be selectively recognized and specifically bound by the dectin-1 receptor expressed on the surface of immune cells, thereby stimulating adaptive immune responses. This, in turn, exerts immuno-activating effects and augments therapeutic efficacy. In addition, compared with both the GS@GLS group and the PTX i.v. group, the PGS@GLS group showed a much stronger tumor inhibition (Fig. 5B, C), demonstrating that the incorporation of GLSO with PTX into GLS-encapsulated self-microemulsions effectively enhances the therapeutic effect.

Furthermore, the change in body weight of the mice indicated that the control group showed a slight trend of body weight gain, which may be attributed to rapid tumor development, leading to severe ascites (Fig. 5D). In contrast, mice in the PTX i.v. group exhibited a notable weight loss trend around day 9, suggesting a toxic effect. The body weights of mice in the other groups remained stable, combined with the results of body condition scores (Fig. S9), indicating no significant toxicity associated with the formulations.

On day 21, we dissected and collected the intestinal tumor tissues from each group of mice and performed H&E staining and immunofluorescence staining to observe the internal structure of the tumor tissues and the changes of various indexes, respectively. As depicted in Fig. 6A, Fig. S10 and Fig. S11, the number and volume of tumors in the intestines and colon of mice in the PGS and PGS@GLS groups were markedly reduced compared to those in the control and PTX i.v. groups. The H&E staining results showed that the tumor tissues of mice in the PGS@GLS treatment group were looser and had lower cell density than those in other groups (Fig. 6B), and the results of TUNEL staining and Ki67 staining showed that the PGS@GLS group presented the strongest apoptosis and the weakest cell proliferation signals (Fig. 6B).

In addition, another set of parallel animal experiments was conducted to examine the effect of the different preparations on the survival of mice. The results indicated that at day 60, 10% of the mice in the PGS group and 20% in the PGS@GLS group remained alive. The median survival time for the PGS@GLS group was 38 days, whereas the control group had a median survival time of 32 days, and the PTX i.v. group had 25 days (Fig. 5E). Collectively, the therapeutic efficacy results demonstrated that PGS@GLS effectively inhibited tumor progression while simultaneously improving survival.

PGS@GLS induced ICD and promotes immune cell infiltration in tumors

To assess the in vivo induction of ICD by PGS@GLS, we examined the exposure of HMGB1 and CRT in tumor tissues across different groups using immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6C). The outcomes aligned with those from in vitro cellular experiments. Notably, the levels of HMGB1 in tumor tissues were markedly lower in both the PGS and PGS@GLS groups than in the control group. Conversely, the levels of CRT were substantially higher in the PGS and PGS@GLS groups compared to the control group. Additionally, there were considerable differences in both HMGB1 and CRT levels between the PGS@GLS group and the PTX i.v. group, demonstrating that the incorporation of GLSO with PTX in PGS@GLS induced a greater degree of ICD than commercial PTX injection.

The release of the above signaling molecules in ICD stimulates the maturation of dendritic cells and enhances their ability to recognize and present tumor antigens effectively. Afterwards, the mature dendritic cells efficiently present tumor antigens to naïve T cells, which are subsequently activated and differentiate into effector T cells, thereby exerting an antitumor immune response [3]. Furthermore, we assessed the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into tumor tissues across various groups. CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells are the two main T cell subsets in the immune system and play an important role in anti-tumor immunity. Among them, CD8+ T cells, also known as cytotoxic T cells, can recognize antigens on the surface of tumor cells and differentiate into effector cells with cytotoxic functions, thereby inducing tumor cell apoptosis; CD4+ T cells, known as helper T cells, regulate the scale and duration; CD4+ and CD8+ T cells work synergistically to exert anti-tumor immunity [52–54]. As depicted in Fig. 6D and Fig. S12, the PGS@GLS group exhibited the most intense fluorescence signals for CD8+ T cells, while there was no significant alteration observed in the CD4+ T cell population (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these data indicate that PGS@GLS is capable of inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells and enhancing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into tumor tissues, thereby impeding tumor cell proliferation and inhibiting tumor growth.

PGS@GLS stimulates potent immune responses and boost the cancer-immunity cycle

Based on the promising in vivo therapeutic efficacy results, flow cytometry experiments were conducted to further examine the in vivo infiltration levels of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and mature dendritic cells in tumors and MLNs after drug administration. As shown in Fig. 7E, the proportion of CD8+ T cells in the GS@GLS group, PGS group and PGS@GLS group were significantly increased compared with the control group and PTX i.v. group. Among them, the proportion of CD8+ T cells in the PGS@GLS group reached 6.3 times that of the intravenous group. Mature dendritic cells (mDCs) possess a robust antigen-presenting capability, enabling them to migrate from peripheral tissues to lymph nodes. They efficiently internalize, process, and present antigens to T cells, thereby activating potent T cell responses and playing a pivotal role in initiating and modulating anti-tumor immunity [55]. As shown in Fig. 7G, the proportions of mDCs in the tumor tissues of mice in both the PGS group and the PGS@GLS group after drug administration were significantly higher than those in the control group, which were 2.0-fold and 3.3-fold higher than those in the PTX i.v. group, respectively.

The aforementioned experiments have confirmed that the MLNs are essential for the lymphatic absorption of PGS@GLS following administration (Fig. 4). At the same time, since MLNs act as a key component of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), serve as a crucial site for antigen presentation and T-cell maturation, and play a significant role in promoting the cancer-immunity cycle [56], we proceeded to further investigate the infiltration of T cells and mature dendritic cells (mDCs) within the MLNs. The results showed that the overall trend of CD8+ T cells and mDCs in MLNs was similar to that in tumor tissues, and the proportion of CD8+ T cells in the PGS@GLS group was 10.9-fold and 6.4-fold higher than that of the control group and PTX intravenous group, respectively (Fig. 7F). The data of mDCs in the PGS@GLS group, on the other hand, reached 4.2-fold and 3.3-fold of the control and PTX i.v. groups, respectively (Fig. 7H). During the process of cancer-immunity cycle, a cascade of interconnected events unfolds continuously in a cyclical manner, comprising key steps of tumor antigen release, antigen presentation, T-cell activation, T-cell migration towards the tumor, T-cell infiltration of the tumor, T-cell recognition of tumor cells, and killing of tumor cells (Scheme 1). The results presented above indicate that PGS@GLS elicited substantial ICD, which in turn promoted the maturation of dendritic cells within MLNs and enhanced the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into tumors. These effects collectively contributed to the enhancement of the cancer-immunity cycle, impacting it from multiple angles.

Furthermore, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) levels in tumor tissues were detected. TNF-α is a pivotal immunomodulatory cytokine that plays a significant role in the activation of dendritic cells. It facilitates the maturation of plasmacytoid dendritic cells by upregulating co-stimulatory molecules and chemokine receptors, such as CD80 and CD86 [57]. IFN-γ, a hallmark cytokine produced by Th1 cells and CD8+ T cells, participates in antiviral, antitumor, and immune regulatory functions. Effector CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment can also produce IFN-γ, thereby enhancing their cytotoxic capabilities to eliminate tumor cells [58]. As anticipated, significantly elevated levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ were observed in the PGS@GLS group compared to both the control and PTX i.v. groups (Fig. 7I and J). Overall, these results elucidate the mechanism of PGS@GLS in the tumor immune microenvironment and the cancer-immune cycle.

The successful translation of these immunomodulatory effects in vivo stems from the complementary roles of the formulation components, a strategy explicitly delineated by our tiered experimental approach. The superior intracellular delivery and potent ICD induction established for PGS in vitro directly underpin its in vivo efficacy within the PGS@GLS system. Critically, the GLS carrier is indispensable in vivo for enabling oral administration, directing lymphatic transport, and contributing immunomodulatory properties, thereby realizing the full therapeutic potential of the integrated system.

Safety assessment of PGS@GLS

The H&E staining results of major organs in mice after administration of different formulations revealed that, compared with the control group, there were no significant changes in the tissues of various organs in the PGS@GLS group mice (Fig. S13). Noteworthy, the blood analysis results are depicted in Fig. S14. The alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total bilirubin (TBIL) levels in the PTX i.v. group were significantly elevated compared with the control group, hemoglobin (HGB) and the red blood cell (RBC) count showed a markedly decrease, while these parameters in the PGS@GLS group exhibited no apparent abnormalities. In addition, other parameters for the PGS@GLS group remained within the normal range. These results suggest that PGS@GLS has a favorable safety profile.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed PGS@GLS, a novel solid self-nanoemulsion formulation of paclitaxel, incorporating GLSO as the oil phase and processed GLS as the solid carrier. This formulation was designed to address the limitations of conventional chemotherapeutic agents in reaching the mesenteric lymph nodes and to enhance the immune response against colorectal cancer. Upon administration, PGS@GLS effectively induced ICD and enhanced the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and mDCs into the tumor and mesenteric lymphoid tissues. This, in turn, stimulated potent anti-tumor immune responses and reinforced key stages of the CIC. Comparatively, PGS@GLS demonstrated improved antitumor efficacy over commercial paclitaxel injections, along with a prolongation of survival time and a reduction in systemic toxicity in a murine orthotopic colorectal cancer model. Our findings suggest that PGS@GLS could offer a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of colorectal cancer by leveraging the oral lymphatic transport pathway to elicit a potent anti-tumor immune response.
