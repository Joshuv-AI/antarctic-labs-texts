Brain functional changes of suicidal ideation and behaviours: a meta-analysis of task-based fMRI studies
Authors: Viktor Enshuo Xu, Kay Ke Fang, Robin Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicidal ideation and behaviour are major global mental health issues and leading causes for mortality. While existing evidence indicates brain functional deficits of suicidal thoughts and behaviours, the neural underpinnings of these deficits remain unclear, possibly due to various sources of methodological heterogeneities.MethodsWe conducted a pre-registered meta-analysis including 22 task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging studies that compared suicidal individuals (N = 459, 63.83% females) with non-suicidal psychiatric controls (N = 681, 52.72% females). Separate analyses were conducted in the emotion and non-emotion (cognitive and risky decision making) domains. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses addressed methodological heterogeneities including patient diagnosis, suicidal characteristics (ideation vs. behaviours) and task type.ResultsIn emotion tasks, general suicidality (pooling across ideation and behaviours) and suicidal behaviours were both associated with hyperactivities in the left sensorimotor cortices and hippocampus/parahippocampus, alongside hypoactivity in the bilateral visual cortices. However, only the hypoactivity among visual areas was consistently replicated in all subgroup analyses. In non-emotion tasks, general suicidality and suicidal behaviours were both associated with hyperactivity in the left precentral gyrus and the right middle temporal gyrus, which were consistently replicated in all subgroup analyses. Meta-regression analyses further identified larger suicidality-associated functional changes in frontotemporal regions in studies with higher female proportions, and in hippocampal and temporal regions in studies with older participants.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that the blunted perceptual emotion processing and heightened motor system reactivity are key neural features of suicidality, which provide potential neural targets for interventions on suicidal individuals.

Introduction

Suicidal ideation and behaviour represent a major public health concern, responsible for about 700,000 deaths annually and ranked among the leading causes of mortality [1]. Given the high lethality of suicidal behaviour, it is critical to identify objective neural markers that can be the target of intervention. Along the spectrum of general suicidality, suicidal ideation and behaviour are distinct but related phenomenon which show high rates of co-occurrence and overlapping trait features such as impulsivity [2, 3]. Although suicidal ideation and behaviours are associated with multiple major psychiatric conditions, they are not specifically linked to a particular illness or symptom [4], nor could they be reliably assessed using self-report tools [5]. Emerging research indicates that suicidal ideation and behaviours are linked to brain alternations across multiple illnesses [6], and they are each associated with distinct changes to brain morphometry and resting-state functional networks [7]. Since suicidality is underpinned by dysfunctional cognitive and emotion processes [8, 9], it is likely to involve changes in brain functions while participants are engaging in these processes. However, there is currently no consensus on the pattern of brain functional changes during key emotion and cognitive processes among suicidal individuals.

Suicidal ideation and behaviours are considered to involve heightened negative emotional states such as self-despair and hopelessness, which are under insufficient top-down regulation from high-level cognitive control systems [4, 7, 10]. Specifically, individuals with suicidal ideations or past suicidal attempts have frequently shown elevated tendencies to perceive negative facial emotions such as anger and sadness, which are accompanied by heightened activities in the sensorimotor areas, the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [11–13]. Increased self-related negative affective processing across social and non-social contexts has also been observed in suicidal attempters, which was associated with altered activities in the precuneus, ACC, and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) [14]. On the cognitive side, existing task-based fMRI evidence indicates that suicidal behaviours are associated with impairments in high-level executive functions such as inhibition control and planning [15, 16], alongside reduced activities in the right ACC and increased activity in the middle frontal and precentral gyri, which may contribute to deficient top-down regulation of negative emotion processes associated with both suicidal ideation and behaviour [16–18]. The impaired emotion regulation and cognitive control functions may collectively result in risky and maladaptive decision making that ultimately leads to suicidal attempts [19]. These findings collectively support that neural activity patterns during negative emotion processing, cognitive control and risky decision-making functions are potential brain markers of suicidality.

Notwithstanding, considerable heterogeneities exist in the past task-based neuroimaging studies on suicidal ideation and behaviour. For example, while increased OFC activity has been reported for individuals with past suicidal attempts during risky decision-making tasks [20], other studies reported the opposite finding, or reduced OFC activity among suicidal attempters when making risky choices compared to non-attempters [11]. Also, increased hippocampal activation has been reported in high-impulsive suicide attempters during emotion memory or autobiographical recall [21], but others failed to detect hippocampal alterations during negative emotional processing [22]. Such finding discrepancies are likely due to considerable methodological differences across studies. First, individuals with suicidal ideations and those who had suicidal attempts may show different neural activity patterns in key frontoparietal and frontostriatal circuitries [7]. Thus, it is essential to distinguish between those who had attempted suicide from those with suicidal thoughts only. Second, while a considerable proportion of past suicidality research has involved patients with major depressive disorder (MDD), other studies included patients with other psychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [17, 23, 24]. It remains unclear whether suicidality comorbid with different psychiatric conditions is underpinned by similar or distinct neural functional processes. Third, while suicidal behaviour has been linked to elevated self-related negative emotion processing [11, 12], it is unclear whether it also involves changes in social emotion processing. Given processing social emotions involves distinct neural systems compared to processing emotion about oneself [25], this might result in discrepancies in study findings. Lastly, suicidal ideation and behaviour have been found to depend on demographic factors such as age and sex [26, 27], which contribute to additional heterogeneity across studies.

While three meta-analyses on suicidality and task-based functional neuroimaging have been published [27–29], they suffer from the following limitations. This meta-analysis also included individuals with suicidal behaviour only. The first meta-analysis did not address the important methodological factors of patient diagnosis and emotion task type, therefore their results cannot inform on the effects of these important sources of heterogeneity [27]. The second meta-analysis only included MDD patients, so their findings cannot generalise to suicidality with other comorbidities [28]. The third meta-analysis did not include studies which employed emotion paradigms, thus its findings cannot inform on the neural underpinnings of emotional processing among suicidal individuals [29]. These limitations underscore the need for a methodologically rigorous meta-analysis that considers the key factors of heterogeneity from suicidal characteristics, patient diagnosis and task type.

Therefore, we conducted an updated task-based fMRI meta-analysis using voxel-wise signed differential mapping (SDM) to characterise neural activation and deactivation patterns associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour. To ensure that the neural correlates of suicidality are delineated from those of psychiatric diagnosis, we only included studies which compared within matching clinical or nonclinical groups. Methodological heterogeneity factors will be examined through subgroup and meta-regression analyses. The analyses were conducted separately for emotion and non-emotional (executive control and risky decision-making) tasks, based on the widely adopted framework that (partly) separates the limbic, sensory and ventral frontal systems engaged during emotion paradigms, and the dorsal, lateral frontal cortex and temporal systems engaged by cognitive and decision-making tasks [28, 30]. The overarching goal was to identify task-evoked neural signatures of suicidal ideation and behaviour that may generalise beyond specific diagnoses and task types. Based on the extant literature, we hypothesised that suicidal ideation and behaviour would be associated with altered activities in the frontal, limbic and sensorimotor systems during negative emotion processing, and in the prefrontal and motor systems during cognitive control and risky decision-making processes. We also hypothesized that suicidality-related brain activity would depend on key methodological factors including patient diagnosis, suicidal attempt (versus thought), and task type.

Methods and materials

This review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration No: CRD420251174951). The PRISMA checklist can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic literature review across four databases: PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and Web of Science. The initial search was completed on December 5, 2025, with the following keywords: (suicid* OR death*) AND (“functional magnetic resonance imaging” OR fMRI OR “task-based fMRI” OR “task-related fMRI”) on Title and Abstract. A backward citation search was performed for relevant meta-analyses [27, 28]; however, no additional articles met the inclusion criteria or provided non-duplicative data beyond those already included.

Following the search, we selected studies according to predefined criteria. The inclusion criteria were studies: (1) Involving participants with clearly defined suicidal ideation or behaviour; (2) Reporting a between-group comparison regarding suicidal ideation or behaviour within clinical or community samples; (3) Employing task-based fMRI and reporting whole-brain activation coordinates in either MNI or Talairach space. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Not original research (e.g., clinical protocols, trial registrations, review articles or conference abstracts); (2) Not reporting any between-group comparisons relevant to suicidal ideation or behaviour; (3) Only reporting region-of-interest (ROI) analyses; (4) Full texts not available; (5) Published in a non-English language. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

The initial search yielded 2694 records, of which 1715 studies remained after removing duplicates (979 studies removed). The study selection procedure adhered to PRISMA guidelines (see Fig. 1). Two reviewers (E.X. and Z.S.) independently screened the titles and abstracts in the first stage. A total of 1666 studies meeting the exclusion criteria were removed. The remaining 49 studies underwent full-text assessment, of which 27 were further excluded. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. A third rater (K.F.) was consulted to resolve any disagreements. In total, 22 papers published between 2010 and 2025 were retained for data extraction.Fig. 1The PRISMA flow chart for screening.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was conducted independently by two authors (E.X. and R.S.). From each eligible study, the following information was systematically extracted: author(s), year of publication, sample size, participant demographics (mean age, sex ratio), clinical diagnosis, symptom and suicidality scores (e.g., Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), Scale for Suicide Ideation (SSI)), fMRI task paradigm, statistical threshold, and key findings including peak coordinates of significant between-group effects.

We utilised the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for Cross-Sectional Studies (NOS-xs) optimised for cross-sectional studies to assess the quality of the included studies. The NOS-xs validates three domains: the adequacy and representativeness of sample selection; the measurement validity of exposure and outcome; the identification, measurement, and adjustment of confounds [31]. The detailed quality ratings are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Task fMRI meta-analysis method

We employed the Seed-based d Mapping (SDM) method to conduct the task-based fMRI meta-analysis. SDM is a robust and validated approach for neuroimaging meta-analysis [32]. The SDM approach enables the synthesis of both significant and nonsignificant fMRI results. By preserving the directionality of effects (i.e., increased vs. decreased neural activity), SDM mitigates biases due to preferential reporting of positive results and enables the differentiation of hyperactivation and hypoactivation in suicidal individuals relative to controls [33].

Task type

In the current study, we examined two major domains of task contrasts: emotion-related and non-emotion tasks. Emotion-related tasks included (1) non-social emotion processing and regulation tasks such as emotion prospective thinking task and negative emotion appraisal task and (2) social emotion tasks such as the emotion face task and the Cyberball task. Non-emotion tasks were defined as paradigms without explicit affective elicitation, comprising (1) executive control tasks including cognitive inhibitory control, interference and working memory tasks (e.g., Go/NoGo, Reading Span, Tower of London), and (2) risk-based decision-making tasks (e.g., Balloon Analogue Risk Task; Iowa Gambling Task). Main voxel-based meta-analysis

For the main meta-analysis, we pooled across studies including samples of suicidal ideation and behaviour to achieve the highest statistical power, based on previous evidence that these phenomena are associated with overlapping neural functional alterations [6, 34, 35]. Notwithstanding, given suicidal ideation and behaviours may also involve distinct neural functional processes, we also conducted subgroup analysis on studies including suicidal attempters (see below).

We performed voxel-based fMRI meta-analyses employing SDM’s standard pipeline. To ensure cross-study comparability, contrast estimates were realigned before analysis so that positive SDM effect sizes consistently represented greater task-evoked activation in the suicidal group relative to controls. For each contrast of interest, we obtained whole-brain significant peak coordinates or reported null results together with their associated t/z values and p values from the included studies. SDM reconstructs study-specific effect size maps by integrating both positive and negative task-related neural activation differences associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour. These study-specific maps were combined using a random-effects model to compute the weighted mean, variance and meta-analytic effect-size maps.

Statistical significance of the study-average map was assessed using SDM’s default 20-mm FWHM anisotropic Gaussian kernel, an uncorrected voxel-level p < 0.005, a peak-height threshold of |SDM-Z| >1, and a cluster-extent threshold of 10 voxels [32, 33]. Map significance was determined via 50-time permutation testing, consistent with developers’ recommendations, which suggested that 50 times is sufficient to obtain a stable null distribution [32]. For each permutation, a null distribution was generated by randomly sampling an effect size from each study at every voxel, against which the observed statistical map was compared to identify voxels exceeding the threshold. This approach has been widely adopted in previous meta-analytic studies and represents a balance between sensitivity and specificity within the SDM framework [36].

Reliability analysis

To evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled effect size, and to assess the overall robustness of the meta-analysis, we performed a whole-brain Jackknife sensitivity analysis [33]. The SDM iterates through each study and removes it from the meta-analysis and the effect-size maps. This procedure identifies brain regions that remained consistent across all leave-one-out replications, thereby demonstrating result reliability.

Subgroup meta-analysis

To account for potential heterogeneity, we performed subgroup meta-analyses for each contrast of interest. Because most studies in both emotion-related (11/15) and non-emotion-related (7/9) included suicidal attempts, subgroup analyses were conducted for all studies utilising this definition. We also examined depressive diagnosis as a subgroup factor, given that more than half of the studies in each domain involved depressive samples (9/15 and 6/9, respectively).

To address variability in experimental paradigms, we conducted additional subgroup analyses for studies employing social-emotional paradigms (8/15) in emotion-related contrasts. For non-emotion contrasts, we performed subgroup analyses on tasks targeted at executive control versus risk-based decision-making tasks (5/9).

Meta-regression analysis

To evaluate whether methodological and clinical moderators accounted for variability in task-evoked group difference, we conducted meta-regression analysis to assess the modulating effects of mean study age and female ratio for both emotion and non-emotion contrasts.

Overlapping analysis

To characterise the convergence of task-evoked neural abnormalities associated with suicidality across heterogeneous paradigms, we performed an overlapping analysis to examine any common brain activities that showed suicidality-related activities across emotion-related and non-emotion-related contrasts.

Software and toolboxes

We conducted the meta-analysis using the AES-SDM toolbox (v5.15, https://www.sdmproject.com/). Anatomical labelling of significant clusters was performed with the xjView toolbox (v10.0; https://www.alivelearn.net/xjview) implemented in MATLAB R2022a (MathWorks). Analyses of demographic variables and correlation analyses were carried out in R (v4.4.1). All neuroimaging figures were generated using MRIcron.

Result

Study and sample characteristics

A total of 22 studies were included, comprising 15 contrasts derived from emotion tasks and 9 contrasts from non-emotion tasks. All studies included samples with suicidal ideations and/or behaviours (total N = 1140, 652 females, Mean Age = 28.91 years). Among these, 21 studies included comparison between clinically diagnosed samples with suicidal ideation or behaviour (N = 445, 283 females) against non-suicidal clinical controls (N = 649, 340 females), and one study included nonclinical samples with (N = 14, 10 females) or without (N = 32, 19 females) suicidal ideation. Detailed study characteristics are summarised in Table 1 and Supplementary Information. The study’s quality assessment scores range from 6 to 9 (out of 9), with a mean score of 7.41, suggesting medium-to-high study quality (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Among the 15 emotion contrasts (N = 895, 564 females, Mean Age=29.44), 9 studies included patients with diagnosed major depressive disorder, and 11 studies included patients with previous suicide behaviour. Eight studies used tasks that assessed social emotions, such as facial emotion processing and social exclusion tasks. All the emotion tasks involved negative emotion stimuli, although 2 studies involved both negative and positive stimuli.

Among the 9 non-emotion contrasts (N = 412, 205 females, Mean Age=27.54), 6 studies included only patients with diagnosed depressive disorder, and 7 studies included patients with previous suicide behaviour. Five reported results based on executive control functions (e.g., no-go versus go), while 4 reported results based on risk decision making (e.g., risk vs. non-risky decisions).

Meta-analysis of suicidality (both ideation and behaviour): Emotion Tasks

Main Meta-analysis

Six clusters demonstrated significantly greater activities in individuals with suicidal ideation and/or behaviour (S+) compared with non-suicidal controls (S-) (Table 2). Relative to the S- group, the S+ group showed heightened neural activities in the right precentral gyrus, the right orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and the right cerebellum (the vermis and lobule III), along with the left precentral and postcentral gyri, the left hippocampus and parahippocampal gyri and the left precuneus. In contrast, the S+ group showed reduced activities in the bilateral visual cortices and in the right rostral anterior cingulate cortex (rACC) (Table 2 and Fig. 2). Jack-knife sensitivity analysis

The increased activities in the right precentral gyrus among the S+ group remained significant in all Jack-knife iterations (15/15), while the increased activities in the right OFC cluster associated with suicidality were significant in 10/15 iterations. All other clusters remained significant in 14/15 iterations. The full Jack-knife results are summarised in Supplementary Table S3.

Subgroup meta-analyses: depression diagnosis

The subgroup analysis restricted to patients with a current depressive disorder diagnosis (N = 9) partly replicated the main analysis. The S+ group showed reduced activity than the S- group in the left visual cortex cluster (42.99% of 670 voxels in the main analysis), as well as in the right fusiform gyrus cluster (100% of 100 voxels). However, none of the increased activities in the S+ group were replicated, nor were the decreased suicidality-related activities in the right rACC.

Subgroup meta-analyses: suicide behaviour

The subgroup analysis of studies including suicide attempters (N = 11) partly replicated our main analysis. The S+ group showed greater activities in the left precentral and postcentral gyrus cluster (100% of 325 voxels) and in the left hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus cluster (58.54% of 41 voxels). On the other hand, the S+ group showed lower activities in the left visual cortex (44.78% of 670 voxels) and the right fusiform gyrus (100% of 100 voxels). However, the suicidality-related activity increases in the right OFC, right cerebellum, right precentral gyrus and the left precuneus, and activity decrease in the right rACC, were not replicated. This analysis also generated a new region in the midcingulate cortex (peak at 0, −24, 42, BA24) that showed increased activity in the S+ versus S- group.

Subgroup meta-analyses: socio-emotion tasks

Studies that employed socio-emotion tasks (N = 8) partly replicated the results of the main analysis. The S+ group showed increased activities in the left precentral and postcentral gyrus cluster (100% of 325 voxels) and in the left hippocampal and parahippocampal gyrus cluster (19.51% of 41 voxels), as well as decreased activities in the left visual cortex (49.25% of 670 voxels) and the right fusiform gyrus (100% of 100 voxels). However, the suicidality-related activity increases in the right OFC, right cerebellum, right precentral gyrus and the left precuneus, and activity decrease in the right rACC, were not replicated. Full results are presented in Supplementary Table S7.

Meta-regression analyses

Meta-regression was performed on the effect size using between-study female ratio and mean age as moderators. Higher female ratio was positively associated with larger between-group effects in the bilateral superior frontal gyrus, the right middle frontal gyrus, and the right superior temporal gyrus. Negative associations with female ratio were observed in the bilateral precentral gyrus and the left postcentral gyrus, the left superior temporal gyrus (STG), bilateral precuneus, and the right cerebellar lobule III.

Older samples showed positive associations between-group effects in the bilateral hippocampus and parahippocampal regions. Negative associations with mean age were observed in the left SFG, right MFG and the right middle cingulate cortex (MCC). Full results are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Meta-analysis of suicidality (both ideation and behaviour): Non-Emotion Tasks

Main meta-analysis

In non-emotional task contrasts, the S+ group exhibited increased neural activity in the left precentral gyrus and the right middle temporal gyrus. No significant clusters were detected for the S+ <S- contrast. Detailed results are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 2.

Jackknife sensitivity analysis

The association between suicidality and increased activity in the right middle temporal gyrus and the left precentral gyrus identified in the main analysis remained stable across 8/9 leave-one-out iterations (Supplementary Table S4).

Subgroup meta-analyses

In subgroup analyses, the S+ >S− effects identified in the main analysis were consistently replicated. Specifically, the results in the left precentral gyrus and the right middle temporal gyrus clusters were replicated in the subgroup of studies including only depressed patients (N = 6; precentral gyrus: 100% of 58 voxels; middle temporal gyrus: 100% of 188 voxels), patients with suicide attempt (N = 7; precentral gyrus: 100% of 58 voxels; middle temporal gyrus: 100% of 188 voxels), and studies using executive control tasks (N = 5; precentral gyrus: 100% of 58 voxels; middle temporal gyrus: 100% of 188 voxels). Detailed subgroup results are reported in the Supplementary Table S8.

Meta-regression analyses

In non-emotion task studies, a higher female ratio was positively associated with larger between-group effects in the right middle temporal gyrus. Negative associations with female ratio were observed in the right cerebellar lobule VI and the right calcarine cortex. For mean age, older samples showed larger between-group effects in the right middle temporal gyrus, and smaller effects in the right superior frontal gyrus. Full results are presented in Supplementary Table S6.

Overlapping analysis across emotion and non-emotion related contrasts

The SDM overlapping analysis did not yield statistically significant clusters. A qualitative examination of the spatial distribution of effects revealed similar anatomical regions across emotion-related and non-emotion-related contrasts. Specifically, both analyses demonstrated involvement of the left precentral gyrus, albeit at different subregions (Table 2).

Supplementary analysis

Supplementary results on the comparison between non-suicidal individuals with and without psychiatric diagnoses are included in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Results). No overlapping region was identified between the activity increases and decreases associated with psychiatric diagnoses, and those associated with suicidality.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we found that compared to non-suicidal individuals, those with suicidal ideation and/or behaviour showed altered neural activities during emotional and non-emotional processes, within the frontoparietal system, the limbic system, the occipital areas and cerebellum. During negative emotion processing, general suicidality was associated with increased activities in the right prefrontal cortex including the right OFC and the right cerebellum, along with the left precentral and postcentral gyri, precuneus and the hippocampal/parahippocampal complex. Conversely, suicidality was related to reduced activities in the bilateral occipital cortices and the right rACC. During executive control and risky decision-making processes, suicidal individuals showed heightened neural activities in the left precentral gyrus and the right middle temporal gyrus compared to non-suicidal individuals. Subgroup analyses on patient diagnosis, suicidal attempt and task type showed that the neural functional substrates of suicidality during emotion processes were only partially replicated, while those during non-emotion processes were fully replicated. These results suggest greater methodological-related heterogeneity in the emotional neural substrates but more stable non-emotional neural substrates of suicidality.

Emotion-related neural signals in general suicidality

We observed heightened functional engagement of various brain regions during negative emotion-processing tasks in relation to general suicidality. Suicidal individuals showed increased activities in the precentral gyrus compared to non-suicidal individuals, which indicates enhanced recruitment of the motor systems when faced with negative emotions, supporting the notion that suicidal ideation and behaviour are associated with an amplified readiness for action or escape from negative affective states [11, 37, 38]. In addition, the elevated hippocampal and parahippocampal activities in suicidal individuals suggest intensified processing of affective memories or contextual cues, which is consistent with past evidence that hippocampal engagement supports persistent rumination of negative experiences [39]. The increased activities in the precuneus indicate enhanced self-processing during negative emotional states and heightened internal focus on emotional pain [15]. In addition, the increased activities in the right OFC among suicidal individuals suggest negatively-biased emotion valuation processes under distress [12], while the observed hyperactivity in the right cerebellum may reflect greater efforts in affective regulation through cerebro-cerebellar networks [40]. On the other hand, suicidal individuals showed reduced activities in bilateral visual cortices during negative emotion processing, indicating diminished perceptual engagement and attentional avoidance of negative emotional stimuli [41]. This concurs with previously identified alterations in visual network functioning among depressed individuals with suicidal ideation or behaviour [42], which may represent a vulnerability neural marker of general suicidality. In addition, suicidal individuals exhibited reduced activity in the rACC, a region critically involved in integrating affective signals with top-down regulatory control [4]. Similar patterns have been observed across mood disorders characterised by difficulties in managing emotional conflict [43, 44]. However, we observed no anatomical overlap between neural processes associated with general suicidality and those associated with psychiatric diagnoses, indicating that the brain functional correlates revealed by our meta-analyses were specifically linked with suicidal ideation and/or behaviours, rather than to general psychiatric status.

Emotion-related neural signals in suicidal behaviour

Our findings further revealed that suicidality-related activity changes in the precentral and postcentral gyri, hippocampal and parahippocampal regions, and visual cortices were replicated within subgroups of studies involving suicide attempters. Previous research indicates that both suicidal ideation and suicidal attempts are associated with heightened processing of negative memories or contextual cues as well as rumination of past negative events [45, 46], which may result from increased hippocampal and parahippocampal activities [47]. Suicidality is also generally associated with heightened perceptual disengagement from negative emotional stimuli as underpinned by reduced activities in visual areas [48]. In addition, suicidal attempters are characterised by increased motor readiness when faced with negative emotional stimuli as reflected by increased activities in the premotor and motor circuitries, which could relate to their self-destructive behaviours [49]. Collectively, these results indicate that over-processing of negative memories, perceptual disengagement from negative stimuli, and increase of motor reactivity to negative stimuli might characterize both general suicidality and suicidal behaviours. These findings are also consistent with both the Integrated Motivational-Volitional model [50] and the Stress-Vulnerability model of Suicidal Behaviour [51], which views suicidal ideations as originated from dysregulated negative emotion processes that generated the intrinsic motivation of self-destruction, while elevated motor reactivity indicative of impaired behavioural control is key to transitioning from ideation to behaviour.

On the other hand, while we were not able to conduct meta-analysis on studies involving suicidal ideation samples, a qualitative examination on these limited findings may shed light on the key neural emotional processes differentiating between suicidal ideation and behaviour. In two studies which compared individuals with suicidal ideation with matched controls, suicidal ideation was associated with increased activities in widespread prefrontal regions including the right OFC and dorsal PFC during negative emotion regulation [37, 49]. The right OFC hyperactivity related to suicidal ideation is particularly noteworthy since this result was not replicated in suicidal attempters and thus indicates ideation-specific neural functional change. Concurring with this, one previous study reported positive resting-state functional projection from the caudate nucleus to the right OFC in depressed patients with suicidal ideation, but not in non-suicidal patients or patients with suicidal behaviour [7]. Given the critical role of the right OFC in both valuation-based decision making and action control [52], it is possible that increased functional recruitment of this region due to subcortical striatal inputs associated with positive emotional processing is important for suicidal ideators to stay away from committing suicidal behaviour; this speculation is also concordant with the Integrated Motivational-Volitional model that considers emotion and motivation deficits as the primary causes of suicidal ideation [50]. On the other hand, hyperactivity in a midcingulate region was specifically revealed among studies on suicidal behaviours. The midcingulate cortex is considered an integral part of a network engaged in processing and regulating negative information and pain, which is important for optimizing behaviours when faced with adversity [53]. The functional connectivity between the midcingulate cortex and the visual cortices has been found to be positively related to capability of suicidal behaviours and high tolerance to pain, indicating that enhanced regulation of physical pain might result in higher propensity to commit suicidal behaviours [54].

Suicidality-related emotion signals: clinical and task factors

Among studies that included only depressed patients, only the reduced activities in the visual cortices were replicated. Past research indicates that blunted visual and perceptual processing to emotional stimuli is a transdiagnostic feature of suicidality across diverse psychiatric comorbidities [21, 22], supporting the notion that attenuated emotional reactivity in the visual system represents a stable feature of suicidality rather than a diagnosis-specific effect [6]. On the contrary, altered functional activity in the motor and hippocampal networks was previously observed among suicidal attempts with high impulsivity [21], suggesting that these functional changes might specifically underpin suicidality comorbid with impulse-control conditions [24]. A further reason for the lack of replication among depressive patients could be due to the fewer available studies including non-depression comorbidities, which limits the power to detect the neural signature of suicidality across diagnoses. Notwithstanding, the present meta-analysis suggests the blunted visual activities during negative emotion processing are a transdiagnostic neural marker of suicidality. This result has clinical and translational implications. First, it might serve as a complementary neural marker for identifying suicidal individuals, particularly when combined with other neural modalities such as structural and functional connectivity [55]. Second, it could be the direct or indirect target for neuromodulation interventions. For example, transcranial direct current stimulation on the PFC may modulate early perceptual signals of emotional stimuli in the occipital cortex, thereby reducing suicidal behaviour [56].

Notably, suicidality-related activity changes in the right rACC, OFC, cerebellum and the left precuneus were not replicated in the depressed patient and socio-emotional task subgroups, which suggest that the association of these functional changes with suicidality depends on the multifaced interactions of clinical comorbidity and the type of negative emotion tasks. For example, the rACC and the right OFC are critically involved in top-down regulation of emotional conflict and emotion regulation [57, 58], but less so during relatively passive emotion exposure such as face emotion viewing. Since the socio-emotion tasks employed by the included studies did not incorporate an explicit regulation component, it remained unclear whether this was the reason for the non-replication. As part of the default mode network, the precuneus is heavily involved in self-referential and introspective emotion processing, and its engagement in emotion processing without specific reference to oneself may be less consistent [19]. Given the heterogeneity of the emotion tasks, the core affective process that is altered in suicidal ideation or behaviour remained uncertain. Moreover, the cerebellum has shown heightened activities in perceptual tasks among bipolar disorder patients with suicidal attempts [59], but not during emotion processing among depressed patients [22]. Given the OFC and the rACC are heavily implicated in inhibitory control functions and impulsivity [60, 61], it is possible that their functional abnormality is more closely tied to suicidality comorbid with impulse-control conditions rather than major depression.

Non-emotion-related neural signals in suicidality

Across non-emotional paradigms assessing executive control and risky decision-making, general suicidality was associated with increased activities in the left precentral gyrus and the right middle temporal gyrus. The heightened engagement of the left precentral gyrus suggests increased recruitment of motor systems during cognitively demanding tasks [62]. Heightened activity of the middle temporal gyrus, which is involved in semantic processing and associative knowledge retrieval, suggests that suicidal individuals may rely more heavily on these processes instead of effortful top-down control processes [63]. The shift from effortful top-down control towards less effortful semantic and associative processing may reflect a suboptimal strategy during cognitively demanding and risk-taking tasks.

Notably, suicidality-related hyperactivity in the left precentral gyrus and the right middle temporal gyrus were fully replicated across subgroups of depressed patients, suicide attempters, and tasks assessing executive control functions. These functional patterns also appeared to be specific to suicidal ideation and/or behaviours rather than to psychiatric diagnoses. These results indicate that over-reactivity of the motor system and reliance on semantic and associative processing in cognitively demanding tasks are stable neural signatures of both suicidal ideation and behaviour, which remained robust across task paradigms that measured “pure” executive control processes and those that assessed decision making under risk. On one hand, suicidal individuals show maladaptive evaluation of life circumstances that reflect both deficient executive control functions and altered valuation systems [11, 64]. Indeed, the hyperactivity of the middle temporal gyrus may indicate excessive retrieval of negative memories and biased semantic processing [65], which could be due to reduced top-down cognitive regulation and impaired holistic self-valuation [21]. On the other hand, elevated motor reactivity, or motor impulsivity, might be an important marker of both suicidal behaviours and suicidality in general, with the hyperactivity in the precentral gyrus being the neural underpinning [29]. Interestingly, suicidality-related hyperactivity in the right precentral gyrus during emotion tasks was not observed within the subgroups of depressed patients and suicidal attempters, contrasting with the replicability of the left precentral gyrus hyperactivity in suicidality during non-emotion tasks. The identified right precentral gyrus was more caudal and corresponds to a supplementary motor area (SMA) subdivision, whereas the left precentral gyrus was more rostral and corresponds to a pre-SMA subdivision showing greater involvement in cognitive and learning processes [66]. Our findings thereby indicate that impaired high-level cognitive process underpinned by hyperactivity in the left rostral precentral gyrus is a more stable neural signature of general suicidality, which is consistent with the Stress-Vulnerability model of Suicidal Behaviour [51] which posits that cognitive and behavioural control deficits are key underpinnings of the transition from suicidal ideation to behaviour. We also obtained some evidence that the left precentral gyrus tended to show suicidality-related hyperactivity across both emotional and non-emotional tasks, although statistical evidence for this pattern was not conclusive.

Modulation by sex and age

Across emotion and non-emotion tasks, studies with higher ratios of female participants showed greater suicidality-related activity increase in the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the right STG, but reduced suicidality-related activities in the bilateral sensorimotor cortices, precuneus, the left superior temporal gyrus (STG) and the right cerebellum. Past evidence indicates that the right STG is engaged in processing and regulating facial and bodily emotions [67], while the left STG is involved in semantic processing of emotions [68]. Thus, the sex differences suggest that suicidality in females is associated with altered cognitive control and nonverbal emotion processing, while suicidality in males is related to greater motor reactivity and self-relevant verbal emotion processing. Studies involving older participants showed greater suicidality-related activity increase in the temporal and hippocampal regions, but less activity in the bilateral prefrontal regions, which indicates that suicidality in older age may be more underpinned by over-retrieval of negative memories, while suicidality in young individuals may depend more on disrupted top-down emotion regulation functions [69].

Limitations

Several limitations of this study need to be considered. First, only a few studies included suicidal individuals comorbid with clinical conditions other than major depression, which limits the power to detect transdiagnostic neural signature of suicidality. Future studies on suicidality with diverse clinical comorbidities are needed to elucidate this issue. Second, almost all the emotion paradigms focused on processing of negative emotions, with a critical lack of evidence on positive emotion functions. Given the key role of positive emotions in suicidality [70], future studies should employ tasks that assess reward processing, behavioural motivation and positive emotion regulation processes. Third, we were unable to conduct separate meta-analyses on suicidal ideation and behaviour, due to few task-based fMRI studies conducted on suicidal ideation samples. Future studies directly comparing individuals with suicidal ideation and behaviour are needed for identifying key neural processes determining the transition from ideation to behaviour. Lastly, the heterogeneity in task paradigms, specifically in the emotion domain, resulted in compromised replicability of the main findings. Future research should employ emotion paradigms that are comparable in key dimensions such as processing versus regulation and social versus non-social.

Conclusions

By considering various methodological heterogeneities in existing task-based fMRI studies among suicidal individuals, our meta-analysis findings indicate that reduced visual reactivity to negative emotional stimuli is a stable neural marker of general suicidality across patient diagnosis, suicidal characteristics and type of emotion processing. Suicidality-related hyperactivity in different subregions of the left precentral gyrus was also found during both emotional and non-emotional cognitive and decision-making tasks. These findings may provide the neural targets for intervention on suicidal individuals.
