Finite‐Element Comparison of a Proximal Femoral Laterally Bionic Intramedullary Nail (PFLBN) Versus PFNA and PFBN for AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 Intertrochanteric Fractures
Authors: Chen Xiong, Lijia Zhang, Xiaofeng Chen, Xiaomeng Zhang, Yichong Zhang, Huijuan Fu, Zhentao Ding, Yanhua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe proximal femoral laterally bionic intramedullary nail (PFLBN) is a novel cephalomedullary fixation system incorporating an additional lateral wall screw. This study aimed to compare the stress distribution and displacement characteristics of PFLBN with those of proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA) and proximal femoral bionic nail (PFBN) in AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric fractures using finite element analysis.MethodFinite element models of an AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric femoral fracture were constructed based on computed tomography data from a healthy adult femur. Three fixation constructs (PFNA, PFBN, and PFLBN) were simulated under axial loading representing single‐leg stance equivalent to three times body weight (2100 N). Von Mises stress distribution and displacement patterns of the femur and implants were evaluated.ResultIn all models, peak implant stress was concentrated at the junction between the main nail and the cephalocervical component. Compared with PFNA, the PFLBN model demonstrated a 17.2% reduction in peak implant stress and a 23.2% reduction in peak femoral stress. Maximum femoral and implant displacements in the PFLBN model were reduced by 19.5% and 19.2%, respectively. Notably, the PFLBN exhibited the highest construct stiffness and effectively mitigated stress concentration on the medial femoral cortex through the load‐redistribution mechanism of the lateral wall screw. While the PFBN showed improved metrics over the PFNA, the PFLBN provided superior mechanical stability specifically for the simulated lateral wall deficiency.ConclusionUnder axial single‐leg stance loading, PFLBN exhibited lower peak stresses and smaller displacements than PFNA and PFBN in this finite element model of AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 fractures. These computational findings describe differences in stress redistribution and construct behavior and warrant further experimental and clinical validation.Keywords: intramedullary nail, intertrochanteric fracture, finite element analysis, femoral lateral wall, stress distribution

Introduction

According to the 2018 AO/OTA classification [1], intertrochanteric femoral fractures are categorized into three types: 31‐A1, A2, and A3. Among them, unstable type A3 fractures account for approximately 15.93% of cases and are increasingly encountered in clinical practice [2, 3], partly due to intraoperative disruption of the lateral femoral wall during treatment of A2 fractures [4, 5]. Because of lateral wall involvement, AO/OTA 31‐A3 fractures represent the most unstable fracture configuration and remain challenging to manage [6]. Intramedullary fixation is widely regarded as the preferred treatment for unstable intertrochanteric fractures owing to its biomechanical advantages over extramedullary devices [7, 8, 9]. Nevertheless, clinical studies have reported notable rates of implant‐related complications, varus deformity, and postoperative femoral neck shortening following intramedullary fixation of A3 fractures, particularly with commonly used devices such as the proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA) [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. To improve fixation stability in complex fractures, the proximal femoral bionic nail (PFBN) [18, 19, 20] was introduced as an alternative solution. Biomechanically, the PFBN is characterized by a triangular support configuration; compared with traditional single screw or blade constructs, this design significantly enhances overall construct stiffness and fixation stability [21, 22]. However, limited clinical studies with small sample sizes have reported complication rates comparable to those of PFNA and InterTAN [23, 24]. Its clinical application in unstable AO/OTA 31‐A3 fractures has revealed specific limitations: while the PFBN improves mechanical stiffness, it does not explicitly reconstruct the lateral femoral wall buttress. Consequently, in cases with severe lateral wall deficiency, residual instability and the risk of varus collapse may persist, underscoring the need for a fixation design specifically targeted at lateral wall reconstruction.

Physiologically, load transmission in the proximal femur is characterized by medial compressive stress and lateral tensile stress. In A3 type fractures, disruption of the lateral wall compromises tensile stress conduction and lateral buttress support, leading to altered stress distribution and instability under axial loading. Biomechanical considerations suggest that lateral wall deficiency may alter stress distribution and displacement patterns in the proximal femur following fixation [25, 26, 27]. To address the mechanical implications of lateral wall deficiency in fracture patterns with partially preserved medial support, the proximal femoral laterally bionic intramedullary nail (PFLBN) [28] was developed. Based on the PFNA design, PFLBN (Figure 1) incorporates an additional laterally oriented screw intended to provide structural support analogous to the lateral femoral wall and to modify load transfer within the fixation construct.

The aim of this study was to compare the stress distribution and displacement characteristics of AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric femoral fractures fixed with PFLBN, PFNA, and PFBN using finite element analysis under identical axial loading conditions. Specifically, this study addressed the following three scientific objectives: (i) to quantitatively compare the magnitude of von Mises stress and displacement among the three fixation constructs to determine the relative stress‐reduction efficacy; (ii) to evaluate differences in construct stiffness and displacement patterns under axial compression to assess the mechanical stability provided by each implant configuration; and (iii) to analyze the stress transmission patterns within the proximal femur to elucidate the biomechanical suitability of these implants for the lateral‐wall‐deficient AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 fracture subtype.

Materials and Methods

Finite Element Model Construction

A left femur from a healthy adult male with no history of fracture, bone disease, tumor, bone defect, or prior femoral surgery was selected for this study. High‐resolution thin‐slice computed tomography (CT) scanning was performed to obtain Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) data, which were imported into Mimics 21.0 (Materialize, Leuven, Belgium). A femoral mask was generated using the CT bone segmentation algorithm, followed by manual editing and solidification to produce a stereolithography (STL) file. The STL model was subsequently imported into 3‐matic 13.0 (Materialize, Leuven, Belgium) for surface smoothing, spike removal, and mesh optimization to establish an initial three‐dimensional femoral model (Figure 2). The processed geometry was then transferred to Geomagic Studio 13.0 (3D Systems, USA), where surface reconstruction was performed to generate a solid model in standard tessellation parameter (STP) format, including both cortical and cancellous bone structures. An AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric fracture pattern was simulated according to the classification definition, and an anatomically reduced fracture configuration was assumed to represent an ideal postoperative condition.

Femoral solid model reconstructed from computed tomography (CT) data, showing the solid model generated from the DICOM mask (A) and the preliminary three‐dimensional mesh model established in 3‐matic (B).

Implant Modeling and Assembly

Based on the geometric parameters of the standard proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA) obtained via laser scanning, proximal femoral bionic nail (PFBN) and proximal femoral laterally bionic intramedullary nail (PFLBN) models were constructed using UG 10.0 (Siemens PLM Software, Germany) with identical nail lengths and diameters, as shown in Figure 3. The evaluated implants represent distinct design philosophies: the PFBN adopts a “triangular support” structure based on the Gamma nail design, incorporating an additional transverse screw that passes through both the main nail and the compression screw to distribute stress via the three screws and enhance compressive and rotational stability, whereas the PFLBN was developed based on “bionic reconstruction” theories to explicitly target lateral femoral wall deficiency. Structurally, the PFLBN (Figure 4) modifies the standard PFNA configuration by incorporating a laterally oriented screw (lateral wall screw) positioned orthogonally (90°) to the compression screw (helical blade); this non‐locking component passes through a dedicated aperture in the main nail to anchor in the distal medial femoral cortex, mechanically functioning as a bionic lateral buttress to redistribute loads and resist varus collapse. The PFLBN main nail retains proximal and distal lateral offsets of 5° and a 130° angle, consistent with femoral anatomy, and all implants were positioned within the femoral model following standard surgical alignment principles to ensure consistency.

Material Properties and Contact Definitions

All materials were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. Cortical bone, cancellous bone, and titanium alloy implants were assigned elastic moduli and Poisson's ratios based on values commonly reported in the literature. Cortical bone, cancellous bone, and titanium alloy were defined with elastic moduli of 17,000, 445, and 113,800 MPa, and Poisson's ratios of 0.3, 0.2, and 0.342, respectively [29]. This simplified material representation was adopted to facilitate comparative evaluation under identical modeling assumptions rather than to reproduce the full nonlinear or anisotropic behavior of biological tissues.

Contact interactions between different components were defined using frictional contact conditions. The coefficient of friction was set to 0.46 for bone–bone interfaces, 0.30 for bone–implant interfaces, and 0.20 for implant–implant interfaces, consistent with previously published finite element studies. All contact interfaces were defined as non‐bonded, allowing relative sliding while preventing penetration.

Meshing, Boundary Conditions, and Outcome Measures

All assembled models were meshed using tetrahedral elements (Figure 5), with mesh refinement applied to regions of interest, including the fracture site and implant–bone interfaces. A mesh convergence analysis was performed to ensure numerical stability, with further mesh refinement resulting in changes of less than 5% in peak von Mises stress and maximum displacement. Based on this analysis, an element size of 2 mm was selected for subsequent simulations, with the corresponding number of nodes and elements in the finite element models detailed in Table 1.

To simulate a single‐leg stance condition, the femur was positioned according to an adduction angle (α) of 10° ± 1° in the coronal plane and an anteversion angle (β) of 9° ± 1° in the sagittal plane [30, 31]. An axial compressive load of 2100 N, corresponding to approximately three times body weight, was applied to the femoral head along the mechanical axis of the femur. The distal 10 cm of the femur was fully constrained in all degrees of freedom to prevent rigid body motion.

The primary outcome measures included von Mises stress distribution within the implants and femoral bone, as well as maximum displacement of both the femur and the fixation constructs, which were used for comparative analysis among the three fixation models. All simulations were performed under identical modeling assumptions to enable relative comparison among the three fixation constructs.

Results

Stress Distribution of the Implants

The von Mises stress distributions of the implants under axial loading are shown in Figures 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. In all three fixation models, peak implant stress was primarily concentrated at the junction between the main nail and the cephalocervical component. In the PFNA model, the peak implant stress reached 552.61 MPa, whereas the PFBN model exhibited a lower peak value of 514.18 MPa. The PFLBN model demonstrated the lowest peak implant stress at 457.70 MPa, corresponding to a 17.2% reduction compared with PFNA. The locations of peak stress were similar across models, although the stress concentration in the PFLBN model was redistributed toward the medial aspect of the nail–screw junction. Analysis of the cephalocervical components showed that peak stresses in the PFNA, PFBN, and PFLBN models were 176.0, 154.5, and 142.9 MPa, respectively (Figure 9). In the PFLBN model, the lateral wall screw exhibited a peak stress of 143.6 MPa, mainly located at the cortical contact regions after passage through the lateral femoral cortex (Figure 10). The peak stresses in the distal locking screws were 66.27 MPa for PFNA, 72.78 MPa for PFBN, and 73.24 MPa for PFLBN (Figure 11).

Stress Distribution of the Femur

The overall femoral stress distributions are presented in Figures 12 and 13. In the PFNA model, the peak femoral stress was 110.25 MPa, located on the lateral cortex of the distal fragment near the tail of the helical blade. In contrast, peak femoral stresses in the PFBN and PFLBN models were observed predominantly along the medial cortex, with values of 96.41 and 84.66 MPa, respectively. Compared with PFNA, the PFLBN model showed a 23.2% reduction in peak femoral stress. When stress distributions of the proximal and distal fragments were evaluated separately, the PFNA model exhibited relatively higher stress concentrations in both fragments, whereas the PFBN and PFLBN models demonstrated lower and more balanced stress distributions across the medial cortices.

Displacement Characteristics of the Femur and Implants

The displacement contours of the femur and implants under axial loading are shown in Figures 14 and 15. In all models, the maximum femoral displacement occurred at the apex of the femoral head. The PFNA model demonstrated the largest maximum femoral displacement (6.15 mm), followed by the PFBN model (5.11 mm). The PFLBN model exhibited the smallest femoral displacement (4.95 mm), representing a 19.5% reduction compared with PFNA. A similar trend was observed for implant displacement. The maximum implant displacement occurred at the tip of the cephalocervical component in all three models, with values of 5.78 mm for PFNA, 4.88 mm for PFBN, and 4.67 mm for PFLBN. Compared with PFNA, the PFLBN model demonstrated a 19.2% reduction in maximum implant displacement. The maximum displacement difference between bone and implant was 0.37 mm in the PFNA model, 0.23 mm in the PFBN model, and 0.28 mm in the PFLBN model, corresponding to a 24.3% reduction for PFLBN relative to PFNA.

Discussion

The present study performed a finite element–based comparative analysis of three intramedullary fixation constructs—PFNA, PFBN, and the newly developed PFLBN—for AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric femoral fractures under a standardized axial loading condition. By applying identical femoral geometry, implant positioning, material assumptions, contact definitions, and boundary conditions, this study aimed to isolate the mechanical differences attributable to implant configuration rather than to anatomical or modeling variability.

Postoperative Stress Transmission and Displacement Trends

Previous biomechanical experiments and finite element analyses have reported that AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric fractures are associated with predominant stress concentration along the medial femoral cortex following fixation [25, 26, 27]. In the present finite element analysis, a similar stress distribution pattern was observed, with peak femoral stresses primarily located along the medial cortex in all three fixation models. Although the PFNA model exhibited peak stress at the lateral wall, the highest stresses within both the proximal and distal fragments remained concentrated medially.

Across all models, the locations of peak implant stress were comparable and were mainly observed at the junction between the main nail and the cephalocervical component. Differences among fixation constructs were therefore reflected primarily in the magnitude of stress and displacement rather than in the overall stress transmission pathways. Compared with PFNA, both PFBN and PFLBN demonstrated lower peak stresses and smaller displacements under the tested axial loading condition, with PFLBN showing the lowest values among the three constructs. Displacement analysis revealed a consistent pattern across models, with maximum femoral displacement occurring at the femoral head and maximum implant displacement occurring at the tip of the cephalocervical component. The reduced displacement magnitudes observed in the PFBN and PFLBN models suggest differences in construct stiffness under axial compression, while overall construct kinematics remained similar.

The more stable mechanical structure of the PFLBN observed under axial loading is attributable to its design philosophy founded on the principles of “bionic reconstruction” of the lateral wall. In contrast to the PFNA, which relies primarily on intramedullary splinting, and the PFBN, which employs a triangular support structure to enhance both compressive and anti‐rotational stability, the PFLBN explicitly targets the restoration of the lateral buttress. The laterally oriented screw functions as a bionic lateral wall, establishing a structural bridge that transfers loads from the unstable trochanteric region to the intact femoral shaft. This mechanism effectively redistributes mechanical loads, thereby mitigating stress concentration on the medial cortex—a result corroborated by the observed 23.2% reduction in peak femoral stress—and enhancing resistance to varus collapse, as reflected by the minimized displacement values. Consequently, this load‐sharing capacity suggests potential biomechanical benefits for fracture patterns characterized by compromised lateral wall competency.

Interpretation Within the Scope of AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 Fractures

The present analysis focused specifically on AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 fracture patterns. These fractures are characterized by disruption of the lateral wall and compromise of the medial arch continuity, resulting in altered postoperative stress distribution compared with more stable intertrochanteric fracture types [1]. The observed medial predominance of femoral stress in all three fixation models is therefore consistent with the known biomechanical characteristics of this fracture subtype.

The PFLBN was designed to address fracture configurations in which partial medial cortical support may be preserved or reconstructed following reduction, as is commonly encountered in AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 and selected AO/OTA 31‐A3.2 fractures. In contrast, AO/OTA 31‐A3.3 fractures are characterized by extensive comminution and loss of both medial and lateral cortical support, representing a substantially different mechanical environment. Accordingly, the findings of the present study should not be extrapolated to AO/OTA 31‐A3.3 fracture patterns, and fixation strategies optimized for A3.1 and A3.2 fractures may not be mechanically applicable to A3.3 fractures. Recent finite element studies focusing specifically on AO/OTA 31‐A3.3 intertrochanteric fractures have demonstrated that this highly unstable fracture subtype requires fixation strategies designed for a fundamentally different mechanical environment, underscoring the distinction between A3.3 fractures and less comminuted A3.1 or A3.2 patterns [32].

Limitations and Future Perspectives

Certain limitations of this study warrant mention. Primarily, the reliance on a single femoral geometry precludes the assessment of anatomical variations, such as differences in cortical thickness or neck–shaft angle. Furthermore, modeling materials as homogeneous and isotropic—while standard for comparative analyses—simplifies the complex anisotropic nature of bone and prevents the evaluation of material failure. The loading scenario was also restricted to static axial compression; consequently, the implant behavior under complex multiplanar or cyclic forces remains to be determined. Additionally, the rigid distal constraint and literature‐based contact parameters may have influenced absolute stress magnitudes. Finally, these computational findings describe theoretical trends and await further experimental or clinical validation.

Within the limitations of this finite element study, the PFLBN demonstrated lower peak stresses and smaller displacements than PFNA and PFBN under axial loading in an AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 fracture model. These findings provide a computational basis for further experimental and clinical investigation but should not be interpreted as evidence of superior clinical performance. Future studies incorporating parametric analyses, alternative loading conditions, experimental validation, and clinical outcome assessment are warranted to further evaluate the mechanical behavior and clinical applicability of these fixation constructs.

Conclusion

This finite element study evaluated stress transmission and displacement characteristics in AO/OTA 31‐A3.1 intertrochanteric femoral fractures and compared the mechanical behavior of PFLBN with that of PFNA and PFBN under axial loading. The results indicate that postoperative stress transmission in A3.1 fractures is predominantly concentrated along the medial femur, with a general tendency toward varus displacement under the tested conditions. Compared with PFNA and PFBN, the PFLBN model demonstrated lower peak stresses and smaller displacements in both the implant and the femur. These findings describe differences in stress redistribution and construct behavior under axial compression and should be interpreted within the defined computational scope. Further biomechanical testing and clinical studies are warranted to validate these results and to assess the clinical applicability of PFLBN.
