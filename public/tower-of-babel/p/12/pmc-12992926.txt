Development and implementation of a modified IMEGH Paediatric Advanced Life Support course in a low-resource setting
Authors: Nita Avrith, Sussana Oad, Ann Pearson, Samantha Langer, Chase Westra, Vanya Zvonar, Bame Masake, Mompelegi Nicoh Dichabeng, Hywet Engida Sleamsaw, Ramona Sunderwirth, Noah Rosenberg, Eugene Tuyishime, Morgan Bowling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
African relevance•Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest burden of childhood and adolescent mortality in the world.•The Initiative for Medical Equity and Global Health (IMEGH) developed context-informed modified resuscitation courses including a Paediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) course for medical providers in low resourced settings.•This paper outlines the implementation of a modified IMEGH PALS course for medical providers in Botswana and reports on the educational outcomes of participants.Keywords: Pediatric advanced life support, Pediatric resuscitation, Low-fidelity simulation

Introduction

Despite advancements in healthcare and technology, over 8 million children – approximately 60% of whom were under 5 years of age – died worldwide in 2022. Of these deaths, most were largely due to preventable and/or treatable causes. Notably, mortality rates are much higher in low- and middle-income countries including many in Sub-Saharan Africa[1].

Botswana, a high middle-income country in Sub-Saharan Africa, had an under-five childhood mortality rate (U5MR) of 38.7 deaths per 1000 live births in 2022[2]. Despite improvement from 48 deaths per 1000 live births in 2017, there is still much work to be done to meet the Sustainable Development Goal of 25 or fewer deaths per 1000 live births by 2030[3,4]. Communicable diseases including respiratory tract infections, diarrhoeal illness and sepsis remain significant contributors to this mortality rate. A child mortality audit between 2011 and 2013, revealed that 33% of all in-hospital paediatric deaths occurred within the first 24 hours and 26% were considered avoidable. These findings suggest that there is likely not only a delay in accessing care, but also in the identification of critical illness and the initiation of quality paediatric resuscitation[5]. Furthermore, providers in Botswana report poor foundational training in paediatric resuscitation techniques and lack the skills to initiate immediate interventions, specifically in regard to respiratory distress and shock[6].

Dedicated life support and resuscitation courses – such as the American Heart Association’s (AHA) Paediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) course – designed to identify and treat critically ill children in a standardized and systematic manner have been shown to be effective in improving knowledge, skills, and reducing paediatric morbidity and mortality[[7], [8], [9]]. While these courses are highly regarded and welcomed by providers and learners in low- and middle-income countries, such courses are often cost prohibitive and logistically difficult to implement due to a lack of trainers and equipment[9,10]. Furthermore, when they are available, these courses are often not contextualized for low-resource settings. To bridge this gap, the Initiative for Medical Equity and Global Health (IMEGH) developed context-informed modified resuscitation courses including a PALS course.

The IMEGH was developed in Rwanda in 2019 by a group of anaesthesiologists in response to the WHO’s Safe Childbirth Checklist Initiative. The project aims to develop equitable relationships between high and low resource institutions, curate affordable continuing medical education courses for providers, engage in quality improvement and research, and create programs to improve the quality of resuscitation and perioperative care in low resource settings[11].

The IMEGH team adapted the National Health Care Providers Solutions curriculum (with permission), which is based on the recent AHA PALS guidelines, to suit low-resource settings[11,12]. Adaptations were accepted by consensus by all team members and included highlighting the current systems of care in low-resource settings in comparison to international standards, focusing on available resources for each topic, adding a detailed lecture on electrocardiograms, and using practical scenarios that reflect common cases seen locally[11,13]. The IMEGH adaptations of the PALS course go beyond content modifications and provide a unique delivery model, by combining adult teaching methods, such as didactic lectures, simulation, workshops, and discussions. Easy access to the IMEGH learning management system for the online portion of the course, also decreases the duration of the course to 1 day (see Supplementary information B for sample schedule). Finally, the IMEGH implementation model accommodates the use of low-fidelity mannequins which are more affordable in low-resource settings, promoting sustainability and scalability. This multi-modal approach enables course participants to achieve learning outcomes applicable to their local context based on the principles of flexibility and adaptability. This approach has been proven successful for Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) training in Rwanda[13].

This study implemented a modified IMEGH PALS course for healthcare providers in Gaborone, Botswana with the administration of pre- and post-course self-confidence surveys, knowledge surveys, simulation assessments as well as collection of participant feedback. It was hypothesized that participation in the course would lead to an increase in paediatric resuscitation knowledge, perceived self-confidence and simulated performance in the care of critically-ill children.

Methods

This is a mixed-methods study design conducted to assess the implementation of a modified IMEGH PALS course. Quantitative pre-post intervention surveys assessed participant knowledge, self-confidence and simulation performance, while qualitative analysis of participant feedback provided insight into the learning experience.

Study population

The IMEGH PALS course was taught as a one-day pre-conference workshop at the African Conference of Emergency Medicine (AfCEM) in Gaborone, Botswana on November 3rd and 4th, 2024. The course was available to all conference attendees and registration was performed online through the AfCEM website. The cost for the conference and pre-conference workshops was stratified based on country of origin (high vs medium vs low-income country) and training level. The registration fee covered administrative costs (room, food, etc.). Enrolment was limited to 20 participants each day based on space and resource availability (equipment, instructor: participant ratios). Instruction was provided by 7 faculty members from Mount Sinai Hospital in New York, New York, including 3 paediatric emergency medicine providers and 4 emergency medicine providers. All faculty were AHA PALS certified, and 3 were certified AHA PALS instructors.

Curriculum adaptation

The existing IMEGH PALS course was enhanced in several ways for this particular implementation study. New content was agreed upon by the IMEGH developers and course instructors via informal consensus. Firstly, the didactic lectures were edited to include additional examples of clinical scenarios, such as common cardiac arrhythmias, oxygen modalities, and aetiologies for different types of shock in the paediatric population. We developed short (30-90 second) videos detailing certain procedural skills, such as proper intravenous line insertion, delivery of quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and automated external defibrillator (AED) pad placement. We also engaged local providers from UB in the development of simulation cases, ensuring local relevance and accuracy. Lastly, we provided each participant with laminated pocket-sized pamphlets with the printed IMEGH PALS algorithms for review (See Supplementary information C).

Study protocol

Prior to the course, participants were given access to pre-course materials available on the IMEGH website, including readings and videos with associated quizzes (https://imegh.net/courses/pediatric-advanced-life-support-pals-course/). Completion of preparatory activities was required prior to course participation, with certificates from the online coursework verified before workshop initiation. On the day of course, the participants first engaged in an introductory low-fidelity simulation to become familiar with the learning environment and key concepts. This was followed by a structured schedule consisting of lectures, instructional videos, and skills stations. Lastly, the program concluded with a final simulation case and multiple-choice test. In order to receive certification, participants were required to complete all these elements and demonstrate competency in key skills such as performing high quality CPR and delivering rescue breaths using a bag-valve mask. See Supplementary information B for sample course schedule and skill stations.

Participants were asked to complete a voluntary pre- and post-course survey. Surveys were distributed electronically using Google Forms. The surveys were de-identified, with participants creating unique IDs to anonymously link survey responses. Surveys were completed immediately before and after the course. Inclusion criteria comprised participants who completed the IMEGH PALS course and the pre- and post-course surveys. Exclusion criteria included participants who did not complete the course or those who did not complete or appropriately pair pre- and post-course surveys using their participant IDs. The survey consisted of questions regarding demographics (age, country of origin, specialty, provider type, facility of practice, previous training, and clinical experience) as well as a 10-question multiple-choice knowledge assessment and a 24-question self-confidence evaluation. The self-confidence assessment included a list of paediatric emergency procedures and clinical scenarios, with respondents rating their confidence on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not confident) to 6 (very confident). In addition to the surveys, participants were also scored on their ability to complete 7 critical actions related to the introductory and concluding simulation cases (See Supplementary information D for checklist). Lastly, participants were able to give open-ended feedback at the conclusion of the post-course survey (See Supplementary information E).

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SAS University Edition. Pre- and post-course multiple choice tests followed a near-normal distribution based on Anderson-Darling and Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality, therefore differences in mean pre- and post-course knowledge results were analysed using the parametric paired t-test. Criteria for normality were not met for the self-confidence data. Thus, nonparametric Wilcoxon signed rank test was utilized to assess pre- and post-course differences in median self-confidence scores, with a reduced sample procedure to exclude data points where the difference between paired observations is zero. McNemar’s Exact test was used to assess for a difference in pre- versus post-course simulation scores. Post-hoc power was calculated using a medium effect size of 0.5 with a two-tailed paired-sample t-test and Wilcoxon signed rank test and a significance level of 0.05. Observed effect size was calculated by dividing the difference in observed means by the difference in standard deviations. Post-hoc sample size to determine number of paired observations needed to detect a 10% difference in simulation scores was calculated using power of 0.8 and significance level of 0.05 with Bonferroni correction.

For qualitative analysis, a grounded theory approach was utilized to analyse participants’ open-ended responses, systematically identifying themes through an iterative coding process. Two independent investigators performed coding, achieving strong congruence in theme identification and classification. Through open and axial coding, key domains emerged that encapsulated participants’ experiences and insights.

The Office of Research and Development at UB approved the project (UBR/RED/IRB/BIO/415) and the study was deemed exempt from the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Board Review (STUDY-24-01218).

Results

A total of 36 participants attended the IMEGH course, 16 participants on the first day that the course was offered and 20 participants on the second day. 34 participants completed the demographic survey and pre- and post-course knowledge assessments whereas 32 participants completed the paired pre- and post-course self-confidence surveys.

Descriptive data

There was a near even distribution of male (44%) and female (56%) participants. The majority were from Botswana (85%), but three participants were from Uganda, one was from Tanzania, and one was from Australia. The majority of participants were medical residents (53%). Very few (18%) had previously taken a PALS course, but many (82%) had participated in simulation (See Table 1).

Outcome data

Knowledge scores

Mean knowledge improved significantly (p<0.05) after course completion from 75.29% (SD=18.30, 95% CI [68.10%, 81.68%] to 90% (SD=10.44, 95% CI [86.36%, 93.64%]) with a mean difference in test scores of 14.71% (SD=20.92, 95% CI [16.88%, 27.54%]). The observed effect size was 0.70.

Self-confidence scores

Overall median self-confidence scores improved from 5 (IQR 3-5) pre-course to 6 (IQR 5-6) post-course (p<0.0001).). Median pre- and post-course self-confidence scores were calculated for all participants who completed both pre- and post-course surveys (original sample) as well as for only those participants who registered a change in self-confidence score (reduced sample procedure). Simulation

The participant small groups performed exceedingly well with the simulation cases, achieving almost all 7 universal critical actions on both pre-course and post-course cases. Mean pre- and post-course scores was 6.5. There was no significant difference in the proportion of groups that achieved 100% of critical actions pre- versus post-course (p=0.3173).

Qualitative analysis- Key Themes

Team Dynamics and Communications in Resuscitation

The importance of closed-loop communication, clear role delineation, and shared decision-making emerged as central themes. One participant stated:

“Team roles need to be well defined at the beginning of a resus, communication should be clear, and it is important to point out if you are not comfortable with an assigned skill.”

Additionally, paediatric-specific resuscitation considerations included addressing parental presence and communication, with one participant stating, “always take care of the parent.”

Mastery of Electrocardiogram (ECG) Interpretation and Arrhythmia Management

Over a third (12/32) of participants commented on the strength of the ECG curriculum, specifically ECG interpretation and arrhythmia recognition. They noted particular improvement in distinguishing shockable vs. nonshockable rhythms, bradycardia, and tachycardia. When asked for a key takeaway from the course, one participant commented:

“Pattern recognition on ECG, shockable and nonshockable rhythms, how to use an AED.”

Procedural Skill Development

The majority of participants cited improvements in procedural competence, most notably in airway management (intubation, laryngeal mask airway placement, airway adjuncts), intraosseous (IO) line insertion, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) technique. When asked the biggest takeaway from the course, one respondent noted:

“ECG, intubation, and insertion of IO.”

Paediatric-Specific Considerations in Resuscitation

Participants highlighted the unique challenges of paediatric resuscitation, including weight-based medication dosing, Broselow tape use, and procedural adjustments for paediatric anatomy. Many reported that the course helped bridge knowledge gaps in paediatric advanced life support (PALS) compared to their prior experience with BLS/ACLS. One participant shared:

“This course was a refresher to the paediatric approach to resuscitation.”

Another acknowledged the value of paediatric-specific training:

“This course was very focused on children, which is one of my weak points in clinical practice.”

Simulation as an Effective Learning Tool

Participants overwhelmingly endorsed simulation-based learning as a highly effective educational strategy, particularly for reinforcing technical skills and decision-making in real time. Several noted that well-designed, realistic scenarios enhanced retention and confidence:

“Simulation was a very useful tool for learning.”

The ability to immediately apply theoretical knowledge to practice was widely appreciated:

“Practical done after each presentation.”

Suggested Enhancements for Future Sessions

While the course was well received, several participants recommended expanding hands-on practice opportunities for procedures such as AED use, central line placement, defibrillation and intubation. Participants also suggested adding case-based scenarios, increasing session time, and ensuring equipment availability to optimize learning:

“More practice on ECGs and more clinical scenarios.”

Others discussed logistical refinements such as providing pre-course materials, increasing space for group practice, and ensuring simulation tools reflect real-world clinical environments:

“Some equipment was missing, so perhaps bringing all the tools to the simulation would help so that we could learn to use them as well.”

Discussion

This study demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in short-term paediatric resuscitation knowledge and confidence following the implementation of a modified IMEGH PALS course at UB. Despite small samples sizes, with 34 participants who completed the pre-post course knowledge tests, the study was adequately powered (80%) to detect a medium effect size of at least 0.50 between pre- and post-training scores using a 0.05 significance level. Thus, the observed effect size of 0.70 indicates a substantial improvement in knowledge following training. Similarly, for the confidence scores, the study was sufficiently powered to detect the observed effect size with a sample size of just 21 participants.

Our findings are comparable to other paediatric resuscitation courses conducted in Botswana. Wright et al. [6], implemented a contextualized version of the AHA’s Paediatric Fundamentals of Critical Care Support (PEARS) course with a cohort of 61 providers (primarily nurses) at UB. The PEARS course is a 1-day course designed to teach front line providers how to recognize a life-threatening paediatric emergency and initiate early stabilizing treatment. They demonstrated significantly improved knowledge of correct management of acute pneumonia and diarrhoea post-course (44% vs. 100%, p < 0.001; 6% vs. 67%, p < 0.001, respectively). Similarly, median post-implementation provider confidence improved (3.8/5 vs. 4.7/5, p < 0.001)[6]. Cox et al. [14], conducted a 2-day Paediatric Fundamentals of Critical Care Support (PFCCS) course with a cohort of providers at UB. Though the PFCCS course is focused on the management of critically-ill paediatric patients for the first 24 hours after initial resuscitation (versus IMEGH PALS is focused on initial resuscitation), their cohort was similar in composition: primarily physicians, from Botswana, who practiced paediatrics in a referral hospital setting. They demonstrated a significantly improved post-implementation mean knowledge score (67% vs 77%; p<0.001)[14]. Additionally, Glomb et al. [15], implemented a novel 2-day simulation-based paediatric resuscitation curriculum for prehospital providers in Botswana. Mean test scores improved by 11% from 75% to 86% (p<0.0001). While these courses are all slightly different in content and targeted study population, they all demonstrated positive knowledge and/or confidence outcomes post-training. In fact, similar findings were reported in a systematic review of 44 manuscripts describing resuscitation trainings in developing countries. Most studies reported positive outcomes in knowledge acquisition and self-efficacy, although none have correlated improved knowledge or simulation performance with improved patient outcomes[5].

In terms of simulation performance, though participants completed most critical actions during both pre- and post-training simulation cases, there was no significant difference between pre- and post-course scores. The effect size may have been muted by the fact that most of the participants (82%) had previous simulation experience, however more likely the study was underpowered. Due to limited space and time, participants in this pilot were assessed in two groups per day (n=4) rather than individually. Studies conducted by Glomb et al. and Levy et al. [[15], [16]], evaluating simulation-based paediatric resuscitation curriculums, were able to demonstrate significant increases in post-course simulation performance. In order to achieve similar results, a post-hoc sample size calculation suggests that future iterations would need at least 20 paired performance evaluations to detect a 10% improvement in simulation scores (power of 0.80 and significance level of 0.05, with Bonferroni correction).

Feedback analysis using a grounded theory framework, identified key themes that underscored the course’s effectiveness in fostering teamwork, procedural skill acquisition, and paediatric-specific resuscitation training. Participants highlighted simulation-based learning as a critical educational tool, while also identifying opportunities for course enhancement, particularly through expanded hands-on practice, increased case-based scenarios, and improved access to essential equipment. These insights can inform future iterations of the course to maximize its educational impact.

This study adds to the growing body of medical literature supporting the implementation of accredited certification programs in low-resource settings. IMEGH has successfully instituted training programs, such as adapted BLS, ACLS, and PALS, in 11 hospitals throughout Rwanda. The IMEGH course in Botswana represents one of the first paediatric-specific adaptations implemented outside of Rwanda and proves that the program can be successful in other countries and contexts[12,13]. Notably, although 71% of the participants in this study were based at referral centres, only 6 participants (18%) had previously completed a PALS course. This highlights a pressing need for more accessible and structured paediatric emergency training in the region.

Limitations

This study utilized a convenience sample of medical professionals attending the AfCEM conference. The majority of participants were paediatric residents from UB which was expected considering the residents were offered protected time to participate and logistical support from their training program to attend the course. However, the sample population of early career, paediatric-trained participants from a referral centre may influence the external validity of the results. It would be beneficial for future iterations to include a broader range of healthcare providers, particularly those working in various emergency settings.

Additionally, participants were assessed at baseline and post-training and therefore acted as their own study controls. However, to strengthen the internal validity of the study, further testing could consider including a separate control group.

Another limitation of this study is that it only demonstrates short-term impact on provider knowledge and subjective self-confidence levels. As per the Kirkpatrick Model of training program evaluation, to fully assess the impact and effectiveness of this course, future studies should focus on long-term retention of knowledge and skill, objective evaluation of use of skills in clinical practice, and patient outcomes [17]. A follow-up questionnaire administered 3-6 months post-intervention could help evaluate whether the knowledge and skills gained are sustained and effectively integrated into clinical practice.

Finally, as noted earlier, simulation performance evaluation was limited by collective rather than individual assessment. Recruiting adequate sample sizes is a common challenge for medical education studies and the pre-conference workshop setting is not conducive to extensive participant testing. Future implementations could consider adding an additional day to allow for individual participant assessments of simulation performance. Multi-centre collaborations could also address this limitation.

Conclusion

The IMEGH PALS training has been shown in this study to improve provider knowledge and self confidence in paediatric resuscitation. The use of grouped simulation performance assessments limited the evaluation of individual participant competency in simulated scenarios. The qualitative insights gained and the lessons learned from the current study’s limitations, will be helpful for refining the training content and informing the design of more robust future studies. Future research should consider incorporating an additional training day, larger sample sizes, more diverse participant groups as well as assessments of long-term knowledge and skill retention.

Dissemination of results

Results from this study was shared with study participants as well as leadership and residents from both paediatric and emergency medicine residency programs at the University of Botswana.
