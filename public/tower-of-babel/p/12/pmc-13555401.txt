A Predictive Tool Using The Society of Thoracic Surgeons Risk Calculator for Safe Operating Room Extubation After Coronary Bypass Surgery
Authors: John G. Byrne, Farhaan Chaugle, Thomas W. Kessinger, Curtis S. Dickey, Ali Asghar, Prince Thommen, Shahistha Hyder, Hannaan Chaugle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOperating room extubation (ORE) after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has proven benefit. However, predicting which patients should undergo ORE is difficult. We hypothesized that The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk scores could be used as a predictive tool for ORE.MethodsBetween January 1, 2023 and November 30, 2025, the STS risk scores of 687 consecutive patients who underwent on-pump CABG were calculated. Risk scores for death (in-hospital or 30-day mortality), stroke (cerebrovascular accident), acute kidney injury (AKI), reoperation, prolonged ventilation, deep sternal wound infection, prolonged length of stay, and blood transfusions were used to generate the predictive model for ORE. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine which STS scores were independent predictors of ORE. A composite score, along with a receiver operating characteristic curve with area under the curve, were also generated.ResultsAmong the 687 patients, 289 (42%) underwent ORE, and 1 of 289 (0.3%) required reintubation. Multivariate analysis determined that the risk of prolonged ventilation <3% (odds ratio [OR], 1.62; P = .03; 95% CI, 1.06-2.46), the risk of in-hospital or 30-day mortality <0.5% (OR, 2.58; P < .01; 95% CI, 1.39-4.80), and the risk of AKI <2% (OR, 1.67; P = .05; 95% CI, 0.99--2.84) were all independently associated with successful ORE. The receiver operating characteristic and area under the curve documented that the model’s performance was accurate 65% of the time. A composite score <7% favored ORE, whereas a composite score >25% favored FTE.ConclusionsORE is safe in selected patients undergoing CABG, particularly if ORE is practiced with high frequency and in patients with an STS calculator risk of prolonged ventilation <3%, in-hospital or 30-day mortality <0.5%, and AKI <2%.

Operating room extubation (ORE) after cardiac surgery is a very rare practice (1.5%-5.4%),1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 performed primarily after minimally invasive valve surgery or off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).1,2 If performed with low frequency, ORE can be unwise and perhaps unsafe.7 However, if practiced with sufficient frequency in a multidisciplinary manner, it can demonstrate improved outcomes, notably lower mortality rates, shortened intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (LOS), shortened overall hospital LOS, lower cost, and lower rates of perioperative atrial fibrillation.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Accordingly, there seems to be a substantial volume-outcome relationship.4,5 Effective ORE also requires institutional and programmatic commitment with an integrated care team with surgeons, anesthesiologists, ICU physicians, ICU nursing, and respiratory therapy.4,5 ORE plays an important role in an enhanced surgical recovery (ESR) program. We hypothesized that some of The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk scores and an STS calculator-derived composite score could be used as a simplified predictive tool for effective ORE vs standard fast track extubation (FTE; <6 hours after arrival in the ICU).

Patients and Methods

Between January 1, 2023 and November 30, 2025, the STS risk scores of 858 consecutive patients who underwent on-pump CABG at HCA Houston Healthcare Clear Lake (Houston, TX) were calculated. No patients were excluded for any reason. We selected 7 of the STS risk calculator–derived scores of adverse outcomes for the predictive model: risk of death (in- hospital or 30-day all-cause mortality), risk of stroke (cerebrovascular accident, any new clinical or radiographic stroke), risk of acute kidney injury (AKI; doubling of baseline creatinine or new dialysis), risk of reoperation (all cause), risk of prolonged ventilation (prolonged ventilation, >24 hours), risk of deep sternal wound infection (infection involving the sternum), and risk of prolonged length of stay (LOS; >14 days postoperatively). We also included the non–calculator derived STS outcomes of failure to use the left internal mammary artery and failure in medication compliance as a surrogate for the STS 3-star paradigm,10 as well as blood transfusions. Missing data across the selected STS variables, blood transfusion, ventilation hours, and record date were handled using listwise deletion. After this process, the final complete-case analytic sample included 687 patients who underwent on-pump CABG (Figure 1). We tracked those patients who underwent ORE vs standard fast track extubation (FTE). Successful ORE was defined as prolonged ventilation hours = 0, whereas FTE was defined as prolonged ventilation hours >0. Stepwise forward multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine which STS risk calculator–derived scores were independent predictors of successful ORE. The study was approved as a Quality Improvement Project, and a waiver of consent was granted. The study was Institutional Review Board (IRB) exempt (HCA IRB # 2025-1016). We tracked patients who underwent ORE (289 of 687; 42%) vs. FTE (398 of 687; 58%). Operating Room and Anesthetic Management

Our practice generally followed those outlined by Subramaniam and colleagues.1,2 We used a multifaceted approach to optimize appropriate patients for ORE by following the tenets of ESR. We began a multimodal analgesic approach with each patient receiving gabapentin and acetaminophen in the preoperative area. For presurgical procedures such as central and arterial line placement we used minimal sedation, which typically is 2 mg or less of midazolam or 100 μg or less of fentanyl as needed, or both.1,2 In the operating room, we used minimal amounts of short-acting opioids, typically less than 500 μg of fentanyl for the entire case, including preoperative, and long-acting opioids were rarely used. Fluid management was judicious in the operating room with the typical amount of crystalloid given being less than 1500 mL. The patients also received a peripheral nerve block, typically a bilateral parasternal block postoperatively or a bilateral erector spinae block preoperatively, to limit the need for opiates further. At the end of surgery, the patients were fully reversed from neuromuscular blockade and carefully assessed to determine whether ORE was appropriate. Communication with our ICU colleagues was made before leaving the operating room. All patients who did not undergo ORE underwent the fast track extubation (FTE) protocol with a goal of extubation in <6 hours.

Intensive Care Unit Management

After ORE, the patients were transported directly to the ICU with high-flow oxygen administered by face mask. Arterial blood gas testing was performed on patient arrival by point-of-care technology. Forms of noninvasive ventilation such as bilevel positive airway pressure, average volume-assured pressure support, or Teleflex, were universally available as needed. Indications for use of noninvasive ventilation included increased breathing work, stridor, and concerning arterial blood gas results, including hypercapnia or hypoxemia. We have an “extubation clock” present in each patient room that alerts the team to when the 6-hour limit is approaching.10

Statistical Methods

First, we computed a composite score by summing the 7 STS risk-calculator–derived scores (Supplemental Table 1). Then, we performed descriptive statistical analyses generated by the STS risk calculator. Next, we conducted univariate analyses to identify variables with a P value <.10, which were subsequently entered into a forward stepwise multivariable logistic regression model. Finally, forward stepwise multivariable logistic regression was used to identify which STS risk scores were independent predictors of successful ORE: risk of prolonged ventilation <3%, risk of in-hospital or 30-day mortality <0.5%, and risk of AKI <2% were independently associated with successful ORE (Supplemental Table 2).

We also plotted receiver operating characteristic curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) to evaluate model performance (Figure 2). Because left internal mammary artery use and medication compliance were both 100% in both groups, these variables were excluded from the multivariable analysis. All analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute). The logistic regression model demonstrated fair discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.65, suggesting reasonable performance of the model. Results

Among the 687 consecutive patients who had on-pump CABG, a substantial proportion (n = 289; 42%) underwent ORE, consistent with a substantial volume-outcome relationship.7,8 The 289 patients who underwent ORE represented a wide range of risk (Supplemental Table 1). One of 289 (0.3%) ORE-treated patients required reintubation. Supplemental Table 2 documents univariate and multivariate analyses. Supplemental Table 3 documents the composite scores stratified into 10 groups to determine proposed cutoff values for ORE vs FTE. Comment

This report’s contribution to the literature is that we developed a predictive tool for successful ORE using the publicly available internet-based STS risk calculator. This predictive tool can be used by any CABG program that uniformly calculates STS risk scores for their CABG patients, along with a comprehensive ORE program. Our data suggest that the STS calculator–derived risk scores of prolonged ventilation <3%, in-hospital or 30-day mortality <0.5%, and AKI <2% are independent predictors of successful ORE.

Mean and maximum prolonged ventilation, in-hospital or 30-day mortality, and AKI (Supplemental Table 1), together with the fact that only 1 of 289 patients who had ORE required reintubation, suggest that ORE is possible for patients with much higher mean and maximum risk scores for prolonged ventilation, in-hospital or 30-day mortality, and AKI, compared with the independent predictors, thus suggesting that patient selection in the operating room is paramount to successful ORE.

We also stratified composite scores into 10 ordered groups and calculated the observed ORE rate for each group. Supplemental Table 3 presents observed ORE rates across composite score strata. The composite score range estimates an individual patient’s likelihood of ORE and incorporated this information into clinical judgment when choosing between ORE and FTE. For example, and demonstrated in Supplemental Table 3, groups 1 and 9, respectively, when the composite score is <0.069, ORE is preferred, whereas a score >0.25 favors FTE. This is likely because of the modest discriminative performance of the model (AUC = 0.65), which limits its ability to clearly separate patients who require FTE from those who do not and consequently results in a higher estimated cutoff value for FTE. Therefore, this cutoff should be interpreted as an exploratory, data-driven reference rather than a definitive clinical threshold.

Study Limitations

There are at least 5 limitations in our report:1.The STS risk calculator data are collected manually from the medical record and are therefore subject to human error.2.The custom designed spreadsheet,10, from which these ORE vs FTE data were generated, is also populated manually and is therefore also subject to human error. We are developing automation processes for data collection from the medical record and population of the spreadsheet.3.For the composite score, for ORE vs FTE, the data demonstrated that <7% favors ORE and >25% favors FTE; because these data come from the same database, these cutoff thresholds should be interpreted as an exploratory, data-driven reference, rather than a definitive clinical threshold (Supplemental Table 3).4.There is a large gap between those patients deemed strongly suitable for ORE and those deemed strongly suitable for FTE. This gap (Supplemental Table 3) (<7% vs >25%) must be filled with clinical judgment at the time of ORE at the end of each case.5.This model is a single-institution retrospective analysis. Validation at our center or other centers is needed for broader application.

Conclusion

Consistent with our ESR paradigm across the organization, ORE is safe and effective in selected patients undergoing CABG, particularly if ORE is practiced with high frequency in a multidisciplinary manner, especially in patients with an STS calculator risk of prolonged ventilation <3%, in-hospital or 30-day mortality <0.5%, AKI <2%; furthermore, a composite score <7% favored ORE, whereas a composite score >25% favored FTE. This report represents a unique and simplified predictive tool for safe and effective ORE after CABG by using the publicly available STS risk calculator.
