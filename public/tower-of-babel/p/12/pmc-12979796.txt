Evaluating resolution requirements for subtle caenorhabditis elegans strain discrimination using classical descriptors and CNN–transformer models
Authors: Jose-Julio Peñaranda-Jara, Santiago Escobar-Benavides, Joan-Carles Puchalt, Antonio García-Garví, Antonio-José Sánchez-Salmerón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurately distinguishing subtle phenotypic differences between Caenorhabditis elegans strains remains a major challenge in functional genetics and behavioural studies. Here, we evaluate how image resolution affects strain discrimination using an automated Multiview system combining macroscopic plate-level imaging with high-resolution microscopic single-worm recordings. Three strains were analysed: wild-type N2, a transgenic line with mild dysfunction of GABAergic neurons leading to subtle locomotor alterations (vltIs66), and a strongly uncoordinated mutant strain (unc-1(vlt10)). The three strains were analysed using traditional locomotion and shape descriptors. While both imaging modalities detected clear differences for the strongly uncoordinated unc-1(vlt10) strain, no traditional morphometric and kinematic descriptors reliably separated vltIs66 from N2. We then trained a CNN–Transformer directly on image sequences. When trained on macro-camera data, the model failed to discriminate between N2 and vltIs66, whereas the same architecture trained on micro-camera sequences achieved robust separation, revealing phenotype-specific patterns not captured by conventional descriptors. To quantify the impact of spatial detail, micro-camera recordings were progressively downscaled by factors of 2, 4, 8 and 16 and the model was retrained at each effective resolution. This resolution sweep showed that performance remains stable under moderate downsampling but degrades markedly at coarse resolutions, indicating a minimum effective pixel density required for subtle phenotype classification. These findings highlight the importance of high-resolution, sequence-based deep learning for detecting fine locomotor differences in C. elegans.

Introduction

Caenorhabditis elegans is a widely used model organism in studies of aging, neuroscience, and genetics1,2 due to its simplicity, short life cycle, and conservation of key molecular pathways with higher organisms3. Among the different metrics employed to study healthspan in C. elegans, displacement and locomotion analysis provide a non-invasive readout for evaluating the functional state and vitality of individuals over time4,5. Quantifying movement allows for the identification of altered motor phenotypes associated with aging6, genetic mutations, or pharmacological treatments7,8.

However, the sensitivity of locomotion-based phenotyping critically depends on the spatial resolution of the imaging system and on the downstream analysis methods used to extract behavioural descriptors. When image quality is limited, centroid-based trajectories may overlook subtle phenotypic signatures, especially between strains with mild locomotion differences. Although it is generally assumed that higher spatial resolution should improve behavioural phenotyping, the extent to which increased resolution translates into measurable gains in strain discrimination has not been systematically quantified. Existing studies do not establish when macroscopic imaging is sufficient for phenotype analysis and when microscopic imaging becomes necessary, nor do they evaluate how modern deep-learning models exploit these differences in information content.

A wide range of image-analysis approaches has been proposed for C. elegans behavioural phenotyping. Traditional pipelines rely on threshold-based segmentation, contour analysis, and traditional morphological and kinematic descriptors9,10, sometimes combined with classical machine learning algorithms such as Random Forest or support vector machines11,12. In parallel, recent work has increasingly leveraged deep learning for worm detection, tracking, and phenotype classification13,14, as well as computational models of worm shape and posture15–18. These methods target different physiological parameters (e.g., centroid motion, posture dynamics, body curvature) and differ in their dependence on image quality: posture-based descriptors and fine-grained texture features typically require higher spatial resolution than coarse centroid trajectories. These approaches can be broadly grouped into three families according to the physiological variables they quantify: centroid-based locomotion metrics, posture-based descriptors, and pixel-level texture or shape features. Each family operates at a different level of spatial detail and therefore exhibits different sensitivity to image resolution, as summarised in Supplementary Table S1.

After grouping these approaches, it is useful to briefly outline what each family captures and why resolution matters. Centroid-based locomotion metrics summarise the animal’s gross displacement (e.g., path length, speed, turning), and can often be extracted robustly from low-magnification plate recordings, but they may miss subtle phenotype signatures when strains differ mainly in fine-scale gait or posture. Posture-based descriptors quantify body shape evolution (e.g., curvature, bending waves, eigenworm coefficients) and therefore require sufficient spatial detail to recover reliable skeletons and width profiles; at coarse resolutions, segmentation and skeletonisation become unstable and informative posture dynamics are lost. Pixel-level texture or shape features operate directly on local appearance and can capture subtle morphological cues, but they are also the most resolution-dependent and are typically inaccessible in plate-level imaging where each worm spans only a few pixels in width.

In this work, we refer to traditional morphometric and kinematic descriptors as conventional, explicitly defined measurements extracted from trajectories and segmentations, including centroid-based locomotion statistics, mask-based shape measures, and skeleton/posture-dynamics metrics. This organisation highlights that only a subset of descriptors truly benefits from microscopic imaging, whereas others can be reliably extracted from macroscopic recordings.

Automation of C. elegans assays has also increased substantially due to advances in imaging hardware and computational analysis. Several systems have explored automated behavioural assays using computer vision to track locomotion and posture at plate or single-worm level7,19,20 while other platforms have specifically targeted large-scale automated lifespan and healthspan measurements21–23. Although these studies provide robust solutions for performing very comprehensive Lifespan and Healthspan experiments using images with several C. elegans (which in this study we consider macroscopic images), they fail to offer image sequences focused on a single C. elegans for its individual study, and they also do not offer a robust solution to resolve the problems of loss of identity when C. elegans cross, aggregate or leave the field of view. In parallel, previous works have introduced multi-camera and active-illumination strategies to improve robustness and throughput in automated C. elegans imaging24,25, further emphasising the practical trade-off between field of view and spatial resolution: low magnification enables high-throughput recordings of many animals per plate, whereas high magnification allows detailed single-worm tracking at the cost of throughput and increased data volume.

In this study we distinguish between macroscopic plate-level imaging (macro), which provides a global view of Petri dishes and enables centroid tracking of multiple worms (In this images, the widht of each C. elegans is aproximately 3 pixels) , and high-resolution microscopic single-worm imaging (micro), which captures detailed morphology and posture dynamics but at lower throughput (In this images, the widht of the central C. elegans is, normally, more than 70 pixels). The Multiview system26 used in this study combines two macroscopic cameras to obtain a global view of Petri dishes and locate individual worms, along with a high-resolution microscopic camera, which is automatically positioned over the individual via an XY motorized stage for detailed analysis. This setup allows the acquisition of displacement centroids at both global and individual scales, with differing levels of resolution.

While macroscopic imaging is often sufficient for studies centred on coarse locomotor activity–such as plate-level tracking, centroid trajectories, or estimates of overall displacement–its limited spatial resolution constrains the extraction of finer behavioural features. Subtle posture dynamics, local body deformations, head–tail asymmetries, or changes in oscillatory patterns typically require a detailed representation of the worm’s morphology that cannot be reliably captured at low magnification. In contrast, microscopic imaging provides a high-resolution, centred view of each individual, enabling more precise quantification of posture-derived descriptors and movement microstructure. Therefore, when the goal is to distinguish strains that differ only slightly in their locomotion phenotype, the additional information contained in microscopic images becomes particularly relevant and may substantially increase discriminative power compared with macroscopic recordings.

In this context, the transgenic strain vltIs66, which carries two copies of the insertion vltIs66[unc-25p::144CAG]; myo-2p::mCherry, provides a particularly demanding test case for locomotion-based phenotyping. Characterisations performed on standard NGM agar plates have shown that vltIs66 worms exhibit only a very subtle motor defect compared with strongly uncoordinated unc-1(vlt10) mutants, and that their locomotion closely resembles that of wild-type N2 animals27. In young adults, simple motility indices can become indistinguishable between N2 and vltIs66. As a result, vltIs66 has been explicitly described as a challenging benchmark for automated motility platforms27, since visual inspection on agar and coarse movement metrics often fail to clearly separate it from N2. This makes the N2 versus vltIs66 comparison a stringent testbed for evaluating whether increased spatial resolution and deep learning–based sequence analysis can reveal phenotypic signatures that escape conventional plate-based assays.

The aim of this work is to determine whether high-resolution microscopic imaging provides additional discriminative information compared with macroscopic recordings when analysing subtle phenotypic differences between C. elegans strains (N2 versus vltIs66). To this end, we first quantify locomotion using traditional centroid-based and morphometric descriptors, and evaluate whether these metrics can distinguish N2 from vltIs66 while correctly identifying the impaired unc-1(vlt10) phenotype. We then evaluate the discriminative power of these traditional descriptors both individually and jointly, and compare them against a sequence-based CNN–Transformer trained directly on image sequences, to contextualise how imaging modality (macro vs micro) and analysis approach interact in this subtle discrimination task.

Finally, we train a CNN–Transformer classifier on image sequences acquired with either macro- or micro-camera recordings, and systematically downscale the micro-camera images by different factors to analyse how performance degrades as a function of effective pixel size. This resolution sweep allows us to estimate the range of spatial resolutions over which subtle phenotypic differences remain detectable and to identify approximate points of diminishing returns in terms of additional information gained versus data volume and acquisition complexity.

Materials and methods

To evaluate the influence of image resolution on the sensitivity for detecting locomotor phenotypic differences in Caenorhabditis elegans, we designed an experimental protocol integrating both macro- and micro-imaging systems within an automated Multiview tracking platform. Below, we describe the technical specifications of the hardware, the image acquisition procedures, and the software used for data processing and statistical analysis.

We analysed three C. elegans strains spanning a range of locomotor phenotypes. The wild-type reference strain N2 is widely used as a control in genetic and behavioural studies. A transgenic line carrying two copies of the insertion vltIs66[unc-25p::144CAG; myo-2p::mCherry] (hereafter vltIs66) exhibits mild dysfunction of GABAergic neurons and subtle locomotor alterations while maintaining an overall phenotype similar to wild type. In contrast, the unc-1(vlt10) mutant strain displays pronounced locomotion defects, serving as a positive control for a strong motor phenotype. This combination of strains provides a stringent testbed to evaluate how resolution and analysis methods affect the discrimination of subtle versus strong locomotor differences.

In this work, the Multiview system is used to assess how image resolution and analytical strategy jointly influence phenotypic discrimination. First, we quantify locomotion using traditional centroid-based and morphometric descriptors under both macro- and micro-camera imaging, evaluating whether these descriptors can distinguish N2 from vltIs66 while correctly identifying the impaired unc-1(vlt10) phenotype. Second, we train a CNN–Transformer classifier on image sequences acquired with either macro- or micro-camera recordings, comparing its performance against the traditional-descriptor baseline. Finally, by systematically downscaling micro-camera images and retraining the same classifier, we analyse how performance degrades as a function of effective pixel size. Combining traditional statistics and deep learning within a unified platform allows us to determine when high-resolution imaging becomes necessary for subtle phenotypic discrimination and how much discriminative power is lost as spatial resolution decreases.

Macro image processing and software

The macro-imaging system was implemented in Python 3.11 to detect and track the centroid of each worm on the plate at every frame. Worm detection in macroscopic images was performed using a YOLO v8 Detection neural network, trained on 4,028 manually annotated images acquired with the Multiview system. In addition, 1,200 synthetic images generated with a simulator, in which synthetic worms were overlaid on the plate background, were also included in the training dataset. Synthetic worms were included to increase the diversity of poses, sizes and plate locations seen during training, improving generalisation to rare configurations (e.g., near the boundary, low contrast, partial occlusions) while reducing the amount of manual annotation required. This simulator was developed following the methodology described in22,29. Input images were resized to 1408 pixels for faster inference. The network was trained for 200 epochs with a batch size of 8, with almost no data augmentation except for minimal scaling (scale=0.02).

This macro-level tracking process is necessary to maintain, for each C. elegans, a unique identity and its position on the plate at every time point. Every worm must be correctly localised in the macroscopic view so that the microscopic camera can subsequently acquire high-resolution recordings of each individual. The macro pipeline therefore provides, for each frame, the (x, y) coordinates and identifier of every worm, which are shared with the other Multiview processes to decide which individual should be imaged next and how to move the XY stepper motors to centre that worm in the microscopic field of view.

An auxiliary segmentation pipeline, based on adaptive thresholding, morphological operations, and area filtering (OpenCV), was integrated to resolve tracking ambiguities. Starting from the original macroscopic image (Fig. 1A), this pipeline generates binary masks highlighting worm silhouettes and plate boundaries (Fig. 1C). Worm identities are maintained by enclosing each individual within the minimum circular region that surrounds it (Fig. 1B); if a circle in the previous frame overlaps with one in the current frame, it is considered the same worm. When multiple overlaps occur, the auxiliary pipeline extracts worm contours from the binary mask and assigns identities based on shape similarity. Because the detection system assigns a unique identifier to each worm to enable simultaneous multi-individual tracking, it is critical to prevent identity switching, particularly when worms aggregate or cross paths. To address this challenge, the tracking strategy was inspired by the methodology proposed by Layana et al.17, which focuses on posture tracking and aggregation resolution in low-resolution C. elegans images. In this approach, worm skeletonization is used to improve identity preservation when individuals are in close contact or overlapping. Building on this, a UNet neural network was employed to resolve cases of worm collisions, while skeletons were extracted using the scikit-image library, ensuring robust tracking and accurate assignment of identities even in aggregations or noisy environments, following the methodology described in this paper29.

Figure 2 illustrates an example of this process during a worm aggregation. For each individual, the UNet-based module predicts a skeleton-likelihood map from the current cropped image and the previous skeleton. From these inputs, a set of plausible skeleton hypotheses is generated by propagating the previous posture. Each candidate skeleton is scored against the UNet prediction (for example, based on the overlap between the candidate centreline and high-probability pixels), and the hypothesis with the highest agreement is selected as the current-frame skeleton for that worm. In this way, collisions are resolved by combining temporal continuity with the UNet segmentation output, without requiring full multi-instance segmentation in a single pass.

This process is repeated iteratively until worms separate, ensuring that their identities are never lost at any point in time. It is important to emphasise that this pipeline not only provides the online positional information required to coordinate the Multiview system, but also generates the macro-camera dataset used for subsequent offline analyses. For each frame, the macro images and the associated worm identities and positions are stored and later used to compute the behavioural descriptors described below (displacements, trajectory statistics, silhouette curves, etc.) and to construct the macro-based image-sequence dataset used to train and validate the CNN–Transformer model for distinguishing between N2 and vltIs66. These analyses are described in detail in the following sections.

Micro image processing and software

The micro-imaging subsystem operates downstream of the macro pipeline and uses the information provided by the macro cameras to acquire high-resolution recordings of individual worms. The macro tracking runs continuously, detecting all C. elegans on each plate and sharing their identities and plate-level (x, y) coordinates with the rest of the Multiview ROS2 nodes. A scheduler process selects, at each step, one worm that has not yet been recorded with the micro camera and designates it as the next target for high-resolution imaging.

Once a target worm has been selected, the XY motorised stage is commanded to move the corresponding region of the Petri dish under the micro camera so that the predicted position of the worm falls within the microscopic field of view. At this point, a dedicated micro-imaging pipeline takes over. Each incoming micro frame is processed by two neural modules that operate on the same image stream. First, another YOLO v8 Detection (different to the one used in the Macro image processing pipeline), trained on manually annotated micro images, attempts to localise the worm within the frame and provides its bounding box. If no worm is detected, the control logic initiates a circular search pattern: the XY stage is moved along a predefined trajectory around the current position until the worm is found or the search path is exhausted. When the detector successfully identifies the worm, a ROS2 control node uses the bounding-box coordinates to compute the offset between the worm and the image centre and sends StepsMsg commands to the XY motors so that the worm remains centred as it moves, effectively following its trajectory under the microscope.

In parallel, a convolutional neural network estimates the distance to the optimal focal plane from each micro frame. Its output is converted into Z-axis corrections that drive the focus stepper motor, ensuring stable sharpness throughout the recording. The YOLO detector and the focus CNN do not depend on each other directly: both process the same micro-camera images, and their outputs are combined by the control logic that updates XY and Z positions in closed loop.

For each target worm, this micro pipeline runs for a fixed duration of 60 s, during which the centred and focused micro frames are stored to disk at the acquisition frame rate. These 60 s image sequences constitute the high-resolution dataset later used for offline analysis, including the computation of traditional descriptors and the training of the CNN–Transformer classifier. Once the acquisition window has elapsed, the XY stage returns to its home position and the scheduler selects the next worm that has not yet been recorded. Throughout this process, the macro tracking continues to run in parallel, updating the identities and positions of all worms on the plate and maintaining a record of which individuals have already been imaged with the micro camera so that no worm is selected twice.

All components of this micro subsystem (image acquisition, YOLO detection, focus estimation, XY/Z motor control and scheduling) are implemented as ROS2 nodes that communicate through standard message topics. Traditional image processing metrics

All macro- and micro-camera recordings were exported and processed offline using custom Python scripts. For each worm and imaging modality, the corresponding 60 s sequence, together with its tracking information, was used to derive a set of traditional locomotion and shape descriptors. These analyses are decoupled from the online tracking pipeline: the macro and micro subsystems are responsible for acquiring and storing high-quality image sequences, and the offline Python code then computes the behavioural metrics described below and assembles the feature vectors used in the statistical and classification analyses.

For each Caenorhabditis elegans and each descriptor, we reduced the 60 s time series to a single scalar using a predefined aggregation rule. We distinguish between (i) quasi-static descriptors, whose frame-to-frame variability is mainly driven by occasional segmentation/skeleton artefacts (e.g., size and global-shape measures), and (ii) dynamic descriptors, whose variability reflects genuine temporal behaviour. For quasi-static descriptors we report the median across frames to obtain a robust central tendency. Additionally, we did not include dispersion statistics (e.g., variance or interquartile range) as separate features in the baseline, because for several descriptors these measures are strongly affected by occasional segmentation/skeleton failures and would disproportionately reflect pipeline noise rather than phenotype. For dynamic descriptors, temporal variability is already captured by the mean absolute frame-to-frame change (or, for descriptors that are themselves defined as frame-to-frame differences, by their mean magnitude over time). Exploring richer sets of aggregation statistics is an interesting direction, but it falls outside the goal of defining a conservative, low-complexity traditional baseline.

Thus, each individual was represented by a feature vector aggregating all traditional descriptors, and the same temporal window (60 frames at 1 fps) was used for both macro- and micro-camera recordings.

traditional-descriptor baselines in this work are intentionally conservative. Our goal is not to design an optimised feature-engineering pipeline, but to provide an interpretable and transparent reference that quantifies how much residual discriminative information remains after summarising standard locomotion and shape descriptors over time. This baseline therefore serves as a low-complexity point of comparison against the image-sequence CNN–Transformer, helping contextualise whether performance gains stem mainly from increased spatial detail, richer representations learned from pixels, or both.

In addition, to provide a multivariate baseline against the CNN–Transformer, we trained a Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) classifier on the aggregated traditional feature vectors to predict strain identity. Although several descriptors show non-Gaussian marginal distributions (hence our use of non-parametric tests in the univariate analysis), GNB is used here as a deliberately simple, low-capacity probabilistic baseline with minimal hyperparameter tuning and stable behaviour under limited data. In this context, the Gaussian assumption is an approximation rather than a claim about the true generative process, and the baseline is evaluated purely by cross-validated predictive performance after feature standardisation. More flexible non-parametric density estimators or higher-capacity classifiers could better match the feature distributions, but are outside the scope of establishing a conservative and interpretable reference for comparing macro vs micro descriptors and for contextualising the CNN–Transformer results.

CNN–transformer architecture and training protocol

The traditional descriptors described above provide an interpretable, feature-based representation of worm locomotion, but their limited discriminative power suggests that important information may be lost in the hand-engineering process. To complement this feature-based branch, we also evaluated a sequence-based deep learning approach that operates directly on raw image sequences. Within the overall analysis pipeline, the CNN–Transformer model sits at the end of the image-sequence branch: it takes as input the same 60 s macro- or micro-camera recordings that are used to compute the traditional descriptors, but bypasses the explicit feature extraction step and learns task-specific representations directly from the images. This design helps contextualise the relative impacts of the effects of spatial resolution (macro versus micro) from those of model capacity (traditional morphometric and kinematic descriptors plus a simple classifier versus a deep sequence model).

Each image in a sequence is first processed by a convolutional backbone (ResNet-18), which converts it into a low-dimensional feature vector that summarises the worm’s appearance in that frame. In practical terms, this step replaces the traditional descriptors computed frame by frame: instead of explicitly defining curvature points or width profiles, the CNN learns such visual features automatically from the data.

The resulting feature vectors are then fed into a Transformer encoder that models how posture evolves over time. To preserve temporal order, we add a positional embedding to each feature vector that tells the model whether a given feature vector comes from the beginning, middle or end of the 60 s sequence.

The same CNN–Transformer architecture and training protocol were applied independently to both the micro-camera and macro-camera datasets (we also used 110 one-minute sequences per class for both macro- and micro-camera training), enabling a direct comparison of the effect of image resolution on classification performance. As detailed in the Results section, the classifier trained on macro images failed to discriminate between N2 and vltIs66, whereas training on micro-camera sequences enabled robust classification. Together with the Gaussian Naive Bayes baseline trained on traditional descriptors, this sequence-based model allows us to separate the impact of spatial resolution (macro versus micro) from the effects of model capacity and feature engineering. Full CNN–Transformer training details (optimizer hyperparameters, learning rate schedule, number of epochs and hardware configuration) are provided in Supplementary Methods.

To explicitly assess how spatial resolution affects sequence-based classification, we designed a resolution sweep on the micro-camera dataset. Starting from the native micro images (approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.0\,\mu \textrm{m}/\textrm{pixel}$$\end{document}), we generated four additional versions of the dataset by downscaling each frame with factors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$$\end{document} using bilinear interpolation. The same CNN–Transformer architecture, optimiser and cross-day cross-validation protocol were then applied independently to the original and to each downscaled dataset, so that changes in performance could be attributed solely to the loss of spatial detail rather than to differences in model capacity or training procedure.

An additional limitation is that the micro-camera tracking does not succeed for all individuals: only about 70–80% of attempted recordings yield a continuous 60 s sequence suitable for analysis. In this study, we mitigated this by recording more worms than strictly needed and by retaining only high-quality sequences for training and evaluation, so that tracking failures affect throughput rather than the reliability of strain labels. Nonetheless, improving online tracking robustness will be essential for future large-scale screening applications.

Results

Time series of locomotion images from three different Caenorhabditis elegans strains were obteined using both the macro and micro cameras of the Multiview system. The data were analyzed to assess which of the two imaging approaches provides clearer detection of significant differences in locomotor activity among strains, based on a variety of statistical analyses.

For clarity, we use the following terminology throughout the Results: (i) macro-CNN–Transformer and micro-CNN–Transformer denote the same architecture trained on macro- and micro-camera sequences, respectively; and (ii) traditional-descriptor baseline denotes the multivariate classifier trained on aggregated traditional morphometric and kinematic descriptors.

Global comparison between capture methods

Qualitatively, N2 and vltIs66 worms display very similar exploratory crawling on NGM plates in both macro- and micro-camera recordings. In the macroscopic view, their trajectories and overall activity are essentially indistinguishable by eye, and no obvious differences in path shape or plate coverage can be reliably detected. In the high-resolution micro-camera sequences, some vltIs66 individuals exhibit slightly shorter excursions or some reduced bending amplitude (not always), but these patterns are subtle and not consistently strong enough to support manual scoring or simple thresholding of single descriptors. This qualitative similarity underscores the difficulty of the N2 versus vltIs66 discrimination task and motivates the use of sequence-based deep learning models to extract weak, distributed phenotypic signatures that are not apparent from visual inspection alone.

Sequences acquired with the macro camera provided full-plate images, which were used to compute displacement-related statistics such as the path length of each C. elegans. Due to the lower resolution per individual, the resulting measures tended to show more similar locomotor behaviors across strains.

In contrast, the image sequences obteined with the micro camera, which focused on a single worm at a time, offered substantially higher resolution (Fig. 5). These results motivated us to first evaluate their joint discriminative power using a simple multivariate classifier, and then to investigate a deep learning–based approach operating directly on image sequences. The same CNN–Transformer architecture was first trained using macro-camera image sequences. However, cross-validation revealed that the classifier failed to reliably separate N2 from vltIs66, with confusion matrices showing substantial misclassification across folds and accuracies approaching chance level. This result indicates that macro-resolution images do not provide sufficient information for deep learning models to capture the subtle phenotypic differences between the two strains.

Multivariate baseline using traditional morphometric and kinematic descriptors

To complement the univariate analysis of individual descriptors, we evaluated a simple multivariate classifier to quantify how much discriminative information is captured collectively by the traditional metrics extracted from macro- and micro-camera recordings. For this purpose, we trained a Gaussian Naive Bayes model on the aggregated feature vectors corresponding to each one-minute sequence of a single worm.

When trained on macro-camera descriptors, the classifier achieved only modest accuracy (mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 0.58$$\end{document} across cross-validation folds), confirming that the traditional metrics extracted at macro resolution are insufficient for reliable discrimination between N2 and vltIs66. When trained on micro-camera descriptors, the same classifier reached only slightly higher accuracy (mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 0.61$$\end{document}), remaining far from a robust separation of the two strains. Discrimination using convolutional neural networks and Transformers

Since traditional morphometric and kinematic analyses (e.g., spline and Bézier curves, sinusoidal amplitude, skeleton or mask differences) did not yield statistically significant differences betweenN2 and vltIs66, we implemented a deep learning approach. A hybrid CNN–Transformer architecture was designed to classify image sequences of C. elegans, it was trained on both macro- and micro-camera sequences using the same architecture and validation protocol, so that observed performance differences can be interpreted primarily in terms of the information content available at each imaging scale, while acknowledging that this is not a formal ablation of spatial vs temporal modelling components.

We first evaluated the macro-CNN–Transformer variant, where the model is trained on full-frame macro-camera sequences, and then the micro-CNN–Transformer trained on high-resolution micro-camera sequences. Both CNN architectures were trained with cross-day validation: in each fold, all recordings from one experimental day were used as the validation set, while the remaining days served as the training set.

Effect of progressive downsampling on CNN–transformer performance

To move beyond a binary comparison between macro- and micro-camera recordings and to quantify more precisely how spatial resolution constrains sequence-based phenotypic discrimination, we performed a systematic resolution sweep on the micro-camera dataset. Starting from the native micro images (effective resolution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 1.0\,\mu \textrm{m}/\textrm{pixel}$$\end{document}), we generated four additional versions of the dataset by downscaling each frame with factors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s \in \{2,4,8,16\}$$\end{document} using bilinear interpolation. For a given factor s, each image was resized to (H/s, W/s) pixels while preserving the full field of view and the temporal sampling (60 frames at 1 fps per sequence). The same CNN–Transformer architecture, optimisation scheme and cross-day cross-validation protocol described above were then applied independently to the original and to each downscaled dataset, so that any changes in performance could be attributed solely to the loss of spatial detail rather than to differences in model capacity or training procedure.

The current tracking pipeline occasionally fails when worms approach plate boundaries, move very rapidly, or form dense aggregations, which can lead to identity losses or shortened trajectories. For both macro- and micro-camera recordings, only sequences that met strict tracking-quality criteria (continuous centring and successful detection over the full 60 s window) were retained for computing descriptors and for training the CNN–Transformer. In practice, this was compensated by recording more worms than strictly needed, so that the final dataset size remained sufficient for all analyses. Tracking errors therefore mainly increase the acquisition effort and reduce the efficiency of the Multiview system, but do not introduce label noise or systematic bias in the phenotypic classification or in the comparison between imaging resolutions.

Discussion and conclusions

The ability to discriminate subtle phenotypic differences between Caenorhabditis elegans strains was assessed, in this benchmark, using image sequences acquired with a multi-view system combining macro and high-resolution micro cameras. Traditional morphometric and kinematic descriptors–including spline and Bézier curves of body shape, skeleton and binary mask differences, displacement metrics, and anatomical distances–did not reveal statistically significant differences between N2 and vltIs66 in the analyzed plates, although some consistent trends were observed.

An important observation from our multivariate baseline analysis is that, under both macro and micro imaging, a simple Gaussian Naive Bayes classifier trained on traditional descriptors achieves only modest accuracies (around 0.58 for macro and 0.61 for micro). This confirms that, although micro imaging slightly improves the quality of the descriptors, the chosen feature set and a simple probabilistic model are not sufficient to robustly separate N2 from vltIs66. In other words, the difficulty of the task is not only due to low image resolution, but also to the limited expressiveness of traditional traditional metrics. The CNN–Transformer therefore serves a complementary role: it provides a fully automated, descriptor-free alternative that operates directly on raw image sequences and is able to learn richer phenotypic representations than those captured by the engineered features. Crucially, the same model architecture allows us to quantify how discrimination performance degrades when spatial resolution is progressively reduced, thereby establishing practical resolution requirements for subtle phenotype classification that could not be inferred from traditional descriptors alone. Consistent with this view, the Naive Bayes baseline remains well below the CNN–Transformer trained on micro images, indicating that the deep model exploits visual cues that are either only partially represented or completely missed by the traditional feature set.

To overcome these limitations, we developed a deep learning model integrating a convolutional backbone (ResNet-18) with a Transformer encoder, designed to process image sequences and capture both complex phenotypic features and temporal dynamics. When trained with macro-camera sequences, the model did not reliably discriminate between N2 and vltIs66, suggesting that the visual information available at this resolution may be insufficient for capturing subtle phenotypic variations. In contrast, using the same cross-day cross-validation protocol, the micro-CNN–Transformer achieved significantly higher discrimination performance, indicating that finer image detail is essential for detecting these subtle motor differences.

Beyond this binary macro-versus-micro comparison, the resolution sweep experiment further clarifies how much spatial resolution is actually required to detect such subtle phenotypes. By progressively downsampling the micro-camera images and retraining the same CNN–Transformer, we observed that performance remained relatively stable for moderate reductions in resolution (downscaling factors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s=2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s=4$$\end{document}), and only degraded substantially for stronger downsampling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s=8$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s=16$$\end{document}). This pattern indicates that there exists a relatively broad regime in which the model is not yet resolution-limited and can still exploit posture dynamics and body shape information, followed by a regime in which discriminative cues are progressively lost and accuracy converges towards the performance of the traditional-descriptor baseline and the macro-based CNN. In this way, the resolution sweep bridges the gap between macro and micro imaging and provides a quantitative estimate of both the minimum effective resolution needed for subtle phenotype discrimination and the point at which further increases in resolution offer diminishing returns.

Consistent with this view, the resolution sweep indicates a broad regime in which performance remains stable up to moderate downsampling, and a clear degradation beyond strong downsampling. In particular, once the images are downscaled by a factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s = 8$$\end{document} (C. elegans widht of approximately 10 pixels), both accuracy and stability across folds deteriorate noticeably, suggesting that this level of spatial detail is close to the practical lower bound for reliable discrimination of the subtle N2 versus vltIs66 phenotype.

Finally, we note that our experiments are not a formal ablation separating the role of temporal modelling (Transformer) from spatial resolution. However, keeping the CNN–Transformer architecture fixed while progressively degrading micro-camera spatial detail provides an informative proxy: the temporal model only becomes useful when the input frames retain sufficient morphological information to encode posture dynamics. As resolution decreases (especially beyond \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s=8$$\end{document}), the available phenotype in the image sequence weakens, and performance collapses towards chance even though the temporal modelling capacity remains unchanged. This supports the interpretation that adequate spatial resolution is a necessary condition for sequence-based models to exploit temporal patterns in this subtle discrimination task.

A limitation of this study is that we evaluate resolution sensitivity using a conservative set of traditional descriptors and a single sequence model family (CNN–Transformer). Other pipelines (e.g., alternative posture embeddings, optical-flow-based descriptors, or different video architectures) may exhibit different resolution–performance curves on the same data. Nevertheless, the resolution-sweep protocol used here is model-agnostic: by keeping the acquisition and validation protocol fixed and degrading spatial detail in controlled steps, it provides a practical way to quantify when resolution becomes limiting for any chosen analysis method. Applying the same sweep to additional descriptor sets and modern video models is a direct next step to further generalise these conclusions.

Overall, our results show that, for the specific subtle discrimination task studied here (N2 vs. vltIs66) and under the Multiview acquisition protocol, high-resolution micro sequences provide the spatial detail required for sequence-based models to achieve robust separation. While this does not establish universal resolution thresholds across assays or strains, the proposed resolution sweep offers a practical way to quantify resolution sensitivity in similar settings.

Our multivariate baseline further confirms that, even when high-resolution micro images are available, the discriminative information captured by relatively simple classifiers trained on traditional descriptors is limited. Thus, both spatial resolution and model capacity play a critical role: high-resolution imaging is necessary but not sufficient on its own, and must be combined with sequence-based deep learning models to fully exploit the subtle phenotypic cues present in the data.

Rather than demonstrating an absolute limitation of low-resolution imaging, our findings emphasize that the choice of spatial resolution critically determines the discriminative capacity of both conventional and deep learning methods. Future work may explore whether improved preprocessing, alternative descriptors, or hybrid multimodal approaches could enhance discrimination performance even at lower resolutions.
