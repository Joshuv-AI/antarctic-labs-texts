PD-L1: From cancer immunotherapy to therapeutic implications in multiple disorders
Authors: Daeun Lee, Minjeong Cho, Eunseo Kim, Youngbin Seo, Jong-Ho Cha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway is the gold standard for cancer immunotherapy. Therapeutic antibodies targeting PD-1, such as nivolumab (Opdivo) and pembrolizumab (Keytruda), and PD-L1, including atezolizumab (Tecentriq), durvalumab (Imfinzi), and avelumab (Bavencio) have received Food and Drug Administration approval and are currently being used to treat various cancers. Traditionally, PD-L1 is known as an immune checkpoint protein that binds to the PD-1 receptor on its surface to inhibit the activity of T cells, which are the primary effector cells in antitumor immunity. However, it also plays a role in cancer progression, which goes beyond traditional understanding. Here, we highlight the multifaceted mechanisms of action of PD-L1 in cancer cell proliferation, transcriptional regulation, and systemic immune suppression. Moreover, we consider the potential role of PD-L1 in the development and pathogenesis of diseases other than cancer, explore PD-L1-focused therapeutic approaches for these diseases, and assess their clinical relevance. Through this review, we hope to provide deeper insights into the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway and present a broad perspective on potential therapeutic approaches for cancer and other diseases.

Introduction

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is a crucial inhibitory immune checkpoint protein that regulates the functional balance of T cells, which is essential for maintaining homeostasis by eliminating pathogens as well as damaged and transformed cells.1 Mechanistically, PD-L1 binds to the programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) receptor, providing negative feedback to activated T cells, causing exhaustion and preventing excessive T cell activation.2,3 Abnormally high PD-L1 expression can interfere with the proper functioning of T cells, hindering the elimination of abnormal cells.4 Conversely, insufficient PD-L1 expression can lead to an autoimmune response due to T cell hyperactivation.5 Dysfunctional cell accumulation and/or impaired autoreactivity, which are closely associated with disease progression, highlight how abnormal PD-L1/PD-1 signaling contributes to various diseases.

Particularly, cancer cells exploit PD-L1 to evade T cell surveillance, and many cancers exhibit elevated levels of this protein, thus neutralizing highly competent cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in tumors with PD-L1 overexpression.6,7 Consequently, PD-L1 and PD-1 have become the gold standards in cancer immunotherapy, with researchers actively developing various immune checkpoint blockers (ICBs) that target these signaling pathways.7

Recent studies have revealed new PD-L1 subtypes and non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways. This review provides a detailed summary of the research on intrinsic PD-1 signaling triggered by its expression in cancer cells, PD-L1 detected in the nucleus where it regulates the expression of genes associated with malignancy at the transcriptional level, and secreted forms of PD-L1 that systemically suppress T cell immunity. Building on these insights, we explored the potential association of the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway with various diseases, including cancer. Furthermore, we evaluated the potential of PD-L1 as a diagnostic marker for various diseases and discussed the possibility of repurposing ICBs initially developed for cancer treatment to treat other diseases using drug repositioning strategies.

Non-canonical pathway of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling

Upon activation, T cells express elevated levels of the PD-1 receptor. Binding of PD-L1 to PD-1 recruits src homology domain 2-containing protein-tyrosine phosphatase (SHP)-1 and SHP-2 to the C terminus of PD-1, thus dephosphorylating crucial signaling factors downstream of the T cell receptor (TCR), ultimately inducing T cell exhaustion2,3 (Figure 1A). This canonical pathway plays an important role in maintaining homeostasis by regulating T cell activity. Recently, several studies have highlighted distinct facets of PD-L1 that deviate from this traditional role. In this section, we summarize the additional roles of the non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 pathway, including PD-L1/PD-1-mediated cancer proliferation, the nuclear transcriptional influence of PD-L1, and systemic immunosuppressive secretory forms of PD-L1. Intrinsic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in tumorigenesis

PD-L1 and PD-1 are widely expressed in various tumor tissues and cancer cell lines.8 Y-box binding protein1 (YB-1) directly binds to the 5′-untranslated regions (UTRs) of PD-1 mRNA, affecting its translation efficiency and promoting PD-1 expression in cancer cells.9 YB-1 also functions as a transcriptional regulator of PD-L1 gene9 (Figure 1B). Interestingly, the intrinsic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway appears to play a significant role in cancer cell proliferation and malignancy in both autocrine and juxtacrine manners, independent of antitumor immunity. However, the role of the intrinsic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway across different cancer types is controversial (Figure 1B). The PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway inhibits tumorigenesis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and colorectal cancer, while promoting tumorigenesis in melanoma, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), thyroid cancer (TC), glioblastoma (GBM), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).10

PD-L1 and PD-1 knockdown or treatment with PD-L1/PD-1-targeting ICBs promote NSCLC cell proliferation and colony formation in vitro as well as tumor growth in severe combined immunodeficient (SCID) xenografts. These effects are primarily mediated by the activation of downstream pathways, including protein kinase B (PKB) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2)11 (Figure 1B, NSCLC). Similarly, nivolumab, the anti-PD-1 therapeutic antibody, promotes proliferation and reduces the sensitivity of PD-1-positive colon cancer cells to chemo/radiotherapy.12 Conversely, PD-1 inhibition in melanoma reduced tumor growth by regulating the downstream mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway13 (Figure 1B, Melanoma). In TNBC, YB-1-regulated intrinsic PD-1 signaling blockade significantly reduces tumor growth and metastasis in vitro and in vivo. When bound to PD-L1, intrinsic PD-1 signaling activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and AKT signaling pathways, thereby enhancing TNBC cell proliferation9 (Figure 1B, TNBC).

These findings highlight the potentially variable functions of intrinsic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in different cancer types, depending on its combination with other activated signaling pathways. Therefore, understanding and clarifying the effects of intrinsic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling on specific cancer types is essential. Furthermore, this information, along with the PD-L1 and PD-1 levels, should be carefully considered as crucial indicators for determining the suitability of ICB therapy targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway.

Nuclear PD-L1 in transcription regulation

Nuclear PD-L1 (nPD-L1) is a pivotal transcriptional regulator in various cancer types, including TNBC, NSCLC, and uveal melanoma (UM) (Figure 1C). The presence or absence of Lys263 acetylation in the C-tail of PD-L1 is associated with nuclear translocation of PD-L1 in TNBC.14 Histone deacetylases (HDAC) 2-mediated Lys263 deacetylation triggers endocytosis, which involves interactions with huntingtin-interacting-protein-1-realted (HIP1R), adaptor-related protein complex 2 (AP2) B1, and clathrin. Subsequently, PD-L1 associates with vimentin for cytoskeletal passage and undergoes nuclear entry facilitated by importin α/β. Interestingly, the nuclear translocation of PD-L1 and p300/cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (CBP)-dependent acetylation are correlated,14 where hypoxia-induced perturbations in acetyl-CoA synthase impact this process.15 Hence, the intricate interplay between hypoxia and PD-L1 acetylation may play a crucial role as a potential initiating factor of nPD-L1 transport.

Notably, karyopherin B1 (KPNB1)-induced nPD-L1 transport orchestrated the regulation of Gas6 mRNA synthesis in NSCLC by binding to the transcription factor Sp1 (Figure 1C, NSCLS_left). This, in turn, promotes Gas6 secretion, activates the ERK and AKT signaling pathways, and enhances cell proliferation.16 Furthermore, nPD-L1 in NSCLC translocates and forms complex with STAT3, C-JUN, and P65, which contributes to the transcriptional upregulation of immunosuppressive molecules, such as PD-L2, VISTA, and B7-H3, consequently inhibiting T cell-mediated antitumor immunity14 (Figure 1C, NSCLS_ middle). Recently, a positive function of nPD-L1 in the conversion of cold-to-hot tumors was reported17 (Figure 1C, NSCLS_right). Under hypoxic conditions, nPD-L1 forms a complex with p-Y705-STAT3 in the cytoplasm and then translocates to the nucleus, where it induces downstream Gasdermin C (GSDMC) gene transcription, converting apoptosis to pyroptosis, a form of immunogenic cell death (ICD) that promotes tumor immunogenicity through damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) release.17 This function not only facilitates susceptibility to chemotherapy, but also promotes anticancer immunity through the ICD effect, presenting a potential advantage for cancer treatment. In UM, a complex of nPD-L1 with STAT3 is a key player in angiogenesis through transcriptional activation of early growth response protein 1 (EGR1), which accelerates tumorigenesis18 (Figure 1C, UM). Collectively, multifaceted nPD-L1 functions are highlighted by its differential expression in various cancer types and microenvironments. Thus, the strategic targeting of nPD-L1 as a therapeutic approach requires subtle refinement tailored to specific cancer types or combination therapies.

Soluble PD-L1 in the systemic immune response

Soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1), an extracellular variant predominantly secreted by cancer cells, plays an important role in systemic immune modulation, particularly through the inhibition of T cell surveillance. The intricate interplay between sPD-L1 and immune responses was evident in co-culture experiments, revealing that it suppresses T cells and induces activated T cell exhaustion in breast cancer models.19 Analysis of serum from patients with cancer has revealed that sPD-L1 is a potential prognostic indicator whose expression level is directly linked to clinical outcomes. Cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-α, and IFN-γ, induce splicing variations that augment sPD-L1 secretion, thus inhibiting T cell activation and proliferation.22 Cleavage mechanisms involving matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinases (ADAMs) contribute to sPD-L1 production,23 with cleavage occurring near the V225 and H240 regions of the PD-L1 structure on the cell membrane, releasing a 37-kDa fragment.24 In addition, alternative splicing, in which the PD-L1-9 splice variant removes 66 bp from exon 4, creates a stop codon, forming sPD-L1 without a transmembrane domain (Figure 2A).

Importantly, the emergence of resistance to PD-L1 blocking therapy is intricately linked to sPD-L1.25 The presence of PD-L1-vInt4, a soluble form that acts as a decoy for anti-PD-L1 antibodies, is associated with increased tumor regrowth. Notably, PD-L1-vInt4 alone cannot suppress immune responses,26 suggesting a potential avenue for investigating the intricate relationships between sPD-L1 therapeutic antibodies and antitumor immunity in patients with a poor response to PD-L1-targeting ICBs. In conclusion, understanding the multifaceted roles of sPD-L1 as a potential prognostic marker and gaining insights into its impact on systemic immune response regulation have important implications for the development of therapeutic strategies targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway (Figure 2C).

Exosomal PD-L1 in the systemic immune response

Exosomes, which are 30- to 150-nm vesicles released by cells, play a crucial role in intercellular communication and protein, lipid, and nucleic acid transport and influence various physiological and pathological processes.27 Tumor-derived exosomes carry proteins like transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), caveolin-1 (CAV-1), hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), and latent membrane protein 1 (LMP-1), stimulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) to facilitate cancer invasion, angiogenesis, and vascular permeability.28

Recent studies revealed that cancer cells release PD-L1-expressing exosomes that are highly prevalent in metastatic cancers.29 These exosomes may create a favorable microenvironment for circulating cancer cells and preemptively suppress antitumor immunity. Exosomal PD-L1 also disrupts T cell activation in the lymph nodes, compromising its efficacy against tumors30 (Figures 2B and 2C). Patients with high plasma exosomal PD-L1 concentrations after radiation therapy exhibit negative outcomes in terms of PFS and local control (LC).31

The endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) system regulates exosomal PD-L1. Hepatocyte growth factor-regulated tyrosine kinase substrate (HRS), a component of the ESCRT-0 complex, is associated with ERK. ERK-mediated HRS phosphorylation increases exosomal PD-L1 levels and suppresses both CD8+ T cell infiltration and activity (Figure 2B). Consistently, treatment with an anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) neutralizes CD8+ T cell suppression by exosomal PD-L1.32 The apoptosis-linked gene-2 (ALG-2)-interacting protein X (ALIX) binds to the ESCRT-III complex and promotes exosomal PD-L1 levels by enhancing PD-L1 packaging33 (Figure 2B). Calcium-dependent ALIX promotes extracellular vesicle generation,34 and nifedipine, a calcium channel blocker, reduces PD-L1 expression in patients with colorectal cancer,35 suggesting that a combination of calcium inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) may synergistically enhance antitumor immunity through exosomal PD-L1.

The significance of exosomal PD-L1 lies in its multifaceted effect on promoting tumor spread and hindering immune responses. As a potential biomarker, exosomal PD-L1 in the blood has diagnostic value, reflecting the metastatic potential of cancers and providing insight into the immune landscape. Monitoring exosomal PD-L1 levels can aid in predicting patient outcomes and guiding treatment strategies. Overall, our understanding of the role of exosomal PD-L1 in cancer progression and immune modulation emphasizes its potential as a valuable diagnostic biomarker with implications for personalized cancer therapy (Figure 2C).

Noncancerous diseases associated with PD-L1/PD-1 signaling

Balancing the regulation of the immune system is crucial for maintaining homeostasis and disrupting this balance can directly cause various diseases. Immune checkpoint proteins are closely related to disease onset and progression, leading to ongoing attempts to use ICBs for the treatment of various diseases.36 Indeed, PD-L1 and PD-1 are overexpressed in inflammatory lesion tissues37,38 and sPD-L1 and exosomal PD-L1 levels are elevated in blood samples of patients with various diseases,39,40,41,42,43 strongly indicating the involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in the development and progression of these conditions. In this section, we summarize the role of PD-L1/PD-1 in various diseases. We have also discussed the potential applications of PD-L1/PD-1-targeted ICBs in the treatment of these diseases.

Aging

Malfunctional senescent cells are typically eliminated by T cell-mediated immunity, and newly differentiated cells derived from adult stem cells replace them to maintain tissue homeostasis.44,45 Aging is a phenotype in which senescent cells accumulate without proper clearance or with a declining bodily functions.46,47 Interestingly, PD-L1 also plays an important role in aging (Figure 3A). Wang et al. reported that senescent cells evade T cell clearance by expressing PD-L1 on their surfaces. Furthermore, compared with young mice, old mice have more senescent cells with high PD-L1 levels in various organs, accompanied by reduced CTL activity. Notably, they demonstrated that anti-PD-1 treatment reduced the number of PD-L1-expressing senescent cells in old mice and ameliorates age-related phenotypes.48 Onorati et al. also supported these findings by showing that lung fibroblasts spontaneously increase PD-L1 levels under various senescence conditions.49Figure 3PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in degenerative diseasesThe involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways in the pathology of (A) aging, (B) Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and Parkinson’s disease (PD), highlighting how these pathways may influence disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 signaling.

Although the pathways through which senescent cells induce PD-L1 expression are not fully understood, senescence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs) secreted by senescent cells may upregulate PD-L1 through the JAK-STAT pathway in paracrine and autocrine manners.49 Similarly, amphiregulin (AREG), secreted by senescent cells, induces PD-L1 expression in the surrounding tumor cells through STAT3 and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) phosphorylation,50 suggesting that SASP signaling in senescent cells may upregulate PD-L1 levels to evade T cell surveillance (Figure 3A, expression regulatory pathway).

Senescent cell accumulation is associated with various geriatric diseases including neurodegenerative disorders, diabetes, and sarcopenia.47,51 Preclinical studies have shown that ICB treatment ameliorates aging phenotypes,48,52 and T cell-mediated senolysis using ICBs targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway may provide a potential therapeutic approach for treating geriatric diseases.

Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are major neurodegenerative disorders that primarily affect middle-aged and elderly individuals and significantly impact their quality of life. While both are neurodegenerative diseases with multiple causes, AD is characterized by the abnormal aggregation and accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau proteins in brain tissue, resulting in neuronal cell death.53 On the one hand, PD is characterized by damage to dopamine-producing neurons, leading to symptoms such as slowed movement, muscle stiffness, and tremors.54 Recent studies have uncovered a connection between these diseases and the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway, exploring the potential therapeutic applications of ICBs in treating these conditions.

PD-L1 and PD-1 expression is closely linked to AD and PD progression (Figure 3B). In patients with AD, PD-L1 is upregulated in various T cell subsets, including CD3+ CD56+ T cells and CD4+ T cells,55 potentially triggered by immune response-derived inflammatory molecules and metabolites like Aβ accumulation (Figure 3B, expression level and expression regulatory pathway_AD). Similarly, exposure to exosomes derived from the α-synuclein A53T mutant, a PD model, induces PD-L1 expression in T cells, which can be stimulated by cytokines, microRNAs, and non-coding RNAs present within the exosomes56 (Figure 3B, expression regulatory pathway_PD).

PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway activation appears to relieve the symptoms of AD and PD. PD-L1 expression increased in the astrocytes surrounding amyloid plaques in an APP/PS1 mouse AD model and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with AD, along with the phagocytic microglia.57 PD-1-deficient APP/PS1 mice showed increased Aβ accumulation and reduced Aβ phagocytosis by microglia (Figure 3B, Function_AD). Similarly, in a PD model using 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) mouse, PD-L1/PD-1 pathway deficiency in microglia exacerbated motor dysfunction by promoting AKT and ERK1/2 expression and increasing inflammatory cytokine production58 (Figure 3B, Function_PD). These findings suggest that the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway may help reduce neuroinflammation and Aβ clearance, thereby mitigating the incidence of AD and PD.

Contrary to these observations, PD-L1/PD-1 pathway inhibition by ICBs has shown therapeutic potential in AD (Figure 3B, ICB treatment_AD). In the 5XFAD mouse model of AD, anti-PD-L1 and anti-PD-1 antibody therapies induced an IFN-γ-dependent systemic immune response, leading to monocyte recruitment, reduced Aβ accumulation, and improved cognitive function.59 Similar benefits were also observed in the tau mouse model, in which ICB treatment decreased inflammatory cytokine levels in astrocytes, increased neuronal survival, promoted monocyte-derived macrophage influx, and enhanced phagocytic activity in the brain parenchyma.60 Consistently, Saresella et al. demonstrated that anti-PD-L1 therapy significantly protects apoptosis of Aβ-specific CD4+ T cells.61 However, Lin et al. reported that weekly treatment with anti-PD-1 mAb for 12 weeks did not enhance cognition or promote tau clearance.62 Similarly, Latta-Mahieu et al. showed that PD-1 targeting ICB can systemically activate the peripheral immune system in AD models, but did not improve monocyte-derived macrophage infiltration into the brain or amyloid pathology.63

These mixed outcomes highlight that the role of the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway and the effects of ICBs are not yet fully understood owing to the complex pathogenesis of AD and PD. Further research is needed to clarify the exact mechanisms of the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway and determine the best strategies for using ICBs, including optimal application and dosage, in treating AD and PD.

Stroke

Stroke is an acute neurological injury caused by cerebrovascular abnormalities, such as intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and ischemia,64 and characterized by the activation of leukocytes that cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and initiate an inflammatory cascade, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines.65 Key symptoms include sudden paralysis on one side of the body, difficulty in speaking, and visual impairment, which require immediate treatment to prevent permanent brain damage or death. Recent studies suggest a link between the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway and stroke progression and prognosis. Interestingly, PD-L1 and PD-1 have distinct roles depending on stroke type and stage.

In a middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model, PD-L1 and PD-L2 expression increased in B cells, while PD-1 was upregulated in microglia and macrophages66 (Figure 4A, expression level). Various signals in the stroke environment, such as inflammatory signals and hypoxia, can upregulate PD-L1 and PD-1 expression. PD-L1 expression can be upregulated by the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway,67 a major inflammatory signaling pathway, as well as the IFN signaling pathway68 and HIF-1α69 (Figure 4A, expression regulatory pathway_PD-L1). PD-1 is upregulated through toll-like receptor (TLR) 2, 3, and 4 and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD) protein activation in the presence of specific ligands, such as lipoteichoic acid for TLR2, poly(I:C) for TLR3, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for TLR4, and peptidoglycan for NOD1 and NOD2.70 Additionally, IFN-α can activate STAT1 and STAT2 via JAK1 and JAK2, thus forming the STAT1/STAT2/p48 (ISGF3) complex, which binds to the IFN-sensitive response element (ISRE) within the PD-1 promoter, enhancing PD-1 expression71 (Figure 4A, expression regulatory pathway_PD-1).Figure 4PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in inflammatory CNS diseasesThe involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways in the pathology of (A) intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) stroke, ischemic stroke, and (B) multiple sclerosis (MS), highlighting how these pathways may influence disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 signaling.

PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in inflammatory CNS diseases

The involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways in the pathology of (A) intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) stroke, ischemic stroke, and (B) multiple sclerosis (MS), highlighting how these pathways may influence disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 signaling.

In the acute phase of ischemic stroke, PD-1 reduces stroke symptoms by inhibiting inflammatory cell infiltration and activation in the brain. Particularly, PD-1 depletion in the MCAO model increased infarct volume, inflammatory responses, and neurological deficits.66 The protective function of PD-1 involves increased numbers of PD-L1- and PD-L2-expressing CD19+ B cells, which inhibit inflammatory T cell activation. PD-L1 and PD-L2 control cellular functions via distinct pathways: PD-L1 modulates both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, whereas PD-L2 modulates CD4+ T cells and regulatory B cells (Bregs). Furthermore, PD-1 signaling suppresses the inflammatory activity of macrophages and microglia (Figure 4A, Function_PD-1).

Conversely, PD-L1 exacerbates stroke progression during the acute phase by intensifying the inflammatory response. While PD-L1 inhibits the entry of regulatory T cells into the central nervous system (CNS) from the periphery, it enhances inflammatory T cell activity by increasing CD80 expression in peripheral antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby worsening stroke symptoms72 (Figure 4A, Function_PD-L1). Consistently, anti-PD-L1 mAb treatment significantly improves stroke severity, reduces infarct volume, improves neurological deficits, and creates an anti-inflammatory environment by decreasing Mmp-9 gene expression and increasing IL-10 expression. It also boosts CD8+ regulatory T cell activity and reduces immune cell infiltration into the CNS73 (Figure 4A, ICB treatment_Anti-PD-L1 treatment). Similarly, sPD-L1 administration in the acute phase of stroke significantly reduced cerebral edema and improved OS in the MCAO model.74

In the recovery phase of ICH stroke, PD-L1/PD-1 signaling has a negative effect by increasing the chance of infection through T cell suppression (Figure 4A, ICB treatment_Anti-PD-1 treatment). Cheng et al. observed increased sPD-L1 levels in the peripheral blood from damaged neurons, which acts as an alarmin to regulate peripheral immune responses.39 Following ICH, T cells and natural killer (NK) cells were significantly suppressed with increased PD-1 expression. Blocking PD-1 prevents the reduction in T and NK cells, spleen atrophy, and lung bacterial burden, suggesting that the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway contributes to the immune suppression often observed after stroke, potentially increasing the risk of infection.

Due to the diverse and opposing roles of PD-L1 and PD-1 in stroke progression and prognosis, the therapeutic use of ICBs in stroke remains controversial. Further studies should clarify the independent roles of PD-1 and PD-L1 in different types and stages of stroke.

Multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease that targets the CNS and is characterized by relapsing-remitting symptoms (RRMS).75,76 During RRMS, immune cells attack and damage the myelin sheath of nerve fibers, leading to disrupted nerve transmission and neurotoxicity. This damage results from the infiltration of overly activated T cells into the CNS, significantly harming the neural tissue. Recent studies have highlighted the role of the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway in MS pathogenesis.

The PD-L1/PD-1 pathway is crucial for maintaining immune tolerance. PD-L1 is significantly expressed in the inflammatory areas of the white matter of patients with MS, particularly in activated microglia and macrophages.77 Membrane-bound PD-L1 expression increased in CD19+ B cells and CD11c+ dendritic cells in patients with MS, along with elevated serum sPD-L1 levels.78 Furthermore, PD-L1/PD-1 expression is notably increased at the glial interface, which includes astrocytes and microglia, and plays a critical role in regulating inflammation and immune responses in MS79 (Figure 4B, Expression level).

Linnerbauer et al. revealed that PD-L1+ astrocytes significantly inhibit the inflammatory functions of PD-1+ T cells in both MS and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) models.79 Astrocytes upregulate PD-L1 in response to aryl hydrocarbon receptor and IFN signaling during acute autoimmune CNS inflammation. Concurrently, activated microglia increase PD-1 receptor expression. These interactions lead to inhibitory signals via the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, which suppresses excessive CNS inflammation by inhibiting microglial activation (Figure 4B, Expression regulatory pathway). PD-L1 overexpression in APCs inhibited the immune responses of autoreactive T cells in EAE, whereas PD-L1-deficient EAE mice had increased levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-17, which exacerbate symptoms80 (Figure 4B, Function). PD-1 blockade in EAE models activates antigen-specific T cells and produces pro-inflammatory cytokines81 (Figure 4B, ICB treatment). Notably, EAE mice treated with a PD-L1 fusion protein showed mild disease severity, attributed to Th17 cell development suppression and ROR-γt and IFN regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) inhibition.82 These findings suggested that the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway plays a protective role in MS by limiting autoimmunity and CNS inflammation.

Accordingly, ICB therapies that target PD-L1/PD-1 interactions could potentially enhance autoreactive T cell responses and worsen disease outcomes in patients with MS. Alternatively, PD-L1 fusion proteins and PD-1 agonists have been shown to inhibit T cell activity and alleviate MS symptom.82,83 The clinical application of these findings requires careful evaluation of the risks and benefits associated with modulating PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in patients with MS.

Collectively, the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway may be essential for maintaining immune tolerance and modulating CNS inflammation in MS. PD-1 and PD-L1 upregulation in response to inflammatory cues underscores their significance in disease pathogenesis. While ICB therapies may exacerbate autoimmunity, alternative strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 interactions may offer therapeutic potential for managing MS. Further in-depth research is essential to fully understand the complexities of this pathway and develop safe and effective treatments for MS.

Chronic respiratory diseases

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive lung disease of unknown etiology that primarily affects elderly individuals.84 This leads to lung tissue fibrosis, causing shortness of breath and chronic coughing. This progressive disease results in gradual extracellular matrix accumulation, distorting the lung structure and impairing gas exchange.85 In a subset of invasive lung fibroblasts isolated from the lung tissue of an IPF mouse model, PD-L1 expression was significantly increased85 (Figure 5A, Expression level). These PD-L1-positive fibroblasts showed stronger migratory and invasive capabilities than the PD-L1-negative fibroblasts. Consistently, PD-L1 overexpression enhanced fibroblast migration in vitro. Furthermore, PD-L1 gene ablation or anti-PD-L1 antibody treatment reduces lung fibrosis by decreasing fibroblast migration and invasion in vivo. Additionally, patients with IPF show higher PD-1 levels in CD4+ T cells than healthy participants.86 These PD-1-positive CD4+ T cells induce pulmonary fibrosis by stimulating human lung fibroblasts (HLF) to accumulate collagen-1 through STAT3-mediated IL-17A and TGF-β1 production. PD-1 inhibition in IPF CD4+ T cells effectively reduced HLF-induced collagen production, suggesting that blocking the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway could be beneficial for treating IPF increased86 (Figure 5A, Function). Supporting this possibility, Lu et al. demonstrated that administering an anti-PD-L1 mAb to mouse lung fibroblasts (MLF) inhibited the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, which increased autophagy and reduced pulmonary fibrosis87 (Figure 5A, ICB treatment_Anti-PD-L1 treatment). Similarly, a clinical trial is currently being conducted to validate the efficacy of atezolizumab, which targets PD-L1, in reducing fibrosis in patients with IPF (NCT05515627).Figure 5PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in chronic respiratory diseasesThe involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways in the pathology of (A) idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and (B) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), highlighting how these pathways may influence disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 signaling.

In contrast, Habiel et al. reported that PD-L1/PD-1signaling can prevent lung fibrosis in IPF.88 They found that CD8+ CD28null T cells, which express similar levels of CTLA-4 and significantly higher levels of PD-1, can induce lung fibrosis by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines. Blocking CTLA-4 or PD-1 accelerates lung fibrosis in a mouse model, suggesting that PD-1 signaling may limit the fibrotic effects of CD28null T cells as negative feedback signaling (Figure 5A, ICB treatment_Anti-PD-1 treatment). Given these conflicting findings, targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway in IPF treatment, a personalized treatment strategy that carefully considers the specific cellular and molecular context of each patient, is essential. More comprehensive studies are needed to determine the exact role of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in IPF and elucidate the potential benefits and risks of immune checkpoint inhibition.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition characterized by chronic airway inflammation, obstructed airflow, and irreversible lung damage.89 It is primarily caused by tobacco abuse and associated with clinical symptoms such as periodic shortness of breath and cough.90,91 In severe cases, patients with COPD may encounter recurrent viral infections that cause lung inflammation, resulting in permanent lung function loss.92 Additionally, they have an increased risk of developing lung cancer.93 Recent studies have suggested that PD-L1/PD-1 signaling may contribute to chronic inflammation progression and antiviral function dysregulation in the lungs of patients with COPD (Figure 5B).

In the COPD model animals, the proportion of PD-1-expressing T cells increased in the lungs. However, PD-L1 expression tends to decrease after viral infection, and CD8+ T cell activity is dysregulated in patients with COPD92 (Figure 5B, Expression level). Aberrant PD-L1/PD-1 signaling may contribute to chronic inflammation progression and structural lung damage through dysregulated CD8+ T cell activity. In patients with COPD, abnormalities in the PD-1/PD-L1 axis are associated with ERK1/2, STAT1, and STAT3 expression (Figure 5B, Expression regulatory pathway). Rui et al. showed that treating human bronchial epithelial cells BEAS-2B with cigarette smoke extract or diesel-related PM2.5 increased the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, and PD-L1. Additionally, both cigarette smoke and motor vehicle exhaust induce ERK1/2, STAT1, and STAT3 phosphorylation in the lungs of COPD model rats.94 The phosphorylated ERK1/2 and STAT pathways induced by cigarette smoke increase PD-L1/PD-1 axis expression in patients with COPD, contributing to immune dysregulation. Furthermore, Ritzmann et al. reported that blocking PD-1 in a COPD mouse model reduced cigarette smoke-induced neutrophilic lung inflammation and mitigated cigarette smoke- and non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae-induced lung structural and functional loss95 (Figure 5B, ICB treatment). As PD-L1/PD-1 pathway activation induces COPD exacerbation through an inflammatory response, PD-L1/PD-1-targeting ICBs may represent a novel therapeutic approach for COPD. However, the precise role of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in COPD progression requires further clarification.

Infectious diseases

Chronic inflammatory responses caused by infectious agents, such as viruses, increase PD-L1 expression in lesional tissues, which is associated with an exacerbating pathology.96,97 Persistent hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection (Figure 6A), which induces chronic liver disease, increases PD-L1 expression in hepatocytes through the PTEN/β-catenin/c-Myc signaling pathway,98 resulting in the functional exhaustion of virus-specific CD8+ T cells and promoting HBV immune evasion.99,100 Consistent with the increased PD-L1 levels at lesion sites, plasma sPD-L1 levels in patients with HBV were higher than those in healthy participants. Therefore, sPD-L1 can be used as a diagnostic marker for HBV infection and progressive liver damage.101Figure 6PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in infectious diseasesThe involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways in the pathology of (A) hepatitis B virus (HBV), (B) hepatitis C virus (HCV), (C) human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), (D) COVID-19, (E) sepsis, and (F) tuberculosis (TB), highlighting how these pathways may influence disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 signaling.

PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in infectious diseases

The involvement of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways in the pathology of (A) hepatitis B virus (HBV), (B) hepatitis C virus (HCV), (C) human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), (D) COVID-19, (E) sepsis, and (F) tuberculosis (TB), highlighting how these pathways may influence disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies targeting PD-L1/PD-1 signaling.

During chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (Figure 6B), PD-1 is upregulated in virus-specific CD8+ T cells, which causes significant dysfunction.102 In this study, the severity of CD8+ T cell exhaustion and its responsiveness to blockade were determined by the level of PD-1 expression and its location in the liver or blood. Intrahepatic HCV-specific CD8+ T cells exhibited high PD-1 expression and profound functional impairment, which were not reversible by PD-1/PD-L blockade. Conversely, circulating HCV-specific CD8+ T cells with intermediate PD-1 levels responded positively to PD-L1/PD-1 blockade. These findings underscore the complexity of T cell exhaustion in chronic viral infections and suggest the need for compartment-specific therapeutic strategies for PD-L1/PD-1 blockade.

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) targets CD4+ T cells, which play a critical role in supporting CD8+ T cells, thus depleting T cell immunity through persistent infection (Figure 6C). PD-1 is upregulated in virus-specific CD8+ T cells from patients with HIV infection. In vitro, HIV-specific PD-1-expressing CD8+ T cells showed reduced proliferation and cytokine secretion, and were easily exhausted. Consistently, blocking the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway with ICB restored the function of CD8+ T cells,103 suggesting the potential of ICBs as adjuvants for HIV treatment.

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is an infectious lung disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection.104 Several studies have investigated the role of the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway in COVID-19 progression, and exploring the therapeutic potential of ICBs is of substantial interest. The patients with COVID-19 had increased PD-1 expression in CD4+, CD8+, and CD19+ T cells as well as sPD-L1 expression40,41 (Figure 6D, Expression level). Caco-2 cells infected with SARS-CoV-2 enhance the signaling of antiviral IFNs and cytokines, such as IFN-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15), IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-12.105 IL-12 boosts the IFN-γ pathway by inducing transcription via JAK2 and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) signaling. IRF1 and NF-κB, activated by IFN-γ and TNF-α, respectively, binds to PD-L1 promoter and upregulates its expression (Figure 6D, Expression regulatory pathway). Lorelli et al. demonstrated that T cells, which lost their function after SARS-CoV-2 infection, were collected from patients and co-cultured with anit-PD-1 mAb, restoring T cell function, including a 2-fold increase in the number of IFN-γ-producing cells, enhancing the anti-SARS-CoV-2-specific immune response106 (Figure 6D, ICB treatment_Anti-PD-1 treatment).

Taken together, enhanced PD-L1/PD-1 signaling due to COVID-19 may inhibit T cell function, resulting in poor viral clearance and worsening of symptoms (Figure 6D, Function). While ICBs may potentially be used to treat COVID-19, PD-L1/PD-1 blockade may be a double-edged sword depending on patient condition. The PD-L1/PD-1 axis can cause immune dysregulation, activate autoimmune CD8+ T cells, and lead to poor prognosis in patients with COVID-19, including immune-mediated pneumonia development. Interstitial hyperplasia, CD8+ lymphocyte infiltration, and alveolar damage have been observed in cancer patients infected with SARS-CoV-2 who received PD-L1 mAb intravenously every 3 weeks107 (Figure 6D, ICB treatment_Anti-PD-L1 treatment). Other studies have also noted that cancer patients with prior ICB exposure have high hospitalization rates and severity after the COVID-19 outbreak, indicating a poor prognosis108,109,110 (Figure 6D, ICB treatment_Prior ICB exposure). Therefore, further research is necessary to define the role of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling at different stages of post-infection COVID-19 progression to maximize the utility of ICB and reduce adverse effects.

Patients with severe COVID-19 exhibit respiratory system impairment, similar to the acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) observed in sepsis, a representative infectious disease due to lung injury.111 sPD-L1 and exosomal PD-L1 levels increase substantially during sepsis,43 suggesting that sPD-L1 could serve as a biomarker of sepsis.112 Consistent with this finding, Xu et al. reported sPD-L1 upregulation in the survivors of direct acute ARDS.113 In this study, sPD-L1 administration to mice with ARDS attenuated inflammatory lung injury and improved survival, suggesting that inflammatory signaling in sepsis may help prevent tissue damage by increasing PD-L1 expression to suppress immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Conversely, beneficial effects of anti-PD-L1 mAb in sepsis have also been reported.114 Therefore, understanding the exact role of PD-L1 in sepsis and careful consideration of the timing and duration of ICB therapy are imperative (Figure 6E).

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), which primarily infects the lungs.115 Mtb is transmitted through the respiratory tract, entering the lungs and being phagocytized by alveolar macrophages. Inside these macrophages, Mtb can survive and replicate, causing lung damage and symptoms like cough, fever, and weight loss. The chronic inflammation seen in TB-infected tissues suggests that the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway, which helps maintain immune balance, plays a key role in TB pathogenesis.

Indeed, Mtb-infected macrophages have increased PD-1 and PD-L1 expression (Figure 6F, Expression level), decreased AKT and mTOR levels, and higher production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.116 Blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway reverses these effects, reducing macrophage numbers through ROS-induced cell death, indicating that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway may help control TB severity by enhancing macrophage survival and modulating immune responses (Figure 6F, Function). Furthermore, PD-1 expression is elevated on Mtb-specific CD4+ T cells in TB patients117 (Figure 6F, Expression level), playing a critical role in TB progression.118 PD-1-deficient mice show higher susceptibility to TB, worse outcomes, and early death due to T cell dysfunction, highlighting the importance of PD-1 in regulating T cell responses against TB (Figure 6F, Function). Conversely, PD-L1-deficient mice exhibit a lesser increase in susceptibility, suggesting that other PD-1 ligands may also regulate T cell activity. Consistently, Sultan Tousif et al. found that PD-1-deficient mice infected with Mtb strain H37Rv had reduced T cell proliferation and were more prone to infection due to increased Tregs and mesenchymal stem cell recruitment in infected tissues119 (Figure 6F, Function). This suggests that PD-1 may regulate Mtb infection through non-canonical pathways rather than just inhibiting T cell activation.

Contrastingly, studies on cancer patients treated with ICB targeting PD-L1/PD-1 indicate that these treatments may increase the risk of TB infection and reactivation (Figure 6F, ICB treatment). A meta-analysis of 27 studies found a 35-fold higher TB incidence in cancer patients treated with these inhibitors compared to the general population.120 A retrospective review using the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) also suggested a higher TB risk in these patients compared to those on other drugs.121 While the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway is crucial for controlling TB by balancing immune responses, its inhibition in cancer patients is linked to a greater risk of Mtb infection and reactivation. Therefore, further research is needed to explore how ICBs affect TB immunity in cancer patients and to compare PD-1 function and ICB responsiveness between cancer and non-cancer TB patients.

Collectively, the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway exhibits complex functions in the pathogenesis of infectious diseases and has important value as a diagnostic and therapeutic target. The detailed role of the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway in the progression of tissue damage caused by an overactivated immune response to infection should be urgently elucidated. Based on these insights, available ICBs could play an important role in overcoming the coming wave of infections triggered by the climate crisis.

Exploring soluble and exosomal PD-L1 as biomarkers for blood diagnosis

Pilot studies are under way to assess the significance of soluble and exosomal PD-L1 as biomarkers for cancer treatment. NCT05426317 primarily investigates the correlation between blood levels of sPD-L1 and β2-microglobulin, measured at the diagnostic stage, and the clinical course of patients with metastatic solid tumors, particularly NSCLC, kidney cancer, or melanoma, who are undergoing first-line ICB therapy. This study examines the correlations between sPD-L1 levels in the blood and membrane PD-L1 levels in the tumor, as well as between sPD-L1 and β2-microglobulin levels in the blood. We also explored the relationship between these blood biomarkers and resistance to ICB therapy.

Meanwhile, NCT05878977 aims to determine whether the human gastrointestinal microbiome (bacterial and viral) and exosomal PD-L1 and IFN-γ mRNA expression can predict the treatment response to PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors for metastatic melanoma. Fecal samples were collected before treatment initiation, at 12 ± 2 weeks, 28 ± 4 weeks, and during events such as suspected disease progression, hyperprogression, or irAEs. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected at these time points for cytological and molecular testing, and histological samples were obtained from the tumor tissue before initiating immunotherapy.

NCT03811600 demonstrated a link between obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) and a high rate of cancer-related deaths. Cohort 1 consists of 45 patients suspected of having OSAS with an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) > 15 per hour. Cohort 2 included 45 patients suspected of having OSAS with an AHI <15 per hour. This trial demonstrated a positive association between OSAS and cancer. This association may be partly due to weakened cellular immune responses observed in patients with OSAS. Researchers have suspected that OSAS disrupts immune cells by amplifying the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway. Exosomes, tiny membrane sacs released by various cells and circulating in the bloodstream, promote cancer cell growth through PD-L1/PD-1 pathway activation.

Collectively, these results highlight the importance of soluble and exosomal PD-L1 as serum biomarkers for cancer diagnosis. By deciphering the intricate interactions between biomarkers and clinical outcomes, these studies provide valuable insights into the prognosis and treatment response of patients with metastatic solid tumors undergoing first-line ICB therapy.

Validating PD-L1/PD-1 blockade in treating patients with early AD

NCT05551741 is currently under way to assess the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics of IBC-Ab002 in patients with early-stage AD. This study is the first human phase 1 clinical trial of IBC-Ab022, a fully human anti-PD-L1 mAb developed by the immunobrain checkpoint (IBC), which studies multiple pathological factors influencing cognitive symptoms and the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. Designed to target factors such as localized inflammation in the brain, Aβ and tau pathology, and neuronal loss, the antibody inhibits PD-L1 to induce an immune response, a pioneering approach to address multiple neurodegenerative disease-associated pathophysiological factors. In this trial, IBC-Ab002 was administered every 3 months to temporarily inhibit the PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway in patients with early-stage AD. The trial included both single- and multi-dose escalation components. Part A was a single-dose escalation trial and Part B was a multiple-dose escalation trial. The two parts of the trial were crossovers; therefore, participants were dosed every 12 weeks. The results of this trial should help elucidate the role of the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway in AD and the value of ICB as a treatment for AD.

Exploring the relationship between PD-L1/PD-1 blockade and autoimmune disease history

While PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors effectively enhance T cell-mediated tumor destruction, treatment with ICBs often leads to irAEs that vary in severity and can affect multiple organ systems. Common irAEs include gastrointestinal toxicities such as colitis, toxicities such as thyroiditis, and dermatological toxicities such as rash and vitiligo.122 More severe and potentially fatal irAEs, including neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and pulmonary toxicity, are observed more frequently in patients receiving combination ICB therapy than in those receiving monotherapy.123 These adverse effects arise from the disruption of immune tolerance, highlighting the need for careful management and monitoring during treatment. Given this, understanding an individual’s history of autoimmune disease may be valuable in predicting and managing potential adverse effects of ICB therapy.

The autoimmune and malignancy-nivolumab (AIM-Nivo) study, registered as NCT03816345, aimed to address this gap by assessing the safety of nivolumab in patients with severe autoimmune diseases such as dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis who were previously excluded from ICB therapy.124 In this multicenter collaborative trial that began in May 2019, patients with autoimmune diseases and advanced malignancies were administered nivolumab every 28 days. This study meticulously evaluated disease severity in each cohort by assessing the safety, toxicity profile, and efficacy of nivolumab against tumors. Since the trial began, the study has continuously recorded and analyzed the results.

The NCT03656627 trial investigated the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of nivolumab, an anti-PD-1 mAb, in a cohort of patients with autoimmune diseases. Two distinct cohorts were enrolled based on the type of autoimmune disease: cohort 1 included patients with rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, giant cell arteritis/polymyalgia rheumatica, and systemic lupus erythematosus; and cohort 2 included patients with other autoimmune diseases such as ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, and MS. Patients received outpatient treatment every 2 weeks, each treatment cycle was 28 days, and nivolumab was administered on days 1 and 15 of the treatment cycle. The results of these trials hold promise in offering significant insights into the landscape of PD-1 targeted therapy for both autoimmune diseases and malignancies.

Overactivated T cells are strongly associated with the progression of autoimmune liver diseases (AILD), such as autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC). Immune-related hepatotoxicity (irH) commonly occurs with ICB combination therapy, raising concerns that ICB therapy may exacerbate liver damage in patients with AILD.125 Coukos et al. conducted a study comparing the clinical, laboratory, and histologic characteristics of 27 patients with liver-associated irAEs (ICB group) and 14 patients with AILD.126 The comparison showed that centrilobular damage and granuloma formation were more frequent in the ICB group. The results showed that centrilobular damage and granuloma formation were more frequent in the ICB group, and there was a trend toward increased CD8+ T cells in this group compared to the AILD group. The pattern of liver function test abnormalities did not correlate with histologic severity in either condition, indicating that, while the two diseases share some common features, they are distinct in clinical, biological, and histological aspects. Kocheise et al. evaluated the safety of ICB targeting PD-L1/PD-1 in patients with AILD and malignancies, who are usually excluded from such trials due to concerns about irAEs.127 Involving 22 patients with AILD, including those with PBC, PSC, and AIH, the study found that 36.4% experienced mild irAEs during ICB therapy, with three reporting liver-related adverse effects. These effects were not severe and did not require discontinuation of therapy, suggesting that a history of AILD may be not an important consideration when deciding on ICB treatment for cancer patients. However, due to the limited number of monitored patients, it is necessary to clearly identify the differences and associations between AILD and liver-associated irAEs through clinical trials based on larger cohorts.

Validating PD-L1/PD-1 blockade in respiratory diseases

Currently, no drugs can effectively halt fibrosis progression in IPF. Preliminary studies have suggested that ICBs offer therapeutic benefits in IPF animal models by modulating the immune response. The clinical trial NCT05515627 focuses on evaluating atezolizumab, an anti-PD-L1 mAb, in patients with IPF. The primary objective of this trial is to establish the safety profile and tolerability of atezolizumab in patients with IPF. Secondary objectives include monitoring changes in forced vital capacity (FVC), qualitatively assessing fibrosis progression using radiographic imaging, and evaluating clinical outcomes over a 28-week period. The results of this trial will provide insights into the efficacy of IPF treatment, potentially paving the way for new therapeutic strategies for this condition.

NCT05578222 aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of pembrolizumab, an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody, as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC complicated by COPD. This study was conducted in three stages: screening, treatment, and follow-up. The patients received a fixed-dose intravenous pembrolizumab infusion and were imaged regularly. Safety evaluations, monitoring of changes in COPD conditions, and tracking of adverse events continued until remission, stabilization, or initiation of alternative antitumor therapies. This study primarily aimed to assess PFS with secondary objectives, including the overall response rate (ORR), acute exacerbation rate, and adverse reaction evaluation. Additionally, exploratory studies have investigated the correlation between PD-L1 expression and CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes in tumor tissues, COPD grade, immune cell infiltration, and treatment efficacy. This single-arm, single-center, prospective, phase II clinical study included 30 patients with clinically, radiographically, and pathologically confirmed advanced (stage IV) NSCLC combined with COPD as the primary treatment. This study is anticipated to provide valuable clinical evidence for the use of immunotherapy in NSCLC patients with COPD and guide its clinical application.

Validating PD-L1/PD-1 blockade in infectious diseases

Currently, four clinical trials are examining the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade in treating COVID-19. NCT04335305 aims to assess the combined therapy of pembrolizumab and tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 mAb) in patients with COVID-19-associated pneumonia. NCT04268537 targets lung damage in patients with severe pneumonia and lymphocytopenia by comparing the effects of thymosin (a treatment for sepsis) and PD-1 inhibitory antibodies. NCT04356508 and NCT04343144 evaluates the safety, efficacy, and SARS-CoV-2 elimination capabilities of nivolumab in these patients. Although the results are pending owing to recruitment challenges and quality control delays, these studies are critical in establishing the potential of PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors as viable treatments for COVID-19 and viral pneumonia.

The ongoing trial, NCT05769816, investigates whether blocking PD-1/PD-L1 signaling can reactivate exhausted HBV-specific CD8+ T cells and promote long-term immunomodulation in patients with chronic hepatitis B. Notably, some patients treated with IFN-α or nucleoside analogs (NAs) have experienced the loss of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg). In this study, patients received subcutaneous or intravenous anti-PD-1 mAbs biweekly or triweekly at lower doses than those typically used in cancer therapy while continuing daily oral NA therapy. This trial tracked several metrics to assess the clinical outcomes, including serum HBsAg, HBV DNA, and ALT levels measured at baseline, 48 weeks after starting treatment, and 24 weeks after the end of treatment. This study also evaluated immune responses from T, B, NK, and bone marrow cells, along with other HBV markers. The genomes were sequenced to identify viral and host genetic interactions and offer insights into new combination therapies using anti-PD-1 mAbs that could lead to a functional cure via HBsAg loss.

Conclusion and perspectives

We explored the non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways and their extended functions (Figures 1 and 2), which may explain the variability in ICB effectiveness depending on the type of cancer and individual patient. Intrinsic PD-1 signaling regulates cancer cell proliferation (Figure 1B), and nPD-L1, acting as a transcription factor, directly controls the expression of genes involved in cancer cell proliferation and antitumor immunity (Figure 1C). These non-canonical pathways play diverse roles in cancer progression but are not yet fully understood. Interestingly, the roles of intrinsic PD-1 and nPD-L1 differ depending on the cancer type, suggesting that these signaling pathways are significantly influenced by cancer cell characteristics and the tumor microenvironment. For patients in whom the non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 pathways act unfavorably toward cancer, ICB effectiveness may be reduced, potentially explaining the low response rates to ICB treatment. Therefore, when determining the overall antitumor effect of ICBs, both the T cell-mediated effects via the canonical signaling pathway (Figure 1A) and intrinsic effects on cancer cells through non-canonical pathways (Figures 1B and 1C) should be considered.

Consistently, the tumors grow rapidly and disease progresses in few patients treated with ICBs, leading to a poor prognosis. This severe outcome, known as hyperprogressive disease (HPD), refers to tumor growth acceleration following ICB therapy. The disease burden increases in some patients by more than 20–30 times after just a few (or even a single) ICB dose.128 The possibility that ICB treatment-influenced non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 pathways mediate HPD occurrence warrants further investigation. Additionally, defining the specific functions of non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 pathways based on the cancer type and environmental factors is essential. Such future studies could provide valuable diagnostic information to improve the therapeutic strategies for selecting patients for ICB therapy, thereby enhancing response rates and preventing HPD.

The existence of non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 pathways supports the complex interplay between intracellular signaling pathways in cancer cells and antitumor immunity. Indeed, the JAK-STAT3 pathway is not only essential for the proliferation and survival of cancer cells129 but also acts as a key transcriptional regulator of PD-L1 expression.7 Activated STAT3 binds with nuclear PD-L1 to promote the transcription of various inhibitory immune checkpoint proteins, thereby further suppressing antitumor immune responses (Figure 1C). Simultaneously, the PD-L1/STAT3 complex facilitates angiogenesis, which supports tumor growth and metastasis (Figure 1C). Consistent with these findings, JAK inhibitors have been shown to significantly enhance the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1-targeting ICBs.130,131,132 This enhancement is due to the inhibition of the JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway, which interferes with cancer cell survival through multiple mechanisms. First, JAK inhibitors suppress the proliferation and viability of cancer cells themselves. Second, JAK inhibitors reduce PD-L1 expression by inhibiting STAT3 activity, thereby increasing the ability of PD-1 inhibitors to activate T cells effectively. Third, by suppressing the non-canonical PD-L1 pathway mediated by STAT3, JAK inhibitors disrupt the transcriptional programs that support tumor survival and create an environment less favorable to tumor growth. These mechanisms provide a strong rationale for combining JAK inhibitors with PD-1-targeting treatments, potentially producing stronger antitumor effects than either therapy alone. Furthermore, crosstalk between PD-L1/PD-1 signaling and other oncogenic pathways, such as EGF,133,134 PI3K/AKT/mTOR,135,136 and Wnt/β-catenin137,138 has been reported. These findings underscore the need for further research to explore the potential complex interactions between other major intracellular signaling pathways in cancer cells and canonical/non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 pathways. Ultimately, such future research could lead to the development of more effective combination therapies.

Circulating biomarkers play important roles in predicting clinical outcomes and assessing treatment responses, which are essential for extending the benefits of ICB in cancer treatment. Considering their associations with membrane-bound PD-L1 levels in cancer cells, systemic immunosuppressive functions, and detectable secretion levels in serum samples, sPD-L1 and exosomal PD-L1 have significant potential as circulating markers for cancer diagnosis. The sPD-L1 and exosomal PD-L1 levels in the blood are significantly correlated with antitumor immune status and survival rates.20,21,31 Specifically, sPD-L1139,140 and exosomal PD-L1141 promote metastasis by creating an immunosuppressive niche, highlighting their value as metastasis-related markers and warranting in-depth research. As an efficacy evaluation marker for assessing response to ICB treatment, sPD-L1 appears to have limitations. Recently, Zhang et al. have found no correlation between ICB efficacy and the sPD-L1 levels in the plasma of patients with various cancer types.142 In contrast, some reports have shown a correlation between exosomal PD-L1 levels and ICB treatment efficacy; however, the results vary across different cancer types and cohorts,143,144,145 leading to a lack of consistent evidence. Thus, at this stage, exosomal PD-L1 is not a suitable clinical biomarker for predicting ICB efficacy. However, further research to validate exosomal PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker and develop companion diagnostic tests to guide treatment decisions remains valuable.

We have identified the potential value of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling in various diseases beyond cancer.41,42,78 Surprisingly, PD-L1/PD-1 signaling is activated in various pathological conditions, including degenerative (Figure 3), inflammatory CNS (Figure 4), chronic respiratory (Figure 5), and infectious diseases (Figure 6). Indeed, PD-L1/PD-1 signaling plays a critical role in their progression, as demonstrated by functional studies involving genetic or pharmacological inhibition. However, different studies using similar disease models have reported conflicting results regarding the function of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling and ICB effectiveness. These broad associations of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling with various diseases and their contradictory roles may be due to the complexity arising from the regulatory system of PD-L1 expression influenced by various factors present in diseased tissues, as well as the functional diversity of the canonical and non-canonical signaling pathways mentioned earlier.

Various diseases have chronic inflammation in common, and lesional tissues are characterized by the presence of pro-inflammatory cytokines from chronic inflammation, hypoxic conditions resulting from tissue and microvascular damage, and abundant growth factors from wound healing. These factors increase PD-L1 expression at both transcriptional and post-translational levels.7 Since PD-L1/PD-1 plays a key role in maintaining the homeostasis of cell-mediated immunity, these upstream factors may increase PD-L1 expression in lesions with active inflammation and tissue remodeling. From this perspective, it is understandable that the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway is activated in various diseases.

As discussed earlier, the non-canonical PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathway, which extends beyond balancing T cell immunity to regulate cell proliferation and various gene expression, can influence disease progression in different ways. The combination of canonical and non-canonical signaling effects differs depending on the environmental composition of the lesion and progression stage. This could explain why the role of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling and the response to ICB treatment varies by disease and stage. Therefore, PD-L1/PD-1 canonical and non-canonical signaling should be examined in a disease- and stage-specific manner and the results should be synthesized comprehensively. These follow-up studies will provide an important basis for expanding the application of ICB and PD-L1/PD-1 diagnostic technologies, initially developed for cancer research, to various diseases under the concept of drug repositioning.
