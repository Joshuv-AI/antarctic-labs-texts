Next-generation mRNA vaccines: strategies to overcome stability challenges
Authors: Abdullah Algaissi, Sivakumar S. Moni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines represent a paradigm shift in rapid vaccine development and pandemic preparedness, enabling unprecedented speed in antigen design and scalable, cell-free manufacturing. Despite proven clinical efficacy, widespread mRNA vaccine deployment remains critically constrained by fundamental molecular instability requiring ultra-cold chain storage. The chemical lability of the RNA phosphodiester backbone, 2′-hydroxyl–mediated transesterification, oxidative nucleobase damage, and lipid nanoparticle (LNP) structural vulnerabilities collectively limit long-term stability and the feasibility of global distribution. This review provides a mechanistic analysis of RNA hydrolysis, oxidative degradation, lipid peroxidation, PEG-lipid desorption, and freeze–thaw–induced nanoparticle aggregation, linking molecular instability to translational challenges in storage and supply chain logistics. We critically evaluate engineering strategies to enhance stability, including optimized LNP architectures, lyophilization with vitrifying excipients, antioxidant incorporation, lipid–polymer hybrid nanocarriers, hydrogel-based depots, self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA), and circular RNA (circRNA) constructs. Stability modeling, cold-chain risk analysis, and regulatory Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) considerations are discussed to contextualize formulation robustness within real-world distribution systems. Advancing mRNA vaccines beyond ultra-cold dependence will require coordinated innovation in RNA engineering, delivery system optimization, and scalable manufacturing to enable thermostable, resilient, and globally deployable vaccine technologies.

Introduction

The mRNA vaccines are third-generation nucleic acid-based vaccines that are a proven and validated platform in clinical trials, have been approved by regulatory agencies in record time, and are being rapidly rolled out globally to tackle COVID-19. The mRNA technology offers many advantages, such as rapid design of the vaccine antigens, potential for large-scale cell-free production, and the ability to easily adapt to emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants. These features make mRNA vaccines a versatile platform for third-generation nucleic acid-based vaccines and the next generation of innovative vaccines to protect against infectious diseases, as well as for cancer therapy. Furthermore, given their relatively short development time compared with conventional approaches and their numerous applications in modern vaccinology, mRNA vaccines will have a significant impact on global health1. Their demonstrated clinical efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 confirmed the practical viability of large-scale mRNA-based immunization and established the platform as a scalable model for rapid vaccine development against emerging pathogens. These outcomes established mRNA vaccines as a flexible platform that enables rapid antigen redesign and scalable cell-free manufacturing, with applications expanding beyond infectious diseases. However, instability results from hydrolytic and oxidative degradation, as well as enzymatic cleavage by RNases, which directly affect formulation design and storage requirements2,3.

Overcoming current limitations requires a mechanistic understanding of the molecular and formulation factors that govern mRNA vaccine stability. Instability results from intrinsic vulnerabilities of the RNA molecule, including susceptibility to hydrolytic and oxidative degradation, as well as enzymatic cleavage by ubiquitous RNases. Additionally, the physicochemical properties of the delivery system— particularly lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)—are critical, as changes in encapsulation efficiency, lipid composition, particle uniformity, or oxidative stress can compromise biological potency. Stability thus depends on preserving both RNA structural integrity and carrier system performance. A systematic understanding of these interconnected molecular and formulation-level processes is essential for the rational design of stable mRNA vaccine systems. DNA vaccines represent an alternative nucleic acid platform; however, concerns about potential genomic integration, prolonged antigen expression, and induction of anti-DNA autoantibodies have raised safety considerations that limit their widespread clinical translation6,7. These limitations underscore the importance of selecting vaccine platforms that balance immunogenic efficacy with biosafety and practical deployability. In contrast, although mRNA vaccines avoid the risks associated with genomic integration, their intrinsic physicochemical instability poses a distinct translational challenge8,9. The single-stranded RNA structure is highly susceptible to hydrolytic and enzymatic degradation, necessitating stringent temperature control during storage and distribution10,11. Consequently, maintaining a temperature-controlled supply chain has been essential to preserve structural integrity and biological potency. However, reliance on ultra-cold storage imposes significant logistical and economic constraints, particularly in resource-limited settings lacking stable infrastructure11. Even in developed regions, the need for specialized freezers, continuous temperature monitoring, and insulated transport systems increases operational complexity and cost. Interruptions in temperature control can result in rapid product degradation, compromising vaccine efficacy and causing material and financial loss. These challenges have driven ongoing efforts to develop thermostable formulations, including lyophilized and room-temperature-stable systems, to enhance distribution flexibility and global accessibility12–15.

Broader global deployment requires addressing formulation and storage constraints that limit the efficiency of distribution. The development of optimized LNP systems and biodegradable carriers has improved delivery efficiency; however, further formulation refinement is needed to enhance thermal stability16,17. Achieving this goal requires three critical elements: improved lyophilization techniques, optimized cryoprotectant systems, and thermostable vaccine formulations. Such formulation improvements may enhance distribution flexibility and facilitate more practical deployment across settings with varying infrastructure capacity. The review examines current vaccine delivery systems while stressing the need for advanced adjuvant systems to enhance both vaccine effectiveness and stability18–25.

mRNA vaccines function by delivering a synthetic RNA sequence encoding the target antigen into host cells, where cytoplasmic ribosomes translate the mRNA into the corresponding protein. Lipid nanoparticle (LNP)–mediated delivery facilitates cellular uptake and endosomal escape, releasing the mRNA into the cytosol and protecting it from premature enzymatic degradation. The expressed antigen undergoes intracellular processing and is presented via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II pathways, activating CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes and CD4⁺ helper T cells, which then support B-cell maturation and antibody production26–32. Advances in codon optimization, untranslated region (UTR) engineering, and nucleoside modification, such as pseudouridine incorporation, have improved translational efficiency and modulated innate immune sensing. However, these molecular design elements also affect RNA stability and formulation requirements, linking antigen expression efficiency directly to physicochemical robustness. Figure 2 summarizes the integrated processes of mRNA encapsulation, intracellular delivery, translation, antigen presentation, and adaptive immune activation, highlighting points where molecular instability and formulation-dependent degradation may affect vaccine performance. This review examines mRNA vaccine stabilization strategies and categorizes them by level of evidence, including clinically validated, preclinical, and exploratory approaches. It provides a comparative and critical analysis of commonly reported stability endpoints, including RNA integrity, encapsulation efficiency, particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), storage temperature, storage duration, and functional potency as indicators of formulation performance and translational feasibility. In addition, the review critically examines current formulation and delivery strategies to improve mRNA vaccine stability and evaluates the potential of emerging technologies to advance next-generation mRNA vaccine platforms16,21–34. It integrates key aspects of molecular degradation chemistry, nanoparticle physicochemistry, formulation engineering, and translational manufacturing considerations within a unified analytical framework. By linking RNA degradation pathways to delivery system design, stability modeling, and global distribution challenges, the review provides a comprehensive mechanistic and translational perspective on the development of next-generation thermostable mRNA vaccines. Results

Current challenges in mRNA vaccine stability

Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines have been proposed as a possible solution for a variety of infectious diseases. Despite promising results from small-scale clinical trials, their use is currently limited by instability and degradation under various storage and handling conditions. The single-stranded RNA structure, which is the basic element of the mRNA, is highly susceptible to hydrolysis of the phosphodiester bond, oxidation of nucleobases, and enzymatic digestion by RNase, which affects the secondary structure of the RNA and the translation of the protein (Fig. 3). mRNA vaccine stabilization strategies

The inherent instability of mRNA is primarily due to its molecular structure. Unlike DNA, mRNA has a reactive 2′-hydroxyl group in the ribose of its sugar–phosphate backbone, which enables intramolecular transesterification reactions. These reactions can cause endonucleolytic cleavage of the phosphodiester backbone, resulting in fragmentation of the RNA molecule and loss of its biological and translational function. Oxidative damage, such as nucleobase modifications, can also be induced in RNA, particularly by ROS produced by activated immune cells. Generally, ribonucleases (RNases) are found abundantly within living organisms as well as their environment. They can hydrolytically cleave RNA molecules into fragments; hence, the development of a vaccine formulation that protects mRNA (i.e., prevents its degradation) will depend on stringent handling, storage, and application in an RNase-free environment27,36. These degradation pathways collectively explain the inherent instability of mRNA vaccines and their resulting dependence on temperature-controlled storage, which remains a central barrier to large-scale global distribution (Box 1).

Vaccine development

mRNA vaccines represent a novel class of nucleic acid vaccines by enabling the expression of sequence-specified antigens through a process that does not require the growth of pathogens or the purification of proteins. Once the mRNA is delivered into cells, the host’s ribosomes translate the antigen, which is subsequently processed and presented via the MHC pathway. This will trigger specific adaptive immune responses (cellular and humoral) to the antigen. mRNA vaccines, unlike traditional inactivated protein or protein subunit vaccines, can quickly adapt to redesigned antigens and use a faster cell-free production method. These attributes result in a quicker turnaround time and a more cost-effective method for producing mRNA vaccines, enabling broader use of vaccines against infectious diseases and in cancer immunotherapy.

Challenges in mRNA vaccine storage and distribution

The mRNA’s single-stranded structure makes it highly susceptible to hydrolytic cleavage, enzymatic breakdown by ribonucleases, and a host of chemical instabilities during storage. As a result, to keep mRNA vaccines functional and maintain their molecular integrity, current formulations require storage at frozen temperatures. This requirement poses a significant barrier to large-scale distribution. In resource-limited and isolated environments, further reductions in operational efficacy, along with increased storage and distribution costs, stem from the need for a controlled cold chain for mRNA vaccines. In addition to these challenges, adverse temperature conditions during transport and storage can accelerate RNA degradation, destabilize LNPs, and ultimately diminish vaccine efficacy and shelf life.

Formulation and adjuvant innovations

The goal of achieving mRNA vaccine stability is multifaceted, as strategies target structural modifications of RNA, lyophilization with stabilizing excipients, biodegradable polymer-based carrier construction, and optimization of LNPs. These strategies complement one another by balancing and improving RNA degradation rates, LNP encapsulation stability, and shelf stability of vaccine formulations. A primary objective is creating mRNA vaccines that are stable at higher temperatures. It is vital for the continuous improvement of cold-chain logistics that antigen expression and immunogenic function are preserved.

Innovations and future directions in mRNA vaccine development

Future advancements in mRNA vaccine development will rely on the synergistic advancement of RNA engineering and novel delivery systems, along with the integration of new manufacturing systems to optimize the physicochemical stability of mRNA systems and support their worldwide distribution. Specifically, the forthcoming development of thermostable formulations, along with the design of adjustably tuned adjuvant systems and decentralized manufacturing systems, is anticipated to enhance vaccine accessibility without compromising their immunogenicity. Therefore, it will be necessary to further refine translational research to optimize packaging, immunological performance, production, and affordable distribution of mRNA vaccines across different tiers of healthcare systems.

Innovative strategies for enhancing mRNA vaccine stability

Stability of mRNA vaccine systems encompasses multiple physicochemical, functional parameters and should not be interpreted as a single, unambiguous endpoint. Depending on the formulation strategy and experimental context, stability may be attributed to preservation of the 5′ cap and 3′ poly(A) tail, maintenance of RNA integrity, reduced RNA fragmentation, encapsulation efficiency, and preservation of particle size and PDI. Furthermore, stability may also be reflected in in vitro translation efficiency, the ability to induce protective immune responses in vivo, and clinical potency. Since studies investigate distinct endpoints under varying storage conditions and analytical methodologies, direct comparisons require careful interpretation and consideration of the degree of standardization. Advances in mRNA vaccine development increasingly focus on overcoming intrinsic molecular instability and formulation-dependent degradation. Stabilization strategies include RNA sequence engineering, optimization of delivery systems, incorporation of stabilizing excipients, and development of alternative RNA architectures to improve physicochemical robustness and extend storage stability37,38. Table 2 lists the major engineering and formulation methods that enhance mRNA vaccine stability. These methods improve RNA stability and translation efficiency, though not without engineering challenges. Improvements in a physicochemical parameter need to be assessed in the context of overall vaccine performance. For example, RNA Integrity, smaller particle sizing, and increasing encapsulation efficiency may impact biological potency and immunogenicity. Thus, a comprehensive approach that combines physicochemical and functional evaluations is needed to assess vaccine performance. Further, there are minimal direct comparative studies on the relative advantages of different stabilization methods. This leaves much to be desired in terms of systematic evaluation of stabilization methods. Other translatability challenges exist, such as the ability to scale up production, obtain regulatory approvals, and perform long-term stability evaluations. These challenges are specifically applicable to advanced RNA constructs. RNA vaccine platforms differ in structural organization, molecular stability, translational behavior, and developmental maturity. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)

LNPs improve the intracellular delivery of mRNA compared to naked RNA. Still, the vaccine performance and its ability to elicit an immune response hinge on the RNA design, the components of the formulation, the quality of the manufacturing process, the storage and the delivery (Box 2). Typical LNP formulations contain four main components: an ionizable cationic lipid that complexes electrostatically with mRNA and enables pH-dependent endosomal escape; structural phospholipids that maintain membrane architecture; cholesterol that regulates membrane fluidity and stability; and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipids that improve colloidal stability and control particle size39–41. This multicomponent structure shields encapsulated mRNA from RNase-mediated degradation and reduces exposure to hydrolytic and oxidative damage during systemic circulation. The ionizable lipid plays a key mechanistic role, as its pKa allows protonation in the acidic endosomal environment, promoting membrane destabilization and facilitating cytosolic release of mRNA37,42. Despite these advantages, the long-term stability of LNP formulations can be affected by lipid oxidation, ester hydrolysis, PEG-lipid desorption, and nanoparticle aggregation, which may reduce encapsulation efficiency and compromise functional potency. Therefore, while LNPs significantly enhance the stability and delivery efficiency of mRNA compared with naked RNA, maintaining the mRNA’s physicochemical integrity remains critical to overall vaccine performance43,44.

LNPs enhance mRNA stability through several physicochemical mechanisms that protect the RNA molecule from hydrolysis and enzymatic degradation. A key component of LNP formulations is the ionizable lipid, whose pKa is typically tuned to approximately 6.0–6.5, enabling efficient encapsulation of mRNA during nanoparticle formation. Under the mildly acidic conditions used during formulation, ionizable lipids become protonated and interact electrostatically with the negatively charged phosphate backbone of mRNA, resulting in stable electrostatic complexation and efficient RNA encapsulation within the lipid matrix. After administration, the near-neutral physiological pH leaves these lipids largely unchanged, thereby reducing toxicity while maintaining nanoparticle integrity and promoting endosomal escape upon cellular uptake16–18. The stability and encapsulation efficiency of LNPs are further influenced by the nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P) ratio, which represents the molar ratio between the cationic amine groups of ionizable lipids and the phosphate groups of the RNA backbone. Optimization of the N/P ratio is critical for achieving stable nanoparticle assembly, preventing RNA leakage, and maintaining uniform particle size distribution. In addition to electrostatic interactions, the lipid matrix provides a hydrophobic microenvironment that shields mRNA from nuclease-mediated degradation during storage and distribution20,21. However, LNP stability may also be affected by lipid oxidation pathways, particularly the peroxidation of unsaturated lipid components under oxidative stress or elevated temperature. Oxidative degradation can generate reactive lipid-derived products that compromise nanoparticle integrity and reduce vaccine potency. Consequently, stabilization strategies commonly involve careful lipid composition design, incorporation of stabilizing excipients, and optimized storage conditions to minimize oxidative degradation and preserve long-term mRNA stability. Beyond LNP, emerging delivery systems such as engineered extracellular vehicles (EVs) have attracted increasing interest as next-generation vaccine carriers. EVs possess intrinsic biocompatibility, natural membrane composition, and the ability to efficiently deliver nucleic acids and proteins, making them promising platforms for mRNA vaccine delivery45. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of extracellular vesicle–based systems for mRNA therapeutics, demonstrating improved biological stability and cellular uptake compared with conventional delivery platforms46. Compared with other stabilization strategies, LNP formulations are currently the most clinically validated platform for protecting mRNA from enzymatic degradation while enabling efficient intracellular delivery, although challenges related to lipid oxidation and long-term storage stability remain.

Lyophilization

The development of lyophilization methods has improved mRNA vaccine stability during storage, enabling the creation of thermostable vaccines that minimize cold chain requirements. The combination of trehalose, sucrose, and PEG as stabilizing excipients effectively protects mRNA vaccines from hydrolytic and oxidative degradation. Scientists are currently exploring hydrogel-based delivery systems and hybrid nanoparticles composed of lipids and polymers to extend the shelf life of mRNA vaccines43. In addition, advances in molecular engineering have led to the development of self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA) and circular RNA (circRNA) platforms that offer improved stability and prolonged protein expression44,47. By reducing residual moisture and limiting hydrolytic degradation, lyophilization can improve thermal stability and reduce reliance on strict temperature-controlled storage. Optimizing freeze-drying cycles and excipient composition is critical to preserving nanoparticle integrity upon reconstitution. Control of residual moisture content and maintenance of a glassy matrix above the formulation-specific glass transition temperature (Tg) are critical for long-term stability. Insufficient drying or collapse during primary drying can compromise nanoparticle structure and reduce biological potency after reconstitution. Compared to conventional frozen storage of mRNA–LNP formulations, lyophilization offers a promising strategy to extend vaccine shelf life and reduce dependence on ultra-cold chain logistics, although optimizing cryoprotectant composition and reconstitution conditions remains essential.

Stabilizing excipients

Stabilizing excipients are essential for preserving both RNA integrity and LNP structure during storage and processing. Hydrolytic and oxidative degradation are the primary pathways of instability, so excipient selection focuses on minimizing water activity, maintaining pH stability, and limiting damage mediated by reactive oxygen species. Disaccharides such as trehalose and sucrose act as cryoprotectants by promoting vitrification and hydrogen bonding interactions that stabilize RNA and lipid bilayers during freeze-drying and storage. Trehalose and sucrose are widely used cryoprotectants in mRNA-LNP formulations because they stabilize nanoparticles during freezing and lyophilization through water-replacement and glass-transition mechanisms. During dehydration, these disaccharides form hydrogen bonds with the phosphate backbone of RNA and the polar headgroups of lipids, replacing water molecules and preventing structural collapse of the nanoparticle matrix. Additionally, trehalose forms an amorphous glassy matrix with a high glass-transition temperature (Tg), which immobilizes lipid components and reduces molecular mobility, thereby limiting hydrolytic and oxidative degradation of encapsulated mRNA during storage. Buffering agents maintain optimal pH to reduce backbone hydrolysis, while antioxidants may mitigate lipid peroxidation. PEG-containing components primarily provide colloidal stabilization by reducing nanoparticle aggregation and maintaining particle-size distribution. Rational optimization of excipient composition is essential for preserving physicochemical integrity and sustaining biological potency under defined storage conditions48,49. While excipient-based stabilization improves the physicochemical stability of mRNA–LNP formulations during storage, emerging RNA engineering strategies, such as circular RNA and self-amplifying RNA, aim to enhance intrinsic molecular stability and translation efficiency.

Hydrogels

Hydrogel-based systems might improve local stability and controlled release, but vaccine performance still depends on the integration of formulation and delivery efficacy. Hydrogel-based delivery system for mRNA in the form of a 3D crosslinked polymeric network is demonstrated. The hydrogel encapsulates mRNA or mRNA–LNP complexes to prevent degradation by enzymes and water (Fig. 5). The hydrophilic nature of the polymer matrices protects the nucleic acid cargo and provides sustained local delivery. The mesh size, crosslinking density, and polymer composition influence diffusion kinetics, enabling controlled antigen release after administration. This sustained release may prolong antigen expression and modulate immune activation without requiring immediate high-dose delivery. In addition to controlled release, hydrogels create a hydrated yet structured microenvironment that can limit RNase diffusion and reduce rapid degradation. By modulating water mobility and polymer–cargo interactions, hydrogel matrices may improve physicochemical stability during storage. However, long-term performance depends on the polymer’s biodegradability, mechanical integrity, compatibility with LNP formulations, and the reproducibility of release kinetics. Although hydrogel-based mRNA vaccine systems remain largely at the preclinical stage, ongoing investigations are evaluating their translational feasibility and scalability. Self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA)

Self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA) platforms incorporate viral replicase elements, including RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) sequences, which enable intracellular amplification of the encoded RNA following delivery. This replication mechanism enables higher antigen expression from a lower initial RNA input than conventional non-replicating mRNA formulations48,49,51. Dose-sparing effects have been observed in preclinical and early clinical studies, although the magnitude of reduction varies depending on formulation, delivery efficiency, and target antigen. While reduced input RNA may theoretically decrease manufacturing burden, overall stability remains influenced by the increased molecular length and structural complexity of saRNA constructs. The larger genome size of saRNA may introduce additional formulation challenges, including susceptibility to shear stress, altered encapsulation efficiency, and potential innate immune activation. Therefore, stability optimization of saRNA platforms requires careful integration of RNA design, LNP formulation, and immunomodulatory strategies. Although saRNA systems offer potential advantages in antigen amplification and dose reduction, further investigation is required to fully characterize their physicochemical stability, storage requirements, and translational scalability. Compared with conventional non-replicating mRNA vaccines, self-amplifying RNA and circular RNA platforms may offer improved stability and reduced dosage requirements due to their enhanced antigen expression capacity. Despite these potential advantages, challenges related to large-scale manufacturing, regulatory evaluation, and long-term clinical validation remain under active investigation.

Circular RNA (circRNA)

Circular RNA (circRNA) platforms represent an emerging approach to improving RNA stability in vaccine applications. Unlike linear mRNA, circRNA molecules lack free 5′ and 3′ ends, reducing susceptibility to exonuclease-mediated degradation51–54. This covalently closed structure may prolong intracellular persistence and support sustained antigen expression. However, efficient translation of circRNAs requires the incorporation of internal ribosome entry site (IRES) elements or alternative cap-independent translation strategies, which may influence translation efficiency. In addition, large-scale production, circularization efficiency, and purification remain technical challenges that can affect reproducibility and scalability. While the enhanced structural stability of circRNA offers potential advantages over conventional linear mRNA, overall vaccine performance remains dependent on formulation compatibility, delivery efficiency, and storage optimization. Ongoing investigations are evaluating the translational feasibility and immunogenic durability of circRNA-based vaccine systems. Programmable aptamer-embedded circular RNA (circRNA) constructs have been proposed as an emerging strategy to enhance RNA stability and enable functional regulation by incorporating ligand-responsive RNA aptamers within circularized RNA architectures. These engineered systems may also facilitate cell-type-specific targeting; however, their application is still in the early stages of development and requires further experimental validation51,52.

Nanopalm

Palm-derived lipid systems enriched with bioactive fractions such as tocotrienols and carotenoids have been investigated as alternative nanocarrier platforms for nucleic acid delivery. These structured lipid matrices, including nanoemulsions, solid LNPs, and nanostructured lipid carriers, have been explored as alternative lipid platforms that may mitigate oxidative stress–mediated degradation of encapsulated RNA52,53. These lipid matrices may provide antioxidant-rich environments that could mitigate oxidative stress–mediated degradation; however, comparative validation against established ionizable lipid systems is required. Compatibility with cryoprotectants such as trehalose and sucrose enables evaluation of freeze-dried formulations, while tolerance to high-shear and high-pressure homogenization supports scalable processing. However, palm-derived nanocarriers are still in the early stages of development for mRNA vaccine applications. Further research is needed to characterize encapsulation efficiency, long-term storage stability, oxidative resistance, and comparative performance relative to established ionizable lipid-based LNP systems53,55.

Molecular mechanisms of mRNA vaccine stabilization

The next critical aspect to consider regarding the stability of mRNA vaccines is the physicochemical molecular interactions that influence mRNA stability under various conditions and the formation and function of nanoparticles. We have already discussed formulations and delivery systems for mRNA vaccines in detail. Some relevant physicochemical molecular mechanisms include excipients that affect the stability of RNA–lipid nanostructures, surface interactions that determine the colloidal stability of nanoparticles, structural RNA design that influences mRNA stability against endonucleases, and membrane disruption events during endocytic delivery16–18,56. An important mechanism involves stabilizing mRNA–lipid nanoparticle systems during freezing and dehydration with vitrifying excipients such as trehalose and sucrose. At the molecular level, these disaccharides interact with polar functional groups in both RNA and lipid headgroups through hydrogen bonding, replacing water molecules removed during dehydration. This water-replacement interaction preserves the structural integrity of the RNA phosphate backbone and lipid bilayer organization. Additionally, trehalose forms an amorphous glassy matrix with a high glass-transition temperature (Tg), which immobilizes molecular components within the formulation and significantly reduces molecular mobility. Reduced molecular mobility limits hydrolytic reactions, oxidative degradation, and structural rearrangements within LNPs, thereby preserving both RNA integrity and nanoparticle architecture during storage44,50.

The colloidal stability of LNPs is also affected by surface polymer interactions, especially those involving PEG-lipids incorporated into the nanoparticle shell. PEG chains extend into the surrounding aqueous environment, forming a hydrated steric barrier that prevents close contact between neighboring particles. This steric stabilization increases interparticle spacing and reduces van der Waals attractions that can cause nanoparticle aggregation. Additionally, the hydrated PEG layer contributes to repulsive interactions that help maintain a uniform particle size distribution and improve colloidal stability during storage and transport. Preserving this nanoscale dispersion is essential because aggregation can decrease encapsulation efficiency and compromise intracellular delivery performance16–20. Structural modification of RNA molecules is another mechanism for improving stability. Circular RNA (circRNA) constructs form covalently closed loop structures that lack free 5′ and 3′ ends, which are typical initiation sites for exonuclease-mediated degradation. The absence of these terminal regions significantly reduces susceptibility to enzymatic cleavage and increases RNA persistence within intracellular environments. Although the translation of circRNAs requires specialized regulatory elements such as internal ribosome entry sites, their enhanced structural resistance to exonuclease degradation offers a promising strategy for extending antigen expression in RNA vaccine platforms51–53.

Efficient intracellular delivery of mRNA relies on endosomal escape mechanisms mediated by ionizable lipids in LNP formulations. These lipids are designed to remain largely neutral at physiological pH but become protonated under the acidic conditions within endosomes after cellular uptake. Protonation increases electrostatic interactions between the LNPs and the negatively charged phospholipids of the endosomal membrane, promoting membrane destabilization and the formation of non-bilayer lipid phases. These structural changes facilitate disruption of the endosomal membrane and release of encapsulated mRNA into the cytoplasm, where ribosomal translation occurs. Efficient endosomal escape is therefore a critical mechanistic step linking nanoparticle formulation design to effective antigen expression and vaccine potency16–20. Discussion

Future development of mRNA vaccines will depend on coordinated advances in molecular stability, delivery optimization, and scalable manufacturing. Although current formulations have demonstrated clinical effectiveness, limitations in thermal stability, storage requirements, and long-term robustness remain key translational challenges. Development of thermostable formulations—including optimized lyophilized systems and excipient-engineered compositions—may reduce reliance on strict temperature-controlled logistics and improve distribution flexibility. However, achieving room-temperature stability while preserving biological potency requires continued formulation refinement and rigorous validation. Beyond conventional LNP systems, alternative delivery platforms—including polymer–lipid hybrid carriers, hydrogel depots, and structured lipid matrices are under investigation to enhance physicochemical stability and controlled antigen expression. Advances in RNA engineering, such as self-amplifying RNA and circular RNA constructs, may improve persistence and dose efficiency, although scalability, reproducibility, and regulatory standardization remain critical considerations. In addition to infectious disease prevention, mRNA-based technologies are being evaluated for broader therapeutic applications. Personalized cancer vaccines targeting tumor-specific neoantigens are undergoing clinical investigation to improve the precision of immunotherapy. Similarly, mRNA platforms are being explored in autoimmune conditions, where controlled expression of tolerogenic or immunomodulatory proteins may help rebalance dysregulated immune responses in diseases such as multiple sclerosis and type 1 diabetes. Applications in rare genetic disorders are also under study, particularly for the transient expression of therapeutic proteins or genome-editing components. While these approaches show significant promise, long-term safety, expression durability, and manufacturing scalability require continued evaluation.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) approaches are increasingly utilized to streamline mRNA vaccine development and design. Multiple studies have shown improvement in translating efficiency and antigen expression through AI-optimized UTRs, codon selection, RNA secondary structure, and composition. AI also helps personalize vaccines through epitope prediction, neoantigen identification, and prioritized sequencing. Other proposed applications in this field, such as predictive modeling for LNP formulation, predicting stability, and designing an urgent-use, fast-response platform for pandemic infections, still require considerable experimental work for validation. AI has the potential to make a real impact on optimizing vaccine development, but its value will ultimately come from the experiments and translational work that accompany it57–60.

Rapid antigen redesign capability and cell-free manufacturing provide important advantages for pandemic responsiveness. However, future preparedness will depend not only on platform flexibility but also on resilient production networks, robust supply chains, and harmonized regulatory pathways. In parallel, robust chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) strategies, including validated analytical assays, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and defined stability-indicating parameters, are essential for ensuring regulatory compliance and long-term product consistency. Integration of RNA sequence optimization, advanced delivery engineering, and decentralized manufacturing strategies will be essential to enhance translational resilience and equitable deployment. Ensuring equitable global access remains a central challenge. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted disparities in vaccine distribution, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where infrastructure limitations, production concentration, and cost barriers restrict availability27,61–63. Strengthening regional manufacturing capacity, supporting technology transfer initiatives, and establishing standardized quality-control frameworks are critical for decentralizing production. Scalable synthetic biology platforms and process optimization may reduce production costs, but sustained international collaboration and policy coordination will be necessary to ensure preparedness for future outbreaks64–67. Continued innovation in RNA design, formulation science, delivery engineering, and global manufacturing infrastructure will collectively determine whether mRNA platforms can transition from pandemic-emergency tools to broadly deployable, stable, and accessible vaccine technologies. Robust evaluation of mRNA vaccine stability requires validated, stability-indicating analytical methods capable of detecting both molecular degradation and structural changes in nanoparticles. RNA integrity is typically assessed using capillary electrophoresis, agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE), and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-based profiling to detect fragmentation or truncation. Encapsulation efficiency and particle size distribution are routinely monitored using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and fluorescence-based quantification, while lipid oxidation products are evaluated through chromatographic techniques. For lyophilized formulations, residual moisture content and Tg are critical parameters influencing hydrolytic stability. Establishing harmonized analytical standards and validated in-use stability testing protocols is essential for regulatory qualification and comparative platform assessment across emerging mRNA technologies.

mRNA vaccine platforms have demonstrated clinical feasibility; however, stability limitations and distribution constraints remain significant translational barriers. Addressing these challenges requires integrated formulation strategies, including optimized LNPs, lyophilization, rational excipient selection, hydrogel-based delivery systems, circular RNA constructs, and self-amplifying RNA technologies to enhance physicochemical robustness and distribution flexibility. Beyond infectious disease prevention, mRNA platforms are advancing into cancer immunotherapy and precision medicine, where controlled antigen or protein expression further expands their therapeutic potential. Sustained progress will depend on the interdisciplinary integration of RNA engineering, delivery science, process optimization, and regulatory harmonization to improve thermal stability and reduce reliance on ultra-cold logistics. Achieving durable mRNA vaccine stability requires a unified translational framework that integrates molecular RNA design, rational lipid chemistry, excipient engineering, scalable manufacturing control, and predictive stability modeling. Harmonized, head-to-head comparative evaluations under clinically relevant stress conditions are critical for rigorously validating emerging platforms and accelerating the development of thermostable formulations. Integrating mechanistic insights into RNA stability with real-world distribution constraints is essential to ensure robust performance, scalability, and global deployability.

Continued advances in RNA engineering and nanoparticle design are expected to accelerate the development of thermostable mRNA platforms that enable broader global vaccine deployment. Currently, formulation-based approaches, such as LNPs and lyophilization, represent the most advanced strategies for enhancing mRNA vaccine stability. In parallel, emerging RNA architectures and biomaterial-based delivery systems offer promising avenues for developing next-generation thermostable vaccine platforms. Future improvements in mRNA vaccine stability will likely depend on coordinated advances in RNA sequence engineering, delivery system optimization, and scalable manufacturing technologies that together enable robust formulations suitable for global vaccine distribution.

Methods

The stability and translational performance of mRNA vaccines are governed by complex interactions among RNA molecular integrity, physicochemical properties of LNP delivery systems, and manufacturing process consistency. As complex biologic formulations, mRNA vaccines are sensitive to a variety of processes throughout their life cycle that can affect their stability, and they are also influenced by vaccine manufacturing processes. To ensure the integrity of mRNA vaccine products and assess their translational efficiency, these vaccines must undergo extensive analytical characterization throughout their life cycle. Importantly, vaccine stability cannot be quantified by a single, universal measure. Each study assesses specific physicochemical and functional parameters under varying experimental conditions. Some of these parameters include RNA integrity, encapsulation efficiency, LNP size and PDI, translational efficiency, and biological activity. Following a comprehensive literature-based approach, this narrative review aims to identify and critique the literature on the stability and formulation of mRNA vaccines. Comprehensive searches of scientific databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, were performed using keywords, including, but not limited to, “mRNA vaccine stability,” “cold-chain logistics,” and “LNP”. Priority was given to recent peer-reviewed primary studies, clinically relevant research, and review research published in the last decade. Peer-reviewed primary experimental studies of historical significance and foundational studies were included. Studies of molecular degradation, formulation and delivery, stability-indicating analytics and efficacy, and manufacturing considerations were critically assessed to provide a detailed summary. A major emphasis was placed on articulating the essential differences between clinically validated and exploratory/preclinical strategies, and on integrating mechanistic evidence with translational relevance.

RNA integrity and stability monitoring

Hydrolysis of the phosphodiester backbone of RNA molecules can reduce antigen presentation. The integrity of mRNA can be confirmed using AGE and capillary gel electrophoresis to analyze breaks in the phosphodiester backbone due to hydrolysis and enzymatic digestion, as well as by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) to analyze sequence-related degradation events occurring during storage and processing of RNA vaccines. These analyzes are often performed during vaccine formulation development and stability studies51,68–70.

Characterization of lipid nanoparticle systems

The stability of the delivery system, in this case the LNP, is as important as the biological activity of the mRNA. The LNP protects the mRNA from enzymatic degradation and enables its uptake into cells. Most data on the physical characteristics of nanoparticles (e.g., number-average diameter, PDI, zeta potential) are obtained from DLS and electrophoretic mobility analysis (zeta potential). These parameters affect colloidal stability and may influence the potential to form aggregates over time. The morphology of the nanoparticles is assessed by TEM or cryo-TEM to confirm structural integrity and encapsulation homogeneity. Encapsulation efficiency is confirmed using fluorescence assays such as the RiboGreen RNA assay, which compares the relative fluorescence of RNA enclosed in the nanoparticles to that of free RNA. These assays allow assessment of nanoparticle stability after several weeks at 4 °C and, at any time, the identification of potential degradation or RNA release before in vivo injection of the nanoparticles71–77.

Stability assessment of lyophilized formulations

Other parameters must be investigated to ensure long-term stability of the frozen lyophilized product. Moisture content of the powder is measured by Karl Fischer titration. It is necessary to balance the risk of rapid hydrolysis, which can be problematic during freeze-drying. DSC is used to determine the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the excipient matrix. This temperature should be higher than the storage temperature to prevent collapse or other physical changes in the final lyophilized product78,79.

Lipid excipient stability

While stabilizing the physical properties of nanoparticles, lipid components can undergo oxidative or hydrolytic degradation and therefore require stability-indicating analytical tests to detect degradation products. Lipid oxidation products and hydrolysis degradation intermediates in the nanoparticles can be detected using HPLC and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)80,81.

Manufacturing process control and stability considerations

The manufacturing process impacts mRNA stability and function. mRNA produced for therapeutic applications is often generated through in vitro transcription (IVT) using bacteriophage RNA polymerases driven by promoters such as T7 or SP6. This method is relatively fast and easily accommodates changes to the mRNA sequence. However, it also yields undesirable species such as IVT contaminants (short lengths, mis-modified or aberrant nucleotides, or sequence mutations) and large amounts of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), which can induce innate immunity through activation of pattern recognition receptors and are known to inhibit translation8,16,82,83. Following transcription, purification via chromatography is required to remove unincorporated DNA template, enzymes, and contaminants such as double-stranded RNA. A purified RNA product yields a more homogeneous product, helps prevent RNA degradation, and leads to a more stable and effective vaccine, resulting in higher translation yields84–86. LNP assembly typically involves mixing a lipid solution in ethanol with an aqueous mRNA solution. Microfluidic mixing provides precise control over parameters such as flow rates, shear, and charge interactions between lipids and mRNA. These mixing parameters directly influence nanoparticle properties such as mean diameter, encapsulation efficiency (EE%), and PDI. The final properties of the nanoparticles affect the stability of the formulated product and, consequently, the stability of the final vaccine product6,17,87. After the formulation step, additional post-formulation steps such as buffer exchange, sterile filtration, filling, and storage preparation may also cause shear damage to the RNA and destabilization of the nanoparticles. Furthermore, certain freezing and lyophilization conditions can compromise the stability of LNPs, leading to lipid phase separation, nanoparticle aggregation, and reduced RNA encapsulation efficiency. Therefore, it is critical to optimize the composition of cryoprotectants and the freeze–thaw cycle conditions to ensure RNA stability and nanoparticle integrity during long-term storage88,89.

Integrated quality control framework

An integrated analytical strategy must be employed to ensure product stability throughout the product life cycle. This can be achieved by combining molecular characterization, nanoparticle analysis, process analysis, and biological activity assays. Stability-indicating analytical techniques such as capillary electrophoresis, RT-qPCR, DLS, and chromatographic lipid analysis can be used to detect any degradation that may occur during manufacture and storage. Additionally, biological potency assays, such as cell-based translation assays, are used to confirm that encapsulated mRNA remains capable of being translated into the antigen of interest after storage. This confirms the vaccine’s potency and complements physicochemical characterization methods83,84. Analytical testing, process optimization, and stability studies are key components in developing a robust CMC document for mRNA vaccines. To satisfy regulatory bodies and ensure consistent vaccine manufacturing, all these aspects are required.
