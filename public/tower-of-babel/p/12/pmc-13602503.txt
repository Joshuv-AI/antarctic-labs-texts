Primary health care transformation and health equity in ageing societies: lessons from Singapore
Authors: Zhiying Lim, Lian Leng Low, Chien Earn Lee, Huso Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation ageing challenges the sustainability of universal health coverage (UHC), underscoring the need to transform primary health care (PHC). PHC adopts a whole-of-society approach to equitably address health needs across the care continuum in people’s everyday environments. Public policy is central to this transformation, aligning public health and primary care (PC) through governance, leadership, multisectoral collaboration, information systems, and sustainable financing. However, PHC research has mainly focused on clinical services, with little attention to policy analysis.MethodsThis study examined the evolution of PHC policies in Singapore over the past four decades using the World Health Organization’s Operational Framework for PHC and identified equity-oriented policy strategies relevant to other ageing societies.ResultsSignificant progress was made in the pillar of integrated health service delivery, whereas the pillars of empowerment of people and communities and multisectoral policy and action remain works in progress. The government plays a central role in policy development, resource stewardship, and budgetary commitments, accelerating reforms over the past decade. The recent policy shift towards place-based care is important for addressing the wider social determinants of health. To advance health equity, two key issues must be addressed: (1) the ‘multisectoral policy and action’ pillar, by strengthening local governance frameworks to coordinate the increasing involvement of multiple policy actors in PHC implementation and aligning financial models across sectors, and (2) the ‘empowered people and communities’ pillar, by embedding community participatory and asset-based approaches.ConclusionsAs many countries face the dual challenges of an ageing population and rising healthcare costs, there is an urgent need to better integrate health service delivery. This policy analysis highlights the importance of developing strategies that support multisectoral collaboration and empower communities. Introduction

The growing ageing population challenges the sustainability of universal health coverage (UHC), raising healthcare demand and fiscal pressure. Older adults with multimorbidity face higher health expenditures – an indicator of UHC – than the general population, potentially limiting their healthcare access [1]. Health policies designed for the general population often fail to address the complex needs of older adults, including multiple chronic conditions, diminished intrinsic capacity, and geriatric syndromes such as frailty; these issues emphasise the importance of health equity and the right to healthcare for older adults [2, 3]. Subsequently, increasing demand for manpower further strains UHC sustainability [4].

Transforming primary health care (PHC) is crucial to maintaining UHC sustainability in an ageing population. The 1978 Alma-Ata Conference and Declaration, a landmark in the development of PHC, expanded the approach to improving health by shifting the focus from biomedical orientation to recognising health as fundamental human right and emphasising equity and community empowerment. PHC adopts a whole-of-society approach to equitably improve population health by addressing needs early along the care continuum – from health promotion to palliative care – within people’s everyday environment [3]. PHC focuses beyond the health sector, while primary care (PC) mainly supports first contact, accessible and coordinated service delivery to promote care continuity, building on trusted relationships and individual-level empowerment [5].

Public policy plays a vital role in transforming PHC to meet the unmet needs of older adults in a person-centred, holistic way as it addresses social determinants of health and integrates public health with PC by aligning leadership, establishing shared goals, shaping governance, facilitating information exchange, fostering multisectoral collaboration and ensuring adequate resource financing [6, 7]. Misaligned policies cause conflicting priorities across sectors, improper resource allocations, and hinder coordination efforts [6]. Despite the importance of understanding the role of policy and health systems in PHC transformation, most research on PHC has disproportionately focused on clinical aspects and health service delivery, with limited attention to policy analysis [8, 9]. In the study, we critically evaluated the development of PHC policies in Singapore over the past 40 years in response to its (super-) ageing population, using the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Operational Framework for PHC [3]. The framework describes the components of a comprehensive PHC approach based on three pillars: integrated health services with an emphasis on primary care and public health functions; multisectoral policy and action; and empowered people and communities. It also delineates how these PHC components promote health, equity, and efficiency (see Fig. 1). We expanded our discussion of effective strategies for transforming PHC, particularly to promote health equity in PHC in other countries facing (super-) ageing populations.

WHO’s operational framework for primary health care

Case study: Singapore

According to the WHO UHC report, Singapore has one of the highest UHC coverage rates, with a score above 80 [10]. The country faces an ageing population, with 19.9% of residents aged 65 and over in 2024, expected to reach 22% by 2030, classifying it as a super-aged society. In a hybrid healthcare system, about 2,000 private general practitioner (GP) clinics (80%) and 26 polyclinics (20%) provide PC services. The government plays a key role in inpatient care, managing 80% of hospital beds and 50% of community hospital beds to contain costs. Singapore’s mixed healthcare financing system, known as “S+3 Ms,” includes (1) subsidies to keep healthcare affordable; (2) MediSave, a mandatory national medical savings account for hospitalisation costs; (3) MediShield Life, a low-cost catastrophic insurance scheme for major illnesses; and (4) MediFund, an endowment fund assisting those unable to pay after using MediSave and MediShield Life.

The social care sector comprises social service offices (SSOs) and social service agencies (SSAs). The Ministry of Social and Family Development (MSF) delivers decentralised social assistance to low-income individuals and families through 24 SSOs, which manage local planning and coordinate services. The National Council of Social Services (NCSS), MSF’s statutory board, oversees over 500 SSAs providing services in five areas: disability and special needs, mental health, seniors, children and youth, and families. Most SSAs rely primarily on government funding and donations. Additionally, community care organisations (CCOs), funded by both public and private sources, provide intermediate and long-term care, including centre-based, home-based, and nursing home services.

Singapore’s health and social care systems emphasise collaboration between the family, the community, and the state in financing and delivering health and social care. The government regulates and oversees health and social development, providing basic needs such as healthcare, education, and housing. The state is regarded as the last resort when family and community resources have been exhausted. However, the social compact is shifting, with the government assuming greater responsibility over the years as Singapore faces an ageing population and reduced social support. Such a shift requires a more effective transformation of PHCs to optimise the use of government resources.

Methods

Readying materials: Search strategy

We adopted Dalglish’s READ document analysis [11]. We conducted a policy analysis using publicly available documents from nine ministries and their associated statutory boards from 1985 to 22 May 2026; the ministries included MOH, MSF, Ministry of Manpower (MOM), Ministry of Finance (MOF), Ministry of National Development (MND), Ministry of Culture, Community and Youth (MCCY), Ministry of Transport (MOT), Ministry of Sustainability and Environment (MSE), and Ministry of Digital Development and Transformation (MDDT). First author (LZY) searched ministry websites and National Library Board Archive Online for relevant documents using the search terms: “primary healthcare” OR “primary care” OR “community care” OR “ageing population” OR “social determinants” OR “financial protection” OR “financial support” OR “financial assistance” OR “marginalised populations” OR “vulnerable populations”. Relevancy was determined by: (1) accessibility: documents publicly accessible; (2) nature: policy, legislation, strategic plans, reports (e.g., budget report, committee report, or annual yearbook), guidelines, speeches, press releases; (3) source: documents developed, authored, released, or commissioned by government departments; and (4) policy focus: older adults aged 60 years and above. Commentary or analysis not written by the government department representative was excluded.

Readying materials: screening and selection of policy documents

LZY screened policy documents by title against the eligibility criteria. Policy documents that met the eligibility criteria were included. Policy documents whose titles were potentially relevant were also included at this stage. Additionally, policy documents primarily targeting the general population were included if their provisions were relevant and had implications for older adults. Subsequently, we assessed the documents identified as potentially relevant against the eligibility criteria. In cases of uncertainty, LZY discussed the matter with the last author (HY) until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction

We extracted key information using an Excel spreadsheet containing title, publication year, composition of authors or committee members, source, type, production context and objectives. The documents were organised chronologically to trace the evolution of policy development and subsequently reorganised several times to gain deeper familiarity with how PHC policies relate to ageing care. Analytic reflections were recorded in memos throughout the process. Iterative searching was undertaken when policy components or source materials appeared incomplete. This occurred most frequently with policy levers related to purchasing and payment systems, and with monitoring and evaluation, where publicly available information was limited. Despite this limitation, sources were selected to cover the principal policies relevant to this policy review. Given the heterogeneity of the source materials, this review synthesises policy-relevant statements from reports, speeches, and press releases, treating them as policy texts rather than treating formal document types as the unit of analysis. Guided by the WHO Operational Framework for PHC, the data were then extracted and mapped to the four strategic and ten operational levers across three pillars [3].

Analysis and distilling findings

We categorised the findings around the WHO’s three key pillars and analysed their implications for health equity. According to the WHO, we defined health equity as the absence of systematic or potentially remediable differences in health status, access to health care and health-enhancing environments, and treatments across populations or social groups defined by social, economic, demographic or geographic factors [3]. This refers to how policy reforms across the three pillars influence equity in terms of accessibility, affordability, acceptability, utilisation, quality, financial protection and coverage [12]. We further drew on literature on integrated care [13], community empowerment [14] and intersectoral collaboration [15] to understand how the three pillars relate to the dimensions of equity.

WHO PHC pillar 1: integrated health services emphasising primary and essential public health functions

This pillar focuses on PC and public health functions to meet the people’s health needs across the life course. According to WHO’s principles of people-centred integrated care, five components are needed: empowering people and communities, reorienting service models, coordinating within and across sectors and providing an enabling environment to achieve integrated, people-centred, equitable health services [13].

At the strategic level, Singapore’s steady, albeit incremental, progress in integrating PC and public health, especially in the last decade, has been underpinned by sustained political commitments, long-term strategic planning and substantial public investments. Since 1998, inter-ministerial committees on ageing have produced strategic documents (e.g., the Action Plan for Successful Ageing) every 5 to 10 years, charting national directions for the care of the ageing population. This continuity was facilitated by a centralised governance structure, prolonged political stability and high levels of public trust, which collectively enable coherent policy development and consistent implementation [16, 17]. Coherent policy development and consistent implementation establish an enabling environment for progressive primary health care reform.

A major shift occurred with the expansion of government stewardship in community care. The early hand-off approach transitioned towards active governance when MOH appointed the Agency for Integrated Care (AIC) as the national healthcare integrator in 2009 and expanded its mandate to coordinate and implement aged care services in 2018, actively overseeing the development of primary and community health-social care and reorienting the models of care towards community-based ones [18]. This was followed by a decade of transferring older adults’ care functions from MSF to MOH in 2013 and 2018, further consolidating governance, reducing fragmentation, and strengthening alignment across health and social care domains [19, 20]. Notably, public investment for PHC has also increased significantly. Public health expenditure increased from 0.9% of GDP in 2006 to 3.2% in 2020, with total spending rising sixfold from $2 million to $15.2 million [21]. This funding enabled the expansion of health and social infrastructure, improving care delivery and strengthening the PHC workforce. The $3.5 billion commitment in 2024 to implement the Age Well Strategy underscored its long-term community-based focus on prevention and integration [22]. Age Well Strategy, a national initiative led by MOH, MOT, and MND, aimed to anchor ageing in the community by encouraging active ageing, enhancing care coordination for those who require care, and improving the built environment. While these policies are still a work in progress, the increasing centralised governance in community care development is critical to addressing capacity (e.g., physical infrastructure and workforce) and fiscal gaps. Central oversight is also necessary to ensure that resources are equitably distributed across the acute and community care sectors to support integrated care development at the operational level, although this remains in progress.

At the operational level, Singapore has trialled various care models to support older adults in the community, thereby promoting both horizontal and vertical integration. These include embedding nurses within community organisations such as active ageing centres and religious groups, bringing care closer to where the older adults live and socialise. Locating healthcare providers in the community reduces geographical and organisational barriers to access for older adults, and through co-location, facilitates relationship-building among service providers, thereby enabling more coordinated and continuous care.

In parallel, to meet the demands and complexity of the ageing population, Singapore has also introduced various PHC workforce expansion schemes (e.g., salary revisions, mid-career conversions) to attract professionals to the public and community sectors, while partnering with higher learning institutions to strengthen the expertise of the medical and nursing fraternity in gerontology and palliative care. Emerging interprofessional education models further seek to overcome siloed practice by cultivating shared competencies and collaborative care processes across disciplines. Collectively, these workforce-specific policy changes underpin the expansion and operationalisation of integrated care models, a central strategy to reduce care fragmentation for an ageing population living with multimorbidity and complex health and social needs.

Two developments have been significant. First, the decentralisation and reorganisation of services into place-based care have improved accessibility and responsiveness to local needs. The Healthcare 2020 masterplan, introduced in 2012, reorganised the healthcare system into regional health systems, enabling acute and community care organisations within defined geographic areas to collaborate in delivering patient-centred care. Subsequent reforms, articulated in the Beyond Healthcare 2020 vision, further shifted its focus from hospital to community care. In parallel, SSOs and Silver Generation Offices were decentralised to improve the reach, enhance accessibility and responsiveness to the local needs of vulnerable and older populations. In primary care, private PC clinics were also organised into primary care networks (PCNs), enabling resource sharing, coordinated care and cross-sharing of best practice. These collective decentralisation and place-based care efforts have laid the foundation for Healthier SG, a whole-of-health system transformation in 2023, which further integrates primary and secondary prevention with PC. Within this architecture, PCNs play a dedicated role in preventive health, while the three healthcare clusters serve as regional- and population-level health managers, responsible for planning and coordinating services within their geographic regions. The care coordination was further reinforced when CCOs were organised into 84 sub-regions and evolved into the Integrated Community Care Provider (ICCP) model. By bringing care closer to the community and strengthening the stewardship and coordination role of regional clusters, the progressive decentralisation of service delivery seeks to address unmet community needs, deepen contextual knowledge of local communities, and improve continuity and coordination of care. These developments collectively advance integrated, place-based care, with the potential to enhance equity of care by reducing geographical and organisational barriers and enabling more tailored responses to the needs of underserved neighbourhoods and population groups.

The second significant development is the reorientation of financing from tertiary-centric to community-centric. MediSave scheme was progressively liberalised to cover chronic disease management, outpatient care, and home care (the latter refers to MediSave Care) within specified limits. Intermediate and Long-Term Care (ILTC) financing was also restructured through the replacement of ElderShield scheme with Careshield Life in 2020 – a universal plan that offers lifelong coverage for individuals with moderate to severe disabilities. Targeted policies that aimed to support low-income seniors have generally expanded to include financial security, long-term care, and healthcare expenses. These include Silver Support Scheme for low-income older adults with limited mandatory national retirement funds, ElderFund for seniors who exhausted their care support and MediSave balance and MediFund Silver to support those with were unable to copay public healthcare and community services. MediFund Silver also gradually extended to ILTC facilities (2001, 2012) and public primary care (2013) [23]. The Home Caregiving Grant also provides means-tested payouts for families caring for Singaporeans with moderate to severe disabilities. Together, these reforms reflect a deliberate shift towards a financing model that supports ageing-in-place and reduces the financial burden associated with family care and ILTC (see the Supplementary Materials 2 for more details on the financial schemes and support available).

Structural gaps and remaining challenges

Despite these advances, two gaps remain. First, inequity persists among low- to middle-income older adults with disabilities. While they may qualify for MediSave Care, which allows monthly withdrawals of up to $200, they may not qualify for ElderFund except through case-by-case appeals. As a result, they may be placed at a relative disadvantage compared with lower-income individuals who receive more financial assistance (see the Supplementary Materials 3 for illustrations). This cost disparity is particularly consequential, given that caregiving costs rise with increasing disabilities severity [24]. In Singapore, where caregiving is strongly shaped by cultural norms of filial piety and the state’s longstanding principle of ‘family as first line of support’, such inequitable financial provision may place low-to-middle-income households under considerable financial strain, thereby undermining the capacity of older adults and their caregivers to sustain ageing in place.

Beyond financial inequity, systemic capacity for research, monitoring and evaluation remains work in progress. Although ageing- and palliative-related national grants are available, research support is considerably stronger in the health sector than in the social sector. Healthcare clusters linked with medical schools to form academic medical centres, while SSAs rely on their internal resources or volunteer research consultants facilitated by NCSS. While more community-oriented indicators (e.g., healthier eating habits, sustained enrolment rate with a family doctor) are gradually incorporated into monitoring and evaluation frameworks, few policy documents – aside from the Service Requirement Guidelines, MOH’s White Paper on Healthier SG, National Mental Health and Well-Being Strategy 2023, the 2023 National Strategy for Palliative Care – address monitoring and evaluation [25–28]. There is no explicit reference to how national strategies, such as Age Well, are monitored, evaluated, or aligned with ministry-level indicators and across ministries. Cross-sector key performance indicators (KPIs) that integrate health and social outcomes could enable holistic monitoring and evaluation across the ecosystem.

WHO PHC pillar 2: empowerment of people and communities

This pillar emphasises people and communities taking control to maximise their health and meet their needs through active participation in self-care, policy engagement, and co-design of information diffusion, decision-making, and service delivery. As Laverack & Labonté’s Community Empowerment framework holds, this pillar extends beyond individual involvement to encompass the development of community capacities and active participation, leadership, organisational structures, problem assessment, critical awareness, resource mobilisation, partnerships and collective management [14].

At the strategic level, Singapore has integrated policy feedback and stakeholder dialogues into its policy cycle to increase citizen engagement. Additionally, statutory boards such as AIC, NCSS and the People’s Association (PA) have incorporated the person-centred principle into their service delivery. These mechanisms suggest a governance model that partly recognises older adults and communities as legitimate actors rather than passive recipients of care. They reflect efforts to widen participation and strengthen the link between communities and formal institutions. However, these mechanisms appear largely consultative and state-led, with control remaining in state hands.

At the operational level, community members are empowered through three main strategies: improving information flow, embedding person-centred care in service delivery, and fostering contribution. Efforts have been made to improve information flow. The Silver Generation Ambassadors, a community outreach programme under the AIC, conduct home visits to disseminate information on government policies and schemes, raise awareness of ageing-related programmes, and connect older adults with relevant services [29]. The eldercare centres are restructured to function as a one-stop hub under the Age Well Strategy, further promoting information dissemination and community engagement linkage [22]. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated digitalisation, prompting additional measures to improve digital health literacy among older adults. Initiatives such as ServiceSG centres provide older adults with guidance on accessing government services, while the Seniors Go Digital programme enhances their digital competencies [30, 31]. Parallel developments in telehealth have increased access to personal health information and activity monitoring, enabling older adults to exercise greater ownership and self-management of their health and well-being.

Individual empowerment often occurs at the service delivery level. AIC requires service providers to engage users individually in intervention planning, emphasising personalised care plans based on autonomy, preferences, and strengths. This supports individual agency, primarily at the level of one’s own care. There is a subtle shift to empower older adults collectively. NCSS under MSF promotes broader user involvement across programme development and service delivery, embedding participation as a core social service principle [32]. Similarly, PA – Active Ageing Council collaborates with its Senior Citizens’ Consultative Committee and works with community members to co-develop and implement active ageing initiatives [30]. These examples move beyond individual choice toward shared planning and community input; therefore, they more closely reflect community empowerment.

Community empowerment is also supported by the development of organisational structures and opportunities for resource mobilisation. The Council for the Third Age, established in 2007 and later merged with AIC in 2025, has focused on encouraging senior volunteerism. The formation of citizen volunteer committees provides a platform for older adults to collaborate, build cohesion and contribute to community initiatives. Dedicated funding streams, such as the Silver Volunteer Fund and SG Partnership Fund, support organisations in creating volunteering opportunities for older adults and enable older adults to initiate programmes. Other statutory boards, such as AIC, HPB, and the Infocomm Media Development Authority, have also introduced their respective volunteering programmes across various sectors. These structures and funding arrangements are important because they create the institutional and material conditions for participation.

Structural gaps and remaining challenges

However, improved knowledge access does not automatically translate into empowerment unless communities are able to collectively use information, identify, interpret and act to influence decisions and address shared problems. Although the adoption of digital tools and technology has the potential to expand older adults’ access to health information and services, the benefits are likely to accrue disproportionately to those with higher levels of education, socio-economic status and digital literacy. Despite the implementation of Seniors Go Digital initiative, the use of mobile phones for functional purposes, including telehealth, remain low among those with lower educational attainment, limited income and lower digital literacy [33, 34]. This is particularly concerning because these groups, especially those who are non-ambulant, may stand to benefit most from telehealth to overcome mobility and transportation limitations. These populations continue to face multiple structural barriers to digital inclusion, including the predominance of English on many mobile applications, non-intuitive service interfaces, the cost of maintaining up-to-date devices, and heightened vulnerability to fraud and scams [35]. Without addressing these structural barriers, they contribute to forms of digital exclusion that may further marginalise already disadvantaged groups and intersecting vulnerabilities may exacerbate existing health inequities among older adults. Thus, empowerment is not achieved solely through information provision or behavioural change, but through strengthening critical awareness, the social, organisational and material conditions that enable communities to exercise control.

Existing strategies – such as involvement in one’s own care planning, social participation, and volunteerism – are used to promote individual empowerment. They are necessary but insufficient to address inequitable access and participation, whose roots lie in structural issues [36]. To address structural issues, community empowerment requires intentional efforts to build social cohesion, communication skills, local leadership, problem-solving capacity, and resource mobilisation among community members [36]. Yet policy strategies to date have mainly focused on individual behaviours and service participation rather than fostering collective agency. While efforts have been made to encourage community participation and to facilitate bottom-up initiatives, it is also critical to consider the characteristics of participation. Previous studies have shown that males, individuals from minority ethnic groups and those of lower socio-economic status are less likely to participate in community-organised activities [37, 38]. Low levels of community engagement among older adults have similarly been reported in Hong Kong [39]. Therefore, strategies to include diverse and vulnerable groups in participation and leadership are needed for community empowerment, so that solutions to the problems reflect reality and enable the vulnerable population to gain control of their lives.

WHO PHC pillar 3: multisectoral policy and action

The third pillar refers to collaborative efforts across health and other sectors to address wider social determinants of health – social, economic, commercial and environmental factors. This requires a multisectoral governance that enable horizontal and vertical governance, culture of collaboration, leadership at all levels, trust and open communication, mutual understanding, system of learning, capacity for joint actions (e.g., shared budget, dedicated manpower) based on WHO’s multisectoral collaboration principles [15]. At the strategic level, Singapore has established mechanisms to coordinate policies across the government. The Ministerial Committee on Ageing, led by MOH, works with ministries, such as MSF, MOE, and MOT, to align cross-ministry policies and promote public-private sector partnerships. Private-sector and community stakeholders have been increasingly involved in policy consultations, highlighting a shift towards more inclusive governance. Since 2015, a coordinating minister for social policies has been appointed to oversee the harmonisation of policies across education, health, and the social portfolio. Collectively, the multisectoral involvement at the strategic level strengthens state’s political commitment in addressing the complex issues associated with ageing population and primary health care, while the appointment of a coordinating minister indicates the state’s accountability in addressing complex issues.

At the operational level, Singapore’s shift towards upstream and place-based care has broadened the range of policy actors across different levels of government to address locality-specific factors that influence the well-being of populations. Alongside HPB and AIC, organisations such as PA and Sport SG established the Active Ageing Council to promote active ageing through grassroots initiatives. These collaborations have enabled seamless care and flexible resource use, including manpower arrangement. Alongside these organisations, the recent assignment of regional health systems under Healthier SG strategy and CCOs under ICCP (Age Well strategy) add to the network of stakeholders.

Structural gaps and remaining challenges

Over the course of policy development, MSF’s role has become less clear in recent policy discourse, such as Healthier SG and the Age Well strategy, despite the diverse needs of community-dwelling older adults, including those facing poverty, disabilities, or impairments, as noted in the Action Plan for Successful Ageing 2023 [22, 26, 40]. These might unintentionally reinforce the divide between the health and social sectors. These SSAs, typically governed by MSF and NCSS, play a central role in supporting vulnerable populations, underscoring their relevance in PHC. The limited involvement of SSAs under MSF and NCSS also risks over-medicalising complex social issues and overlooking social interventions that could address the wider social determinants of health. Given their service philosophies of empowerment and co-production, SSAs are well-positioned to facilitate knowledge exchange between the health and social care sectors. Moreover, MOH and MSF integrate care at different levels. MOH emphasises structural integration through regional health systems to facilitate horizontal and vertical coordination, whereas MSF focuses on service integration at the local level, thereby contributing to place-based care [41, 42]. These complementary approaches offer an opportunity for partnership and knowledge sharing in translating and implementing health-social care integration at local settings, potentially enhancing service accessibility and effectiveness. Taken together, these approaches create important opportunities for partnership, mutual learning and knowledge sharing in translating and implementing health-social integration at the local level, with the potential to improve both service accessibility and overall effectiveness.

Operationally, collaboration between ministries, statutory boards such as AIC, HPB, and PA, healthcare clusters, and eldercare centres at the local level is bilateral, primarily focused on service delivery rather than on a structured governance framework. For example, healthcare clusters serve as regional health managers responsible for building an integrated ecosystem and overseeing the health of their assigned population, while AIC is the funder and governing body of the community care sector and general practitioners, setting their key performance indicators and accountability requirements. This illustrates that alignment between policy and funding arrangements remains less clear, contributing to a less explicit leadership at the operational level. Without a clear leadership structure at operational level, it may risk fragmentation without oversight and effective coordination and collaboration [43].

There is a lack of shared budget at community level as each sector is operating on different funding models across sectors, which in turn hinder coordination. Under Healthier SG, private PC physicians are financed through case-based and pay-for-performance payments, with annual fees tied to patient profiles and progress. SSAs, by contrast, depend on public donations, government grants, and programme income, making fundraising a core function. Government support includes tax rebates, one-to-one matching grants, and funding schemes administered by AIC or NCSS. Donations often cover operational and developmental shortfalls. These divergent models limit coordination, long-term planning, and equitable service provision for communities [44].

One critical enabler can be cross-sector data sharing. For example, SGO collects data on the Clinical Frailty Score of the older adult population during preventive health visits, similar to the assessment conducted by Community Health Post. AIC is transitioning to adopt Inter-RAI as a standardised assessment for clinical care, with its aggregated data providing population health insights. Meanwhile, SSOs and SSAs conduct locality-specific assessments to identify emerging needs and assets. Facilitating data sharing across these systems will enable ministries and organisations involved in population health management to design more targeted and effective services and to address the social determinants of health. Overall, Singapore demonstrates a strong governance at strategic level, while the governance structure (e.g. leadership, financing) and structure for learning remains less clear at operational level.

Discussion

Singapore has developed PHC to varying degrees aligned with the WHO PHC pillars. While notable progress has been achieved in integrated service delivery, structural gaps remain in the pillars of empowerment of people and communities and in multisectoral policy and action, particularly in relation to health equity. Although this study is situated in Singapore, the challenges identified are widely shared across health systems confronting rapid population ageing, rising multimorbidity, and widening socioeconomic inequality and health disparity. Across high-, middle-, and low-income countries, PHC reforms have increasingly prioritised service integration; however, advances in community empowerment, social participation, and cross-sectoral collaboration have been more limited [45]. Singapore’s experience, therefore, reflects a broader global pattern in PHC development, in which system-level integration has often outpaced people-centred, community-driven, and equity-oriented transformation.

PHC policy for long-term care should extend greater attention to low-to-middle income families beyond the low-income families. Health inequities are patterned along a social gradient; accordingly, efforts to reduce social gradients require adherence to the principle of proportionate universalism, whereby support is provided at a scale and intensity commensurate with the level of disadvantage [46]. In the domain long-term care financing, the introduction of universal CareShield Life represents an important step toward reducing financial barriers to access to long-term care. However, enrolment remains optional for individuals born before 1980, indicating that certain cohorts may continue to face inadequate financial protection in the event of severe disability. Thus, while sustained support for low-income families remains essential, extending meaningful assistance to low-to-middle income households is also necessary to narrow the social gradient of health. It is therefore timely for the state to review long-term care financial arrangements to ensure equitable protection for older adults and their caregivers who could not or have not benefited from universal long-term care insurance. This include making CareShield Life mandatory to older adults who meet the inclusion criteria or broadening ElderFund’s eligibility criteria.

PHC policy should prioritise community empowerment and collective agency. Evidence indicates that community members possess experiential knowledge to identify structural issues that shape their health and to solve problems related to their health and lives [47]. A paradigm shift is required to view community members as ‘experts’ in their own lives and to enable them to take greater control over decisions that affect their health and well-being. Studies have shown that their health outcomes improve when they are empowered to contribute to solutions for equity [48]. This requires intentional efforts to build relational structures, as a study conducted in the UK found that dedicated facilitators play an important role in establishing coalitions among community members and optimising participatory spaces for engagement [49]. Formal institutions and community-based stakeholders play a crucial role in cultivating collective efficacy and facilitating collective action. They serve as an asset by linking community members to relevant stakeholders with resources and decision-making power, thereby enhancing the community’s social capital. Embedding participatory structures – raising critical consciousness about the causes of issues, co-identifying community needs, co-designing solutions and participating in community-led evaluation – in organisational practice ensures that services reflect the needs of vulnerable populations [50]. Correspondingly, the PHC workforce requires competencies in community participatory and asset-based approaches, alongside clinical skills. This also calls for knowledge exchange among statutory boards that are based on a community participatory approach, leveraging a multisectoral approach to empower communities.

Without a clear multi-level governance framework, an expanding ecosystem risk functional overlap, inefficiencies, and resource wastage, which may not sufficiently address community needs [51]. Recognising that PHC transformation for an ageing population inherently requires cross-ministerial and multisectoral collaboration, delineating responsibilities among ministries and agencies may not always be feasible [52]. Effective implementation, therefore, requires strong leadership, a clear governance framework, and shared goals [51].

Seven key governance gaps – information, capacity, fiscal, policy, administrative, objective, and accountability – should be addressed in multi-level governance to inform the policy decision on whether and how PHC reform can be implemented into practice [53]. Information gaps arise when the health and social care sector lacks a data-sharing mechanism or shared intelligence on local needs, thereby constraining coordination and population-based planning. Capacity gaps may be particularly pronounced among local organisations and smaller SSAs, which often face workforce, competency, infrastructure limitations in delivering new care models [54]. Another common barrier is the emergence of fiscal gaps when funding arrangements remain fragmented across sectors, limiting incentives for collaboration [54–56]. Cooperative partnership could also be challenged, especially in an scarcity environment when elements of competition (e.g., funding competition, recognition, demonstration of achievements, turf wars) are embedded in service delivery, thereby undermining trust and mutuality needed for collaboration [57]. Collaboration is further hindered when providers face accountability frameworks that generate competing priorities and misaligned performance indicators, resulting in accountability gaps, reinforcing siloed working relationships and limiting attention to broader determinants of health [58, 59].

These gaps are situated in a broader context. Policy and administrative gaps rise when policy translation is hindered by regulations, a mismatch between local organisations’ resources and capacities or ambiguous goals [60]. Ambiguous goals may lead to different interpretations of policies, leading to misunderstanding and uncertainty. This tension is compounded by sectors operating under different institutional logics, leading to objective gaps [61]. For instance, private sector operates on market principles, whereas the social sector is more likely to operate on welfare principles. Thus, negotiating across different institutional logics becomes complex, thereby influencing the extent of cooperation and collaboration. Collectively, these governance gaps undermine collaboration and constrain attention to equity.

Mechanisms that foster horizontal and vertical collaboration across ministries, social services, and healthcare agencies are required to enhance coordination and policy effectiveness. Some policy recommendations are as followed: First, accountability frameworks and financing mechanisms should be structured to align with social determinants of health and support coordination, while aligning accountability frameworks across sectors to ensure coherence [53]. An integrated financing approach could help to streamline reporting frameworks, encourage pooling funding and establish shared outcome measures and monitoring to collectively address community needs [58]. Shared payment models cover a wide range of population profiles, include models of care across multiple sectors and have a long-time horizon are found to have the potential to incentivise collaboration and integration [62]. Second, establishing leadership structure with clear roles and responsibilities at the operational or meso- level to establish shared goals, promote mutual understanding, joint capacity and negotiate with leadership at strategic or macro-level [15, 63]. Third, mechanisms should be established to facilitate cross-sectoral data-sharing for population health planning and learning health system. Shared data are found to be an important mechanism for monitoring and evaluation and decision-making [43]. Fourth, involving the social sector, in particularly MSF, NCSS and SSAs, in primary health care reform as they play a central role in designing and implementing social interventions that address wider social determinants of health, as well as having the local knowledge of the community. Fifth, have a dedicated facilitator to build social cohesion among them through coalition building, and establish a mechanism to share power with the communities by involving them in the governance structure, identifying the causes of issues and co-producing solutions. Lastly, review the long-term care financial support for cohorts whose CareShield is optional for them.

Conclusion

In line with the WHO PHC Operational Framework, Singapore’s experience offers broader relevance for other countries with ageing populations seeking to strengthen PHC. Long-term political commitment, progressive decentralisation and a reorientation towards preventive and community care are critical to mitigating increased care needs and addressing inequity. Singapore’s trajectory underscores the importance of strengthening local governance, aligning financing models and promoting community participation to address wider determinants of health. It is important to recognise that Singapore’s PHC reform success is partly shaped by its political stability and high public trust, which enable the continuous and coherent policy design and implementation. As the governance structures differ across countries, the transferability of Singapore’s experience lies less in its specific institutional structures than in the broader principles of integrated care delivery, empowerment and multisectoral collaboration. These principles may offer relevance to other ageing countries facing similar demographic and systemic challenges in response to (super-)aged populations, but their application must be adapted to their governance contexts and the organisation of health and social care systems. As the countries enter a super-aged phase, balancing structural coherence with local flexibility will become increasingly important. Strategies that meaningfully engage a diverse older adult population in care design and delivery, alongside strengthened local governance, will be essential to advance health equity and ensure that PHC transformation remains responsive to the needs of an ageing population.
