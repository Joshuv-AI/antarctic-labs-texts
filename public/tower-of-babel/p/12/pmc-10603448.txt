Caring behaviour and its associated factors among nurses working at public hospitals in Gamo zone, southern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mathewos Ashagere, Tomas Yeheyis, Dereje Addisu, Workineh Abera, Tsegahun Amlaku, Fikru Tadesse, Bereket Beyene, Tinbete Samuel, Alemneh Kabeta Daba
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to assess nurses caring behaviours and its associated factors among nurses working at public hospitals in the Gamo zone of southern Ethiopia in 2022.DesignInstitutional based cross-sectional study was used.SettingThis study was conducted in five public hospitals in Gamo zone, southern Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 360 nurses of different level of qualification were included in this study using a proportional allocation method from the five hospitals and final study participants were recruited from all wards using a simple random sampling method from 20 June to 20 July 2022.Outcome measurementsCaring behaviour was assessed using 24 standardised Caring Behaviour Inventory (CBI) scales. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and, analysed using the logistic regression method, and those variables with a p value of <0.05 in multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant.ResultThe overall percentage of nurse caring behaviour in this study was 53.3%. Most of the respondents practiced technical aspects of caring behaviour rather than psychosocial aspects of caring. Being married (adjusted OR (AOR)=0.027 (0.003–0.263), having work experience of (0–5 years) (AOR=5.547 (1.42–21.64)), (6–10 years) (AOR=8.693 (2.317–32.6), being satisfied with motivation and prospect (AOR=0.473 (0.290–0.770)) and being satisfied with the nursing profession (AOR=1.716 (1.065–2.765) were significantly associated with nurses caring behaviour.ConclusionThe proportion of nurses with a good caring behaviour found in this study was relatively poor. Being married, having lower work experience, being satisfied with motivation and prospects, and nursing profession were associated with nurses caring behaviour. This study recommends the need to focus on including caring behaviour courses in the nursing curriculum and training nurses focusing on those with a few experiences on the meaning and importance of caring behaviour.Keywords: Quality in health care; Hospitals, Public; Health Workforce; Nurses; Nursing Care

Introduction

Caring is the essence of nursing and the profession’s underlying disciplinary core, and it is best expressed and practiced interpersonally.1 Caring behaviours are actions concerned with the well-being of a patient, such as sensitivity, comforting, attentive listening, honesty and nonjudgmental acceptance.2 Caring for patients is a way of dealing with patients that expresses genuine care and concern simply because they are human. Professionals should always present an open and accommodating presence to their patients.3

Generally, there are two major areas of caring in nursing: instrumental (technical) abilities and expressive (affective) behaviours. Technical caring involves actions that promote patients’ physical health and comfort, for example, serving medications, demonstrating competency in skills, teaching and solving problems. Whereas expressive caring involves, interactions that promote trust, hope and emotional support such as active listening, respecting patients, expressing sensitivity to their feelings, and establishing trust, faith and honesty are all important aspects of expressive behaviours. Healthcare today has placed great emphasis on the provision of patient-centred care where nurses are expected to perform both technical and expressive aspects of care. Thus, the teaching of caring behaviours and attitudes is a basic component of nursing curriculum.4 5

Every year, approximately 100 million people worldwide seek medical attention in hospital.6 Professional nurses must demonstrate caring behaviour when providing nursing care. Unless nurses implement good caring behaviour, they will cause harm to patients, nurses and hospital parties. The number of patients visiting the hospital will be reduced, resulting in a decrease in financial income. It will undoubtedly harm the hospital party because patients’ trust will be eroded.7

Nurses spend the majority of their time and energy following physicians’ instructions, writing reports and doing secretarial duties due to a nursing staff shortage and a heavy patient load. Such a situation would make them tired, anxious and unable to provide the level of patient care that was required of them. This can lead to patient dissatisfaction and poor nursing care, with a bad prognosis as a result.6

Patient dissatisfaction and clinical outcomes are two important areas of patient outcomes that are influenced by bad nursing care. Missed appointments were linked to lower patient satisfaction. Patient satisfaction is now becoming increasingly crucial in the quality of care reforms and healthcare delivery, according to the WHO. According to this survey, 46% of all patients who visit various healthcare facilities were dissatisfied with the treatment they got. Dissatisfaction raises anxiety and irritation in patients, resulting in a longer recovery period and more hospital beds being occupied.8 Pressure ulcers, medication mistakes, nosocomial infections, patient falls, critical episodes, 30-day hospital readmission and death were among the clinical outcomes influenced by poor nursing care, according to reports.9

Various researches on nursing care quality and patient satisfaction discovered that overall patient satisfaction with inpatient healthcare services was low. In Indonesia, only 41% of patients were satisfied with nursing care service.7 A study in Ghana showed only 33% of patients were satisfied with nursing care service.10 The overall national level of patient satisfaction with nursing care in Ethiopia was 55.15%, with the highest level of patient satisfaction (61.84%) in Addis Ababa and the lowest level of patient satisfaction (44.06%) in the SNNP area.11

Studies done in Ethiopia showed a high amount of nurses had practiced good caring behaviour while caring for their patients, 80.3% in Jimma, 68.2% in Gondar and 51.6% in Hara.12–14 Despite this, in Ethiopia, nearly one out of every two patients was dissatisfied with their nursing service. It is true that patient satisfaction with nursing care is a reliable sign of a positive prognosis and a factor in hospital healthcare quality.11

Nursing is seen as a demanding profession with high, complex expectations and too much responsibility but insufficient authority, resulting in occupational stress and discontent. Different aspects such as sociodemographic features, educational background, workload, job satisfaction, positive connection with doctors, contentment with the profession, working location and work experience have all been linked to nurse caring behaviours.15

A study of nurses’ caring behaviours was conducted in the developed world, but just a few studies were conducted in low-income and middle-income countries. In Ethiopia, there is a lack of literature on nurse caring behaviours. As a result, the purposes of this study were to assess nurses caring behaviours and associated factors among nurses of different level of qualification at public hospitals in the Gamo zone of southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this study, we used an institutional-based cross-sectional study. The study was conducted at five public hospitals in Gamo zone of southern Ethiopia. Among those five hospitals, one general and one primary hospital (Arbaminch General Hospital (AGH) and Dilfana Primary Hospital (DPH)) were located in Arba Minch town and the remaining three primary hospitals (Kamba (KPH), Geresse (GPH), Chencha (CPH)) are hospitals distributed at woreda levels. There were 388 nurses at those five hospitals (223 nurses at AGH, 38 nurses at DPH, 41 nurses at CPH, 40 nurses at KPH and 46 nurses at GPH).

Eligibility criteria

Nurses who were active on duty during data collection period were included in this study and those nurses, who were assigned out of clinical duty, were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using simple population proportion formula by considering a prevalence of 80.3% from previous studies conducted at Jimma University specialised comprehensive hospital and a 5% margin of error and a 95% CI.12 Power 80 was used13 and finally, the sample size was taken to 366 after adding 10% non-response rate.

Study participants were proportionally selected from each hospital using the proportional allocation formula Ni=NhNt×N. So, 38 nurses from KPH, 210 from AGH, 36 from DPH, 39 from CPH and 43 from GPH were included in this study. Nurses who were active on duty during the data collection period were included in this study and those who were assigned out of clinical duty, were excluded from the study.

Data collection procedure and measurements/instruments

Data were collected from 20 June to 20 July 2022 using a self-administered structured questionnaire. The data collection was conducted by trained BSc nurses who had previous exposure to quantitative data collection. The questionnaire was administered using anonymously to ensure the confidentiality of the participant. The questionnaire was adopted from previous similar studies and modified to the context of this study. Pretest was conducted on 5% of nurses working at Konso General Hospital before the actual period of data collection. Corrections were made based on the result of the pretest data. The collected data were checked for completeness and consistency by trained supervisors.

Caring behaviour was measured by 24 standardised questions which was developed by Wu et al.16 Each question has 6-point Likert scale options, ranging from score of 1 meaning never to score of 6 meaning always perform caring behaviour. It has two components (technical professional 10 questions) and (psychosocial has 14 questions). The assurance and respectfulness subscale of CBI was taken as a psychosocial component and the knowledge and connectedness subscales were taken as technical professional components. For each subscale and component, the sum, mean and SD were calculated. This tool which has been used by this study to measure caring behaviour of nurses is psychometric and it has been used previously in Ethiopia.

Job satisfaction was measured by 5-point Likert scale standardised questions which was developed by Warr et al,17 ranging from 1 very dissatisfied to 5 very satisfied. Fifteen standardised questionnaires were used (five questions asking about personal satisfaction, five for motivation and prospect and five about professional satisfaction). The total sum score was calculated for each subscale and then the mean and SD were calculated for each subscale.

The nurse and physician interaction scale have 27 standardised questionnaires developed by Ushiro.18 Each question was answered on a 2-point yes no response question, a score of 1 means no and 2 means yes. This scale has three subscales (joint participation in cure care and decision making (12 questions), sharing of patient information (nine questions) and cooperation between nurse and physician (six questions)). A total sum score was calculated for each subscale and then the overall mean and SD were calculated for each subscale.

The working environment was measured by 10 standardised questions developed by Moos.19 Each question was answered on a 2-point yes or no question, a score of 1 indicates no and 2 means yes. This scale has two subscales (staffing and support (six questions) and nursing management (four questions)). For each subscale overall sum score was calculated and then the mean and SD were calculated. In this study, caring behaviour was classified as poor caring behaviour when the nurses’ score on the CBI-24 scale was less than the overall mean and good caring behaviour when their score is greater than the overall mean.

Term and operational definitions

Caring behaviour

The acts, conduct and mannerisms enacted by professional nurses that convey to the patient concern, safety and attention.20

Job satisfaction

The feeling of pleasure and achievement that you experience in your job when you know that your work is worth doing, or the degree to which your work gives you this feeling.

Physician–nurse interaction

The professional interaction, cooperation, communication and collaboration that exist between physicians and nurses in patient care.

Poor caring behaviour

If nurses’ overall score on the CBI scale is less than the overall mean value.

Good caring behaviour

If nurses’ overall score on the CBI scale is greater than or equal to the overall mean value.

Satisfied with their job

If the nurse’s total score on the job satisfaction scale question is greater than or equal to the overall mean value.

Unsatisfied with their job

If the nurses’ total score on the job satisfaction scale question is less than the overall mean value.

Satisfied with nurse–physician interaction

When the nurse’s score to the physician–nurse interaction scale score is greater than or equal to the overall mean value.

Unsatisfied with nurse–physician interaction

When nurses score to the physician–nurse interaction scale score is less than the overall mean value.

Satisfied with the working environment

If nurses’ overall score on the working environment scale is less than the overall mean value.

Unsatisfied with the working environment

If a nurse’s overall score on the working environment scale is greater than or equal to the overall mean value.

Statistical analysis

The data were coded and entered into Epi data V.4.6 and then it was exported into SPSS V.26 for analysis. Descriptive analysis was done to calculate frequencies, mean scores and proportions. A binary logistic regression model was fitted for analysis. Model fitness was checked using Hosmer Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The p value of the Hosmer Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was 0.123, which confirms that the model is correctly specified. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify associated factors with nurses caring behaviour.

All covariates with a p value less than 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were taken into a multivariable analysis to control confounding factors and to identify true predictors of caring behaviour. Those variables that showed a p value of less than 0.05 with 95% CI and adjusted OR (AOR) were defined as factors significantly associated with nurses caring behaviour.

Quality assurance

The training was provided for data collectors and supervisors for 1 day and a pretest was carried out on 5% of the sample size at Konso District Hospital. During the data collection period, strong supervision and daily check-up of a questionnaire for completeness were done.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

Among 366 proposed study participants, 360 nurses participated in the study giving a response rate of (98.6%). More than half of the participants were male (53.9%) and the majority of them (59.7%) were between the ages of 30–39. Almost greater than half (51.9%) of them were single and (53.1%) hold a BSc degree. Most of the study participants were protestant (40%) and (24.7%) of them were working in the medical ward. Regarding work experience (58.3%) had experienced less than 5 years and (33.9%) had work experience of 6–10 (table 1).

The mean and SD for staffing and support were 3.967±1.03 and nurse management 2.37±0.93. Factors associated with nurses caring behaviour

On bivariate logistic regression analysis; factors like marital status, working unit, religion, work experience, satisfaction with motivation and prospect, professional satisfaction, sharing of patient information among nurses and physicians, and satisfaction with staffing and support had a significant association with the caring behaviour of nurses.

After adjusting for possible confounders marital status (married), work experience (0–5, 6–10), satisfaction with motivation and prospect, and satisfaction with the nursing profession were significantly associated with nurses caring behaviour.

In this study marital status was associated with caring behaviour, nurses who were married are 0.027 times less likely to have good nursing behaviour than single nurses, AOR=0.027, 95% CI 0.003–0.263. Nurses who had work experience of 0–5 years were 5.547 times more likely to have good nursing behaviour than those who have >11 years of experience, AOR=5.547, 95% CI 1.422–21.644. Nurses who had work experience of 6–10 years were 8.693 times more likely to have good caring behaviour than those who have >11 years of experience AOR=8.693, 95% CI 2.317–32.607. Nurses who were unsatisfied with motivation and prospect are 0.473 times less likely to have good caring behaviour than those who are satisfied with motivation and prospect AOR=0.473, 95% CI 0.290–0.770. Nurses who were satisfied with their profession are 1.716 times more likely to have good caring behaviour than those who were unsatisfied with their profession, AOR=1.716, 95% CI 1.065–2.765) (table 3).

Discussion

The proportion of overall caring behaviour of nurses in this study was 53.3%. This finding is lower than the study conducted in Indonesia (64.7%), Gondar (68.2%), Jimma (80.3%)12 13 15 and is higher than the study conducted in Harar (51.67%).14 The variation in result may be due to scope of study area. Most of the studies were conducted on single hospital mostly on referral hospitals but this study tried to assess caring behaviour of nurses from general and primary hospitals. It also may be due to variation in assessment tool and method of analysis. Also, it may be due to poor caring environment, poor interaction between nurses and physician and dissatisfaction of nurses by their job. This can influence overall caring for their patients.

In this study, nurses demonstrated technical aspects of caring (49.4%) more than psychosocial aspect of caring (46.9%). This finding is in agreement with study conducted at Jordan, Jimma, Gondar and Turkey which showed nurses practiced technical aspects of caring more than psychosocial aspects.12 13 21 22 This showed that even if nurses in this study prioritised physical care, their performance of expressive caring behaviours was not neglected. The reason may be nurses take physical concrete aspects of caring behaviour more than emotional aspect of caring.

The finding of this study showed there is statistically significant association between marital statuses of nurses and caring behaviour of nurses. Nurses who were married were 0.027 times less likely to have good nursing behaviour than those who were single. There was no previous study which showed similar association. But the possible reason could be nurses who were married have a lot of family responsibility beyond their job; like taking care of their family and participating in different social activities. However, those who were single have energy, commitment, and have less family and social responsibility than those who were married.

In this study work experience of nurses were significantly associated with their caring behaviour. Nurses who had work experience of 0–5 years are 5.547 times more likely to have a good caring behaviour than those have >11 years of experience. Nurses who have work experience of 6–10 years are 8.693 times more likely to have a good caring behaviour than who have >11 years of experience. This finding is in contrast with a study conducted at Croatia, Gondar and Jimma which showed nurses who had longer work experience have good caring behaviour than those nurses who had lower experience.12 13 23 In this study, levels of caring behaviour were inversely proportional to years of experience. The reason for this might be a phenomenon that can be the result of a variety of negative factors in everyday clinical practice (eg, experience-damaged relationships, negative supervisory relationships, poor cooperation and deficient communication among team members). Also in the early years, nurses do have energy and commitment in doing their job.

In this study satisfaction of nurses by their job were significantly associated with caring behaviour of nurses. Nurses who were unsatisfied with motivation and prospect are 0.473 times less likely to have good caring behaviour than those who are satisfied with motivation and prospect. This finding supports previous studies conducted at Indonesia, Gondar and Jimma referral hospitals.13 15 24 Also nurses who are satisfied with their profession are 1.716 times more likely to have a good nursing behaviour than those who are unsatisfied with their profession. This finding is in agreement with a study done at Jimma and Gondar referral hospitals.12 13 Which showed nurses who were satisfied with their job practiced caring behaviour more than nurses who were unsatisfied with their job. The reason may be nurses need attention and motivation from their organisation as incentive, increase in salary and other benefits. If they are motivated and satisfied with their profession the more likely they will practice caring behaviour.

Though the study has a number of strengths like using a standardised measurement scale it also has limitations like a methodological limitation where the study has employed a cross-sectional study design which may not show temporal relationship between exposure and outcome. Moreover, due to this design the study is liable to interviewer bias and a social desirability bias, especially on data related to job satisfaction and nurse–physician interaction.

Conclusion

The level of nurses caring behaviour in this study was relatively poor. A relatively high number of nurses practiced the technical aspect of caring than the psychosocial aspect of caring as their nursing practice. This study found among sociodemographic characteristics marital status and work experience had a significant association with the perception of caring behaviour. Also, this study found a significant association between nurses’ satisfaction with motivation and prospect and professional satisfaction with nurses caring behaviour. The result of this study showed that attention should be given to the caring behaviour of nurses. The nursing curriculum must incorporate caring behaviour courses for both undergraduate and diploma-level students. So that nurses could demonstrate caring behaviour while they are engaging with their patients.
