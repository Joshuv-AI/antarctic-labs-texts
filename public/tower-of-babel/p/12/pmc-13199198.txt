Identification of salivary lipid biomarkers for noninvasive diagnosis of reflux esophagitis via UHPLC-MS-based lipidomics
Authors: Ying Wang, Ying Zhou, Jian Gao, Lin Zhang, Jing Liu, Lele Zhang, Tongcan Cui, Jingran Zhang, Xiangqin Ou, Xiumei Gao, Lifeng Han, Hai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionReflux esophagitis (RE) is a common upper gastrointestinal disorder, and its diagnosis currently relies primarily on invasive endoscopic examination. The lack of reliable non-invasive biomarkers substantially limits early detection and large-scale screening. Saliva represents a promising biofluid for metabolomics research, as it can reflect metabolic alterations associated with upper gastrointestinal pathology.ObjectivesThis study aimed to identify potential salivary lipid biomarkers associated with RE, and to develop a non-invasive diagnostic model using metabolomics and lipidomics.MethodsSaliva samples from patients clinically diagnosed with RE and healthy controls were analyzed. The analysis included a discovery cohort (n = 144) and an independent validation cohort (n = 146). Differential metabolites were screened using the untargeted metabolomics approach of ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS), and then quantitative verification was conducted using targeted lipidomics. Multivariate statistical analysis, random forest algorithms, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis were applied.ResultsUntargeted metabolomics revealed significant metabolic differences between RE patients and healthy controls, with marked enrichment of sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism. Targeted lipidomics identified six consistently dysregulated salivary lipids: DAG (18:1_18:2), S-1-P, PE (P-16:0_18:1), DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), and DAG (16:0_18:1). A multimetabolite model based on these lipids effectively distinguished RE patients from healthy controls, achieving an AUC of 99.45% in the discovery cohort and 97.17% in the validation cohort.ConclusionThis study identified a salivary lipid signature associated with RE and supports the potential of this lipidomic approach as a non-invasive method to distinguish RE from healthy controls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-026-02435-3.

Introduction

Reflux esophagitis (RE) is a common chronic upper gastrointestinal disease with a global prevalence ranging from 6.1% to 15.5% (Richter & Rubenstein, 2018). Typical symptoms of RE include heartburn, regurgitation, esophageal chest pain, and dysphagia (Desai et al., 2025). Patients with severe RE may progress to complications, including esophageal ulceration, stricture formation, and Barrett’s esophagus (BE). Notably, BE has the potential to progress to esophageal adenocarcinoma, with an annual progression rate ranging from 0.33% to 6.67% (Desai et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2014).

The diagnosis of RE mainly depends on the use of endoscopic examination and reflux monitoring techniques. Endoscopy makes direct visualization of esophageal mucosal inflammation possible and is seen as the gold standard for diagnosis. In addition, the Los Angeles classification system is used to appraise the severity of RE (Gyawali et al., 2024). Nonetheless, being invasive, endoscopic examination can lead to excessive pain and discomfort among the patients. It is especially difficult with children, elderly persons, and patients with multiple comorbidities, and also predisposes the risk of complications (Shahsavari et al., 2022). Moreover, its diagnosis depends on the level of skill of the operator to a large extent. Valuable differences in the diagnostic findings can be observed among various operators as well as within an operator(Malfertheiner et al., 2017). Therefore, the creation of non-invasive diagnostic equipment is also important in enhancing the management of RE. Saliva is proposed as a biofluid because it is in direct contact with the mouth cavity and the upper gastrointestinal mucosa. It is able to reflect the local pathological physiological conditions (Cui et al., 2017; Hyvärinen et al., 2021). Compared with blood or urine samples, saliva not only can reflect the endogenous metabolites from the oral glands, but also can directly capture the dynamic interactions between the oral microenvironment and the reflux substances of the upper digestive tract (such as pepsin and bile acids). These advantages make saliva metabolomics an ideal tool for studying upper gastrointestinal diseases (Dosedělová et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). In recent years, the application scope of saliva analysis has been continuously expanding, and its diagnostic potential has now been extended to the fields of systemic diseases such as diabetes(Jiang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025). Previous studies have shown that dysregulation of some key metabolic pathways, including bile acid homeostasis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, arachidonic acid signaling, and sphingolipid biosynthesis, is closely associated with the pathogenesis of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (Perez et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2021).

In recent years, ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) has significantly advanced the research on saliva metabolomics due to its high resolution, high sensitivity, and high throughput. This advancement offers a new, powerful approach for non-invasive diagnosis of RE. Lipidomics is an important branch of metabolomics. It can systematically reveal the alterations in lipid metabolic networks, thereby providing a crucial means for identifying disease-specific lipid biomarkers. Moreover, machine learning algorithms, such as random forest (RF), facilitate the screening of discriminative features from metabolomics and lipidomics datasets, thereby facilitating the development of reliable predictive models (Deng et al., 2024).

On the basis of the aforementioned background, saliva samples were collected from clinically diagnosed patients with RE and healthy controls. Untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics analyses were performed via UHPLC‒MS to identify differential metabolites between the two groups. Furthermore, a diagnostic model for RE was constructed and validated on the basis of selected potential biomarkers by integrating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and RF algorithms. Additionally, this study aimed to preliminarily explore the potential metabolic mechanisms associated with RE.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Xiqing Hospital (xqyyll-2024-01). Patient participants were recruited from the Department of Gastroenterology at Tianjin Xiqing Hospital between February 2024 and September 2024. After screening against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 161 RE patients and 129 controls were enrolled. These participants were then randomly assigned to two independent cohorts: a discovery cohort (n = 144) and a validation cohort (n = 146) (Fig. 1). Demographic and clinical data, including age, height, weight, and other relevant information, were obtained from medical reports.

Exclusion criteria

(1) History of gastrointestinal surgery or alcohol or substance abuse; (2) history of or current suspicion of malignancy; (3) primary or secondary esophageal motility disorders such as pancreatic achalasia, nutcracker esophagus and systemic sclerosis; (4) severe underlying cardiac, hepatic, pulmonary, renal, pancreatic, or other diseases that could compromise survival; (5) significant psychiatric or cognitive conditions that would impair the ability to provide consent or comply with the study protocol; and (6) pregnant or lactating women.

Patient classification

Patients were classified into RE or healthy control groups on the basis of the GERD-Q score and gastroscopic findings (Vakil et al., 2006). RE patients were then further stratified into four severity grades (LA-A, LA-B, LA-C, and LA-D) according to the LA classification system (Armstrong et al., 1996).

Data collection

General information

Sex, age, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, coronary heart disease, cerebral infarction, sleep apnea syndrome (SAS), smoking and alcohol, beverages, comorbidities with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and dietary preferences were recorded.

Collection of saliva samples

Saliva was collected from all participants via the Salivette® saliva collection system (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany). All samples were collected in the morning between 9:00 and 11:00 AM under fasting conditions. Prior to collection, the participants were required to abstain from smoking, eating, and drinking for one hour, rinse their mouths once with purified water, and avoid strenuous exercise and emotional stress. During collection, the participants remained seated upright. Unstimulated Salivette® swabs were placed sublingually or in the oral cavity for 5 min without chewing to yield a minimum of 1 mL of saliva. The swab was then transferred to the provided collection tube and centrifuged at 3461 × g for 5 min. During sample collection and processing, samples with visible blood contamination were discarded based on visual inspection. The resulting saliva supernatant was aliquoted into cryovials and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

UHPL‒CMS analysis

Chemicals and materials

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade solvents, including acetonitrile, methanol, and isopropanol, were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Ultrapure water was obtained from a commercial source (Watsons Food & Beverage Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). Formic acid (ACS grade) was obtained from Fisher Scientific (Wilmington, WA, USA) and used as received. Ammonium acetate (HPLC grade) was obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich.

Saliva metabolomics study

To ensure comprehensive and reproducible metabolomic coverage, we systematically optimized the saliva sample pretreatment protocol. In positive ion mode, a greater number of detectable metabolites, higher signal intensity, and broader chemical coverage were observed than in negative ion mode (Fig. S1). Therefore, the positive ion mode was selected as the evaluation mode for subsequent pretreatment optimization. First, all samples underwent a single freeze-thaw cycle to minimize metabolite degradation (Figs. S2, S3). Next, several protein precipitation solvents were tested, such as acetonitrile, methanol, and a 50% acetonitrile solution (acetonitrile: water = 1:1). Eventually, acetonitrile was picked as it showed superior protein precipitation efficiency and metabolite recovery rate (Figs. S4, S5). Finally, the volume ratio of the sample to acetonitrile was optimized. It was found that a ratio of 1:2 was the most suitable, which is applicable for the processing of large quantities of samples (Figs. S6, S7).

The optimized sample pretreatment process is as follows: Take the saliva sample from the − 80℃ refrigerator and place it in a 4℃ environment for thawing. Transfer 200 µL of the thawed saliva sample to a 1.5 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube, add 400 µL of acetonitrile, and perform 5 min of vortex mixing. The sample was then centrifuged at 20,817 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to precipitate the proteins. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully transferred to a new vial and dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen gas. The dried residue was reconstituted in 100 µL of 50% (v/v) aqueous methanol by vortexing. Finally, a 50 µL aliquot of the resulting supernatant was injected for UHPLC-MS analysis.

Sample separation and detection were accomplished via the Ultra High Performance Liquid Chromatography Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-orbitrap-MS) platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Separation was achieved via a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) provided by Waters Corporation (Ireland) at a temperature of 35 °C, with a constant flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of a 0.1% aqueous formic acid solution (A) and acetonitrile (B). The following gradient was applied: 5% B (0–1 min), increased linearly to 100% B over 10 min (1–11 min), held at 100% B for 0.5 min (11–11.5 min), returned to 5% B over 0.1 min (11.5–11.6 min), and equilibrated at 5% B for 1.4 min (11.6–13 min). The total run time was 13 min. The injection volume was 2 µL.

The heated electrospray ionization (HESI) parameters were as follows. For the formal untargeted metabolomics analysis, data from all study samples were acquired in full MS/dd-MS2 mode with positive and negative ion switching, and data from both ionization modes were included in the downstream analysis. The spray voltage was set to 3.5 kV (positive) and − 3.0 kV (negative). The full scan range was 100–1000 m/z at a resolution of 60,000. The capillary temperature was 320 °C, and the auxiliary temperature was 350 °C. The normalized collision energy (NCE) was − 35 V. Sheath gas (N2) was 35 arbitrary units (Arb), and auxiliary gas (N2) was 10 Arb. Quality control (QC) samples were used to test instrument stability. The QC sample was prepared by mixing 10 µL of each saliva sample.

Compound Discoverer 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to extract the features and align them with the raw data. The tolerance limit was defined as an error of 5ppm and cross-validation was carried out with mzCloud, mzVault, MassList and the internal database. The SIMCA software (version 14.1, Umetrics) was applied to the multivariate statistical analysis with the principal component analysis (PCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Among the tests was model validity determined through a permutation test (200 iterations). Metabolites were considered differentially expressed if they satisfied the following criteria: Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-adjusted p value < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 1.2 or < 0.833. Metabolic pathway analysis and heatmap generation were accomplished via the online platform MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/ModuleView.xhtml). The diagnostic potential of individual and combined metabolites was evaluated via ROC curve analysis. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Targeted lipidomic studies

Saliva samples were thawed at 4 °C, and 100 µL aliquots were transferred to 1.5 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes. For lipid extraction, 300 µL of ice-cold isopropanol was added to each saliva aliquot. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and then incubated at -20 °C for 1 h. After the incubation period, the samples were centrifuged at 13,200 × g for 20 min. A 100 µL aliquot of the clear supernatant was carefully transferred to a new vial for subsequent UHPLC-MS analysis.

To monitor analytical quality and system stability, a QC sample was prepared by combining 10 µL aliquots from each sample and was processed in parallel with the experimental samples. Chromatographic separation was conducted at 35 °C utilizing a Shimadzu LC-30AD UHPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C8 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters). The mobile phase was made up of a combination of water, methanol, and acetonitrile at a volumetric ratio of 3:1:1 (v/v) (A) along with isopropanol (B), with 5 mM ammonium acetate in both solvents. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 µL. The following gradient was applied: 0–0.5 min, 20% B; 0.5–1.5 min, 20% to 40% B; 1.5–3 min, 40% to 60% B; 3–13 min, 60% to 100% B; 13–14 min, 100% B; and 14–17 min, 20% B.

Mass spectrometry analysis was done via a QTRAP 6500 + triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) furnished with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Analysis was done in the scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (sMRM) mode, with time windows for transitions in positive and negative polarity, both being optimized. In total, 1112 lipid species were monitored using 1112 sMRM transitions, including 586 transitions in positive ion mode and 526 transitions in negative ion mode. The following key mass spectrometry parameters were established: ion source gas 1 (GS1), 50 psi; ion source gas 2 (GS2), 50 psi; curtain gas (CUR), 35 psi; and temperature, 400 °C. The ion spray voltage was set at + 5500 V in positive ion mode and + 4500 V in negative ion mode. Detailed information on lipid species, Q1/Q3 transitions, retention times, dwell times, and collision energy (CE) values is provided in Table S1. The declustering potential (DP) was set to 10. The cycle time was 2.0 s.

All the raw data were acquired and processed via Analyst software (version 1.6.2). Peak integration and area calculations were performed via Sciex OS software (version 1.4, AB Sciex).

Data cleaning and quality control for metabolomics and lipidomics

Prior to statistical analysis, both the untargeted metabolomics and targeted lipidomics datasets underwent preprocessing and quality control. Solvent blank samples were injected throughout the analytical sequence. Blank filtering was performed using a sample-to-blank ratio threshold of 5 to reduce background and solvent-related signals. Features with more than 20% missing values in the samples were excluded, and the remaining missing values were imputed using the median value. Additionally, signals with retention times too close to the void volume or the end of the chromatographic gradient were removed because of their lower analytical stability. Samples were injected in a randomized order, with a pooled QC sample injected every 10 samples. Since the QC samples were generally tightly clustered and exhibited acceptable analytical stability, no additional QC-based drift correction was applied.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism (version 9.5, GraphPad Software) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27, IBM Corp.), and SIMCA software for multivariate analysis, as previously described. The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Normally distributed data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{x}$$\end{document} ± s). Nonnormally distributed data were analyzed via the Mann‒Whitney U test and are presented as medians (Q1, Q3). Intergroup comparisons for normally distributed data were performed via the unpaired Student’s t-test. Categorical data are expressed as case numbers (n) and percentages (%) and were compared via the chi-square test, unless otherwise specified. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results of untargeted salivary metabolomics related to RE

After data cleaning and quality filtering, 8615 metabolic features were retained from the saliva samples for subsequent untargeted metabolomics analysis. Representative total ion chromatograms (TICs) of the RE and healthy control groups are shown in Fig. S1. The tight clustering of the QC samples in the PCA model indicated excellent instrumental stability throughout the acquisition (Fig. 2a). Although PCA indicated a trend of separation, supervised OPLS-DA revealed clear separation between the RE and control groups (R²Y = 0.973, Q² = 0.884; Fig. 2b). A 200-iteration permutation test verified the robustness and predictive ability of the OPLS-DA model. This test effectively ruled out overfitting (Fig. 2c). The permutation test yielded intercepts of R2 = (0.0, 0.851) and Q2 = (0.0, -0.34).

The volcano plot (Fig. 2d) displays the differentially expressed metabolites between the RE group and the control group. The screening criteria for differential metabolites were set as Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 and FC < 0.833 or > 1.2. These differential metabolites were then compared against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), the PubChem database, and the authentic reference standards available in the laboratory. Eventually, 27 differential metabolites were obtained and identified (Table 3). The trend of change is depicted in Fig. 3A. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these alterations were significantly enriched in seven metabolic pathways (Fig. 3B). Sphingolipid metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism were the two most significantly affected pathways. Notably, a large proportion of these differential metabolites were lipids, highlighting the potential role of lipid metabolism disorders in the pathogenesis of RE. On the basis of these untargeted metabolomics findings, a subsequent targeted lipidomics analysis was performed to quantify lipid alterations and identify potential biomarkers precisely.

Screening and validation of differential lipid biomarkers for RE through targeted lipidomics

Targeted lipidomics was performed on the discovery cohort to validate the lipid-centric alterations identified in the untargeted metabolomics results. This targeted analysis aimed to confirm dysregulation of specific lipid pathways and precisely quantify the involved lipid species, particularly sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism. Furthermore, the ultimate objective was to identify the most discriminative lipid combinations for diagnostic model construction. The entire stepwise strategy is summarized in Fig. S8. Multivariate statistical analysis revealed a clear separation between the RE and control groups. PCA revealed a separation trend (Fig. 4A). Although slight signal drift was observed in the QC samples on the PCA score plot, the QC samples remained closely clustered overall, indicating acceptable instrument stability. This separation trend was further refined and confirmed by the OPLS-DA model (R²Y = 0.974, Q² = 0.838; Fig. 4B). The robustness and superior predictive ability of the OPLS-DA model were rigorously verified through 200 permutation tests with R² = (0.0, 0.923) and Q² = (0.0, -0.159), and overfitting was effectively excluded (Fig. 4C). As seen in Fig. 4D, the volcano plot clearly showed significantly different lipidomic profiles of the two groups. The purpose of targeted lipidomics analysis was not only to quantify lipid changes but also to screen for lipid biomarkers to develop diagnostic models. Therefore, we screened for differentially expressed lipids based on the criteria of p < 0.05, VIP > 1, and FC > 2. Ultimately, 256 differentially expressed lipids were identified, made up of 95 downregulated and 161 upregulated lipids (Table S2). Figure 4E shows the heatmap with the differential expression patterns of these lipids.

To determine whether the baseline imbalance in SAS influenced the identified lipid signature, we performed a sensitivity analysis in the discovery cohort by excluding these patients. Finally, 25 RE patients were compared with 53 controls. As shown in Fig. S9, the overall trends for the six lipids remained consistent with previous findings. PE (P-16:0_18:1) and S-1-P remained significantly elevated in RE patients, while DAG (18:1_18:2), DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), and DAG (16:0_18:1) remained significantly reduced. These findings suggest that the identified lipid profiles are not primarily driven by the baseline imbalance in SAS.

The ROC curve analysis evaluated the diagnostic potential of individual differential metabolites and multi-metabolite combinations for RE. As Fig. 8a illustrates, the multi-metabolite combination had better performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 99.45%. To verify the robustness of this biomarker combination, we applied it to an independent validation cohort (Tables S3, S4). The patterns of relative abundance of DAG (18:1_18:2), S-1-P, PE (P-16:0_18:1), DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), and DAG (16:0_18:1) in the validation cohort were similar to patterns observed in that in the discovery cohort (Fig. S10). The PCA of these six lipids displayed considerable differentiation between the RE group and the control group in the validation cohort (Figs. 8b, c). Additional ROC revealed that the AUC of the six combinations of lipids reached 97.17% (95% CI, 94.57% to 99.76%) (Fig. S11). At the optimal cut-off value, the sensitivity of the model was 90.77%, and the specificity was 94.38%. These findings indicate that this multi-metabolite model effectively distinguishes RE from healthy controls, exhibiting strong diagnostic potential.

Discussion

RE imposes a heavy burden on patients and healthcare systems worldwide. Its diagnosis depends on invasive endoscopy, which restricts the possibility of screening of large scale (Richter & Rubenstein, 2018). Saliva, a non-invasive and easily accessible biological fluid, has been found to be diagnostic in pathologies such as salivary gland tumors (Kowalska et al., 2025), periodontal disease (Han et al., 2025), and burning mouth syndrome (Moreau et al., 2023). We have used UHPLC-MS in this study as a strategy for metabolomic and lipidomic analysis of saliva samples in the case of RE patients compared with the control group of healthy individuals. Six significantly dysregulated lipids were identified: DAG (18:1_18:2), S-1-P, PE (P-16:0_18:1), DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), and DAG (16:0_18:1). Furthermore, this model was evaluated in a separate cohort. These findings suggest that salivary lipidomics may have potential value in distinguishing between RE patients and healthy controls. Further research is needed to determine whether it can be used clinically to distinguish RE from other upper gastrointestinal disorders with similar symptoms.

General information on the study population

This study revealed a significantly elevated risk of RE in individuals with SAS, IBS, or constipation. According to the epidemiological analysis, moderate and severe SAS patients are at risk of developing RE threefold more than the healthy control group (Shao et al., 2015). Pathologically, during apneic events, the inspiratory effort causes a sudden drop in intrathoracic pressure, directly compromising the integrity of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES). At the same time, intermittent hypoxia triggers sympathetic nerve activation, indirectly impairing the tension of the LES (Fox et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2017). In patients with diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D), the incidence of RE reaches 97%. The occurrence of these two diseases simultaneously is due to the fact that they both share the pathological feature of visceral hypersensitivity (El‑Salhy et al., 2019). Furthermore, under the combined influence of inflammation, mucosal barrier, and psychological factors, the regulation of the gut-brain axis is disrupted, leading to the mutual promotion and aggravation of these two diseases (Stănişor 2014). Constipation is an independent risk factor for acute exacerbations of RE. During defecation, the intra-abdominal pressure rises sharply, which may cause temporary relaxation of the LES (Wang, 2021). Therefore, clinicians should consider these patients as high-risk individuals and strengthen the monitoring of RE.

Living habits of the study population

The prevalence of sleep disorders was significantly higher in the RE group (p < 0.0001). RE and sleep disorders exhibit a bidirectional relationship. On one hand, RE can disrupt sleep-wake rhythms, thereby triggering sleep disorders. On the other hand, sleep disorders may also exacerbate RE symptoms. Studies indicate that reduced sleep quality is associated with increased nocturnal reflux episodes and prolonged reflux duration (Huang et al., 2024). The proposed mechanisms include weakened esophageal motility, reduced acid clearance capacity, and the loss of the anti-reflux effect of gravity in the supine position (Elkalawy et al., 2024; Fujiwara et al., 2012). Patients with RE consume carbonated beverages and coffee more frequently than healthy controls. A large-scale American cohort study showed that consuming less than two cups (approximately 480 milliliters) of coffee or carbonated beverages per day was independently associated with the alleviation of reflux symptoms (Mehta et al., 2021). These beverages may increase intra-gastric pressure, cause gastric dilation, and reduce the tension of the LES, thereby promoting the occurrence of reflux (Zhang et al., 2021).

Development and validation of a multimetabolite diagnostic model

This study aimed to develop a robust, noninvasive diagnostic model for RE via salivary metabolomics via a stepwise strategy. The initial untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that lipids were the metabolic class with the most obvious abnormalities in RE patients. The pathway enrichment analysis also revealed that the most affected metabolic pathways were sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism. This highlights the central role of lipid metabolism disorders in the pathogenesis of RE. Subsequently, we applied targeted lipidomics methods for precise quantification. We identified a diagnostic panel of biomarkers based on multivariate statistical analysis and machine learning algorithms, and identified six lipids as the diagnostic panel. Through multivariate statistical analysis and machine learning algorithms, we established a diagnostic biomarker panel comprising six lipids. These six lipids were DAG (18:1_18:2), S-1-P, PE (P-16:0_18:1), DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), and DAG (16:0_18:1). The final biomarker combination was successful in verifying the initial untargeted metabolomics findings. Firstly, the lipid molecules in this combination had a direct correlation with the significantly dysregulated pathways in the untargeted results. Additionally, key lipid components such as S-1-P and PE showed consistency in both non-targeted and targeted results. These six lipids were integrated into a multi-metabolite diagnostic model.

The analysis of the ROC curve indicated that the combined model had high diagnostic performance with an AUC of 99.45%. It significantly outperformed a single metabolite. This finding highlights the powerful role of multi-metabolite combinations in capturing the complex pathological process of RE.

Finally, the model was further evaluated in an independent cohort and showed comparable discriminatory performance. Notably, the model also demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy, with an AUC of 97.17%.

One important limitation of this study is that the control group consisted only of healthy individuals and did not include a control group with upper gastrointestinal disorders exhibiting similar symptoms. Moreover, the selection of the healthy control group was based on relatively strict exclusion criteria, and the RE group included some patients with functional gastrointestinal disorders, such as irritable bowel syndrome and constipation. This may have amplified the statistical differences between groups and resulted in higher AUC values, thereby limiting real-world applicability. Therefore, the ability of the lipid panel identified in this study to distinguish RE from other conditions with overlapping symptoms, such as Non-Erosive Reflux Disease (NERD), functional heartburn, or Barrett’s esophagus, cannot be fully determined at this stage. In addition, no biological normalization based on salivary flow rate, osmolality, or total protein content was performed in the present study. Although saliva collection was conducted under standardized conditions, the influence of inter-individual differences in salivary concentration cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should include clinically relevant disease control groups and incorporate appropriate biological normalization strategies. These steps are essential to further evaluate the clinical specificity and translational utility of these lipid biomarkers.

A further limitation of this study relates to the assessment of sample purity. Although saliva was collected under standardized conditions and visually screened for blood, no biochemical hemoglobin testing or formal periodontal examinations were performed. Therefore, the possibility that minor bleeding or subclinical oral pathology may have influenced salivary S-1-P levels cannot be completely excluded. Future studies should incorporate oral health assessment and objective markers of blood contamination to further validate the biological interpretation of salivary S-1-P.

Unlike traditional diagnostic methods, the salivary lipidomics model has the advantages of being non-invasive and suitable for repeated sampling. Therefore, it may represent a promising non-invasive tool for distinguishing patients with RE from healthy controls. However, further validation in clinically representative populations is needed before its application in disease screening and monitoring.

Characteristics of differential lipids in the multi-metabolite model

Multiple diacylglycerol (DAG) species were decreased in the saliva of RE patients, including DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), DAG (16:0_18:1), and DAG (18:1_18:2). This finding is consistent with the glycerophospholipid metabolic disorder observed in the untargeted results. Not only is DAG a precursor in the production of biosynthesis of glycerophospholipids and triacylglycerol, but it is also a vital lipid second messenger (Brindley & Waggoner, 1998). DAG regulates the inflammatory process by stimulating the action of downstream mediators, including protein kinase C (PKC) (Lutkewitte & Finck, 2020). The reduced levels of DAG may be associated with alterations in the PKC pathway and inflammatory regulation. In this context, the reduction in DAG levels may reflect a remodeling of the local lipid metabolic network in RE patients induced by gastric acid exposure and inflammatory stress. Long-term exposure to acid may inhibit the activity of phosphatases such as lipoprotein enzymes, impairing the conversion of phosphatidylcholine (PA) to DAG. Concurrently, during the reparation and regeneration of membranes, which will follow the mucosal injury, DAG may be extensively mobilized. This process leads to an accelerated consumption of DAG (Yao et al. 2025a). Therefore, lower levels of salivary DAG may be a probable RE non-invasive biomarker. This may also indicate a potential link between the DAG signaling pathway and mucosal stability. Impaired DAG signaling may correlate with compromised mucosal defense functions and potentially facilitate disease development (Yao et al. 2025b).

Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) has been validated through untargeted metabolomics as well as targeted lipidomics. This suggests that PE may be associated with pathophysiological changes related to RE. PE is a non-bilayer phospholipid and a major constituent of mitochondrial and cell membranes. It can maintain the integrity of mitochondria and support oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production (Tasseva et al., 2013). High PE levels in the saliva of RE patients may reflect multiple pathophysiological processes. Firstly, PE stimulates the repair of epithelial cells by ensuring membrane fusion (Tsuchiya et al., 2014). Excessive exposure to gastric acid results in damage to the mucosa in RE. The body may initiate compensatory mucosal repair. During this process, the release of PE from shed epithelial cells or salivary glands increases, leading to an elevated level of PE in saliva. Additionally, PE is also involved in the autophagy process to counteract cellular stress (Calzada et al., 2016). Therefore, the elevated PE levels may be associated with enhanced autophagic activity. This is an adaptive response triggered by mucosal damage. However, PE plays an important role in oxidative phosphorylation and maintaining mitochondrial stability. Persistent increase of PE can also be a pointer to a malfunction of mitochondrial activity (Calzada et al., 2016; Tasseva et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2023).

Targeted lipidomics analysis revealed that sphingosine-1-phosphate (S-1-P) is a major differential lipid. This finding validates the results of untargeted metabolomics. S-1-P is a biologic metabolite sphingolipid. It is vital in regulating inflammation and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity (Abbasi & Garcia, 2013; Weigert et al., 2019). In this study, salivary S-1-P levels were significantly elevated in RE patients. S-1-P exerts its effects by binding to G protein-coupled receptors (S1PR1–S1PR5). In epithelial cells, S-1-P binds to S1PR1/3 and activates the NF-κB and STAT3 signaling pathways. This results in higher levels of release of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α (Abbasi & Garcia, 2013; Weigert et al., 2019). Additionally, S-1-P can stimulate the Rho/ROCK signaling by interacting with S1PR2 to cause the contraction of the cytoskeleton and destabilization of tight junction proteins (Abbasi & Garcia, 2013). This mechanism has been reported to cause mucosal damage. In RE, gastric acid reflux can provoke the liberation of S-1-P of the damaged epithelial cells (Young & Van Brocklyn, 2006). Elevated S-1-P may also interfere with tight junctions of the epithelium. This disruption could initiate a vicious cycle that further exacerbates mucosal injury. Moreover, S1PR3 amplifies the signaling of pain in the sensory neurons through TRPV ion channels. This suggests that increased S-1-P levels may be related to worsening heartburn symptoms (Pyne & Pyne, 2017; Takabe & Spiegel, 2014). In salivary glands, the expression of S-1-P transporters, such as Spns2, facilitates the secretion of S-1-P into saliva (Hla & Dannenberg, 2012). In addition, S-1-P is more stable in saliva than in plasma (Proia & Hla, 2015). Therefore, S-1-P may represent a potential salivary biomarker for RE.

This study revealed significant alterations in the salivary lipidome of RE patients. These findings indicate a potential association between lipids and the pathogenesis of the disease. The downregulation of multiple DAGs may reflect a remodeling of lipid metabolism in response to gastric acid, potentially compromising PKC-mediated mucosal defenses. PE upregulation could represent a compensatory response, which has been hypothesized to facilitate membrane fusion and autophagy during mucosal repair. The significant increase in S-1-P may trigger proinflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB/STAT3, potentially damaging tight junction proteins and enhancing pain perception. Overall, these lipid changes may offer valuable insights into the underlying pathophysiology of RE. Finally, the coordinated decrease in DAG species and increase in PE and S-1-P suggests their potential as a non-invasive biomarker panel to distinguish RE from healthy controls.

Conclusions

This study employed metabolomics and lipidomics techniques based on UHPLC-MS, integrating an untargeted screening and targeted validation strategy. The aim was to identify and validate a panel of potential salivary biomarkers for distinguishing RE from healthy controls. The results showed significant lipid metabolic disorders in RE patients. Through systematic analysis, six differentially expressed lipids were screened and integrated into a biomarker model. These six lipids were DAG (18:1_18:2), S-1-P, PE (P-16:0_18:1), DAG (16:0_18:2), DAG (18:1_18:1), and DAG (16:0_18:1). The model demonstrated good reproducibility in the independent validation cohort and showed potential value in distinguishing between RE patients and healthy controls. These findings further suggest that lipid signaling pathways may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of RE. However, the specificity of this biomarker panel for disease diagnosis still needs to be further validated in patients with symptom-overlapping upper gastrointestinal disorders.
