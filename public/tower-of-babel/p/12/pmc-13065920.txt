Abiotic CO2 reduction promoted by carbonate and phyllosilicate minerals on the primitive seafloor
Authors: Yuan Zhong, Ning Zhang, Daoming Huan, Jingxiang Low, Isabelle Daniel, H. James Cleaves, Chao Zhang, Yamei Li, Yawen Jiang, Xinyu Wang, Christopher R. Glein, Jiawei Li, Yu Bai, Yaping Li, Fang Huang, Liping Qin, Andrew H. Knoll, Jihua Hao, Ran Long, Yujie Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Geoelectrochemical reduction of CO2 is proposed as a potentially significant abiotic synthesis pathway catalyzed by sulfide minerals under planetary conditions, but whether this reaction could be catalyzed by geologically abundant carbonate and phyllosilicate minerals is unknown. Here we show that adsorption of trace transition metal cations, such as Cu(II) and Zn(II), endows common Ca/Mg-carbonates and phyllosilicates with high catalytic performance for CO2 reduction to form methane, formic acid, carbon monoxide, and C2 organics. We also observe viable synthesis of C-N bonded compounds (mainly acetamide) when ammonia is present. During these reactions, the adsorbed metal cations are partially reduced into metallic states and become catalytic, while mineral substrates facilitate the water dissociation to supply protons for CO2 hydrogenation. This facile electrochemical reduction of CO2 catalyzed by carbonates and phyllosilicates could facilitate the origin of life on the primitive Earth and help explain the detection of organics on other habitable planetary bodies.

Introduction

The origin of life is a perplexing problem that probably depends on the availability of organic compounds as building blocks for the chemical evolution on the early Earth1–3. Beyond Earth, planetary exploration also focuses on the search for organics as a key criterion for assessing the habitability of extraterrestrial environments for possible life4. In principle, the reduction of inorganic carbon can be driven by hydrogen (H2) or other reducing species (e.g., H2S) released during water–rock interactions in planetary systems5–7. However, these reduction reactions, although thermodynamically favorable in many cases, are often plagued by slow kinetics, even in the presence of mineral catalysts, under temperature-pressure conditions potentially relevant to the emergence of life7.

Recently, the geoelectrical process has been demonstrated to readily drive the reduction of CO2 to more reactive inorganic (e.g., carbon monoxide (CO)) and organic (e.g., formic acid (HCOOH)) species under planetary surface conditions8–10. This geoelectricity, functioning naturally under the same principle as fuel cells, is electron transfer induced and sustained by chemical gradients of redox state, pH, and temperature built across some conductive mineral surfaces during progressive water–rock interactions11–14. This process has been observed both in situ in the modern seafloor and simulation experiments13,14. Geoelectrochemistry is also proposed to have been widespread on the primitive Earth9 and other potentially habitable planetary bodies, like Mars10 and icy moons15, as ubiquitous alteration of the Fe-rich crustal rocks could build steep chemical gradients of pH and redox16,17. However, current geoelectrochemical studies focus mainly on metal sulfides18, largely ascribed to their high electrical conductivity and their occurrence in black smoker-type hydrothermal fields. In reality, alteration of ultramafic rocks in the presence of primitive and planetary seawater containing high concentrations of HCO3−/CO32− but low concentrations of HS− 19–21 would inevitably produce abundant carbonate and phyllosilicate minerals with trace sulfide minerals19,21.

Here, we investigated the catalytic effects of geologically abundant carbonate and phyllosilicate minerals impregnated with transition metal cations on abiotic electrochemical CO2 reduction reactions (CO2RR) using a flow cell (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2), similar to the apparatus used in previous studies (Supplementary Table 1)9,22. Our experiments tested the catalytic performance of synthetic carbonates (MCO3, where M = Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, or Bi) (Supplementary Figs. 3–18), their mixtures, and CaCO3 (calcite) or MgCO3 with surface adsorption of common dissolved transition metal cations. We also tested the catalytic performance of several naturally occurring carbonate or phyllosilicate minerals with surface adsorption of the transition metals. All electrochemical experiments were performed under a wide range of electric potentials (−0.6 to −1.56 V vs. SHE) at pH values relevant to early seawater (pH ~719,23;) or alkaline serpentinization fluid (pH > 1016,24;). Apart from these externally charged experiments, we also designed a dedicated apparatus to simulate geoelectrochemical processes relevant to natural hydrothermal systems of planetary oceans and investigated the feasibility of directly using H2 as the electron donor to drive CO2RR. To improve the conductivity of the systems, we used graphite paper as a substrate electrode supporting carbonate or phyllosilicate minerals, resembling naturally occurring mixture of major minerals with trace but more electrically conductive minerals (e.g., oxides or sulfides). In addition, incorporation or adsorption of the dissolved transition metals could also enhance the conductivity of carbonate or phyllosilicate minerals25,26.

Results and discussion

Electrochemical CO2 reduction catalyzed by carbonate minerals

Our results show very different catalytic performance and selectivity of carbonate minerals for the CO2RR (Fig. 1a). Specifically, Mg/Ca/Sr/Ba/Mn/Fe/Co/Ni-CO3 show no CO2RR yield but H2 as the predominant product, similar to the blank control (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 19). This poor performance likely stems from the low intrinsic reactivities of the corresponding metal sites in carbonates for CO2RR27,28. Indeed, there is no noticeable change in X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of these carbonates after electrolysis (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21), suggesting well-preserved crystal structures. In contrast, Cu2(OH)2/Zn/Cd/Pb/Bi2O2-CO3 exhibit high catalytic performance for CO2RR (Supplementary Table 2). For example, the experiment using Cu2(OH)2CO3 produced substantial reduced C, mainly composed of ethylene (C2H4), ethanol (C2H5OH), and CO, while other experiments predominantly yielded two-electron transfer C1 products (i.e., CO and formate). Moreover, a physical mixture of non-catalytic Ca/Mg-carbonates with those catalytic carbonates could result in a pronounced yield of CO2RR products (Supplementary Fig. 22). Our XRD measurement of the catalytic carbonates after electrolysis shows zero-valent metal peaks (Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, and Bi) (Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating that some metal cations in these minerals were partially reduced to native metals under the applied negative potentials. Thus, these carbonates behaved like the corresponding metals in terms of catalytic performance towards CO2 reduction28. However, alteration of ultramafic/mafic rocks on the early Earth should produce predominantly Ca/Mg carbonate minerals19, similar to the modern Earth, due to the high partial pressure of CO2 in the atmosphere and the high abundances of Ca and Mg in the oceanic crust and seawater19,29. In addition, early anoxic seawater had moderate concentrations of Fe(II) (~1 mM30;) and to a lesser extent, Mn(II) (~0.1 mM31;), which might favor the precipitation of Fe(II)/Mn(II)-enriched carbonate minerals32–34. Nevertheless, the formation of pure Fe(II)/Mn(II)-carbonates was still rarer than Ca/Mg-carbonates in geological records35. More importantly, our results suggest low CO2RR catalytic performance of these geologically abundant carbonate minerals (Ca/Mg/Fe(II)/Mn(II)-carbonates) for CO2RR. In contrast, other transition metal carbonates, despite their better catalytic performance for CO2RR, are less likely to occur naturally, given the low abundances of these metals in the oceanic crust29 and/or their low solubilities in early ferruginous oceans36.

Instead of forming the pure end-member carbonates, trace transition metals tend to be adsorbed onto or incorporated into geologically abundant carbonate minerals, such as Ca- and Mg-carbonates, as water–rock interaction releases alkalinity in nature37. Our results show that the adsorption of catalytic transition metal cations, such as Zn(II) and Cu(II), would greatly improve the catalytic performance of Ca- or Mg-CO3 towards CO2RR (Figs. 1b–e, 2a–c, Supplementary Figs. 24, 25, and Supplementary Tables 3, 4). For example, the adsorption of Zn(II) on Mg- or Ca-CO3 would greatly promote the synthesis of CO and HCOOH, while the Cu(II)-adsorbed carbonate minerals would promote the synthesis of methane (CH4), CO, and C2H4. We further conducted multiple sensitivity tests using Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 as the model catalyst. At lower electric potentials (−0.7 to −0.9 V vs. SHE) relevant to natural geochemical observations13 but lower to the values used in previous studies (Supplementary Table 1), we could still observe the abiotic reduction of CO2 to generate reducing and organic products, including CO and CH4 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 27). Notably, the competing reaction (e.g., H2 formation) becomes increasingly important at lower electric potentials (Supplementary Fig. 27). Moreover, the yield of more complex organics (C2 species) declined substantially (Supplementary Fig. 27). Therefore, the electrical potential should be one major factor to affect the effectiveness and product of geo-eletrochemical reduction of CO2 in natural settings. Here, the −0.7 V sensitivity experiment could be interpreted as a medium or upper bound on plausible rates and product distributions under naturally electrical conditions (0 to −0.95 V vs. SHE; Fig. 1c). Future efforts should measure the catalytic fluxes at lower potentials (<−0.7 V vs. SHE) in order to cover natural abundant conditions. Another potential factor is the CO2 level. Given the large uncertainty about CO2 levels on the early Earth38 and in other planetary systems39,40, we also conducted our catalytic reactions under a wide range of pCO2,g (0.1–1 bar) and, surprisingly, observed little effects on the organic products (Supplementary Fig. 28), particularly at moderate levels of pCO2,g (≥0.2 bar) relevant with the primitive atmosphere.

Our long-term stability tests show that the catalyst still exhibited good performance towards CO2 reduction over 2.5 h of continuous electrolysis (−1.56 V vs. SHE; Supplementary Fig. 29a). However, post-electrolysis SEM imaging revealed signs of catalyst degradation and copper leaching on the carbonate surface, although the substrate showed no obvious damage (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 30). The degradation kinetics can be expected to be much slower at lower electric potential relevant for natural geoelectrochemistry (Supplementary Fig. 29b). Moreover, the catalytic system could be well replenished by re-adsorption of transition metal cations on carbonate substrates during the intervals of weak or no geoelectrochemical activities in nature12. Overall, the observed electrochemical reduction of CO2 catalyzed by Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 (and most likely other mineral systems, considering their shared mechanisms as elaborated below) could occur under a wide range of geological conditions relevant with the early Earth and planetary waters. However, given large uncertainties or unknowns with all of these environmental factors, particularly the wide range of natural electrical potentials, the actual synthesis fluxes and therefore the prebiotic significance of the geo-electrochemical synthesis is hardly quantifiable, at least for now, for the actual geological or planetary settings.

It is also important to note that surface adsorption or incorporation of Cu(II) and Zn(II) cations on Ca/Mg-carbonates might occur prevalently in the primitive ocean. Among all transition metals, Cu and Zn are relatively abundant (~124 and ~106 ppm, respectively; Supplementary Table 5)41 in oceanic crust formed at superfast-spreading ridges characterized by a high melting rate42, potentially relevant to early crustal formation. A recent study revealed that the interaction of early seawater with the reducing primitive mantle would release substantial levels of transition metals, including Cu(II) and Zn(II)17. Subsequent upwelling and mixing of such metal-rich hydrothermal fluids with carbonate-rich seawater19 could favor surface adsorption or co-precipitation of transition metal cations on carbonate minerals. Moreover, the primitive Earth was frequently impacted by meteorites, including iron meteorites rich in Cu and Zn43. Alteration of these meteorites would also generate abundant carbonate with surface adsorption or incorporation of transition metals in a CO2-rich atmosphere at that time44. Apart from these common geochemical processes, one should also acknowledge the possible complexity of primitive environments and surface adsorption. For example, some acidic hot springs or hydrothermal vents would inhibit the precipitation of carbonate minerals45,46 and the surface adsorption of transition metals at low pHs47. Moreover, the transition metals adsorption is also sensitive to competing ions and complexation, which are poorly constrained and likely varied spatially on the early Earth.

Mechanisms of CO2 electrochemical reduction over cation-adsorbed carbonate minerals

To understand the underlying mechanism of CO2 reduction over the cation-adsorbed carbonates, we chose alkaline earth carbonates (AECO3, AE = Mg, Ca, Sr, and Ba) with adsorbed Cu(II) as model systems, and performed a comprehensive evaluation of their electrochemical activities (Supplementary Figs. 31–36). These four mineral systems show large differences in mineral surface area (as measured using Brunner−Emmet−Teller (BET) measurements; Fig. 2f). This total accessible surface would largely affect surface adsorption of transition metal cation and CO2, which is the initial step of electron transfer reactions. It is notable that these four carbonates are particle aggregates with no preferentially exposed crystal facets (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 3–15), instead of finely separated crystals. Thus, the observed catalytic performance is less relevant for any crystallographic preference of adsorbates on the carbonate crystal facets. Unlike the BET, these investigated carbonate systems have similar catalytic performance (Supplementary Figs. 34–36). Coincidently, these systems also have similar electrochemical surface areas (ECSA) (Fig. 2f), which determine the active portion of mineral surface participating in electrochemical reduction. Therefore, the ECSA likely controls the electrochemical steps and catalytic performance in the proposed reaction schedule here. However, broader environmental scenarios would likely require conjoint consideration of both total surface area governed adsorption equilibria and the electrochemical active area.

Our XRD analyses show that the adsorbed Cu(II) cation was partially reduced to metallic Cu(0) dendrite on the carbonate surface upon electrolysis, while AECO3 preserved the crystal structures (Supplementary Figs. 37–41). We further conducted kinetic investigations, including Tafel analysis and kinetic isotopic effect (KIE) of H/D. According to the Tafel slope (close to 120 mV dec−1) on the surface Cu(0), electron transfer is involved in the rate-determining step, whereas the slope decreases in the presence of AECO3, suggesting that the electron transfer is enhanced and the subsequent chemical reaction becomes the rate-determining step (Supplementary Fig. 42)48,49. In addition to the above-mentioned analyses, we also used in situ Raman spectroscopy to monitor the reaction intermediates of CO2 conversion. Compared with the control experiment using only metallic Cu but no carbonate substrates, the Raman spectra of the Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 experiment showed a substantially weaker intensity of the surface-bound *CO peak at 1800–2200 cm−1 during the negative potential scanning (Supplementary Fig. 43a), implying the faster consumption of *CO. Moreover, we observed two peaks at ~1760 and ~1499 cm−1, primarily attributed to the *CHO and *HCHO species (Fig. 3b)50,51, respectively, on Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 during the CO2RR experiment, demonstrating an effective hydrogenation process. Conversely, these Raman fingerprints of hydrogenated intermediates could hardly be detected over the metallic Cu catalyst alone under the same electrochemical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 43b). Altogether, these observations strongly indicate that the adsorbed Cu(II) serves as the catalytic sites for CO2 reduction, while the carbonate substrate necessarily facilitates water activation and proton transfer to accelerate the CO2 hydrogenation and organic synthesis (Fig. 3c). These mechanisms are likely to be generic to alkaline earth carbonates with surface adsorption of other catalytic metal cations, including Zn(II).

Electrochemical synthesis in the presence of nitrogen and phyllosilicates

Besides CO2, ammonia (NH3) may also have been relatively abundant in the reducing waters on the primitive Earth52,53 and other extraterrestrial planetary bodies (e.g., >1 wt% in comets54; ~0.8 wt% in the subsurface ocean of Enceladus55). Moreover, under these reducing planetary conditions, any supply of oxidized nitrogen would tend to undergo reduction to form NH3, which can be catalyzed by minerals56. In our experiments, the introduction of 1 molal NH3·H2O resulted in the generation of detectable levels of acetamide (CH3CONH2, ~0.039 mmolal) in the Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 run, but not in the Zn(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 run (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 44), in addition to the C–H–O organics observed in the NH3-free runs. The synthesis of acetamide on Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 likely results from the nucleophilic addition of NH3 to a surface-bound ketene intermediate that would otherwise form acetate (CH3COOH) in the absence of NH357. However, the facile desorption of *CO (a key intermediate of CO2 conversion) on Zn(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 makes it more difficult for the ketene intermediate, and thus, acetamide fails to form58. Apart from the mineral catalyst, the electrochemical synthesis rate of organic N may be also affected by the concentration and speciation of NH3 in natural settings. In particular, lower NH3 concentration or pH has been shown to undermine the electrochemical selectivity of acetamide59, despite of the different mineral catalyzed used previously.

In addition to carbonates, we also evaluated the catalytic performance of another geologically abundant mineral group, phyllosilicates, and found that the adsorption of catalytic transition metal cations also empowers natural saponite and serpentine (mainly composed of lizardite and clinochlore) (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Figs. 45 and 46). This observation echoed a previous study showing catalytic reduction of CO2 by Cu(II)-adsorbed lunar soil (composed mainly of augite, olivine, and plagioclase) and florisil22. Detailed characterizations revealed that silicate minerals first undergo surface carbonation during the electrochemical CO2RR (Supplementary Fig. 47), consistent with previous observations using in situ Raman spectroscopy22. This surface carbonation occurs readily within seconds, and then, the major catalytic steps for these phyllosilicates are likely same to the carbonate systems, as elaborated above, resulting in their similar catalytic performance (Fig. 1e). In addition, phyllosilicates have surface Lewis and Brønsted acid sites, which could help stabilize intermediates and readily facilitate the transformation of organics3,60. This may help explain the slight difference in the selectivity of organic products compared with carbonate runs (Fig. 1e). Overall, surface adsorption of transition metal cations appears to empower the catalytic performance of geologically abundant sedimentary minerals, particularly carbonates and phyllosilicates, towards abiotic reduction of CO2 on the primitive Earth.

Implications for the habitability of early Earth and other planetary waters

In principle, our observed electrochemical reduction of CO2 catalyzed by metal-adsorbed mineral surfaces could have been important on the primitive Earth. The early Earth had a higher heat flow61 and presumably an enhanced hydrothermal alteration flux. Hydrothermal alteration of early Fe(II)-rich ultramafic crust would release alkalinity, which favors the adsorption or precipitation of transition metal cations on carbonate or phyllosilicate minerals47, and H2 as well as other reductants24. Meanwhile, a higher outgassing rate of acidic volcanic gases, including CO2 and SO2, would result in weakly acidic or circumneutral seawater19,23. Thus, intensive alteration of the early crust could establish large chemical gradients of redox potential, pH, or temperature flanking the serpentinization mineral products, inducing the geoelectricity to drive abiotic synthesis reactions8,9. In addition to seafloor weathering, much more frequent impact events would result in intensive hydrothermal alteration and steep chemical gradients around the craters on the primitive Earth62. These environmental settings would favor the geoelectrochemical reduction of CO2 catalyzed by pervasive transition metal-adsorbed carbonate or phyllosilicate minerals.

To test the feasibility of the electrochemical reaction in naturally relevant settings mentioned above, we built a electrochemical apparatus comprising H2 as an electron source in the oxidation chamber (“Methods”) and Cu(II)-adsorbed carbonate as the catalyst in the reduction chamber (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 48–52). This facility mimics the primitive hydrothermal vent setting where the oxidation of reductants (H2, here) on a conductive surface supplies electrons to reduce oxidants (CO2, here) on the external surface in contact with seawater. We observed facile CO2 conversion (within 1 h) at temperatures (≥523 K; Fig. 4b) relevant to natural hydrothermal settings16. Apart from carbonate or phyllosilicate minerals, geoelectrochemical processes could also be catalyzed by native metal or metal sulfides, as emphasized previously (Supplementary Table 1). These alternative pathways might even become predominant in certain environments where precipitation of sulfide minerals instead of carbonates is favored, such as the acidic hot spring or hydrothermal vents45,46. Nevertheless, reduced carbon products synthesized via all proposed geoelectrochemical processes could play significant roles in the chemical evolution of life on the primitive Earth (Fig. 5a). For example, CO is proposed to be a reactive carbon source that can readily transform into key organics, such as formate and formaldehyde under ambient conditions63,64. CH4 and other hydrocarbons are potent greenhouse gases that could help sustain a clement climate under a faint young Sun65. Moreover, these hydrocarbons could also transform into various building blocks of life upon early UV radiation66. The O-bearing organics, including alcohols and carboxylic acids, are proposed to be involved in primitive metabolisms67. Acetamide can drive the phosphorylation of nucleosides to nucleotides68 and also plays a vital role in the integrity of microbial cell walls69. In addition to O- or N-bearing organics, recent studies also reported electrochemical synthesis of thioesters in the presence of sulfide70. Such geoelectrochemical processes might be important in geological settings on other potentially habitable planetary bodies. For example, alteration of Fe(II)-rich ultramafic crust or impactors could create chemical gradients71,72 and thus, induce geoelectricity on early Mars10, similar to the primitive Earth. Indeed, Steele et al. verified the feasibility of electrochemically driven abiotic synthesis reactions with products similar to those from our experiments10, although they used a mixture of spinel-group minerals and sulfides as mineral catalysts. Such type of water–rock interaction and resulting electrochemical processes may still be occurring in some subsurface aquifers on modern Mars, which could help explain the elusive detection of CH4 in the Martian atmosphere73. In the outer solar system, several icy bodies, including Enceladus, Europa, Ceres, and Pluto, show strong evidence of water–rock interactions74,75 and, in some cases, hydrothermal activity beneath their icy shells76,77 (Fig. 5b). Their rocky mantles/cores are mostly undifferentiated (for small bodies) or less differentiated (for relatively large bodies) than the Earth78, indicating high abundances of transition metals in the seafloor silicates79. Alteration of these rocks would generate large chemical gradients, as well as secondary minerals and/or mineral surfaces rich in transition metals. Under such circumstances, geoelectrochemical processes are likely to occur and foster the availability of organic building blocks and thus support the habitability of these subsurface oceans. Moreover, future space exploration might well manually facilitate this geoelectrochemical process to fix atmospheric CO2 and generate organic compounds for in situ human uses22.

Methods

Sample preparation

In our experiments, 100 mg MCO3 (M = Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Cd, Pb, and Bi) were first dispersed in X mL (0 < X < 10) of deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ cm). Next, (10 − X) mL CuCl2 solution (10 mg mL−1) was added to the dispersion dropwise at room temperature, followed by magnetic stirring for 1 h. The Cu(II)-adsorbed MCO3 was collected by centrifugation, washed twice with DI water, and finally dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight. The Cu(II)-adsorbed MCO3 catalysts with different contents of Cu(II) were prepared by adjusting the volume of the CuCl2 solution.

For the preparation of Zn(II)-adsorbed carbonates, 30 mg MgCO3 or CaCO3 were dispersed in 10 mL Zn(II) solution (67 ppm), followed by magnetic stirring for 1 h. Then, the Zn(II)-adsorbed MCO3 was collected using the same protocols as above. The concentration of Zn(II) (67 ppm) is adopted from the reported abundance of Zn in the continental crust79. Details of the chemicals used are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Electrochemistry experiments

The geoelectrochemical experiments were performed in a three-channel flow cell and controlled by an electrochemical workstation (CHI 660e) equipped with a current amplifier (CHI 680c). To prepare the working electrode, 2.5 mg of catalyst was dispersed by sonication into a mixture of isopropanol (985 μL) and Nafion ionomer solution (5%, Sigma-Aldrich) (15 μL) for 30 min to obtain the catalyst ink. Subsequently, 180 μL of the catalyst ink was dropped onto a graphite paper (YLS 30T, Fuel Cell Store) as the cathode electrode (1.5 × 1.5 cm2). A piece of IrOx-coated Ti foam and saturated Ag/AgCl reference electrode were used as the anode and reference electrodes, respectively. 20 mL catholytes (0.5 molal NaCl saturated with CO2) and 20 mL anolytes (0.5 molal NaHCO3) were separated by a cation exchange membrane (Nafion 115, Fuel Cell Store).

During the experiment, we purged high-purity CO2 (99.999%, Linde) into the cathode, and the gas flow rate was set as 50 mL min−1 via a mass flow controller (D08-1F, Sevenstar). Experiments simulating natural geoelectrochemical processes without externally applied voltage

BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.1Yb0.1O3 − δ (BCZYYb) powders are synthesized by a modified citric acid combustion method (detailed synthesis methods are provided in the Supplementary Material). Anode-supported single cells with the configuration of NiO-BCZYYb || BCZYYb || Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 are fabricated by co-pressing and co-sintering processes. First, NiO and BZCYYb powders are ground uniformly at a mass ratio of 65:35, during which the proper amount of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is added as the pore former. The NiO-BZCYYb-supported BZCYYb bilayers are uniaxially pressed into a Φ13 mm stainless steel mould under 300 MPa, followed by calcination at 1350 °C for 5 h in the air to form half cells. Next, Cu(II)-adsorbed CaCO3 powders are mixed with terpineol in a weight ratio of 1:1.5 to form a uniform slurry. Then, the slurry is brushed onto the surface of the BZCYYb electrolyte to serve as a cathode. In the homemade button cell reactor, humidified CO2 (30 mL min−1) and H2 (15 mL min−1) are fed into the cathode and anode via a mass flow controller, respectively. Under high temperature and atmospheric pressure, the hydrogen oxidation reaction in the anode can power CO2 reduction, and provide protons and electrons.

Mineral characterization and product analyses

Low-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images are collected on a Hitachi 7700 transmission electron microscope. High-resolution TEM images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping profiles are acquired using JEOL JEM-F200 and JEM-2100F transmission electron microscope. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and backscattered electron (BSE) SEM images are acquired on a Gemini SEM 500. Powder XRD is performed on a Philips X’Pert Pro Super X-ray diffractometer (Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å).

Gas chromatography (Agilent 8860 and 7890B gas chromatograph) was used to analyze the gas products (e.g., H2, CO, CH4, and C2H4, etc.). H2 was analyzed by a thermal conductivity (TCD) detector, and CH4 and C2H4 were analyzed by a flame ionization (FID) detector. CO was converted to CH4 via a methanizer and then analyzed by a separate FID detector. The quantification of these gas products was achieved using an external standard method80. The corresponding calibration curves and regression equations are provided now as the new Supplementary Fig. 19. The isotope-labeling products are analyzed with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS, Agilent 7890A and 5975C, He carrier). The organic products (e.g., ethanol, acetamide, etc.) in aqueous phases were analyzed by Bruker DRX Avance 400 MHz NMR spectrometer with water suppression mode. We used noesygppr1d as the pulse sequence program. Typically, 500 μL of the reaction solution after electrolysis was mixed with 100 μL of D2O and 10 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide solution (DMSO, 22 ppm; internal standard). The presented data represents the average results of 64 scans. The full representative NMR spectra is provided as the Supplementary Fig. 44. In situ Raman spectra are recorded on a WITec Alpha 300R Raman spectrometer with a 633 nm laser used as the excitation light source.
