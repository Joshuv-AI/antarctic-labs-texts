Characterization of drought-responsive subtilisin genes in upland rice: the role of OsSub14 in ABA signaling and ROS accumulation
Authors: Yifan Wang, Fang Chen, Wencong Jin, Xiaorong Xiao, Zhixin Xiang, Qiaoyu Tang, Zhi Hou, Yifeng Zhou, Yanke Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought stress presents a significant challenge to rice production, affecting global food security. The protein degradation pathway is vital for regulating plant drought stress responses. Subtilisins, a family of serine proteases, are known to be involved in various stress responses in plants, yet their specific roles in drought tolerance are still not well understood in rice.Results In this study, we identified 13 out of 63 subtilisin genes in upland rice that respond to drought stress. Phylogenetic analysis classified these 13 genes and their homologs into nine distinct groups. Collinearity analysis indicated that subtilisin-encoding genes have undergone evolutionary gene duplication events, with a greater number of collinear gene pairs observed between rice and wheat compared to Arabidopsis and maize. Cis-element analysis revealed stress-related regulatory motifs in the promoters of these 13 genes. Furthermore, qRT-PCR results demonstrated their responsiveness to multiple abiotic stresses, with OsSub14 exhibiting pronounced upregulation under various stress conditions. To investigate its function, we generated rice mutant plants with an OsSub14 frameshift mutation. Phenotypic characterization revealed increased sensitivity of Ossub14 mutant to ABA and elevated ROS accumulation under external stimuli, suggesting its crucial role in regulating ABA signaling and ROS content.Conclusions The study identified and characterized 13 subtilisin genes in upland rice that respond to drought, offering important insights into the role of OsSub14 in the regulation of ABA response and ROS accumulation. Background

As sessile organisms, plants are constantly and directly exposed to environmental fluctuations. With global warming leading to an increased frequency of extreme weather events and water scarcity, drought has emerged as a significant abiotic stress factor that severely affects crop growth, development, yield, and quality [1]. To combat drought stress, breeders have been dedicated to developing water-efficient and drought-resistant crop varieties, with the aim of enhancing drought tolerance through genetic improvement [2]. The technologies for cultivar development have evolved from traditional hybrid breeding to molecular-assisted breeding and now to precision molecular design breeding [3]. However, regardless of the breeding approach, the identification of drought-resistant genes and the elucidation of their functional mechanisms remain fundamental [4]. Therefore, identifying key drought-responsive genes through various strategies, deciphering their molecular mechanisms, and providing critical genetic loci for breeding programs have consistently been primary objectives for researchers engaged in fundamental studies.

Under drought stress conditions, plants accumulate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), singlet oxygen(1O2), superoxide anion (·O2−), and hydroxyl radical (·OH), which disrupt of cellular redox homeostasis [5]. The overproduction of ROS in plant cells due to stress is highly reactive and toxic to proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, ultimately resulting in cellular damage and death [6]. In addition to their toxicity, ROS are also regarded as signaling molecules that activate pathways regulating plant development and responses to abiotic stress [7, 8]. The plant hormone ABA serves as a key regulator of ROS homeostasis and drought stress adaptation in plants [9]. Under drought conditions, ABA biosynthesis is rapidly induced through the upregulation of ABA key biosynthetic enzymes, triggering a complex signaling network mediated by PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors. Consequently, the core ABA signaling cascade is activated, leading to the activation oftranscription factors andion channels (such as SLAC1 and KAT1) that regulate stomatal function, thereby fine-tuning ROS production and scavenging to prevent oxidative damage [10].

The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway serves as the predominant protein degradation system in plants, with numerous associated genes playing significant roles in drought regulation [11]. In addition to the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), plant proteases-including cysteine proteases [12], metalloproteases [13], aspartic proteases [14], and serine proteases [15]-serve as complementary and alternative mechanisms for protein degradation mechanisms that also contribute to drought response. While the ubiquitin-proteasome system is well-characterized, the roles of proteases in drought tolerance remain less explored, and new protease-encoding genes involved in plant drought resistance continue to be identified.

Serine proteases represent a crucial class of proteolytic enzymes in plants, with studies demonstrating their direct involvement in plant senescence and drought tolerance [15–17]. Subtilisins, initially isolated from Bacillus subtilis, constitute a distinct subfamily of serine proteases that are ubiquitously distributed across archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes [18]. The Arabidopsis genome encodes 56 subtilisin-like proteases (SBTs), which have been implicated in diverse biological processes including protein turnover, the development of leaf epidermal cells, seed and fruit maturation, the processing of peptide growth factors, programmed cell death (PCD), and responses to abiotic and biotic stresses [19, 20]. Although 63 subtilisin-encoding genes were identified early in rice (Oryza sativa), functional characterization remains limited to only a subset of these genes [21]. For instance, OsSub4 modulates cold tolerance through cell wall modification, while OsSUBSrP1 (a putative SUBTILISIN-LIKE SERINE PROTEASE 1) participates in anther cuticle biosynthesis and panicle development [22, 23]. Nevertheless, the biological functions of numerous SBTs remain uncharacterized, underscoring the need for further systematic investigation into the roles of subtilisin-like proteases in rice.

In this study, we identified 13 OsSub genes responsive to drought stress from the transcriptome data of the upland rice variety IAPAR9 and analyzed their evolutionary relationships, physicochemical properties, chromosomal distribution, gene and protein structures, and cis-acting elements. Using quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), the tissue-specific expression of these genes and their responses to various stress conditions were examined. Furthermore, we successfully generated OsSub14 knockout plants and identified its role in regulating ABA response and ROS accumulation. This research lays a strong foundation for further investigations into the functions of OsSub genes in rice and provides crucial insights for understanding subtilisin-mediated drought stress regulation and its molecular mechanisms.

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties of the drought‑responsive OsSubs

Compared to lowland rice, upland rice exhibits remarkable water-saving and drought-resistant characteristics, likely attributable to its distinct gene expression patterns under drought conditions [24]. Consequently, conducting transcriptome sequencing and analyzing differentially expressed genes upland rice materials before and after drought treatment serves as an effective approach for identifying key genes involved in the regulation of drought response. In our previous study, we performed transcriptome sequencing on the upland rice cultivar IAPAR9 under both well-watered and drought-stressed conditions (accession number SRP273943). Through comprehensive analysis of these transcriptomic data, the current study has successfully identified 13 differentially expressed OsSubs responsive to drought stress (Table S1). Chromosomal localization analysis demonstrated that these genes were distributed across six chromosomes in the rice genome, including Chr1, Chr2, Chr3, Chr4, Chr5 and Chr9 (Fig. 1).

Chromosomal localizations of drought-responsive OsSubs. 13 drought-responsive OsSubs are unequally localized on 6 chromosomes of rice

The physicochemical properties of the identified 13 OsSubs were first analyzed (Table 1). Among them, OsSub39 and OsSub14 consist of 472 and 496 amino acids, respectively, while OsSub32 is composed of 2731 amino acids. The remaining OsSub proteins each contain approximately 800 amino acids. Among these 13 proteins, 8 were alkaline proteins with OsSub58 exhibiting the highest pI value of 9.37, while the remaining 5 were acidic proteins, among which OsSub27 showed the lowest pI value of 6.1. The protein instability index is an important indicator for assessing protein stability, with values above 40 suggesting lower stability of the protein. Among the 13 OsSubs, 4 (OsSub32, OsSub39, OsSub45, and OsSub14) exhibited instability indices greater than 40, while the remaining nine showed values below 40, indicating that most of the proteins encoded by these drought-responsive OsSubs are stably expressed. Hydrophilicity analysis revealed that 5 OsSubs possess a GRAVY value below 0, indicating a hydrophilic nature, whereas the remaining 8 F-box proteins are hydrophobic with GRAVY values above 0. These analyses provide a comprehensive overview of the physicochemical properties of the 13 OsSub proteins, highlighting their varying sizes, pI values, and stability profiles. The majority of these proteins exhibit stable expression, with specific characteristics such as hydrophilicity suggesting diverse functional roles within the plant. The subcellular localization of these OsSubs was also predicted using WoLF PSORT (Table 1, Fig. S1). Eight OsSubs were localized to the chloroplast (OsSub3, OsSub8, OsSub14, OsSub31, OsSub39, OsSub45, OsSub57, OsSub58), two to the endoplasmic reticulum (OsSub24, OsSub27), two to the extracellular region (OsSub12, OsSub47), and one to the nucleus (OsSub32). These localization patterns indicate that many OsSubs may play significant roles in chloroplast-related processes, while others may be involved in extracellular functions or nuclear activities, thereby contributing to the plant’s overall response to drought stress.

Structures and phylogenetic analysis of the drought‑responsive OsSubs

Phylogenetic relationships, identification of conserved domains, and analysis of gene structure were conducted base on the complete sequences of 13 OsSub proteins (Fig. 2). As illustrated in Fig. 2B, all drought-responsive OsSubs possess both the PA_subtilisin_like domain, characteristic of the subtilisin-like serine protease family, and the Peptidase_S8_3 domain, a distinctive structural feature of the subtilisin superfamily (S8 family). Notably, with the exception of OsSub32 and OsSub39, the remaining 11 OsSubs contain an additional Inhibitor_I9 domain that may competitively bind to the enzyme’s active site, thereby modulating substrate binding efficiency. Furthermore, the fn3_6 domain, potentially functioning as a regulatory module or substrate-binding domain, is present in all drought-responsive OsSub proteins except for OsSub14 and OsSub32. Gene structure analysis revealed that among these 13 genes, four (OsSub31, OsSub45, OsSub24, and OsSub47) contain only one exon, while OsSub57 consists of two exons and one intron. The remaining eight genes exhibit more complex genomic architectures with multiple exons and introns (Fig. 2C). Given the crucial regulatory roles of introns in gene expression, these multi-intron genes may be more susceptible to environmental influences and transcriptional regulation.

To investigate the phylogenetic relationships of these 13 OsSubs between rice and other species, the amino acid sequences of these proteins were used as queries for a BLAST search against GenBank to retrieve homologous sequences from various species. Proteins with sequence similarity greater than 85% were selected for subsequent analysis (Table S2). The results revealed that all 13 genes have homologous counterparts across a range of species, with these homologs were exclusively present in plants, and no detection in microorganisms or animals. This observation suggests that these genes are plant-specific and likely perform unique functions in plants. Further analysis showed that OsSub24 has the fewest homologs with only 2 identified, while OsSub32 has the highest number of homologs with 86. An unrooted phylogenetic tree was then constructed using MEGA11 software based on the alignments using the maximum likelihood method (Fig. 3). Based on phylogenetic relationships, 268 homologs of drought-responsive OsSub proteins in various plants were categorized into 9 distinct groups, labeled Group 1 to Group 9. Within these groups, the genes OsSub8 and OsSub14, along with their homologs, were clustered together; similarly, the genes OsSub24 and OsSub47, along with their homologs, were also grouped; and the three genes OsSub3, OsSub12, and OsSub58, along with their homologs, formed a separate group. The six genes OsSub27, OsSub31, OsSub32, OsSub39, OsSub45, and OsSub57, along with their homologs, each occupied their own individual groups. These findings elucidate the phylogenetic relationships among the 13 OsSub genes, highlighting their plant-specific nature and the diverse distributions of homologs across various species. The presence of distinct clusters within the phylogenetic tree indicates that these OsSubs may share similar structural and functional characteristics, suggesting potential conservation of roles in drought response among different plant species.

Gene duplication and collinearity analysis of the drought‑responsive OsSubs

Gene duplication, a pivotal mechanism in genome evolution, generates novel gene copies through the replication of existing genes, profoundly influencing biodiversity, adaptive evolution, and the emergence of new functions [25]. To investigate whether these 13 drought-responsive OsSubs underwent gene duplication events, we performed gene replication analysis. Three duplication gene pairs were identified (Fig. 4A, Table S3): OsSub47 matched with OsSub10, OsSub12 matched with OsSub50, and OsSub24 matched with OsSub60, highlighting the significance of gene duplication in the evolution of drought-responsive OsSub genes, indicating potential functional diversification that may enhance adaptive responses to environmental stressors.

Collinearity analysis of drought-responsive OsSubs. A Chromosome locations and duplications of 13 drought-responsive OsSubs in rice genome. Bright colors represent gene pairs with colinearity. B Collinearity analysis among Oryza sativa and Triticum aestivum, Zea mays, and Arabidopsis thaliana. The red line indicates the collinear gene pairs

Collinearity analysis serves as a powerful tool to uncover evolutionary conservation and variation in genomes, providing critical insights for comparative genomics and functional evolution studies [26]. The preliminary evolutionary analysis revealed that these 13 drought-responsive OsSubs genes possess homologs across diverse plant species. Therefore, collinearity analysis was performed between rice and three representative plants: wheat, maize, and Arabidopsis (Fig. 4B, Table S4). The results demonstrated that rice shares three homologous gene pairs with Arabidopsis, thirty-one with wheat, and ten with maize. The notably higher number of homologous gene pairs between rice and wheat may be attributed to polyploidization events preserving a greater number of ancestral genomic segments in wheat, which preserved more ancestral genomic segments shared with rice.

Cis-elements in the promoters of the drought‑responsive OsSubs

Cis-elements within plant genomes precisely regulate gene expression patterns by binding specific transcription factors, thereby influencing plant growth and development, stress resistance, metabolic pathways, and agronomic traits [27]. Therefore, identifying cis-elements associated with target genes is essential for understanding gene expression regulation, plant development, environmental adaptation, and crop improvement. In this study, we analyzed the 2-kb upstream sequences of 13 drought-responsive genes to investigate their potential cis-elements, providing insights into the functional roles and regulatory mechanisms of these genes (Table S5). A total of 25 distinct cis-elements were identified in the promoter regions of these 13 genes and classified into four categories: light response, stress response, hormone response, and growth and development (Fig. 5A). Within the stress-responsive cluster, we detected three cis-elements involved in drought, low-temperature, and anaerobic responses, along with a broad-spectrum defense and stress-responsive element, suggesting that these 13 genes may contribute to abiotic stress resistance. Additionally, five hormone-responsive elements associated with gibberellin (GA), auxin (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), salicylic acid (SA), and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) were identified, implying that these 13 OsSubs genes may be regulated by hormonal signaling pathways and could mediate stress responses and developmental processes in rice.

Different cis-elements are represented by different colored boxes. B The statistical results of the occurrence frequency of various cis-elements in each drought-responsive OsSubs. C Statistical analysis of cis-elements across 13 drought-responsive OsSubs promoters

In addition, we conducted a statistical analysis of the occurrence frequency of various cis-acting elements across the studied genes. Specifically, the light-responsive elements GAATA-motif and G-box, along with the hormone-responsive elements TGACG-motif and ABRE (abscisic acid-responsive element), showed the highest occurrence frequencies among the different genes (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, several functionally important cis-elements were identified in more than 10 drought-responsive OsSubs, including: Light-responsive elements: GT1-motif, GATA-motif, and G-box, Hormone-responsive elements: ABRE and TGACG-motif, Stress-responsive element: ARE (antioxidant response element), Growth and development-related element: CAT-box (Fig. 5C). The results strongly suggest that these OsSubs are likely involved in regulating various biological processes, including light signaling, hormone responses, stress adaptation, and growth/development. These findings underscore the critical role of cis-elements in regulating the expression of drought-responsive OsSub genes, revealing their potential involvement in various biological processes such as stress adaptation, hormonal signaling, and developmental pathways and enhancing our understanding of their contributions to plant resilience and growth under challenging environmental conditions.

Tissue specific expression analysis of the drought‑responsive OsSubs

To fulfill the specialized functional requirements of different tissues, plants precisely regulate gene expression in a tissue-specific manner. This sophisticated mechanism ensures the production of necessary proteins while minimizing wasteful transcription and translation processes, thereby optimizing energy utilization. Investigations into plant tissue-specific gene expression provide crucial insights into gene functions related to growth regulation, developmental control, and stress response mechanisms. To investigate the tissue-specific expression of 13 drought-responsive OsSubs, we conducted qRT-PCR experiments on rice roots, the first internode, second internode, third internode, leaf sheath, and leaf tissues from plants grown under normal conditions for 2 months. The results were presented in the form of a bubble plot, where larger bubble sizes and darker colors indicate higher gene expression level. Except for OsSub58, which was undetectable in all five tissues, the expression profiles of the other 12 genes were successfully mapped (Fig. 6A). The results indicated that the expression levels of OsSub3, OsSub57, OsSub12, OsSub39, and OsSub8 were significantly higher in aboveground tissues compared to roots. Among these, OsSub3, OsSub39, and OsSub8 exhibited the highest expression in the leaf sheath, while OsSub57 and OsSub12 were predominantly expressed in the leaves. OsSub14 displayed comparable expression levels in both roots and leaf sheaths, with levels higher than those in other tissues. OsSub31 exhibited the highest expression in the first internode and the lowest in the third internode, whereas OsSub47 demonstrated the opposite pattern, with the lowest expression in the first internode and the highest in the third internode. Notably, OsSub32 had the highest expression in the leaves, exceeding that of the roots by over 300 times, and also displayed high levels in the leaf sheath, which were more than 80 times that of the roots. However, it exhibited the lowest expression in the second internode, at only 0.06 times that of the roots. OsSub45 exhibited higher expression levels in all internodes compared to other tissues, with the highest expression in the first internode and the second highest in the second internode. In contrast, OsSub27 had lower expression levels across all internodes compared to other tissues, with the lowest expression observed in the second internode; however, this gene displayed the highest expression in the leaves, more than three times that of the roots. These findings suggest that each drought-responsive OsSub gene exhibited a distinct tissue expression pattern, indicating their potential involvement in the regulatory mechanisms of drought stress.

Expression profiles of the drought‑responsive OsSubs under abiotic stress and exogenous ABA treatment

In this study, we identified that 13 out of the 63 OsSubs in rice are responsive to drought stress, suggesting that these genes may play a significant role in the regulation of drought stress. Additionally, our analysis of the promoters of these 13 genes revealed the presence of cis-elements that respond to various abiotic stresses, indicating their potential involvement in response to other abiotic stress conditions. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted a qRT-PCR study to assess the expression levels of these 13 genes under different abiotic stress treatments. Two-week-old ZH11 seedlings were treated with 20% PEG6000, 150 mM NaCl, high temperature (40 °C), low temperature (4 °C), and 50 µM ABA for 3, 6, and 12 h. Subsequently, the transcriptional expression of the 13 drought-responsive OsSubs was assessed. The results indicate that, consistent with the tissue-specific expression analysis, OsSub58 was undetectable, while the expression of all other genes was successfully confirmed.

Under PEG6000-induced osmotic stress (Fig. 6B), the expression levels of OsSub32, OsSub12, OsSub39, OsSub45, OsSub24, and OsSub27 were downregulated by more than twofold at all time points. Additionally, OsSub3 and OsSub31 exhibited slight downregulation at each of the three time points. The expression of OsSub47 showed minimal change at 3 h, followed by a gradual decrease at 6 and 12 h. In contrast, OsSub57, OsSub8, and OsSub14 displayed a wave-like pattern, initially upregulated and subsequently downregulated across the three time points. Overall, these findings suggest that the expression of these genes is modulated under osmotic stress, indicating their potential role in regulating osmotic stress responses.

Under salt stress induced by 150 mM NaCl (Fig. 6C), OsSub14 was upregulated at all three treatment time points, while OsSub8 exhibited a slight upregulation at 3 h, followed by significant downregulation at 6 and 12 h. With the exception of these two genes, the remaining ten genes were downregulated to varying degrees across all three time points, with OsSub12, OsSub39, and OsSub45 showing the most pronounced trends, exhibiting over fivefold downregulation at each time point. The strong transcriptional response of these genes to salt stress indicates their probableinvolvement in the regulatory processes of salt stress signaling.

High temperature is a significant abiotic stress factor that rice often encounters. Under 40 °C heat stress (Fig. 6D), the expression levels of OsSub57, OsSub32, OsSub39, OsSub8, and OsSub45 were downregulated across all three treatment time points. In contrast, OsSub14 was the only gene consistently upregulated at all time points, showing a gradual increasing trend. OsSub3, OsSub31, OsSub12, and OsSub27 exhibited an initial downregulation followed by gradual recovery or slight upregulation relative to the 0-hour baseline. The expression of OsSub47 increased initially before decreasing at the three time points, while OsSub24 demonstrated an initial increase followed by a decrease and then another increase. These results suggest that OsSub genes are likely involved in the signaling regulation of heat stress.

Under 4 °C cold treatment (Fig. 6E), the expression levels of OsSub3, OsSub57, OsSub32, OsSub12, OsSub39, and OsSub27 were downregulated at all three time points. OsSub47, OsSub8, and OsSub45 showed a pattern of slight upregulation followed by downregulation. OsSub24 was downregulated initially but recovered to levels comparable to baseline expression. Notably, OsSub14 was significantly upregulated at all three time points, while OsSub31 showed a slight upregulation. Overall, most genes exhibited a strong transcriptional response to low temperature, suggesting their important role in regulating cold stress.

When plants are subjected to stress, the internal ABA levels typically rise, and plants regulate their resistance to abiotic stress through ABA-dependent signaling pathways, which is why ABA is often referred to as a stress hormone. To explore whether the transcription of OsSub genes is also influenced by ABA, we applied a similar treatment to the aforementioned abiotic stress conditions, using 50 µM ABA to treat 2-week-old ZH11 seedlings for 3, 6, and 12 h. The qRT-PCR results indicated that most genes responded to ABA (Fig. 6F), with OsSub3, OsSub14, OsSub47, and OsSub57 showing the most significant upregulation, while OsSub12 exhibited the most pronounced downregulation. OsSub8 was initially upregulated and then downregulated. Although OsSub32, OsSub45, and OsSub24 were also downregulated, the magnitude of their downregulation was relatively small. OsSub31, OsSub39, and OsSub27 displayed a wave-like expression pattern, characterized by initial downregulation, followed by upregulation, and then another downregulation. Compared to the previously mentioned abiotic stresses, only a subset of OsSub genes showed a strong response to ABA. These findings suggest that these drought-responsive OsSubs, at least some of them, might regulate stress responses in an ABA-dependent manner.

OsSub14 is involved in ABA response and ROS accumulation in rice

Under drought stress conditions, plants exhibit increased biosynthesis of abscisic acid (ABA), which enhances their drought resistance [10]. Expression profiling analysis of 13 drought-responsive OsSubs revealed that OsSub14 showed significant transcriptional changes in response to PEG, ABA, NaCl, 40 °C heat, and 4 °C cold treatments, suggesting its potential crucial regulatory role in stress responses. Based on this, we generated a knockout mutant of this gene and successfully obtained homozygous Ossub14 mutant. In this mutant, a base G was inserted into the second exon of OsSub14, resulting in a frameshift mutation in the protein and causing premature termination of translation at the 52nd amino acid position (Figure S2). Then, uniformly germinated ZH11 and Ossub14 seeds were cultured on half-strength MS medium containing 2.5 µM or 5 µM ABA for 4–5 days. No phenotypic differences were observed between ZH11 and Ossub14 under normal growth conditions. However, ABA treatment (2.5 µM and 5 µM) resulted in significantly shorter shoots and roots in Ossub14 compared to wild-type plants, indicating that OsSub14 negatively regulates ABA signaling transduction (Fig. 7).

ROS are metabolic byproducts generated in plants under stress conditions, exerting severe toxic effects on plant growth. To investigate whether OsSub14 influences ROS accumulation in rice and consequently affects drought resistance, detached leaves from 2-month-old ZH11 wild-type and Ossub14 mutant plants were subjected to ABA, NaCl, and H2O2 treatments respectively and ROS levels were assessed via DAB and NBT staining (Fig. 8). Results suggested no significant differences in DAB and NBT staining between ZH11 and Ossub14 under control conditions. However, ABA and NaCl treatments triggered markedly deeper DAB and NBT staining in Ossub14 compared to ZH11. Strikingly, H₂O₂ treatment resulted in deeper DAB staining but negligible NBT staining in Ossub14, indicating minimal singlet oxygen production. These findings demonstrate that OsSub14 is involved in ROS accumulation in rice under external stimuli.

These findings underscore the significant role of OsSub14 in regulating ABA signaling and ROS accumulation under external stimuli conditions. The observed phenotypic differences in the Ossub14 knockout mutant highlight its negative regulatory effect on ABA-mediated growth responses. Additionally, the increased ROS accumulation in Ossub14 under external stimuli treatments suggests controlling the content of ROS is one of the ways to regulate the plant’s response to external stimuli. Overall, this research provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms through which OsSub14 modulates stress responses in rice, indicating its potential as a target for improving stress tolerance in crops.

Discussion

Due to long-term adaptation to different moisture environments, upland rice and lowland rice exhibit significant differences in drought resistance. Under natural selection in prolonged drought conditions, upland rice has developed characteristics different from those of rice, such as morphological traits with a well-developed root system, enhanced osmotic regulation capabilities and antioxidant defense systems, unique gene expression patterns and regulatory signaling pathways and unique cis-regulatory elements or single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within its drought resistance genes [24, 28–31]. Given that upland rice has evolved more refined drought resistance mechanisms, its genome may contain drought resistance genes that have weakened or been lost in lowland rice. Therefore, utilizing upland rice for the exploration of drought resistance genes can not only uncover new regulatory factors but also reveal potential targets for improving drought resistance in lowland rice.

The subtilisin family consists of a large group of serine proteases that are widely distributed across archaea, bacteria, eukaryotes, fungi, and yeasts [18]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the 13 drought-responsive subtilisin genes have homologous genes present in plants, while no closely related homologs were found in bacteria or animals. This indicates that these 13 genes are unique to plants and likely play specific roles, such as participating in the regulation of drought stress. Through synteny analysis, we identified three pairs of syntenic gene in rice, all of which are subtilisin genes, suggesting that despite only 63 subtilisin genes in rice, they have undergone gene duplication events during evolution. Furthermore, interspecies synteny analysis showed that, compared to Arabidopsis and maize, the 13 subtilisin genes in rice are associated with a greater number of syntenic gene pairs in wheat. It is possible that the following two reasons led to this result: (1) Shared ancestry and divergence: Rice (Oryza sativa, diploid), wheat (Triticum aestivum, hexaploid), and their common ancestral grasses all diverged from a common monocot ancestor [32]. Following their divergence, each lineage underwent its own unique evolutionary trajectory. Rice has maintained a largely diploid and stable genome, while wheat experienced a series of recent allopolyploidization events. (2) The gene dosage effect of polyploidization in wheat: The modern hexaploid wheat genome (AABBDD) is the result of two hybridization and polyploidization events: first between Triticum urartu (AA) and a species similar to Aegilops speltoides (BB) to form tetraploid emmer wheat (AABB), and later a hybridization between emmer wheat and Aegilops tauschii (DD) to form hexaploid wheat (AABBDD) [33, 34]. A critical consequence of these events is gene dosage preservation [35]. When the diploid progenitors (each with two copies of every gene, one per chromosome) merged, the resulting polyploid initially contained multiple homologous copies (homeeologs) for a vast majority of genes-effectively six copies for many genomic regions once the genome stabilized. During the process of diploidization, many redundant genes were lost, but a significant fraction was retained. This means that for a single ancestral gene that existed in the common ancestor of rice and wheat, wheat has a higher probability of retaining at least one functional copy across its three subgenomes (A, B, and D). Thus, the hexaploid wheat genome acts as a “multi-copy archive” of ancestral grass genes. Its polyploidization history provided a buffer against gene loss, effectively preserving a greater number of ancestral genomic segments that are still recognizable and share synteny with those in the diploid rice genome. This genomic structure significantly increases the probability of identifying orthologous pairs between rice and wheat.

The interplay between positive and negative regulators is crucial for maintaining a balanced ABA signaling pathway. Previous studies have highlighted the complexity of ABA signaling, where the positive regulators play a crucial role in it. Positive regulators like SnRK2s are crucial for the activation of ABA signaling pathways, promoting responses such as stomatal closure and stress tolerance. For instance, the SnRK2.6 (OST1) kinase has been well-documented for its role in mediating ABA-induced gene expression and physiological responses [36]. Besides, ABFs also act as positive regulators by binding to ABA response elements in target genes, thereby facilitating the expression of genes that promote stress tolerance [37]. In contrast, our findings regarding OsSub14 as a negative regulator underscore its unique role in fine-tuning the ABA signaling pathway, with its mutation resulting in heightened sensitivity to ABA. As a negative regulator of ABA signaling, OsSub14 may contribute to the regulation of this pathway by specifically cleaving and degrading positive regulators, such as SnRK2s or ABFs, which modulates their activity. Another possibility is that OsSub14 plays a role in the feedback regulation of ABA by influencing the transcriptional expression of positive regulators, thereby affecting ABA signaling. Both of these possibilities require validation at the protein and gene transcription levels. Moreover, the role of subtilisins, including OsSub14, in stress responses is an emerging area of research. While many studies have focused on the involvement of subtilisins in plant defense mechanisms, our work emphasizes their potential role in hormonal regulation, particularly in the context of ABA. This adds a new dimension to the functional characterization of subtilisin genes, suggesting that they may have broader implications in plant stress physiology than previously recognized.

Regarding the functions of subtilisin, past research has mainly focused on its roles in plant defense, such as pathogenicity and programmed cell death [19]. There are some reports, though relatively few, on its response to drought stress and involvement in ABA signaling regulation. In addition to our study, which identified 13 subtilisin genes that respond to drought stress and participate in ABA signaling in rice, there are other reports in different species. For example, in Arabidopsis, the subtilisin gene SASP is involved in the regulation of ABA signaling and drought stress [15]. SASP interacts with the key regulatory protein OST1 in the ABA signaling pathway and regulates its protein abundance. Additionally, SASP mutations enhance drought resistance in plants. In common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.), two novel subtilases genes PvSLP1 and PvSLP2 were found to response to drought stress [38]. In recent years, researchers found that the expression of subtilisin genes can respond to various abiotic stresses in cotton, including drought, and that interference with the GhSBT27A gene reduced the plant’s resistance to osmotic stress [39]. Cui et al. conducted a quantitative analysis of 18 identified subtilisin-like protease genes in maize and found that most respond to drought stress [40]. By applying drought treatment to three different maize varieties, they observed varying levels of drought resistance, suggesting that subtilisin-like protease-encoding genes may be involved in regulating drought stress. Li et al. constructed an expression profile of subtilase-encoding genes in peanuts under hormone treatments, including ABA, and drought stress [41]. These studies suggest that subtilisin genes may play a role in ABA and drought stress regulation across different plants.

Environmental stress often leads to the accumulation of ROS, while plants can mitigate ROS damage by regulating intracellular ROS levels, thereby alleviating stress responses [42]. Additionally, ABA plays a crucial regulatory role in plant stress resistance. When plants face adverse stress, ABA levels typically increase, activating the internal ABA signaling pathway, which ultimately regulates plant resilience by modulating stomatal closure or the expression of other stress-related genes [43]. In this study, we found that under salt stress and ABA treatment, the accumulation of ROS in Ossub14 was significantly higher than in ZH11, indicating that OsSub14 has a regulatory function on ROS. Furthermore, we observed that OsSub14 is more sensitive to exogenous ABA, suggesting that OsSub14 may participate in drought stress responses in an ABA-dependent manner. However, this work still has certain limitations. That is, although we obtained the corresponding OsSubs genes under drought conditions through transcriptome analysis, currently only one gene involved in ABA signal regulation has been identified. Whether this gene has the function of regulating drought stress still needs further research. Additionally, the functions of the other genes responding to drought stress also need to be further explored. Future work will focus on the functional characterization of OsSub14 and other drought-responsive OsSubs in drought stress and the elucidation of their underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

In this study, transcriptome data analysis revealed that 13 out of 63 subtilisin-encoding genes in rice can respond to drought stress. The expression of these genes varied across different tissues and under various abiotic stress treatments. Notably, OsSub14 exhibited significant upregulation under PEG, NaCl, high temperature, low temperature, and ABA treatments, suggesting that OsSub14 is likely one of key genes involved in abiotic stress within the subtilisin gene family. Phenotypic analysis indicated that the Ossub14 mutant showed increased sensitivity to exogenous ABA. Additionally, under ABA, NaCl, and H2O2 treatments, the Ossub14 mutant accumulated higher levels of ROS, implying that OsSub14 may regulate ROS accumulation in rice through an ABA-dependent pathway in response to stress. This study lays the foundation for further elucidating the functions of subtilisin family members in abiotic stress in rice, particularly drought stress.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The Brazilian upland rice (cv. IAPAR9) was used for drought treatment and RNA sequencing. The ZhongHua11 (ZH11) rice variety was used for total RNA extraction and gene expression analysis, including tissue-specific expression and abiotic stress response analysis. ZH11 and Ossub14 mutant were used for functional characterization of OsSub14.

Identification of drought-responsive OsSubs in upland rice

In previous study, we subjected the upland rice variety IAPAR9 to drought treatment, and leaf tissue was collected for total RNA extraction followed by transcriptome sequencing (accession number: SRP273943) [29]. Differentially expressed genes (|Log2FoldChange|>1, P < 0.05) affected by drought stress were analyzed, and functional annotation was performed on these genes. To screen and identify drought-responsive subtilisin genes, the “subtilisin” were used as a keyword to search in the list of differentially expressed genes, resulting in the identification of 13 subtilisin-related genes. Furthermore, we have provided the expression levels of all subtilisin genes (Table S1). However, in subsequent qRT-PCR analyses, the expression of the differentially expressed gene OsSub58 was not able to be detected, suggesting the low level of transcriptional expression level, making it unlikely to have a functional role.

Method of OsSub14 gene editing

To obtain Ossub14 mutant, the gene editing targart primers were designed by the online website (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/crispr2/) on the first exon of OsSub14 (Target sequence: TGCGATGCGGTGACCACAGATGG). The primers were annealed with 5 µL F primer (10 µM) and 5 µL R primer (10 µM) and the reaction was performed in a thermocycler using the following parameters: 37℃, 30 min; 95℃, 5 min and then ramped down to 25℃ at 5℃/min. At the same time, the construct Osu6-2sk was digested with BbsI and purified. Then ligation reaction with 1 µL (50 ng) BbsI digested Osu6-2sk construct, 1 µL annealed oligo duplex, 1 µL 10×T4 DNA ligase Buffer, 6 µL ddH2O and 1 µL T4 DNA Ligase was carried out and incubated at 4℃ for 4 h followed by transformation. After that, KpnI and HindIII were used for digesting sgRNA-Osu6-2sk, HindIII and EcoRI were used for digesting 35 S-CAS9-SK, KpnI and EcoRI were used for digesting pCAMBIA 1300, respectively. At last, the three fragments were linked together by T4 DNA ligase and the recombinant construct was used for rice transformation through Agrobacterium-mediated method.

Prediction of physicochemical properties and subcellular localization

The physicochemical properties, including molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability index, and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of the 13 subtilisin proteins from rice were obtained using the Expasy website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Additionally, these protein sequences were submitted to the WoLF PSORT website for the prediction of their subcellular localization (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Analysis of conserved motifs, protein domains, and gene structure

The amino acid sequences of the 13 subtilisin proteins from rice were submitted to the ClustalW tool in MEGA12 software for sequence alignment. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method (Bootstrap = 1000) and saved in “NWK” format. The MEME website was utilized to search for conserved motifs in these 13 protein sequences, setting the number of motifs to be retrieved at 15, with results saved in “XML” format. The Batch CD-search tool on the NCBI website was used for bulk searches of conserved domains, and the “Hitdata” file was saved. Gene annotations for these 13 subtilisin proteins were extracted using the “GXF Select” tool in TBtools and saved in “GFF” format. Finally, the “NWK” file of the phylogenetic tree, the “XML” file of conserved motifs, the “Hitdata” file of protein domains, the “FASTA” file of protein sequences, and the “GFF” file of gene annotations were submitted to the “Gene Structure View (Advanced)” tool in TBtools for visualization.

Phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequences of the 13 subtilisin proteins from rice were submitted to the Protein BLAST tool on the NCBI website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi? PROGRAM=blastp&PAGE_TYPE=BlastSearch&LINK_LOC=blasthome) for homologous comparison, and sequences with more than 85% homology were downloaded. The ClustalW tool in MEGA12 software was used to perform sequence alignment of these 13 rice subtilisin proteins and 268 homologous proteins, followed by the construction of a phylogenetic tree using the maximum likelihood method (Bootstrap = 1000). The phylogenetic tree was then Optimized using the iTOL website (https://itol.embl.de).

Collinearity analysis

Genome assembly and protein files for wheat, maize, and Arabidopsis were obtained from the Ensembl Plants website (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). The genome assembly and protein files for rice were submitted alongside those of wheat, maize, and Arabidopsis to the “One Step MCScanX - Super Fast” tool in TBtools for collinearity analysis. The genome assembly file of rice was also submitted to the “Fasta Window Stat” tool in TBtools to obtain gene density files. The “Advanced Circos” tool was used to visualize the collinearity within the rice species and its gene density. The “CTL,” “Collinearity,” and “GFF” files obtained from the collinearity analyses of rice with wheat, maize, and Arabidopsis, along with the gene IDs of the 13 rice subtilisin proteins, were submitted to the “Dual Systeny Plot for MCScanX” tool for visualization, which was further Optimized using Adobe Illustrator2021.

Cis-elements analysis in the promoters of drought responsive OsSubs

The 2000-base-pairs promoter sequences upstream of ATG start codon of the 13 subtilisin genes were extracted from the rice genomic sequence and submitted in FASTA format to the PlantCARE website for cis-element prediction. The results obtained from the website were subjected to advanced filtering to select the desired cis-regulatory elements, which were then visualized using TBtools for further analysis.

Seedlings treatment and gene expression analysis

The rice variety ZH11 was cultivated in soil for 2 months. After this period, roots, first internodes, second internodes, third internodes, flag leaves, and leaf sheaths were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted and reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). The reaction system was as follows: 2 µL of 5×gDNA Eraser Buffer, 1 µL of gDNA Eraser, 2 µg of total RNA, and RNase Free dH2O added to a total volume of 10 µL, incubated at 42 °C for 2 min, then cooled on ice. Next, 1 µL of PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix I, 1 µL of RT Primer Mix, 4 µL of 5×PrimeScript Buffer 2, and 4 µL of RNase Free dH2O were added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min and at 85 °C for 5 s. The reaction was then stored at −20 °C. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was conducted for tissue specificity analysis of the 13 OsSubs. The sequences of primers are listed in Table S6.

To investigate whether the transcriptional expression of the 13 OsSubs was affected by different abiotic stresses, uniformly grown ZH11 seedlings were hydroponically treated for 2 weeks with 20% PEG6000, 150 mM NaCl, 40 °C, 4 °C, and 50 µM ABA. Leaf samples were collected at 0 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 12 h post-treatment, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted for reverse transcription into cDNA for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis. qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with the following reaction system: 5 µL of 2× ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 1 µL of cDNA template, and 3.2 µL of deionized water, totaling 10 µL. The qRT-PCR reaction was conducted on an ABI StepOne Plus system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with the following program: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, including a melting curve analysis using the instrument’s default program. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. All qRT-PCR results were calculated from three biological replicates, each value of the biological replicates was the mean of triplicate quantification (technical replicates).

ABA treatment and phenotype analysis

Seeds of ZH11 and Ossub14, harvested at the same time, were placed in petri dishes containing tap water and germinated at 30 °C for 2 days. The uniformly germinated seeds were then transferred to MS liquid medium containing 0, 2.5 µM, and 5 µM ABA, and cultivated at 30 °C for 4 days. The phenotypes were observed, and root and shoot lengths were measured. This experiment was repeated at least three times and yielded similar results each time.

Stress treatment and ROS staining

For the detached leaf treatment, leaves from 2-month-old rice plants grown in soil under normal conditions were excised into 1–2 cm segments and incubated in MS liquid medium supplemented with either 5 µM ABA, 150 mM NaCl, or 1% H₂O₂. The samples were then maintained under standard growth conditions for 3 days.

For DAB and NBT staining, DAB and NBT solutions were prepared at a working concentration of 1 mg/mL in distilled water. Leaves of ZH11 and Ossub14 (both untreated and treated) were submerged in the respective staining solutions and vacuum-infiltrated overnight. After incubation, the solutions were removed, and the leaves were destained in a graded ethanol series (100%, 90%, and 75%) for 10 min per step until complete chlorophyll removal. Images were captured after decolorization.

Statistical analysis method

Statistical analyses were conducted using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The significance of differences in gene expression levels between the various treatments and the control was assessed using Student’s t-test (*P < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using R software, with three sample replicates for each group of data. Line and bar charts were created using R, while the heatmap was generated using TBtools.
