Conservative management of mad honey poisoning presenting with bradycardia: a case report
Authors: Sushan Ghimire, Salina Subedi, Navaraj Paudel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMad honey is a type of honey with hallucinogenic properties found in the Himalayas of Nepal. It is used by locals for its supposedly medical and recreational properties. It contains a toxin named ‘Grayanotoxin’ which is mainly derived from the nectar of few Rhododendron species native to Himalayas.Case presentationThis is a case report of 38-year-old male who presented to emergency department of our center with dizziness, three hours following ingestion of mad honey. He had heart rate of 45 beats per minute. Electrocardiogram showed bradycardia without any other abnormality. He was managed conservatively in coronary care unit for 48 h without any complication.Clinical discussion“Grayanotoxin” is a neurotoxin that acts on voltage-gated sodium channels, preventing their inactivation and causing persistent depolarization, leading to a wide range of symptoms. Early recognition prevents unnecessary investigations and interventions.ConclusionMad honey poisoning should always be considered in patient particularly those living in endemic areas presenting with unexplained dizziness and bradycardia. Most of these cases respond well to supportive therapy emphasizing the importance of early recognition and close clinical observation.Keywords: Mad honey, Grayanotoxin, Bradycardia, Conservative, Atropine, Case report

Introduction

Mad honey is commonly used by people in mountainous region of Nepal, Tibet, Turkey, India, and some parts of south America in belief of its use in various conditions like hypertension diabetes, gastritis, ulcers, infection and cold [1, 2]. Grayanotoxin present in wild honey is responsible for rare occurrence of wild honey poisoning. Patient may present with wide variety of signs and symptoms like dizziness, nausea, vomiting, sweating, decreased consciousness, hypersalivation, paresthesia, hypotension, bradycardia and cardiac arrhythmias [3].

Here we present a case of mad honey poisoning in a 38-year-old male presenting to Emergency with symptomatic bradycardia and hypotension who was managed conservatively.

This work has been reported in line with CARE checklist [4].

Case presentation

A 38-year-old male presented to Emergency with complaints of dizziness and vomiting three hours following the ingestion of wild honey (about one tablespoon in one glass of warm water). Dizziness was acute in onset (half an hour after ingestion of wild honey) and was associated with tingling sensation all over the body. There was no history of syncope, loss of consciousness, vomiting, abnormal body movement, chest pain, palpitations, shortness of breath. He had no significant past medical and surgical history. There was no history of cardiac disease, drug intake and toxin exposure other than honey consumption.

On presentation, the patient appeared anxious and oriented to time, place and person with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15/15 and bilaterally round, regular and reactive pupils. His heart rate was 45 beats/minute, blood pressure was 90/60 mmHg, respiratory rate was 20 breaths/minute, sPO2 was 98% in room air and temperature was 98°F. His general random blood sugar (GRBS) was 174mg/dl. Systemic examination showed a clear chest with no added sounds, normal heart sounds without any murmurs, a soft and non-tender abdomen with normal bowel sounds and grossly intact central nervous system.

Initial 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained at presentation showed marked sinus bradycardia (heart rate approximately 48 beats/min) with normal PR interval, narrow QRS complexes, and no significant ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities. No atrioventricular conduction block or ischemic changes were noted (Fig. 1). Blood investigations were sent and are listed in Table 1. The patient demonstrated mild neutrophilic leukocytosis (12.53 × 103 cells/mm3 with 84% neutrophils). In the absence of fever, localizing symptoms, or other laboratory evidence suggestive of infection, this was considered most likely due to a transient stress response associated with the acute poisoning episode. Grayanotoxin level was not tested as it was not available at our center.

In this patient, several differential diagnoses were considered. Cardiac causes such as sinus node dysfunction, atrioventricular conduction block, and acute ischemic events were considered. However, the patient had no prior history of cardiac disease and cardiac biomarkers were within normal limits. Electrocardiographic changes resolved rapidly with supportive care. Metabolic causes including electrolyte imbalance, particularly hyperkalemia were also excluded through normal laboratory parameters. Drug-induced bradycardia from agents such as beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, or digoxin was unlikely given the absence of co-morbidities and drug usage history. A diagnosis of mad honey poisoning was made with clear history of symptom onset three hours after ingestion of mad honey and absence of any other explanations for acute onset dizziness and bradycardia.

At emergency, he was managed conservatively with intravenous (IV) fluids and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). He was then admitted in coronary care unit (CCU) for observation, evaluation and further management. Throughout his CCU stay he was managed with IV fluids- Normal saline, PPIs- Pantoprazole 40 mg IV once a day and anti-emetics- Ondansetron 4 mg IV three times a day. Atropine was planned if his heart rate fell below 40bpm. However, his CCU stay was uneventful. His heart rate progressively started to increase and the patient was shifted to ward after 48 h and subsequently discharged the following day in stable condition. At the time of discharge, his 12-lead ECG showed restoration of normal sinus rhythm (heart rate approximately 67 beats/min) with normal cardiac conduction parameters and no ST-T abnormalities, indicating clinical and electrophysiological recovery following supportive treatment (Fig. 2). He reported no further complaints on follow up visits.

Discussions

Mad honey poisoning is generally attributed to grayanotoxin, a diterpenoid compound found in pollens of different species of Rhododendron in variable quantities [2]. Around 18 different types of grayanotoxin are identified out of which grayanotoxin 1 and 2 are considered most potent [5] [6]. It is a neurotoxin that acts on voltage-gated sodium channel in its open state preventing its inactivation and therefore leads to persistent depolarization [7]. This interferes with the transmission of action potential in nerves and muscles which produces a toxidrome characterized by gastro-intestinal symptoms, dizziness, syncope, hypotension and bradyarrhythmia including sinus bradycardia and varying degrees of atrioventricular block with rare progression to severe conduction failure [8, 9]. Arrhythmias observed are primarily due to autonomic imbalance with predominant vagal efferent activity leading to suppression of sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes. Experimental evidence suggests that bradycardia and other conduction defects are abolished by vagotomy and reversed by atropine suggesting vagus-dependent mechanism [10].

Although severity of symptoms is dose dependent, amount of honey consumed doesn’t corelate reliably with clinical manifestations as concentration of grayanotoxin varies widely among different honey samples. However, multiple studies done in Nepal and abroad have reported about the higher toxin potency of mad honey from Nepal compared to other areas [9, 11, 12].

The majority of cases reported in literature are male. It is generally thought to be due to socio-cultural practices as mad honey has been traditionally used as an aphrodisiac. It also aligns well to thrill seeking nature of wild honey harvesting and consumption [11, 13].

Diagnosis is clinical usually based upon history of recent wild honey consumption and non-specific symptoms like nausea, vomiting, dizziness, bradycardia, hypotension that are otherwise unexplained. Management is generally supportive. Intravenous fluids for hypotension, PPIs and anti-emetics for gastrointestinal discomfort and atropine for clinically significant bradycardia [5, 9].

Most cases are self-limiting with symptoms resolving within 24 to 48 hours without any long-term sequelae. These cases are rarely fatal [14].

In our case, patient presented to emergency with complaints of dizziness and vomiting. In patients presenting with acute onset dizziness with bradycardia, several differential diagnoses must be considered. In this context, the clear history of recent mad honey ingestion together with the characteristic clinical presentation of dizziness, vomiting and bradycardia strongly supported the diagnosis of mad honey poisoning. During management, atropine was not administered as the patient remained hemodynamically stable and was closely monitored in a high-dependency setting. Conservative management with intravenous fluids and observation was sufficient. The patient’s heart rate gradually normalized as the toxin effects resolved. This clinical course is consistent with previous reports describing the generally self-limiting nature of mad honey poisoning once toxins are metabolized.

Beyond management considerations, there exists a critical distinction between mad honey poisoning cases reported from turkey and areas around black sea and those occurring in Nepal. In Turkey, majority of poisoning occurs from consumption of honey produced by domesticated bees that is contaminated with grayanotoxins when bees forage and feed on “Rhododendron ponticum” and related species that are native to the region [14] In contrast, in Nepal mad honey is mainly harvested from wild bees that typically resides in cliffs and mountains and is intentionally harvested and consumed for its perceived medicinal and aphrodisiac properties. So, poisoning here is mainly due to deliberate use of honey for its perceived beneficial properties.

This report has several limitations. It represents a single case and therefore conclusions regarding management strategies cannot be generalized. Laboratory confirmation of grayanotoxin exposure and toxicological analysis of the honey sample were not available at our institution. Therefore, the diagnosis was made clinically. Specific Rhododendron species containing grayanotoxins in our region are not known as limited research exists in this regard.

Conclusions

Mad honey poisoning is a reversible cause of bradyarrhythmia and hypotension. It should be considered when evaluating cases of unexplained bradycardia and hypotension in endemic regions. Early diagnosis and supportive care under close monitoring is important in management of mad honey poisoning and atropine remains an established treatment of symptomatic bradycardia. Further research on composition and potency of grayanotoxins in Nepalese honey may help to refine clinical management.
