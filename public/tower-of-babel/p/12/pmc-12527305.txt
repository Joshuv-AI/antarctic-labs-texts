Allergy to acetylsalicylic acid among patients with acute coronary syndrome
Authors: Alexandre Haddad, Adhora Mir, Ana Maria Copaescu
Published: 2025

Roughly 1% to 5% of patients with coronary artery disease report adverse effects from acetylsalicylic acid (ASA)1

Among patients with acute coronary syndrome, for whom ASA may decrease morbidity and mortality (odds ratio 0.75),2 a short but detailed history of reported adverse effects may allow clinicians to safely administer ASA.

Patients with ASA-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) should avoid ASA if desensitization is not possible3–5

Patients with AERD report adverse reactions to ASA, asthma, and recurrent nasal polyposis. These patients are at risk of acute respiratory symptoms after the ingestion of cyclooxygenase (COX-1) inhibitors (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen, ASA), as well as bronchospasm upon re-exposure.3

Patients with a history of urticaria or angioedema after intake of COX-1 inhibitors may tolerate low doses of ASA4

These reactions are dose dependent, and around 85% of patients will not experience symptoms at low doses of ASA (e.g., 81 mg).5 A graded challenge with 2 doses of ASA (40.5 mg each), spaced 90 minutes apart, can be considered. 5 Nonsedating second-generation antihistamines (e.g., cetirizine 10 to 40 mg orally) can be used to manage ASA-associated urticaria or angioedema.3

Patients who report isolated gastrointestinal effects or have tolerated another COX-1–inhibiting nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) medication may not have ASA allergy3,4

Patients without a true ASA allergy (e.g., gastrointestinal bleeding) do not have a risk of hypersensitivity reaction with re-exposure. They can safely receive ASA following a risk–benefit discussion. Patients who have tolerated other COX-1–inhibiting NSAIDs since their reported reaction to ASA may safely receive ASA.3,4

Desensitization and questions about the safety of ASA administration require consultation with an allergy and immunology specialist4

The desensitization protocol should be selected based on the patient’s history and clinical stability, and contraindications (e.g., severe adverse reactions following ASA ingestion) must be absent.4
