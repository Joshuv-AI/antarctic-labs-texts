graphpancake: a Python package for representing organic molecules as molecular graphs utilizing electronic structure theory
Authors: Sneha Sil, Mark A Maskeri, Karl A Scheidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputational methods for predictive modeling have been increasingly utilized in the early stages of drug discovery to supplement high-throughput screening. The advent of highly efficient and complex machine learning architectures necessitates new methods of collating the plethora of topological, geometrical, and quantum chemical data from small molecule drug candidates to interface with these advanced cheminformatics methods. Graph-based molecular representations encoding atomic and bonding features allow physicochemical meaning to be leveraged by computational methods to predict quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSAR) with high precision and accuracy. We present graphpancake, an open-source Python package that translates quantum mechanical data from density functional theory (DFT) and post-Hartree Fock (HF) wavefunction calculations into molecular graphs of small organic molecules. graphpancake is a quantum chemical graph generator intended for cheminformatics pipelines featuring command line utility, hierarchical graph types with varying feature complexity, and thorough user documentation. To test graphpancake’s utility, we have curated several datasets (regression and classification) from the literature. Using random forest and message passing neural network architectures, we demonstrate how graphpancake’s quantum-enriched features can predict quantitative properties more accurately (R2 values 0.3–0.5 higher) compared to SMILES-generated features. Introduction

Molecular graphs are mathematical representations of chemical compounds which encode fundamental physicochemical properties via molecular connectivity patterns such as substructures, symmetry, and functional groups [1, 2]. Attributes condensed across ensembles of molecular graphs—where atoms are represented as nodes and bonds as edges—inform measures and metrics used to define physicochemical, electrostatic, geometrical, topological, and quantum–mechanical properties [1–3].

The most recognizable molecular representation is the chemical line structure, the lingua franca of organic chemistry. A trained chemist will intuit myriad physicochemical properties of a given structure using their knowledge of pKAs, conformational analyses, orbital interactions, etc. However, such intuitive chemical information is a significant challenge to convey to computational programs. Indeed, a key objective in the field of cheminformatics is the ability to generate molecular representations that can translate key structural and chemical information for use in computer software, mainly to explore resource-intensive problems, complex patterns in chemical datasets, and pharmacologically relevant quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSAR) [1, 2, 4–6]. The translation of molecular representations to software before the advent of machine learning has largely mirrored a drawn chemical line structure—essentially, lacking distinct electronic information. Common representations such as SMILES strings are easily parsable and computationally efficient, but they linearize molecular topology and thus lose three-dimensional (3D) conformations and proximities. Molecular fingerprints and descriptors, although extremely detailed, often depend on predefined statistical physicochemical relationships and arise from functional group specificity or measured properties. Such representations lack context-dependent physicochemical properties crucial to the realistic representation of a given molecule. As electronic structure theory is the foundation of all physical and chemical properties of a given molecule, an attractive proposition is to base molecular representations on quantum mechanical calculations, providing software with unique chemical insight such that a computer program can “visualize” compounds and intuit key features as chemists do.

Machine learning enabled by context-rich molecular representations allows for exploration of chemical space for in silico drug design via chemical property prediction, high-throughput automated screening, and hit generation and lead optimization [7–9]. As molecular graph representation learning is being increasingly used in modern drug discovery, resolving the disparity between mathematical molecular descriptors and complex ab initio electronic structure theory encoding 3D information becomes an especially significant challenge [8, 10]. As an innovative solution, we present graphpancake, an open-source Python package that serves as a quantum chemical molecular graph representation generator. graphpancake utilizes DFT and post-HF calculations for small organic molecules (elements H, B, C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Br, I), incorporating optimized spatial coordinates, molecular orbital characteristics, and thermodynamic calculations (Fig. 1). In this work, we provide validation of our software on regression and classification tasks using two different machine learning models, demonstrating how graphpancake derives molecular graphs from computed quantum chemical data.

Implementation

The information-rich construction of molecular graph representations leverages their innate ability as a computational data structure to encode 3D information in node, edge, and global features, circumventing the inherent two-dimensionality of line structures and rendering them extremely powerful tools for computational analysis (Fig. 2) [2, 11–13]. In recent years, unique efforts have been made towards the incorporation of geometric and electronic information into molecular graphs, each with their own creative blend of quantum chemistry and neural network architectures. As one example, Fang et al. [14] developed an algorithm utilizing geometry-enhanced molecular representation learning. Boiko et al. [11] introduced stereoelectronics-infused molecular graphs via enhancement of node features corresponding to bond orbitals, lone pairs, and interactions between them. Additionally, Nouman et al. [15] explored the utilization of Natural Bond Orbital analysis (NBO) to introduce orbital occupancy and density into graph neural networks trained for reaction prediction. We were particularly inspired by the work of Kneiding et al. [12], which demonstrates the construction of transition-metal complexes as molecular graphs using NBO analysis. We build upon these previous works to represent organic molecules in an application agnostic manner for property, reaction, or synthesis predictions.

Reconciling chemical intuition with quantum mechanics

Concepts such as atomic charges, the nature of the chemical bond, molecular strain, and stereoelectronic aspects such as hyperconjugation are frequently relied upon by organic chemists to understand the intricacies of mechanistic behavior [16]. Computational tools provide quantitative information which, when reconciled with such intuitive concepts and applications to real-world chemistry, elucidates deeper understanding of the fundamentals of physical organic chemistry [16]. To explore this conduit between chemical intuition and quantum mechanics, orbital localization schemes are commonly utilized—the most popular of which being Natural Bond Orbital analysis [16, 17]. NBO methods are a suite of algorithms for analyzing electronic wavefunctions from Hartree–Fock, DFT, and post-HF computations, enabling expression of fundamental bonding concepts (i.e., localized Lewis-like chemical bonds) [18].

When applied to ab initio wavefunctions, NBO analysis lends support to a chemical interpretation of the bonding scheme based on Lewis donor–acceptor type orbital interactions and the basic Pauling-Slater-Coulson picture of bond hybridization and polarization [19]. NBOs were meant to correspond to the basic units of molecular structure as a “chemist’s basis set:” localized bonds and lone pairs [19]. In this regard, the NBO analysis methods have been shown to be effective in bridging wavefunction calculations with the concept of “chemical intuition” [17, 18]. The NBO analysis scheme assigns each one-center lone pair or two-center bond pair as either an idealized Lewis-type representation or a non-Lewis type NBO, which describes residual resonance delocalization. Any aspect of the wavefunction can be expressed in terms of these Lewis or non-Lewis type contributions, resolving modern wavefunction theory with fundamental concepts in bonding and valency. This NBO donor–acceptor scheme extends to resonance theory, bond order, hybridization, and bond type [18]. As a consequence of the wealth of chemical knowledge resulting from an NBO analysis, one expects a molecular graph incorporating results from an NBO analysis to be exceedingly detailed. Hence, graphpancake features the option to conduct NBO analysis through a user’s quantum calculation program of choice and use the data generated to inform molecular graphs.

NBO analysis begins with Natural Population Analysis (NPA), which consists of a transformation of the density matrix into a set of orthonormal natural atomic orbitals (NAOs) and summation of the diagonal elements of this matrix for the basis functions pertaining to a given atomic center [17]. There is much precedent for utilizing NBO analysis in molecular graph construction, but not so much for NPA alone [11, 12, 15]. Yet, the construction of a complete orthogonal set of NAOs is crucial for NBO analysis, as NAOs form the basis for key atomic properties (e.g., atomic charge and electron configuration. Furthermore, NAOs reflect electronic variations in shape and size, for example, angular deformations due to adjacent steric clash or increased anionic or cationic character altering diffuseness, all salient descriptors of a molecule. This attention to stereoelectronic detail distinguishes NPA analysis from free-atom forms, even without proceeding to NBO construction, suggesting that NPA alone would inform a robust molecular graph [18]. This notion is supported by the fact that NPA charge assignments do not vary significantly with basis extension nor wavefunction form, and generally agree with well-defined structure–function relationships in physical organic chemistry [18].

The software used to conduct NBO analysis, NBO 7.0, is under license, while implementations of NPA such as JANPA are open-source. While acknowledging the intellectual property protections of closed-source codes, open-source code in scientific software plays a crucial role in the reproducibility of computational results [17, 20–22]. As JANPA is freeware and therefore readily available, we deemed the option to construct NPA-informed molecular graphs to be a simple-to-execute method to imbue useful molecular graph features while supporting the utilization of open-source code in the larger scientific community. Hence, we have implemented a hierarchical system of four types of graph construction in graphpancake to seamlessly parse data from HF/DFT/post-HF calculations, thermodynamics calculations, and subsequent post-processing with JANPA or NBO, depending on the licenses and resources available to the user.

ab initio electronic structure calculations

Before any molecular graph can be constructed with graphpancake, it is necessary to generate the source descriptors, as graphpancake is designed to utilize results derived from electronic structure calculations. At a high level, details unique to molecular conformations in space include atomic spatial coordinates, interatomic distances, bond angles, dispersion, and non-covalent interactions [8]. For the purposes of dataset curation to test graphpancake (see Dataset curation), we aimed to incorporate these spatial details into our molecular graphs by conducting the following calculations and analyses.

If the calculations failed at any point during these steps, the corresponding molecule would be removed from our datasets. We note that this approach likely biases our datasets toward more rigid molecules and thus limits their scope. However, these overrepresentations can be ameliorated with molecular augmentation (i.e., generating noise) during the machine learning pipeline [49]. All optimized geometries were confirmed to have no imaginary modes less than − 10 cm -1, which we have deemed a sufficient level of accuracy for our purposes, as we had to consider resource allocations for several thousand calculations [50, 51]. r 2 SCAN-3c was chosen as the geometry optimization method of choice as it is tailored for the task of structure optimization, and combines a triple-zeta basis set, D4 dispersion correction, and gCP basis set superposition error correction, therefore providing accurate and efficient results at relatively low computational cost [29, 30, 50, 52, 53].

graphpancake construction

Python’s scientific userbase has grown exponentially due to its unique flexibility with compiled languages and useful toolkits for data processing and analysis [5, 13]. Hence, we wrote graphpancake in Python to provide users with the ability to integrate our package into a well-rounded cheminformatics pipeline. We used the MolSSI Cookiecutter project to package graphpancake for distribution. This library template was designed to be a starting point for Python-based code with best practices incorporated, allowing developers to focus on the scientific aspects of their project rather than programming logistics [54].

DFT geometry optimizations allow us to embed spatial positions into nodes, forming the basis for our graphs. Additional node features were implemented such as atomic mass, electronegativity, and covalent radius [55, 56]. Euclidean distances between atoms were used to construct bonds, and encoded onto graph edges. Nodes features were augmented by NPA, including the number of bound hydrogens, total node degree, electron population, natural minimal basis population, and natural population analysis charge. Additionally, NPA provides the bond order from Wiberg-Mayer bond indices (WBOs, based on the density matrix in the NAO basis, see Wiberg-Mayer bond orders and conventional bond orders) and the number of 2-centered bonding orbitals. NBO analysis provides electronic information on orbital occupancies and energies (core, lone pair, bonding, antibonding) as well as natural charge, core, valence, Rydberg, and total populations for global features. Thermostatistical calculations were all consolidated into global features. In graphpancake, after all of these node, edge, and global features are parsed from quantum chemical output files, they are transferred to a SQLite database for ease of querying, storage and exports. A view on the database can be invoked to export only quantitative features for ML. We show how the node, edge, and global/target features and attribute vectors can be implemented into a ML pipeline in Fig. 3. Wiberg-Mayer bond orders and conventional bond orders

When deriving the formula to convert WBO indices to conventional bond orders, we noted that there has not been an extensive benchmarking of WBO indices for organic molecules explored in the literature thus far. To provide a solution and subsequently add a layer of specificity to our program for organic systems—which are often characterized by resonance structures, zwitterions, and hybridization—we calculated the WBO values for each bond in almost 250 organic molecules and manually classified the type of bond (single, resonant or aromatic, double, triple) for around 4000 bonds. This dataset was curated from Balaban [3], and the full list of structures analyzed along with a labeled example can be found in the Supplementary Information (Figure S1). Dataset curation

We compiled benchmark datasets from publicly available resources and the literature to test graphpancake in a property prediction pipeline. Our main concern was the tradeoff between “the more data, the better” for deep learning and the availability of computational resources for expensive DFT and post-HF calculations [57]. As a quick meta-analysis, we selected the datasets directly relevant to chemical and pharmacological applications (57 out of 109 datasets) as determined by label type from the Benchmark Data Sets for Graph Kernels and calculated measures of center [58]. Compared to the mean (18,128 with standard deviation 21,777), the median size of 9,036 datapoints was more appropriate as a model for dataset size when considering our computational resources and the sometimes-unreliable nature of available experimental datasets [59]. Even so, our largest dataset consists of 4,684 molecules due to limited availability of computational resources and curated experimental data for labelling (see ab initio electronic structure calculations).

We have curated five distinct, quantum mechanically enriched datasets, each containing either regression or classification data and catalogued from the literature. Details of each dataset can be found in Table 2. Each dataset contains coordinate files, Shermo output files, JANPA output files, and NBO output files, all of which are available in our GitHub repository for graphpancake.

Results and discussion

Practicality and features of graphpancake

graphpancake finds utility as an application package by employing end-user tools such as a command-line interface (CLI) for database operations, batch processing using parallel multiprocessing, and a memory-optimized design pattern. The CLI, constructed as a user-friendly interface for database operations, has multiple subcommands (create-db, load-data, query, delete) and detailed documentation. The batch processing workflow uses a YAML-based configuration template system to provide memory and CPU allocation flexibility for processing setup. Considering that a dataset of quantum chemical data for several thousand molecules can contain gigabytes of data, processing speed was a key consideration during development (see Table 3 and Table S7). From file input, graphpancake uses memory-mapped file input and output, zero-copy file reading and automatic file caching; during processing, graphpancake uses lazy loading to avoid expensive recomputations of features; finally, when loading into a SQLite database, graphpancake is designed to monitor and manage memory with cache clearing to prevent out-of-memory errors during dataset processing [64, 65]. A complete list of Python libraries and the versions used in our work can be found in the Supplementary Information. Overall, graphpancake is a scientific software package that provides domain-specific library functionality for quantum chemistry and molecular graph analysis, designed for cheminformatics workflows.

Machine learning validation

We next sought to explore the relative differences in statistical metrics between conventional machine learning and deep learning models trained on quantum mechanical features, as well as understand the extent to which using QM features generated from graphpancake improves the performance of message-passing neural network (MPNN) compared to SMILES-generated features. Thus, we have set up three models: a random forest, a MPNN trained on features extracted from SMILES strings using RDKit, and the same MPNN model trained on graphpancake’s QM features [23]. The RDKit-derived features include atom type, degree, formal charge, chiral tag, hybridization, aromaticity, total valence, bond type, conjugation, ring membership, and stereoconfiguration. The models were tested in both regression- and classification-based applications, and the models were adjusted depending on the application. Our classification model was designed for robustness and generalization, necessitating extensive regularization. In contrast, our regression model was constructed to prioritize capacity and expressiveness and was constructed with larger hidden dimensions and without regularization. Additionally, our classification model has more complexity in handling class imbalance, which is critical for binary classification tasks but irrelevant for regression tasks [66]. Further details on these models can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Regression results

Our regression models were tested on datasets SS01 (label: logP), SS04 (label: TPSA), and SS05 (label: logP). The full quantum mechanical feature set from graphpancake used in random forest and MPNN models outperformed the SMILES-generated feature set by a large margin, using R 2 as our chosen comparison metric (Fig. 4, Table S8). For NBO, NPA, and QM graph types, RF and MPNN perform similarly, and for the DFT graph type, RF outperforms MPNN. From these results, we can draw these conclusions: quantum mechanical features utilizing wavefunction theory analysis are informative for regression models regardless of conventional machine learning or deep learning model (dataset < 5000 datapoints); DFT data (i.e., coordinates and thermodynamic data) are not enough to solely inform a MPNN instead, SMILES-generated features may hold more predictive power at a lower computational cost. With molecular augmentation (shuffling nodes and edges around), field-specific enrichment (e.g., including tautomers), and a more powerful model, we anticipate MPNN regression models trained on quantum mechanical graphs to perform significantly better than a random forest model trained on the same QM features.

Classification results

All of our classification datasets are based on cell line cytotoxicity assays (SS02, SS03a-d), with the negative class representing a no-hit and the positive class representing a hit. As our datasets are heavily imbalanced with respect to the negative and positive classes, our chosen metric of comparison was the area under the precision-recall curve (AU-PRC). The AU-PRC is more informative of learning capabilities when dealing with imbalanced datasets compared to metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AU-ROC) [67]. For all but SS03b, the graphpancake MPNN outperformed the SMILES MPNN, although not by as large a margin as in the regression testing. The random forest model and graphpancake MPNN performed similarly across NPA, NBO, and QM graph types, and the previous point about DFT data being insufficient for MPNNs still holds true for our classification testing. These results, even when combined with metrics such as AU-ROC, Matthews’ correlation coefficient, and F1-score (see Table S9), do not show clear evidence that quantum mechanical graphs consistently perform better than SMILES-generated features for classification-type datasets. Within the scope of our curated datasets, our results suggest that a complex quantum mechanical feature set may not be necessary for a classification-based model, and further exploration will be reported in due course.

Datasets, models, and future directions

Real-world data inevitably contains noise due to experimental variability, error in measurement, and mistakes during database curation [68]. We anticipate the performance of deep learning models utilizing graphpancake to improve with more data. Additionally, an imbalance within datasets, especially in classification tasks, is a known issue of chemistry datasets curated from the literature. Such imbalances can lead to a situation where if 95% of datapoints are negative, a model could achieve 95% accuracy on predicting negative values alone [68]. Our classification datasets were pulled from biological assays, which operate in the binary: hit (1) and no-hit (0). This leaves little room for error quantification—if a substance is cytotoxically inactive, measuring the extent of inactivity would result in a regression problem (i.e., quantifying toxicity). Unless one has several thousands of datapoints from the same biological assay and tunes their ML model to optimize precision-recall balance, our results support that quantum mechanical graphs are a more effective tool for predicting quantitative values for smaller datasets (< 5000 datapoints).

Overall, the performance of each model—especially the MPNNs—would likely improve with a larger training dataset [69]. In this work, we rely heavily on physics-based computational methods, which are limited by their complexity, resource utilization, and reliance on experimental data, though they offer high accuracy in examining molecular structures and energetics [70]. This accuracy is crucial to a predictive model’s power, as a model is only as good as the data it is trained on. Indeed, our utilization of quantum mechanical features via graphpancake allowed a remarkably simple random forest model (without grid-search or cross-validation) to achieve relatively good predictive power (R 2 > 0.7), especially when considering the comparative computational cost of conventional machine learning and deep learning methods.

Regarding model complexity, we implemented basic schemes for both our RF and MPNN models, which will be improved in terms of intricacy and efficacy in future work. Coley et al. [71] demonstrated the ability of their graph neural network (GNN) to learn physical properties with fewer than 200 training examples, showing promise for a paradigm shift towards both effective molecular representations and deep learning algorithms that leverage substructural features and atom environments. Augmentation, knowledge of the field, and supplementing our datasets with more calculations may improve results regardless of the model. In terms of model improvements, we envision future directions to involve pretraining models (which could increase accuracy and mitigate the computational cost of running QM calculations) and/or implementing of hierarchical, multimodal, or ensemble learning methods [2, 9, 10, 70].

Conclusion

As developments in the field of graph-based molecular design closely follow novel machine learning architectures, the advancement of more efficient molecular representations will greatly enrich cheminformatics and drug discovery [2, 72]. graphpancake is an efficient open-source Python package that translates quantitative quantum mechanical data into molecular graphs for cheminformatics applications. We observed how graphpancake performed in property prediction with machine learning and found that quantum mechanical features leveraging molecular orbital analysis (i.e., NBO, NPA) informed regression models better than SMILES-generated features, regardless of conventional machine learning or deep learning model. Thus, graphpancake can serve as a valuable computational analysis tool toward the reconciliation of chemical “intuition” with quantum mechanics-enriched feature sets to facilitate informative translations of chemical data and precise physicochemical property prediction for cheminformatics software in drug discovery.
