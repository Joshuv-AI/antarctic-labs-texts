Loneliness and its association with nursing students’ academic self-efficacy and performance across five universities: the mediating role of social media addiction
Authors: Daniel Joseph E Berdida, Noura Alhudaib, Lugain Talal Marashli, Yasmine Salem Darrag, Niela Sacro-Rosales, Larry Terence O Cornejo, Grace Medalyn Tungpalan-Castro, Minerva Pingue-Raguini, Maaidah M Algamdi, Modi Al-Moteri, Ingrid Jacinto-Caspillo, Albert B Balace, Ma Teresa C Madrilejos, Lorena Gutiérrez Puertas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a wealth of nursing literature about nursing students’ loneliness, self-efficacy, and influence on academic performance; however, the mediating role of social media addiction among these associations remains scant after the pandemic and in a Middle Eastern context.ObjectivesTo investigate the mediating effect of social media addiction in the interrelationships between loneliness, academic self-efficacy, and academic performance among nursing students.MethodsCross-sectional and correlational study design was used. Five nursing colleges in Saudi Arabia were selected as the study settings using convenience sampling. Nursing students (n = 775) were consecutively invited to participate and completed three standardized self-report scales (6-item Loneliness Scale, 6-item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, and the 5-item General Academic Self-Efficacy Scale). For data analysis, the covariance-based structural equation modeling was utilized. Data were analyzed using covariance-based structural equation modeling with maximum likelihood estimation. Path analysis was conducted to test direct and mediating relationships, and model fit was evaluated using χ2/df, root mean square error of approximation, comparative fit index, Tucker-Lewis index, and standardized root mean square residual.ResultsLoneliness was negatively associated with social media addiction (β = − 0.18, p = 0.001) and positively associated with academic self-efficacy (β = 0.14, p = 0.001) and academic performance (β = 0.14, p < 0.05), contrary to the hypothesized directions. Social media addiction was positively associated with academic self-efficacy (β = 0.19, p = 0.001) but negatively associated with academic performance (β = − 0.11, p < 0.05). Social media addiction significantly mediated the indirect effect of loneliness on academic self-efficacy (β = − 0.03, p = 0.005), but not on academic performance. Loneliness explained 3.33% of the variance in social media addiction, while loneliness and social media addiction explained 4.57% and 3.89% of the variance in academic self-efficacy and academic performance, respectively.ConclusionSome findings diverged from theoretical expectations, suggesting that the relationships among loneliness, social media addiction, academic self-efficacy, and academic performance may be context-dependent. Social media addiction mediated only the relationship between loneliness and academic self-efficacy. Nursing educators and policymakers should interpret these findings cautiously and consider multifactorial strategies that address loneliness, problematic social media use, and broader academic and psychosocial support needs among nursing students.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Academic self-efficacy, Academic performance, Loneliness, Nursing students, Social media addiction

Introduction

The World Health Organization [1] has identified social isolation and loneliness as priority global public health concerns. Loneliness affects individuals regardless of age, gender, occupation, or race [1], and around 15% of adolescents and young adults experience it [1]. Evidence shows that loneliness and social isolation adversely affect quality of life, overall health, and lifespan [2], and are linked to obesity, smoking, drug addiction, and sedentary lifestyle, which are risk factors for chronic disease [3, 4]. High-quality social relationships are therefore essential to individual well-being [1].

Nursing students, who are largely adolescents and young adults, may be particularly vulnerable to loneliness because of academic demands, clinical internship stress, and the developmental challenges associated with professional training [5–7]. As digital natives, they may also increasingly use social media to maintain social connection; however, excessive use may progress to social media addiction (SMA) [8]. At the same time, self-efficacy has been identified as an important internal protective factor that may buffer the adverse effects of loneliness, depression, stress, and anxiety [9, 10]. Despite these concerns, the interrelationships among loneliness, SMA, academic self-efficacy (ASE), and academic performance among nursing students remain insufficiently understood.

Background

Nursing students require strong psychological well-being and academic functioning to cope with the demands of academic coursework and clinical internships [11]. These capacities are important not only for successful program completion and readiness for professional practice [12], but also for strengthening the future nursing workforce amid ongoing staffing shortages [13]. However, several factors, including loneliness [5, 7], inadequate social support, and SMA [14–16], may adversely affect nursing students’ ASE [17] and academic performance [18, 19].

Although related, social isolation and loneliness are distinct constructs. Social isolation refers to an objective lack of social interaction, whereas loneliness reflects the subjective perception of insufficient intimacy or social connection [20, 21]. These experiences may occur independently or concurrently; thus, individuals with broad social networks may still feel lonely, whereas others with fewer social contacts may not [21].

Loneliness among nursing students has been increasingly documented, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Higher loneliness has been associated with lower life satisfaction among European nursing students [5]. Among Filipino nursing students, loneliness weakened the positive effect of social support on quality of life, whereas resilience buffered its adverse influence [7]. COVID-19 phobia was also found to increase loneliness and intention to leave nursing among Filipino students [22]. Similarly, a phenomenological study among Chinese students during lockdown described the causes, impacts, acceptance, and reconstruction of life in the experience of loneliness [6].

Prior studies also suggest that loneliness may have important academic and digital-behavioral correlates. Loneliness negatively predicted university students’ ASE, whereas female sex and stronger problem-solving skills were associated with better grade point average (GPA) [17]. Among Iranian nursing students, loneliness intensified the effect of fear of missing out (FoMO) on smartphone addiction and was associated with excessive internet and social media use [18]. Loneliness also amplified the negative academic implications of FoMO, contributing to poorer learning experiences [18]. However, the interrelationship of loneliness with SMA, ASE, and academic performance has not been sufficiently examined among nursing students.

SMA refers to a pattern of excessive, compulsive, and difficult-to-control engagement with social media platforms [14], characterized by persistent preoccupation with use and continued involvement despite potential negative effects on daily, academic, or psychosocial functioning [15, 17]. It has been linked to internet addiction, smartphone addiction, FoMO, and nomophobia [15–17]. Because nursing students are frequent users of digital technologies, they may be especially vulnerable to maladaptive social media use. Prior studies have shown that smartphone addiction among Chinese nursing students was positively associated with academic burnout and partly mediated the effect of professional attitude on burnout [16]. In contrast, Filipino nursing students were reported to be at relatively low risk of SMA [14]. Among healthcare students and professionals in Saudi Arabia, SMA was positively correlated with burnout [23]. However, other findings suggest that social media use may also have adaptive functions; for example, SMA was associated with greater life satisfaction through enhanced social connectedness among Turkish nursing students [24], and sustained social media use during the pandemic supported learning activities among Filipino nursing students [15]. As far as we know, no previous study has specifically examined whether SMA mediates the relationships among loneliness, ASE, and academic performance.

Academic performance, often represented by GPA, reflects students’ overall academic achievement. Prior studies have identified several factors associated with nursing students’ academic performance. Among Romanian nursing students, poorer academic performance was linked to SMA, depression, and anxiety [19]. Among Iranian nursing students, academic performance was negatively associated with loneliness, smartphone addiction, and FoMO [18]. In Chinese college students, internet addiction predicted academic burnout, while loneliness partly intensified this relationship [25]. In nursing education, GPA is particularly important because it may influence program completion and success in licensure examinations [26]. Thus, identifying factors such as loneliness and SMA that may affect academic performance is important for student retention and professional preparation.

ASE refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to perform academic tasks successfully and achieve academic goals [27]. It plays an important role in promoting academic achievement among nursing students [28]. Higher ASE has been observed among nursing students engaged in clinical placements, case presentations, and nursing care plan development [29]. ASE has also been shown to reduce academic burnout and strengthen the beneficial influence of professional attitude on burnout [16], while higher ASE has been linked to better academic and clinical performance [22]. However, previous studies have focused more often on general self-efficacy among nursing students [30, 31], and the relationships among ASE, loneliness, SMA, and academic performance remain insufficiently explored.

Investigations of nursing students’ loneliness, ASE, academic performance, and the mediating role of SMA remain limited, particularly in the post-pandemic period and in Middle Eastern contexts. Understanding how these variables interact is important because nursing students continue to face academic pressure, clinical training demands, and changes in social and digital learning environments that may affect their psychological well-being and academic functioning. In Saudi Arabia, strong family ties, cultural expectations, gender norms, and rapidly expanding digital engagement may further shape how students experience loneliness and use social media. Therefore, this study was conducted to address this gap. Its findings may help nursing educators and administrators identify students at risk, design culturally responsive support strategies, strengthen ASE, reduce loneliness and problematic social media use, and sustain academic performance.

Theoretical underpinnings

This study uses Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) [32] along with Compensatory Internet Use Theory (CIUT) [33]. SCT posits that an individual’s functioning stems from interactions among personal factors, behaviors, and outcomes. Self-efficacy plays a central role in motivation, coping, and performance [32]. In this context, loneliness is a psychological stressor that can affect nursing students’ behaviors and academic results. ASE serves as a key cognitive pathway that students use to manage learning and performance. CIUT [33] explains how people use social media to cope with negative emotions, such as loneliness. These behaviors may help at first, but excessive or uncontrolled use can lead to poor outcomes. Both theories support the model: loneliness affects ASE and academic performance directly and via the mediation of SMA (Fig. 1). This highlights the complex links between mental health, coping behaviors, and academic functioning in nursing students.

Theoretical and hypothesized model of the associations of loneliness, social media addiction. academic self-efficacy, and academic performance

Although prior theories (i.e., SCT and CIUT) [32, 33] and evidence generally suggest that loneliness and problematic social media use are associated with poorer academic functioning [17, 18, 25], these relationships may vary across sociocultural and educational contexts. Therefore, the present hypotheses were theory-informed but tested with recognition that the direction and magnitude of effects may differ in nursing students exposed to highly structured academic demands.

Research hypotheses

Guided by the theoretical underpinnings and the limitations of previous studies, this investigation hypothesized that loneliness would positively associate with SMA (H1a) and negatively associate with ASE (H1b) and academic performance (H1c). ASE was hypothesized to positively associate with academic performance (H2), whereas SMA would negatively associate with ASE (H3a) and academic performance (H3b). Moreover, SMA was hypothesized to mediate the relationships between loneliness and ASE (H4a) and between loneliness and academic performance (H4b) (Fig. 1).

Methods

Design

This research utilized a cross-sectional and correlational design while employing structural equation modelling (SEM) to illustrate the study variables connections. The STROBE checklist guided the reporting of the study’s essential aspects.

Setting, sampling, participants

Five nursing colleges (2 private and 3 public) in Saudi Arabia served as the study settings for this investigation. Study setting 1 and 2 are the biggest private and public nursing colleges in the northern province. Study setting 3 is a private college with a nursing department in Makkah province. This college only caters to female nursing students and has a training hospital. Study settings 4 and 5 are public owned nursing colleges administered by a state universities in the northcentral and northwestern regions. Potential participants were recruited via consecutive sampling. The eligibility criteria were the following: (a) matriculated nursing students in the study settings, (b) completed at least one semester, and (c) voluntarily consented to participate.

The five study settings were selected using convenience sampling at the institutional level. These colleges/universities were chosen based on accessibility and feasibility, including the availability of professional contacts who facilitated coordination and data collection. Additionally, each setting had a sufficient number of enrolled nursing students to support participant recruitment and achieve the required sample size.

The sample size was guided by an a priori assumption for SEM analysis, which recommends at least 200 participants to achieve adequate statistical power [34]. A total of 1,000 nursing students were invited to participate through their college email and social media group chats. Of these, 792 students returned the survey forms, yielding a gross response rate of 79.20%. Seventeen respondents did not provide consent and were therefore excluded. As a result, 775 complete and consenting responses were included in the final analysis, corresponding to a usable response rate of 77.50%.

Ethical considerations

The study received ethics approval from the Ethics Review Committee of North Private College of Nursing (Protocol code: NCN-2122024-21; Approval date: 05/12/2024), the institution where the primary investigator was employed. This approval covered the conduct of the multicenter study across the participating nursing colleges. Before data collection, formal permission was also obtained from the appropriate authorities in each participating college, including the dean and relevant administrators. Therefore, although separate ethical approval was not sought from each external college, site permission was secured from all participating institutions.

Voluntary participation was operationalized by providing potential participants with the study description, aims, potential risks, benefits, confidentiality procedures, and withdrawal conditions in the first section of the online survey form. Participants indicated consent by clicking “I consent to participate in this study” before accessing the questionnaire. Participants were assured that their decision to participate, decline, or withdraw would not influence their academic standing, clinical training opportunities, institutional status, or interactions with college personnel.

Anonymity was maintained by not collecting personally identifiable information, such as names, student identification numbers, email addresses, home addresses, or cellphone numbers. Confidentiality was ensured by storing the dataset in a password-protected laptop used only for this study and accessible only to the researchers. Nonmaleficence was upheld by using a noninvasive online survey, avoiding sensitive identifiable data, not providing incentives that could unduly influence participation, and allowing participants to stop answering the survey at any point. The researchers adhered to the ethical principles of the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki [35].

Measures

A 4-part survey questionnaire online survey to collect data. Part 1 obtained the participants’ demographic characteristics (e.g., sex, year level, age, GPA). Academic performance was represented by students’ latest self-reported GPA. Participants provided their most recent GPA in the online questionnaire. In Saudi Arabia, GPA is generally calculated by dividing the total grade points earned by the total number of enrolled course units. Self-reported diagnoses of ADHD and anxiety disorder were collected to describe the sample because these conditions may influence students’ attention, emotional regulation, social functioning, social media use, ASE, and academic performance. However, due to the low number of reported cases, these variables were used only for descriptive purposes and were not included in the structural model.

Part 2 included the 6-item Loneliness Scale (SILS) [36]. Part 3 contained the 6-item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) [37]. Lastly, Part 4 was the 5-item General Academic Self-Efficacy Scale (GASE) [38].

Participants’ loneliness was evaluated with the 6-item SILS [36]. This two-factor (emotional: 3 items; social: 3 items) structure scale includes questions such as “I miss having people around” that is rated on 6-point Likert scale (0–1 = not lonely, 2–4 = moderately lonely, 5–6 = severely lonely). The possible score range is 0 to 36, with elevated ratings indicating increased loneliness experience. The SILS’s original Cronbach’s alpha ranges between 0.77 and 0.87 [36]. This instrument was previously validated and used in student nurses [7].

The 6-item BSMAS [37] assessed nursing students’ SMA. Each item reflects the contains six addiction elements: 1 (salience), 2 (craving/tolerance), 3 (mood modification), 4 (relapse/loss of control), 5 (withdrawal), and 6 (conflict/functional impairment). An statement from this instrument is “How often during the last year have you tried to cut down on the use of social media without success?” The scale was scored on a 5-point Likert rating (1 = very rarely to 5 = very often). Scores are ranging between 6 and 30, with higher score denotes high-risk SMA. The scale’s former Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88 [37]. This scale was previously used among nursing students [14].

Nursing students’ ASE was ascertained by the 5-item GASE [38]. An example item in the scale is “I know I can stick to my aims and accomplish my goals in my field of study.” This scale is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The scores range from 5 to 25; thus higher scores indicate excellent ASE. The tool’s original Cronbach’s alpha was 0.81 [38]. This scale was validated and translated to Arabic by Berdida et al. [39] and was used by Saudi nursing students. This scale had a Cronbach alpha of 0.89.

Scales’ validity and reliability

Since GASE already has an established validity and reliability, only the SILS and BSMAS underwent translation to Arabic and back-translated to English. To assess semantic equivalence [40], three language experts—two English professors and one nursing associate professor—were involved. For SILS, the overall content validity index was 0.95 while it was 0.93 for BSMAS. Following scales’ translation, they were pilot tested in a sample nursing students (n = 21) to determine their reliability. The Cronbach’s alpha of SILS was 0.91 and 0.90 for BSMAS, indicating good reliability [40].

Data collection

Data were collected from December 7, 2024 to April 20, 2025 using an online survey form. To minimize potential coercion or perceived pressure, no faculty member who was part of the research team directly or personally recruited students or distributed the survey link to them. Instead, the online survey questionnaire was forwarded to the college or department research committee in each participating institution. These committees disseminated the survey link to potential participants through institutional email. Potential participants were also allowed to share the survey link in their student group chats to enhance participation. No incentives were provided. Participants’ completion and return of the survey form indicated their consent to voluntary participation.

To enhance participation, reminder emails were sent every two weeks through the same institutional channels, and class representatives were notified to inform their classes about the study. To maintain anonymity, participants’ personal information, such as names, email addresses, home addresses, and cellphone numbers, was not collected. Data confidentiality was ensured by storing the collected data in a password-protected laptop used only for this study and accessible only to the researchers. The participants were assured that participation, nonparticipation, or withdrawal from the study would not affect their grades, clinical placement, academic evaluation, or relationship with their college or university.

Data analysis

Before final statistical analysis, the dataset was pre-screened, and incomplete, invalid, or unfinished survey attempts were removed. STATA MP – Parallel Edition Statistical Software, Version 18, College Station, TX: StataCorp LP was employed for all statistical analyses. A p-value of < 0.05 was accepted statistically significant. Data normality, both univariate and multivariate, were analyzed and were achieved using Shapiro-Wilk’s and Doornik-Hansen tests. With estimated p-values > 0.05, except for age, all continuous-level data were normally-distributed. Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. To analyze the linear associations of loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance, Pearson’s R was initially utilized [41]. Afterwards, covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM), using maximum likelihood estimation, was then performed to examine the theoretical model of the interrelationships between and among loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance. To evaluate the hypothesized and emerging models, the following model fit parameters and acceptable thresholds were used: χ2/df ≤ 3.00, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08 [42], comparative-fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) ≥ 0.90, and a standardized root mean squared residual ≤ 0.08 [43]. Path analyses were conducted to determine mediation effects.

Associations of loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance

Table 2 illustrates the correlation analyses of the associations of loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance. Results showed that loneliness had a statistically significant, negative association with SMA (r=–0.183, p = 0.001) but had statistically significant and positive correlation with ASE (r = 0.111, p = 0.016) and academic performance (r = 0.164, p = 0.001). SMA, on the other hand, had a positive and significant association with ASE (r = 0.160, p = 0.001) and negative, significant correlation with academic performance (r=–0.138, p = 0.003). Results also showed that ASE and performance were not statistically associated (r=–0.005, p = 0.919).

Validity and reliability of measurement models

The validity and reliability of the measurement models of loneliness, SMA, and ASE were evaluated. For loneliness, the subscale of emotional loneliness had factor loadings of 0.59 to 0.88, a subscale reliability of 0.738, a composite reliability (CR) of 0.752, and an average variance explained (AVE) of 0.511. The dimension of social loneliness had item loadings of 0.58 to 0.84, a subscale reliability of 0.744, a CR of 0.761, and an AVE of 0.520. The construct of loneliness had an overall scale reliability of 0.780, a CR of 0.861, an AVE of 0.515, and the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio was 0.116. The two-factor model of loneliness had acceptable fit: χ2/df = 2.35, RMSEA = 0.053, CFI = 0.985, TLI = 0.971. Common methods bias (CMB) was analyzed using single factor test and latent common method approach. The former analyses indicated poor model fit for a single-factor structure of loneliness. Likelihood, the latter analyses indicated that CMB was not statistically significant (p = 0.085). These results suggest that CMB was not substantial in the analyses.

Analyses also showed that the factor loadings of the six items of SMA ranged from 0.60 to 0.93, and the one-factor structure had good model fit (χ2/df = 2.01, RMSEA = 0.046, CFI = 0.994, TLI = 0.987). The scale and composite reliabilities were 0.855 and 0.857, respectively, while the AVE was 0.506. For ASE, the five items had factor loadings varying from 0.61 to 0.92, and the one-factor structure had good fit indices (χ2/df = 1.711, RMSEA = 0.039, CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.977). These results denote that loneliness, SMA, and ASE had acceptable internal consistency and convergent validity since factor loadings ≥ 0.40, Cronbach’s alpha and CR were ≥ 0.70 and the AVE estimates were ≥ 0.50. Discriminant validity was also ascertained since HMTM criteria were ≤ 0.90.

Structural model of the associations of loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance

Figures 1 and 2 show the hypothesized and trimmed models of the associations of loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance. Analysis indicated that the association between ASE and academic performance (r=–0.001, p = 0.919) was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, due to the strong theoretical and empirical association between these variables, two models were analyzed – the hypothesized model with the association of ASE and academic performance and the trimmed model omitting the ASE-academic performance association. Afterwards, the fit indices of these two models were evaluated and compared. In both the hypothesized and trimmed model, results showed that loneliness directly and negatively associated with SMA (β=–0.18, p = 0.001), denoting a 0.18-unit decrease in SMA scores for every one-unit increase in loneliness. In addition, results showed that loneliness positively and directly associated with both ASE (β = 0.14, p = 0.001) and academic performance (β = 0.14, p < 0.05). SMA, for this part, had a direct, positive associated with ASE (β = 0.19, p = 0.001) while a direct, negative association can be noted for academic performance (β=–0.11, p < 0.05). Mediation analyses also showed that SMA mediated the indirect effect of loneliness on ASE (β=–0.03, p = 0.005) (Table 3). Results also showed that loneliness explained 3.33% of the variance of SMA, while both loneliness and SMA measured 4.57% and 3.89% of the variance of ASE and academic performance, respectively (Fig. 2).

Evaluating the fit indices (Table 4), results showed that the two models were not significantly different (Δχ2/df = 0.004, p = 0.950). Majority of the fit indices, specifically RMSEA, CFI, TLI, and SRMR, also indicate good model parameters. It can also be noted that the Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values for the hypothesized model were lower than those of the trimmed model. These model fit indices indicate that the hypothesized model (with ASE-academic performance association) had better model fit and parsimony.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first to examine the mediating role of SMA in the relationships among loneliness, ASE, and academic performance among nursing students. Four key findings emerged. First, loneliness was negatively associated with SMA. Second, loneliness was positively associated with both ASE and academic performance. Third, SMA was positively associated with ASE but negatively associated with academic performance. Finally, SMA significantly mediated the relationship between loneliness and ASE, but not the relationship between loneliness and academic performance. Together, these findings provide additional insight into the complex mental health, digital behavior, and academic experiences of nursing students, while also suggesting the need for cautious interpretation because several findings differed from the hypothesized directions.

Contrary to Hypothesis 1a, loneliness was negatively associated with SMA, indicating that nursing students who reported higher loneliness also reported lower problematic social media use. This finding differs from previous literature suggesting a positive or bidirectional relationship between loneliness and problematic digital engagement [18, 44]. Earlier studies, for example, found that loneliness was associated with increased mobile phone use or SMA among Chinese and Iranian nursing students [18, 44], and that loneliness mediated the relationship between social anxiety and problematic phone use [44]. However, the present result suggests that the loneliness–SMA association may not be uniform across contexts and may vary according to individual, sociocultural, and educational factors.

One possible explanation is that loneliness may not always lead to compensatory online behavior. Although some individuals may use social media to cope with loneliness, others may disengage from both offline and online social interactions. Previous evidence has shown that loneliness may be linked to reduced concentration, cognitive engagement, and emotional functioning [45, 46]. Under such conditions, some lonely students may be less likely to sustain the repetitive engagement patterns commonly associated with SMA. Moreover, while social media may help maintain social connection, it may also intensify perceived disconnection when online interactions are experienced as superficial or unsatisfying [47]. Bonsaksen et al. [45] reported that individuals who used social media mainly to maintain relationships experienced greater loneliness, while Wu et al. [46] observed increasing loneliness alongside problematic social media use among Chinese nursing students. These findings suggest that the relationship between loneliness and SMA may depend on how students use and experience social media, rather than on loneliness alone.

In the present study, contextual factors may have contributed to the negative association observed between loneliness and SMA. For example, some students may have used non-digital coping strategies, such as spending time with family, engaging in face-to-face interaction, listening to music, or practicing mindfulness and meditation, rather than relying heavily on social media [5, 48]. Saudi Arabia’s family-oriented culture, which is characterized by strong intergenerational relationships and close family networks, may also influence how students respond to loneliness [49]. However, these explanations should be interpreted cautiously because the study did not directly measure coping strategies, family support, or reasons for social media use. Therefore, the negative association between loneliness and SMA should be viewed as a context-specific finding that warrants further examination in longitudinal and cross-cultural nursing education research.

The explained variance for the main study outcomes was low, indicating that the model accounted for only a small proportion of the variability in SMA, ASE, and academic performance. Although several structural paths were statistically significant, the practical predictive contribution of loneliness and SMA appears modest. From a theoretical perspective, this suggests that the proposed model offers only a partial explanation of SMA, ASE, and academic performance among nursing students. Other relevant determinants not included in the present study may play more substantial roles, including personality traits, anxiety, depression, stress, self-esteem, resilience, academic motivation, family support, institutional support, and other contextual influences [14]. From a practical perspective, these findings suggest that interventions targeting loneliness or SMA alone may be insufficient to produce meaningful improvements in students’ academic and psychological outcomes. Nursing educators and student support services should therefore consider broader and multifactorial approaches that address psychological, social, and academic influences on student well-being and performance.

Contrary to Hypotheses 1b and 1c, loneliness was positively associated with both ASE and academic performance. These findings differ from much of the existing literature, which generally suggests that loneliness is associated with poorer self-efficacy and academic outcomes [18, 48, 50]. Prior longitudinal research has also indicated that loneliness may predict sustained declines in ASE and academic performance among adolescents [51]. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that loneliness improves academic functioning. Rather, they should be understood as unexpected associations observed in this specific sample and study context.

Several methodological and contextual factors may help explain these unexpected findings. First, loneliness may have been experienced differently among participants and may not have uniformly reflected academic disengagement or dysfunction. Second, the structured nature of nursing education may have helped some students maintain academic routines despite feelings of loneliness. Third, cultural and family-related factors in Saudi Arabia may have influenced how students experienced and managed loneliness [49]. However, these explanations remain tentative because the study did not directly assess these mechanisms. The cross-sectional design also prevents conclusions about directionality. Therefore, future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether loneliness precedes changes in ASE and academic performance, or whether other unmeasured factors explain these associations.

The positive association between loneliness and academic performance should be interpreted with particular caution because academic performance was assessed using self-reported GPA rather than verified institutional records. Self-reported GPA is vulnerable to recall bias and social desirability bias and may not always accurately reflect students’ actual academic standing. Some participants may have overestimated or underestimated their GPA, which could have influenced the direction or magnitude of the observed association. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that loneliness enhances academic performance, but rather as an association that requires further verification using objective academic indicators.

Although ASE was theoretically expected to be associated with academic performance, the path was not statistically significant in the present study, thereby not supporting Hypothesis 2. This finding may be explained by several methodological and sample-related factors. First, the relatively high mean ASE score may indicate a possible ceiling effect, which could have reduced the measure’s ability to detect meaningful differences among participants. Second, the restricted variability in academic performance may have attenuated the observed association. Third, the unequal distribution of participants across year levels, with most respondents coming from the first and third years, may have limited the comparability of academic experiences across groups. Finally, because ASE was measured through self-report, participants may have overestimated or underestimated their academic capabilities. Taken together, these factors may help explain why ASE was not significantly related to academic performance in this sample despite its theoretical relevance.

The model also showed that SMA was positively associated with ASE, contrary to Hypothesis 3a, while negatively associated with academic performance, consistent with Hypothesis 3b. This pattern suggests that the role of social media use in nursing education may be complex. Previous studies have more commonly reported negative or indirect relationships between problematic internet or social media use and self-efficacy [52, 53]. For example, internet addiction among Iranian nursing students was found to adversely affect academic and clinical experiences [53], and similar patterns were observed among Peruvian university students [52]. However, in the present study, higher SMA was associated with higher perceived academic capability but lower academic performance.

One possible explanation is that students may use social media for both academic and non-academic purposes. Some students may rely on social media to access learning materials, coordinate with peers, participate in group discussions, or engage with instructional videos and other academic resources. Such use may be associated with greater perceived academic competence or confidence. At the same time, excessive or difficult-to-control social media use may reduce study time, fragment attention, and interfere with sustained academic work, which may explain its negative association with academic performance. However, this interpretation should be treated cautiously because the study did not measure the specific purposes, duration, quality, or context of students’ social media use. Future studies should distinguish between academic, social, recreational, and compulsive social media use to clarify their different relationships with ASE and academic outcomes.

These findings suggest that social media use may have different associations with perceived academic capability and measurable academic performance. In this study, SMA was positively associated with ASE but negatively associated with self-reported GPA. This finding is consistent with prior evidence showing poorer academic outcomes associated with SMA [17, 19], including meta-analytic evidence on the harmful association between social networking addiction and university students’ academic achievement [54]. For nursing students, this issue is important because reduced academic performance may affect clinical learning, professional preparedness, and workforce development. Nevertheless, the positive association between SMA and ASE should not be interpreted causally, as the cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions about whether SMA increases ASE, whether students with higher ASE use social media differently, or whether both are influenced by other unmeasured variables.

SMA significantly mediated the relationship between loneliness and ASE, supporting Hypothesis 4a. Specifically, the indirect effect was negative, suggesting that SMA weakened the positive association between loneliness and ASE. However, SMA did not significantly mediate the relationship between loneliness and academic performance; therefore, Hypothesis 4b was not supported. This clarification is important because it indicates that SMA may help explain the association between loneliness and perceived academic capability, but not the association between loneliness and academic performance. Moreover, because loneliness and SMA explained only a small proportion of the variance in ASE and academic performance, additional psychological, social, academic, and cultural factors should be considered in future models.

Although previous literature has often shown that loneliness is associated with higher SMA and poorer academic or self-efficacy outcomes [8, 17, 19, 21, 54], the present findings suggest that these relationships may vary by context. Cultural norms, family support systems, educational structure, and students’ reasons for using social media may influence how loneliness and SMA relate to academic outcomes. Consequently, nursing education institutions should assess students’ loneliness and problematic social media use while also considering broader contextual factors that may affect academic functioning and well-being.

Finally, the findings should be considered as theory-informing rather than theory-confirming. From the perspective of SCT [32], the positive association between loneliness and ASE was unexpected and may indicate that self-efficacy is influenced by additional personal, social, and contextual factors not captured in the present model. From the perspective of CIUT [33], the negative association between loneliness and SMA suggests that negative emotional states may not always lead to compensatory online behavior. However, because the study was cross-sectional and did not directly measure coping motives or social media use patterns, these theoretical interpretations should be viewed as preliminary. Longitudinal and mixed-methods studies are needed to clarify the temporal ordering, mechanisms, and cultural conditions underlying these relationships.

Implications for nursing education and practice

This study’s findings have implications for nursing policymakers, education, and practice. Nursing policymakers may need to collaborate with key stakeholders in nursing education and practices, such as nursing educators, healthcare administrators, and nurse managers, to create a streamlined policy addressing loneliness and SMA. This policy is comprehensive and integrated, cutting across nursing education and practice. Thus, applicable in nursing education and clinical internship settings. Policies may include programs that contain instruments that assess loneliness and SMA, and interventions to prevent or alleviate them. For instance, buddy programs, peer mentoring, and inclusive spaces effectively address students’ loneliness. Time and self-management courses, responsible smartphone use, engaging in hobbies or sports, and participating in friends and family events could prevent and divert students from excessive social media use. Finally, instituting individual and group trainings that foster ASE, such as creating attainable and realistic goals, an optimistic and growth mindset, positive and constructive feedback, and empowerment, may boost academic performance while alleviating loneliness and SMA.

Limitations and recommendations

This investigation had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precluded any causal inference regarding the relationships among loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance. Second, the use of consecutive sampling across five study settings may have limited participant representativeness and reduced the broader generalizability of the findings. Third, all data were obtained through self-report questionnaires, which are vulnerable to recall bias and social desirability bias and may have resulted in overestimation or underestimation of responses. In particular, academic performance was represented by self-reported GPA, which may distort associations with psychological variables because students may overreport or underreport their academic standing due to recall error, impression management, or social desirability. As a result, the observed relationships between GPA and variables such as loneliness, SMA, and ASE may have been attenuated, inflated, or otherwise affected by measurement inaccuracy. This limitation is especially relevant to the unexpected positive association observed between loneliness and academic performance, which should therefore be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken as definitive evidence that loneliness enhances objective academic outcomes. No direct verification of GPA against official academic records was conducted. Because the study was implemented across five universities using an anonymous self-administered survey, linking participants’ responses to institutional records was not feasible within the approved study procedures and would also have required additional administrative permissions and identifiable data handling.

Furthermore, unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the observed relationships. The unexpected directional findings observed in the model should also be interpreted cautiously, as these associations may reflect context-specific mechanisms, suppression effects within the multivariable SEM model, measurement-related nuances, or the limitations of applying broad psychological theories uniformly across different cultural and educational contexts. Accordingly, the present findings should be viewed as theory-refining rather than theory-confirming, and robust generalization cannot be fully assured.

Given the low proportion of variance explained by the model, future research should first prioritize longitudinal, prospective, or experimental designs to clarify the direction, stability, temporal ordering, and possible causal associations among loneliness, SMA, ASE, and academic performance. Second, future studies should use probability-based sampling, larger and more geographically diverse samples, and objective academic records to improve representativeness and measurement precision. Third, expanded models should incorporate additional contextual and individual-level factors, such as culture, coping styles, family support, psychological distress, resilience, academic motivation, and personality traits, to better explain the observed associations. Finally, qualitative or mixed-methods approaches may provide deeper insight into how nursing students experience loneliness, social media use, and academic functioning within specific sociocultural and educational contexts.

Conclusion

This study found that loneliness was negatively associated with SMA and positively associated with ASE and academic performance among nursing students. SMA was positively associated with ASE but negatively associated with academic performance. SMA significantly mediated the association between loneliness and ASE, but not the association between loneliness and academic performance. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the study was cross-sectional, the explained variance was modest, and several associations differed from the hypothesized directions. The findings suggest three important considerations for nursing education. First, nursing institutions and educators should assess loneliness and problematic social media use as part of broader student support strategies, while recognizing that these factors alone may not fully explain students’ academic self-efficacy or performance. Second, faculty members, advisers, and student support personnel who assist students experiencing loneliness or problematic social media use may benefit from training in early identification, referral, and supportive intervention. Finally, nursing students should be encouraged to develop positive coping strategies, responsible social media habits, and academic self-efficacy to support their well-being and academic functioning.
