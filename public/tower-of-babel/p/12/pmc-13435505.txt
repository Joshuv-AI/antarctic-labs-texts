Adaptation of Snyder adult dispositional hope scale into Chinese: a cross-sectional study on reliability and validity test in older adults with coronary heart disease
Authors: Yatian Cheng, Yubing Xia, Wei Zhang, Fang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults with coronary heart disease (CHD) express hope differently from the general population due to disease progression, physiological decline, and changing social roles. However, hope assessment in China lacks culturally validated tools for this group.ObjectiveThis study translated and culturally adapted the Adult Dispositional Hope Scale (ADHS) into Chinese and evaluated its reliability and validity in older adults with CHD.MethodsThe ADHS was translated using the Brislin model. Psychometric properties were evaluated in 473 older adults with CHD recruited via convenience sampling across three rounds.ResultsThe Chinese ADHS comprises 12 items across two dimensions, featuring four non-scored distractor items. Item analysis demonstrated significant inter-group discrimination between high/low scorers (P < 0.001) and robust item-total correlations (P < 0.001), hence affirming discriminant validity. Reliability analyses demonstrated internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.923; split-half reliability = 0.933) and composite reliability meeting thresholds for pathways thinking and agency thinking dimensions. High test-retest r = 0.951, (2-week interval) indicated measurement stability. The content validity was robust (scale-level CVI = 0.938; item-level I-CVI range = 0.786–1.000). Exploratory factor analysis identified two factors that accounted for 78.406% cumulative variance. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated satisfactory model fit (CFI = 0.951, RMSEA = 0.084), supporting structural validity. Convergent validity was evidenced by strong positive correlations with the Herth Hope Index (r = 0.907, P < 0.001), whereas discriminant validity was established via negative correlations with the Geriatric Depression Scale (r = − 0.832, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe Chinese ADHS is a reliable and valid tool for assessing hope in older Chinese CHD adults. However, generalizability is limited by sample homogeneity and contextual factors. Further validation in broader populations is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07698-y.Keywords: Hope, Older adults, Coronary heart disease, Cross-cultural adaptation, Reliability and Validity

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of high incidence, disability, mortality, and healthcare burden worldwide [1]. China is experiencing the most rapid CVD epidemic transition, with CVD now the primary contributor to national disability-adjusted life years [2]. CHD accounts for the majority of CVD-related mortality globally [3], and over 11.39 million documented CHD cases in China have imposed substantial strain on healthcare delivery and generated significant socioeconomic burden [4].

In China, older adults represent a high-risk group for CHD, with markedly increased vulnerability to comorbid mental health disorders [5]. Older adults with CHD commonly experience disease-related physical morbidity, functional deterioration, and compromised self-care ability [6]. These sequelae trigger adverse psychological consequences. These include elevated distress, reduced life engagement, and heightened illness-related uncertainty [7]. Such psychopathology has been shown to independently exacerbate cardiovascular and metabolic risks, and predict major adverse cardiovascular events [8]. Thus, integrated clinical management targeting mental health comorbidities is critical to improve long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Increasing evidence has confirmed the significant cardioprotective effects of positive psychosocial factors. A 20-year longitudinal study identified positive psychological dimensions as independent predictors of sustained cardiovascular health [9]. Targeted positive psychology interventions can effectively enhance hope in populations with chronic disease. Hope, in turn, acts as a validated buffer against disease-induced psychosocial stress [10]. As a core construct of positive psychology, hope exerts multidimensional motivational influences: elevated hope levels correlate with improved immunoregulatory function and adaptive health behaviors, facilitating disease self-management and psychological resilience [11]. It also serves as a key coping strategy for navigating illness-related adversity and maintaining psychological homeostasis [12], and acts as a protective psychological factor inversely associated with anxiety and depression severity in clinical populations [13]. These findings highlight the substantial clinical value of hope-targeted biopsychosocial interventions for older adults with CHD, with important implications for clinical practice and public health [14].

Validated, culturally appropriate tools for hope assessment remain limited in clinical settings. The ADHS, developed by Snyder and colleagues based on the seminal hope theory, is the most widely used theory-grounded instrument for dispositional hope measurement [15]. Designed for individuals aged 15 years and older, the ADHS comprises 12 items assessing two core dimensions: pathways thinking and agency thinking. Since its development, the ADHS has been translated and validated across diverse cultural contexts. However, findings regarding its factor structure have been inconsistent. While Snyder’s original two-factor model has been widely supported in North American cross-ethnic samples [16] and Asian populations including Japan [17] and general Chinese communities [18], other studies have challenged this dimensionality. For instance, Brouwer et al. [19] reported a unidimensional structure in a Dutch sample, suggesting that the two intercorrelated components may be statistically indistinguishable in certain populations. These inconsistencies across populations, combined with the complete lack of validation in older Chinese CHD adults, highlight a critical gap. It is essential to empirically examine whether the original two-factor structure holds, or whether a more parsimonious one-factor model provides better fit for this specific clinical group.

Previous Chinese adaptations of the ADHS have primarily focused on university students or general community samples, with very limited validation in clinical settings. Consequently, these existing versions lack disease-specific contextualization required for older adults with CHD, who typically conceptualize hope through the lens of chronic illness management rather than general life ambition. In addition, the methodological rigor regarding conceptual equivalence for geriatric populations has been inconsistent across previous adaptations, compromising their measurement accuracy in older clinical cohorts [20]. This has resulted in a critical shortage of validated hope scales suitable for medical contexts in China. Notably, most contemporary domestic intervention studies adopt Snyder’s hope theory as the theoretical framework, yet predominantly use the Herth Hope Index (HHI) for outcome assessment [21]. This theoretical mismatch between intervention design and evaluation instrument substantially undermines the scientific validity and practical applicability of research findings. Thus, there is an urgent need for psychometrically robust, culturally and clinically adapted hope measurement tools for Chinese clinical populations.

Older adults with CHD face unique age- and disease-related psychosocial challenges that necessitate targeted hope assessment tools [22]. While the ADHS has been validated in diverse groups including nurses [23], caregivers [24], patients with schizophrenia [25], and patients with cancer [26, 27], and its Korean and Thai versions have been validated in patients with hemiplegic stroke [28] and acute myocardial infarction [29] respectively, its applicability to older adults with CHD remains completely unexplored. This subgroup experiences cumulative impacts from chronic disease progression, physiological decline, and altered social roles, which may lead to distinct patterns of hope expression compared to the general population. Therefore, this study aimed to conduct rigorous cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the ADHS specifically for older adults with CHD, to establish a validated, clinically appropriate instrument for hope assessment in this cohort.

While embedding CHD-specific clinical realities into the scale adaptation may risk narrowing the construct toward health-related hope, this work remains fundamentally anchored in Snyder’s dispositional hope theory. For older adults navigating chronic cardiovascular disease, the primary domain of goal-directed cognition is inherently tied to illness management and survival. Thus, capturing agency and pathways thinking within this dominant, highly salient life context reflects the localized, clinical expression of dispositional hope, rather than a departure from the core theoretical framework.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted to translate and culturally adapt the ADHS into Chinese, and evaluate its psychometric properties in older adults with CHD. The study was implemented in three sequential rounds with unique participant cohorts to ensure complete sample independence for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), with no data overlap across rounds. Round 1 (January 2025) was a pilot test involving 30 older adults with CHD recruited via purposive sampling. Rounds 2 and 3 (March–April and May–June 2025) were used for formal psychometric evaluation.

Study participants

Older adults with CHD were recruited using convenience sampling from multiple hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Aged ≥ 60 years; (2) Clinically confirmed diagnosis of coronary artery disease by attending cardiologists; (3) Hemodynamically stable, and able to complete the questionnaire independently or with minimal assistance; (4) Provided written informed consent prior to study participation.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Severe comorbid physical, psychiatric, or neurological disorders; (2) Cognitive impairment or communication barriers that hindered questionnaire completion; (3) Experienced major life events accompanied by significant emotional distress within the past 3 months; (4) Concurrent participation in similar interventional studies.All eligibility criteria were rigorously verified via electronic medical record review, oversight from attending cardiologists, and a standardized screening interview during the informed consent process.

Sample size estimation

Sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1). With 12 scale items, parameters set to α = 0.05, β = 0.20, and effect size f² = 0.30 [15], the minimum required sample size was 150 for EFA and 200 for CFA to ensure adequate statistical power for stable parameter estimation. In Round 2, 280 questionnaires were distributed, and 255 valid responses were returned (effective response rate: 91.07%). In Round 3, 240 questionnaires were distributed, yielding 218 valid responses (effective response rate: 90.83%). For test-retest reliability evaluation, 40 patients from Round 2 were re-administered the questionnaire after a 2-week interval, in line with the 30–40 participant recommendation from classical test theory [30].

Measures

Adult Dispositional Hope Scale (ADHS)

The original ADHS, developed by Snyder et al. (1991) [15], is a widely used self-report instrument to assess dispositional hope in individuals aged ≥ 15 years. It consists of 12 items, including 4 items measuring pathways thinking, 4 items measuring agency thinking, and 4 non-scored distractor items. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Definitely False to 4 = Definitely True), with total scores ranging from 8 to 32; higher scores indicate higher levels of dispositional hope. The original scale has demonstrated good psychometric properties across diverse populations, with internal consistency Cronbach’s α of 0.74–0.88 and test-retest reliability r ≈ 0.80.

With formal permission from the American Psychological Association (the copyright holder of the original scale), the ADHS was translated and culturally adapted into Chinese using a modified Brislin translation model integrated with an expert committee procedure, in accordance with international guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of patient-reported outcome measures [31]. The rigorous process included: (1) Forward translation: Two independent bilingual PhD candidates translated the original English scale into Chinese, with a third bilingual translator reconciling the two versions to produce a preliminary Chinese draft; (2) Back-translation: Two bilingual experts with no prior exposure to the original scale independently translated the Chinese draft back into English, with the research team revising semantic inconsistencies between the back-translated versions and the original scale; (3) Cross-cultural adaptation: A panel of 6 domain experts (2 psychologists, 2 cardiovascular disease prevention specialists, 2 geriatric health management experts, 1 nursing specialist, and 1 English language specialist) evaluated the scale for conceptual equivalence, cultural appropriateness, and clinical relevance to older adults with CHD; (4) Pilot testing: The pre-final version was administered to 30 older adults with CHD to systematically assess item comprehension via a hybrid quantitative-qualitative approach. No items were added or deleted during the adaptation process; only minor wording modifications were made to enhance clarity and clinical contextualization for the target population, while strictly adhering to the core theoretical constructs of the original scale.

Herth Hope Index (HHI)

The HHI, originally developed by Herth (1992) [21] and translated into Chinese by Zhao (1999) [32], is a widely validated instrument to assess hope levels in Chinese clinical populations. It consists of 12 items across three subscales: positive attitude toward the present and future, positive initiative, and sustaining interpersonal connections. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree), with 2 reverse-scored items. Total scores range from 12 to 48, with higher scores indicating greater hopefulness. The Chinese version has demonstrated good psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s α of 0.85 and content validity index of 0.77. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the HHI was 0.916.

Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15)

The GDS-15, developed by Sheikh and Yesavage (1986) [33], is a geriatric-specific self-report instrument to assess depressive symptoms in older adults. It includes 15 dichotomous (yes/no) items measuring depressive symptoms over the preceding week, with total scores ranging from 0 to 15; higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The Chinese version of the GDS-15 has been widely validated in Chinese older populations, with good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α of the GDS-15 was 0.889.

Data collection procedure

All questionnaires were administered by uniformly trained graduate research assistants. Prior to questionnaire distribution, standardized instructions were provided to participants regarding the study objectives, questionnaire completion guidelines, and confidentiality protections. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Most questionnaires were self-administered, with interviewer assistance provided for participants with limited education or visual impairment. Completed questionnaires were collected immediately upon completion, with dual verification by two research assistants to check for missing data or logical inconsistencies.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0) and IBM Amos (version 28.0). A two-sided P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance, unless otherwise specified.

Item analysis

Item analysis was evaluated using two standard psychometric methods [34]: (1) Extreme groups method: Independent samples t-tests were used to compare item scores between the top 27% and bottom 27% of total scale scorers [35], with a significance threshold of P < 0.001 set for acceptable discrimination; (2) Item-total correlation: Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between each item and the total scale score, with r > 0.40 set as the threshold for acceptable item relevance.

Reliability evaluation

(1) Internal consistency: Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability were calculated for the total scale and each dimension, with Cronbach’s α > 0.70 considered acceptable [36]; (2) Composite reliability (CR): CR was calculated for the pathways thinking and agency thinking dimensions, with CR > 0.70 considered acceptable [37]; (3) Test-retest reliability: The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, two-way mixed effects model, single measures, absolute agreement) was calculated with a 2-week interval, with ICC > 0.70 considered acceptable [38]; (4) Measurement error: In accordance with COSMIN guidelines [39], standard error of measurement (SEM) and smallest detectable change at the 95% confidence level (SDC₉₅) were calculated using the following formulas:

Validity evaluation

(1) Content validity: Eight experts involved in the cross-cultural adaptation process rated the relevance of each item to the target construct on a 4-point scale (1 = irrelevant, 4 = highly relevant). Item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level CVI based on universal agreement (S-CVI/UA) were calculated, with I-CVI > 0.78 and S-CVI/UA > 0.90 set as acceptable thresholds [40].(2) Construct validity: EFA was performed using principal component analysis with varimax rotation, with factors retained if eigenvalues > 1. The suitability of data for factor analysis was verified using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test (KMO > 0.8 considered adequate) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < 0.001 considered significant) [41]. Items were retained if they had a primary factor loading > 0.50 and cross-loadings < 0.30. As a sensitivity analysis, principal axis factoring with parallel analysis (factors retained if eigenvalues exceeded the 95th percentile of random data eigenvalues) was also conducted [42]. CFA was performed using maximum likelihood estimation, with model fit evaluated using multiple indices: χ²/df, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), normed fit index (NFI), relative fit index (RFI), incremental fit index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and comparative fit index (CFI), with thresholds as recommended by Hair et al. [43]. Discriminant validity between the two dimensions was assessed using the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT < 0.85 considered acceptable) and chi-square difference test between the hypothesized two-factor model and the alternative one-factor model.(3) Criterion validity: In accordance with a priori hypotheses, convergent validity was evaluated by calculating the Pearson correlation between the Chinese ADHS total score and the HHI score (hypothesized strong positive correlation: r > 0.50); discriminant validity was evaluated by calculating the Pearson correlation between the Chinese ADHS total score and the GDS-15 score (hypothesized strong negative correlation: r < − 0.50) [39].

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The principle of voluntary participation was followed throughout the study, with all participants informed of their right to withdraw at any time without negative consequences. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained, with all data anonymized and stored securely. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of NanFang Hospital of Southern Medical University (Approval No.: NFEC-2022-126). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Patient and public involvement

Older adults with CHD were involved in the pilot testing phase. Thirty patients provided feedback on item comprehension, cultural appropriateness, and clinical relevance of the scale, which informed the final revision of the Chinese ADHS. All participants in the formal study completed the questionnaires and provided the core data for psychometric evaluation.

The expert consultation procedure comprises two rounds. In the first round, all eight distributed questionnaires were returned valid, resulting in a 100% response rate. The second round of consultation did not have any additions or deletions but led to minor refinements in the phrasing of several entries. The resulting pre-test version of the Hope scale for older adults with CHD comprised 12 items across two dimensions.

The quantitative ratings indicated that the items scored highly in clarity, while the patients’ oral feedback further confirmed that the wording was straightforward and easily understood. Overall, no significant comprehension barriers were reported. The average completion time was within 10 min, reflecting a low respondent burden. Item analysis

The critical ratio method was utilized to evaluate item discrimination. All subjects were ranked in descending order according to their overall scale scores. The highest 27% of respondents were designated as the high-score group, and the lowest 27% were classified as the low-score group. An independent samples t-test was performed for each item to compare the scores of the two groups. Statistically significant differences between high- and low-scoring groups were observed for all items (P < 0.001), demonstrating satisfactory discriminant validity. Furthermore, item-total correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the homogeneity and internal consistency of the scale. The results revealed that all item-total correlation coefficients varied between 0.757 and 0.845, with each correlation being statistically significant (P < 0.001). A correlation coefficient exceeding 0.40 signifies a strong relationship between the item and the total score. In this study, all item-total correlations were significantly above this threshold, affirming the items’ strong alignment with the core construct of the scale and supporting the content validity and conceptual coherence of the measurement. Items can be retained in their entirety. Table 3 presents the detailed results.

Reliability

Following the item analysis, the reliability of the scale was assessed. This study comprehensively evaluated the reliability of the Chinese version of ADHS using multiple metrics. The results indicated positive internal consistency and temporal stability of the scale.

All values exceeded recommended thresholds (α > 0.70), indicating good internal consistency. For the total scale, Cronbach’s α was 0.923 (95% CI [0.907, 0.936]), and split-half reliability was 0.933. The Pathway Thinking subscale yielded values of 0.924 and 0.901, respectively, while the Agency Thinking subscale values were 0.882 and 0.889. According to conventional psychometric criteria, values exceeding 0.70 are deemed acceptable, those above 0.80 are regarded as good, and those above 0.90 are classified as excellent. In this study, all values surpassed 0.88, with the total scale and Pathway Thinking subscale exceeding 0.90, suggesting exceptionally high internal consistency and robust item homogeneity across the entire scale and within each dimension. In this study, CR values were 0.906 for the Pathways Thinking dimension and 0.881 for the Agency Thinking dimension, both surpassing the recommended threshold of 0.80. These results substantiate the high reliability of the latent constructs across dimensions and affirm that the scale robustly measures the core traits of Pathways Thinking and Agency Thinking.

A subset of 40 participants was randomly selected from the initial cohort of 255 older adults with CHD for retesting two weeks after the initial assessment. The test-retest reliability coefficients were 0.951 for the total scale, 0.900 for the Pathways Thinking dimension, and 0.950 for the Agency Thinking dimension. All values surpassed 0.90, with the total scale and Agency Thinking dimension exceeding 0.95. The test–retest reliability (ICC) was 0.951, indicating excellent stability over time. Furthermore, the measurement error indices demonstrated a Standard Error of Measurement (SEM) of 1.136 and a Smallest Detectable Change at the 95% confidence level (SDC95) of 3.149. These values suggest that the Chinese version of the ADHS has high measurement precision and is sensitive to identifying real clinical changes in older CHD adults. (Table 4).

Validity

After confirming the scale’s internal consistency and stability, we evaluated its construct and criterion validity. Content validity analysis indicated item-level content validity indices (I-CVI) between 0.786 and 1.000, with a scale-level CVI based on universal agreement (S-CVI/UA) of 0.938. Consistent with established criteria, I-CVI values exceeding 0.78 and S-CVI/UA above 0.90 signify strong content validity. In this study, all items met the acceptable I-CVI threshold, and the overall S-CVI/UA surpassed 0.90. These results confirm that the items comprehensively capture the fundamental conceptual domains of adult trait hope, align well with the theoretical model, and substantiate the high content validity of the instrument. For the detailed expert rating matrix, please see Supplementary Table S2.

EFA was performed to examine the structural validity of the scale and determine whether the items aligned with the expected dimensions. The KMO measure was 0.881, signifying sampling adequacy; values between 0.8 and 0.9 are typically regarded as meritorious. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 1587.865, P < 0.001), indicating the data appropriateness for factor analysis [44]. Principal component analysis utilizing varimax rotation identified two common factors. Factor 1 possessed an eigenvalue of 3.225, representing 40.312% of the variance; Factor 2 exhibited an eigenvalue of 3.047, representing 38.093% of the variance. The cumulative variance contribution rate was 78.406%. The cumulative variance contribution rate exceeding 50% often signifies a valid factor structure; the significantly higher value obtained in this study demonstrates that the two factors effectively account for the total variance of the scale, thereby endorsing a stable factorial construct. Consistent with Snyder’s hope theory, the factors were interpreted as Pathways Thinking (items 1, 4, 6, 8) and Agency Thinking (items 2, 9, 10, 12), totaling eight items. The resultant factor structure aligns well with the theoretical model, affirming structural validity. Table 5 presents the results. Furthermore, to test the robustness of the factor structure and address recent methodological recommendations, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) with Parallel Analysis (PA). The PA results based on common factor eigenvalues indicated that the first two actual eigenvalues (4.92 and 0.73) significantly exceeded the 95th percentile thresholds derived from random data (0.41 and 0.27, respectively), providing strong support for the two-factor solution. As the factor structure remained consistent across both extraction methods, we report the PCA results in the main text to facilitate comparison with prior literature (Table 6).

CFA was performed to assess the adequacy of the two-factor structure identified in the EFA. The model fit indices obtained were as follows: CMIN/DF = 2.522, RMSEA = 0.084 (90% CI [0.054–0.114], P = 0.031), RMR = 0.021, GFI = 0.951, NFI = 0.957, RFI = 0.937, IFI = 0.974, TLI = 0.961, and CFI = 0.973. All model fit indices met or approached established reference standards. Moreover, all factor loadings significantly exceeded the acceptable threshold of 0.5, confirming that each item served as a robust indicator of its intended latent construct. The two-factor model exhibited a good fit with the actual data, which further validates the rationality and stability of the scale’s structure in this specific sample. However, given the RMSEA value of 0.084 and the small degrees of freedom for this 12-item model, these results require cautious interpretation. Notably, the interpretability of the factor structure is further constrained by the geographic and clinical homogeneity of the convenience sample used in the present study. The correlation between Pathways Thinking and Agency Thinking was 0.83, signifying a strong positive association. To further evaluate the robustness of the model structure, we inspected the Modification Indices (MIs) for potential cross-loadings and error correlations. The analysis revealed no significant cross-loadings in the regression weights, supporting the clear differentiation of the two factors. While minor error covariances were suggested (maximum M.I. = 10.222), they were not included in the final model to verify the principle of parsimony, as the baseline model fit was already excellent (CFI > 0.95) and theoretically sound. This reflects the intrinsic conceptual connection between the two dimensions within the “hope” construct, while simultaneously affirming their distinctiveness, consistent with the two-dimensional framework of Snyder’s hope theory. To robustly test for discriminant validity and assess potential unidimensionality, we compared the proposed two-factor model with a constrained one-factor model where all items loaded onto a single latent variable. The one-factor model demonstrated poor fit indices (χ2 = 116.96, df = 20, CFI = 0.914, RMSEA = 0.138). In strict contrast, the two-factor model provided a significantly better fit to the data (χ2 = 39.87, df = 19, CFI = 0.973, RMSEA = 0.084). A Chi-square difference test confirmed the statistical superiority of the two-factor structure (Δχ2 = 77.09, Δdf = 1,P < 0.001). These results provide strong empirical evidence that the two constructs are distinct, refuting the hypothesis of unidimensionality.

Criterion-related validity analysis revealed that the total score and the two factor scores of the Chinese version of ADHS exhibited significant positive correlation with the total and subscale scores of HHI, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.746 to 0.907 (P < 0.001). Conversely, significant negative correlations were observed with the GDS-15, with coefficients ranging from − 0.751 to − 0.832 (P < 0.001). Table 8 presents a detailed correlation.

Discussion

This study culturally adapted and psychometrically validated the Chinese version of the ADHS specifically for older adults with CHD. The results confirm the scale’s excellent reliability and validity, filling a critical gap in China’s clinical research: most existing hope interventions are designed based on Snyder’s hope theory, but outcome assessment predominantly relies on the HHI, creating a theoretical mismatch that undermines the scientific validity of related research. This study provides a theory-aligned, clinically appropriate tool for hope assessment in older Chinese CHD adults.

Comparison with the original and other language versions

Compared with the original ADHS [15], as well as validated Korean [28] and Thai versions [29], the Chinese version demonstrated higher internal consistency and temporal stability. Several factors may explain this. First, the disease-specific contextualization during adaptation anchored abstract hope constructs to tangible illness management, reducing semantic ambiguity while naturally increasing inter-item correlations. Second, the clinical and geographic homogeneity of our sample likely contributed to more uniform response patterns. Third, cultural consensus on goal attainment and coping within this population may further improve consistency. Notably, common-method bias was ruled out via Harman’s single-factor test, confirming these high psychometric indices reflect robust instrument performance rather than statistical artifacts.

Factor structure and clinical interpretation

The original two-factor structure (pathways thinking and agency thinking) of the ADHS was well supported by both EFA and CFA in this study, which is consistent with Snyder’s core theoretical framework. The root mean square error of approximation slightly exceeded the conventional stringent cutoff of 0.08, which is likely attributable to the small degrees of freedom in the 12-item model, a known factor that can inflate RMSEA estimates. Given that the comparative fit index and Tucker-Lewis index indicated excellent model fit, the overall two-factor structure was deemed satisfactory. The strong inter-factor correlation and heterotrait-monotrait ratio further confirmed that the two dimensions are theoretically distinct yet closely interrelated, in line with the original hope theory. Pathways thinking items, particularly Q8, showed the highest factor loadings. This reflects that older CHD adults prioritize tangible, actionable disease management strategies when expressing hope. Agency thinking items also exhibited robust loadings, indicating that patients’ motivation to cope with illness is as critical as their ability to identify concrete solutions [46]. This finding aligns with clinical observations that low motivation is a major barrier to rehabilitation adherence in older CHD adults, highlighting the scale’s value in identifying patients in need of targeted psychological support.

High correlation with HHI and conceptual overlap

The Chinese ADHS showed an exceptionally strong positive correlation with the HHI, which exceeded our a priori hypothesis and supported good convergent validity. However, this high correlation also raises concerns about potential construct redundancy. The HHI measures a broader, multidimensional concept of hope including interpersonal connection and spiritual dimensions, whereas the ADHS focuses specifically on goal-directed cognitive processes. This strong correlation may indicate that the disease-specific contextualization narrowed the construct toward health-related hope. This would align the ADHS more closely with the clinical focus of the HHI. This may reduce the incremental validity of the scale, meaning the Chinese ADHS may not capture unique variance in hope beyond existing tools. Future research should examine whether the two scales differentially predict key clinical outcomes, such as treatment adherence, cardiac rehabilitation completion, or major adverse cardiovascular events.

Context-specific manifestation of hope

The Chinese-adapted ADHS reflects a context-specific manifestation of dispositional hope for older adults with CHD. This positioning aligns with Snyder’s Hope Theory, which views hope as a dynamic construct that varies across disease contexts and cultural settings. For this population, goal-directed cognition is inherently tied to illness self-management and functional maintenance. Thus, the scale captures hope as it is naturally experienced in chronic cardiovascular care, representing a theoretically aligned and clinically appropriate localization. Furthermore, its concise structure (8 scorable items) and brief completion time (< 10 min) ensure high feasibility for routine clinical assessment. These features make it an ideal tool for evaluating the efficacy of hope-targeted psychological interventions in busy clinical settings.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we fully acknowledge that formal, structured cognitive interviewing—a core requirement for establishing robust patient-centered content validity under the COSMIN guidelines—was not performed in this study. This represents a methodological limitation in the qualitative depth of our content validity assessment. However, this gap was substantially addressed and mitigated through a rigorous, large-sample pilot testing phase (N = 30) prior to scale finalization, a practice widely supported by classical scale development frameworks [47, 48]. In accordance with patient-reported outcome (PRO) development norms, this pilot testing adopted a systematic quantitative-qualitative hybrid approach. We utilized a 5-point Likert scale to quantify the clarity, comprehensibility, and interpretability of each item, supplemented by standardized structured oral debriefings. This allowed us to collect targeted feedback on semantic consistency and the fit between item descriptions and real-world disease experiences. While this approach primarily focuses on comprehensibility—an indispensable foundational dimension of content validity under the COSMIN framework—rather than the deeper relevance explored in formal interviews, the high sample size provides greater statistical stability for clarity ratings than typical small-scale qualitative groups. Consequently, this robust verification ensures that the items are linguistically precise and culturally adapted for older patients, laying a solid groundwork for the scale’s patient-centered clinical application. Second, the sample was recruited via convenience sampling from hospitals in a single province, resulting in geographic and clinical homogeneity. This may limit the generalizability of the findings, and may have artificially inflated the reliability and validity coefficients. Multi-region, stratified sampling across diverse healthcare settings and patient subgroups is needed to further validate the scale. Third, the high correlation with the HHI suggests potential conceptual overlap between the two scales. Future research should further assess discriminant validity against related constructs and test the incremental validity of the Chinese ADHS. Specifically, studies should examine whether the scale predicts unique variance in clinical outcomes beyond the HHI. Fourth, the scale was validated exclusively in older adults with CHD. Its applicability to other age groups or clinical populations requires further cross-validation.

Conclusion

This study developed and psychometrically validated a Chinese version of the ADHS specifically for older adults with CHD. The scale exhibits excellent reliability and validity, providing a much-needed theory-consistent tool for hope assessment in this population, and addressing the long-standing mismatch between Snyder’s hope theory-based interventions and non-aligned evaluation instruments in domestic clinical research. Consistent with the reviewer’s recommendation, we acknowledge that the adapted scale may partially reflect a context-specific manifestation of dispositional hope oriented toward chronic illness management, rather than a purely general trait, as supported by the strong correlation with the HHI and disease-focused item contextualization.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample was recruited exclusively via convenience sampling from a single province, resulting in high geographic and clinical homogeneity. This not only restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions or patient subgroups, but may also have artificially inflated the reliability and validity coefficients. The sample size for confirmatory factor analysis, while meeting the minimum requirement, fell below more conservative sample size recommendations for psychometric validation. Second, the absence of systematic cognitive interviews with target patients—a key requirement for content validity under the COSMIN framework—remains a critical methodological gap. Future research should address these limitations through multi-stage stratified sampling across multiple geographic regions, with recruitment from both tertiary and secondary hospitals to ensure representation of adults with diverse healthcare access and clinical profiles. To fully establish the scale as a robust patient-reported outcome measure, subsequent studies should conduct full COSMIN-aligned evaluations, including systematic cognitive interviews, rigorous assessment of measurement error, and validation of responsiveness to clinical change, in more heterogeneous and geographically diverse samples.
