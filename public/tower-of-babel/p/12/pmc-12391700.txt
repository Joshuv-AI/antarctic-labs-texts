Self-assembled hybrid hydrogel microspheres create a bone marrow-mimicking niche for bone regeneration
Authors: Zhi He, Peilun Hu, Zifan Li, Kaige Mao, Jingchuan Zheng, Chun-Yi Yang, Yuyang Luo, Jia Yang, Zheng Cao, Jingsong Lu, Xiaobin Luo, Sengpav Tong, Zhijun He, Kunkoo Kim, Yaosai Liu, Xiaodan Sun, Lingyun Zhao, Yongwei Pan, Yongping Cao, Yu Wang, Xiumei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone marrow (BM), a natural niche rich in growth factors and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), provides an optimal regenerative microenvironment and is widely used in clinical applications. However, the limited proliferative capacity of BMSCs and the mismatch between bone regeneration and growth factors release constrain their effectiveness in treating critical bone defects. Drawing inspiration from the regenerative properties of BM, we developed self-assembled hybrid microspheres to replicate its function and address these challenges through a tissue engineering approach. This BM-mimicking niche enriched BMSCs via fast-degrading gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) microspheres, which were loaded with exogenous BMSCs and conjugated with stem cell homing peptides (SKP) to recruit endogenous BMSCs. SKP further enhanced the stemness of BMSCs, thereby promoting angiogenesis and resolving inflammation. Slow-degrading chitosan methacryloyl (ChitoMA) microspheres facilitated sustained release of angiogenic (KLT) and osteogenic (OGP) peptides, supporting blood vessel maturation and osteogenesis. The early release of BMSCs and SKP, followed by the subsequent release of OGP and KLT, aligned with the dynamic process of bone regeneration. In a rat critical femoral condyle defect model, the BM-mimicking niche formed an in-situ ossification center, significantly enhancing bone regeneration. This study introduces a novel BM-mimicking niche characterized by a BMSC-enriched environment and the sequential release of therapeutic factors, offering a promising strategy for treating critical bone defects.

Introduction

Severe bone defects remain one of the most challenging conditions in orthopedics, with autologous bone transplantation widely recognized as the gold standard for bone regeneration [1,2]. Despite limitations due to donor shortages and variable graft quality, the remarkable repair effects of autogenous bone grafts remain undeniable [3,4]. To further enhance the repair effects of autogenous bone, it is often combined with bone marrow (BM) aspirate in clinical practice [[5], [6], [7]]. BM not only supports regeneration in autogenous bone transplantation but is also frequently extracted to treat various orthopedic conditions. Its therapeutic effects are primarily attributed to bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and a variety of growth factors [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. The components of BM create a complex regenerative microenvironment, supporting the survival and differentiation of BMSCs, regulating immune response, and promoting angiogenesis, all of which are essential for bone defect repair [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Therefore, considering the unique role of BM in bone regeneration, creating a BM-mimicking niche presents a promising strategy for addressing bone defects.

BM, located in the central cavities of long bones and axial bones, is a highly cellular, gelatinous tissue filled within spongy cavities composed of trabecular bone [16,17]. BM is classified into two types: yellow BM and red BM. Yellow BM is primarily composed of adipose tissue, serving a space-filling function; red BM houses the active components responsible for hematopoiesis and provides the essential microenvironment for the survival, proliferation, and maintenance of differentiation potential (stemness) of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and BMSCs [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Moreover, BM encompasses endothelial cells that maintain vascular networks and immune cells that regulate inflammatory response, both vital for maintaining the homeostasis of the BM niche [22,23]. BM is also abundant in a variety of growth factors and cytokines, primarily including bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (b-FGF), transforming growth factor -β1(TGF-β1), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [[24], [25], [26]]. These bioactive ingredients make BM as a key player in the treatment of bone defects. BM modulates the healing process when aspirated and applied to bone defects. The paracrine effects of HSCs and BMSCs, along with the growth factor in BM aspirates, promote the transformation of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing the angiogenic ability of the injured site and recruiting additional BMSCs to participate in repair and microenvironment regulation [12,[27], [28], [29]]. As healing progresses, the natural extracellular matrix degrades rapidly, exposing HSCs and BMSCs that interact with the surrounding matrix and cells, subsequently entering the stage of osteogenic differentiation [[29], [30], [31]].

Although BM remarkably regulates the early healing process, it faces many limitations in addressing critical bone defects. The inadequate mechanical properties of the BM matrix restrict its application as a supportive material. Consequently, it is necessary to combine BM with autologous bone or demineralized bone matrix (DBM) to enhance its mechanical stability and achieve the desired therapeutic effect [7,32]. Moreover, the insufficient concentration of BMSCs in BM aspirate, ranging from 0.001 % to 0.02 %, compromises the reliability of treating critical bone defects. Clinically, bone graft substitutes incorporating in vitro expanded BMSCs with biomaterials have increasingly been used to solve the shortages of autologous bone supply and the limited number of BMSCs in BM aspirate [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. Despite the promise shown by current clinical strategies, such as combining BMSCs with biomaterial scaffolds, their efficacy in the treatment of critical bone defects remains insufficient [[36], [37], [38]]. Stable and continuous growth signals from BM remain essential for the subsequent stage of new bone formation at the critical bone defect [30,39,40].

A novel strategy has been proposed to recapitulate the dynamic regenerative microenvironment throughout the entire bone regeneration process through the sequential delivery of growth factors using biomaterial carriers with precisely tuned degradation profiles [41,42]. For example, Shen et al. and Kim et al. utilized rapidly degrading biomaterials to deliver stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) and TGF-β during the early stage to recruit endogenous BMSCs, followed by the use of slowly degrading biomaterials to deliver BMP-2 to promote in-situ osteogenesis of BMSCs [43,44]. Wan et al. enhanced new bone formation by administering aspirin to mitigate inflammation during the early stage and BMP-2 in the late stage [45]. Dan et al. successfully coordinated stem cell homing and vascularization via sequential SDF-1 and VEGF delivery [46]. Notably, vascularization is critical throughout the entire bone regeneration process, particularly the regeneration of functional blood vessels, such as H-type blood vessels during the later remodeling phases [[47], [48], [49]]. These findings collectively highlight that the biomimicry of the BM niche and temporal regulation of growth factor signaling cascades are vital for critical-sized bone defect regeneration. Current research on biomimetic BM microenvironment primarily emphasizes HSC expansion and immunomodulation using biopolymer scaffolds, with applications in hematopoiesis modeling and drug screening [[50], [51], [52]]. However, few systems have been specifically engineered to replicate the osteogenic niche components essential for bone repair applications.

Therefore, we here proposed a self-assembled hybrid hydrogel microsphere system aimed at mimicking the regenerative features of the BM niche to enhance bone regeneration. Natural polymer-based hydrogels have been extensively employed in the fabrication of stimuli-responsive, crosslinked scaffolds for bone tissue engineering applications. These bioactive matrices, when combined with BMSCs, demonstrate significant regenerative potential due to their favorable biocompatibility and biomimetic properties [[53], [54], [55], [56], [57]]. Among them, hydrogel microspheres have emerged as a versatile platform for temporally controlled growth factor delivery, stem cell niche recapitulation, and engineering spatiotemporally heterogeneous biomimetic microenvironments, owing to their precisely tunable mechanical properties, excellent injectability, and remarkable capacity to mimic native cellular microenvironments [53,58]. In this study, two types of hydrogel microspheres with distinct degradation rates were designed: gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) microspheres (GM), characterized by rapid degradation, and chitosan methacryloyl (ChitoMA) microspheres (CM), exhibiting slower degradation. To mimic the BM niche, stem cell homing peptide SKP, osteogenic peptide OGP, and VEGF mimetic peptide KLT were selected as bioactive ingredients. It has been reported that SKP recruits stem cells through the Ras/ERK signaling pathway; OGP promotes stem cell proliferation and differentiation through multiple pathways including RhoA/ROCK, heme oxygenase (HO)-1/-2, and TGF-β; and KLT promotes angiogenesis by activating the VEGF pathway [[59], [60], [61], [62]]. The GM were loaded with photocrosslinkable SKP-MA and exogenous BMSCs to mimic the stem cell environment of BM, collectively referred to as GMSKP. The CM were loaded with the OGP-MA and KLT-MA to confer osteogenic and angiogenic activities, collectively referred to as CMOGP/KLT. The self-assembled hybrid hydrogel microspheres were injected into a 3D-printed Poly (D,L-lactic-co-glycolic acid)/hydroxyapatite (PLGA/HA) scaffold to establish a BM-mimicking niche within the tissue-engineered bone scaffold. Result

Preparation and characterization of the hydrogel microspheres and 3D-printed PLGA/HA bone scaffold

Two types of hydrogel microspheres, composed of bioactive peptide-functionalized GelMA and ChitoMA, respectively (Fig. 1A), were prepared to construct a gelatinous matrix mimicking BM. The Fourier transform infrared spectrum (FTIR) (Fig. 1B(I)) showed a shift in the absorption peak corresponding to C=N stretching vibration, indicating the successful grafting of MA groups onto gelatin and chitosan. Moreover, the results of the 1H NMR spectrum (Fig. S1A and S1B, Supporting Information) indicated the grafting rates of GelMA and ChitoMA were approximately 60 % and 20 %, respectively. Uniform spherical GelMA and ChitoMA hydrogel microspheres were fabricated using a T-junction microfluidic chip (Fig. S2, Fig. 1C). The average diameters of GM and CM were 179 ± 15 μm and 216 ± 23 μm, respectively. To verify the successful conjugation of peptides onto the GM and CM microspheres via photocrosslinking, green fluorescent dye FITC-labeled non-photocrosslinkable (FAM)SKP and photocrosslinkable (FAM)SKP-MA were respectively characterized (Fig. 1D). In the group containing (FAM)SKP, strong FITC fluorescence signal was detected at the microsphere periphery and in the surrounding solution, indicating rapid diffusion of (FAM)SKP peptides from the microspheres. In contrast, the group with (FAM)SKP-MA exhibited no peripheral FITC signal but exhibited strong green signals within the microspheres, confirming successful peptide conjugation with few unbound peptides. SEM images of freeze-dried GM and CM (Fig. 1E and F) revealed distinct granular structures on the surfaces of GMSKP and CMOGP/KLT, in contrast to the smoother surfaces of untreated GM and CM, indicating successful peptide grafting. Rheological analysis (Fig. S3A–C) demonstrated that the incorporation of photocrosslinkable peptides diminished the molecular network crosslinking of GelMA and ChitoMA backbones, resulting in lower storage and loss moduli compared to GM and CM. The reduced molecular network strength, coupled with the inherent hydrophilic nature of the peptides, resulted in greater water absorption in GMSKP and CMOGP/KLT compared to GM and CM, respectively (Fig. S4). The in vitro peptide release profiles and degradation behaviors of the microspheres were then evaluated. Fig. 1G revealed an initial burst release of peptide within 24 h for all groups, with GMSKP demonstrating substantially higher release quantities compared to CMOGP/KLT. By day 10, SKP peptides had reached a near-complete release (92 %), while OGP/KLT maintained a sustained release profile. These results demonstrate distinct release patterns: GMSKP exhibited rapid initial release followed by depletion, whereas CMOGP/KLT showed prolonged, controlled release kinetics. The degradation profiles of GM and CM showed significant differences. The GM and GMSKP degraded more rapidly under the influence of the collagenase than CM and CMOGP/KLT, with the introduction of peptides slightly accelerating microsphere degradation (Fig. 1H). Zeta potential measurements revealed that the GM and GMSKP were negatively charged, whereas CM and CMOGP/KLT were positively charged, facilitating their self-assembly via electrostatic interactions (Fig. S5). Moreover, the hybrid microspheres system GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT, created by combining GMSKP and CMOGP/KLT, exhibited shear-thinning properties, enabling successful extrusion from a syringe (Fig. 1I).

The PLGA/HA bone scaffold was fabricated via 3D printing (Fig. S6), with filaments oriented perpendicularly to create interconnecting pores that support the self-assembled hybrid microspheres (Fig. S7A and S7B). The average spacing between the extruded filaments was 370 ± 24 μm. After freeze-drying, solvent removal resulted in micropores with a diameter of 5 ± 4 μm within the filaments (Fig. S7C). Compression tests performed on a 1 cm3 supportive scaffold (Fig. 1J) showed a compressive strength of 4.96 ± 0.54 MPa and an elastic modulus of 0.72 ± 0.04 GPa, which were comparable to those of cancellous bone (Fig. S8 and 1K). In the subsequent experiments, the self-assembled hybrid microspheres composed of GMSKP and CMOGP/KLT were injected into the supportive scaffold to create a niche mimicking bone marrow, thereby constructing a bioactive composite scaffold (Fig. 1L).

In vitro biocompatibility of the bioactive hydrogel microspheres and PLGA/HA supportive scaffold

To investigate the biocompatibilities of the bioactive peptide-functionalized hydrogel microspheres and the 3D-printed supportive scaffold, BMSCs were seeded onto the surfaces of the microspheres and the 3D-printed PLGA/HA filaments (Fig. 2A). Results from Live/dead and cytoskeleton staining showed that BMSCs exhibited good adhesion, spreading, and viability across all sample groups, demonstrating their excellent biocompatibility. Notably, BMSCs cultured in the CM group exhibited limited spreading and adopted a more rounded morphology. Consistent with previous reports, BMSCs exhibited poor spreading on unmodified chitosan (Fig. 1A), likely attributable to the material's inherent lack of protein adhesion sites necessary for effective cell attachment and spreading [63], which was markedly ameliorated in the CMOGP/KLT group following OGP/KLT functionalization, with BMSCs demonstrating significantly improved spreading and morphological characteristics. Additionally, BMSCs-loaded GM and GMSKP were effectively prepared using a microfluidic chip (Fig. S10). BMSCs were uniformly dispersed in the GelMA solution at a concentration of 2.5 × 106/mL and subsequently encapsulated in microspheres through photo-crosslinking within the chip channel. Live/dead and cytoskeleton staining showed that BMSCs exhibited a high survival rate and were capable of proliferating and spreading within the microspheres. Notably, the presence of SKP significantly enhanced their spreading and proliferation (Fig. 2B and C). Moreover, unlike the surface-adhesive BMSCs that established 2D-like adhesions only with curvature changes, encapsulated BMSCs exhibited more extended spreading within the 3D microenvironment. In addition, the surface-adhesive BMSCs primarily engaged with surface-immobilized peptides, whereas 3D-encapsulated BMSCs within GMSKP experienced omnidirectional exposure to SKP peptides. These results collectively demonstrate that BMSC behavior is fundamentally influenced by both the spatial configuration (2D vs 3D) and the distribution profile of bioactive peptides in their microenvironment.

In vitro recruitment and stemness analysis of BMSCs in the self-assembled hybrid microspheres-based bone marrow-mimicking niche

In this study, GMSKP and CMOGP/KLT were blended at a ratio of 1:1 to construct a bone marrow-mimicking niche, hereafter referred to as the GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT group. The ratio between the two types of microspheres was dominantly determined considering their respective degradation rates. A 1:1 ratio of GM and CM was acceptable for ensuring the cell attachment and tissue invasion throughout the healing process. Fig. 3A illustrates the experimental setup designed to evaluate the bioactivities of the self-assembled hybrid microsphere-based BM-mimicking niche. Additionally, the GM/CM, GMSKP/CM, and GM/CMOGP/KLT were evaluated as controls. The recruitment and stemness maintenance of BMSCs were assessed in vitro. Initially, the Transwell migration experiment and scratch assay were conducted in groups without BMSCs seeding. Qualitative and quantitative analyses from both experiments demonstrated that SKP and OGP/KLT significantly enhanced the migration of BMSCs across the Transwell membrane and increased the migration rate in scratch assays (Fig. 3B–E). Notably, SKP exhibited a more pronounced effect compared to OGP/KLT. To simulate the recruitment of endogenous BMSCs and the transplantation of exogenous BMSCs, BMSCs were either seeded on the surface of microspheres or encapsulated within them, and subsequently evaluated. SRY-box transcription factor 2 (Sox2), Krüppel-like factor 2 (Klf2), and Nanog homeobox (Nanog) represent canonical markers for maintaining mesenchymal stem cells stemness [[64], [65], [66]]. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of surface-adherent BMSCs demonstrated that both GMSKP/CM and GM/CMOGP/KLT treatments enhanced BMSC stemness, with the GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT combination exhibiting the most substantial effect (Fig. 3F). Moreover, the stemness of BMSCs influenced the secretion levels of various growth factors and cytokines. We quantitatively analyzed the levels of VEGF and IL-10 in the supernatants from different groups. ELISA results demonstrated significantly elevated levels of VEGF and IL-10 in the GMSKP/CM, GM/GMOGP/KLT, and GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT groups compared to the CM/GM group (Fig. 3G), indicating that the incorporated peptides effectively potentiated the paracrine secretory activity of BMSCs. Notably, encapsulated BMSCs showed similar but more pronounced responses. The bioactive peptide-modified microspheres induced significantly higher expression levels of Sox2, Klf2, and Nanog in encapsulated BMSCs compared to their surface-adherent counterparts (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, ELISA results confirmed that GMSKP/CM, GM/CMOGP/KLT and GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT significantly enhanced the secretion of VEGF and IL-10 by BMSCs encapsulated within microspheres (Fig. 3I). Notably, GMSKP microspheres exhibited a stronger ability to promote BMSC stemness compared to CMOGP/KLT. These results demonstrate that the BM-mimicking niche may effectively recruit endogenous BMSCs and preserve their functionality (stemness), while also maintaining the stemness of the implanted encapsulated exogenous BMSCs, thereby supporting the regulation of regenerative microenvironment during the early stages of tissue repair.

In vitro analysis of vascularization in the self-assembled hybrid microspheres-based bone marrow-mimicking niche

Proliferation, migration, and differentiation of vascular endothelial cells are crucial for successful construction of vascularization [67]. The in vitro angiogenesis assays including the CCK8 proliferation assay, migration assay, and tube-like structure formation assay of HUVECs under the influence of bioactive peptides were assessed. In the groups without BMSCs seeding, the results demonstrated significantly faster cell proliferation in the GM/CMOGP/KLT and GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT (Fig. S11). Moreover, qualitative and quantitative analyses of Transwell migration experiments and scratch assays revealed that after 24 h of co-culture, HUVECs exhibited significantly enhanced migration in GM/CMOGP/KLT and GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT compared to GM/CM and GMSKP/CM (Fig. 4A–D). Notably, SKP did not significantly affect HUVEC proliferation and migration, suggesting that KLT was primarily responsible for the observed effect. Furthermore, the intrinsic ability of HUVECs to form capillary/tube-like network structures is considered as a marker of endothelial cell morphological differentiation [68]. Initially, we evaluated the impact of bioactive peptides on tube-like structure formation of HUVECs in vitro, and the findings were consistent with those of the migration experiment. After 6 h of co-culture, HUVECs demonstrated enhanced tube formation abilities in the group of GM/CMOGP/KLT and GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT (Fig. 4E and F). Subsequently, to evaluate the effect of BMSCs secretion on HUVECs from various groups with surface-adhered BMSCs, we applied the supernatants as conditioned medium to culture HUVECs. The tube formation ability of HUVECs increased across the groups: GM/CM, GMSKP/CM, GM/GMOGP/KLT to GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT (Fig. 4G and H). This phenomenon was attributed to the synergistic effects of growth factors present in the supernatants and the bioactive peptide KLT. The qRT-PCR results showed that, compared to HUVCEs treated with standard culture medium, the genes associated with vascularization, including VEGF, PDGF, fibroblast growth factors (FGF), platelet and endocrine cell adhesion molecule 1 (CD31), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and enzyme endocrine oxide synthesis (eNOS), were all up-regulated in the groups exposed to supernatant. The GMSKP/GMOGP/KLT group exhibited the highest gene expression levels (Fig. 4I). Similarly, the use of encapsulated BMSCs supernatants in tube-like structure formation assay and qRT-PCR test on HUVECs indicated a significant enhancement in the tube-like structure formation and up-regulated the vascularization-related genes in HUVECs (Fig. 4J-L). Besides the paracrine effects of stem cells, the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs further facilitates in-situ bone regeneration. ALP staining and activity assays were performed on 2D cultures of BMSCs over 7 and 14 days (Fig. 6A and C). At day 7, no significant difference was observed between GM/CM and GMSKP/CM, which may be attributed to the role of SKP in regulating stemness and promoting proliferation in the early stage. Both groups containing CMOGP/KLT showed enhanced ALP expression. By day 14, GMSKP/CM demonstrated greater ALP expression compared to GM/CM, whereas GMSKP/CMOGP/KLT displayed the highest ALP activity. Discussion

BM aspirate modulates the local microenvironment by releasing growth factors and BMSCs during bone repair. As the BM matrix degrades, BMSCs interact with surrounding tissues and matrices, gradually differentiating into osteoblasts to enhance bone regeneration. However, its reparative effect remains limited in critical-size bone defects, even when combined with mechanical support such as autogenous bone or DBM [32,33]. Two primary reasons contribute to this limitation: (1) BMSCs in BM aspirate are non-amplified, with a content of only 0.001 %–0.02 %, making it challenging to support critical bone defect regeneration; (2) the rapid degradation of the BM matrix results in a swift release of growth factors that do not align with the bone regeneration timeline. Moreover, although attempts have been made to amplify BMSCs in vitro, the therapeutic effect remains limited because BMSCs are isolated from the BM niche, losing essential support for bone regeneration [[36], [37], [38]]. This highlights the remarkable regenerative potential of natural BM and underscores the importance of constructing an optimized BM-mimicking niche as a key research direction for addressing critical bone defects.

In this study, we developed self-assembled hybrid microspheres designed to mimic the natural BM niche and address its inherent limitations. These self-assembled hybrid hydrogel microspheres replicated the gel-like characteristics of natural BM. The two types of microspheres exhibited distinct degradation rates. SKP released by GMSKP recruited endogenous BMSCs in the early stage, enhancing the stemness of both endogenous and exogenous BMSCs with more secretion of beneficial growth factors, thereby enhancing the regenerative microenvironment in the early stage and addressing the limitations associated with non-amplified BMSCs. Moreover, after rapid degradation, GMSKP facilitated the interaction of BMSCs with the surrounding tissue and matrix, initiating in situ osteogenesis akin to that of natural BM. Meanwhile, CMOGP/KLT supported sustained vascular formation and functional maturation, promoting osteogenic differentiation in situ due to its slower degradation, which aligns with the dynamic bone regeneration process. Our BM-mimicking niche demonstrated enhanced bone regenerative capacity, effectively validating its functionality.

To improve the survival rate of MSCs within tissue-engineered scaffolds [70], encapsulating them within hydrogel has been proposed as a strategy to ensure their prolonged viability [[71], [72], [73]]. Moreover, beyond survival and proliferation, MSCs in bone repair must preserve their stemness to secrete essential growth factors that modulate the early regenerative microenvironment [12,74], similar to the role of the BM niche in treating bone defects. To mimic the BM niche, we selected hydrogel microspheres over bulk hydrogel as cell carriers due to their inherent porosity, which facilitates cell infiltration, promotes angiogenesis, and provides additional adhesion sites [[75], [76], [77]]. Additionally, GelMA microspheres have been proven to preserve the stemness of stem cells [78]. In this study, we therefore employed GMSKP hydrogel microspheres as carriers for exogenous BMSCs. The SKP peptide further enhanced the stem cell niche [79] by upregulating the expression of BMSC stemness-related genes and promoting the secretion of growth factors and cytokines such as VEGF and IL-10. Additionally, SKP facilitated the recruitment of endogenous BMSCs, thereby creating a niche enriched with BMSCs. Unlike other strategies that rely solely on either exogenous or endogenous stem cells [80,81], our approach underscores the value of combining both, effectively increasing stem cell abundance and accelerating regeneration. Notably, GMSKP degraded rapidly after modulating the early inflammatory microenvironment, enabling BMSCs to interact with the surrounding tissues and matrix to initiate in situ osteogenesis. Furthermore, bone regeneration is a dynamic process involving blood vessel maturation, osteogenic differentiation, and remodeling, following inflammatory cell infiltration and stem cell recruitment [82]. To emulate this dynamic process, the sequential release of bioactive ingredients within the BM-mimicking niche is crucial for effective bone regeneration [42]. Compared to other sustained-release systems, this system enables peptides to interact directly with in situ cells [59]. This direct interaction prevents issues such as uneven release, rapid diffusion, or premature depletion of active ingredients [59], emphasizing the significance of a well-optimized BM-mimicking niche for bone regeneration.

Despite the notable therapeutic efficacy of the self-assembled hybrid microspheres designed to mimic bone marrow niche for critical bone defect regeneration in rats, there remain differences in composition and function compared to natural bone marrow. The natural BM niche is a complex microenvironment, our BM-mimicking niche lacks HSCs and other related growth factors. Future investigations should explore the incorporation of HSCs to augment paracrine-mediated osteogenesis, and identify appropriate carriers to more accurately mimic the BM niche [12,31]. Beyond BMSCs and HSCs, other progenitor populations, including periosteum-derived stem cells (PSCs) and skeletal stem cells (SSCs), also contribute significantly to bone regeneration through complex cellular crosstalk. A critical research direction will be developing engineered niche systems capable of integrating these multiple stem cell populations and coordinating their synergistic interactions [83,84]. Furthermore, the functional peptides OGP, KLT, and SKP demonstrated excellent osteogenic, angiogenic, and stem cell recruitment/regulatory activities in this study. However, their optimal concentrations have not been systematically evaluated. The peptide concentrations used in this work were selected based on previous experimental evidence. Further investigation is warranted to determine the ideal peptide concentrations and their synergistic effects for maximal therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the function of the biomimetic bone marrow, thereby we employed a simplified 3D-printed PLGA/HA supportive scaffold with intentionally delayed degradation kinetics, thus minimizing confounding effects from scaffold degradation during niche efficacy assessment. To achieve better repair outcomes, the 3D-printed scaffold requires further optimization. For instance, the results from the MSPC group revealed that the 3D-printed supportive scaffold retained most of its volume after 12 weeks. Although the pores were nearly filled with newly formed bone and blood vessels, the residual scaffold materials may hinder bone remodeling, lead to mechanical mismatches, and induce chronic inflammation. Therefore, the degradation rate of the 3D-printed bone scaffold could be further optimized by adjusting the composition and porosity of PLGA/HA filaments. For instance, increasing porosity or replacing HA with mineralized collagen could enhance osteogenic activity while simultaneously improving the synchronization between scaffold degradation and bone regeneration [85,86]. An additional study limitation concerns the incomplete elucidation of specific signaling mechanisms through which these peptides modulate stemness maintenance and osteogenic differentiation. While our data demonstrate the functional outcomes of peptide treatment, detailed mechanistic investigations of the underlying pathways warrant dedicated future research.

The integration of artificial bone materials with the BM-mimicking niche presents significant advantages for clinical translation in bone tissue engineering. Unlike conventional bone grafts, this composite system provides a bioactive and mechanically stable construction that enhances osteogenesis, vascularization, and integration in compromised healing environments, offering a clinically viable and customizable regenerative solution for large-segment and non-union bone defects repair. Large bone defects often suffer from insufficient vascular supply and poor endogenous cell recruitment, leading to nonunion or delayed healing. BMSC-laden microspheres address this by secreting osteogenic and immunomodulatory cytokines, which promote endogenous repair mechanisms. The sustained release of bioactive factors from degradable microspheres creates a regenerative niche, stimulating both osteoblast differentiation and neovascularization, especially enhancing graft survival in poorly vascularized defect sites.

Conclusion

In summary, two hydrogel microspheres, BMSCs-encapsulated GMSKP and CMOGP/KLT were successfully fabricated and self-assembled into a hybrid system via electrostatic interaction to mimic natural bone marrow niche for promoting bone regeneration. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that this system efficiently regulated the dynamic processes of bone regeneration, including in-situ stem cell recruitment (both endogenous and exogenous), stemness enhancement, osteogenic differentiation, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenesis, thereby establishing an in-situ ossification center. This strategy will offer new insights into bone tissue engineering and stem cell therapy approaches.

Materials and methods

Preparation of hydrogel microspheres and 3D-printed supportive scaffold

Preparation of GelMA and ChitoMA

The synthesis procedure followed the method described in reference [87]. Briefly, for GelMA synthesis, 10 g of type A gelatin (300 g Bloom, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved into 100 mL of deionized water at 50 °C. Subsequently, 8 mL of methacrylic anhydride (MA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added dropwise to the gelatin solution being stirred. After reacting at 50 °C for 3 h, a 5-fold volume of deionized water was added to stop the reaction. The solution was dialyzed using a 12–14 kDa cut-off dialysis membrane (Biosharp, China) for one week to remove unreacted MA. The final solution was lyophilized and then stored at −20 °C for future use.

For ChitoMA synthesis, 3 g of deacetylated chitosan (100–300 kDa, J&K Scientific, China) was dissolved into 100 mL of deionized water containing 1 M acetic acid solution and stirred overnight at room temperature. Subsequently, 9 mL of MA was added dropwise to the chitosan solution being stirred. The procedure for stopping the reaction, dialysis, and lyophilization were as previously described.

Preparation of bioactive peptide- and BMSCs-loaded microspheres

The sterilized powders of KLT (GGGKLTWQELYQLKYKGIGG), KLT-MA (MA-GGGKLTWQELYQLKYKGIGG), SKP (GGGSKPPGTSS), (5-FAM) SKP (GGGSK(5-FAM)PPGTSS), SKP-MA (MA-GGGSKPPGTSS), (5-FAM) SKP-MA (MA-GGGSK(5-FAM)PPGTSS), OGP (GGGALKRQGRTLYGFGG), and OGP-MA (MA-GGGALKRQGRTLYGFGG) were all custom-synthesized by BioAct Peptide Biotechnology LLC. with the purity >95 %. According to the previous researches, each peptide concentration was set to be 500 μg/mL [[88], [89], [90], [91], [92], [93]].

For GelMA microspheres fabrication, the mineral oil (Biosharp, China) with 2 % Span 80 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was injected into a T-junction microfluidic device at a flow rate of 2.2 mL/h. The device was wrapped in tin foil, leaving an uncovered crosslink area of approximately 1 cm at the outlet's end. A 10 wt% GelMA solution containing either 5 × 106/mL BMSCs (2.5 × 106/mL BMSCs for cell biocompatibility analysis), peptide (SKP/SKP-MA: 500 μg/mL) or no BMSCs/peptides, with 0.25 % lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, Yinchang Xincai, China), was sheared into uniform droplets and subjected to UV crosslinking (405 nm, 5 mW/cm2) within the device and the outlet pipe (Tygon, USA) for 2 s. GelMA microspheres were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 2 min to remove the oil layer and washed with DPBS (Biosharp, China) for three times. The obtained GelMA microspheres without BMSCs were lyophilized and then stored at −20 °C for future use. The obtained GelMA microspheres with BMSCs were used immediately after fabrication. To fabricate BMSCs-loaded GelMA microspheres, GelMA was cut into pieces for sterilization in the biosafety cabinet for 1 h UV light exposure and then dissolved in sterilized DPBS containing 0.25 wt% LAP and 500 μg/mL SKP-MA. Mineral oil containing 2 % Span 80 (Biofil, China) was sterilized using a filter flask.

For ChitoMA microspheres fabrication, the mineral oil containing 2 % Span 80 was injected into the T-junction microfluidic device by a gas pump (Wuhan Jianmi Zhikong TechnologyCo., Ltd) at 200 Pa. A 1.5 wt% ChitoMA solution, with or without peptide (OGP/OGP-MA: 500 μg/mL, KLT/KLT-MA: 500 μg/mL) and containing 0.25 wt% LAP, was injected into the device using the gas pump at 150 Pa. At the merging point of the two fluids, the mixture was sheared into uniform droplets and UV-crosslinked (405 nm, 5 mW/cm2) within an outlet pipe placed on a mirror for 30 s. The ChitoMA microspheres were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 2 min to remove the oil layer, followed by three times of washes with isopropanol (Macklin, China) and DPBS, respectively. The resulting ChitoMA microspheres were lyophilized and then stored at −20 °C for use.

Preparation of cryogenic 3D-printed scaffold

The 3D-printed scaffold was fabricated as described previously [94,95]. Briefly, 3 g of Poly (D, L-lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA, 0.8 dl/g) (Jinan Daigang Biotechnology Limited, China) was dissolved in 10 mL dichloromethane (DCM, Macklin, China). Subsequently, 2.5 mL of deionized water containing 1.5 wt% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 3 g of hydroxyapatite (EKEAR, China) were added to the solution. After vortexing for 3 min, a uniform white emulsion was obtained.

The emulsion was transferred into a 20 mL syringe with a V-shape nozzle (inner diameter: 0.6 mm) for cryogenic 3D printing (Bioscaffold GiSim, Germany), following a pre-designed cube CAD model to print the scaffold at −10 °C. The printing process was conducted at a speed of 6 mm/s, with a layer thickness of 0.35 mm, and an extrusion pressure of 100 kPa. The printed scaffolds were lyophilized to remove the residual DCM and water.

Characterization of bone marrow-mimicking scaffold

Grafting rate

GelMA and ChitoMA were characterized by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) to determine the degree of methylation. The gelatin and GelMA were dissolved in deuterium oxide (D2O, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), while chitosan and ChitoMA were dissovled in the deuterated acetic acid (CD3CO2D, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The 1H NMR spectra were obtained by the 600M NMR spectrometer (JNM-ECA600, JEOL Ltd., Japan). The peptides and freeze-dried hydrogels were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, TG-MS-FTIR-X70, NETZSCH Groups, Germany) with wavenumbers from 4000 to 400 cm−1 to determine the successful grafting of MA.

Morphological characterization

The general morphology of the 3D-printed scaffold and different microspheres was observed using an optical microscope (Leica, Germany). For SEM characterization, all samples were coated with a 10 nm platinum film prior to examination using a field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, CarlZeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM 900, Zeiss, Germany) was used to characterize the microspheres loaded with grafted fluorescent (5-FAM)SKP or (5-FAM)SKP-MA. The diameters of different microspheres, as well as the void and pore sizes of the 3D-printed scaffold, were analyzed using ImageJ 1.53k (Wayne Rasbamd, NIH, USA).

Mechanical properties characterization

The rheological properties of various microspheres and the 3D printing ink were characterized using a rheometer (MCR301, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria) equipped with a parallel plate of 25 mm in diameter. The viscosity was measured at 37 °C over a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were measured using a dynamic frequency sweep test conducted at frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 Hz at a strain of 1 %.

Three 1 cm3 3D-printed scaffolds were prepared for compressive testing. A universal mechanical testing machine (SHIMADZU AG-IC, Japan) was utilized to measure the compressive strength and elastic modulus of the 3D-printed scaffolds under a 2000 N load. The load was released once the scaffolds were compressed to approximately 30 % of their original dimensions. The elastic modulus was calculated from the slope of the stress-strain curve, while the compressive strength was determined at the intersection point corresponding to 20 % strain.

Zeta potential measurement

Prepolymer solutions were prepared by dissolving GelMA, GelMA-SKP, ChitoMA, and ChitoMA-OGP/KLT in deionized water (PH = 7.4), respectively. The dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Nanosizer instrument (Wyatt, USA) to determine the zeta potential of the prepolymer solutions.

Swelling ratio measurement

Various groups of freeze-dried microspheres were initially weighed and recorded as W0. Then all microspheres were immersed in the deionized water overnight to achieve full swelling. After centrifuging and discarding the supernatant, the fully swollen microspheres were weighed and recorded as W1. The swelling ratio (SR) was calculated using the following formula:SR=W1−W0W0

Peptide releasing profiles from the microspheres

For peptide release analysis, 50 mg GMSKP or CMOGP/KLT were incubated in 2 mL PBS under constant agitation at 37 °C. Supernatant samples were collected at different time points (12 h, 1, 4, 7, and 10 days). 200 μL of supernatant was withdrawn and mixed with an equal volume of fresh PBS. Peptide concentration in the supernatants was quantified spectrophotometrically at 205 nm using a Nanodrop system (Thermo Scientific, USA), with reference to predetermined standard curves.

Degradation behaviors of the microspheres

Various groups of freeze-dried microspheres were initially weighed and recorded as W0. Subsequently, all microsphere samples were immersed in PBS with 0.5U collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated in a 37 °C water bath shaker for 5 days. To measure the dry weight of the samples, they were first centrifuged to remove the supernatant. Then, the samples were freeze-dried to obtain microsphere powder. The dry weight of the samples was marked at the specific time points (Wn, n = 1, 3, 5). The degradation ratio (DR) was calculated using the following formula:DR=1−W0−WnW0×100%

For degraded BMSCs-loaded microspheres, the images were taken at different time points (7, 14, and 21 days) under the optical microscope.

Characterizations of in vitro cell activities

Biocompatibility of the microspheres and 3D-printed scaffold

Rat bone marrow-derived mesenchymal cells (BMSCs, OriCellbio, China) were cultured in the complete culture medium (OriCellbio, China) at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. BMSCs were used in passage 4 for all experiments. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, Procell, China) were cultured in the HUVECs complete culture medium (Procell, China) at 37 °C with 5 % CO2.

The biocompatibility of microspheres (GM, GMSKP, CM, CMOGP/KLT) and 3D-printed scaffold were characterized by cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay, Live/Dead staining assay, and cell skeleton staining assay. For the CCK8 experiments, BMSCs or HUVECs were seeded on a 96-well plate overnight before adding various microspheres. In the Live/Dead and cell skeleton staining assays, BMSCs were seeded on the surfaces of various microspheres, the 3D-printed scaffold, or encapsulated within the GM or GMSKP. In the cell viability assay, 1 × 104 BMSCs were co-cultured with 100 μL of different microspheres in the medium and 4 mm3 scaffold, respectively in a 96-well plate. After 24 h, all materials in the well were removed, and the CCK8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) was carried out using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer Enspire, Singapore) at a wavelength of 450 nm. In the cell proliferation assay, 2 × 103 BMSCs or HUVECs were co-cultured with 100 μL of different groups of microspheres in the medium in a 96-well plate and then analyzed at multiple time points (1, 3, and 5 days) using the CCK8 kit. The Live/Dead staining kit (Beyotime, China) and cell skeleton staining kit (Solarbio, China) were used for staining after a 3-day co-culture of 1 × 104 BMSCs with 100 μL different groups of microspheres (surface-adhesive BMSCs) or a 3D-printed scaffold in medium. Additionally, these kits were used for 0-, 3-, 7-, and 14-day cultures of BMSCs-loaded microspheres (encapsulated BMSCs) in a 96-well plate. The results were further imaged using CLSM. Image J 1.53k software was used to quantify the day-0 cell viability in the encapsulated BMSCs fabrication process by calculating the ratio of the live to dead cells.

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

To detect the ROS generated during the fabrication of encapsulated microspheres, the mineral oil was collected from the microsphere fabrication process at two stages: the chip crosslinking stage (∼2 s) and the tube crosslinking stage (∼15 s). A 1,3-Diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) solution was mixed with the collected mineral oils and analyzed using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 420 nm.

Cell migration assay

Transwell-based crystal violet and wound healing assays were employed to assess the migration ability of BMSCs and HUVECs. In the transwell-based crystal violet assay, 1 × 104 BMSCs or HUVECs were seeded on a 24-well transwell insert with 8 μm pores (Kirgen, China). Subsequently, 200 μL of microspheres (GM or GMSKP: CM or GMSKP: CMOGP/KLT, 1:1) were added to the bottom of the 24-well plate. After 24 h of culture, cells were fixed with 4 % glutaraldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 30 min, and then the upper layer of cells on the transwell was gently removed using a cotton swab. Following staining with crystal violet (Beyotime, China) solution for 20 min and 3 times of PBS washing, the results were visualized using an optical microscope. In the wound healing assay, a scratch was created using a 200 μL pipette tip on a 24-well plate after BMSCs or HUVECs reached confluence. After treatment with 1 μg/mL mitomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 h to prevent cell proliferation, 200 μL of various microspheres in medium was added. After removing the microspheres, the images were captured at specific time points (0 and 24 h) using an optical microscope. ImageJ 1.53k software was used to measure the migration distance.

Tube-like structure formation assay

Matrigel (Corning BD Biocoat, USA) was utilized for conducting a tube-like structure formation assay on a 96-well plate. After seeding 1 × 104 HUVECs onto the Matrigel, the cells were cultured using a conditioned medium mixed with HUVECs medium at a 1:1 vol ratio. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 3 min, the conditioned medium was collected from a 3-day culture of various pure microspheres, or microspheres with surface-adhesive or encapsulated BMSCs. The images were captured at 6 h using an optical microscope. The quantification of the mean number of branches per field was performed according to the method described in reference [96].

ELISA assay

The secretion levels of VEGF and IL-10 in the 3-day culture medium of BMSCs from various microspheres were evaluated using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent (ELISA) kit (MMBIO, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance values were measured using a microplate reader.

Flow cytometry analysis

RAW 264.7 cells (Procell, China) were cultured in DMEM high glucose complete medium (Procell, China) at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. For flow cytometry analysis, RAW 264.7 cells were cultured for two days with 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) or LPS combined with various conditioned medium from a 3-day culture of BMSCs-containing microspheres (mixed with DMEM high glucose at a volume ratio of 1:1) in a 24-well plate. Subsequently, the cells were isolated using trypsin (Procell, China) and washed three times with a blocking solution (PBS containing 2 % FBS). The isolated cells were stained for 1 h in the blocking solution with FITC-conjugated F4/80 (1:500, BD Biosciences, USA), PE-conjugated CD206 (1:200, BD Biosciences, USA), and PE-CF594-conjugated CD86 (1:500, BD Biosciences, USA). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescent staining

The RAW 264.7 cells were utilized after polarization following the same process as described in the flow cytometry section. Following polarization, the RAW 264.7 cells were fixed with 4 % glutaraldehyde for 30 min. After treatment with 0.1 % v/v% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) and 10 % v/v% goat serum (Solarbio, China) in PBS, the primary antibodies iNOS (M1 marker, 1:100, Proteintech, USA) and CD206 (M2 marker, 1:200, Abcam, USA), were applied overnight at 4 °C, respectively. After 3 times of PBS washes, the secondary antibodies, Alexa Flour 488 conjugate anti-mouse and Alexa Flour 594 conjugate anti-rabbit (1:1000, Thermofisher, USA), were applied for 30 min followed by DAPI staining (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and three additional PBS washes. The results were visualized using CLSM. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

Osteogenesis assay

For osteogenesis assay, 1 × 105 BMSCs were seeded with 200 μL of various microspheres in the medium within a 24-well plate. After 3 days of culture, the osteoblast differentiation medium (Oricellbio, China) was applied, with half of the medium being replaced every 3 days. The alkaline phosphate (ALP) activity tests and ALP staining were conducted at days 7 and 14. The culture medium was utilized to assess the ALP activity following the instructions provided by the ALP activity test kit (Njjcbio, China). For ALP staining, BMSCs cultured on both the microspheres and in the well were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by 3 times of PBS washes. Then, the ALP staining dye (Puhe Biotech, China) was applied for 12 h. The staining and quantitative analysis using alizarin red S (ARS, Oricellbio, China) were conducted at 21- and 28-days post-fixation of BMSCs in 4 % paraformaldehyde. The results of ALP and ARS staining were imaged using an optical microscope. For the quantitative analysis of ARS staining, cetylpyridinium chloride (Solarbio, China) was dissolved in deionized water at a concentration of 10 % w/v% and subsequently added to the ARS-stained well. The absorbance was measured at 562 nm using the microplate reader.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay

qRT-PCR was conducted to analyze the gene expression levels related to BMSCs stemness, HUVECs angiogenesis, RAW 264.7 polarization, and BMSCs osteogenesis. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from different types of cells in the well plate or within the microspheres using a Total mRNA Extraction kit (Tiangen, China). A FastQuant RT kit (Tiangen, China) was used to reverse transcribe to cDNA. cDNA was then mixed with the SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA), followed by a measurement of CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). The gene expression was finally normalized by GAPDH and the 2-△△Ct method was used for data processing. The primer sequences of targeted genes are shown in Table S1.

Characterization of in vivo cell recruitment, anti-inflammatory, osteogenesis, and angiogenesis assay

Surgical procedures

All animal experiment procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tsinghua University (animal protocol number: 24-WXM1). Totally 40 SD rats (male, 8 weeks old, weight: 200–220 g) were randomly divided into 4 experimental groups with 10 rats each group. The four groups included: (1) the 3D-printed scaffold group (Control), (2) the scaffolds loading with microspheres (GM: CM = 1:1) devoid of peptide (MS), (3) the scaffolds with microspheres containing peptides (GMSKP: CMOGP/KLT = 1:1) (MSP), and (4) the scaffolds with microspheres containing both peptides and exogenous BMSCs (MSPC). Following intraperitoneal injection of zoletil combined with xylazine (0.1–0.12 mL/100 g) for anesthesia, a bone defect measuring 3 mm × 3mm × 4 mm was created at the distal femur using an electrical drill. Subsequently, various groups of materials, each with a scaffold size of 3 mm × 3 mm × 4 mm, were implanted into the bone defect. Finally, the muscles and skin were sutured closed. Five rats per group were euthanized at 1 and 12 weeks for sample collection to evaluate early inflammatory responses and long-term bone regeneration, respectively.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

The rats were anesthetized following the same procedure used during surgery. The femur locations were conducted in vivo imaging and 3D reconstruction using micro-CT (Skyscan 1276, Bruker, Germany) at 6 weeks. At the 12-week endpoint, the rat femora were harvested and subjected to micro-CT imaging with subsequent quantitative analysis, including bone tissue volume/total tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation/spacing (Tb.Sp).

Bone histological analysis

Femur specimens were collected post-surgery at week 1 and week 12, then subjected to decalcification, dehydration, and embedding in paraffin. Histological analyses included immunohistochemical staining (CD 90, iNOS, CD 206, and CD 31), as well as hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining, all observed using a Pannoramic SCAN scanner (3DHIESTECH, Hungary). For immunofluorescent staining of CD 31 and Emcn, 5 μm-thick sections were prepared. Briefly, after dewaxing and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate solution (Seville) in a microwave oven. The samples were then soaked in 0.3 % Triton X-100 and 10 % normal goat serum for permeabilization and blocking. Following the removal of the blocking solution, primary antibodies against CD31 (1:200, Abcam) and Emcn (1:200, Abcam) were added and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, the samples were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody at a dilution ratio of 1:1000. Finally, the slides were treated with DAPI mounting medium (Abcam, UK).

Systemic toxicity analysis

Following harvest at 12 weeks post-implantation, the liver/kidney/spleen were decalcified, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for H&E staining to evaluate systemic toxicity.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (Dotamatics, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups, while t-tests were utilized for comparisons between two independent groups. A p-value greater than 0.05 was considered not significant (ns), whereas a p-value less than 0.05 was deemed significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
