Fecal microbiota transplantation in ulcerative colitis: evidence, mechanisms, and practice considerations
Authors: Jinghong Liu, Xiaojuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease strongly associated with intestinal dysbiosis, reduced microbial diversity, and disrupted microbial metabolite profiles. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) aims to restore microbial homeostasis and has shown a signal of benefit for induction of remission in some trials, but results are heterogeneous and long-term maintenance efficacy remains uncertain. In this narrative review, we synthesize randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews/meta-analyses, and recent guideline and regulatory updates on FMT in UC, and integrate mechanistic insights from microbiome and metabolomics research. Across RCTs, intensive lower-gastrointestinal regimens using pooled, multidonor material, and/or anaerobic processing have most consistently achieved modestly higher steroid-free clinical and endoscopic remission than placebo in mild-to-moderate UC (approximately 25%–32% vs 5%–10% in representative studies), whereas upper-gastrointestinal delivery or oral lyophilized formulations and highly restrictive donor selection have yielded mixed or negative results. Mechanistically, responders commonly demonstrate engraftment of short-chain fatty acid producing taxa and restoration of secondary bile acid pathways. Safety profiles in trials are generally comparable to placebo for common mild adverse events, but rare severe transmissions (e.g., multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli and SARS-CoV-2) have driven stricter donor screening and have limited routine use outside regulated programs. Current guidelines recommend against FMT for UC outside clinical trials. Future work should prioritize standardized protocols, biomarker-guided personalization, combination strategies (diet/priming), and development of defined microbial therapeutics to improve efficacy and safety.Keywords: bile acids, fecal microbiota transplantation, guidelines, gut microbiome, randomized trials, safety, short-chain fatty acids, ulcerative colitis

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) arises from a complex interplay of host genetics, immune dysregulation, environmental exposures, and the gut microbiome. Compared with healthy controls, patients with active UC often exhibit reduced alpha-diversity, depletion of obligate anaerobes, expansion of pathobionts, and altered microbial metabolite production, including reduced short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and disturbed bile acid pools. These observations provide biologic plausibility for microbiome-targeted interventions.1–4

FMT transfers a complex community of microorganisms from a screened donor to a recipient with the goal of restoring microbial structure and function. In recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI), FMT and microbiota-based products have demonstrated robust efficacy; however, UC is mechanistically and clinically distinct from rCDI, and treatment effects are more modest, protocol-dependent, and uncertain in durability. This review focuses on (i) mechanistic rationale; (ii) clinical evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses; (iii) practical and safety considerations; and (iv) guideline and regulatory context for translating evidence into practice. 5

Methods: Evidence identification and synthesis approach

This is a narrative review informed by a structured evidence identification process. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), supplemented by ClinicalTrials.gov and major guideline/regulatory websites, from January 1, 2000 to December 1, 2025 (last searched December 1, 2025). Search concepts included “ulcerative colitis,” “fecal microbiota transplantation”/“fecal microbiota transplant,” “microbiome,” and terms related to randomized trials and clinical outcomes, with database-specific controlled vocabulary where applicable. We prioritized: (i) randomized, placebo-, or autologous-controlled trials of FMT in UC; (ii) systematic reviews/meta-analyses of RCTs; and (iii) major society guidelines or regulator safety communications. Mechanistic studies were included when they linked microbial or metabolomic changes to clinical outcomes or immune pathways. We excluded single-patient case reports and reports without interpretable clinical outcomes. Evidence was synthesized narratively, emphasizing protocol-level patterns that were reproducible across independent datasets, and we explicitly noted heterogeneity and certainty limitations where relevant.

Mechanistic rationale: Microbial, metabolic, and immune pathways

Across multiomics studies, UC response to FMT has been associated with (a) engraftment of obligate anaerobes and increased microbial diversity and (b) restoration of microbial metabolic functions.6–8

Microbial dynamics

Responders commonly show increased relative abundance of SCFA-producing taxa such as Eubacterium and Roseburia and decreased pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria. Engraftment appears more reliable with colonic delivery, repeated dosing, and anaerobic processing, consistent with improved survival of oxygen-sensitive organisms.6,8,9

Metabolic reprogramming

Increased butyrate and other SCFAs can enhance epithelial barrier integrity, support mucosal healing, and promote regulatory immune programs. Restoration of secondary bile acids may influence inflammation through nuclear and membrane bile acid receptors, with downstream effects on epithelial function and innate/adaptive immune signaling.6–8

Immune modulation

Proposed pathways include shifts toward more regulatory immune phenotypes, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine tone, and improved barrier function that limits antigenic stimulation. These mechanisms are biologically plausible but largely correlative in humans; therefore, causal claims should be made cautiously and ideally supported by interventional or longitudinal data linking specific taxa/metabolites to immune readouts and clinical endpoints.6–8

Clinical evidence in UC

Randomized controlled trials

By contrast, trials using upper-GI delivery with few doses, or oral lyophilized formulations, have reported neutral results.2,10 In the LOTUS trial (lyophilized oral induction regimen with stringent donor selection and antibiotic priming), the combination of lyophilization, oral delivery, and an induction-only schedule may have constrained the effective dose and colonic engraftment, contributing to the neutral outcome. 10 Potential explanations include reduced viable anaerobe delivery, lower colonic engraftment, differences in adjunctive antibiotic priming, early termination/limited power, and high placebo response in some settings. These hypotheses remain to be tested directly.1–4,10,11

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

Meta-analyses focusing on double-blind RCTs generally find that FMT increases the odds of combined clinical and endoscopic remission compared with placebo in active UC, without a clear increase in serious adverse events. In these analyses, trial-level effect sizes are derived from the 2 × 2 table of remission events in the FMT and control arms. When odds ratios (OR) are used, the OR for each trial is computed as (remissionFMT/nonremissionFMT)/(remissionControl/nonremissionControl), and effect estimates are typically pooled on the log scale under random-effects models (e.g., Mantel–Haenszel in RevMan). Some meta-analyses instead report risk ratios, but the underlying calculation similarly begins with event counts in each arm. However, effect estimates vary and statistical heterogeneity is common, reflecting protocol diversity and differences in endpoints. Certainty of evidence is often rated low to moderate due to imprecision, inconsistency, and risk of bias in smaller trials. Long-term maintenance outcomes remain a major evidence gap.12–14

Special populations

Pediatric data are limited and largely derived from small observational studies; efficacy remains uncertain. Immunocompromised patients and those with severe, fulminant disease have been underrepresented in clinical trials; therefore, extrapolation to these groups is inappropriate outside carefully monitored research settings. 5

Practical considerations and protocol optimization

Clinically relevant questions in UC often center on who to treat, how to deliver FMT, and how to maximize efficacy while minimizing risk.

Patient selection

Current evidence best supports studying FMT as an adjunct to standard medical therapy in mild-to-moderate active UC, particularly in patients with inadequate response to conventional treatments. Use in severe flares, malnutrition, active sepsis, or profound immunosuppression should be avoided outside protocolized trials.5,12

Donor strategy

In RCTs, multidonor pooling is frequently paired with repeated dosing. These features likely act together: pooling increases functional diversity and buffers donor-to-donor variability, whereas repeated dosing increases the probability of durable engraftment. Claims of “superiority” should therefore be framed as protocol-level effects rather than isolated donor effects.3,4,11

Processing and delivery

Anaerobic processing may preserve obligate anaerobes and improve engraftment. Colonic delivery by colonoscopy followed by enemas has the most consistent induction signal. Upper-GI infusion and oral capsule delivery have been less consistent for induction, possibly due to reduced colonic delivery of viable organisms and exposure to gastric/duodenal conditions.3,4,10,13

Adjunctive strategies

Antibiotic priming may reduce colonization resistance by transiently depleting resident communities and altering bile acid/SCFA ecology, potentially creating ecological niches for donor strains; however, it may also increase adverse effects and confound interpretation of FMT-specific effects. Anti-inflammatory diets may act as substrate-level modifiers by increasing fermentable substrates and supporting SCFA biosynthesis in engrafting consortia. Early clinical signals (e.g., FMT paired with anti-inflammatory diets in open-label designs) require confirmation in blinded trials before routine adoption.10,15

Probiotics and synbiotics

Evidence that adjunctive probiotics enhance FMT efficacy in UC is limited and heterogeneous, with substantial strain-, dose-, and duration-dependence and few adequately powered blinded RCTs testing combination strategies. In theory, targeted strains could support engraftment via substrate utilization and SCFA production, but they could also compete with donor communities or increase bloating; safety considerations are particularly relevant in immunocompromised patients. Well-designed trials are needed to define whether probiotics add incremental benefit beyond optimized FMT protocols.

Safety profile and risk mitigation

Across RCTs in UC, common adverse events are usually mild and self-limited (e.g., bloating, abdominal discomfort, transient fever) and occur at similar frequencies to placebo. The critical safety concern is rare but severe transmission of pathogens, including multidrug-resistant organisms and viruses.12,16–18

Risk mitigation requires: (i) standardized donor history screening; (ii) laboratory testing for enteric pathogens, multidrug-resistant organisms (e.g., ESBL/CRE), and relevant viruses; (iii) consideration of quarantine and repeat testing for stored material; (iv) rigorous chain-of-custody documentation and traceability; and (v) postprocedure surveillance with predefined response plans. These safeguards are particularly important given reported transmission of drug-resistant Escherichia coli bacteremia (including fatalities) and regulator alerts during the SARS-CoV-2 era, and because the risk–benefit balance in UC differs from rCDI due to more modest and uncertain efficacy.16–18 Regional consensus documents provide practical operational frameworks for donor screening, processing, and traceability, but implementation remains heterogeneous.19,20

Guidelines and regulatory landscape

Major society guidance currently recommends against routine FMT for UC outside clinical trials. In contrast, microbiota-based products are endorsed for rCDI in appropriate settings, including via approved products (e.g., fecal microbiota, live-jslm (Rebyota), and fecal microbiota spores, live-brpk (Vowst)). This distinction is crucial to avoid conflating strong rCDI evidence with the more limited and heterogeneous UC evidence. 5 Editorial perspectives have similarly emphasized that UC remains a “holding” indication for FMT until efficacy becomes reproducible under standardized protocols and rigorous safety oversight. 21

Predictors of response and biomarker development

Identifying responders remains a key translational priority. Reported candidate predictors include higher baseline microbial diversity, specific community structures (e.g., enrichment of certain Clostridiales/Bacteroidales), and metabolic features suggesting preserved SCFA biosynthesis. Post-FMT engraftment of SCFA-producing taxa and restoration of secondary bile acids have been associated with remission in observational analyses. To date, no clinically validated “optimal microbiota composition” has been established for UC; available data support candidate functional/ecological signatures rather than a fixed taxonomic recipe.8,9,22 Recent data also suggest age-dependent variation in UC microbiota profiles and gut inflammatory markers (e.g., fecal calprotectin and zonulin), which may affect biomarker interpretation across populations. 23

At present, these biomarkers are not validated for routine clinical decision-making. Future trials should prespecify biomarker hypotheses, use harmonized sample collection and omics pipelines, and test whether biomarker-guided donor-recipient matching improves outcomes.6–9,22

Evidence gaps and future directions

Key gaps include (i) uncertainty about durability and optimal maintenance strategies; (ii) lack of standardized endpoints and protocol reporting; (iii) limited data in pediatric, elderly, and immunocompromised populations; and (iv) unclear incremental benefit over modern biologics and small molecules.

Future directions include protocol harmonization with transparency in donor selection, processing, and dosing; incorporation of mechanistic endpoints (metagenomics, metabolomics, immune profiling); and development of defined microbial consortia or next-generation live biotherapeutics that can provide consistent composition, scalable manufacturing, and improved safety control compared with donor-derived material.5,12,24–26

Conclusion

FMT is a biologically plausible microbiome-based intervention for UC and has demonstrated modest induction benefit in some RCTs, particularly with intensive lower-GI regimens using pooled multidonor and/or anaerobically processed material. Nevertheless, heterogeneity, low certainty, and negative trials underscore that FMT is not ready for routine UC care. Until standardized protocols and validated responder biomarkers are established, FMT for UC should remain confined to regulated clinical trials with rigorous safety oversight. The field is likely to advance most rapidly through mechanistically informed trial design and transition toward defined, standardized microbiota-based therapeutics.3–5,12
