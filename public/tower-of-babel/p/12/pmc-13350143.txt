Prospective observation study of incision and drainage vs drainage and initial fistula management for perianal abscesses
Authors: Praveena Gaddam, Samina Ruquaya, Athira Gopinathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerianal abscess is a common anorectal emergency, and simple incision and drainage (I&D) remains the standard initial treatment. However, failure to address an underlying fistulous tract at the primary surgery may result in high rates of abscess recurrence and subsequent fistula formation. The role of drainage with concurrent initial fistula management remains debated due to concerns regarding anal continence.AimTo compare outcomes of simple incision and drainage versus drainage with initial fistula management in patients with acute perianal abscess, with special reference to abscess recurrence.MethodsThis Prospective observation studies was conducted in the Department of General Surgery at SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre from march 2025 to December 2025. A total of 142 patients with acute perianal abscess were randomized into two groups: Group A underwent simple I&D (n = 71) and Group B underwent I&D with primary fistula management (n = 71). Patients were followed for 10 months. Primary outcomes included abscess recurrence and fistula formation. Secondary outcomes included anal incontinence assessed using the Fecal Incontinence Severity Index (FISI), postoperative pain, wound healing time, and hospital stay. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v25.0 with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsAbscess recurrence was significantly higher in the simple drainage group (23.9%) compared to the drainage with fistula management group (4.2%) (p = 0.001). Fistula formation occurred exclusively in the simple drainage group (25.4%) (p < 0.001). Mean FISI scores were higher following simple drainage (5.10 ± 3.15) than drainage with fistula management (3.30 ± 2.13) (p < 0.001). Hospital stay was significantly shorter in the combined procedure group (p < 0.001), while postoperative pain scores were comparable.ConclusionDrainage with initial fistula management significantly reduces abscess recurrence and fistula formation without increasing postoperative pain, and is associated with better continence outcomes and shorter hospital stay compared to simple drainage alone.Keywords: Perianal abscess, Incision and drainage, Fistulotomy, Abscess recurrence, Fecal incontinence

Introduction

Perianal abscess and fistula-in-ano are common anorectal disorders that constitute a major workload for general and colorectal surgeons worldwide. The disease arises predominantly from infection of the anal glands located at the dentate line, a mechanism first described in the cryptoglandular theory that remains the most accepted etiopathogenic explanation today [1]. The glands open into the anal crypts and penetrate through the internal sphincter into the intersphincteric plane; when obstruction occurs, bacterial overgrowth and stasis lead to pus accumulation, forming a perianal abscess [1, 2]. Depending on the direction of spread, abscesses are categorized anatomically as perianal, ischiorectal, intersphincteric, or supralevator [2]. Although considered a benign condition, the acute phase is painful and may progress rapidly, sometimes leading to systemic sepsis, cellulitis, or deep pelvic infection if not promptly managed [3]. Epidemiologically, anorectal abscesses are one of the most frequent surgical emergencies, with adult males being roughly twice as likely as females to develop the condition (Adult males vs females ~ 2:1). The disease is also observed in neonates and infants, where the presentation, anatomy, and therapeutic approaches differ from adults [4]. The standard treatment of perianal abscess remains surgical incision and drainage (I&D), which aims to relieve pain, evacuate pus, and prevent sepsis [5]. However, this approach does not address the underlying glandular source, and recurrence or fistula formation after simple drainage continues to challenge clinicians. Historically, simple incision and drainage were favoured for their safety, technical simplicity, and minimal immediate risk of sphincter injury. The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) guidelines emphasize timely drainage as the cornerstone of therapy and recommend that procedures be performed as close to the anal verge as possible to shorten potential fistula tracts.

Pathophysiologically, perianal abscesses represent the acute phase of the same disease spectrum that manifests chronically as fistula-in-ano. Thus, the therapeutic dilemma revolves around the timing and extent of addressing both components.

Recent practice-based surveys indicate heterogeneous approaches across institutions. Some centers prefer staged management, draining the abscess first and treating the fistula later once inflammation subsides. Others advocate one-stage procedures in selected cases with low tracts or visible internal openings [6, 7]. This lack of consensus highlights the necessity for prospective Prospective observation studies comparing the two techniques directly in adults. From a physiological standpoint, the anal sphincter complex possesses remarkable regenerative capacity when only the superficial portion is divided. Studies of low-tract fistulotomy demonstrate negligible long-term effects on continence [8, 9]. No patient reported major incontinence, and minor transient flatus incontinence resolved spontaneously [10]. Likewise documented temporary flatus incontinence in a few cases that recovered within two weeks. These findings challenge the earlier apprehension that primary fistulotomy inevitably jeopardizes anal function. [11]

Nevertheless, not all authors agree. Some practitioners caution that acute inflammation obscures anatomy, potentially leading to over-excision or misidentification of tracts [12]. Furthermore, in cases associated with Crohn’s disease, tuberculosis, or immunodeficiency, simple drainage remains advisable to avoid impaired healing [13, 14]. Consequently, patient selection, surgeon expertise, and proper postoperative care are pivotal determinants of outcome. Modern understanding of perianal abscess also incorporates insights from molecular and inflammatory biology. Elevated activin A levels, as demonstrated [15]. Correlate with tissue inflammation and delayed wound healing. Reduction of these levels following radical drainage suggests better control of local infection and systemic response. [16]. Such biomarkers may, in future, assist in prognosticating outcomes or tailoring interventions [17]. Ayurvedic and traditional surgical approaches like Ksharasutra therapy, though effective in chronic fistula management, highlight the long-standing global pursuit of minimally invasive yet definitive cures [18]. However, evidence-based validation remains essential, and integration of modern randomized methodologies can help bridge traditional and contemporary paradigms in anorectal disease treatment.

Material and methods

Study design

The present study was designed as a prospective observation studies conducted in the Department of General Surgery at tertiary care teaching hospital equipped with advanced surgical, anaesthetic, and diagnostic facilities, which provided an ideal environment for conducting controlled clinical research. The total duration of the study was 10 months, the study period from march 2025 to December 2025. Inclusion Criteria was patients aged 18 years and above, all patients diagnosed clinically and radiologically (when necessary) with a primary perianal abscess, patients who provided informed written consent to participate and patients fit for surgery under general anaesthesia. While the exclusion criteria were patients with recurrent abscesses or pre-existing perianal fistula at presentation, patients with a history of inflammatory bowel disease, malignancy, or anal incontinence, patients under immunosuppression, including those with HIV infection or chronic steroid therapy. patients with previous perianal surgeries or any condition that could confound postoperative assessment. The total sample size was 142 patients, divided equally between the two groups (71 patients per group). A simple random sampling technique was used to allocate patients into one of two study groups. Upon confirming eligibility, participants were randomly assigned to either Group A (simple incision and drainage) or Group B (incision, drainage, and initial fistula management) using a computer-generated randomization table. Data were entered and analysed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Study procedure

All patients were admitted and optimized for surgery with routine preoperative evaluation including complete blood count, random blood sugar, renal and liver function tests, and serological screening for infectious diseases. Surgery was performed under general anaesthesia with patients positioned in the lithotomy position.

A cruciate incision was made at the point of maximal fluctuance to allow optimal drainage. The abscess cavity was explored using the opposite index finger to break loculi, and adequate haemostasis was secured. Pus was collected in sterile containers for culture and sensitivity testing.

Group A Procedure: Standard cruciate incision was made over the point of maximum fluctuance, pus was evacuated, the cavity was explored, and haemostasis was achieved with packing.

Group B Procedure: Similar drainage was performed; the internal opening was identified by probing or dye tests (methylene blue or hydrogen peroxide) and, upon confirmation, fistulotomy was performed.

Surgical procedure

Locating the internal opening by examination under anaesthesia

- Hypertrophied papilla: Indicator of internal opening close by.

- Pus discharge coming from internal opening.

- Puckering, scar, ulcer of overlying mucosa.

- Induration felt on bimanual palpation.

- The mucosa near the internal opening is immobile.

- Palpable cord like fistula tract from external opening towards anus.

- Bogginess: Indicative of abscess and induration at dentate line.

- Lateral or posterior induration indicates ischiorectal or postanal extension.

Operative maneuvers to locate the internal opening

-Gentle probing of external opening, while keeping the finger at the dentate line; avoid force which may create false passage.

-Probe from the internal opening when it fails from external opening.

-Methylene blue test (few drops hydrogen peroxide [H₂O,]) instill from external opening. Dye will come out from the internal opening, do not force.

-Smaller Sim's to identify the internal opening. large speculums can block the dye coming out from the internal opening.

-Inject 2 cc saline into the mucosa at the suspected internal opening site. The surrounding mucosa elevates, but not at the internal opening.

-Dissect the fistula tract from the external opening towards the anus. Giving tugs on the tract will reflect on the site internal opening.

-Coring or dissecting the tract through the external opening, with the tract held by an Allis clamp, will guide towards the internal opening.

Fistulotomy

Anaesthesia and position are surgeon's preference. However, in the case of long posterior tracts, prone position offers better exposure. Methylene blue with a few drops of H, O, is instilled from the external opening. The internal opening is identified by the presence of methylene blue.

The fistula probe is passed gently from the external opening up to the internal opening of the fistula tract, noting the distance from the anorectal ring and the amount of muscle over the probe before incising. The surgeon should know how much external anal sphincter (EAS) is being left behind. The fistula tract is incised and laid open (using scalpel, cautery, or bare fiber Laser). The open tract is curetted, and the fibrosed thick margins are trimmed. Excise the internal opening, check for any abscess or fistula extension into the deep intersphincteric space. The tract from the site of the internal opening (dentate line) to the anal verge is closed with polyglycolic acid (PGA) 3/0 (vicryl) while performing an anal verge repair. The track outside is marsupialized with PGA 3/0 (vicryl). The anal verge is closed to prevent keyhole deformity and preventing stool from going into the fistulotomy wound. Marsupialization is done to make the fistulotomy wound shallow and narrow leading to early healing. Simple dressing is done.

Postoperatively the patient is instructed to clean the area well after each stool and take a warm sitz bath. In case of sensation of retained fecal matter in anus, the patients are instructed to walk around for a few minutes and sit again at the toilet and clean the area. Metronidazole containing ointment can be used. Postoperatively patient to be examined on regular basis by the operating surgeon to assess progress and healing.

External anal sphincter rule

An intact external anal sphincter is the most essential component for continence. The external anal sphincter rule states that if the anal abscess is medial to the EAS the drainage is done from inside the anal canal. If the abscess is crossing the EAS or levator ani at any point, the abscess drainage is from outside.

Fistulectomy

Anaesthesia and position are surgeon's preference. However, in the case of long posterior tracts, prone position will facilitate dissection.

Methylene blue with a few drops of hydrogen peroxide is instilled from the external opening. The internal opening is identified by the appearance of methylene blue the methylene blue will stain the tract facilitating its identification during dissection, alerting the surgeon when the tract is breached, and it also helps in identification of secondary tracts and abscesses.

The fistula probe is passed from the external opening and negotiated gently across the tract up to the internal opening However, it may not always be possible to pass the probe. It is advisable to refrain from forceful probing, otherwise it can create an iatrogenic opening.

If the probe is passed, palpate the sphincter muscle over the probe and feel the anorectal ring, this will help in assessing how much muscle will be spared.

Inject saline with adrenaline around the external opening and along the tract. Cut circumferentially around the external opening and proceed with dissection in the subcutaneous plane while remaining close to the tract. The tract dissection should progress towards the EAS. As the EAS is reached, a tag on the tract will reveal the internal opening. Incise the skin and anoderm up to the internal opening. Dissect the fistula by incising the EAS, internal anal sphincter (IAS). and the internal opening. All secondary tracts and abscesses are excised. The wound is washed liberally with saline. At the internal opening, check for any abscess or fistula extension into the deep intersphincteric space. Primary sphincter repair is advised, as it prevents incontinence and fecal discharge from coming onto the wound.

Closure of mucosa and internal sphincter-Identify the mucosa, IAS, and EAS. The first step is to close the mucosa and internal sphincter with PGA 3/0 (vicryl 3/0). The sutures are taken from IAS to anoderm and back, then knotted. Two to three sutures will reconstruct the anal canal. The anal verge is closed to prevent keyhole deformity and preventing stool from entering into the fistulotomy wound.

Closure of EAS- The EAS is approximated with 1/0 or 2/0 polydioxanone suture (PDS) suture from inside to outside in a single layer. Starting from close to the anal canal and extending to the upper part of the EAS. The sutures should only approximate the sphincter muscle and not be tied too tightly, as this may strangulate the tissue.

Marsupialization- The remaining wound is marsupialized to make it smaller and shallower. Inside-out sutures taken from the depth to just below the skin. This leads to reduction of wound closure time by 50%. A simple dressing is applied.

Postoperative Care- The patient is instructed to keep the wound clean with irrigation and warm sitz bath. Metronidazole-containing ointments can be used.

Ischiorectal abscess management

Operative steps- The type of drainage depends on whether the internal opening is identified.

Internal opening identified- fistulotomy: If less than 30% of the EAS is involved, the incision should start at the internal opening and extend laterally towards the abscess in the ischiorectal fossa. It is important to remember that the abscess cavity itself is merely a collection point with no active disease process. The active central disease extending from the internal opening to the intersphincteric space must be excised to minimize the risk of recurrence.

No internal opening identified- Incision and drainage (The procedure involves draining the pus, irrigating with saline, and curetting the cavity.)

Supralevator abscess

Surgical approach- Insert a drop of methylene blue in the internal opening to demarcate the underlying unhealthy granulation tissue. Excise the internal opening along with mucosa and submucosa to remove the central source of infection. One can see unhealthy granulation tissue or pus emerging out from the intersphincteric space. The goal is to access the supralevator space while staying within the intersphincteric plane. Gently probe the space to reach the supralevator space. Drain the abscess, curette and irrigate the supralevator space. A primary fistulotomy can be done up to the anal verge for drainage purposes extending from the excised internal opening up to the anal verge.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and received prior approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study’s purpose, surgical procedures, potential risks, and benefits in their native language.

Discussion

The present study compared outcomes of incision and drainage alone versus drainage with fistulotomy in cryptoglandular perianal abscess. By randomizing 142 patients and assessing recurrence, fistula formation, continence, pain, hospital stay, and microbiological factors, we evaluated whether definitive tract management at the index operation offers advantages over conventional drainage. Our findings address a longstanding dilemma in anorectal surgery, where concerns about incontinence and lack of protocols have led to under-treatment of occult fistulas and high recurrence rates. Demonstrating that drainage with fistulotomy reduces recurrence and fistula formation without compromising continence or increasing pain has direct implications for surgical practice, patient counselling, training, and resource allocation. These results support the concept that perianal abscess often represents part of an abscess–fistula complex, and that early, anatomy-based management may prevent chronic morbidity. Integrating clinical, functional, and resource outcomes, this study provides evidence for adopting definitive, anatomy-guided algorithms to improve long-term results and reduce repeat procedures. Age distribution showed no significant association with treatment allocation or outcomes. Patients aged 18–29 years comprised 33.8% of the drainage group and 26.8% of the drainage + fistulotomy group, while those aged 30–49 years formed the largest proportion in both arms (39.4% vs. 47.9%). Representation of patients aged 50–70 years was similar (26.8% vs. 25.4%). These findings confirm effective randomization and minimize confounding from age-related variations in healing or recurrence risk. Comparable demographic parity has been reported in prior randomized trials of abscess and fistula management. Saber and Bayumi randomized 200 patients with balanced age stratification, attributing recurrence differences to intervention rather than age [19]. Studies in Crohn’s perianal fistulas (de Groof, Wasmann) reported consistent age profiles across therapeutic groups [20, 21]. Collectively, these data reinforce that age does not determine surgical outcome when randomization is adequate. In line with earlier trials, our results indicate that treatment strategy, not demographic variables, remains the dominant determinant of recurrence and healing. Sex distribution showed no significant association with treatment allocation. Females comprised 45.1% of the drainage group and 54.9% of the drainage + fistulotomy group, while males represented 54.9% and 45.1%, respectively. This balance confirms that postoperative recurrence, fistula formation, and functional outcomes were driven by treatment strategy rather than sex-based differences. Similar demographic neutrality has been reported in randomized trials of anorectal disease. Galanis et al. likewise reported comparable sex ratios, with recurrence and continence outcomes unaffected by sex [22]. Studies in Crohn’s perianal fistulas (de Groof, Wasmann) and Sørensen’s trial of needle aspiration versus drainage also documented balanced sex profiles, reinforcing methodological rigor and ruling out sex as a determinant of healing [20, 21, 23]. Collectively, these findings align with the present trial: differences in recurrence and fistula formation reflect surgical approach, not sex characteristics, consistent with prior randomized evidence. Occupation showed no significant association with treatment allocation. Drivers, farmers, labourers, office workers, shopkeepers, and teachers were similarly distributed across groups, with labourers forming the largest subgroup (61.5% vs. 38.5%), followed by farmers (52.0% vs. 48.0%) and teachers (38.5% vs. 61.5%). This uniformity indicates that recurrence, fistula formation, and functional outcomes were determined by treatment strategy rather than occupational demands or socioeconomic factors. Although no prior randomized trial has directly assessed occupation as a determinant of outcome, several studies have reported comparable occupational diversity across groups. Galanis et al. likewise noted similar occupational backgrounds, with lower recurrence in the fistulotomy arm [22]. Crohn’s fistula studies (de Groof, Wasmann) also reported wide occupational variability, yet outcomes were linked to therapeutic approach, not occupation [20, 21]. These findings align with the pathophysiology of cryptoglandular sepsis, which arises from glandular obstruction rather than environmental or musculoskeletal strain. Thus, our results reinforce that occupation does not influence disease behaviour or surgical outcome; the choice of treatment particularly primary fistulotomy remains the key determinant of successful resolution and prevention of recurrence.

Comorbidity analysis showed a significant association with treatment allocation. Among diabetics, 66.7% underwent simple drainage versus 33.3% fistulotomy, while combined diabetes and hypertension favored fistulotomy. Hypertensive patients and those without comorbidities were evenly distributed. This imbalance may reflect surgical caution in isolated diabetics or random variation. Diabetes is linked to impaired healing and infection risk, prompting trials of adjunctive antibiotics. Abcarian reported no protective effect, with ~ 30% fistula formation, though diabetics were considered exceptions [24]. Islam and Rahman found no difference in recurrence or fistula formation among diabetics randomized to packing versus nonpacking [25]. In our study, comorbidity was not an independent predictor of recurrence. Overall, evidence suggests metabolic diseases may influence postoperative care but definitive outcomes depend on adequacy of drainage and fistula management.

Abscess type showed no significant association with treatment allocation. Intersphincteric abscesses comprised 64.5% of the drainage group and 35.5% of the fistulotomy group, while ischiorectal, perianal, and supralevator abscesses were proportionally distributed. Thus, baseline morphology did not bias surgical decision-making, and recurrence differences reflect treatment strategy. Abcarian reported higher fistula risk with ischiorectal (eightfold) and intersphincteric (threefold) abscesses compared with perineal types, independent of postoperative care [24]. Randomized trials by Saber and Bayumi and by Galanis et al. found similar abscess distributions across arms, yet drainage with fistulotomy consistently reduced recurrence (5% vs. 20% and 6% vs. 28%) [19, 22]. Yi et al. demonstrated that meticulous decompression of horseshoe abscesses lowered recurrence from 25 to 8% using RCTD [26]. Collectively, these findings and our results confirm that while abscess type contributes to disease complexity, long-term outcomes are determined chiefly by surgical strategy particularly whether the fistula tract is addressed at the initial procedure.

Internal opening status was strongly associated with treatment allocation. All 44 patients without an identifiable opening underwent simple drainage, while among 98 with a recognized opening, 72.4% received fistulotomy and 27.6% drainage alone. This reflects the principle that fistulotomy is performed when the opening is clearly defined to avoid unnecessary sphincter division. Prior trials confirm its prognostic importance: Galanis et al. reported higher recurrence when the tract was not treated (28% vs. 6%) [64], In Crohn’s disease, de Groof, Wasmann, and Wiese emphasized accurate mapping, with re-intervention highest when openings were left untreated (67% in chronic seton groups) [20, 21, 27]. Yi et al. also demonstrated reduced recurrence in horseshoe fistulas using radial incision and tunnel drainage (25% to 8%) [26]. Although internal opening status did not independently predict recurrence in our logistic regression (p = 0.170), outcomes were primarily determined by treatment choice. These findings reaffirm that identification of the opening guides surgical decision-making, but definitive management particularly fistulotomy remains the key determinant of success.

Parks classification showed no significant association with treatment allocation. Extra-sphincteric, inter-sphincteric, supra-sphincteric, and trans-sphincteric fistulas were proportionally distributed, indicating that baseline anatomy did not influence allocation or outcomes. Prior trials confirm that tract complexity alone does not determine success when definitive treatment is applied. Saber and Bayumi reported lower recurrence (5% vs. 20%) and fistula formation (3% vs. 18%) with fistulotomy regardless of classification [19]. Al Sebai demonstrated sphincter-preserving LIFT reduced incontinence risk in low trans-sphincteric fistulas [28], while Yi et al. showed RCTD lowered recurrence in horseshoe fistulas (25% to 8%) [26]. Collectively, these findings and our results reinforce that surgical strategy particularly addressing the primary tract remains the key determinant of healing and recurrence. Recurrence analysis demonstrated a significant reduction when drainage was combined with fistulotomy: 23.9% recurrence with incision alone versus 4.2% with fistulotomy (χ2 = 11.407, p = 0.001). This sixfold reduction highlights the importance of managing the tract at initial presentation. Saber and Bayumi reported similar benefits (20% vs. 5%) [19], and Galanis et al. observed recurrence of 28% versus 6% [22]. Sørensen showed needle aspiration carried higher recurrence (41% vs. 15%) [23]. In Crohn’s disease, de Groof and Wasmann documented high re-intervention with seton drainage (67%) compared with biologics or closure [20, 21]. Yi et al. further reduced recurrence in horseshoe fistulas using RCTD (25% to 8%) [26]. These findings align with our trial, confirming that recurrence depends primarily on definitive tract treatment, strongly supporting incorporation of primary fistulotomy when anatomy permits.

Fistula formation displayed a striking and clinically meaningful difference between treatment groups. In the present study, fistula development occurred exclusively in the simple drainage group, where 18 of 71 patients (25.4%) progressed to fistulous disease, whereas none of the 71 patients in the drainage + fistulotomy group experienced fistula formation (χ2 = 20.613, p < 0.001). This finding emphasizes that addressing the primary tract at the time of abscess drainage offers strong protective benefit against chronic fistula evolution. Comparable conclusions have been reached in previous randomized trials. Galanis et al. similarly reported lower fistula formation in the group receiving primary fistula management (6%) relative to the incision-only group (28%), reinforcing that residual untreated tracts predispose to chronic suppuration [22]. Abcarian’s randomized study further established that antibiotics were ineffective in preventing fistula formation, with approximately 30% of patients developing fistulas regardless of antibiotic use, and even higher incidence in the antibiotic arm (P = 0.044), highlighting that surgery, not medical therapy, defines fistula risk [24]. In Crohn’s disease–related fistulas, de Groof, Wasmann, and Wiese collectively demonstrated that persistent tracts treated only with seton drainage or inadequate closure strategies showed higher long-term failure rates, including 67% re-intervention with chronic seton and lower healing with nonsurgical approaches [20, 21, 27]. Yi et al. reported reduced recurrence and chronicity when drainage architecture was optimized, with recurrence decreasing from 25 to 8% using the RCTD technique in horseshoe fistulas [26]. The present study’s absence of fistula formation in the fistulotomy group underscores that effective early elimination of the tract is critical to preventing chronic disease, aligning strongly with prior randomized evidence across cryptoglandular and Crohn’s-related fistulas.

Multivariate logistic regression identified treatment strategy as the dominant predictor of recurrence, with drainage + fistulotomy reducing odds by ~ 93.5% (B = –2.728, p = 0.001; OR = 0.065, 95% CI 0.013–0.334). Other variables age, sex, BMI, internal opening, and abscess type were not independent predictors, though some abscess contrasts reached significance. This underscores that recurrence is treatment-driven rather than demographic-dependent. Comparable findings are reported in major trials: Wasmann’s PISA trial confirmed inferior outcomes with seton placement alone (P = 0.02) [21]. Wiese’s EUS-guided biologic titration highlighted that long-term recurrence reflects structural pathology rather than early drainage differences [27]. Im’s analysis of > 300 patients found biologics effective only when combined with proper drainage [29]. Collectively, these studies parallel our results, demonstrating that definitive tract management particularly fistulotomy remains the key determinant of recurrence.

Functional outcomes assessed by FISI score showed significantly better continence preservation with drainage + fistulotomy. Mean FISI was 5.10 (SD 3.15) in the drainage group versus 3.30 (SD 2.13) in the fistulotomy group, a mean difference of 1.80 (95% CI 0.91–2.70, p < 0.001). Treating the tract early reduces recurrence and prevents chronic scarring or sphincter disruption. Prior trials confirm continence safety: Saber and Bayumi found no difference between groups despite lower recurrence with fistulotomy [19], and Galanis et al. reported minimal incontinence (2% vs. 1%) [22]. For complex tracts, LIFT reduced incontinence (0% vs. 13%) but had higher recurrence (20% vs. 7%) [28]. Our superior FISI scores align with this evidence, supporting early tract eradication to minimize long-term impairment.

Linear regression confirmed treatment allocation as a significant predictor of continence. The model (R = 0.319, R2 = 0.102) showed fistulotomy independently reduced FISI by ~ 2 points (B = –1.803, p < 0.001). Other variables were not predictive. These findings mirror randomized trials: Saber and Bayumi [19] and Galanis [23] reported continence preservation with fistulotomy, while LIFT offered sphincter safety but lower healing rates [28]. Even though fistulotomy is associated with low rates of incontinence, it should only be performed by experienced surgeons. Crohn’s studies likewise demonstrated durable healing without continence loss when anatomy and inflammation were addressed [30]. Overall, our regression confirms that treatment strategy particularly fistulotomy remains the most influential factor in functional recovery.

Pain outcomes showed no significant difference between groups (mean 5.08 vs. 4.97, p = 0.623), indicating that fistulotomy does not increase postoperative discomfort and may reduce sepsis-related inflammation. Prior studies suggest pain is more influenced by adjuncts than surgical extent. Perera reported lower scores in nonpacking versus packing groups (VAS 0.00 vs. 2.00, p = 0.030) [31], and Islam and Rahman similarly found higher pain with packing (7.25 vs. 4.24, p = 0.000) [25]. In Crohn’s disease, biologics such as adalimumab and infliximab reduced drainage but did not improve pain unless combined with surgery [27, 29]. Collectively, these findings confirm that well-executed fistulotomy does not worsen pain, and recurrence prevention not pain reduction is the key determinant of long-term benefit. Hospital stay was significantly shorter with drainage + fistulotomy (mean 4.08 days) compared with simple drainage (5.06 days), a mean reduction of 0.97 days (p < 0.001). This reflects the benefit of addressing the fistulous tract at the initial procedure, minimizing infection and prolonged wound care. Similar findings are reported in randomized trials: Saber and Bayumi observed faster healing with fistulotomy (3.8 vs. 5.6 weeks, p < 0.05) [19], and Galanis et al. noted earlier symptom resolution and longer recurrence-free intervals [22]. Packing studies indirectly support this principle, with Perera and Islam showing faster recovery when packing was omitted [25, 31]. In Crohn’s disease, de Groof and Wasmann demonstrated that strategies enabling earlier tract closure reduced prolonged drainage and re-intervention [20, 21]. Yi et al. further showed shorter healing times and fewer complications using RCTD in horseshoe abscesses [26]. Collectively, these findings confirm that definitive fistula management shortens hospitalization and accelerates recovery. Symptom duration prior to presentation did not differ significantly between groups (7.01 vs. 7.56 days, p = 0.433), confirming comparable chronicity and no bias in treatment allocation. Thus, recurrence and fistula outcomes were not influenced by preoperative duration. Prior randomized trials support this: Galanis et al. likewise found outcomes dictated by tract management rather than chronicity [22]. Packing studies showed the same principle Perera and Islam observed healing differences based on wound care, not symptom duration [25, 31]. In Crohn’s disease, de Groof, Wiese, and Wasmann emphasized that long-term healing depends on therapy choice (biologics, closure, or seton drainage) rather than symptom duration [20, 21, 27]. Collectively, these findings and our results confirm that preoperative symptom duration is not a determinant of outcome; definitive intervention strategy remains the key factor in recurrence and fistula prevention. Microbiological culture results showed no significant association with treatment allocation (χ2 = 0.770, p = 0.857). Isolate distribution was nearly identical across groups E. coli (25.4% vs. 21.1%), Klebsiella (21.1% vs. 26.8%), mixed flora (26.8% each), and no growth (26.8% vs. 25.4%). Thus, microbial composition did not influence surgical choice or outcomes. Prior studies highlight the limited role of microbiology when drainage is adequate. Abcarian reported fistula formation in ~ 30% despite antibiotics, with paradoxically higher incidence in the antibiotic group (p = 0.044) [24]. Perera and Islam found healing and pain outcomes depended on packing practices, not flora [25, 31]. In Crohn’s disease, Wiese and de Groof showed treatment success was driven by inflammatory control and tract management rather than bacterial species [20, 27]. Yi et al. noted reduced pathogenic flora after RCTD but emphasized that optimized drainage not microbial change lowered recurrence (25% to 8%) [26]. Collectively, these findings and our results confirm that while cultures guide antibiotic use, surgical strategy remains the dominant determinant of healing and recurrence. Culture Result and Antibiotic Use no significant association was found between culture profile and antibiotic selection (p = 0.117). E. coli cases most often received amoxiclav (57.6%), Klebsiella cases ciprofloxacin + metronidazole (41.2%), while mixed flora and no-growth showed evenly distributed use. This pattern reflects empirical rather than culture-directed prescribing. Prior trials confirm antibiotics play little role in preventing fistula or recurrence. Abcarian reported higher fistula incidence with antibiotics versus placebo (p = 0.044) [24]. Perera and Islam showed outcomes depended on wound management, not antibiotic choice [25, 31]. Crohn’s studies (Wiese, de Groof, Wasmann) likewise found healing driven by biologic or surgical strategies, not microbial variation [60,61,66]. Yi et al. emphasized drainage optimization, not flora change, as the driver of reduced recurrence [26]. Thus, antibiotic use varied but did not influence outcomes when tract management was definitive. Recurrence and Antibiotic Use Recurrence was not associated with antibiotic regimen (p = 0.122). Among non-recurrence cases, amoxiclav, ciprofloxacin + metronidazole, and piperacillin–tazobactam were used in 78.8%, 93.3%, and 86.7%, respectively; proportions in recurrence cases were lower but nonsignificant. Evidence supports that surgical adequacy, not antibiotics, determines recurrence. Abcarian [24], Perera [31], and Islam [25] all found antibiotics ineffective in preventing fistula. Crohn’s trials (Wiese, de Groof, Wasmann) and darvadstrocel therapy [30] confirmed durable healing depends on tract closure, not antibiotic exposure. Amoxiclav was given to 59.6% of drainage patients vs. 40.4% of fistulotomy patients; ciprofloxacin + metronidazole to 42.2% vs. 57.8%; and piperacillin–tazobactam to 46.7% vs. 53.3%. This variability reflects clinician preference rather than treatment modality. Prior trials consistently show antibiotic regimen does not alter long-term outcomes. Crohn’s studies (de Groof, Wasmann, Wiese) and darvadstrocel therapy [30] confirmed antibiotics are supportive but not outcome-determinant. Our findings align: operative approach, not pharmacologic adjuncts, dictates prognosis.

Conclusion

This randomized trial shows that drainage with primary fistulotomy is superior to simple incision and drainage for cryptoglandular perianal abscess. In 142 patients, baseline demographics were comparable, confirming outcomes depended on treatment. Recurrence was markedly lower with fistulotomy (4.2% vs. 23.9%, p = 0.001), and fistula formation was eliminated (0% vs. 25.4%, p < 0.001). Logistic regression identified treatment strategy as the sole independent predictor of recurrence (OR 0.065, p = 0.001). Functional outcomes favoured fistulotomy, with lower FISI scores indicating better continence (3.30 vs. 5.10, p < 0.001). Short-term results also supported fistulotomy: pain was similar, but hospital stay was shorter (4.08 vs. 5.06 days, p < 0.001). Symptom duration, microbiology, and antibiotic use showed no significant impact. Overall, perianal abscess should be managed as part of the abscess–fistula spectrum, with fistulotomy preferred when anatomy permits, as it reduces recurrence, prevents fistula formation, preserves continence, and shortens recovery. Simple drainage remains appropriate only when fistulotomy is unsafe, acknowledging higher risk of recurrence and need for staged surgery.
