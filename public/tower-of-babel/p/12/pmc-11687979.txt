Adapting Clinical and Research Practice to Global Warming
Authors: Dion Groothof, Adrian Post, Naser B.N. Shehab, Stephan J.L. Bakker, Reinold O.B. Gans
Published: 2024

Climate change progresses rapidly, manifesting as increased droughts and heatwaves that jeopardize many aspects of life. Rising temperatures and heat extremes will make resilience to heat stress increasingly important to survival in the coming decades. Therefore, clinical practice guidelines should provide provisions to mitigate climate-related health risks. Moreover, the potential of both dietary and pharmacologic interventions to increase the risk of hypertonicity or volume depletion should become a routine consideration in research. Recent findings presented in the 2023 AR6 Synthesis Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/) prompted us to suggest that global warming will increase hypertonicity and volume depletion episodes that can exacerbate kidney injury, especially in individuals prone to develop fluid and electrolyte disturbances. Traditional health management approaches, particularly recommendations on fluid and dietary sodium restriction, warrant reevaluation in the context of rising global temperatures. We will explain our prediction based on the currently recommended dietary and pharmacologic interventions on sodium balance alongside the critical role of maintaining proper hydration.

Body fluid is distributed between the intracellular fluid and extracellular fluid (ECF), with the latter being subdivided into blood plasma and interstitial, lymph, and transcellular fluids.1,2 Small molecular, water-soluble substances are constantly exchanged between ECF compartments through capillary membrane pores, maintaining nearly identical concentrations, except for proteins. The intracellular fluid, however, is isolated from the ECF by cell membranes that are highly permeable to water but largely impermeable to many solutes, facilitating osmosis.1,2 Before discussing how low-sodium diets affect bodily fluids and kidney function, distinguishing between hypertonicity (formerly referred to as dehydration) and volume depletion is crucial.2 While typically conflated, these terms represent distinct states that differentially affect kidney function.2 Hypertonicity arises from disproportionately increased effective osmoles within the ECF, whereas hypotonicity arises from disproportionately decreased effective osmoles. These states can originate from imbalances in electrolyte-free water consumption and electrolyte intake, as well as from altered fluid and electrolyte handling by the kidneys. Isotonic states are maintained when electrolyte-free water and effective osmoles are balanced. Conversely, volume depletion involves a reduced ECF volume, which can present with hypertonic, hypotonic, or isotonic alterations, with each state potentially compromising tissue perfusion.1,2 Rising global temperatures will promote hypertonicity and volume depletion,3,4 making proper understanding of the influence of sodium intake and proper hydration on ECF tonicity and volume critical.

In steady-state conditions, the ECF volume is primarily determined by total bodily sodium, which is the resultant of sodium intake, renal excretion, and perspiration (intestinal sodium loss is negligible in the absence of diarrhea).1,2 Reducing sodium intake contracts the ECF volume, which benefits BP control and aids in lowering proteinuria and preventing volume overload,1,5 explaining the long-lived history of low-sodium diets as core recommendation in the management of hypertension and CKD. Still, the exact limit to which sodium intake should be restricted remains unclear.6,7 Recent Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, including the 2021 guideline for the management of BP in CKD (p.S32), the 2022 guideline for diabetes management in CKD (p.S24), and the 2024 guideline for the evaluation and management of CKD (p.S211), all recommend restricting daily sodium intake to below 2 g as a lifestyle intervention. The 2009 KDIGO guideline for the care of kidney transplant recipients does not specify daily sodium intake recommendations. Notably, only the 2021 guideline notes that “it is unknown whether sodium intakes far below 2 g per day are safe or not” (p.S32). Recently, a Cochrane review affirmed that reducing sodium intake reduces BP, proteinuria, and ECF volume in adults with any stage of CKD.5 Although the authors found a substantially increased risk of symptomatic hypotension attributable to dietary sodium restriction, such episodes were mostly mild or resolved by cessation or reduction of the dosage of antihypertensive medication.5 This suggests that restricting daily sodium intake to below 2 g is a safe lifestyle intervention to lower BP in patients with CKD. However, the Cochrane review's conclusions were reached before publication of the 2023 report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, which provides a grim prospect of our warming planet. The report's insights prompt the theoretical concern that stringent sodium restriction, although clearly beneficial under stable climatic conditions, imparts safety risks for individuals predisposed to fluid and electrolyte imbalances.

Low-sodium diets, which reduce the ECF volume, may decrease resilience against conditions of increased fluid loss—a capability that is becoming increasingly vital in a changing climate. On October 24, 2023, Copernicus Climate Change Service reported that the summer of 2023 hallmarked Europe's hottest summer ever recorded, with many countries across the continent having reached temperatures above 40°C (104°F), peaking on the island of Sicily with 46.3°C (115.3°F) (https://climate.copernicus.eu/2023-track-be-hottest-year-ever-whats-next). Such extraordinarily hot ambient conditions constitute a challenge even for otherwise healthy individuals. Importantly, older patients are more frequently advised to follow a low-sodium diet, a recommendation linked to the management of chronic conditions, like CKD and heart failure.8 However, these individuals concurrently are less able to control their fluid balance, mainly because of age-related diminished abilities to regulate body temperature and initiate thirst in response to increased blood tonicity1,3 and, thus, are especially susceptible to acute kidney dysfunction.6 In addition, the classes of medication that these patients typically are prescribed—diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor type 1 blockers, and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors—further dampen the already limited capacity to retain sodium.1 The combination of low-sodium diets and said medication creates a compounding effect that brings forth a special vulnerability to hypertonicity and volume depletion,3 posing an imminent threat considering rising global temperatures. Recurrent episodes of hypertonicity, exacerbated by poor hydration, subtly injure the kidneys3,4,9 and are possibly driving the insidious CKD epidemics observed in hot, rural regions—conditions labeled CKD of unknown etiology (CKDu).3,4,9 While both hypoperfusion-related kidney injury from volume depletion, commonly seen in the elderly,6 and hypertonicity-related kidney injury could contribute to these conditions, we are currently unable to effectively differentiate these two factors as causal in CKDu. Notably, outdoor workers, who are typically younger, may more frequently experience hypertonic stress than volume depletion due to effective homeostatic mechanisms that counteract volume depletion.1,3,6 This ongoing uncertainty underscores the need for further research to clarify their respective roles in CKDu.

The association of these CKD cases with heat and insufficient water access suggests a link with hypertonic stress, in which nephrotoxic effects of both vasopressin and the aldose reductase pathway elicit tubulointerstitial nephritis with fibrosis (Figure 1). In this process, activation of the aldose reductase pathway facilitates the stepwise conversion of glucose to fructose. Both endogenously produced and exogenously absorbed fructose are readily converted to fructose-1-phosphate in an energy-intensive step by adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent fructokinase, an enzyme that is highly expressed in the proximal tubule.11 The ensuing ATP depletion causes reduced GFR.1,3,4,9,11 Maintaining sufficient hydration, while strictly avoiding fructose-containing beverages, is imperative to prevent this pathological cascade.4

None of the current KDIGO guidelines make adequate provisions for how to act in the event of weather extremes. Increasingly frequent heat extremes set an ultimatum to call for action now. Considering the gaps in the current guidelines concerning heat extremes, defined here as ambient temperatures exceeding 40°C (104°F), we have developed a set of interim recommendations for practitioners managing patients at high risk of hypertonicity or volume depletion (e.g., due to an impaired thirst mechanism or inability to access fluid) during such conditions. These recommendations, detailed in Table 1, are applicable both during heat extremes and in research settings to guide clinical practice, irrespective of heat extremes. Interim recommendations for managing high-risk patients during heat extremes in clinical practice and research

While these recommendations are currently deemed appropriate and necessary for managing high-risk patients during heat extremes, observational studies and, preferably, clinical trials are warranted to confirm their effectiveness and optimize their implementation.

Evidence levels categorize the foundation of each recommendation for clinical practice and are defined as: level A: recommendation is based on textbook knowledge or supported by solid research, indicating high reliability and consensus; level B: recommendation is based on expert opinion or best clinical practices in the absence of extensive empirical evidence, suggesting moderate reliability; level C: recommendation lacks direct empirical support, indicating it is primarily based on hypothetical or theoretical frameworks or extrapolated knowledge from related areas or clinical judgment. These are provisional recommendations, awaiting validation from future research.

As many practices during heat extremes are not yet fully supported by robust clinical trials, the evidence level of each recommendation has been categorized to indicate where future studies are essential to validate or refine these guidelines.

While it is crucial to address clinical risks during heat extremes, the overarching disease burden resulting from elevated BP currently still exceeds potential harm from heat extremes. Thus, although our recommendations emphasize precautions during heat extremes, they are designed to be temporary, exposure-dependent, patient-specific adjustments. Integrating these recommendations can proactively address the challenges posed by a changing climate and ensure a safer and more resilient health care environment for all. Adapting our health care and research practice accordingly is not only a matter of future-proofing, it is imperative to protect the most vulnerable from future climate-related harm.
