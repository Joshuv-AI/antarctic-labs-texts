A comparative study to assess the efficacy of atomized versus drops administration of intranasal midazolam as premedication in pediatric patients undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia—A prospective randomized double-blind study
Authors: Parag Mandaknalli, Imran Sholapur, Rachana Shivanand Kori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Preoperative anxiety is one of the major challenges in perioperative care of pediatric surgical patients. Intranasal midazolam is a preferred route of drug administration owing to its ease of administration, non-invasive delivery, and rapid absorption through the highly vascular nasal mucosa, but the method of administration affects the efficacy.Aim:To compare the efficacy of atomized versus drop administration of intranasal midazolam (0.3 mg/kg) as a premedication in pediatric patients aged 2–6 years undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia.Methods:This prospective double-blind study was conducted in 60 pediatric patients randomly allocated into the Atomized Group or the nasal drops group. Drug acceptance by a 4-point Medicine Acceptance Scale, Sedation, anxiolysis at the time of shifting, assessed by the Parental Separation Anxiety Scale (PSAS), and adverse events.Results:Drug acceptance was superior in the atomized group compared to the nasal drops group (56.7% vs 3.3%; P < 0.001). The atomized group demonstrated statistically significantly higher sedation scores (2.53 ± 0.776 vs. 1.93 ± 0.583; P = 0.001) at 5 mins, indicating faster onset of sedation. PSAS scores were better in the atomized group (1.17 ± 0.592 vs. 1.53 ± 0.629; P = 0.024). Incidence of adverse events was comparable between both groups (P = 0.379), and no respiratory depression or hemodynamic instability was noted.Conclusion:Atomized intranasal midazolam via mucosal atomization device is superior to conventional nasal drops in achieving higher drug acceptance, faster sedation, and better parental separation anxiolysis in pediatric patients.

Introduction

Pediatric anesthesia presents unique challenges in the perioperative care of children, with preoperative anxiety being one of the most significant concerns. Preoperative anxiety is nearly universal in children undergoing elective procedures, affecting up to 75% of pediatric surgical patients in North America.[1] Separation from parents, the unfamiliar hospital environment, the presence of strangers in surgical attire, and fear of needles collectively contribute to distress in these young patients. The consequences of unmanaged preoperative anxiety extend well beyond the immediate perioperative period; children may develop emergence delirium, increased postoperative pain sensitivity, prolonged hospital stay, new-onset maladaptive behavioural changes, sleep disturbances, separation anxiety, and psychological sequelae such as phobias in later life.[1] These potential long-term harms have led anaesthesiologists and pediatric healthcare providers to prioritize evidence-based strategies aimed at minimizing pre-surgical anxiety and ensuring a smooth, stress-free perioperative experience.

Premedication is a cornerstone strategy to reduce anxiety in children scheduled for surgery. The ideal premedication agent should reduce anxiety, facilitate smooth parental separation, permit safe mask induction of general anesthesia, minimize the risk of postoperative emergence delirium, act rapidly, maintain hemodynamic stability, produce minimal side effects, and ideally provide some degree of perioperative amnesia.[2] Among available agents, benzodiazepines such as midazolam in particular, have become the most widely accepted group of medications for pediatric premedication worldwide. Midazolam enhances Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) neurotransmission in the central nervous system, producing sedation, amnesia, muscle relaxation, and anxiolysis with faster onset and short duration of action, and can be administered by several routes.[2] As the exploration of non-invasive delivery routes has advanced, the intranasal route has emerged as one of the most promising alternatives to oral premedication, offering non-invasive delivery with favorable pharmacokinetics.[34] The nasal mucosa’s rich vascularity enables rapid systemic absorption, avoidance of first-pass metabolism, and potentially a direct nose-to-brain pathway for some drugs, while the ease of administration, requiring neither patient cooperation nor sterile technique, makes intranasal midazolam an increasingly preferred option in pediatric anesthesia centers globally.[5]

Despite the widespread adoption of intranasal midazolam, the optimal delivery method remains a subject of ongoing investigation. Conventional nasal drops, while simple and economical, have significant physical limitations: gravity causes the liquid to flow along the nasal floor and drain into the nasopharynx, where it may be swallowed, resulting in first-pass metabolism; large drop size and high surface tension further limit mucosal contact and lead to poor, variable bioavailability.[6] The mucosal atomization device (MAD) was developed to address these limitations by converting liquid medication into a fine aerosol spray of 30–100 micrometer droplets, providing a greater surface-to-volume ratio, broader mucosal coverage, reduced nasopharyngeal runoff, and potentially higher and more consistent bioavailability compared to drops.[7] Although several preliminary studies have compared atomized versus drop administration of intranasal midazolam in pediatric patients, their findings regarding onset time, depth of sedation, and overall efficacy have been conflicting, attributable to differences in methodology, patient populations, equipment, and outcome measures.[8] Furthermore, clinical considerations, including whether the demonstrated advantages of atomization justify its added cost and complexity over the simpler and more economical drop method, remain important for evidence-based decision-making in resource-limited settings.[910] This study was therefore designed to provide a direct, controlled comparison of MAD-based atomized delivery versus conventional nasal drops for intranasal midazolam premedication in children between 2–6 years of age undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia, evaluating sedation, anxiolysis, drug acceptance, and adverse events.

Materials and Methods

A prospective, randomized, double-blind clinical trial was done in a tertiary care hospital, Dharwad, Karnataka, over a period of 2 years. The trial was approved by the ethics committee and registered in the Clinical Trial Registry of India. The clinical research was conducted after obtaining written informed consent from parents or legal guardians in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 1975.

Inclusion criteria

Pediatric patients of either sex belonging to American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status 1 and 2 and between 2 years to 6 years of age undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia.

Exclusion criteria

Children with nasal pathology, significant organ dysfunction, mental retardation, and allergy to drugs used in the study were excluded.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the comparison of two independent means using data from previous literature by Åsa Bromfalk et al. on sedation scores. Assuming a mean difference between groups of 1.06 and a pooled standard deviation derived from prior literature (S1 = 1.2561, S2 = 0.95332), the effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated as 0.95332. With a two-sided alpha error (α) of 1% (99% confidence level), a power of 85% required total sample size was estimated to be 60.

Randomization and blinding

Sixty patients were randomly allocated into two groups of 30 each, receiving intranasal midazolam (Neon Laboratories Ltd., Mumbai, India) by using a predetermined computer-generated randomized sequence www.random.org, and allocation concealment was done using sealed opaque envelopes.

Group M (Atomized Group)—(0.3 mg/kg) via a MAD.

Group S (Nasal Drops Group)—(0.3 mg/kg) via a conventional syringe dropper.

This trial was double-blinded as both the patient and anaesthesiologist involved in the study were blinded to the drug being administered. The drug was administered 30 minutes prior to shifting to the operating theater, with each nostril receiving half the calculated dose in the recumbent position.

Data collection and outcome measures

All data were collected using a pre-designed and pre-tested proforma. The data collection form included participant details such as identification number, name, age, weight, sex, diagnosis, and proposed surgery.

The primary outcome, being drug acceptance, was recorded immediately after administration using a 4-point Medicine Acceptance Scale (1 = Excellent, 4 = Poor)

Secondary outcomes like sedation was assessed by a 5-point Sedation Scale (1 = Agitated, 2 = Alert, 3 = Calm, 4 = Drowsy, 5 = Asleep) at 5, 10, 20, and 30 mins, a score of ≥3 was considered adequate sedation, parental separation anxiety assessed using the 4-point Parental Separation Anxiety Scale (PSAS; 1 = Easily separates, 4 = Crying and clings to parents) at the time of shifting to the operation theater and adverse events including cough, sneezing, vomiting, nausea, respiratory depression, and hallucinations if any were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Data were analyzed by SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., USA), and continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or frequencies (%). Categorical variables were analyzed by using the Chi-square test, and the independent samples t-test compared continuous variables between groups. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The clinical trial was conducted in accordance with CONSORT guidelines [Figure 1] in 60 patients. Discussion

Pediatric surgical patients experience significant preoperative anxiety in up to 75% of cases,[11] and unmanaged anxiety can result in emergence delirium, increased analgesic requirements, prolonged recovery, and adverse postoperative behavioural changes in up to 54% of children.[12] Pharmacologic premedication with midazolam, which acts on GABA-A receptors to produce rapid, consistent anxiolysis with anterograde amnesia and minimal respiratory depression, represents the current standard of care for pediatric anxiolysis management.[13] The intranasal route is feasible in children due to its being non-invasive, bypassing first-pass metabolism and rapid absorption through the highly vascular nasal mucosa, without the need for an intravenous line.[14] The conventional nasal drop method is associated with nasopharyngeal runoff, variable drug distribution, and poor patient acceptance. The MAD converts liquid drugs into fine droplets of 30–100 micrometers, potentially enhancing mucosal contact with rapid absorption and drug distribution. Despite the increasing clinical utilization of MAD, there is a lack of studies directly comparing MAD-based intranasal drug delivery to conventional nasal drops for premedication with midazolam in pediatric patients. Therefore, the study was designed to evaluate these two methods, with device acceptance as the primary outcome and sedation score at 30 minutes, anxiolysis at shifting, and adverse events as secondary outcomes. The primary finding of this study was that drug acceptance, a critical determinant of practical premedication success in children was superior with the MAD. About 56.7% of children in the Atomized Group achieved excellent acceptance compared to only 3.3% in the nasal drops group, with 50% of the nasal drops group rated as only fair acceptors (P < 0.001). This dramatic difference likely reflects the more comfortable mist delivery of MAD versus the liquid bolus of drops, which tends to flow into the nasopharynx, causing discomfort, gagging, and distress. Mayel et al. (2020)[15] reported a similarly striking acceptance advantage of 89.8% with intranasal MAD versus 36.9% with oral administration (P ≤ 0.001), while Ahlawat et al. (2019)[16] noted a trend toward better acceptance with nasal spray over drops (80% vs. 56.7%), though this was not statistically significant in their study possibly due to use of a binary rather than graded acceptance scale.

Both methods were found to achieve equivalent sedation scores at the 30-minute primary endpoint (4.30 ± 0.915 vs. 4.13 ± 0.730; P = 0.439). Group M demonstrated significantly higher sedation scores at 5 minutes (2.53 ± 0.776 vs. 1.93 ± 0.583; P = 0.001), indicating a quicker onset of sedation, an advantage with atomization, which has better mucosal deposition and higher bioavailability. This was similar to the study done by Ahlawat et al. (2019),[16] who demonstrated significantly better Ramsay sedation scores with intranasal spray compared to drops, with excellent parent–child separation in 90% of the spray group (0.25 mg/kg) versus 56.7% of the drops group at 20 minutes. Similarly, a higher sedation score was found in Mehdi et al. (2019)[17] with intranasal midazolam spray than oral midazolam (3.21 ± 0.49 vs. 2.18 ± 0.53; P < 0.001), attributing this to the higher bioavailability of the intranasal route (55%–83%) compared to oral administration (40%–50%), while Mayel et al. (2020)[15] confirmed a significantly faster onset with intranasal MAD (17.94 ± 8.99 min) compared to oral midazolam (34.50 ± 11.45 min; P ≤ 0.001), with equivalent final sedation levels similar to our study.

Regarding anxiolysis, the Atomized Group achieved significantly better PSAS scores at the time of shifting (1.17 ± 0.592 vs. 1.53 ± 0.629; P = 0.024), indicating superior parental separation anxiolysis. The mechanism likely relates to the improved mucosal deposition achieved by atomized particles, resulting in higher and more consistent bioavailability compared to drops, which tend to pool in the nasal vestibule or flow posteriorly into the pharynx, where absorption is suboptimal. This finding is supported by Ahlawat et al. (2019),[16] who reported significantly better parent–child separation scores with intranasal spray (mean 1.13 ± 0.43) compared to drops (1.53 ± 0.68; P = 0.021) values remarkably close to our own results. Tyagi et al. (2023)[18] and Mehta et al. (2022)[19] both demonstrated that MAD-administered midazolam resulted in excellent-to-good parental separation in 56.67% of patients in their respective midazolam groups, with both studies reporting comparable effect sizes that serve as corroborating benchmarks for the level of anxiolysis achievable with atomized intranasal delivery. Finally, the reported incidence of adverse events did not differ significantly between the groups (6.7% vs. 20.0%; P = 0.379). The slightly higher rate in the nasal drops group was primarily accounted for by cough and mucosal irritation events, consistent with greater nasopharyngeal runoff in drop-based delivery. Mayel et al. (2020)[15] similarly reported a non-significant difference in adverse events between the intranasal and oral group (29.7% vs. 15.8%; P = 0.15). The single case of hallucination in the nasal drops group represents a known paradoxical reaction to midazolam with a reported incidence of 1%–15%,[14] and Bhakta et al. (2007)[20] confirmed the safe use of intranasal midazolam in pediatric patients with no respiratory depression or apnea, confirming that the dose used in our study of 0.3 mg/kg was both efficacious and safe across both delivery methods.

Conclusion

In preschool children aged 2–6 years, premedication with intranasal midazolam via a MAD had better drug acceptance than conventional nasal drops.

Although both methods achieved comparable sedation, MAD offers faster, more effective, and better-tolerated delivery without added risk and should be preferred in routine pediatric anesthesia practice.
