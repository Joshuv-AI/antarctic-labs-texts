Chidamide induces ROS-driven ZAKα-dependent ribotoxic stress in endometrial cancer and enhances radiosensitivity in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Haocheng Zhang, Zhiling Wang, Xinqin He, Yulong Mu, Xiaonan Fan, Xuan Wei, Hongluan Mao, Shuqi Chi, Zunling Li, Xuehui Pang, Wei Lu, Beihua Kong, Qing Zhang, Yingxin Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chidamide is a selective histone deacetylase inhibitor with antitumor activity in several malignancies, but its mechanism of action in endometrial cancer (EC) remains poorly understood. In this study, we investigated the cytotoxic effects and underlying mechanism of chidamide in EC using cell lines, patient-derived organoids (PDOs), cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Chidamide markedly inhibited EC cell growth and induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, DNA damage, G2/M-phase arrest, and apoptosis. Mechanistically, transcriptomic and functional analyses showed that chidamide disrupted translational homeostasis and activated a ROS-dependent, ZAKα-mediated ribotoxic stress response, leading to p38 and JNK activation. Scavenging ROS with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) attenuated ZAKα–MAPK signaling, and partially rescued chidamide-induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Similarly, pharmacological ZAK inhibition or ZAKα silencing weakened stress signaling and diminished the cytotoxic effects of chidamide, supporting the central role of the ROS–ZAKα–MAPK axis. Chidamide also activated the GCN2–eIF2α–ATF4 integrated stress response, and ISRIB further enhanced stress signaling and apoptosis, suggesting an adaptive buffering role of this pathway. In addition to its activity in vitro, chidamide showed broad antitumor efficacy in PDO, CDX, and PDX models. Chidamide also enhanced tumor radiosensitivity in vivo, supporting its potential utility in combination with radiotherapy. Together, these findings identify ROS-driven, ZAKα-dependent ribotoxic stress signaling as a key mechanism underlying chidamide-induced cytotoxicity in EC and provide a mechanistic rationale for the further development of chidamide-based strategies in this disease.

Background

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common malignant gynecological tumor in developed countries; its incidence has continued to increase in recent decades, and there is a similar upward trend worldwide [1, 2]. Although surgery is still the cornerstone of radical treatment, patients with high-risk EC still face a high recurrence rate and poor long-term prognosis even after undergoing radiotherapy and systemic treatment [3]. In particular, the survival rate of late-stage patients is still very low, which highlights the urgent need to develop more effective treatment strategies for patients with high-risk EC.

Given the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies in EC, epigenetic therapies have emerged as a promising area of investigation. Histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis) are a class of epigenetic drugs with a wide range of antitumour activities. Chidamide is a selective HDACi that targets class I HDACs (HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC3) and HDAC10. Compared with first-generation pan-HDACis, such as vorinostat and trichostatin A, chidamide has greater subtype selectivity and better therapeutic effects. In addition to its established clinical activity in malignant tumors of the blood system [4], chidamide has been shown to have good antitumor effects on a variety of solid tumors, including breast cancer [5], colorectal cancer [6], and non-small cell lung cancer [7]. Previous studies have shown that chidamide can induce cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, autophagy, and antiangiogenic activity [8, 9] and may enhance antitumor immunity when combined with the anti-PD-1 antibody toripalimab [10]. However, the therapeutic potential mechanism of chidamide in EC is unknown.

Recent evidence has shown that in addition to its classic epigenetic effects, chidamide may affect ribosome biogenesis and stress signaling. In acute myeloid leukemia, chidamide disrupts ribosome biogenesis by interfering with c-MYC activity [11]. Ribosome stalling and collision are considered key stress perception events triggered by various types of cell damage, including UV [12] and genotoxic damage [13], and their mechanisms of action are being increasingly emphasized. Mild ribosomal stress can activate the integrated stress response (ISR), leading to the global suppression of protein synthesis [14–16], while ribotoxic stress is perceived by MAP3K ZAKα, which in turn activates the ribotoxic stress response (RSR) and the downstream p38 and JNK signaling pathways, ultimately leading to cell cycle arrest [17, 18] and apoptosis [19].

However, whether the stress response pathway is involved in the cytotoxicity of chidamide in EC is unclear. In this study, we first investigated the molecular basis of chidamide-induced cytotoxicity in EC cell lines and further validated the key mechanistic findings in PDO model. In parallel, CDX and PDX models were used to assess the in vivo antitumor activity of chidamide and its ability to enhance tumor radiosensitivity. We found that chidamide inhibits the growth of EC cells by inducing a ROS-dependent RSR, thereby activating the ZAKα–MAPK signaling pathway and leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism through which chidamide plays a role in EC and provide a theoretical basis for its therapeutic development, especially for the treatment of high-risk diseases.

Material and methods

Reagents

The chemicals and inhibitors used in this study were as follows: chidamide (Selleckchem, #S8567), puromycin (InvivoGen, #ant-pr-1), N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC, MedChemExpress, #HY-B0215), harringtonine (HTN, Selleckchem, #S9063), ZAK inhibitor (gifted from Xiaoyun Lu, Jinan University, Guangzhou, China), ISRIB (Sigma-Aldrich, #SML0843), GCN2 inhibitor A-92 (Selleckchem, #E2987) and PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 (SelleckChem, #S7307), collagenase IV (Sangon Biotech, #A004186), ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (Selleck, #S1049), Matrigel (Corning, #356231).

Cell lines and cell culture

AN3CA, HEC-1A, and RL95-2 cells were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Ishikawa cells were sourced from the ECACC (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures). AN3CA cells were maintained in MEM (Gibco, #11095080) supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, #11360070), 1 × NEAA (Gibco, #11140050), and 10% FBS (Gibco, #A5256701). HEC-1A cells were propagated in McCoy’s 5A (Gibco, #16600082) containing 1× sodium pyruvate and 10% FBS. Ishikawa cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, #11965092) with 10% FBS, whereas RL95-2 cells grew in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, #11320033) with 0.005 mg/mL insulin and 10% FBS. All cultures were maintained in an incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling before experimental use. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely tested and excluded.

TCGA database analysis

RNA-seq expression data and corresponding clinical information for EC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Differential expression and survival analyses were performed using the Xiantao Academic online tool (https://www.xiantaozi.com/). For survival analysis, patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups according to the optimal cutoff value provided by the platform. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were used as the survival endpoints.

Tissue specimens

Primary EC tissues were obtained from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Qilu Hospital, Shandong University, after written informed consent was provided by the patients. All aspects of this study were approved by the Ethics Committee on Scientific Research of Shandong University, Qilu Hospital, Jinan (Approval No. KYLL-202409-048).

Western blot

Whole-cell lysates were prepared by RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, #89900) with protease inhibitors (APExBIO, #K1007) and phosphatase inhibitors (APExBIO, #K1015), then lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C using a filtration column (NCMBiotech, #WB3050). Concentrations of protein were detected by a BCA kit (Thermo Scientific, #23225). Proteins were mixed with loading buffer (Beyotime, #P0015) and denatured by boiling for 5 min. The samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, #GVHP00010). Membranes were blocked in TBST containing 5% skim milk at room temperature (RT) for 1 h to prevent non-specific binding, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. The next day, membranes were washed three times with TBST for 10 min each. The membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT and washed again for 3 times. Lastly, membranes were visualized by Image Quant LAS4010 (GE Healthcare) with ECL Ultra Western HRP substrate (Millipore, #WBULS0100). Detailed information about the antibodies is in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell viability analysis

EC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000–5000 cells per well and exposed to increasing concentrations of chidamide or increasing doses of radiation. 10% CCK-8 solution (MeilunBio, #MA0225) in fresh medium was added to each well, with blank wells receiving CCK-8-containing medium alone as background controls. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Cell apoptosis assays

Apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry using Annexin V/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (GOONIE, #100-105). Cells were detached with Accutase (Sigma-Aldrich, #A6964), washed with PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL 1 × Binding Buffer. Each sample was incubated with 5 μL Annexin V and 5 μL 7-AAD for 10 min at RT in the dark. Stained cells were acquired on a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.4.

Cell cycle assays

For cell cycle, cells were processed using the Cell Cycle Kit (MultiSciences, #CCS012). After permeabilization, cells were stained with PI and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine phase distribution. Data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.4.

ROS detection assays

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA (Beyotime, #CS0033). The probe was diluted 1:1000 in PBS to a final concentration of 10 μM. After removal of the culture medium, cells were incubated with the diluted DCFH-DA solution (at least 1 mL per well in a 6-well plate) at 37 °C for 20 min. Cells were then washed three times with extracellular solution. Fluorescence was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Comet assay

DNA damage was detected using the comet assay (Beyotime, #C2041). After the indicated treatments, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and resuspended at approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were mixed with 0.7% low-melting-point agarose and applied onto slides precoated with 1% normal-melting-point agarose. After solidification at 4 °C, slides were lysed in cold lysis solution (lysis buffer:DMSO = 9:1) at 4 °C overnight. Following lysis, slides were incubated in prechilled alkaline electrophoresis buffer for 20 min and subjected to electrophoresis at 25 V for 20 min in the dark. Slides were then neutralized, stained with PI, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. Comet parameters were quantified using CASP software.

Immunofluorescence

The experiment used a detection kit (Beyotime, #C2035S). Cells cultured on glass slides were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at RT and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 5 min. After blocking with normal goat serum for 30 min at RT, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γ-H2AX, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min at RT. Slides were mounted with antifade medium and sealed with coverslips. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope (Sunny).

RNA-seq and data analysis

For RNA sequencing, HEC-1A and Ishikawa cells were treated with either DMSO or chidamide (5 μM) for 24 h. Total RNA was collected using TRIzol, with three independent biological replicates prepared for each condition. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the limma-voom R package. Genes with a P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Biological process enrichment analysis was executed utilizing the clusterProfiler R package. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed to identify enriched biological processes and pathways.

SUrface SEnsing of Translation (SUnSET) assays

Cells were treated with the indicated reagents, followed by the addition of puromycin (10 μg/mL; InvivoGen, #ant-pr-1) to the culture medium for the specified duration. To ensure consistency, all samples were exposed to the same puromycin concentration for an identical incubation period. After treatment, cells were washed with cold PBS and immediately lysed. The incorporation of puromycin into nascent polypeptide chains was detected by Western blot using anti-puromycin antibodies.

Argyrophilic nucleolar organizer region (AgNOR) staining

AgNOR staining was used to assess ribosome biogenesis using a commercial kit (Saint-Bio, #R23062). The staining solution was freshly prepared by mixing solution A and solution B at a 1:1 ratio. After fixation, cells were incubated with the staining solution and reacted for 30 min at RT. The cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye and imaged by microscope (Olympus). Quantification of AgNOR staining was performed using ImageJ. All experiments were conducted in three independent biological replicates.

Cell transfection

siRNAs and negative control (siNC) targeting ZAKα were bought from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfections of siRNA/siNC were carried out by Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, #11668019). For siRNA transfection, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at approximately 2.5 × 105 cells per well. Transfection was performed after 12 h, when cells reached 30–50% confluence. Cells were incubated for 48 h after transfection before subsequent experiments were performed. Sequences were: si1-ZAKα: 5’- GAAGUGGAUUGUAGGAAUA (dTdT); si2-ZAKα: 5′- CCCAGAGCAAUCCUAUUCU (dTdT); si3-ZAKα: 5′- CAGCAUUCCACUCCUUCAA (dTdT).

Organoid culture

Fresh EC tissues were minced with sterile scissors, washed repeatedly in Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-free PBS, and digested in collagenase IV (2 mg/mL) supplemented with the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (10 µM), then filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer. The filtrate was centrifuged, and the pellet was optionally treated with red blood cell lysis buffer. After centrifugation and one wash with DPBS, cells were resuspended in a mixture of 60% Matrigel and 40% advanced DMEM/F12. Aliquots of 30 µL were plated as droplets in prewarmed 24-well plates and overlaid with 500 µL of organoid culture medium (bioGenous, #K2166-EC), which was refreshed every 5 days. For passaging (every 10–20 days), Matrigel was dissolved by incubating organoids in Cell Recovery Solution (Corning, #354253) at 4 °C for 30 min. Organoids were gently collected using BSA-coated pipette tips and tubes, then dissociated with enzymatic dissociation solution (bioGenous, #E238001) at 37 °C for 7 min. The reaction was quenched by diluting the mixture fivefold with culture medium. The resulting single cell or small cluster suspension was used for replanting, cryopreservation, or downstream experiments.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and scoring

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval with either citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H₂O₂, and non-specific binding was blocked using 5% goat serum. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ki-67 (1:1500), p-p38 (1:600), or p-JNK (1:600), followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB kit (Proteintech, #PR30018). Staining was assessed semi-quantitatively by combining the percentage of positive tumor cells and staining intensity. The percentage score was defined as follows: 0 (<5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (25–50%), 3 (50–75%), 4 (≥75%). Intensity was scored as follows: 0 (negative), 1 (weak, light yellow), 2 (moderate, yellow), or 3 (strong, brown). A final immunoreactivity score (range: 0–12) was obtained by multiplying the two scores.

Synergy evaluation of chemo-radiotherapy in vitro

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at an optimized density and allowed to adhere overnight. They were then treated with chidamide at specified concentrations and exposed to defined doses of X-ray irradiation. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay following the manufacturer’s protocol. Drug–radiation interactions were analyzed with SynergyFinder v3.0, and combination effects were quantified using the Zero Interaction Potency (ZIP) model. A ZIP score <−10 indicated antagonism, −10 to 10 indicated additive effects, and >10 indicated synergism.

Xenograft studies

Nude mice (BALB/c; female; 4–6 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech Company (Jiangsu, China). For CDX model, HEC-1A cells (at 1.0 × 106 cells per mouse) were injected subcutaneously into the right axillary region of nude mice. Treatment was initiated when the tumor volume reached approximately 75 mm³; each group included six mice. For PDX, NCG (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt; female; aged 4–6 weeks) were also purchased from GemPharmatech Company. PDX models were established following previously described protocols. Briefly, freshly resected tumor specimens were minced into fragments approximately 1–2 mm³ in size and suspended in PBS containing 1 × penicillin-streptomycin-Amphotericin B (Thermo Scientific, #15240062). The tissue fragments were then taken subcutaneous implantation into the axillary region of NCG mice within 3 h of surgical collection. Successfully engrafted tumors were designated as passage 1 (P1). When P1 tumors reached a volume of approximately 750 mm³, mice were performed euthanasia, and tumor fragments were either implanted into mice to generate passage 2 (P2) or cryopreserved in 90% FBS and 10% DMSO for future use. P3 tumors were used for the subsequent experiments. When the tumor volumes reached about 75 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (five mice per group). The corresponding treatments were given: Vehicle, chidamide (25 mg/kg), X-ray (2 Gy), and combined usage of chidamide and X-ray. Medication and radiation treatments were administered on alternate days, and tumor volumes were monitored throughout the experiment. At the end, mice were euthanized, and tumors were taken out, photographed, weighed, and their dimensions recorded. All animal handling and experimental procedures followed the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Shandong University and were reviewed and approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of Shandong University, Jinan (Approval No. 24049).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.0. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (ns not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).

Results

Chidamide induces cytotoxic stress, G2/M arrest, and apoptosis in endometrial cancer cells

To provide supporting evidence for HDAC-targeted therapy in EC, we assessed class I HDAC expression and clinical relevance. TCGA analysis and immunoblotting of clinical specimens showed increased class I HDAC expression in EC, while survival analysis indicated that elevated HDAC2 and HDAC3 expression was associated with poorer outcomes (Fig. S1A–F). These findings provided a rationale for evaluating chidamide, a selective class I HDACi, in EC models.

Chidamide reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner after 48 h of treatment at the indicated concentrations, with IC50 values of 1.234 μM, 14.76 μM, 13.31 μM, and 15.14 μM in AN3CA, HEC-1A, Ishikawa, and RL95-2 cells, respectively (Fig. 1A). HEC-1A and Ishikawa cells were selected for subsequent mechanistic studies because they showed moderate chidamide sensitivity and represented biologically distinct EC models, allowing stress signaling analysis while avoiding excessive acute cytotoxicity. Consistently, clonogenic growth was markedly suppressed (Fig. S1G). Chidamide also induced apoptosis and caused G2/M arrest, as demonstrated by immunoblotting of apoptosis- and cell cycle-associated markers (Figs. 1B, C and S1H, I), and by flow cytometric analysis showing increased apoptotic cell fractions and G2/M-phase accumulation (Fig. 1G–J). Chidamide increased γ-H2AX expression (Figs. 1D and S1J), DNA fragmentation (Fig. 1E, F), and these findings were consistent with the immunofluorescence results (Fig. S1K, L). Chidamide disrupts ribosomal homeostasis and activates ribotoxic stress signaling in endometrial cancer cells

To define the molecular basis of chidamide activity in EC, we performed RNA sequencing using HEC-1A and Ishikawa cells treated with chidamide at 5 μM, a biologically active concentration selected to induce measurable molecular changes while minimizing secondary effects from extensive cell death. Chidamide induced broad transcriptional remodeling in both cell lines (Figs. 2A and S2A). In HEC-1A cells, GO analysis of downregulated genes revealed the prominent enrichment of ribosome-associated processes, including ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, ribosome biogenesis, rRNA metabolic process, and rRNA processing (Fig. 2B). Similar enrichment was not observed in Ishikawa cells (Fig. S2B), indicating heterogeneity in the transcriptional response to chidamide. We next examined whether chidamide directly impairs translation. AgNOR staining revealed reduced nucleolar activity after treatment (Fig. 2D, E). SUnSET assays consistently revealed a marked reduction in puromycin incorporation following chidamide treatment, indicating the rapid suppression of global protein synthesis (Figs. 2F and S2D). Within the first 24 h of treatment, this effect occurred without the obvious induction of cleaved PARP, suggesting that translational inhibition precedes overt apoptosis. Because ribosome dysfunction can activate stress-responsive signaling, we next analyzed downstream pathways. KEGG analysis revealed the MAPK signaling pathway among the most significantly enriched pathways in both HEC-1A and Ishikawa cells (Figs. 2C and S2C). Consistent with the activation of the RSR, chidamide increased the phosphorylation of ZAKα, p38, and JNK; the phosphorylation of eIF2α was also elevated (Figs. 2H and S2F, G). Together, these results indicate that chidamide rapidly disrupts ribosomal homeostasis at both the biogenesis and translational levels while engaging parallel ribotoxic and adaptive stress signaling programs.

ROS accumulation drives chidamide-induced translational stress and downstream cytotoxic signaling in EC cells

Given that chidamide disrupted translational homeostasis and activated ribotoxic stress signaling, we next examined whether reactive oxygen species (ROS) act upstream of this response. Flow cytometric analysis using the probe revealed that chidamide increased intracellular ROS levels in both HEC-1A and Ishikawa cells (Fig. 3A, B). To determine whether translational suppression depends on ROS, cells were pretreated with the ROS scavenger 10 mM NAC for 30 min before chidamide exposure and then subjected to SUnSET analysis. NAC markedly restored puromycin incorporation (Figs. 3C and S3A), indicating that ROS contribute to chidamide-induced translational inhibition functionally. Immunoblotting analysis further revealed that chidamide increased the phosphorylation of ZAKα, p38, JNK, and eIF2α; however, pre-treatment with NAC substantially attenuated these effects (Figs. 3D and S3B, C). At the phenotypic level, NAC significantly reduced apoptosis (Fig. 3E, F), partially reversed G2/M arrest (Fig. 3G, H), decreased apoptotic marker activation (Figs. 3I and S3D, E), and partially restored p-CDK1T161/CDK1 and Cyclin B1 levels (Figs. 3J and S3F, G). To exclude potential off-target effects of pharmacological inhibition, we next performed genetic validation using siRNA-mediated ZAKα knockdown. Among the three candidate siRNAs, si-ZAKα#1 and si-ZAKα#2 showed the highest knockdown efficiencies and were selected for subsequent experiments (Figs. 4G and S4C). And this effect was further validated with an independent siRNA, si-ZAKα#2, in the supplementary experiments (Fig. S4D). We then examined whether ZAKα is required for downstream stress signaling. Immunoblotting analysis showed that pharmacological ZAK inhibition and transient ZAKα knockdown using two independent siRNAs markedly attenuated chidamide-induced phosphorylation of p38 and JNK (Figs. 4J, K and S4E–H). Together, these pharmacological and genetic loss-of-function data support ZAKα as a key upstream mediator linking chidamide-induced stress signaling to p38/JNK activation, thereby contributing to apoptosis and G2/M arrest in EC cells.

Chidamide activates a GCN2–eIF2α-dependent adaptive stress response that restrains ribotoxic signaling in EC cells

To further determine whether the downstream pathway of the ISR was activated, we examined the expression of ATF4, a canonical downstream effector of eIF2α phosphorylation. To identify the upstream kinase responsible for eIF2α phosphorylation, we pharmacologically inhibited GCN2 or PERK using GCN2 inhibitor (A-92, 1 μM, 30 min pre-treatment) and PERK inhibitor (GSK2606414, 1 μM, 30 min pre-treatment), respectively. These data revealed that the correlation between the ISR and translational stress induced by chidamide is stronger than that between the ISR and endoplasmic reticulum stress. To determine whether the ISR functionally modulates chidamide-induced ribotoxic stress signaling pathway, we pretreated cells with integrated stress response inhibitor (ISRIB, 1 μM, 30 min pre-treatment). ISRIB acts downstream of eIF2α phosphorylation by restoring eIF2B-mediated translation initiation, thereby counteracting ISR-associated translational repression. Chidamide dose-dependently increased the phosphorylation of p38 and JNK (Fig. 5C, lanes 1–3; Fig. S5C), and ISRIB pretreatment further enhanced this effect (Fig. 5C, lanes 4–6; Fig. S5C). ISRIB also increased the ZAKα phosphorylation induced by chidamide (Figs. 5D and S5D) and further increased the levels of cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3 (Figs. 5E and S5E). These results indicate that chidamide can activate the GCN2–eIF2α-dependent ISR pathway, which has an adaptive inhibitory effect, limiting ZAKα–p38/JNK signaling and weakening apoptosis. Therefore, in addition to the RSR that promotes apoptosis, chidamide can also activate a parallel protective pathway to buffer translational stress in EC cells.

To evaluate the molecular changes related to treatment in vivo, we conducted IHC analysis of PDX tumors. Compared with those in the control group and the single treatment group, p-p38 and p-JNK staining tended to be greater in the combined treatment group, a finding that was consistent with further regulation of the stress MAPK signaling pathway in vivo (Fig. 7I–K). γ-H2AX and cleaved caspase-3 staining in the combined treatment group increased, and the expression of Ki-67 decreased (Fig. 7I, L–N), indicating that DNA damage increased, apoptosis increased, and cell proliferation decreased. Discussion

Because the mechanism of action of chidamide in EC has remained unclear despite its known antitumor activity in other malignancies, this study aimed to define its antitumor effects in EC and elucidate the molecular basis underlying its cytotoxicity. Our research revealed that chidamide, a subtype-selective HDACi that has been confirmed to be active against hematologic malignancies, exerts marked antitumor effects in EC. In addition to the classical role of HDACis in reshaping chromatin accessibility and transcription, our data support a model in which the oxidative stress induced by chidamide disrupts translational homeostasis and activates the ZAKα-dependent RSR signaling pathway, linking ribosomal stress to downstream p38/JNK activation and cytotoxicity [23]. Previous studies have shown that HDACis can perturb redox homeostasis by impairing antioxidant defense [24, 25] and promoting mitochondrial ROS production [26]. Thus, chidamide-induced ROS accumulation in EC cells may arise from broader HDACi-associated redox dysregulation and serve as an upstream trigger for translational stress and ZAKα/MAPK activation. The pharmacological and genetic disruption of ZAKα confirms that ZAKα plays a core role in this pathway. Overall, these findings indicate that chidamide exerts antitumor activity in EC through a mechanism involving ROS-driven translational stress and activation of the ZAKα–MAPK axis, in addition to its epigenetic effects.

In addition to this central mechanism, an important conceptual development of this study is that chidamide not only activates the pro-death stress pathway but also activates adaptive responses. Like the RSR, chidamide also activated the GCN2-dependent ISR, indicating that it activates an adaptive stress program associated with translational disturbance, not classic endoplasmic reticulum stress [27]. ISRIB further enhanced stress signaling and apoptosis; this finding is consistent with the previous studies [28, 29]. The ISR acts as a protective buffer and limits the cytotoxic signaling induced by chidamide. In cancer contexts, the ISR often functions as a pro-survival mechanism, enabling tumor cells to adapt to stress and resist therapy [30, 31]. These findings support a model in which EC cells engage in two parallel but functionally different programs after being stimulated by chidamide: ZAKα-dependent RSR, which can amplify cytotoxicity signals, and GCN2-dependent ISR, which can limit the spread of stress. Therefore, the balance between these pathways may be an important determinant of therapeutic sensitivity and may help explain the differences in cellular responses. In line with this interpretation, basal class I HDAC expression alone may not fully explain the differential sensitivity of EC cell lines to chidamide, suggesting that context-dependent stress responses and apoptotic susceptibility may also influence drug sensitivity.

Consistent with this interpretation, our research revealed that ribosomal stress and translational stress are the core effects of chidamide in EC. Although HDACs are often regarded as epigenetic regulators [32], there is growing evidence that their downstream effects may converge in a broader network of stress adaptation [33, 34]. Rather than concluding that chidamide directly damages ribosomes, our findings support a model in which oxidative stress converges on ribosomal function [35], leading to translational disturbances and activating ribosome-surveillance pathways, consistent with previous findings [21]. Our results further support that stress signaling centered on ribosomes can serve as an important interface in cell fate control in cancer cells.

Importantly, these mechanistic findings from the present study were verified not only in traditional EC cell models but also in PDOs and xenograft models, supporting its biological robustness and translational significance. In PDOs, NAC and ZAKi rescue experiments supported the functional involvement of ROS accumulation and ZAKα signaling in chidamide-induced growth suppression. Xenograft models further confirmed the in vivo antitumor efficacy of chidamide and showed increased p38/JNK phosphorylation in tumor tissues, although whether this MAPK activation is directly mediated by the ROS–ZAKα axis in vivo remains to be further validated. In addition, chidamide enhanced the antitumor effect of radiotherapy both in vitro and in vivo. This radiosensitization was associated with increased DNA damage, reduced proliferation, and increased apoptosis signals in tumor tissue, indicating that chidamide may enhance the radiation response by amplifying stress-related damage signals and limiting the recovery of tumor cells [36, 37].

The enhanced antitumor effect observed with chidamide plus radiotherapy may be partly explained by the convergence of radiation-induced DNA damage and oxidative stress with chidamide-induced ROS–ZAKα signaling. Ionizing radiation can directly induce DNA damage [38] and also generate ROS through water radiolysis and radiation-associated metabolic oxidative stress [39], while ROS are closely involved in DNA damage responses induced by genotoxic therapies [40]. Recent evidence further shows that DNA damage can trigger p53-independent apoptosis through ribosome stalling, supporting a functional link between genotoxic stress and ribosome-associated stress signaling [41]. Thus, radiotherapy-induced DNA damage and oxidative stress may cooperate with chidamide-induced translational disturbance and ZAKα-dependent p38/JNK activation, thereby lowering the threshold for apoptosis. However, whether the ROS–ZAKα axis directly drives chidamide-mediated radiosensitization remains to be validated in the combined treatment setting. Given that radiotherapy is still an important part of the postoperative treatment of high-risk EC, these findings warrant further evaluation of chidamide as a potential radiosensitizer in EC treatment.

Taken together, the antitumor effects of chidamide, the involvement of the ROS–ZAKα–MAPK axis, and the adaptive buffering role of the ISR may have broader translational significance. Despite the use of multimodal treatment, high-risk EC is still associated with a high recurrence rate and poor long-term prognosis, which highlights the need for treatment strategies that address tumor-specific vulnerability. Our results show that tumors with increased oxidative stress levels, impaired translational regulation, or increased dependence on the adaptive stress buffer mechanism may be particularly sensitive to chidamide-based treatment. Although this possibility needs to be further verified, the components of the ROS–ZAKα–MAPK axis and markers of ISR activation should be explored as candidate biomarkers for response stratification. More broadly, the observation that the ISR inhibits the cytotoxicity induced by chidamide shows that the combination of chidamide with drugs that inhibit the adaptive stress response may be a reasonable strategy to improve therapeutic effects [42].

The following limitations need to be noted. EC is a heterogeneous molecular disease. We observed situation-dependent differences between different model systems, especially at the transcriptional level, which indicates that the relative contributions of the RSR and ISR may vary depending on the tumor subtype or cell state. Although our data support the central role of ZAKα, further research is needed to clarify more accurately how the oxidative stress induced by chidamide is coupled with ribosomal dysfunction at the biochemical level. Although our functional data are consistent with those of previous studies, which link translational disturbance with ZAKα-dependent ribotoxic stress signaling, we did not use higher-resolution methods such as sucrose gradient fractionation or ribosome profiling to directly evaluate ribosome distribution. Future studies using these approaches may help define how oxidative stress perturbs ribosome dynamics, clarify the precise step at which translation is impaired, and identify subtype-specific vulnerabilities that could inform patient stratification and combination therapy design.

Our study supports a mechanistic model in which chidamide induces oxidative stress-associated translational disruption, leading to activation of ZAKα-dependent MAPK signaling and concurrent engagement of the ISR. This dual stress response suggests that HDACi can engage ribosome-associated stress signaling and promote programmed cell death. These interconnected pathways appear to underlie the intrinsic cytotoxicity of chidamide and may also contribute to radiosensitization. Notably, pharmacological ISR antagonism with ISRIB partially overcomes the adaptive pro-survival buffering induced by chidamide, thereby further enhancing stress signaling and apoptosis. Together, our findings provide a rationale for further investigating chidamide in high-risk EC and support the development of rational combination strategies that exploit the balance between stress adaptation and apoptotic commitment.
